MS4A1 regulates M1-polarized tumor-associated macrophage infiltration, angiogenesis, and cancer progression through the HIPPO pathway in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Weitong Gao, Ruqiong Wang, Shuangshuang Yang, Yingnan Shi, Mengen Cui, Renjie Jiang, Rou Li, Yan Yu, Dexin Jia, Dehai Che
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) treatment remains a substantial clinical challenge, warranting a thorough investigation of the molecular mechanisms underlying tumor progression. In our study, the expression patterns and clinical implications of the macrophage M1 related gene, membrane spanning 4-domains A1 (MS4A1) were assessed using quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR), western blotting, and immunohistochemistry in LUAD. A co-culture system involving LUAD cells and macrophages assessed macrophage polarization and recruitment using RT-qPCR, flow cytometry, immunofluorescence, and macrophage chemotaxis assay. Additionally, transcriptome sequencing, tube formation, and co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated the roles and mechanisms underlying MS4A1 in HIPPO pathway and angiogenesis in cancer progression and M1 polarization. Furthermore, the efficacy of antagonists against angiogenesis and HIPPO pathway including ivonescimab and verteporfin was assessed in MS4A1 low tumor-bearing mouse receiving immunotherapy. Results showed that the significant prognostic association of MS4A1 expression was specific to LUAD. Furthermore, MS4A1 facilitated the malignant progression of LUAD by modulating macrophage M1 polarization, migration, and angiogenesis. Mechanistically, MS4A1 interacted with Yes-associated protein to regulate macrophage polarization and cancer progression through the HIPPO pathway. In vivo experiments also demonstrated that verteporfin inhibited the in-situ tumor progression and ivonescimab enhanced the efficacy of immunotherapy in MS4A1 low tumor-bearing mouse. The MS4A1/M1 macrophage axis was identified as a crucial regulator of malignancy in LUAD, indicating MS4A1 as a promising novel therapeutic target for advanced LUAD treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04213-x.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, M1 macrophage, Membrane spanning 4-domains A1, HIPPO pathway, Angiogenesis

Background

Lung cancer represents one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies worldwide [1]. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), a predominant subtype, accounts for approximately 40% of all lung cancer cases [2]. Immunotherapy and targeted therapy have moderately enhanced the overall survival (OS) rate of patients with LUAD [3, 4]. Nevertheless, treatment heterogeneity frequently leads to drug resistance and progress to metastatic LUAD [5]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD onset and development is central to exploring therapeutic targets and improving prognosis.

Tumor growth, metastasis, and anti-tumor immune responses occur within a complex ecosystem; thus, understanding the composition and regulatory networks within the tumor microenvironment (TME) is crucial to improving patient prognosis [6]. Macrophages within the TME are functionally classified into two major subgroups, namely M1 and M2, which exhibit distinct functions and a high degree of heterogeneity [7]. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are central to infection resistance, immune regulation, and tissue repair [8]. M1 macrophages are primarily stimulated by tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and lipopolysaccharides (LPS); they secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, including C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), TNF-α, and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ). Moreover, they express high levels of cluster of differentiation (CD)80, CD86, and inducible nitric oxide synthase, contributing to anti-tumor activity and immune activation [9]. The M1 polarization of TAMs inhibits tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis and is inversely correlated with malignant progression and poor prognosis in cancer, making it a potential immunotherapy target for LUAD [10–12].

Membrane spanning 4-domains A1 (MS4A1), also termed CD20, belongs to the membrane-spanning 4A gene family; it is predominantly located on the surface of B lymphocytes and is involved in B cell maturation and differentiation into plasma cells [13, 14]. The role of MS4A1 in solid tumors is unclear; nonetheless, its prognostic implications in various cancers and its association with immune cell infiltration have been described in bioinformatics studies [15–18]. For instance, MS4A1 serves as an independent prognostic biomarker in breast cancer, where elevated MS4A1 expression is correlated with good prognosis [15]. High MS4A1 expression has been strongly associated with TME activation and immune cell related pathways. Additionally, in human soft tissue sarcoma, the prognostic effect of MS4A1-expressing B cells is observed primarily in low-infiltrated TME of M2 macrophages, implying immunosuppressive macrophages diminish the favorable prognostic impact of CD20 + B cells in tumors [19]. However, the role of MS4A1 expression in tumor cells and its interaction with macrophages remains unclear.

In this study, we confirmed the role of macrophage M1 related gene, MS4A1 in the tumor growth inhibition at the cellular, tissue and in vivo levels as well as in the macrophage polarization and recruitment using the co-culture system involving lung cancer cells and macrophages. Mechanistically, MS4A1 not only regulated the angiogenesis but interacted with Yes-associated protein(YAP) through the HIPPO pathway to regulate macrophage polarization and cancer progression. Methods

Analysis of immune cell landscape in LUAD

Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) is an algorithm designed for deconvolution. It utilizes the expression levels of 547 marker genes to define the immune cell composition within tissue samples through linear support vector regression [20]. In this study, CIBERSORT was utilized to assess the relative abundance of 22 tumor-infiltrating immune cell types in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-LUAD cohort.

Establishing co-expression network

To analyze TCGA-LUAD expression data, the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) package was utilized. It focused on correlating the expression of M1 macrophages in the samples and identified the gene cluster most strongly associated genes with M1 macrophage abundance across LUAD cases [21]. First, transcript expression levels were transformed into a similarity matrix, which was converted into a topological overlap matrix based on an optimal soft thresholding parameter. Second, similar expression patterns were assigned to their respective modules; the module that exhibited the strongest correlation with M1 macrophage after module and similarity analysis was identified.

Identification of prognostic macrophage-related genes

To identify high-confidence genes linking M1 macrophages to LUAD prognosis, we integrated WGCNA with differential expression analysis [22]. This strategy prioritized genes central to the M1 co-expression network (ME-black module) that are also clinically significant. The final gene set was defined as the intersection between the ME-black module genes and prognostic DEGs, visualized in a Venn diagram. This intersection ensures the selected genes are simultaneously part of the M1-associated network, dysregulated in LUAD, and predictive of patient outcome.

Construction of the risk score model and gene selection

Patients from TCGA-LUAD cohort were randomly categorized into training and validation sets. A risk prediction model was developed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis, conducted with the R package ‘‘glmnet’’. LASSO regression enables variable screening and model complexity adjustment for fitting generalized linear models. The risk score was calculated as follows: Risk score = (expression level of gene A * Coefficient (Coef) A) + (expression level of gene B * Coef B) + … + (expression level of gene N * Coef N). Survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier (KM) method, and survival rates between groups were compared using the log-rank test. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted using the R package ‘‘survival ROC,’’ and the corresponding area under the ROC (AUC) was calculated. Subsequently, the gene with the largest absolute value of the coefficient in the LASSO model was selected for subsequent experimental validation, as it contributes most significantly to the predictive power of the risk score.

Specimens and cell culture

All patient tissue samples were obtained from the Department of Pulmonology, Harbin medical university cancer hospital after providing informed consent. The pathological findings were evaluated by a minimum of two experienced pathologists from the Department of Pulmonology, Harbin medical university cancer hospital. The H1650, H3255, A549, human bronchial epithelial (HBE), 795, THP-1, Raji B cells and Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cells were cultured in 1640 medium (Gibico, C11875500BT) or Endothelial Cell Medium (science cell, 1001) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (PAN, P30-3306) and Penicillin / Streptomycin / Amphotericin B (Solarbio, P7630). All cells were kept in an incubator set at 37 °C with the CO2 concentration of 5%.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from the cells utilizing total RNA kit (OMEGA, R6834-01) following the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. Reverse transcription of total RNA was conducted with FastKing One Step RT-qPCR Kit (SYBR) (TIANGEN, FP313). RT-qPCR analysis was conducted using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche) by Thermo Fisher Scientific QuantStudio™ Real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR instrument. β-actin was utilized as a normalized internal control, and the relative expression levels were determined using the 2 (−∆∆Ct) method.

Western blotting analysis (WB)

WB analysis was conducted following the protocol we previously outlined [23]. Primary antibodies included anti-MS4A1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 60271-1-Ig), anti-vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) (1:1000, Proteintech, 19003-1-AP), anti-YAP (1:1000, baijia, IPB0668), anti-Phosphorylation YAP (1:1000, baijia, IPH0211), anti-MST (1:1000, Proteintech, 22245-1-AP), anti-Phosphorylation MST (1:1000, Proteintech, 80093-1-RR), anti-TEAD (1:500, Abways, CY9838), and Beta tubulin (1:1000, Proteintech, 66240-1-Ig).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Specimens of tumor or adjacent non-tumor tissue were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned to a thickness of 4 μm. The IHC score was identified by the staining positive rate score (negative, 1–25%, 26–50%, 51–75%, 76–100% were set as 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4, respectively), as delineated in previous investigations. We classified IHC scores of the tumor sections ≥ 6 as “high expression” group.

Plasmids and transfection

MS4A1-short hairpin RNA (shRNA) and MS4A1 overexpression plasmid were provided by Miaoling Biology. All constructed plasmids were transfected into cells using jetPRIME (Polyplus, 101000046) based on the manufacturer’s instructions.

Wound healing assay

H1650, A549 and H3255 cell lines were cultured in six-well plates until 80% confluence was achieved. A 200 μl pipette tip was used to create three scratches across the cell layer. The cells were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove floating cells; subsequently, they were incubated in a medium containing 0.5% FBS for 12 h. Photographs of the scratches were captured at 0 and 12 h under a microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., USA) at a 10 × magnification.

Transwell assay

Cells at a density of 3 × 10^4-5 × 10^4 were starved overnight and seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (Corning 3422), with or without 40 µl diluted Matrigel for invasion and migration assays, respectively. The lower and upper chambers consisted of 1640 medium, with or without 10% FBS, respectively. After incubation, the cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China). The stained colonies were photographed in three random fields per well using a microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., USA) and counted using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Two-dimensional colony formation assay

H1650, A549 and H3255 cell lines were seeded at a density of 600 cells per well in six-well plates and cultured for 10 to 14 days until colony formation. The colonies were treated with 4% PFA for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China). Finally, the stained colonies were photographed using a microscope and counted using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Cell proliferation assays

Cells were counted and plated in 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 10^3 cells per well. After 24 h of incubation, 10 μl of the Cell Counting Kit-8 ( CCK-8) reagent (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) was added to the culture medium and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. The optical density (OD) was recorded at 450 nm using the SpectraMax Paradigm multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Device, CA, USA). All values were normalized by referencing the OD values obtained from untreated cells.

Coculture assays

An in vitro coculture system was established for macrophages and lung cancer cells using a 24 mm transwell chamber with polycarbonate membranes containing 0.4 µm pores (Corning, USA). First, THP-1 monocytes (1 × 10^6 per well) were differentiated into M0 macrophages using 150 nM phorbol 12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA) (Med Chem Express, China) in the upper chamber. In contrast, LUAD cells (5 × 10^5 per well) were plated in the lower chamber. After 24 h, the upper chamber was plated onto the lower chamber for cocultivation. After an additional 12 h, the cells were harvested for analysis.

Flow cytometry (FC) analysis

First, macrophages derived from THP-1 cells were collected using cold PBS. Second, the cells were resuspended in staining buffer (1 × PBS containing 1% FBS) and stained with an anti-CD68 antibody (BioLegend, #333805, 1:100 dilution). Third, after treatment with a fixation and permeabilization solution (BD Biosciences, USA), the cells were stained with an anti-CD80 antibody (BioLegend, #305231, 1:100 dilution) for 40 min on ice. Cell suspensions from murine tumors were prepared by mechanical disruption with collagenase digestion (37 °C, 1-2 h), followed by 70 μm filtration. After viability staining (BioLegend), cells were washed with PBS and immunostained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies (BD Biosciences anti- CD45 antibody #557659, anti- F4/80 antibody #565411, anti- CD11b antibody #557397, Elabscience anti- CD80 antibody E-AB-F0992H) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Finally, they were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Biosciences, USA). Data were processed with FlowJo software.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

For cell IF, cells on glass coverslips were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min and blocked with 5% FBS for 1 h. The cells were incubated overnight in a humidified chamber with primary antibodies, including CD68 (Invitrogen, 14-0688-82, 1:300 dilution) and CD80 (Invitrogen, PA5-85913, 1:300 dilution) at 4 °C. The next day, secondary antibodies, namely CoraLite488 anti-rabbit and CoraLite594 anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech, USA), were applied to the cells at 1:300 dilution. Nuclei were stained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 5 min. Finally, coverslips were mounted onto glass slides and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., USA). Data were processed using ImageJ software.

Macrophage chemotaxis assay

The effect of lung cancer cells on M1 macrophage chemotaxis, induced by LPS (100 ng/ml) (Peprotech, USA) and IFN-γ (20 ng/ml) (Peprotech, USA), was assessed using Transwell inserts containing an 8 µm pore-size polyethylene membrane (Corning, USA) in a 24-well plate. M1 macrophages (5 × 10^4 per well) were seeded into the upper chamber for 4 h. The supernatant from the coculture system was applied to the lower chamber. After 48 h of incubation, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime, China). The stained colonies were photographed for three random fields per well under a microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., USA) and counted using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA).

Multiplexed Immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

For mIHC, tissue microarray sections (Shanghai Outdo Biotech Co., Ltd) underwent standard deparaffinization using xylene followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series. Heat-induced epitope retrieval was performed using antigen retrieval buffer. Subsequent blocking procedures included: (1) quenching endogenous peroxidase activity with peroxidase blocking solution, and (2) preventing nonspecific antibody binding with bovine serum albumin (BSA).The antibody panel comprised primary antibodies against MS4A1/Pan-CK/CD19/Ki67 and MS4A1/ CD80/CD86. Following primary antibody incubation, sections were treated with Opal polymerized HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse/rabbit). Multiplex fluorescent labeling was achieved through sequential incubation with Opal Polaris fluorophores (670,480,570 and 520; 650, 520 and 570; 1:100 dilution per manufacturer's protocol). Whole-slide digital imaging was performed at 20 × magnification using the Vectra Polaris multispectral imaging system (Akoya Biosciences, USA) for high-resolution acquisition of stained tissue sections.

Tube-formation assay

Matrigel was spread into 24-well plates at a volume of 200 µl per well and incubated for 30 min at 37℃. HUVECs at a density of 9 × 10^4 per well were plated with 200 µl Endothelial Cell Growth Medium (Science Cell, #1001) and 10% FBS. The cells were incubated for 6 h. Photographs were captured for three random fields per well under a microscope (Leica Microsystems Inc., USA). Additionally, the number of junction branches was counted using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA).

RNA sequencing and data analysis

First, total RNA was extracted from lung cancer cells using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) following the standard protocol. RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. Second, poly-A-containing mRNAs were isolated using magnetic beads conjugated with oligo (dT) and fragmented randomly using Fragmentation Buffer (New England Biolabs, USA). Third, cDNA libraries were constructed through mRNA reverse transcription, followed by polymerase chain reaction. Fourth, the libraries were sequenced using a HiSeq X platform (Illumina). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the H1650 shNC and shMS4A1 groups were detected using the DESeq2 software. Finally, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the ClusterProfile software. A single-sample gene set enrichment analysis was conducted utilizing the GSVA package.

Co-immunoprecipitation

The cells were washed three times with cold PBS, and cell lysates were collected at 4 °C using a lysis buffer with protease inhibitors (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Pre-clearing of 500 μl of cell lysates (containing 200-1000 µg of total protein) was conducted using 5 μl of protein A/ G agarose beads (Med Chem Express, China) for 2 h at 4 °C. The cell lysates (500 μL) were mixed with 4 μg of a specific antibody or a normal IgG antibody and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Immune complexes were collected and washed six times with cold immunoprecipitation buffer, centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 2 min each time, and analyzed by WB.

Animal experiments

Female nude mice (BALB/c, 4 weeks) and female mice (C57BL/6, 6 weeks) were bought from Beijing Vital Li Hua Experimental Animal Technology Company of Beijing, China. For the construction of subcutaneous xenograft model, 2.5 × 10^7/150 μl H3255 cells without or with stable overexpression of MS4A1 (H3255 vector and H3255-MS4A1) and 2.5 × 10^7/150 μl H1650 cells without or with MS4A1 knockdown (shNC and shMS4A1) were subcutaneously injected into the left or right of 4-week-old BALB/c nude mice (n = 6 per group). The advancement of xenograft growth was assessed on day 7 and 28 using in vivo imaging system.

For the construction of orthotopical xenograft model, 1 × 10^6/50 μl 795 shMS4A1 cells were injected orthotopically into the mice (n = 16). For the drug intervention study, one week after the completion of the model construction, the mice were randomly allocated into four groups (n = 4 per group). For immunotherapy and antiangiogenic therapy, in situ tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice were injected intraperitoneally with 200 mg Ivonescimab or Tislelizumab twice per week for 2 weeks. For targeting HIPPO pathway, Verteporfin (100 mg/kg) (Med Chem Express, China) was intraperitoneally injected into mice once every three days for six times. The advancement of xenograft growth was assessed after completing the treatment using in vivo imaging system. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized, the tumor was dissected, weighed, and fixed in formalin for using IHC and HE staining. The animal experiments were carried out under a project license (NO.KY2017-12) awarded by the Third Clinical Ethics Committee of Harbin Medical University. All animal studies were conducted in line with the guidelines and regulations of Harbin Medical University institutional animal care.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted independently three times. Statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 6.0 software (San Diego, California, USA). All data were shown as the mean ± SD, unless declared. Data were analyzed using Student’s t-test for two groups or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for three or more groups. P value of < 0.05 was regarded as statistical significance. Significance levels are denoted as follows: ns (not significant), *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

Results

Identification and functional characterization of M1 macrophage-related DEGs

First, the CIBERSORT algorithm was utilized to analyze the expression patterns of 22 types of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in TCGA-LUAD cohort. It facilitated assessing the abundance of immune cell subpopulations across all samples (Table S1). The infiltration level of immune cells in LUAD and normal tissues was shown in Figure S1A. Second, WGCNA was applied to TCGA-LUAD expression data, based on macrophage abundance, to identify the most relevant gene clusters. The soft-thresholding power was set to 5 (Fig. 1A–B). Figure 1C illustrates that WGCNA identified eight modules, with the black module being strongly correlated with M1 macrophages; therefore, they were identified as M1-associated MRGs (r = 0.4, P = 5e-23, Table S2-3). Figure 1D depicted the convergence of our gene selection criteria. The overlap between genes in the M1-correlated ME-black module and LUAD-specific DEGs yielded a core set of 13 candidates. This intersection thereby identifies genes that are not only integral to the M1 macrophage network but also predictor of LUAD prognosis, ensuring high biological and clinical relevance (Table S4). Additionally, we constructed a protein–protein interaction network for the 60 genes comprising the ME-black module using the STRING database. Strikingly, network topology analysis revealed that the 13 prognostic M1-associated MRGs were predominantly occupied central hubs within the network, forming a densely interconnected core (FigureS1B). The inter relationship among 13 genes was depicted in Figure S1C-D. The correlation of 13 genes with prognosis of LUAD patients was shown in forest plot, implying the 13 genes were protective factors for prognosis (Fig. 1E). The volcano map in Fig. 1F indicated the 2421 DEGs related to LUAD prognosis in which the 13 genes mentioned above were marked. Moreover, the relationship of 60 MRGs with M1 infiltration was illustrated in Fig. 1G. GO and KEGG analysis results suggested that these 13 M1-associated MRGs were enriched in immune-related biological processes, including B cell proliferation, activation, differentiation, cell surface receptor pathways, immune regulation, primary immunodeficiency, and B cell receptor signaling pathways (Fig. 1H–I).

Selection and validation of the M1-associated MRG, MS4A1 for prognosis in LUAD

Utilizing TCGA-LUAD cohort as the training set, LASSO regression analysis was conducted to reduce dimensionality. Three M1-associated MRGs, namely Toll-Like Receptor 10 (TLR10), MS4A1, and Cluster of Differentiation 79A (CD79A), were identified as the most suitable candidate genes (Figure S3A-B, lambda.min = 0.0321). Above three genes correlated with M1 infiltration, based on CIBERSORT results, are illustrated in Figure S1E-G. The correlation of TLR10, CD79A and MS4A1 with lymph node metastasis and stage was shown in Figure S2A-B. KM survival analysis consistently demonstrated a significant survival difference between high and low MS4A1 expression groups across both the TCGA-LUAD cohort and independent validation datasets (GSE72094 and GSE37745 cohort), underscoring its robust and independent prognostic value (Figure S2C-E). In contrast, the survival curves for TLR10 and CD79A expression groups did not reach statistical significance in one or more datasets (Figure S2C-E).

The risk score was calculated as follows: Risk score = (−0.0045) *TLR10 + (−0.1136) * MS4A1 + (−0.0016) * CD79A (Table S5). The patients were categorized into high- and low-risk groups based on the median value. The prognosis was assessed for both training and validation sets. In TCGA-LUAD training set, scatter plots of survival status indicated that high-risk patients were more likely to experience mortality, whereas low-risk patients were more likely to survive. The heatmap demonstrated lower gene expression in high-risk patients, compared with low-risk patients (Figure S3C). KM curve analysis indicated a prognostic difference, with low-risk patients reporting a better prognosis (P < 0.05) (Figure S3D). The time-dependent ROC generated AUC values of 0.665 for the first year’s survival, respectively, exhibiting the predictive performance (Figure S3E). In the GSE72094 validation set, the KM difference was observed between the high- and low-risk patients (P < 0.05) (Figure S3F). The time-dependent ROC generated AUC values of 0.76 for the first year’s survival, respectively (Figure S3G).

MS4A1 was identified as the dominant contributor to the model, evidenced by its largest absolute coefficient. Consequently, MS4A1 was chosen for further investigation. To validate the specificity of MS4A1 in LUAD prognosis, we analyzed MS4A1 expression levels in both cell lines and tissue samples of breast cancer carcinoma (BRCA), and esophageal cancer (ESCC) using WB and IHC analysis. MS4A1 levels showed no consistent pattern of dysregulation in these cancer types compared to their normal counterparts (Figure S4A-F). Additionally, KM survival analysis across multiple TCGA cancer types demonstrated a heterogeneous pattern. MS4A1 expression was a significant prognostic factor in several malignancies, including LUAD. However, its prognostic value was not universal, as no significant survival difference was observed between MS4A1 high- and low-expression groups in other major cancer types, such as lung squamous cell (LUSC), bladder urothelial Carcinoma (BLCA), BRCA, ESCC, low-grade glioma (LGG), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), stomach adenocarcinoma (SATD) (Figure S4G). This pan-cancer analysis indicates that the prognostic significance of MS4A1 is context-dependent and may be particularly relevant to the biological landscape of LUAD.

Downregulated MS4A1 is correlated with poor clinical prognosis in LUAD

Correlation analyses between MS4A1 expression and LUAD grades in TCGA-LUAD cohort suggested that higher tumor grade was associated with lower MS4A1 expression (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the differences in G1 to G4 patient prognosis validated the correlation between MS4A1 expression and LUAD prognosis (Fig. 2B). And the correlation of MS4A1 expression with TNM stage and survival status was shown in Fig. 2C, demonstrating the I stage and alive status were highly related with the high MS4A1 expression. The mRNA and protein expression levels of MS4A1 were assessed in HBE cells and lung cancer cell lines. The results of RT-qPCR demonstrated lower MS4A1 mRNA expression in lung cancer cell lines than in HBE cells (Fig. 2D). WB analysis suggested identical patterns of protein expression (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, we included Raji B cells as a positive control and performed comprehensive RT-qPCR and IF analyses. The results revealed that MS4A1 maintains characteristic membrane localization in both Raji cells and normal HBE epithelial cells while H1650 cells demonstrate a distinct biological behavior with not only significantly lower expression but also a remarkable shift toward cytoplasmic distribution (Fig. 2D and G).

Subsequently, we analyzed MS4A1 mRNA and protein expression in eight paired fresh LUAD samples. MS4A1 mRNA and protein expressions were lower in LUAD tissues than in adjacent noncancerous tissues (Fig. 2F and I). IHC results demonstrated abnormally downregulated MS4A1 expression in LUAD tissues and upregulated expression in adjacent noncancerous tissues in 108 LUAD patients (Fig. 2H). To further explore the MS4A1 expression in LUAD tissues, we have performed mIHC co-staining for MS4A1, panCK (epithelial/tumor cells), CD19 (B cells), and Ki67 (proliferation marker). MS4A1 expression originating from tumor cells (MS4A1 + panCK +) has been distinguished from that of infiltrating B cells (MS4A1 + CD19 +). Importantly, the density of MS4A1-positive tumor cells showed a clear inverse correlation with Ki67 expression, indicating suppressed proliferative activity (Fig. 2J). The KM curve indicated that the progression free survival (PFS) and OS of patients with high MS4A1 expression were significantly longer than those of patients with low MS4A1 expression (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2K).

MS4A1 inhibits cell proliferation, migration and invasion in LUAD

MS4A1 is significantly downregulated in LUAD tissues, compared with para-cancerous tissues; therefore, we conducted functional experiments to explore its role in LUAD. MS4A1 expression was silenced in H1650 as well as A549 cells using two independent short hairpin RNAs, namely 1650 sh1, 1650 sh2 and A549 sh1, A549 sh2. MS4A1 expression was overexpressed in H3255 cells using 3255OE, an overexpression plasmid. RT-qPCR and WB results confirmed stable MS4A1 knockdown or overexpression in H1650, A549 and H3255 cells (Fig. 3A–B). To investigate cell proliferation, we conducted colony and CCK-8 formation assays. Colony formation analysis suggested that sh1 and sh2 of H1650 and A549 cells formed more colonies than H1650 and A549 cells as well as H3255 cells formed more colonies than 3255 OE (Fig. 3C). The CCK-8 analysis confirmed these findings, indicating the higher proliferation potential of sh1 and sh2 of H1650 and A549 cells and H3255 cells (Fig. 3D). Next, we investigated that MS4A1 inhibited LUAD tumorigenesis in vivo. A xenograft tumor mouse model was constructed by subcutaneously injecting 2.5 × 10^7/150 μl H3255-Vector and H3255-MS4A1 cells as well as H1650 shNC and shMS4A1 cells into the left and right armpits of 4-week-old BALB/c nude mice. MS4A1 inhibits LUAD progression by regulating macrophage polarization and migration

MS4A1 was identified as an M1-associated MRG through bioinformatics analysis. We investigated its potential role in M1 macrophage-mediated tumor progression. PMA induced the differentiation of THP-1 monocytes into macrophages. RT-qPCR and FC confirmed the successful differentiation of THP-1 cells by evaluating CD68 expression after 48 h of treatment with 200 nM PMA (Fig. 5A–B). A co-culture system was utilized to facilitate interactions between LUAD cells and macrophages (Fig. 5C). RT-qPCR analysis suggested decreased transcription levels of M1 markers, such as CXCL10, CXCL9, TNF-α, and IFN-γ, compared with macrophages co-cultured with MS4A1high LUAD cells (H1650, A549 and H3255OE cells) (Fig. 5D–E). THP-1–differentiated macrophages co-cultured with MS4A1low LUAD cells (H1650/A549 sh1, sh2, and H3255 cells) exhibited a CD80low phenotype, as demonstrated through FC analysis (Fig. 5F–H). Additionally, IF confirmed that MS4A1 promoted CD80 expression on macrophages, indicating that MS4A1 induced M0 to M1 polarization (Figure S6A-D). Furthermore, chemotaxis assays suggested that the supernatants from co-cultures with MS4A1high LUAD cells promoted M1 macrophage migration (Figure S6E-H). FC analysis of CD80 + macrophage infiltration in subcutaneous tumor tissues from 795 shNC and shMS4A1 groups demonstrated a significant reduction in the shMS4A1 group (Fig. 5I-J).

We investigated the effect of MS4A1-mediated macrophage modulation on the clinical outcomes in LUAD. IHC and mIHC indicated a positive correlation between MS4A1 and CD80 expression in LUAD tissues (Fig. 6A–B, Table S6). Survival analysis indicated that patients with MS4A1high/CD80high expression experienced significantly better OS and PFS than patients with MS4A1low/CD80low expression (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the expression of MS4A1/CD80 expression as well as clinicopathologic characteristics were enrolled for univariate and multivariate COX regression analysis and the results showed that MS4A1high/CD80high expression was the independent prognostic factor for LUAD patients (Fig. 6D, Table S7). Therefore, the modified tumor infiltration and function of macrophages caused by MS4A1 deficiency negatively affect LUAD prognosis.

MS4A1 inhibits angiogenesis to regulate macrophage M1 infiltration

In LUAD, MS4A1 plays a tumor-suppressive role and modulates macrophage polarization; thus, we explored the underlying molecular mechanisms. As showed in Fig. 7A, the scatter and volcano plot depicted the upregulated and downregulated genes in H1650 shNC and shMS4A1 group based on transcriptome sequencing. Results from transcriptome sequencing and GO functional enrichment analysis confirmed that the differential expression of MS4A1 significantly enriched in angiogenesis pathways (Fig. 7B). WB experiments assessed the impact of MS4A1 depletion on this process. MS4A1 depletion increased VEGFA protein levels in H1650 cells, whereas its overexpression decreased VEGFA protein levels in H3255 cells (Fig. 7C). Additionally, HUVECs were cultured in a conditioned medium derived from LUAD cells. The CCK-8 and transwell experiments have demonstrated that MS4A1 inhibited the proliferation and migrated capacity of HUVECs, respectively (Fig. 7D–E). The tube formation assay showed that MS4A1 depletion enhanced angiogenesis, whereas its overexpression impaired angiogenesis (Fig. 7F and H). To confirm that MS4A1 might induce M1 polarization via inhibiting angiogenesis, we performed comprehensive IHC analyses on human LUAD specimens using CD31 (vascular endothelium), CD68 (pan-macrophage), CD80 (M1-like macrophage), and MS4A1. The results revealed a clear correlative pattern that MS4A1 expression levels showed a negative association with CD31 but a positive correlation with CD80 (Fig. 7G).

Antagonists against angiogenesis and HIPPO pathway enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in MS4A1 low tumor-bearing mouse

GO functional enrichment analysis based on transcriptome sequencing showed that the differential expression of MS4A1 significantly enriched in the HIPPO signaling pathways (Fig. 8A) Furthermore, GSEA analysis also demonstrated that the HIPPO pathway was significantly upregulated in MS4A1-high samples (NES = −1.33, P < 0.05, Fig. 8B). The HIPPO pathway regulates cell proliferation and differentiation via YAP/Transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ). During HIPPO pathway inactivation, YAP/TAZ resides in the nucleus and combines with the transcriptional enhanced associate domain (TEAD), activating the genes that promote cell proliferation and survival [24, 25]. Conversely, HIPPO pathway activation leads to YAP/TAZ phosphorylation by large tumor suppressor 1/2 kinases, binding it to 14–3-3 proteins and causing cytoplasmic retention. Subsequently, transcriptional activity, cell proliferation, and survival are inhibited. We investigated the mechanism by which MS4A1 enhanced HIPPO pathway activity in LUAD cells. Co-IP assays confirmed that MS4A1 regulates the HIPPO pathway through interactions with YAP in H1650 and H3255 cells (Fig. 8C). WB in Fig. 8D also confirmed the role of MS4A1 in activating the HIPPO pathway; it indicated higher phosphorylation of HIPPO-related molecules, such as YAP and mammalian STE20-like kinase (MST), and lower TEAD expression in MS4A1-high samples.

To explore the role of immune regulation, angiogenesis and HIPPO signaling pathway in tumor growth effects of MS4A1, we constructed the in-situ tumor transplantation C57BL/6 mouse model with 795 shMS4A1 which received the treatment of tislelizumab, ivonescimab, ivonescimab + DMSO and ivonescimab + verteporfin, respectively. Tislelizumab as a PD-1 inhibitor class tumor immunotherapy drug activates the human immune system by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway, helping to recognize and kill tumor cells [26]. Ivonescimab is a bispecific antibody targeting PD-1 and VEGFA, which enhances anti-tumor activity by concurrently inhibiting the binding of PD-1 to PD-L1/L2 and the interaction of VEGFA with its receptor via a dual mechanism of ‘‘tumor immunity + anti-angiogenesis’’ [27]. In addition, verteporfin is a YAP inhibitor that inhibits the interaction between YAP and TEAD [28]. Figure S7A-C have shown that compared with the group receiving tislelizumab, tumor luciference signal and number of nodules in the group receiving ivonescimab significantly decreased, emphasizing the effects of angiogenesis in the tumor promotion. Furthermore, IHC analyses were demonstrated that the xenograft tumors in the group receiving ivonescimab presented the lower level of VEGFA while the higher level of CD80 compared with the group receiving tislelizumab (Figure S7D), implying that MS4A1 might induce the M1 polarization via inhibiting angiogenesis. In Fig. 8E–G, the tumor luciference signal and number of nodules in the group receiving ivonescimab + verteporfin were significantly less than those in the group receiving ivonescimab + DMSO, highlighting the significance of the interaction between YAP and TEAD in the tumor growth injected with shMS4A1 cells.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a core set of 13 prognostic M1-associated MRGs by intersecting the M1 macrophage-associated co-expression network (ME-black module) with prognosis-related DEGs in LUAD. To further refine this gene set, we performed LASSO regression analysis. Among the candidates, MS4A1 was selected for subsequent functional investigation due to its largest coefficient in the risk model, indicating its strong contribution to the relevance of M1 phenotype and prognostic prediction. Based on converging evidence from KM prognostic analyses, WB, and IHC across multiple cancer types, MS4A1 demonstrates a tightly restricted pattern of differential expression and clinical significance specific to LUAD, highlighting its unique role within this particular malignancy. Furthermore, MS4A1 demonstrated substantial roles in LUAD proliferation, migration, invasion in vivo and vitro. MS4A1 differently expressed tumor cells also regulated the M1 polarization and recruitment of macrophages through the HIPPO pathway, thereby inhibiting LUAD progression, suggesting that MS4A1 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for LUAD via macrophage regulation.

Interestingly, while MS4A1 is a canonical B-cell marker, our study reveals its unique expression and function within LUAD tumor cells. mIHC definitively localized MS4A1 to panCK + tumor cells, distinguishing it from infiltrating B cells. This tumor cell-intrinsic expression correlates with both reduced proliferation and enhanced B-cell recruitment, suggesting MS4A1 plays a non-canonical, tumor cell-specific role in shaping the LUAD immune microenvironment. This context-dependent role is further highlighted by our comparative analysis in BRCA, where despite prognostic significance of MS4A1 mRNA in TCGA data, we found no differential protein expression between carcinoma and normal breast epithelium. This discrepancy suggests that in BRCA, MS4A1's prognostic value primarily reflects B-cell infiltration rather than tumor cell-intrinsic expression, thereby reinforcing the distinctive nature of its functional expression in LUAD. Previous bioinformatics research has correlated MS4A1 with immune cell infiltration [15, 16]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to validate its role in macrophage polarization and recruitment at cellular and histological levels. Specifically, MS4A1 expression on lung cancer cells promotes M1 polarization and recruitment, characterized by CD80 expression, as explained by RT-qPCR, FC, and transwell analysis in co-culture systems, along with M1 infiltration in LUAD tissues via FC and mIHC.

The HIPPO signaling pathway is a highly conserved cascade that regulates cell proliferation, survival, differentiation, cell fate determination, organ size maintenance, and tissue homeostasis [29]. Disrupted HIPPO signaling pathway is central to the progression of cancer and other diseases [30]. The HIPPO pathway supports cancer stem cell-like properties and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in patients with LUAD [31]. Aligning with this finding, GSEA analysis of transcriptome sequencing indicated that the HIPPO signaling pathway was significantly enriched in MS4A1-high samples. Li et al. reported the regulatory role of the HIPPO pathway on MS4A1 expression in lymphoma [32]. TAZ accumulation in the nucleus suppresses MS4A1 transcription by disrupting the interaction of TEAD2 with the MS4A1 promoter region. The CO-IP analysis identified a novel MS4A1 interaction with YAP, indicating HIPPO pathway activation in solid tumors. HIPPO pathway dysregulation affects tumor onset and progression by modulating the M1/M2 macrophage polarization, thus necessitating further studies to explore its impact [33–35]. Zhang et al. demonstrated that Spondin2 (SPON2), a secretory protein, was positively associated with M1 TAM recruitment in HCC [36]. Recombinant SPON2 markedly enhanced YAP nuclear translocation in THP-1 cells. According to Chen et al., LUAD-derived exosomes that deliver miR-19b-3p to TAMs through the HIPPO pathway suppressed signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) dephosphorylation, activating STAT3 and promoting M2 polarization [37]. Huang et al. demonstrated that YAP1, a key HIPPO pathway protein, serves a dual role; YAP1 inhibition hinders tumorigenesis and M2 TAM polarization [38] by reducing interleukin (IL)-4 and IL-13 levels, which are essential for M2 polarization.

A heterogeneous population of macrophages exits in TME, including both bone marrow-derived and embryonically derived tissue-resident macrophages, which may respond differently to signals. Our in vitro experiments indeed utilized a model of monocyte-derived macrophages (differentiated THP-1 cells) primarily reflects the behavior of macrophages derived from circulating monocytes. Crucially, existing literature demonstrates that the HIPPO/YAP pathway is actively involved in the maintenance and polarization of various tissue-resident macrophage populations. One study by A Rum Kim etc. suggested that hepatic TAZ, the transcriptional coactivator in HIPPO pathway facilitated the infiltration and activation of Kupffer cells which are specialized macrophages in the liver through regulation of cytokine expression [39]. Another study also demonstrated that the YAP-TEAD transcriptional complex can recruit the tumor infiltrated type II (M2) macrophages by activating transcription of chemokine CCL2 and growth factor CSF1 [40]. In addition, activation of the HIPPO signaling pathway is the key driver behind miR-223-3p-induced autophagy and ferroptosis in alveolar macrophages (AMs), as reported by Ye et al. [41]. Therefore, although our in vitro data directly demonstrates the effect on monocyte-derived macrophages, it is highly plausible that the MS4A1-YAP signaling axis could similarly influence the polarization state of tissue-resident macrophages in the TME. Furthermore, our in vivo results like FC and IHC provided indirect evidence that MS4A1 may influence the overall M1 infiltration across the entire macrophage pool, which includes both monocyte-derived and tissue-resident populations.

Our study identifies MS4A1 (CD20) as a functional tumor cell-intrinsic molecule in LUAD, suggesting novel therapeutic opportunities. While CD20-targeting antibodies like Rituximab are well-established in B-cell lymphoma [42, 43], their application in solid tumors remains largely unexplored. We propose that LUAD patients with high tumor MS4A1 expression could benefit from anti-CD20 therapy through a dual mechanism including direct elimination of MS4A1-positive tumor cells via antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and/or complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC), and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment by depleting immunosuppressive B-cell subsets [44]. Furthermore, our findings that MS4A1-expressing LUAD cells promote M1 macrophage polarization and foster an anti-tumor immune microenvironment provide a compelling rationale for combining CD20-targeting antibodies with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. The MS4A1-driven ‘‘hot’’ tumor microenvironment, characterized by increased M1-like macrophages and enhanced B-cell recruitment, creates a favorable immunological context for anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy. Specifically, the M1-skewed milieu may secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines that promote antigen presentation and activate cytotoxic T-cells, while the recruited Bcells can contribute to antigen presentation and anti-tumor antibody production [45]. This multi-faceted immune activation would be strategically positioned to synergize with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, which function to remove the brakes on pre-existing but suppressed T-cell responses [46]. We hypothesize that MS4A1-high LUAD patients, identified by IHC or transcriptional profiling, would exhibit improved responses to anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy due to this pre-activated immune contexture. Thus, our study establishes MS4A1 as a key immune regulator in LUAD, proposing a novel combination immunotherapy for MS4A1-high patients by rendering their tumors anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapy-susceptible. Future work will therefore focus on validating this combo-immunotherapy approach in preclinical models and ultimately identifying biomarkers to select LUAD patients for tailored clinical trials.

While these findings establish a novel MS4A1/HIPPO/M1 macrophage signaling axis, the precise mechanism through which the HIPPO pathway and angiogenesis drive macrophage polarization requires further elucidation. We hypothesize that cytokine networks may serve as critical mediators in this process. In subsequent studies, we will employ comprehensive cytokine profiling and functional blockade with neutralizing antibodies to systematically identify and validate the specific factors through which the MS4A1-HIPPO/angiogenesis axis shapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that MS4A1 expression in LUAD cells activates the HIPPO pathway and regulates angiogenesis, consequently promoting M1 macrophage polarization and inhibiting tumor progression, indicating MS4A1 as a promising therapeutic target for advanced LUAD treatment.
