Prevalences and determinants of newborn feeding practices in the Netherlands: insights from the 2023 national survey
Authors: Ingrid I E Staal, Nicole Manshanden, Anton Schreuder, Marijn J Vermeulen, Chris H P van den Akker, Milena Buurman, Karen van Drongelen, Joost Velzel, Merian B R Bouwmeester-Landweer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreastfeeding is acknowledged as the optimal source of nutrition for newborns. Data from 2018 indicated a downwards trend in breastfeeding in the Netherlands. Our primary aim was to assess the current prevalence of breastfeeding, formula feeding, and combination feeding. Additionally, we explored factors influencing maternal feeding choices. A cross-sectional survey study was conducted from 2 November 2023 to 2 December 2023. Dutch parents of infants were recruited via Youth Health Care services and online channels, who completed a structured questionnaire on feeding practices, sociodemographic factors, and reasoning. A total of 4803 questionnaires were filled in, of which 215 (4.5%) were excluded because the child was older than 1.5 years or no feeding information was provided. Among mothers, 64% (2949/4588) initiated exclusive breastfeeding after birth, declining to 53% (2329/4385) at 1 month, 49% (1586/3260) at 3 months, and 33% (679/2083) at 6 months. The primary reason for initiating breastfeeding was the belief that breastfeeding is healthier for the baby (2053/3207, 64.0%). When breastfeeding was discontinued, the predominant reason was insufficient milk supply or fear thereof (352/1260, 27.9%). Of 756 mothers who stopped earlier than planned 73.1% (553/756) felt disappointed. High maternal education, home delivery, and first skin-to-skin contact duration of at least 1 hour were positively associated with exclusive breastfeeding. Targeted interventions and standardized data collection are needed to improve breastfeeding support and enable consistent monitoring of trends over time.

Introduction

Breastfeeding is widely recognized as the optimal source of nutrition for newborns, offering significant health benefits for both infants and mothers [1, 2]. Yet, global prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding up to 6 months postpartum remains substantially below target: only 44% of infants worldwide were exclusively breastfed from 2015 to 2020, against the World Health Assembly’s goal of 50% by 2025 [3]. High-income settings lag further behind: for example, just 24.9% of infants in the USA remain exclusively breastfed at 6 months of age compared to 58.5% in India [4–6]. The World Health Organization (WHO) and United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF) recommend early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of birth, exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life, and continued breastfeeding alongside complementary feeding until at least 2 years of age [7]. Both international and national guidelines advise introducing complementary foods between 4 and 6 months of age [8, 9]. The most recent data on breastfeeding in the Netherlands were from 2018 and indicated a clear declining trend. Between 2001 and 2015, breastfeeding initiation rates remained relatively stable at approximately 74%–81%; however, by 2018, the proportion of mothers initiating breastfeeding had declined to 69% [10].

Various barriers impede the continuity of breastfeeding. In the acute postpartum phase, factors such as maternal-infant separation, medical conditions in infant or mother, and insufficient in-hospital lactation support can compromise timely initiation. During the first weeks postpartum, mechanical challenges such as poor latching, nipple pain, and worries about milk supply often precipitate early cessation. Beyond 6 weeks, structural obstacles including inadequate paid parental leave, unsupportive workplace environments, and inconsistent lactation services increasingly undermine continued breastfeeding [6, 11, 12]. Additionally, cultural perceptions and societal norms play a significant role in breastfeeding initiation and duration [11, 13].

Accurate and current data on breastfeeding prevalence and influencing factors are essential for national and international public health policymaking. Identifying key determinants of feeding choices offers valuable insights for healthcare providers and policymakers in needed interventions, parental support initiatives, breastfeeding promotion, and maternal care.

The primary objective of this study is to assess the prevalence of breastfeeding, formula feeding, and mixed feeding from birth to 6 months postpartum. By conducting a national survey, we aim to provide an updated representation of feeding practices in the Netherlands. In addition, a secondary objective is to explore maternal- and context-related factors that may contribute to observed breastfeeding patterns. Given that breastfeeding prevalence is known to be influenced by individual motivations and structural circumstances [12, 14], we included additional survey items addressing maternal reasons for initiating or discontinuing breastfeeding as well as factors such as maternal educational level, employment status, and first skin-to-skin contact after birth. These variables were selected to contextualize breastfeeding prevalence in 2023 and to examine whether established factors continue to play a role in contemporary feeding practices.

Methods

Design and study population

We performed a cross-sectional survey study where data were collected via an online questionnaire from 2 November 2023 to 2 December 2023. The questionnaire was brought to the attention of parents of newborns up to 1 year old throughout the Netherlands when they visited the well-baby clinic and through social media partners (e.g. Groeigids, WIJ Media, and How About Mom). The parents had to be residents and sufficiently proficient in Dutch reading. Exclusion criteria were births which had occurred more than 1.5 years before the time the questionnaire was filled-in (if birth date was reported) and when no information was provided regarding feeding practices (Supplementary Fig. S1). Permission from the ethics committee was waived as observational studies do not fall under the Dutch Act on Medical Research Involving Human Subjects. However, informed consent from participants was obtained by presenting a consent statement in the online questionnaire. Participants provided their consent by selecting ‘I agree’.

Questionnaire

The used questionnaire was based on an existing survey which was last used in 2015 [15]. This questionnaire contained questions about milk feeding type (breastfeeding, formula feeding, or mixed feeding) initially after birth and at the time of filling-in, the age of the child when formula feeding was introduced, the age of the child at which breastfeeding was completely stopped, and primary and secondary reasons for starting and stopping breastfeeding and formula feeding (whenever applicable). In addition, questions were asked about the parent’s background, care received during pregnancy and delivery, and the reasoning and motivation behind each decision. Participants had to answer between 30 and 53 questions depending on their responses. The complete questionnaire, translated into English, is included as Supplementary File S1.

Study variables

The primary outcomes of our study were the prevalences of feeding practices of infants at weekly time points within the first month of age and monthly time points until, at maximum, 6 months. Specifically, mothers were asked whether the infants were given breastmilk, formula milk, or mixed feeding; the latter refers to a combination of breastmilk and formula milk. When the feeding practice initially after birth was not reported, it was assumed to be the same as the feeding practice in the first week. When feeding practices at any other time point was not reported, it was assumed to be the same as the feeding practice at the prior time point (as long as it did not contradict with future feeding practices).

Secondary outcome measures were the mothers’ reasons for their feeding choices and the associations between feeding practices and parental education, maternal work status, delivery location, delivery mode, skin-to-skin contact initiated within an hour of birth, and first skin-to-skin contact duration of at least an hour. Demographic characteristics which were collected were maternal age, maternal self-reported ethnicity, maternal body mass index (BMI), family situation, and each parent’s attained education and work status. Other collected pregnancy and perinatal characteristics were parity and gestational age.

Statistical analyses

Data preparation and statistical analyses were performed in statistical program R. Weights were assigned to each included questionnaire based on the prevalence of maternal attained education, parity, and age in the sample compared to that of the Dutch population in 2023 [16]. Weighted and unweighted prevalence rates of the known feeding practices were summarized at each time point. For the remaining analyses, missing data (excluding feeding practices and mothers’ reasons for their feeding choices) were handled using multiple imputations with 15 iterations from which ten imputed datasets were created [17].

Robust (modified) weighted Poisson regression was used to estimate risk ratios (RRs) between exclusive breastfeeding and formula, or mixed feeding for exposures to maternal and delivery characteristics [18, 19]. Results were reported at three selected time points: initially after birth, 3 months of age, and 6 months of age. Crude and adjusted analyses were performed with and without weighting, where the variables selected for the adjustment of each association were from a minimally sufficient adjustment set determined by a directed acyclic graph (DAG) [20, 21] (Supplementary Fig. S2). DAGs are a tool to distinguish between confounders and variables in the causal pathway to avoid overadjustment bias. Results from the regression analyses were pooled across the imputed datasets. Associations were reported to have been found when the weighted and adjusted RR 95% confidence interval does not include 1.

Results

Study population

A total of 4803 questionnaires were filled in of which 34 (0.7%) were excluded because the child was older than 1.5 years at the time of survey completion and 181 (3.8%) were subsequently excluded because no information was provided on feeding practices. The final dataset consisted of 4588 questionnaires, of which 747 (16.3%) were missing the child’s date of birth.

Ninety-six percent (4391/4588) of included mothers were of Dutch ethnicity, 68% (3133/4588) had attained a high educational qualification, and 64% (2940/4588) had one child (Table 1); these proportions were all higher than those in the general Dutch population. Feeding practices

Sixty-four percent (2949/4588) of mothers start with exclusive breastfeeding after birth, declining to 53% (2329/4385) after 1 month, 49 (1586/3260) after 3 months, and 33% (679/2083) after 6 months (Fig. 1). In contrast, the prevalence of mixed feeding was 19% (854/4588) after birth and 42% (869/2083) after 6 months. Fourteen percent (647/4588) performed exclusive formula feeding at birth, increasing to 26% (533/2083) at 6 months. The feeding practices of the remaining minorities were missing. Though applying weights and excluding the subgroup missing the child’s date of birth changed the proportions of feeding practices at some time points, it did not affect the overall trajectories (Supplementary Figs S3 and S4, respectively).

Feeding practices.

Reasons for feeding practices

Most mothers started with the feeding that they had planned to give: 75.8% (3020/3986) planned to start with exclusive breastfeeding, 14.8% (590/3986) with exclusive formula feeding, 7.0% (281/3986) with mixed feeding, and 2.4% (95/3986) had been undecided (Supplementary Table S1). The primary reason for mothers to start breastfeeding was the belief that breastfeeding is healthier for the baby (2053/3207, 64.0%). The next two most popular primary reasons were to have close contact between mother and child (612/3207, 19.1%) and because it was thought that breast milk is the most natural food (484/3207, 15.1%).

The primary reasons why breastfeeding was stopped at any point within the first 6 months was due to an insufficient milk supply (279/1260, 22.1%), or the fear thereof (73/1260, 5.8%) (Supplementary Table S1). Otherwise, the time since birth influenced the other reasons for discontinuation. Mothers who stopped breastfeeding within the first 2 months claimed the primary reasons to be pain (102/1260, 8.1%), medical reasons (59/1260, 4.7%), tiredness (77/1260, 6.1%), and the baby being unable to latch (142/1260, 11.3%). After 2 months, the primary reasons for discontinuation were the mother’s desire for more freedom (87/1260, 6.9%) and because it was difficult in conjunction with her job (79/1260, 6.3%). Among mothers who stopped breastfeeding earlier than planned, 73.1% (553/756) felt somewhat or very disappointed, though 16.8% (127/756) felt somewhat or very relieved.

For mothers who started with exclusive formula feeding after birth, the top five primary reasons given were: fits with the family norms (109/699, 15.6%), medical reasons (98/699, 14.0%), to allow more partner involvement with feeding (96/699, 13.7%), to know the exact intake (94/699, 13.4%), and more freedom for the mother (56/699, 8.0%) (Supplementary Table S1).

Associations with exclusive breastfeeding

Several maternal and delivery characteristics were found to be associated with exclusive breastfeeding after birth, 3 months, and 6 months (Tables 2 and 3; Supplementary Tables S3 and S4 also report weighted crude and unweighted adjusted RRs). Compared to the RRs calculated from the unweighted crude analyses, the adjusted analyses resulted in smaller magnitudes across all associations. Adding weights to the analyses had mixed effects on the magnitudes, depending on the association of interest. For the weighted and adjusted analyses, high maternal education, delivery at home, and first skin-to-skin contact duration of at least 1 hour were found to be positively associated with exclusive breastfeeding at all time points. Vaginal delivery without an epidural was also found to have a positive association with exclusive breastfeeding when compared to vaginal delivery with epidural (after birth and 3 months) and elective C-section (3 months). Negative associations were also found between induced labour and exclusive breastfeeding after birth, and 6 months. No associations were found for skin-to-skin contact initiated within an hour of delivery. There was also no association found for mother’s working hours, though it was notable that the direction of association changed from after birth to 6 months. Specifically, mothers who worked less than 24 h per week were less likely to breastfeed after birth compared to mothers who worked at least 24 h per week, though more likely to do so at 6 months.

Discussion

This study provides contemporary insights into breastfeeding practices in the Netherlands, revealing critical public health concerns. We found that while 64% of mothers initiated exclusive breastfeeding after birth, this declined to 33% at 6 months postpartum. Most mothers who stopped with exclusive breastfeeding within the first month switched over to exclusive formula feeding. From 2 months postpartum onwards, a gradually increasing proportion of breastfeeding mothers began mixing formula feeding into their feeding practices. These findings underscore a significant public health challenge and highlight the pressing need for targeted, evidence-based interventions to support breastfeeding continuation.

Our data indicate a persistent downward trend in exclusive breastfeeding rates, now falling below the Dutch national prevalence rates from 2018 (64% in 2023 vs 69% in 2018) [10]. However, this decline in exclusivity does not necessarily reflect an overall decrease in breast milk feeding. The percentage of children who received mixed feeding from birth increased to 19% compared to 7% in 2018, meaning that a larger proportion of infants are receiving at least some breast milk. Furthermore, the 2023 data show a less steep decline in breastfeeding rates by infant age compared to previous surveys. For example, 33% of infants were still exclusively breastfed at 6 months of age in 2023. Internationally, the global prevalence of exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months stands at approximately 44% [3]. The Netherlands hereby aligns broadly with other high-income Western nations but remains substantially below the global target set by the WHO and UNICEF [22].

Uniquely, this study combines nationally representative data on infant feeding practices with analysis of maternal and perinatal determinants of exclusive breastfeeding in the Netherlands. Our findings reveal several maternal and perinatal characteristics that are positively associated with exclusive breastfeeding after birth, 3 months, and 6 months postpartum, including home births and vaginal deliveries without epidural. The most striking association was found with first skin-to-skin contact duration of at least 1 hour, which was linked to higher exclusive breastfeeding rates across all time points. This observation supports a growing body of evidence suggesting that early and sustained skin-to-skin contact promotes breastfeeding initiation, stabilizes infant physiology, enhances maternal–infant bonding, and boosts maternal self-efficacy in feeding [23]. The mechanism behind this association likely involves both hormonal and behavioural pathways, including increased oxytocin release, earlier recognition of feeding cues, and more effective latch and milk transfer [24]. Given these benefits, skin-to-skin contact should be actively promoted and facilitated in maternity care settings, especially in the first hours after birth.

In line with prior research, maternal educational level also emerged as a significant determinant of breastfeeding duration [12, 14, 25]. The strong association between higher education and prolonged breastfeeding highlights systemic inequalities in breastfeeding outcomes. Educational disparities likely reflect underlying variations in health literacy, access to lactation support services, and cultural norms regarding breastfeeding [14, 26]. It is therefore crucial that future interventions also systematically address social determinants of health, ensuring equitable breastfeeding support across diverse socioeconomic strata.

Strongly associated with educational level are job type and working hours, where international evidence indicates that workplace policies influence breastfeeding duration [25–27]. Specifically, extended maternity leave, workplace flexibility, and availability of lactation accommodations correlate consistently with higher breastfeeding rates [26–29]. In our analysis, the association between maternal working hours and exclusive breastfeeding did not achieve statistical significance. However, the direction of association was reversed by 6 months postpartum, where mothers with more working hours were less likely to have persisted with exclusive breastfeeding. This demonstrates a change in behaviour when mothers return to their regular jobs. Given the Netherlands’ comparatively short maternity leave of 10 weeks postpartum, enhancing parental leave and improving workplace breastfeeding support policies may provide substantial public health benefits by facilitating sustained exclusive breastfeeding practices among working mothers.

Complementing these structural and clinical insights, our study also sheds light on mothers’ personal motivations and lived experiences that drive infant feeding decisions. Most mothers followed through on their prenatal feeding intentions. However, many mothers discontinued earlier than planned. When this occurred within the first couple of months postpartum, it was mostly due to challenges related to perceived insufficient milk supply, pain, or fatigue. Importantly, perceived insufficient milk supply is frequently linked to improper latching technique, which is often the result of inadequate early support and education rather than true physiological insufficiency [6]. Thereafter, practical and psychosocial factors gained prominence in early discontinuation, including work-related difficulties and a desire for greater personal autonomy. Notably, 73% of mothers who stopped with breastfeeding earlier than intended reported feelings of disappointment, underscoring the emotional toll of unmet feeding goals. This high level of disappointment may also have lasting effects, potentially reducing motivation or intentions to breastfeed future children, which makes it a relevant concern not only for individual maternal well-being but also from a broader public health perspective.

A notable strength of our study lies in its population-based national approach, in which all Dutch-speaking parents of an infant below 1 year of age were eligible to participate. While our sample compromised 3% of the population [16] (4803 infants were included out of 164 487 Dutch live births in 2023), we applied post-stratification weighting to correct for differences between our sample and the national birth population on key demographic characteristics. This weighting procedure substantially enhances the generalizability of our findings to the broader Dutch population. Nevertheless, considerable methodological variations exist between international breastfeeding surveys, complicating cross-country comparisons. This limitation highlights a crucial gap in breastfeeding research: the lack of a standardized methodology for data collection, reporting, and interpretation.

To accurately monitor global breastfeeding trends and evaluate the effectiveness of interventions, international breastfeeding research requires methodological harmonization. We strongly advocate for the development and widespread adoption of a core outcome set and standardized survey instrument—a ‘core-outcome breastfeeding survey’—aligned with WHO and UNICEF guidelines. Such an internationally agreed-upon framework would encompass unified definitions, standardized outcome measures, validated instruments, and consistent data collection time points. Methodological harmonization would not only enable robust international comparisons but also strengthen global advocacy efforts by providing high-quality, comparable data for policymaking. In addition to methodological alignment, sustained funding mechanisms are essential to ensure the periodic repetition of such surveys.

Limitations of this study include potential recall bias, as data were collected retrospectively. Beyond this design-related limitation, the scope of available variables also constrained the extent to which confounding could be fully addressed. Although we adjusted our analyses for several maternal and perinatal factors known to influence breastfeeding outcomes, not all potentially relevant confounders could be included. In particular, information on infant health at birth and during the first year of life was not available in the national survey. Additionally, other factors associated with breastfeeding success or early discontinuation, such as neonatal morbidity, mother–infant separation and maternal mental health problems, were not systematically captured. Consequently, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the observed associations should be interpreted with appropriate caution. This limitation underscores the need of standardized data collection frameworks, such as a core outcome set that includes key maternal, perinatal, and infant health indicators, to enable more comprehensive adjustment for confounding in future breastfeeding research. Another important limitation concerns the limited representativeness of specific groups, including mothers with lower educational attainment, non-Dutch ethnic backgrounds, and those with multiple children. This underrepresentation may have affected our findings, particularly given that breastfeeding rates tend to be lower in these groups. As such, our reported prevalence figures may be somewhat overestimated, and should be interpreted with this potential upward bias in mind. Furthermore, our reliance on cross-sectional data precludes causal inference. While our use of statistical weighting helps mitigate selection bias and improve representativeness, the question remains as to who chooses to participate in such surveys and who does not, an issue that may influence the generalizability of the findings. Future studies would benefit from prospective longitudinal designs and more targeted recruitment strategies to ensure inclusion of underrepresented populations. We advocate for an internationally harmonized standard for breastfeeding surveillance that enables valid benchmarking between countries. This standard should include a clearly defined baseline dataset capturing social background characteristics, with a minimum of education, migration context, and language to support interpretable equity analyses. We further recommend developing and implementing a Core Outcome Set for breastfeeding outcomes, using a multi-stakeholder Delphi process, to standardize definitions of exclusive breastfeeding and any breast milk feeding, as well as minimum reporting time points across countries.

In conclusion, this study offers a comprehensive overview of current breastfeeding, formula feeding, and mixed feeding practices in the Netherlands, highlighting critical sociodemographic determinants that inform targeted public health strategies. To enable global progress towards improving breastfeeding practices, we emphasize the urgent necessity for international consensus on a standardized methodological framework. Implementing a core-outcome breastfeeding survey will significantly enhance our collective ability to assess, compare, and improve breastfeeding practices worldwide, ensuring evidence-based and equitable support for mothers and infants across all contexts.
