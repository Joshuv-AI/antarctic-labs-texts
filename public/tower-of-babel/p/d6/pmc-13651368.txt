Establishment of a blocking ELISA using a newly generated monoclonal antibody against Salmonella flagellin for detecting antibodies in infected chickens
Authors: Zimin Huang, LiYan Cao, Jun Liu, Zhenjinjiang Ma, Xilong Kang, Xinan Jiao, Chuang Meng, Zhiming Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalmonella is an important zoonotic pathogen that is widely distributed in every link between livestock and poultry production. Salmonella contamination not only affects the healthy development of the livestock industry but also poses a serious threat to public health, as it can infect humans through farming, processing, and consumption of meat and egg products. Therefore, establishing detection methods capable of simultaneously identifying different Salmonella serotypes is crucial.ResultsThis study focused on using Salmonella flagellin as an immunogen to screen and prepare monoclonal antibodies, ultimately establishing a blocking ELISA (bELISA). In this study, flagellin from S. Enteritidis was extracted by acid lysis and used to immunize BALB/c mice. Cell fusion was performed following three immunizations, resulting in the generation of six hybridoma cell lines that stably secreted monoclonal antibodies. Monoclonal antibody (MAb) of 1F6 exhibited excellent reactivity with Salmonella flagellin, demonstrating an affinity of 1.11 × 1010 M−1. Specificity analysis indicated that MAb 1F6 reacted with all flagellated Salmonella species, but not with non-flagellated S. Pullorum or other bacterial species, confirming its strong specificity and suitability for this method. Subsequently, an array titration method was employed to determine the optimal coating concentration of the antigen for the bELISA, which was found to be 1 μg/ml, while the optimal concentration for the enzyme-labeled antibody was 534 ng/ml. Various experimental optimizations were conducted to identify the optimal detection conditions. The results demonstrated specific recognition of Salmonella and no cross-reactivity with other sera, when testing chicken sera infected with eleven different pathogenic microorganisms. Furthermore, this method was applied to 227 chicken serum samples, achieving a conformity rate of 97.35%. In addition, 205 serum samples from layer chickens at a certain farm were analyzed, and Salmonella was detected on day 90, with an overall positive rate of 4%, consistent with previous studies, indicating that our method can be used for Salmonella monitoring in production enterprises.ConclusionsThe bELISA method was developed by utilizing Salmonella flagellin as an antigen and exhibited excellent stability, strong specificity, and sensitivity, making it suitable for broad-spectrum screening of Salmonella in infected chicken.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05387-x.

Background

Salmonella is a significant zoonotic pathogen that is widely distributed worldwide, with over 2,600 identified serotypes [1, 2]. In recent decades, foodborne disease outbreaks caused by Salmonella have occurred frequently, leading to serious public health issues [3–5]. From 1951 to 2019, approximately 325 serotypes of Salmonella were detected in China [6]. Gradually, the detection rate of Salmonella serotypes has increased. The most common Salmonella species that cause cross-infection between animals and humans are S. Enteritidis, S. Typhimurium, and S. Derby [7]. While most serotypes are common, some less common ones, such as S. Choleraesuis and S. Dublin, exhibit high pathogenicity and can cause severe disease [8, 9]. Salmonella infects a wide range of hosts. In young animals, it often leads to septicemia, while in adults, it may cause localized inflammation, abortion in breeding females, and other health issues that affect poultry development [10]. More importantly, Salmonella can infect humans through contamination of poultry meat, eggs, and other products, causing diseases such as typhoid, paratyphoid, gastroenteritis, and food poisoning. This poses a serious threat to global public health and results in significant economic losses [11–13]. Therefore, implementing reliable and efficient detection methods for Salmonella in farms and throughout the food production chain is essential.

Although detection methods relying on traditional bacterial culturing have been used as the gold standard for the detection of Salmonella, it is a time-consuming and laborious process, and cannot meet the urgent need of real-time detection [14]. Additionally, the intermittent excretion of Salmonella may lead to inaccurate detection results [15]. In contrast, serological testing methods is that they enable high-throughput testing at relatively low cost [16]. Among these, the bELISA method based on monoclonal antibodies offers significant efficiency advantages, making it particularly suitable for rapid Salmonella screening [17]. The bELISA method uses serum samples, requiring only centrifugation of the collected blood to obtain serum, without any pre-treatment of the samples [18]. It is cost-effective, simple to perform, and allows for the quantitative analysis of antibodies in serum based on the detection results. In the blocking ELISA method, the serum sample is first added and allowed to react for a period of time. Then the monoclonal antibody is added for blocking. If the serum does not contain antibodies that can react with the coated protein, the monoclonal antibody can bind to it. Ultimately, the OD₄₅₀ value of the test result is inversely proportional to the antibody content in the serum. Additionally, serological testing reduces the risk of missed detections, resulting in more reliable outcomes. However, current serological assays are predominantly limited to detection of a few serotypes of Salmonella, restricting their utility for broad-spectrum detection [19–21]. Identifying conserved antigenic epitopes of Salmonella flagellin across serotypes could address this limitation [22].

The flagellin of Salmonella is a long fibrous structure distributed around the bacteria cell surface. All Salmonella species are flagellate with only two exceptions, S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum [23]. In addition, the flagellin was considered as a potential target candidate for Salmonella detection [22], since the protein is abundantly expressed, located on the surface of bacteria, and has good antigenicity [24]. Utilizing conserved antigenic epitopes of Salmonella flagellin to establish a bELISA method provides advantages for broad-spectrum detection of Salmonella.

In this study, naturally extracted flagellin from S. Enteritidis were used to immunize mice. A simultaneous screening strategy for the flagellin in both S. Enteritidis and S. Rissen was employed to select and prepare MAbs against Salmonella flagellin. Then a bELISA method was established for detecting Salmonella antibodies in the serum, based on the identified MAbs that recognize common antigenic epitopes of different serotypes of Salmonella flagellin. This method can detect specific antibodies against Salmonella in serum samples, providing a convenient method for diagnosing Salmonella infection in poultry.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, serum samples, and animals

All strains used in this study were cultured in LB medium, mixed with 50% glycerol, and stored at −80 ℃ in Jiangsu Key Laboratory of Zoonosis (Yangzhou, China). Prior to use, the strains were thawed and re-cultured in LB medium. Positive chicken serum for Salmonella enterica serotypes, including S. Enteritidis, S. Typhimurium, S. Infantis, S. Pullorum, S. Derby, S. Kentucky and S. Gallinarum, were obtained from specific pathogen free (SPF) chickens experimentally exposed to the corresponding serotypes. Negative chicken serum obtained directedly from the wing veins of SPF chickens that received no experimental exposure. All serum were prepared and stored at the Jiangsu Key Laboratory of Zoonosis. Positive sera for common avian pathogens such as Infectious Bursal Disease Virus, Newcastle Disease Virus, Avian Influenza Virus, and Proteus mirabilis were kindly provided by Prof. Yabin Dai of the Institute of Poultry Science of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. BALB/c mice for studies were acquired from the Comparative Medical Center of Yangzhou University. SPF chicks (3 to 7 days old) were procured from Boehringer Ingelheim (Jiangsu, China). All the mice and chickens were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions.

Extraction of flagellin from Salmonella

Flagellin from S. Enteritidis and S. Rissen were extracted by acid treatment as described previously with modifications [25]. Briefly, 5 mL of modified M-broth medium supplemented with 1 mM IPTG was inoculated with bacteria and incubated statically at 37 °C for 16 to 18 h. The resulting culture was then used to inoculate 500 mL of fresh M-broth medium and incubated under the same conditions. The bacterial pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of sterile PBS, the pH was adjusted to 2.0–3.0, and the suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min on a shaker at 150 rpm. Subsequently, the solution was centrifuged at 4 °C with 1,503, 6,010, 9,391 and 13,523 × g RCF for 5 min sequentially. After each centrifugation step, the supernatant was transferred to a new centrifuge tube and the next centrifugation step was performed. Stepwise centrifugation is performed to better remove debris and more gently enrich flagellin. Then, the final supernatant was collected and the pH value was adjusted to 7.2 using 1 N NaOH before dialyzing against sterile PBS buffer at 4 °C for 6 h. Further details on the preparation of the M-broth medium are provided in the supplementary material (Table S1). The purified flagellin was subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and the immunoreactivity of the purified flagellin was verified by Western blotting using 1:5,000 diluted mouse-derived polyclonal antiserum against Salmonella flagellin. The membrane was then incubated with an ECL chromogenic kit (SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate, Thermo Scientific) and scanned using the Amersham Imager 600 imagers (GE Healthcare, IL, USA) for images.

Immunization of Mice with flagellin of S. enteritidis

Six- to eight-week-old female BALB/c mice (n = 4) were immunized twice with S. Enteritidis flagellin (80 μg per mouse), protein concentration measured by BCA assay (Vazyme, E112), via subcutaneous (s.c.) injection in the abdomen, with a two-week interval between immunizations [26]. Freund's complete adjuvant (Sigma Aldrich, F5881) was used for the first immunization, followed by Freund's incomplete adjuvant (Sigma Aldrich, F5506) for the booster dose. Three days before the fusion, antibody titers in the serum of immunized mice were measured; the mouse with the strongest immunoreactivity against S. Enteritidis flagellin received an additional boost via intraperitoneal injection. The spleen cells obtained from the immunized mice were then fused with SP2/0 cells by using the lymphocyte hybridoma technique, as described previously[27], to prepare the B cells producing antibodies against Salmonella flagellin.

Screening of MAbs against Salmonella flagellin

An indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (iELISA) was used to screen hybridoma cells. Two types of flagellin from S. Enteritidis and S. Rissen were used to coat the plates at concentration of 0.5 μg/mL. The plates were blocked at 4 ℃ for 12 h, washed three times with PBST (0.05% Tween in PBS), and then blocked with 1% BSA in PBS at 37 ℃ for 2 h. After three additional washes with PBST, 100 μL hybridoma cell supernatants were added to wells and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h (SP2/0 cell supernatant and immunized mouse serum were used as negative and positive controls, respectively). Subsequent five times washes were followed by the addition of 100 μL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)- goat anti-mouse IgG (ABClone, AS003) diluted 1: 5000 in PBS into the plates and then incubated for 1 h at 37 ℃. Following seven times washing, 100 μL of tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (Solarbio, PR1200), was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 ℃ for 3 min, followed by the addition of 50 μL of 2 M H2SO4 per well. The results were measured using a spectrophotometer (Synergy2, Biotek, USA) at an optical density of 450 nm (OD450). The positive results were determined according to the following formula: OD450 of sample/OD450 of positive control ≥ 2.1. If the detection results for both flagellin were positive, it is preliminarily determined that the hybridoma cells target the common antigenic epitope of Salmonella flagellin or the Salmonella H:g antigenic epitope. The hybridoma cell supernatant is tested twice, if both results are positive, it is deemed a positive cell line, and subcloning was performed according to the previously described [28]. After three subcloning steps, ascites was prepared using the amplified hybridomas 1F6 and 7A10 MAbs, separately [29]. The collected ascites were purified by affinity chromatography and subsequently conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) label by Periodate Oxidation Method, both of which were performed by Nanjing Kingsley Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The MAbs were identified by SDS-PAGE as previously described, and titer (coated antigen was S. Rissen flagellin) were determined by iELISA. We used an Subclass ELISA kit (Biodragon, BF06002) to determine the subclasses of the Mabs, following the manufacturer’s protocol strictly.

Whole-bacterial ELISA for the Preliminary Specificity of the MAbs

Whole-bacterial ELISA steps have been described previously [30]. First, ELISA plates were treated with 5% glutaraldehyde (100 μL/well) and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. After incubation, the plates were washed thrice with distilled water and then coated with 1 × 108 CFU of bacterial strains (100 μL/well) overnight at 56 ℃. The bacterial coatings were fixed with absolute methanol for 30 min. Following three additional washes with distilled water, the plates were blocked with PBS containing 1% BSA and incubated at 37 ℃ for 2 h. The plates were then washed three more times with PBST before adding MAbs at two-fold serial dilutions, starting at 1:100, and incubating at 37 ℃ for 2 h. HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (diluted 1:5000) was added to each well (100 μL/well) and incubated at 37 ℃ for 1 h. Finally, 3,3’, 5,5’-TMB substrate was added and incubated for 3 min, the reaction was stopped with 2 M H2SO4, and the optical density at 450 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer.

Assessment of the immunoreactivity, affinity and specificity of MAb 1F6

The immunoreactivity of MAb 1F6 to purify flagellin from S. Enteritidis and S. Rissen was analyzed by Western blotting. Flagellin was loaded for SDS-PAGE, and then transferred to a nitrocellulose (NC) membrane. The NC membrane was blocked with 5% nonfat milk and incubated with 1:5,000 diluted MAb 1F6 as the primary antibody and HRP- goat anti-mouse IgG as the primary and secondary antibody, respectively. The membrane was colored and scanned as previously described.

The affinity of MAb 1F6 was measured by iELISA, as described previously with slight modifications [31]. In brief, the plate was coated with flagellin of S. Enteritidis diluted to concentrations of 1, 0.5 and 0.25 μg/mL overnight at 4 ℃. MAb 1F6 was serially diluted two-fold, starting at 1.730 mg/ml and diluted to 1:1,024,000, and added to the coated wells. The remaining steps remained unchanged. Substitute into the formula K = (n-1)/2(nAb’-Ab) to calculate the affinity constant, where Ab and Ab’ represent the antibody concentrations (mol/L) that produce half the absorbance value when the antigen concentrations are Ag and Ag’, respectively, and n = Ag/Ag’. Then compare them in pairs. When n = 2, three K values can be obtained, and when n = 4, two K values can be obtained. Calculate the mean of the five K values as the final result.

The specificity of MAb 1F6 against different serotypes of Salmonella (S. Typhimurium, S. Infantis, S. London, S. Indiana, S. Corvallis, S. Dublin, S. Enteritidis, S. Derby, S. Kentucky, and S. Pullorum) and pathogenic bacteria (Shigella sonnei, Listeria monocytogenes, Campylobacter jejuni and Escherichia coli) was assessed using Western blotting. The bacterial solution was lysate by sonication and sonicated bacterial lysate was centrifuged at 4 ℃. The supernatant of the lysates was adjusted to the same concentration (5 mg/mL) and loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels before being transferred to an NC membrane. DnaK antibody (1:5,000 dilution, Abcam, ab69617) and MAb 1F6 (1:4,000 dilution) were used as the primary antibodies. After washing with PBST, the membranes were incubated with an HRP- goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody diluted 1:5000 in PBS. The membrane was then colored and scanned as previously described.

Construction of a blocking ELISA assay based on MAb HRP-1F6

The blocking ELISA conditions for the major steps were investigated under varying conditions. These conditions included the amount of the antigen coated on each well of the plate, the working concentrations of MAb HRP-1F6 (horseradish peroxidase-conjugated form of the Mab 1F6), blocking solution, blocking time, serum dilution, serum incubation time, and substrate reaction time.

The checkboard titration was used to determine the amount of antigen coated and the working concentration of MAb HRP-1F6. In brief, different dilutions of S. Rissen flagellin(25, 50, 100, 200 and 400 ng/well) were used to coat the ELISA plate at 4 ℃ for 12 h. Washed three times with PBST and then blocked with 2% skimmed milk in PBS at 37 ℃ for 2 h. After three further washes, the serum samples (diluted 1:10, positive, negative and block controls) were added into a corresponding well at 37 ℃ for 1.5 h. The wells were then mixed with 100 µL of HRP-labeled MAb 1F6-HRP (1.068 mg/mL), diluted two-fold from 1:1,000 to 1:16,000, for 30 min. Following sevent times washing, HRP activity was detected by adding TMB substrate and incubating at 37 ℃ for 6 min. The reaction was stopped with 2 M H2SO4 and the optical density at 450 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer. The optimal concentration of the coated S. Rissen flagellin and the MAb HRP-1F6 dilution were determined when the OD450 was approximately 1.0 in the bELISA.

Optimization of other conditions based on the optimal antigen coating concentration and optimal MAb HRP-1F6 concentration. Experiments were conducted according to the above steps using single-factor variable control method. According to the calculation for the percent inhibition (PI) = (1-OD450 of the serum sample/OD450 of the blank control) × 100%. The optimal conditions were selected when the P/N ratio (PI of positive/PI of negative) was relatively high.

Determination of cut-off value for the blocking ELISA assay

Fifty S. Pullorum-positive serum samples, 50 SPF chicken sera, and 82 serum samples positive for S. Enteritidis and S. Derby (validated by a commercial kit: The IDEXX Salmonella enteritidis Antibody Test (IDEXX SE Ab X2 Test)) were tested by the optimized blocking ELISA to determine the cutoff value, diagnostic sensitivity, and diagnostic specificity of this method. The PI values of the test samples were calculated via the following formula: PI = (1-OD450 of the serum sample/OD450 of the blank control) × 100%. The data were then analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 8.0) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to calculate the diagnostic sensitivity, diagnostic specificity and Youden index of the assay. The optimal cut-off value of the blocking ELISA was then selected based on the assay's Youden index.

Cross-reactivity analysis of blocking ELISA assay

The established bELISA method was used to detect positive sera for common avian pathogens, including S. Typhimurium, S. Derby, S. Pullorum, S. Gallinarum, S. Infantis, S. Kentucky, Infectious bursal disease virus (IBDV), Newcastle disease virus (NDV), Avian influenza virus H9, E. coli, and Proteus mirabilis. The specificity of the method was tested based on a determined threshold.

Sensitivity analysis of blocking ELISA assay

The sensitivity of the bELISA was determined using three different Salmonella-positive serum diluted at 1:10, 1:50, 1:100, 1:200, 1:400, 1:600, 1:800, and 1:1600. The PI values for each dilution were measured using an established bELISA method.

Repeatability and reproducibility analysis of blocking ELISA assay

Intra-assay repeatability experiment: Using enzyme-linked plates coated with the same batch of antigen, eight positive Salmonella serum samples and one SPF chicken serum sample were tested according to the established bELISA for intra-assay repeatability. Three measurements were performed and blank controls simultaneously. The coefficient of variation of the OD450 values among the samples were analyzed.

Inter-assay reproducibility experiment: Enzyme-linked plates coated with three different batches of antigen were used to test eight positive Salmonella serum samples, one SPF chicken serum sample and blank controls simultaneously, following the established bELISA method. The inter-assay repeatability was evaluated by calculating the CV of the OD450 values among the samples.

Agreementof the bELISA with commercial ELISA kit, iELISA, and PAT

Positive sera for S. Enteritidis and S. Derby were obtained from 81 and 15 samples, respectively, as verified using the IDEXX Salmonella enteritidis Antibody Test (IDEXX SE Ab X2 Test). Using iELISA, 15 positive serum samples for S. Typhimurium and 32 positive serum samples for S. Pullorum (as determined by PAT) were measured, along with 84 SPF chicken serum samples. The established bELISA method was used to test 227 serum samples, and the coincidence rate of the bELISA method was calculated using the following formula: (coincidence rate = serum with the same result/total number of sera × 100%).

Application of the blocking ELISA assay to clinical samples

The bELISA method for flagellin was used to test 205 layer chicken serum samples (30 days n = 62, 60 days n = 50, 90 days n = 93) obtained from a certain layer chicken farm in Jiangsu Province, China. Serum was collected three times at 30-day intervals.

Statistical analysis

Data were drawn and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) and SPSS 27.0. (SPSSInc., Chicago, USA). The ROC analysis and Youden’s statistic were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 8. The degree of agreement (Kappa value) of the bELISA with commercial ELISA kit, iELISA and PAT result was calculated using SPSS software (version 27.0.).

Establishing a bELISA assay for Salmonella infection

To detect Salmonella infection, a bELISA was developed using purified flagellin from S. Rissen, which was coated on a plate to capture specific antibodies in serum, and HRP-labelled MAb 1F6 as the detection antibody. The checkerboard titration method revealed that the appropriate concentration of the coating flagellin was 1 µg/ml, and the working dilution of the HRP-1F6 was 1:2000 (534 ng/ml) (Fig. 3A-B), and working dilution ratio of antisera was 1:10 (Fig. 3E). The parameters for competing incubations were further optimized to improve performance of the bELISA performance. The optimal working conditions were formulated, after about 100 μL flagellin (1 µg/ml) were coated onto 96-well plates in carbonate bicarbonate buffer (CBS, pH 9.4) at 4 ℃ for 12 h and washed with PBST (0.05% Tween 20 in phosphate-buffered saline) three times; 200 µL/well PBS containing 5% skim milk PBS solution (Fig. 3C) was added again for blocking for 2 h at 37 ℃ (Fig. 3D). After washing with PBST three times, 100 µL 1:10 diluted chicken serum (PBS for blank control) were added at 37 ℃ for 1 h (Fig. 3F) and then 100 µL HRP-labeled MAb 1F6 were added for 0.5 h. Discussion

Salmonella is one of the leading causes of foodborne illness worldwide, and it can infect humans through poultry and livestock products, causing food poisoning with serious public health consequences over the past several decades [32, 33]. Among approximate 2,600 serotypes of Salmonella, there are several predominant serotypes, including S. Typhimurium, S. Derby, S. Enteritidis, S. Indiana, S. Rissen and S. Kentucky, which are widely distributed in different animal and human populations [6]. These serotypes have diverse O and H antigens and are classified into several groups, such as B, C, and D, based on serological typing, which complicates the diagnosis and makes comprehensive testing challenging [34]. In order to detect a wider range of Salmonella serotypes, we generated a panel of anti-flagellin MAbs and selected 1F6 MAb that is specific for Salmonella species (except S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum) developing a blocking ELISA method for detecting Salmonella antibodies in infected chicken.

Salmonella flagellin, an outer membrane protein, not only aids bacterial motility and acts as an immune adjuvant, but also possesses good immunogenicity [35, 36]. Common antigenic epitopes of Salmonella flagellin have been confirmed as ideal diagnostic targets and are widely present in the Salmonella genus [22]. These flagellin can be obtained through E. coli expression system [6, 22, 37] or directly extracted; however, their construction is more time-consuming than direct extraction. Currently, high purity flagellin obtained using direct extraction methods [25]. In the present study, the flagellin obtained by acid hydrolysis had higher purity and concentration than those obtained by shear force and heat treatment [38]. During flagellin purification, it was found that traditional dialysis methods can cause some degradation of flagellin. In this study, by reducing the dialysis time to 6 h, the purification efficiency was improved. The extracted Salmonella flagellin was then used as an antigen for monoclonal antibody production.

When identification the specificity of the 1F6 monoclonal antibody via Western blotting, we observed differences in the intensity of the flagellin characteristic bands among different serotypes of Salmonella, despite these differences not being reflected in the whole-bacterial ELISA results. This inconsistency may be related to the expression levels and exposed structural features of the flagellin [38]. Interestingly, the band intensity of the flagellin from S. Corvallis was significantly lower than that of other strains. Through sequence alignment, we found that S. Corvallis indeed possesses a shorter 46 kDa flagellin (fliC) (Fig. S3). A study on the relationship between the deletion of the hypervariable domain region of Salmonella flagellin and their functional role in motility indicated that the highly conserved N- and C-termini of flagellin are important for their export and self-assembly, and that the middle sequence region (hypervariable region D2 and D3 domains) of different serotypes differ significantly in size and composition [39]. The hypervariable region D2 and D3 domains are associated with stabilizing the folded conformation of flagellin and enhancing the mechanical stability and propulsive force of flagella fibers [19]. Although transcriptional detection of fliC by qPCR has occasionally been reported in S. Gallinarum, such signals generally do not correspond to the synthesis of functional flagellin protein. Consequently, S. Gallinarum is expected to behave similarly to S. Pullorum, producing no detectable flagellin and therefore showing no cross-reactivity in flagellin-based assays, as supported by published findings [40, 41]. Since S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum are naturally non-flagellated, monoclonal antibodies targeting Salmonella flagellin cannot recognize or bind to these serovars. This is consistent with the results of this experiment. In the reaction spectrum analysis of the 1F6 monoclonal antibody (Table S2, S3), we found that S. Abortusequi, which lacks the antigen, can still react with the 1F6 MAb that recognizes H (phase 1) flagellin. By comparing their amino acid sequences, we found that the flagellin fljB of S. Abortusequi shares high similarity with the conserved regions of the flagellin fliC of Salmonella (Fig. S4). This also explains why our 1F6 MAb can detect S. Abortusequi.

Currently, most serotype detection studies focus on one or a few specific Salmonella serotypes [21, 42]. The bELISA method established in this study for detecting Salmonella antibodies enables broad-spectrum detection of Salmonella containing flagellin, with sensitivity and specificity reaching 97.56% and 100.00%, respectively, comparable to the detection capabilities of methods targeting single Salmonella strains [19]. Compared to traditional biochemical identification methods, these new serological antibody detection methods exhibit higher sensitivity and specificity. The bELISA method established in this study can detect suspected samples within 3 h through pre-coated plates, reducing the secondary antibody incubation time compared to the indirect ELISA method [43], and achieving faster detection than antigen detection [44]. However, this method cannot detect Salmonella lacking flagellin, such as host-adapted S. Pullorum. It is worth noting that with the advancement of purification efforts, most poultry farms in China have achieved purification of S. Pullorum, and the prevalence of S. Pullorum in poultry farms has decreased. Some scholars also believe that S. Pullorum has been effectively controlled in poultry farms [45]. In recent years, infections of S. Enteritidis, S. Typhimurium, S. Indiana, and S. Kentucky in chicken populations have gradually become dominant [6]. Looking abroad, S. Kentucky is also prevalent in many poultry farms worldwide, even surpassing the previously dominant strains to occupy the top ecological niche, which may be related to its high drug resistance [45, 46]. Therefore, the blocking ELISA method established for Salmonella flagellin still has great application prospects.

Since there are currently no commercially available kits for detecting Salmonella species, we evaluated the method's concordance rate by testing known positive Salmonella serum, achieving a concordance rate of 97.35%, comparable to most detection methods [19, 21, 42]. We tested Salmonella infection in layer chicken (n = 205) aged 30, 60, and 90 days at a poultry farm and found no Salmonella infection in the first 60 days. Infection rates began at 4% by 90 days, with the average inhibition rate increasing gradually with age. It is evident that the Salmonella infection rate in layer chicken at this farm is relatively low, which may be related to its scale, as the farm is a comprehensive enterprise primarily engaged in feed production, poultry breeding, and modernized layer chicken farming. A study reported that the poultry Salmonella isolate across 12 provinces in China ranged from 5.17% to 12.94%, indicating that the prevalence of Salmonella may be influenced by certain geographical factors [47]. A study in Egypt reported that the detection rate of Salmonella in small-scale layer chicken flocks (28.50%) was higher than in large-scale farming (3.3%) [48], which is similar to the findings of this study. A similar situation was observed in Canada, with literature reporting Salmonella prevalence rates of 29%, 8%, and 7% in egg-laying chickens in Ontario (ONSS1a and ONSS1b) and British Columbia (BCSS2a), respectively. The 29% Salmonella recovery rate was from earlier years [49]. In Bangladesh, the detection rate of Salmonella in small-scale commercial layer chickens with lower biosecurity standards can reach 25.75% (197/765), indicating that the detection rate of Salmonella in layer flocks is associated with farm size and management practices [50]. Compared to broiler chickens, the detection rate of Salmonella in layer chickens is significantly lower. In China, broiler chickens are typically marketed at 30–45 days of age [51, 52]. Studies have shown that the detection rate of Salmonella increases from poultry farms (9.10%) to slaughterhouses (9.50%) and reaches its highest level in retail markets (41.80%), indicating that the isolation rate of Salmonella also varies depending on the environment [1, 53, 54]. Therefore, timely monitoring of Salmonella infection in chicken at the front end and effective control of Salmonella cross-contamination in downstream processes are beneficial for the implementation of Salmonella control measures.

Overall, the blocking ELISA method established in this study has a high concordance rate for detecting known positive Salmonella serum and can be used for the detection of clinical samples. The detection results for clinical samples are consistent with those reported by other researchers, indicating reliability. This method provides a new technology for the rapid and broad-spectrum diagnosis of Salmonella in chickens and holds promising application prospects. However, it is important to note that this detection method cannot be used for antibody testing in cases of S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum infection in chickens. In practical applications, this may lead to missed detections of Salmonella, resulting in a lower positive rate. However, given the effective control measures in place for S. Pullorum and S. Gallinarum in chickens, this concern is not overly significant. In the future, efforts should be directed toward developing more comprehensive antibody detection methods targeting the Salmonella species.
