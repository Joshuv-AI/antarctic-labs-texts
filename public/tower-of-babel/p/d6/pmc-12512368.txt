Improving racial data equity among minority groups in South Carolina using COVID-19 as an example: application of principal components analysis
Authors: FNU Rubaiya, Janet O’Connor, Lyubomir N. Kolev, James M. Antill, Margaret Iiams, LaNaya A. Martin, Chantaezia Z. Joseph, Claire Youngblood, Jennifer Almeda-Garrett, Linda E. Kelemen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundData inequity occurs when racial and ethnic groups are aggregated during data collection or reporting despite their differences. To demonstrate racial data equity importance, we re-analyzed South Carolina’s (SC) census data and COVID-19 case-rate and death-rate distributions according to age, sex, and new combined single and multiracial categories.MethodsThe new combined single and multiracial categories included individuals who identified as a single race alone (such as American Indian or Alaska Native, AI-AN) with those who identified as more than one race (such as AI-AN and White) regardless of Hispanic or Latino heritage. We compared those distributions to the single race categories using the American Community Survey 2018–2022 and COVID-19 case and death surveillance data, 2020–2023, for SC. We used principal components analysis to test for differences in age-sex distributions between single race alone and new combined single and multiracial categories for each race.ResultsCompared to the combined single and multiracial categories, single race alone categories lose information, underestimate the population of younger-aged people of AI-AN, Asian, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (NH-OPI) races, and result in COVID-19 case and death rates with extreme values across age groups, particularly for AI-AN and NH-OPI populations. Among AI-AN, certain age groups had different COVID-19 case rate patterns between females and males, but this was explained by race categorization (single race alone vs. combined single and multiracial, P < 0.0001).ConclusionsCombined single and multiracial categories achieve data equity by avoiding data suppression or aggregation of small diverse populations. Differences in COVID-19 case rates across some age groups between females and males may be biased depending on how race is defined. Younger generations are increasingly multiracial and will be underrepresented if only single race categories are used in public health reporting practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-025-00419-4.

Introduction

Data inequity occurs when racial and ethnic groups are aggregated during data collection or reporting despite their differences. Data privacy laws are one factor that contribute to data inequity in public health practice, resulting in data suppression of small populations or else data aggregation with other groups [1]. Data aggregation enhances statistical stability for calculation of disease or death rates by combining multiple small populations even though the interpretation of the resulting rates is not particularly meaningful for targeted public health intervention. This practice obscures differences in exposures, risk factors, and disease rates and outcomes, results in missed opportunities for health interventions, and perpetuates health disparities.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, race data in South Carolina (SC) were summarized as White single race, Black or African American (Black-AA) single race, unknown race, or a catch-all category of Other races. At that time, the catch-all category of Other races included individuals of the smaller populations of single race American Indian or Alaska Native (AI-AN), single race Asian, single race Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander (NH-OPI), single race Other, and individuals of two or more races. Reports from other studies showed that, when data were disaggregated, Black-AA, AI-AN, Hispanic, NH-OPI and Asian people had substantially higher rates of COVID-19 infection, hospitalization, and death compared to White people [2, 3]. Despite this, these groups were often lumped into “Other” race in public dashboards, masking their elevated risk. Furthermore, unless explicitly stated by authors, mentions of a specific race typically refer to individuals identifying solely with that race (i.e., single race alone). The method of reporting single race alone can underestimate counts and rates from some smaller populations such as AI-AN. Indeed, data from the 2020 census showed that 87% of all individuals who reported White as a race were classified as one race alone (the remaining 13% were classified as White in combination with another race) [4]. Likewise, 88% of all Black-AA and 83% of all Asian Americans were classified as one race alone. In contrast, just 39% of AI-AN people were classified as one race alone and the majority (61%) were classified as AI-AN in combination with another race [4]. This means that AI-AN are grossly underestimated as a population when only evaluated as a single race.

To demonstrate the importance of racial data equity, we re-evaluated population distributions and COVID-19 case-rate and death-rate distributions in SC using newly created combined single and multiracial categories and compared those distributions to the single race alone categories.

Materials and methods

Race category and ethnicity definitions

Race and ethnicity were derived from two separate questions [5]. The single race populations included those who selected only one of the five main races (White, Black-AA, AI-AN, Asian or NH-OPI), regardless of Hispanic or Latino heritage. The combined single and multiracial category included those who selected one or a combination of the five main races, regardless of Hispanic or Latino heritage. Consequently, a multiracial individual was represented in more than one race category and was reflected in the denominators of each combined single and multiracial category for the calculation of percentages or rates. Individuals who responded to the question, “Are you Hispanic or Latino?”, were identified as Hispanic or Latino heritage regardless of race. Responses from the two questions were not combined, for example, to create non-Hispanic AI-AN or Hispanic Black-AA.

The definition of Other race varied according to the data source and did not depend on Hispanic or Latino heritage. In the population data (see below), the Other single race category included people who identified with a single race alone, excluding White, Black-AA, AI-AN, Asian or NH-OPI. The Other single and other multiracial category included people who identified with a single race alone or in combination with other races, excluding White, Black-AA, AI-AN, Asian or NH-OPI. In the surveillance data, we defined the Other single and all multiracial category as a catch-all category similar to what was used during the pandemic except that it excluded any single race not included in the main five categories, but included all multiracial individuals (e.g., combinations like AI-AN and White, White and Black-AA, or Asian and Other). We defined the Other single and other multiracial category to include people who identified with a single race alone or in combination with other races, excluding White, Black-AA, AI-AN, Asian or NH-OPI.

Population data

We used counts from the American Community Survey (ACS) 2018–2022 5-year population estimates to examine distributions of 100 race combinations to assess the relative size of racial groups in SC [6]. We created population pyramids for sex and 5-year age groups for the SC population overall, single race categories, combined single and multiracial categories, and for Hispanic or Latino individuals regardless of race.

COVID-19 surveillance data

We used case and death counts from the SC Department of Public Health (DPH)’s COVID-19 surveillance database and population counts from the ACS to create COVID-19 rate pyramids by sex and 5-year age groups for the SC population overall, single race categories, combined single and multiracial categories, and for Hispanic or Latino individuals regardless of race. Data were restricted to the pandemic period (March 4, 2020 to May 11, 2023). Individuals with missing or unknown race were excluded from analyses.

Group comparisons

Our reference groups were the single race alone categories for White, Black-AA, AI-AN, Asian, and NH-OPI populations, the Other single race category (population data), and the Other single and all multiracial category (surveillance data). Our comparison groups were the combined single and multiracial categories for White, Black-AA, AI-AN, Asian, and NH-OPI populations, and the Other single and other multiracial category (population data and surveillance data).

Statistical analyses

Data visualizations were created with Power BI (version 2.121.903.0; Microsoft Corporation). We used principal component analysis (PCA) to decompose the data into a smaller set of uncorrelated variables to statistically compare the difference between the shapes of the single race alone category and combined single and multiracial category pyramids. Briefly, we implemented a joint PCA using the FactoMineR package by combining the pyramid data for each race into a matrix of 38 age groups (rows) by two sexes (columns) denoted by a grouping variable (single race alone category vs. combined single and multiracial category). Numeric data were normalized prior to implementing PCA. To meet normality assumptions for statistical testing, we used the Box-Cox transformation on principal components (PCs) and applied Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA) to jointly test differences in PC1 scores and PC2 scores. Statistical differences were calculated with the F statistic that compared the ratio of the between-group variance to the within-group variance. We used the factoextra package in R to produce biplots to visualize the outputs of the PCA.

Results

According to the ACS 5-year population estimates, there were 5,142,761 residents in SC. Of these, single race alone categories comprised most of the population for White (65.1%), Black-AA (25.8%), Other (2.4%), Asian (1.7%) and NH-OPI (0.05%). In contrast, more individuals reported the racial combination of AI-AN and White (0.5%) than for AI-AN single race alone (0.3%).

Females exceeded males as a percent of the SC population in all age groups over 25 years (Fig. 1). The peak of the female population was at 60–64 years, whereas the peak of the male population was at 15–19 years. Each race as well as people of Hispanic or Latino heritage had a unique population distribution. Compared to the combined single and multiracial categories, single race alone categories were biased against younger ages for AI-AN, Asian, and NH-OPI populations. For example, single race alone categories underestimated the size of the Asian female population aged less than 20 years, the percentage of AI-AN males aged 25–29 years, and the percentage of NH-OPI individuals in various age groups particularly under 40 years. Figure 2 shows COVID-19 rate pyramids for 1,818,217 cases in SC. Supplemental Table 2 lists all counts and rates. The SC COVID-19 case-rate pyramid included 133,810 cases with missing race and 166,776 cases with reported unknown race. Both were excluded from further analysis. The combined single and multiracial categories show fewer extreme rates, particularly for AI-AN and NH-OPI populations. Although the shape of the COVID-19 case-rate pyramid differed between races and from people of Hispanic or Latino heritage, rates were noticeably higher among females than males in most working age groups for each combined single and multiracial category and for people of Hispanic or Latino heritage. Also, males over age 80 of Black-AA, Asian and NH-OPI multi-races and males over age 80 of Other single and other multiracial category had higher case rates than did White males of the same age. However, the small numbers of AI-AN, Asian, and NH-OPI populations may lead to unstable rates at older ages and should be interpreted cautiously.

PCA reduces multidimensional data into principal components, each representing a combination of variables that capture patterns of variation. In this analysis, the two primary components — PC1 and PC2 — were derived from variables representing COVID-19 case rates for females and males across age groups. PC1 captures variation predominantly aligned with overall case rate magnitude, regardless of sex, and reflects broader distributional trends across age cohorts. PC2 captures differential patterns between sexes, highlighting how female and male case rates diverge for specific race categories and age groups. The black arrows labeled “Female” and “Male” represent the loading vectors, indicating the direction and contribution of each sex variable to the PCs. The fact that these arrows point in opposite directions along PC2 suggests that female and male case rates are negatively correlated for specific age groups. Data points representing different age groups and racial categories are projected onto the PCA space (Fig. 4). Their positions indicate how closely their case rate patterns align with the directions of the female and male vectors. For example, in Fig. 4A, age groups within the AI-AN population in the single race alone category cluster asymmetrically with respect to the sex arrows revealing distinct structures in case rates between AI-AN females and AI-AN males. Conversely, in the combined single and multiracial category, AI-AN subgroups display more centralized and overlapping projections, indicating less differentiation between male and female case rates. This indicates that certain age groups among AI-AN had different case rate patterns between males and females that is almost entirely explained by differences in the way race was categorized. Figure 4B shows the corresponding biplot for Other race, with a similar interpretation of the data. Differences in PC scores between single race alone and combined categories were statistically significant for AI-AN (P < 0.0001) and for Other race (P = 0.0002), reinforcing that the observed variation is not random but reflective of structural differences in how race categorization impacts case rate distribution. P values for the other group comparisons are found in Supplemental Table 4.

We attempted to evaluate any potential impact of the exclusion of missing or unknown race on our findings. In sensitivity analyses, we used PCA to compare the age and sex distributions of the SC COVID-19 case pyramid in Fig. 2 and the SC COVID-19 death pyramid in Fig. 3 that included missing or unknown race to the SC COVID-19 case and death pyramids, respectively, that excluded missing or unknown race (counts and percentages are found in Supplemental Table 5). Discussion

Compared to combined single and multiracial categories, single race alone categories lose information, are biased against younger ages for AI-AN, Asian and NH-OPI, and underestimate the AI-AN state population by more than half. Single race alone and combined single and multiracial categories were also statistically different for COVID-19 case rate patterns among AI-AN and Other race. As younger generations are increasingly multiracial [7], they will be inequitably represented if only single race alone categories are used in public health reporting practices.

Recent reports advocate for data disaggregation using non-mutually exclusive categories [4, 8–11]. Shephard and colleagues [9] disaggregated data among pregnant persons in Massachusetts into 12 racial and 34 ethnic groups to examine COVID-19 vaccination coverage. They reported that, although differences in coverage existed between broad race and ethnic categories, the true extent of the differences was only apparent when data were disaggregated. Springer and colleagues [10] disaggregated tuberculosis data among AI-AN individuals in the United States and found that exclusion of multiracial or Hispanic AI-AN persons from analysis substantively underestimated racial and ethnic health disparities in this group. These results underscore that data equity and equitable data systems are needed to effectively address health inequities.

Although COVID-19 affected more females than males in most working age groups in our data, death rates were higher among males than females across almost all age groups for each combined single and multiracial category in our study. Explanations cited for higher female case rates include more women in care-related occupations including school, daycare or health care, and as residents in long-term care facilities [12]. An analysis of weekly case and mortality rates by sex for each state found considerable variation of the sex disparity mortality over time and across states that may be attributable to health behaviors, preexisting health conditions, occupation, race and ethnicity, and other markers of social factors [13]. However, similar to our findings, data for males in SC in that study [13] show case rates to be generally lower, and mortality rates to be generally higher, over time compared to females.

Others reported that COVID-19 deaths disproportionately affected non-White groups [2, 3]. Our results align with those reports but varied somewhat by the way race was classified. When the single race alone categories were used, death rates among those aged 85 + years were higher for Black-AA females and males, AI-AN males, Asian males, and Other single and all multiracial females and males compared to their White counterparts. When the combined single and multiracial categories were used, death rates among those aged 85 + years were higher for Black-AA females and males, Asian males, and Other single and other multiracial females and males compared to their White counterparts, with rates for NH-OPI females being close to rates for White females. These important findings underscore the need for disaggregated data to understand group-specific impacts.

Our findings of a statistically significant difference in COVID-19 case rate distributions across age-sex groups between single race alone and combined single and multiracial category pyramids were restricted to AI-AN and Other race. This may be explained by the relatively large contribution of multiracial individuals in these two groups. For example, AI-AN is the only major race in SC that has a larger population of multiracial individuals (AI-AN and White) than of AI-AN single race alone. There was also a large number of multiracial individuals who could be categorized as White, Black-AA, AI-AN, Asian, or NH-OPI that were moved from the Other single race and all multiracial category. Thus, these differences for AI-AN and Other race pyramids likely increased the ratio of the between-group to the within-group variance resulting in a large value of the F statistic.

The findings in this report are subject to at least three limitations. First, we excluded data with missing (7.4%) or unknown (9.1%) race, thereby underestimating COVID-19 rates. Missing and unknown race in our COVID-19 surveillance data were almost equally attributable to White and Black-AA race for a sample of 5,683 individuals for whom we also received patient records using electronic case reporting [14], suggesting the difference in rates between the races in the current study may be relatively accurate. Indeed, we found no overall difference in SC COVID-19 case and death distributions in sensitivity analyses comparing distributions with and without individuals who had missing or unknown race. Second, COVID-19 surveillance data likely contained reporting errors for race, leading to results that were affected by noise, bias or both. Third, although the sample size increased for combined single and multiracial categories, small population sizes in extreme age groups contributed to unstable rate estimates. One solution is to use broader age categories, which is preferable to race-aggregated data.

Implications for public health practice

Using combined single and multiracial categories is a feasible and effective approach to achieving data equity by avoiding data suppression or aggregation of small diverse populations. Consideration of combined single and multiracial categories prevents underestimation of minority groups’ composition to the population and is necessary to accurately evaluate health disparities.
