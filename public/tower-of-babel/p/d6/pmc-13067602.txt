Physician attitudes and system factors associated with non-medically indicated cesarean sections in Thailand: a prospective implementation study
Authors: Vitaya Titapant, Saifon Chawanpaiboon, Attapol Kanjanapongporn, Julaporn Pooliam, Sanitra Anuwutnavin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCesarean section rates are rising globally, and Thailand has experienced steady growth in non-medically indicated cesarean sections (NMICS). Because clinician and administrator perspectives influence practice, mapping their attitudes and perceptions is essential to design interventions that reduce unnecessary cesareans.MethodsWe conducted a prospective descriptive implementation study approved by the Siriraj Ethics Committee (Si-802/2023) and registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20230913007). Methods comprised in-depth interviews, questionnaire development, and a nationwide survey. Between March and July 2025, a validated questionnaire was administered to 120 physicians at tertiary hospitals in Thailand, with sample size based on a 95% confidence level and 5% allowable error. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and multivariable logistic regression, and associations were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsOf 120 respondents, 60.8% reported performing NMICS and 63.0% acknowledged agreeing to patient requests. In multivariable analysis, fear of litigation (aOR 2.5, 95% CI 1.1–5.8) and workload pressures (aOR 2.0, 95% CI 0.9–4.2) were associated with NMICS practice. Agreement to patient requests was more likely in private hospitals (aOR 1.9) and among physicians citing litigation concerns (aOR 2.1). Support for hospital reduction policies was linked to the presence of an action plan (aOR 2.8) and to the belief that NMICS increases costs (aOR 2.2). Despite these concerns, many physicians minimized NMICS consequences, and fewer than one-third of hospitals had active reduction initiatives.ConclusionsNMICS in Thailand reflects not only litigation risk and workload but also attitudes of unconcern, normalization of cesarean delivery, cultural beliefs, and systemic barriers. Background

Cesarean section (CS) is a major operation performed when medically indicated to protect maternal or neonatal health. Common indications include fetal distress, malpresentation, placenta previa, cephalopelvic disproportion, maternal medical conditions, labor complications, and select infections. Some procedures are planned, whereas others are urgent, particularly in high-risk settings or when a prior CS elevates complication risk [1]. Clinicians must balance risks and benefits, ensure informed consent, consider resources and surgical expertise, respect patient preferences, and avoid non-medically indicated or unnecessary interventions to maximize safety for mother and child.

Although generally safe, CS carries higher risk than vaginal birth. Complications include wound or uterine infection, increased blood loss requiring transfusion, injury to adjacent organs such as bladder or bowel, and adhesions that may affect future pregnancies [2]. Recovery is typically longer, with potential persistent pain and emotional distress. Rare but serious events include maternal mortality, primarily from pulmonary embolism and hemorrhage. Risks increase with prior CS, obesity, prolonged labor, or emergency procedures [2].

CS rates have risen sharply worldwide, with marked regional variation. Reports from The Lancet and the 2018 International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics World Congress in Brazil indicated that the global CS rate increased from 12.1% in 2000 to 22.1% in 2015 [3]. Latin America reached 44.3%, the highest regional rate [3]. In China, the rate was already 39% by 2002 [4]. In Thailand, CS increased from 7.06% in 1990 to 37.7% in 1997 [5], reaching 44.46% in 2010 [6]. By 2022, the average across Thailand’s 12 health regions was 42% (range 32%–50%), and a tertiary-care hospital in Bangkok reported 49% in 2017 [7, 8].

In 2019, the Ministry of Public Health and the Royal Thai College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists introduced measures to curb this trend and promoted the Robson Classification to evaluate CS indications [9, 10]. Nevertheless, CS rates continue to rise. This study therefore explored determinants underlying the persistent increase and sought tailored criteria that can more effectively guide clinical decision-making while addressing root causes of unnecessary CS.

Methods

Ethics approval and registration

This study was approved by the Siriraj Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital (Si-802/2023), and registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20230913007).

Study design

We conducted a prospective descriptive implementation study with research and development components to investigate the attitudes, perceptions, and opinions of healthcare managers regarding non-medically indicated cesarean sections (NMICS). This survey-based research aimed to inform development of an intervention model to address rising CS rates.

A purposive sampling approach was used to recruit physicians and obstetricians providing maternity care from tertiary, general, private, and university hospitals. Invitations were distributed through institutional networks and professional communication channels, and participation was voluntary.

Sample size calculation

The physician sample size (n = 120) was derived from the main NMICS research project, which estimated the required sample of pregnant women using P = 0.5, α = 0.05, and d = 0.05, yielding a minimum of 385 cases. Samples were proportionally allocated among pregnant women, obstetricians, and administrators in a 10:3:1 ratio, resulting in a target of 116 physicians, with 120 ultimately recruited. Physicians were selected using proportionate sampling to ensure representation across clinical settings.

Study phases

This project is part of a multi-phase implementation research framework. Phase I involved prospective in-depth interviews, and Phase II consisted of questionnaire development and validation. The present manuscript reports findings from Phase III, which was conducted as a cross-sectional survey among physicians at a single time point. Although the overall project was planned prospectively, the analytic design for this publication is cross-sectional, and results reflect physician perspectives during the survey period.

This research comprised 3 phases.

Phase I: in-depth interviews

Between July 1 and December 31, 2024, we conducted in-depth interviews to gather information in 4 domains: general participant information; and attitudes, perceptions, and opinions regarding NMICS. We recruited 10% of the total required sample, yielding 12 participating physicians.

All interviews were conducted by two researchers with professional backgrounds in obstetrics and social science, each trained in qualitative interviewing techniques. Both interviewers had prior experience in conducting in-depth interviews and in research related to maternal health. To minimize interviewer bias, they underwent standardized training, used a structured interview guide, applied neutral probing techniques, and conducted all interviews in a private setting to encourage open and unbiased discussion.

Participants were invited to a private counseling room, where the study was explained in detail and questions were addressed. Involvement was voluntary, and participants could decline or withdraw at any time. Those who agreed provided written informed consent before interviews began.

Demographic and clinical data were collected using a standardized questionnaire, and attitudes, perceptions, and opinions regarding NMICS were assessed with validated instruments. Semistructured interviews were audio-recorded with consent to explore decision-making processes related to NMICS. The total data collection time per participant was approximately 30 min.

Phase II: questionnaire development and validation

Between January 1 and February 28, 2025, we analyzed Phase I data to derive means and standard deviations. These results informed a comprehensive questionnaire, which was tested for accuracy and validity before Phase III. The questionnaire developed from Phase I findings underwent content and face validation by experts in obstetrics, social science, and medical ethics. It was then pilot-tested in an additional 10 physicians to assess clarity and feasibility, after which internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and items were revised. The finalized, validated questionnaire was subsequently administered in Phase III.

Phase III: survey administration

During March 2025 to July 31, 2025, we administered the validated questionnaire to 120 physicians at tertiary hospitals across Thailand. This phase collected detailed information on attitudes, perceptions, and opinions regarding NMICS.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data were summarized with descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means and standard deviations. Attitudes, perceptions, and opinions were summarized similarly. We used univariable chi-square tests to explore factors associated with CS. Variables with a P value < 0.10 in univariable logistic regression were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. A slightly liberal threshold was selected to avoid excluding clinically relevant variables that may show significance only after adjustment for confounders, which is an approach commonly used in exploratory logistic regression modelling. Associations were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Phase 1: qualitative exploration

Twelve physicians participated in in-depth interviews. Thematic analysis identified multiple factors influencing non-medically indicated cesarean section (NMICS), including medicolegal concern, workload burden, convenience of scheduling, institutional inefficiencies, and cultural or patient-driven beliefs such as auspicious timing, fear of labor pain, and family expectations. Some participants perceived NMICS as normalized or less problematic, while others expressed ethical tension between respecting maternal autonomy and promoting evidence-based practice. These findings were used to construct the questionnaire and guide variable selection for the quantitative analysis, and a summary of the qualitative themes is presented at the beginning of the Results section to provide context and a logical transition to Phases II and III.

Phase II: questionnaires development

In Phase II, the draft questionnaire was pilot-tested in 10 physicians, who confirmed good clarity, feasibility, and item comprehension. Minor wording adjustments were made, and internal consistency was acceptable before the finalized questionnaire was administered in Phase III.

Phase III: quantitative survey findings

Participant characteristics and practice patterns

A total of 120 physicians participated; most were women in their 30s, trained as general obstetrician-gynecologists, and employed in government hospitals. Most still performed CS regularly, and more than half had performed NMICS (Supplementary Tables 1–2).

Problem recognition and hospital policy environment

Physicians generally recognized NMICS as a hospital problem, although only a minority regarded it as urgent (Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Tables 3–5; Fig. 1). More than half believed CS should be limited to medical indications; however, formal hospital policies or action plans were inconsistently implemented (Tables 1 and 2; Supplementary Tables 3–5; Figs. 2 and 3).

Discussion

Most obstetricians in Thailand recognized NMICS as a hospital problem, yet a substantial proportion reported performing it or agreeing to patient requests. Fear of litigation and workload pressures were important predictors, and attitudes of disbelief, unconcern, and normalization further promoted NMICS by framing it as less serious, less consequential, or safer and easier to control. Administrators highlighted system-level influences, including economic incentives and the absence of national education campaigns.

These findings align with international evidence that physician perspectives drive rising cesarean rates. In Mexico, Vallejos Parás et al. reported rates exceeding 59%, and many physicians cited preference for cesarean delivery, perceived safety, scheduling convenience, and maternal request as justifications [11]. Our data similarly indicate that physician attitudes, particularly normalization of NMICS, shape practice.

Ethical tensions identified in our survey mirror global challenges: balancing maternal autonomy with professional responsibility. Montanari Vergallo et al. emphasized the dilemmas when women request NMICS, underscoring the need to balance autonomy with clinical judgment [12]. Our study echoes these concerns because nearly all physicians reported counseling patients, yet many doubted its effectiveness, and some acknowledged using non-scientific or weak justifications under pressure from litigation risk or workload.

Although the questionnaire included the item ‘Income from cesarean section is higher than vaginal birth’ based on themes emerging from Phase 1 interviews, no participant selected “Yes” in the survey. This discrepancy suggests that while financial incentive was raised qualitatively as a potential driver of NMICS, physicians may be reluctant to openly acknowledge income-related motivation due to social desirability and ethical sensitivity, reflecting an underlying but rarely verbalized influence.

The ethical tension surrounding NMICS reflects the broader conflict between respecting maternal autonomy and ensuring safe, evidence-based care. Montanari Vergallo et al. [12] concluded that, after counseling, physicians must respect a woman’s insistence on cesarean delivery without medical indication. Our findings suggest Thai physicians navigate the same dilemma but often without the full safeguards of a robust consent process.

Professional guidance underscores informed consent as central to decision-making. Consent Advice No. 14 from the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists recommends early, individualized counseling for elective cesareans, well before labor, with balanced information on benefits, risks, and alternatives [9]. Counseling should cover maternal risks such as hemorrhage, infection, abnormal placentation in subsequent pregnancies, and neonatal respiratory morbidity, while also emphasizing the advantages of vaginal birth. In our study, physicians reported that patients generally trusted and followed their advice; however, some acknowledged persuading patients with questionable reasons, which jeopardizes the integrity of informed consent.

A dedicated consent form for NMICS, supplemented by standardized patient information leaflets or decision aids, could strengthen transparency, provide medicolegal protection, and ensure that patients make informed decisions. Such documentation would explicitly state that cesarean delivery was requested despite no medical indication, thereby balancing maternal autonomy with professional accountability. In Thailand, where cultural beliefs such as auspicious birth dates, fear of labor pain, and family expectations shape choices, structured consent processes are particularly valuable [13, 14]. They create space to correct misconceptions, explore motivations, and mitigate social and cultural pressures. Future research should test whether standardized consent forms and culturally tailored decision aids improve patient knowledge and reduce unnecessary cesarean rates.

Our results confirm that patient requests are a central barrier to reducing NMICS. This aligns with Thai studies reporting that fear of pain, desire for convenience, and cultural beliefs drive cesarean preference [13, 14]. These reports documented the persistence of traditional childbirth beliefs in modern settings, whereas Bubpawong et al. described how limited patient participation in clinical decisions reinforces physician dominance [15]. Together, these dynamics sustain demand for NMICS and constrain shared decision-making.

At the institutional level, administrators identified lack of action plans, inconsistent policy enforcement, and economic incentives as barriers. Although many hospitals reported policies to reduce NMICS, few had designated committees or systematic monitoring. This policy–implementation gap mirrors global findings. The Quali-Dec (“Quality Decision-Making by Women and Providers”) project, integrating audit, feedback, decision support, and labor companionship, has demonstrated promise in reducing unnecessary cesareans across diverse settings [16, 17]. Adapting these non-clinical strategies to Thailand could address physician disbelief and patient-driven demand.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is its inclusion of both physicians and administrators, providing a multilevel perspective on NMICS. Structured questionnaires enabled systematic assessment of attitudes, perceptions, and opinions. However, as a cross-sectional survey, these data cannot establish causality. Social desirability bias may have led participants to underreport practices, such as motivating patients toward NMICS. Because recruitment centered on tertiary hospitals, findings may not generalize to smaller or rural facilities.

Implications and future research

This study highlights critical questions for future research. Key issues include how patients interpret and respond to NMICS counseling. Additional priorities are identifying strategies that shift preferences toward vaginal birth and balancing medicolegal protections with autonomy to reduce defensive practices among physicians. Economic questions include how income models or hospital incentives could be modified to limit systemic drivers of NMICS.

Therefore, future studies should include qualitative investigations of patient decision-making and intervention trials of structured counseling and decision aids. Evaluations of policy-level initiatives are also needed, including public education campaigns, standardized consent processes, and institutional monitoring of NMICS rates. Integrating culturally sensitive approaches, such as those promoted by the Quali-Dec project, will be vital for addressing physician- and patient-level determinants of unnecessary cesarean section in Thailand.

Conclusions

NMICS in Thailand is influenced not only by litigation risk and workload but also by disbelief, unconcern, normalization of cesarean delivery, cultural traditions, and systemic barriers. Therefore, reducing NMICS requires a multifaceted strategy that strengthens informed consent, provides culturally sensitive education, ensures institutional accountability, and adapts proven international interventions to promote safe, ethical, and appropriate childbirth practices.
