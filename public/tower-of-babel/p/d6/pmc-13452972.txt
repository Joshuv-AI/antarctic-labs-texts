Dietary metabolizable energy and crude protein affect growth, serum indices, and fecal metabolomics in early-weaned gray-feathered King pigeon squabs☆
Authors: Yuanhao Li, Xiaobin Li, Huiguo Yang, Haiying Li, Yafei Liang, Mingcong Ding, Aikemu Maimaitijiang, Jie Ren, Honglei Sun, Jiajia Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of dietary metabolizable energy (ME) and crude protein (CP) levels on growth performance, serum traits, and fecal metabolomics in early-weaned gray-feathered King pigeon squabs. A 3 × 3 factorial design was used with three ME levels (11.9, 12.2, and 12.5 MJ/kg) and three CP levels (18, 19, and 20%), producing nine treatments. A total of 216 healthy 15-D-old squabs were randomly allocated to the nine treatments, with eight replicates per treatment and three squabs per replicate, for a 28-d trial. Growth performance, body measurements, and serum biochemical, immune, and antioxidant traits were determined. Based on preliminary body-weight records available at sample selection and contrasting dietary nutrient structures, the 18% CP + 12.5 MJ/kg ME and 20% CP + 11.9 MJ/kg ME treatments were selected for untargeted fecal metabolomics. Dietary ME and CP significantly affected growth performance, some body measurements, and multiple serum variables, and significant interaction effects were detected for some traits. In the overall phenotypic assessment, responses to dietary energy-protein combinations were trait-specific, and the 20% CP + 12.2 MJ/kg ME treatment showed a favorable growth trend. Untargeted metabolomics revealed clear separation between the 18% CP + 12.5 MJ/kg ME and 20% CP + 11.9 MJ/kg ME groups in both electrospray ionization negative mode (ESI−) and positive mode (ESI+). Differential metabolites were mainly annotated as lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic acids and their derivatives, and organic heterocyclic compounds, and were mainly associated with sphingolipid metabolism, porphyrin-related metabolism, cofactor metabolism, and central carbon metabolism. Correlation analysis further indicated broad statistical associations between several key fecal differential metabolites and serum biochemical, immune, and antioxidant variables. Overall, dietary ME and CP jointly affected growth, serum physiological status, and representative fecal metabolic characteristics in early-weaned gray-feathered King pigeon squabs.

Introduction

Dietary metabolizable energy (ME) and crude protein (CP) are two major factors regulating nutrient supply, growth, and physiological status in poultry (Li et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2024). Their effects are closely linked through the dietary energy-protein balance because energy supply influences feed intake, nutrient partitioning, and the extent to which amino acids are used for tissue deposition rather than catabolism, whereas protein supply determines amino acid availability for growth and metabolic functions (Cao et al., 2024; Heijmans et al., 2021; Sklan and Plavnik, 2002). Therefore, clarifying the response of early-weaned pigeons to different ME and CP combinations is important for practical diet formulation.

Compared with chickens and ducks, nutritional information for pigeons remains limited, particularly during early weaning. Pigeons are altricial birds, and weaning involves a rapid transition from parental feeding to independent feed intake. This transition is accompanied by changes in nutrient source, digestive function, intestinal barrier status, and metabolic activity. Previous studies have shown that early weaning can impair growth and intestinal health in squabs and can activate inflammatory and oxidative responses (Wen et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2022, 2023). Studies in pigeons also indicate that dietary energy and protein levels or energy-to-protein ratios can influence growth performance, intestinal development, and physiological status (Liu et al., 2025; Omar et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2023a,b; Sales and Janssens, 2003).

In addition to growth performance, serum biochemical, immune, and antioxidant variables provide useful phenotypic information for evaluating nutritional responses. These variables reflect protein and energy metabolism, lipid metabolism, inflammatory status, immune function, and oxidative balance, and have been used in pigeons and other poultry species to assess the physiological effects of dietary nutrient supply (Liu et al., 2025; Song et al., 2023).

Untargeted fecal metabolomics can complement conventional phenotypic evaluation by revealing metabolic differences associated with nutritional treatments. In poultry nutrition research, metabolomics has been used to identify metabolic pathways and potential biomarkers related to growth, feed efficiency, and health status (Abd El-Hack et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024). Fecal metabolites are particularly useful for exploring diet-related changes in intestinal and host metabolic interactions, although they should be interpreted as representative metabolic outputs rather than direct measures of all tissue-specific processes (Zhou et al., 2020).

Accordingly, this study used a 3 × 3 factorial design to evaluate the effects of dietary ME and CP levels on growth performance, body measurements, and serum biochemical, immune, and antioxidant variables in early-weaned gray-feathered King pigeon squabs. In addition, two representative treatments with contrasting nutrient structures were selected for untargeted fecal metabolomics to compare metabolic responses between a higher-energy lower-protein pattern and a lower-energy higher-protein pattern. This study aimed to provide a basis for optimizing dietary energy and protein levels in early-weaned gray-feathered King pigeon squabs and to characterize representative fecal metabolic responses under contrasting dietary energy-protein balance patterns.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval statement

The animal study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee for Laboratory Animals of the Institute of Animal Science, Xinjiang Academy of Animal Husbandry Sciences (Approval No. 202601).

Experimental design

A total of 216 healthy 15-d-old gray-feathered King pigeon squabs with similar body weight were used in a 3 × 3 factorial experiment. The experimental factors were three dietary metabolizable energy (ME) levels (11.9, 12.2, and 12.5 MJ/kg) and three dietary crude protein (CP) levels (18, 19, and 20%). These levels were established on the basis of published pigeon and monogastric/poultry nutrition studies and preliminary trials conducted by our group so as to cover a practical range of nutritional treatments for early-weaned squabs (Liu et al., 2025; Omar et al., 2017; Peng et al., 2023a,b; Sales and Janssens, 2003; Yu et al., 2024). The birds were randomly allocated to nine treatment groups, with eight replicates per treatment and three squabs per replicate. The trial lasted 28 d. Experimental squabs were provided by Moyu County Lanhai Pigeon Industry Development Co., Ltd., and the breed was American gray-feathered King pigeon.

Feeding and routine management

The experiment was conducted from July to September 2025 at Moyu County Lanhai Pigeon Industry Development Co., Ltd., Hotan, Xinjiang, China. Before the trial, the pigeon house was thoroughly cleaned and disinfected, and the squabs were vaccinated according to the farm immunization program. During the trial, house temperature ranged from 20 to 34°C, with an average of 29 to 31°C, and relative humidity ranged from 46 to 62%, with an average of 56 to 63%. The birds were housed in the middle tier of vertical cages, with three squabs per cage (45 × 50 × 60 cm). Throughout the trial, the squabs had free access to feed and water. A complete pelleted diet was provided. Manual feeding began daily at 1000 h, and feed withdrawal started at 2100 h. Feeders were checked four times daily to ensure that some feed remained before final feed removal. Water cups were examined daily and cleaned as needed to maintain water quality. Artificial lighting duration was adjusted according to bird age. Natural ventilation was combined with positive-pressure ventilation, and the management schedule followed routine farm practice. Growth performance

Body weight (BW) was measured for all squabs at 15, 22, 29, 36, and 43 days of age. From 16 to 43 days of age, feed intake was recorded weekly for each replicate to calculate average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G).

Body measurements

At 15 and 43 d of age, breast width, breast depth, tibial length, keel length, and body length were measured. Breast width was defined as the distance between the two shoulder joints and measured with a Vernier caliper. Breast depth was measured on the body surface as the distance from the first thoracic vertebra to the anterior margin of the keel using a Vernier caliper. Tibial length was measured with a Vernier caliper as the straight-line distance from the upper tibial joint to the space between the third and fourth toes. Keel length was measured along the body surface from the anterior edge of the keel to the tip of the keel using a tape measure. Body length was measured along the body surface from the shoulder joint to the ischial tuberosity using a tape measure. All measurements followed the Terminology and Measurement Calculation Methods of Poultry Production Performance (Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs of the People's Republic of China, 2020).

Serum sample collection and determination of biochemical, immune, and antioxidant parameters

After a 12-h fast, with free access to water, one squab was randomly selected from each replicate at 43 d of age before morning feeding (9 treatments, n = 8 per treatment). Approximately 2 mL of blood was collected from the wing vein into vacuum tubes without anticoagulant (red-cap tubes). Samples were allowed to clot upright at room temperature (20–25°C) for 30 to 60 min and were then centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. The serum supernatant was collected, aliquoted into sterile centrifuge tubes, and stored at −20°C until analysis.

Serum biochemical variables included total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), uric acid (UA), glucose (GLU), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and creatinine (CREA), which were measured using commercial biochemical assay kits (Jiangsu Aidisheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Yancheng, Jiangsu, China). Serum immune variables included immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin M (IgM), interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and complement C3 (C3), which were measured using ELISA kits (Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd., Yancheng, Jiangsu, China). Antioxidant variables included total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activity, and malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration, which were measured using the corresponding commercial kits according to the manufacturers' instructions (Jiangsu Aidisheng Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Yancheng, Jiangsu, China).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Based on the preliminary body-weight records available at the time of sample selection and the differences in dietary nutrient structure among treatments, two representative treatments (18% CP + 12.5 MJ/kg ME and 20% CP + 11.9 MJ/kg ME; n = 8 per group) were selected for untargeted fecal metabolomics analysis. The 18% CP + 12.5 MJ/kg ME treatment showed a body-weight advantage during the preliminary growth monitoring period, whereas the 20% CP + 11.9 MJ/kg ME treatment represented a contrasting lower-energy higher-protein dietary pattern. Therefore, these two groups were selected to compare fecal metabolic responses between a higher-energy lower-protein pattern and a lower-energy higher-protein pattern. This selection was intended to characterize representative metabolic differences between contrasting nutrient structures rather than to cover all nine dietary combinations.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were preliminarily organized in WPS Excel 2025 and then analyzed using SPSS 27.0. A two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) based on the general linear model (GLM) was used to evaluate the main effects of dietary ME level and CP level and their interaction. When a main effect or interaction was significant, means were separated using Duncan's multiple range test, which was selected in the original statistical plan for multiple comparisons among dietary nutrient levels and treatment combinations. For factorial tables, superscript letters for treatment-combination means were retained only when the ME × CP interaction was significant, whereas superscript letters for ME or CP main-effect means were retained only when the corresponding main effect was significant. Each replicate served as the experimental unit. Results are presented as means ± standard error of the mean (SEM), and P < 0.05 was considered significant. For untargeted metabolomics, transformed data were subjected to principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and model stability was evaluated by permutation testing. Student's t-test was used for univariate analysis of metabolite differences between the two groups, and fold change (FC) values were calculated. Differential metabolites were defined as those with variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1.0, P < 0.05, and FC > 1.5 or FC < 0.667. Differential metabolites were further subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis, and P < 0.05 was considered significant enrichment. Six key differential metabolites were selected from each of the ESI− and ESI+ modes, yielding 12 metabolites in total, and Spearman correlation analysis was performed between these metabolites and 12 serum phenotypic variables. Correlation heatmaps were generated using Origin.

Dietary ME and CP had no significant interaction effects on chest width, chest depth, tibial length, or keel length (P > 0.05). Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Quality Control and Metabolite Annotation Overview. Quality control (QC) results indicated good analytical stability and reproducibility in this untargeted metabolomics analysis. A total of 2,908 metabolic features were retained in negative-ion mode and 3,141 in positive-ion mode. In negative-ion mode, the median coefficient of variation (CV) of QC samples was 6.97%; 86.80% of features had CV values no greater than 20%, and 96.39% had CV values no greater than 30%. In positive-ion mode, the median CV was 6.89%; 87.90% of features had CV values no greater than 20%, and 97.07% had CV values no greater than 30%. In addition, Pearson correlation coefficients among QC samples ranged from 0.994 to 0.995 in negative-ion mode and from 0.993 to 0.994 in positive-ion mode, indicating high consistency of the analytical data and supporting subsequent analyses.

Metabolite annotation yielded 1,432 Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) entries, 610 Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) entries, and 409 LIPID MAPS entries in negative-ion mode, and 1,585 HMDB entries, 805 KEGG entries, and 400 LIPID MAPS entries in positive-ion mode. Primary-class annotation showed that metabolites in both ion modes were dominated by lipids and lipid-like molecules. In negative-ion mode, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic heterocyclic compounds, and organic acids and their derivatives accounted for 29.88, 17.02, and 16.85%, respectively. In positive-ion mode, the corresponding proportions were 31.39, 18.75, and 19.20%, respectively. Overall, annotated metabolites in both ion modes were mainly lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic acids and their derivatives, and organic heterocyclic compounds, indicating that the subsequent between-group differences were primarily superimposed on these metabolic backgrounds.

Multivariate Statistical Analysis. PLS-DA score plots (Fig. 1A and B) showed clear separation between the 18CP-12.5ME and 20CP-11.9ME groups in both negative- and positive-ion modes. In negative-ion mode, the first principal component (PC1) and second principal component (PC2) explained 20.59 and 27.66% of the variance, respectively, and the model goodness-of-fit (R2Y) and predictive ability (Q2) values were 0.99 and 0.87. In positive-ion mode, PC1 and PC2 explained 20.64 and 11.89% of the variance, respectively, and the corresponding R2Y and Q2 values were 0.99 and 0.92. These results indicate clear differences in fecal metabolic profiles between the two dietary energy-protein combinations, with slightly better predictive performance in positive-ion mode. Permutation testing (Fig. 1C and D) further supported model reliability. The R2 and Q2 intercepts were 0.76 and −1.01 in negative-ion mode and 0.84 and −0.85 in positive-ion mode, respectively. Primary-class annotation of differential metabolites further showed that between-group differences were still dominated by lipids and lipid-like molecules. In negative-ion mode, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic heterocyclic compounds, and organic acids and their derivatives accounted for 32.48, 15.47, and 14.96%, respectively. Among representative differential metabolites, the FC direction showed that, relative to the 20CP-11.9ME group, the 18CP-12.5ME group had significantly lower abundances of lysophosphatidylcholine [LysoPC(20:2)], 17β-estradiol 3-glucuronide (17β-E2-3-G), citric acid, and isocitrate in ESI− mode. Among them, LysoPC(20:2) showed the greatest decrease, with an FC of 0.1299, a VIP value of 2.2655, and a P-value of 6.74 × 10^−9. In contrast, l-ornithine and pimelic acid were significantly increased, with FC values of 1.8890 and 1.8378, respectively. In ESI+ mode, sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), ceramide [Cer(d18:1/12:0)], and biliverdin were significantly decreased in the 18CP-12.5ME group. By contrast, sphingomyelin [SM(d18:1/12:0)], thiamine, and 5-amino-6-ribitylaminouracil (5-amino-6-RU) were significantly increased, and 5-amino-6-RU showed an FC of 4.5106 and a P-value of 1.75 × 10^−4. Overall, these key differential metabolites were mainly distributed among lipid signaling molecules, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle intermediates, steroid hormone-related metabolites, and vitamin cofactor-related metabolites, indicating coordinated alterations across multiple metabolic modules rather than changes limited to a single pathway.

KEGG Pathway Enrichment Analysis. As shown in Fig. 2B, in negative-ion mode, differential metabolites were mainly enriched in steroid hormone biosynthesis, the citrate cycle, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, efferocytosis, galactose metabolism, and biotin metabolism. The P-values for steroid hormone biosynthesis, the citrate cycle, and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism were 0.0718, 0.1207, and 0.1792, respectively. Together with the key differential metabolites, these results suggest that 17β-E2-3-G was mainly associated with steroid hormone-related metabolism, whereas citric acid and isocitrate were mainly associated with the TCA cycle and central carbon metabolism. Overall, pathway enrichment signals in ESI− mode were mainly trend-based and had relatively limited statistical support.

In contrast, enrichment signals in positive-ion mode were more concentrated and better supported statistically. Significantly enriched pathways included sphingolipid metabolism, necroptosis, porphyrin metabolism, and sulfur relay system, with P-values of 0.0044, 0.0273, 0.0331, and 0.0406, respectively. Metabolite mapping showed that S1P, Cer(d18:1/12:0), and SM(d18:1/12:0) jointly pointed to sphingolipid metabolism; biliverdin was associated with porphyrin metabolism; and thiamine was associated with the sulfur relay system and cofactor metabolism. Overall, metabolic differences between the 18CP-12.5ME and 20CP-11.9ME groups were more strongly concentrated in sphingolipid metabolism, porphyrin-related metabolism, and cofactor metabolism networks under ESI+ mode.

Correlation Analysis Between Key Fecal Differential Metabolites and Serum Parameters. As shown in Fig. 2C, Spearman correlation analysis was performed between 12 key differential metabolites and 12 serum phenotypic variables. The 12 metabolites included LysoPC(20:2), 17β-E2-3-G, isocitrate, citric acid, l-ornithine, and pimelic acid in ESI− mode, and S1P, Cer(d18:1/12:0), SM(d18:1/12:0), biliverdin, thiamine, and 5-amino-6-RU in ESI+ mode. Among the 144 metabolite-phenotype pairs, 115 correlations were significant, including 60 positive and 55 negative correlations, indicating broad statistical associations between key fecal differential metabolites and serum biochemical, immune, and antioxidant variables.

Among the strongest positive correlations were GLU with 17β-E2-3-G (r = 0.8807, P = 6.69 × 10^−6), UA with S1P (r = 0.8735, P = 9.87 × 10^−6), ALB with biliverdin (r = 0.8609, P = 1.85 × 10^−5), and T-AOC with 5-amino-6-RU (r = 0.8569, P = 2.23 × 10^−5). Strong negative correlations included IgA with 17β-E2-3-G (r = −0.8647, P = 1.54 × 10^−5), C3 with Cer(d18:1/12:0) (r = −0.8353, P = 5.64 × 10^−5), C3 with 17β-E2-3-G (r = −0.8265, P = 7.92 × 10^−5), and IL-6 with 5-amino-6-RU (r = −0.7941, P = 2.39 × 10^−4).

Overall, metabolites such as LysoPC(20:2), 17β-E2-3-G, citric acid, S1P, and biliverdin were predominantly positively correlated with AST, ALB, MDA, UA, TG, GLU, and IL-6, but negatively correlated with T-AOC, GSH-Px, C3, and IgA. Pimelic acid, thiamine, 5-amino-6-RU, and some SM(d18:1/12:0) showed the opposite pattern. These results indicate that the key differential metabolites were not randomly associated with serum traits but instead formed two oppositely directed correlation patterns. It should be noted that these results are exploratory and mainly describe statistical association patterns between key fecal differential metabolites and serum variables. Some of these correlations may partly reflect coordinated variation driven by overall between-group separation; therefore, they are more suitable for identifying potential leads than for direct causal interpretation.

Discussion

Growth performance appeared to be influenced more by dietary energy-protein balance than by either ME or CP alone (Delezie et al., 2010, Son et al., 2024). Here, dietary ME significantly affected ADG, whereas dietary CP affected early BW and ADFI, suggesting that the tested nutrient levels influenced both nutrient intake and nutrient utilization. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence that dietary CP, essential amino acid intake, and dietary energy interact to affect broiler performance and protein retention efficiency, and that dietary CP and apparent metabolizable energy levels jointly influence nitrogen utilization, protein synthesis, and amino acid metabolism in young broilers (Sklan and Plavnik, 2002; Yu et al., 2024). From a nutrient-partitioning perspective, when dietary energy is insufficient relative to protein supply, a greater proportion of absorbed amino acids may be directed toward catabolism and nitrogen disposal rather than retained for tissue protein deposition. Conversely, when ME supply is adequate and CP supply is sufficient but not excessive, amino acids may be used more efficiently for growth and maintenance. The numerical growth advantage observed in the 20% CP + 12.2 MJ/kg ME treatment therefore may reflect a more favorable energy-to-protein balance that supported amino acid utilization and body-weight gain. However, because the ME × CP interaction for BW, ADG, and F/G was not significant, this response should be interpreted as a favorable growth trend rather than definitive treatment superiority. The absence of a significant effect on F/G further indicates that the tested ME and CP levels influenced feed intake and weight gain more strongly than feed conversion efficiency.

Body measurements provide additional phenotypic information on structural development and body conformation, and linear body measurements have been used to describe body size and its relationship with body weight and carcass traits in poultry (Assan et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2022). In this study, dietary CP affected keel length and body length, whereas dietary ME had limited effects on body-size traits. Because the body-measurement response was not uniformly improved by increasing CP, this result should be interpreted as a trait-specific phenotypic response rather than direct evidence that higher protein universally promoted structural growth. Mechanistically, this response is consistent with the broader protein-energy interpretation described above: structural tissue accretion requires both amino acid availability and sufficient energy for efficient nutrient utilization. Thus, the observed body-measurement responses support the concept that appropriate ME-to-CP matching is more important than increasing either nutrient independently.

The serum biochemical responses further indicate that dietary ME and CP altered protein, nitrogen, glucose, and lipid metabolism. Increasing dietary CP increased UA, CREA, TG, TC, ALT, and AST, especially under lower ME conditions, suggesting greater nitrogen disposal and metabolic load. This interpretation is supported by evidence that dietary protein level can affect uric acid metabolism, creatinine, urea nitrogen, renal function, and inflammatory responses in young poultry, and that CP and apparent metabolizable energy levels jointly influence nitrogen utilization and amino acid metabolism (Chen et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2024). In the present study, the concurrent increases in UA and CREA under higher CP conditions suggest that excess amino acids may have been deaminated and converted into nitrogenous end products when protein supply exceeded the capacity for efficient tissue deposition. The accompanying increases in ALT and AST, particularly under lower ME conditions, may therefore reflect a broader metabolic burden associated with protein catabolism rather than isolated changes in liver-related indicators. In contrast, greater ME availability may reduce reliance on amino acid catabolism and favor the use of dietary protein for growth and maintenance.

The immune-related responses also appear to be linked to dietary energy-protein balance through both systemic nutrient supply and intestinal responses to luminal nutrients (Mi et al., 2024, Zhou et al., 2021). Previous poultry studies have shown that dietary ME and CP restriction can affect serum immunoglobulins and immune-related gene expression, and that dietary CP levels can alter intestinal antioxidant status, tight-junction protein expression, and amino acid transporter levels in squab pigeons (Cheng et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). In the present study, increasing dietary ME was generally associated with higher IL-2, C3, IgA, IgG, and IgM and lower IL-6, whereas increasing CP tended to increase IL-6 and reduce several immune-related variables. This pattern suggests that adequate energy supply may support immune protein synthesis and reduce inflammatory pressure, whereas excessive CP, particularly when not matched by sufficient energy, may increase undigested nitrogenous substrates, intestinal metabolic burden, and inflammatory signaling. From the perspective of intestinal nutrient sensing, shifts in luminal nutrient availability may influence gut barrier function, microbial activity, amino acid transport, and immune signaling, thereby linking dietary ME-to-CP balance with systemic immune status (Cheng et al., 2024; Jha et al., 2026). The significant ME × CP interactions for C3 and IgM further support the view that immune responses in early-weaned squabs are sensitive to the balance between dietary energy and protein supply.

The antioxidant results can be interpreted through the combined effects of dietary nutrient supply, metabolic load, and oxidative metabolism. Antioxidant defense in poultry is nutritionally modulated, and nutritional challenges can disturb the balance between reactive oxygen species production and antioxidant defense capacity (Bacou et al., 2021; Surai et al., 2019). In the present study, increasing dietary ME improved T-AOC, SOD, and GSH-Px and reduced MDA, suggesting lower lipid peroxidation and better antioxidant defense within the tested ME range. In contrast, the 20% CP level was associated with lower antioxidant enzyme activities and higher MDA than the 18% CP level, indicating that excessive protein supply may increase oxidative pressure. A possible explanation is that greater amino acid catabolism and nitrogen metabolism increase mitochondrial substrate flux and reactive oxygen species generation, especially when additional protein is not efficiently retained for tissue deposition. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that dietary energy and amino acid supply can influence antioxidant capacity in broilers (Mao et al., 2024). Adequate ME may reduce the need for amino acids to be oxidized for energy, whereas excessive CP may increase oxidative metabolic burden and weaken antioxidant homeostasis. These findings indicate that antioxidant status is regulated jointly by energy availability, protein supply, amino acid catabolism, and metabolic burden.

Fecal metabolomics provided further evidence that contrasting dietary energy-protein patterns were associated with different metabolic outputs. The 18CP-12.5ME and 20CP-11.9ME groups were clearly separated in both ion modes, and the differences were mainly concentrated in lipid-related metabolites, central carbon metabolism, and cofactor-related metabolism. The lower abundances of S1P, Cer(d18:1/12:0), LysoPC(20:2), and biliverdin in the 18CP-12.5ME group, together with changes in SM(d18:1/12:0), suggest coordinated remodeling of lipid signaling and membrane-related metabolic pathways. These pathways are closely related to inflammatory signaling, immune-cell activity, and oxidative homeostasis, and therefore may help connect dietary nutrient structure with the serum immune and antioxidant responses observed in this study. This interpretation is supported by evidence that S1P participates in immune-cell regulation and inflammation resolution, whereas bilirubin-related tetrapyrrole metabolism may act as a metabolic signaling pathway (Stec et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024).

Differences in citric acid, isocitrate, l-ornithine, thiamine, and 5-amino-6-RU suggest that the two selected dietary patterns also differed in central carbon metabolism, nitrogen-related metabolism, and cofactor metabolism. Lower-energy higher-protein feeding may increase reliance on amino acid catabolism and nitrogen disposal, whereas higher-energy lower-protein feeding may shift fecal metabolic output toward a different balance of lipid signaling, microbial cofactor-related metabolites, and energy metabolism. The increase in 5-amino-6-RU, a metabolite associated with microbial riboflavin biosynthesis, also suggests that dietary nutrient structure may influence gut-derived metabolic signals that interact with mucosal immunity. These findings support a nutrition-gut-host interaction framework rather than a single-pathway explanation. The link between riboflavin-biosynthesis-related metabolites and mucosal immunity is consistent with evidence that endogenous riboflavin biosynthesis pathways can modulate host-microbe interactions and mucosal immune responses (Thakur et al., 2024).

Correlation analysis between key fecal metabolites and serum variables showed two opposite association patterns, linking lipid-related metabolites and cofactor-related metabolites with serum markers of metabolism, immunity, and oxidative status. These correlations suggest coordinated variation between fecal metabolic output and host physiological responses. However, they should not be interpreted as evidence of direct causality. Because only two selected treatments were included in the metabolomics analysis, part of the correlation structure may reflect overall between-group separation. Thus, these results are best viewed as exploratory leads for identifying metabolic pathways that may be involved in dietary energy-protein responses.

This study has several limitations. First, untargeted fecal metabolomics was performed only on two selected representative treatments with contrasting dietary energy-protein structures; therefore, these metabolomic findings cannot fully represent all nine dietary combinations or directly explain the growth response observed in the 20% CP + 12.2 MJ/kg ME treatment. Second, fecal metabolites represent intestinal metabolic outputs and cannot fully reflect tissue-specific metabolism in the liver, muscle, or intestinal mucosa. Third, the metabolomics results were based on untargeted LC-MS annotation and exploratory correlation analysis (Alseekh et al., 2021, Amara et al., 2022, Di Minno et al., 2022); therefore, future studies should include targeted metabolite validation, additional dietary combinations, and functional analyses of intestinal nutrient sensing and host metabolic pathways.

Conclusions

In summary, dietary metabolizable energy and crude protein levels jointly affected growth performance, selected body measurements, and serum biochemical, immune, and antioxidant variables in early-weaned gray-feathered King pigeon squabs, and significant interaction effects were observed for some variables. In the overall phenotypic evaluation, responses to dietary energy-protein combinations were trait-specific, and the 20% CP + 12.2 MJ/kg ME treatment showed a favorable growth trend. Untargeted fecal metabolomics of two selected representative treatments showed clear separation between the 18% CP + 12.5 MJ/kg ME group and the 20% CP + 11.9 MJ/kg ME group, with differences mainly involving lipid-related metabolism, central carbon metabolism, porphyrin-related metabolism, and cofactor metabolism. These metabolomics results should be interpreted as representative metabolic differences between the two selected dietary patterns rather than as a direct explanation for all nine dietary combinations or for the 20% CP + 12.2 MJ/kg ME treatment. Overall, this study provides a basis for optimizing dietary energy and protein levels in early-weaned gray-feathered King pigeon squabs and for further investigation of the metabolic mechanisms underlying nutritional regulation.
