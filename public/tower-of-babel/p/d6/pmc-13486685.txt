Self-heating-induced blocking in nanopores enables neuromorphic ionic computing
Authors: Qinyang Fan, Changhui Xu, Wei Liu, Zhenyu Zhang, Yunfei Chen, Jingjie Sha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInspired by the brain’s efficient ionic processing, fluidic memristors using ions as charge carriers have emerged as a promising platform for future neuromorphic systems. To better replicate the brain’s dynamic functions, it’s essential to explore additional memristive materials and switching mechanisms. In this work, we present a self-heating-induced blocking memristor (SIBM), in which the memory effect arises from thermally triggered reversible precipitation. When subjected to periodic voltage stimulation, SIBM shows threshold-type unipolar resistive switching and features a clear negative differential resistance (NDR). We demonstrate various neuromorphic functions and experimentally validate a fluidic memristor array capable of repeated memory operations. These results provide insights for the design of next-generation neuromorphic fluidic devices.Subject terms: Nanofluidics, Electrical and electronic engineering, Nanopores

Introduction

The development of hardware with biological similarity is of great significance for the realization of neuromorphic computing architectures. Memristors are recognized as one of the most promising candidate devices for enabling neuromorphic computing1–4. However, the majority of existing memristors are solid-state devices that rely on electrons or holes as primary charge carriers, and their operational mechanisms differ fundamentally from those of biological systems based on ions and small molecules5–8. This difference has driven researchers’ attention to an emerging field: fluidic memristors9–12.

Fluidic memristors expand the neuromorphic concept from solid-state to fluidic systems by controlling internal ionic dynamics to match the dynamic behaviors of biological neural systems. So far, many fluidic memristors have been proposed, including ion concentration polarization13,14, mechanical deformation7, and electrochemical reactions15,16. Despite the prospective applications, current fluidic memristors still present challenges, including complex fabrication processes and limited diversity in memristive dynamics. These issues significantly hinder their application in neuromorphic computing17–19.

Exploring additional memristive mechanisms may be beneficial. In conventional integrated circuits, localized heating is generally regarded as an unnecessary and even harmful side effect20. However, in biological neural systems, thermal signals are closely linked to essential life processes21. They significantly affect neuronal functions, including ion channel activation, action potential conduction speed, and firing patterns22–24. Therefore, accounting for temperature effects in fluidic systems allows for more precise simulation of living organisms’ physiological states. This not only provides an alternative approach for emulating the dynamic behavior of biological neurons, but also offers a method for creating neuromorphic systems with bio-similar temperature regulation25,26. As far as we know, the self-heating effects in fluidic systems have been rarely mentioned in the field of neuromorphic computing.

In this study, we report a self-heating-induced blocking memristor (SIBM). In contrast to strategies that rely on asymmetric structural design16,27–29, the device integrates localized Joule heating within nanopores with the phase transition of a salt-water system, thereby exhibiting distinctive resistive‑switching (RS) dynamics. Furthermore, the device’s simplified structure improves the system’s scalability. By implementing the developed memristor, we demonstrate a range of neuromorphic functions, including paired-pulse depression (PPD), peak-rate-dependent plasticity (SRDP), memory forgetting, and chemical synapse function. Concurrently, we experimentally show a nanofluidic array capable of repeated memory operations. This work presents an intrinsic physical mechanism for creating advanced nanofluidic memristors and opens promising avenues for developing next-generation biomimetic intelligent systems.

Results

The resistive switching mechanism of SIBM

Our experimental setup is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1a, where the nanopore chip is sandwiched between two reservoirs filled with a mixed solution of Ce2(SO4)3 and KCl, and Ag/AgCl electrodes are inserted into the respective reservoirs. (Unless otherwise specified, the KCl concentration is 3 M, the Ce2(SO4)3 concentration is 3 mM, and the pH value is 4.8). Nanopores with diameters of 300–400 nm were fabricated on a 20–nm-thick suspended silicon nitride membrane using focused ion beam technology. The detailed fabrication procedure of the nanopore chips is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1b.

Figure 1a illustrates the RS process of SIBM. Owing to the notable difference in cross-sectional area between the reservoir and the nanopores, the potential drop becomes concentrated within the nanopores as the voltage increases, thereby forming localized hotspots30. Ce₂(SO₄)₃ is an inorganic salt with retrograde solubility, meaning its solubility decreases as temperature rises31. Additionally, abundant ions supplied by the high concentration of KCl enhance the local Joule heating. Thus, at high voltages, the precipitation of Ce₂(SO₄)₃ will block the pores, driving the device into a high-resistance state (HRS). When the voltage drops, a decrease in temperature will promote precipitate removal, restoring the ionic pathway and allowing the device to reset to low-resistance state (LRS).

The design of self-heating-induced blocking memristor (SIBM).

a Under high voltage, localized hot spots generated by Joule heating induce precipitation that clogs the pores, setting the device into a high-resistance state (HRS). When the voltage decreases, the temperature drops, promoting precipitate removal and restoring the ion transport pathways, thereby resetting the device to a low-resistance state (LRS). b I-V characteristics of the mixed solution (green) and 3 M KCl solution (orange) under triangular wave voltage excitation at a scan rate of 1 V/s, along with the scanning electron microscopy image of the nanopore.

Experiment results illustrate the RS behavior of SIBM as shown in Fig. 1b. During a triangular-wave voltage sweep at 1 V/s, the device exhibits a unipolar hysteresis loop with threshold switching (green trace). Upon application of a bias voltage, the current response initially increases nonlinearly until the threshold voltage is reached, at which point the device switches to HRS. As the bias is reduced, the device switches back to the LRS. In contrast, under identical experimental conditions, the current-voltage (I-V) curve in the pure 3 M KCl solution exhibits nonlinear behavior without a memory effect (orange trace).

To rule out the influence of other possible mechanisms, we conducted additional experiments to verify this concept. First, through thermocouple measurements32, we confirmed the presence of localized heating in the nanopore region (Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). Since an increase in temperature will enhance ionic conductivity33, this accounts for the nonlinear ionic current increase observed in Fig. 1b. Using a finite element model, we simulated the heating process within the pores and confirmed that the nonlinear increase in ionic current is attributed to Joule heating. (Supplementary Figs. 4–6). Although this nonlinear dynamic supports memristive behavior, the memory curve only appears under high-speed scanning conditions due to the rapid heating rate within the nanopore (Supplementary Fig. 7)34,35. Thus, the RS mechanism in SIBM cannot be attributed to the effect of Joule heating on solution conductivity. Furthermore, Joule heating may cause boiling within the nanopores, generating nanobubbles that block ionic current. However, the generation and quenching of nanobubbles have a much faster dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 8), which is different from the stable current curve obtained in the mixed solution (Fig. 1b). Therefore, the RS mechanism cannot be attributed to the translocation of nanobubbles either.

Previous studies have demonstrated that electrochemical reactions at the electrode surface can alter electrical conductivity15,36,37. To investigate this, the following electrical test was performed: a constant 5 V voltage was applied to drive the SIBM into HRS. Subsequently, the Ag/AgCl electrodes were removed entirely from the reservoirs, and new electrodes of identical specifications were reinserted. If the RS mechanism were rooted in the formation of a blocking layer or diffusion layer at the electrode surface, the measurement would be expected to recover to LRS. However, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, the system remained in the HRS after electrode replacement. Concurrently, energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) studies of the Ag/AgCl electrode after repeated experiments revealed that no blocking layer that affects conductivity formed on its surface (Supplementary Fig. 10), indicating that the RS mechanism cannot be ascribed to electrochemical reactions at the electrode interface.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) imaging results show that after SIBM is induced into the HRS, obvious precipitates are observed within the pores. In contrast, no significant changes in surface morphology are detected in devices that did not undergo a resistance state transition. (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). This confirms that the RS mechanism originates from the precipitation process in the solution. Further EDS results indicated that the main component of the precipitate was the crystalline Ce₂(SO₄)₃ (Supplementary Fig. 13). Importantly, since the experiments were conducted in high-concentration (>1 M) electrolyte solutions and large-diameter nanopores (>100 nm), this precipitation cannot be caused by ion redistribution induced by the electric field38. Therefore, we concluded that the precipitation formation is caused by Joule heating.

To investigate the influence of Joule heating on the dynamical properties and performance of SIBM, we tuned a series of key parameters governing Joule heating. The heating power (H) within the nanopore can be characterized by H = JE, J = σE, where E is the electric field strength, J is the current density, and σ is the electrical conductivity. The average electric field strength inside the nanopore (Ep) can be approximated by EP=V/L+π4D, where V is the bias voltage, L is the pore length, and D is the pore diameter34. First, the effect of the voltage sweep range on the I-V curves was investigated, as shown in Fig. 2a. At a sweep rate of 1 V/s and a voltage range from −3 V to 3 V, no apparent hysteresis was observed. As the scanning range was extended (−4 to 4 V and −5 to 5 V), the device exhibited a stable resistive switching threshold. This phenomenon occurs because a sufficiently high temperature is needed to reach the nucleation threshold for Ce2(SO4)3 precipitation. This threshold behavior is similar to the “all-or-nothing” response of action potentials in biological neurons, in that it can only induce a state transition in the device when the stimulus reaches or exceeds a specific threshold39,40.

Subsequently, we investigated the effect of KCl concentration on the electrical performance. A series of SIBMs with varying KCl concentrations was established. As shown in Fig. 2b, with decreasing KCl concentration, the voltage threshold shifted toward higher voltages, while the relative area of the hysteresis loop gradually diminished and completely vanished at a KCl concentration of 1 M. Furthermore, we investigated the influence of pore size on device performance. Since pore size strongly affects the Joule heating power within the nanopore, smaller pore sizes may lead to more pronounced local temperature increases. As pore size increases, both the memory effect and the nonlinear I–V behavior will attenuate and eventually disappear. (Supplementary Fig. 14), further underscoring the critical role of Joule heating in the RS process. We further examined the device performance under different electrolyte conditions. First, KCl in the mixed solution was replaced with LiCl, NaCl, and RbCl at the same concentration. The experimental results show that as the solution conductivity increases, the switching threshold voltage gradually decreases (Supplementary Fig. 15). Additionally, other inorganic salts with retrograde solubility (e.g., Ca(OH)₂ and Li₂CO₃) were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 2c, their mixed solutions also exhibited apparent memory effects (Supplementary Figs. 16, 17), thereby confirming that the resistive switching mechanism originates from Joule heat-induced precipitation blocking.

We further investigated the mechanism of SIBM conductance recovery. By applying a set voltage (V = 5 V, W = 0.5 s) to drive the device into HRS, and then applying reset voltages of different amplitudes (W = 3 s) and monitoring the conductance evolution. The experimental results show that subthreshold voltage can effectively promote conductance recovery, and the recovery rate increases with the magnitude of the reset voltage (Fig. 2d). Notably, this effect is sensitive only to voltage magnitude, and voltage polarity exerts no significant influence. This phenomenon may relate to electroosmotic flow within the nanopore or electrophoresis of the precipitates. The memory effect of SIBM has also been demonstrated to correlate with the scanning rate. Generally, a larger hysteresis loop area indicates a more substantial memory effect. The I–V scanning hysteresis loops at different scan rates are shown (Fig. 2e). When the scan rate increases to 50 V/s, the I–V curve no longer forms a hysteresis loop, indicating that the memristive effect disappeared. This frequency-dependent resistance switching aligns well with Chua’s memristor definition35. This trend can be attributed to higher scan rates reducing the duration the voltage exceeds the threshold, thereby limiting the heating time required for complete precipitate formation. Additionally, the device underwent over 60 consecutive switching cycles, and the SIBM showed good consistency (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 18). Furthermore, we observed that the response speed of SIBM is correlated with its stimulation history. To investigate this, we designed a training protocol that used continuous pulses to induce precipitate formation and clearance within the nanopore. The results show that the response time gradually shortens as training progresses, eventually reaching about 12 ms in a well‑trained device (Supplementary Fig. 19). We also measured the retention time of SIBM under different SET voltages. The experimental results indicate that the retention time increases with the duration of the input voltage, reaching approximately 1500 s at maximum (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Negative differential resistance in SIBM

Because the SIBM operation is based on thermally induced blocking, the resistance state transition occurs in the same manner under both positive and negative biases. The difference in the dominant mechanism at different voltage magnitudes leads to hysteresis. The dynamic behavior of ionic current and conductance (Fig. 3b–d) can be explained by following the applied voltage in stages 1 → 4, as indicated (Fig. 3a). (1) When the bias voltage is relatively low, the nanopore’s conductance remains in the initial state. (2) As the bias voltage increases, the Joule heating effect gradually becomes apparent, affecting solution conductivity and causing the current to exhibit a nonlinear rise. (3) As the voltage continues to increase, the solute precipitates rapidly and blocks the nanopores, causing the device to switch to HRS. (4) During the voltage sweep-back, as the temperature inside the pore decreases, the precipitation reaction weakens, and the clearance effect begins to dominate; consequently, the formed precipitates are removed, and the conductance recovers to its initial state.

Negative differential resistance (NDR) in SIBM.

a Four stages of SIBM under periodic voltage scanning. b I–V characteristics (green) with a magnified inset (yellow). Two NDR regions appear in stages 3 and 4. c I-t (green) and V-t (orange) curves of SIBM. d Conductance change image during the scanning process. The sweep rate for all scans is 1 V/s.

Notably, the current curve exhibits two NDR (dIdV<0) regions at Stages 3 and 4. This temperature-induced RS dynamic is reminiscent of Mott memristors41–43. Previous studies have confirmed that devices with NDR exhibit local activity, which underlies action potential generation, self-oscillation, and chaotic behavior44–46. This indicates that SIBM has potential application value in the field of bionic neurons.

Neuromorphic functions based on SIBM

Living organisms are constantly exposed to dynamic environmental conditions and thus require various learning behaviors47,48. Synaptic plasticity in biological systems is widely regarded as the fundamental mechanism underlying learning and memory (Fig. 4a). Notably, SIBM’s dynamic modulation capability provides a viable platform for emulating a range of neuromorphic functionalities in fluidic environments.

Neuromorphic functions of SIBM.

a Schematic illustration of biological neurons and synapses. b Paired-pulse depression (PPD) index changes with different intervals of time (Δt) and interval voltage (Vi) of SIBM. c Peak values of the response current under pulsed voltages (V = 5 V, 50 ms) with inter-pulse intervals of 0.1 s (green) and 1 s (orange). d Emulation of biological memory and forgetting processes: alternating memory (orange) and forgetting (green) pulses (upper panel) enable repeated modulation of the ionic current in SIBM (lower panel). e Bidirectional pulse voltages (upper panel) and corresponding ionic currents (lower panel). f Ionic current response of the associative learning experiment.

We first emulated the PPD phenomenon, defined as the second stimulus amplitude having a lower response magnitude than the first. An interval voltage (V = Vi, W =∆ti) is applied between two voltage pulses (V = 5 V, W = 0.1 s) for the paired-pulse response test of SIBM. PPD index is defined as (A1–A2)/A1, where A is the current amplitude and the subscript indicates the pulse number. As shown in Fig. 4b, the PPD index reduces with the growing ∆ti, exhibiting a time-dependent plasticity profile. Additionally, with a fixed interval time, increasing the magnitude of Vi results in a smaller PPD index. It should be noted that this ability to regulate memory time plays a crucial role in achieving functions such as time filtering, sensory adaptation, and short-term information maintenance in biological neural circuits.

In addition, SRDP was observed in the SIBM device. Pulse voltages (V = 5 V, W = 50 ms) with an interval time (Δt) were applied. We studied the current change with various Δt, as shown in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 21. The ionic current remained constant when Δt exceeded 1 s, but decreased at higher frequencies due to rapid precipitate formation. This phenomenon demonstrates that the SIBM possesses frequency-selective response capability, which is consistent with the information processing characteristics of biological synapses48.

One of the brain’s distinctive features is its capacity to forget experiences49, and the SIBM’s repeated modulation of ionic conductance mimics biological processes of memory and forgetting. As shown in Fig. 4d, a sudden drop in ionic current was observed under the memory voltage (V = 5 V, W = 0.1 s). In contrast, the current gradually increased before plateauing under the forgetting voltage (V = −2.5 V, W = 1 s). Crucially, the device maintained performance even after over 190 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 22). While low-magnitude voltage facilitates the forgetting process, the device exhibits a continuous inhibitory effect under bidirectional voltage-pulse stimulation exceeding the threshold, as shown in Fig. 4e. Under bidirectional voltage pulses (V = ± 5 V, W = 50 ms), after the SIBM device was induced into HRS, the conductance remained persistently, similar to the mutual inhibition phenomenon in bio-synapses50.

Figure 4f shows an associative learning experiment. Before training, the current induced by the bell pulse (V = 0.5 V, W = 0.1 s) exceeded the memory threshold, indicating a non-responsive state. Subsequently, food pulse (V = 5 V, W = 0.1 s) was applied, and a significant decrease in current was observed. Subsequently, the food and bell pulses were alternately delivered. After training, the bell pulse alone was sufficient to elicit a current response below threshold, demonstrating that it successfully recapitulated the associative learning process. These findings indicate the SIBM holds broad application potential in bio-inspired neuromorphic systems.

Synapses are categorized into two primary types based on their transmission mechanisms: electrical synapses and chemical synapses. In the mature human nervous system, chemical synapses are predominant. In these synapses, neurotransmitters released from the presynaptic membrane cross the synaptic cleft and bind to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, thereby inducing changes in synaptic weight. Meanwhile, neurotransmitters are cleared through mechanisms such as reuptake or enzymatic breakdown51,52, enabling the postsynaptic neuron to continuously receive new signals (Fig. 5a).

Chemical synaptic device based on SIBM.

a Schematic diagram of chemical synaptic signal transmission. b Optical image and c structural schematic of the chemical synaptic device. d Schematic illustration of chemical stimulation controlling the switching of resistance state. e Ionic current response of the SIBM under chemical stimulation. The green arrow represents the chemical stimulus. f Ionic current response under continuous chemical stimulation.

Inspired by biological neurons and leveraging the chemically tunable properties of SIBM, we designed a chemical synaptic device that can sense chemical stimuli and convert them into electrical signals. The device structure is illustrated in Fig. 5b, c. The upper and lower polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) channels were separated by a nanopore chip. Two Ag/AgCl electrodes were connected to Inlets 1 and 3, with a constant voltage of 5 V applied and the ionic current recorded. To simulate the clearance process of neurotransmitters53, Inlets 1 and 3 were continuously perfused with a mixed solution of 3 M KCl and Ce₂(SO₄)₃ at a low flow rate. When Inlet 2 was opened, a 3 M KCl solution with a pH of 0.5 was rapidly and briefly injected into the upper channel as an artificial neurotransmitter. All fluid injections were controlled via syringe pumps.

The detailed process and corresponding current changes are shown in Fig. 5d, e. When Inlet 2 was closed, precipitation occurred due to Joule heating, switching the device in HRS, analogous to neuronal inhibition. Upon opening Inlet 2, the pH near the nanopore changed rapidly. Since Ce₂(SO₄)₃ exhibits higher solubility in acidic solutions54, the precipitate in the pore dissolved quickly, increasing the conductance, which corresponded to the response of the postsynaptic neuron to neurotransmitters. After Inlet 2 was closed, the pH near the pore gradually recovered, causing reprecipitation and returning the neuron to an inhibited state, ready for the next stimulus. The current changes under multiple consecutive chemical stimulations are shown in Fig. 5f.

In contrast, at a constant voltage of 2 V, the current showed negligible response to chemical stimuli, indicating that the observed current variation was not caused by changes in solution conductivity (Supplementary Fig. 23). These results demonstrate the capability of SIBM to transduce chemical stimuli into electrical pulse signals and further highlight its potential for implementing chemically mediated signal modulation in neuromorphic computing systems.

Image memory based on SIBM

To recapitulate the functionalities of more complex biological neural networks, scaling up from individual nanochannels to the synergistic operation of multiple devices is imperative. We successfully created a 5×4 SIBM-based image storage array; the schematic and optical photograph are presented in Fig. 6a, c. Two PDMS membranes with parallel channels were assembled in a perpendicular cross configuration. The nanopore chips were integrated at each intersection node, and a perforated adhesive film was used to enable component bonding and electrical isolation (Fig. 6b). This architecture enables independent addressing of individual SIBM devices.

Image memory based on SIBM.

a Schematic illustration of addressable SIBM array. b Structural design of the SIBM array. c Optical photograph of the SIBM array. d Schematic of the voltage operation sequence for the SIBM array, ordered chronologically from left to right; different color-coded regions denote specific voltage operations: read (pink), write (black), hold (yellow), and erase (red). e Readout results from the SIBM array, where a color gradient from light to dark indicates different levels of memory strength.

Each pixel corresponds to an individual device unit. A voltage sequence was designed for each pixel node, as shown in Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 24. The operations in the voltage sequence include four types: write (V = 5 V, W = 1 s), erase (V = −2.5 V, W = 2 s), hold (V = 0 V, W = 2 s), and read (V = 0.5 V, W = 0.2 s). After each read operation, the state of each pixel was assessed: if the state was to remain unchanged in the next step, a hold operation was performed; if a state transition was required, a write or erase operation was performed. Subsequently, the conductance (Cm) was recorded via subsequent read pulses. The current responses at each node were recorded individually and temporally aligned (Supplementary Fig.25). Memory strength is defined as [(Cb - Cm) / Cb] × 100%, where Cb is the baseline conductance obtained from the initial reading. As shown in Fig. 6e, the device could be switched from the “S” state to the “E” state, and further to the “U” state, confirming its write, erase, and rewrite capabilities. These results highlight the potential of SIBM for integrated computing and information storage applications.

Discussion

This study reports a fluidic memristor whose memristive dynamics are co-regulated by self-heating-induced precipitation blocking and electric-field-driven clearance. This leads to a specific NDR characteristic, providing an interesting approach to expanding nonlinear behavior in fluidic systems. Additionally, owing to its independence from external heating components (e.g., lasers or micro-heating coils)55,56, it offers a compact structure and facile integration. In our case, SIBM successfully emulates diverse neuromorphic dynamic behaviors and demonstrates a reconfigurable integrated fluidic neuromorphic array that supports image storage, erasure, and rewriting.

Furthermore, compared to solid-state memristors, the development of fluidic memristors started relatively late and requires further exploration and optimization (Supplementary Table 1). To further enhance the performance of SIBM, a feasible optimization strategy is to precisely modulate nanopore structure, surface properties (e.g., surface roughness and charge distribution), and solution chemical composition to enable stable, reversible precipitation-clearance cycles within small-scale nanopores, thereby minimizing operational energy consumption. Meanwhile, the high chemical tunability inherent to the precipitation process holds promise for enabling an integrated functional platform that combines thermal, chemical, and iontronics, which could further emulate the complex dynamic behaviors of biological systems. In conclusion, this study offers a promising paradigm for thermal applications in nanopores and establishes a critical foundation for advancing neuromorphic nanofluidic devices.

Methods

Materials

KCl, NaCl, LiCl, Ca(OH)2, and Li2CO3 (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Inc.), Ce2(SO4)3·8H2O, and RbCl (Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Inc.) Deionized (DI) water (Integral 3, Milli-Q Inc., 18.2 MΩcm). All materials were used as received without further purification.

Fabrication process of nanopores

Nanopore chips were fabricated using MEMS technology. The SiNx layer was deposited via chemical vapor deposition, followed by photolithography patterning, localized membrane thinning, and silicon etching to release free-standing ultrathin membranes. The nanopores were fabricated by the focus ion beam (FIB, Helios 600i, FEI Inc.) in the free-standing SiNx membrane, with the ion beam energy set to 30 keV. Electrical measurements

The signals were measured using a CHI660E (CH Instruments Inc.) electrochemical workstation. The nanopore chip was cleaned in piranha solution (H₂SO₄:H₂O₂ = 1:3) at 120 °C for 30 min to remove organic contaminants, then sequentially rinsed with ethanol and DI water, and dried with nitrogen gas. The cleaned chip was then sandwiched between two polymethyl methacrylate reservoirs, sealed with a silicone elastomer gasket. Ag/AgCl electrodes were polished to remove surface oxides, immersed in 5 wt% NaClO for 30 min to form an AgCl layer, and rinsed thoroughly with DI water. In the thermocouple experiments, the thermoelectric voltage was measured using a source meter (Keithley 2612 A, Keithley Inc.).

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Helios 600i, FEI Inc.) and atomic force microscopy (AFM, Cypher ES, Oxford Instruments Inc.) were used to characterize the surface morphology of the nanopores and precipitates. Energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, INCA X-act, Oxford Instruments Inc.) was used to analyze the Ag/AgCl electrode and precipitates for elemental composition.

Fabrication of nanopore arrays

First, for the preparation of PDMS membranes integrated with microchannels, Sylgard 184 silicone elastomer (Dow Inc.) was cast into a mold fabricated via 3D printing, then cured statically until complete solidification. To ensure robust adhesion and electrical insulation, the nanopore chip was sandwiched between two layers of 0.1-mm-thick polyethylene terephthalate (PET) double-sided adhesive films. At each node site, 1-mm-diameter through-holes were ablated on the PET films using laser processing to ensure an effective connection between the nanopore area and the solution environment. Finally, the components were bonded in sequence, the solution was injected into the microchannel, Ag/AgCl electrodes were inserted, and the microchannel openings were sealed with PDMS to finalize the integrated device encapsulation.

Fabrication of chemical synaptic device

First, the polyimide (PI) adhesive film and the upper and lower channels were fabricated via laser processing. A through-hole was reserved at the center of the lower channel to interconnect the upper and lower channels. The lower channel was bonded and sealed to the glass substrate using plasma cleaning. Next, the nanopore chip is sandwiched between two layers of PI adhesive film, with through-holes in the adhesive film that allow the nanopore area to effectively connect to the liquid environment. Then, the various components are stacked sequentially to complete the assembly.
