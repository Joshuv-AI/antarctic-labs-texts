Bacterial fecal microbiome composition associated with HIV stage among rural and peri-urban adults living with HIV in Uganda
Authors: Bożena M. Morawski, Elise R. Morton, Miya Yunus, Claudia Muñoz-Zanzi, Rojelio Mejia, Ran Blekhman, David R. Boulware, Sarah M. Lofgren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGut microbiome composition in HIV-infection provides insight into HIV pathogenesis and potentially offers avenues to adjunctive HIV therapies. Although two-thirds of people living with HIV (PLHIV) are in Africa, studies of the gut microbiome PLHIV have focused on non-African populations. Additionally, social, cultural, and environmental factors influence the gut microbiome and differ substantially between African and Western and urban and rural communities. Here, we quantify the relationships between immune status and fecal microbiome composition in 175 rural Ugandan PLHIV.MethodsIn 2013, 175 Ugandan PLHIV contributed a single fecal sample as part of a cohort study of 202 participants. We measured CD4 + T cells at baseline and followed the individuals longitudinally via chart review for 12 months. Fecal samples were assessed for parasitic infection, and standard 16S bacterial rRNA sequencing determined microbiome composition. We assessed alpha and beta diversity by clinical factors, e.g., ART duration, CD4 + T cells/µL, parasitic infection, and differential relative abundances via non-parametric tests and adjusted regression. We assessed the relationship between taxa and longitudinal change in CD4 + cells/µL via linear mixed models.ResultsThis analysis of microbiome data among 175 participants found that alpha diversity was lower in participants with < 100 CD4 + T cells/µL versus their peers with higher CD4 + counts. Only the presence of Anaerococcus was inversely associated with CD4 + T cells/µL (Spearman correlation − 0.3207; p-value = 0.0000152). We observed no trends in beta diversity across clinical or demographic groups. Presence of Sutterella and Alcaligenaceae at baseline were associated with increasing CD4 + T cells/µL over time.ConclusionsWe identified three taxa associated with clinical parameters, and, importantly, that the fecal microbiome is less diverse at lower CD4 + T cells counts, which is concerning for relatively increased dysbiosis and worse outcomes. Given the large prevalence of HIV in Sub-Saharan Africa, key differences in factors that influence microbiome, and the apparent importance of the microbiome in health status, future microbiome research focused on diverse and rural African populations is warranted.Clinical trialNot applicable. This was not a clinical trial as no intervention was applied to the participant. Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12992-6.

Background

According to UNAIDS, there were 40.8 million people living with HIV (PLHIV) in 2024, of which 21.1 million – or 1 in 2 – live in eastern and southern Africa [1]. HIV infection is characterized by a loss of CD4 + T cells and progressive immunodeficiency [2], much of which is initiated by changes to the gut [3]. Although the gut microbiota are normally protected from the host immune system by a strong mucosal barrier, HIV infection damages the integrity of this mucosal lining [4]. The initial and ongoing viral replication found in HIV infection cause progressive damage to the gut mucosa [4] and dysbiosis of bacterial communities of the gut [5, 6]. Further, HIV increases immune activation and decreases the CD4 + count in the mucosa [7]. A model of HIV using simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV)-infected rhesus macaques found that SIV infection resulted in structural changes to the gut mucosa, including discontinuities in the epithelial layer and microbial translocation in the lamina propria [8]. PLHIV also have lower relative abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria in the gut, which is associated with translocation of microbes and immune activation [9]. While HIV medications enable immune reconstitution, the fecal microbiome in PLHIV is permanently altered [10].

Gut bacterial species diversity present within an individual, commonly referred to as alpha diversity, has generally – although not uniformly [11] – been found to be lower in PLHIV when compared with HIV-uninfected peers [12, 13]. Alpha diversity does rebound in PLHIV who start antiretroviral therapy (ART) [14, 15]. However, the fecal flora of PLHIV remains altered, characterized by an overgrowth of Bacterioides uniformis [16] and Prevotella copri, which are linked to inflammatory conditions [17]. Dillon et al.. demonstrated lower ratios of Bacterioides spp. to Prevotella spp. in PLHIV versus those who were HIV-uninfected [18]. Gori et al. observed a higher abundance of potential pathogens Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Candida albicans, and lower abundances of “healthy” Bifidobacteria and Lactobacilli species in PLHIV relative to historical HIV-uninfected controls [19]. More recently, Dolo et al.. document that gut dysbiosis and related metabolic changes exist prior to ART initiation and, although they improve with the initiation of ART, persist at least 12-months post-ART initiation [20].

Recent research has shown the importance of social and geographical context interpreting analysis of the gut microbiome [21, 22]. In a study from Kazakhstan, people from urban regions consumed more salt, cholesterol, and protein, while people from rural regions ate more carbohydrates and fiber; the resulting microbiomes showed less diversity in urban areas and more in rural areas [21]. A study evaluating the gut microbiome in the United States, Uganda, and Botswana found significant taxa differences between men who have sex with men and those who did not; elevated inflammatory markers in stool from PLHIV; and that specific markers and taxa varied by geographic region [22]. These studies, and others, underscore the importance of evaluating the complex relationship between immune status and the gut and fecal microbiomes as influenced by diet, geographic, region, and ethnicity, among others. Few studies to date have examined the fecal microbiome composition of adults living with HIV in Africa [22–30] despite the fact that 66% of HIV infections are clustered in the region [1], and high levels of regional variation in gut flora have been demonstrated across different geographic and dietary contexts [31, 32] – including among PLHIV [22].

The present analysis characterizes the fecal microbiome of adult Ugandans living with HIV who are enrolled in care at an outpatient HIV clinic in Mbale, Uganda. Mbale is a city in southeastern Uganda with a large rural catchment area [33] and a population of nearly 100,000 in 2015. The AIDS Support Organisation (TASO) Mbale clinic provides care for the rural and peri-urban population surrounding Mbale. This is the largest cohort of Ugandan PLHIV and the third largest of African PLHIV who have undergone 16S sequencing of their fecal microbiome. We hypothesized that composition of the fecal microbial community and/or specific taxa will be associated with duration of ART, CD4 + T cells/µL, and intestinal parasite infection. We also characterize the relationship between gut community composition and CD4 + T cells/µL recovery over time.

Methods

Study participants and study design

We recruited adult Ugandans receiving outpatient HIV care at TASO’s Mbale clinic into a study evaluating the burden of gastrointestinal parasites, bacterial microbiome, and clinical correlates. Methods and results of the parent study are described elsewhere [34]. The present analyses focus on the fecal microbiome of members of this cohort. Of the 202 participants enrolled in the study who provided one baseline stool sample, 175 participants are included in these analyses. Participants were excluded if they had used an anti-parasitic medication in the last 3 months.

Stool samples were self-collected at clinic and not treated with fixatives. They were frozen on site at -20 °C within 1–2 h of collection. Stool specimens were transported on a weekly basis to Kampala, Uganda for long-term − 80 °C storage during enrollment and kept frozen until extraction using MP BIOMEDICALS FastDNA™ SPIN Kit for Soil (Santa Ana, CA, USA). A blood draw was performed at enrollment for same-day CD4 + T cells/µL. Participants were passively followed for up to 24 months via medical chart review for CD4 + T cells/µL values over time [34]. Gastrointestinal parasites were detected using a multi-parallel real-time quantitative PCR as previously described [35, 36].

Clinical categories were a priori designated as: (1) parasitic infection (any parasitic infection vs. none; hookworm infections vs. no hookworm; Giardia. intestinalis vs. no G. intestinalis; protozoal infections vs. no protozoal infections; helminth infections vs. no helminth infections; 5 category infection status: none; protozoa only; helminth only; ≥ 1 protozoa; ≥ 1 helminth infection); (2) months on ART at enrollment; and (3) CD4 + cells/µL at study enrollment (< 100 cells/µL, 100 to < 200 cells/µL, 200 to < 350 cells/µL, 350 to < 500 cells/µL, and 500 + cells/µL. A posteriori, CD4 + cells/µL was also categorized as < 100 cells/µL, 100 to < 350 cells/µL, and ≥ 350 cells/µL. Our primary longitudinal CD4 + T cells/µL outcome was at 12 months.

Bacterial 16S rRNA extraction, sequence preparation, and operational taxonomic units (OTU) selection

DNA was extracted from stool samples using the MP BIOMEDICALS FastDNA™ SPIN Kit for Soil (Santa Ana, CA, USA) per the manufacturer’s protocol in Uganda, which has been used in previous microbiome research in a resource limited country [37]. The Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) sequenced the V5-V6 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene at the University of Minnesota Genomics Center, as we have done previously [38, 39]. PCR methods, including information about sequencing cycles and temperatures, are included in Supplementary Materials. Negative controls excluded sample material; upon sequencing, no DNA was detected in these samples. No positive controls were analyzed.

Operational taxonomic units were identified from these sequences via closed-reference selection in QIIME v1.8.0 using the USEARCH method [40, 41], whereby sequences from unknown samples were compared against the Greengenes reference database v13_8 [42] at a 97% similarity cutoff, which is sufficiently specific to accurately group sequences originating from the same genera [39]. To capture each participant’s fecal bacterial community diversity, we performed rarefaction analyses using three different alpha diversity metrics: (i) species counts based on operational taxonomic units, (ii) phylogenetic diversity based on total taxonomic branch length per sample, and (iii) the Chao1 estimator [43]. Samples with a read count < 5,000 were excluded from further analyses per our sequencing depth per sample [44]. Alpha diversity metrics were calculated in QIIME v1.8.0 using the alpha_diversity.py script.

Diversity analyses

We evaluated the relationship between alpha diversity metrics (number of observed species, phylogenetic diversity, Chao1, and Shannon-Weaver diversity [45]) and clinical characteristics (CD4 + T cells/µL, time on ART, parasitic infection) via univariable and adjusted ANOVA and linear regression. Covariates included age, months on ART, and CD4 + T cells/µL. When ANOVA indicated a significant overall relationship between the alpha diversity metric and clinical characteristics, further exploration of the relationship between alpha diversity and CD4 + T cells/µL was conducted via Tukey-Kramer pairwise comparisons. Correlations between Simpson diversity [46] and clinical characteristics were evaluated via non-parametric tests. β-diversity was quantified via weighted and unweighted unifrac distance matrices [47, 48]. Weighted and unweighted unifrac β-diversity matrices were visually assessed by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) for clustering by the above-described metadata groups: parasitic infection; months on ART; and CD4 + cells/µL at study enrollment.

Cross-sectional analyses

We qualitatively evaluated the abundance of phyla Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Firmicutes, which are associated with an HIV-associated loss in microbial diversity [49]. We also evaluated the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes relative abundances, which has previously been demonstrated to correlate with ageing and comorbidities, e.g. obesity [50, 51]. Non-parametric correlations quantified relationships between individual genera with clinical parameters, e.g. parasitic infection, months on ART, CD4 + cells/µL, and demographic characteristics, e.g. farming occupation, age, sex. Taxa identified in < 5% of participants were not considered in analyses.

Longitudinal analyses

Restricted maximum likelihood linear mixed models (LMM) evaluated the longitudinal relationship between specific taxa sampled at enrollment and 12-month CD4 + T cells/µL recovery. LMM included unstructured covariance matrices, participant-specific random intercepts, random effects for time, and an interaction term for time from enrollment and the species or genus. LMM were adjusted for months on ART at enrollment, body mass index (BMI), and age. Statistical analyses were conducted in Stata/SE 14.2 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX, USA), against an overall alpha level of 0.05. P-values for each outcome were adjusted for multiple comparisons via the Benjamini–Hochberg technique [52].

Results

Cohort description and included samples

Of 202 parent study participants, 181 (90%) provided fecal specimens for sequencing. Of 181 samples, 6 had < 5,000 reads and were excluded from analyses. The remaining 175 samples had a median of 46,515 sequences (min. 17,058, max. 149,393). Of 175 participants, 121 (69%) were women; 156 (89%) were receiving ART at enrollment with a median duration of 15 (IQR: 5–29) months; 174 (99.4%) participants were receiving trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole) primary prophylaxis, which was the standard of care for all PLHIV in Uganda regardless of CD4 + T cells/µL. Data describing parasitic infection are described previously [34] and in Table 1, along with additional sociodemographic and clinical data for this group. Characteristics of this cohort closely reflect characteristics of the overall 202 study participants (data not shown).

Microbiome composition

Participants’ fecal microbiome composition was predominantly Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, which collectively accounted for a median relative abundance of 86.6%. The median relative abundance of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria was 47.9% (IQR: 36.3, 59.2), 38.7% (IQR: 28.2%, 48.3%), and 0.8% (IQR: 0.2%, 2.5%), respectively. No Fusobacteria were exhibited in 86.9% of participants, with a maximum relative abundance of 1.1%.

Baseline correlations between the relative abundance of individual genera and clinical factors of CD4 + T cells/µL, months on ART, and parasitic infection yielded no statistically significant correlations after adjustment for multiple comparisons, with one exception. We observed an inverse relationship between the Anaerococcus genus and CD4 + T cells/µL (Spearman correlation − 0.3207; p-value = 0.0000152 against an FDR p-value of 0.000179; Fig. 1). Table 2 shows results of analyses of other common genera with p-values < 0.01. We also plotted the alpha diversity by CD4 T Cell Groups as seen in Supplemental material 3.

Clinical characteristics and microbiome diversity

Of the clinical factors evaluated for their association with fecal alpha diversity, only CD4 + T cells/µL was associated with within-participant community richness (observed species; phylogenetic tree; Chao1; Table 3). No differences in Shannon and Simpson diversity were observed across CD4 + T cells/µL categories. Differences in alpha diversity were largely driven by differences between participants with < 100 CD4 + T cells/µL, who had markedly lower alpha diversity scores than participants with ≥ 100 CD4 + T cells/µL (Fig. 2). No clustering was observed in weighted or unweighted beta diversity across any of the above-described clinical factors; and weighted unifrac beta diversity PCoA plots (not shown).

Relationship between microbiome composition and CD4 + T cells/µL trajectory

All participants had baseline CD4 + T cells/µL results. In the 12 months post-enrollment, 84 (48%) had one follow-up CD4 + T cells/µL measurement, 11 (6%) had two follow-up CD4 + T cells/µL measurements, and one participant had three follow-up CD4 + T cells/µL measurements. The reasons for CD4 + T cells/µL assessments in the 12 months post-follow up are unknown, although standard clinical practice at the time dictated a CD4 + T cells/µL assessment every 6 months.

Results of longitudinal analyses indicated that two operational taxonomic units, both Proteobacteria, were correlated with relative increases in CD4 + T cells/µL over time. This association was sustained when adjusted for age, BMI, and months on ART: Sutterella (βrelative abundance – time: 39.3; 95% CI: 22.1, 56.5; p-value = 0.0000171) and an unclassified genus of the Alcaligenaceae family (βrelative abundance – time: 37.6, 95% CI: 20.7, 54.5; p-value < 0.001). Model based prediction figures are included as Supplementary Fig. 1, based on cohort mean values.

Discussion

We present fecal microbiome analysis from 175 adult rural Ugandans with HIV. The goal of the study was to better understand the relationship between immune status and microbiome composition in PLHIV in rural and peri-urban Africa. We found that alpha diversity was similar across most clinically significant levels of CD4 + T cells/µL, apart from people living with advanced HIV disease with < 100 CD4 + T cells/µL. Severely immunocompromised participants demonstrated decreased overall bacterial community diversity, relative to their peers, even after adjusting for the effects of long-term HIV care and participant age. We found an inverse correlation between the relative CD4 + T cells/µL, but no other baseline clinical factors were associated with abundance of Anaerococcus and differential distribution of taxa after correction for multiple comparisons. We also that found two taxa – Sutterella and an unspecified genus of the Alcaligenaceae family – were positively associated with increases in CD4 + T cell trajectory over the 12-months post-study enrollment. These analyses – particularly the longitudinal analyses – are meant to be hypothesis generating, and should be further explored in future cohorts.

A study evaluating the gut microbiome of healthy, rural Uganda controls found similarly high mean relative abundance of Bacteroidetes (47.4%; Standard Deviation (SD) = 3.8%) and Firmicutes (41.0%; SD = 2.8%) as in this cohort, which quantified the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes at 47.9% (IQR: 36.3, 59.2) and Firmicutes at 38.7% (IQR: 28.2%, 48.3%) [53]. We also confirm what has been observed in other African [24, 26, 54] and Western [13, 49, 55] cohorts with respect to alpha diversity, which correlate lower alpha diversity with lower immune function – albeit only among participants at the extremely low end of the immunologic spectrum here and in another Ugandan cohort in Monaco et al.., which focused on < 200 CD4 + T cells/µL [24]. Shenoy et al.. found that PLHIV in the lowest CD4 + quartile had much lower Faith’s diversity versus the highest CD4 + quartile [26]. Studies comparing PLHIV to those without HIV demonstrated lower alpha diversity (based on varying indices) compared to those without HIV [27, 28]. Rocafort et al.. only found alpha diversity differences between PLHIV versus those without HIV using the abundance-based coverage estimator, and with Shannon or Chao1 indeces [23]. They did not evaluate alpha diversity among the severely immunosuppressed however, and CD4 + T cell/µL in their HIV cohorts were high, with median nadir value of 430 CD4 + T cells/µL among the Uganda cohort and 493 CD4 + T cells/µL among the Botswana cohort. Nowak et al. also did not demonstrate significant differences by Shannon index and HIV status [55]; CD4 + T cells/µL were not provided, however, so it is difficult to compare this work to other studies.

We demonstrated an inverse relationship between the potentially pathogenic Anaerococcus genus relative abundance and CD4 + T cells/µL. Clinically, Anaerococcus in semen has been linked to low sperm quality [56], and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [57]. It has been found in vaginal fluid of women with HIV and bacterial vaginosis, whereas not in their HIV-uninfected peers [58]. Even in the presence of stable ART, children with HIV in Zimbabwe were found to have Anaerococcus-enriched rectal microbiomes [59]. Conversely, Nowak et al.. evaluated rectal swabs from men who have sex with men (MSM) in Nigeria [60], and found that Anaerococcus was enriched among those receiving HIV therapy as compared to their untreated peers; the authors opine that co-trimoxazole may be responsible for this shift, as opposed to HIV infection. With respect to our research, the finding of an inverse relationship between the relative abundance of Anaerococcus and CD4 + T cells/µL may serve as a marker for immune dysfunction in the presence of ART. More research to elucidate the implications of this finding is, however, required.

Sutterella has been identified as both a potential commensal and immunomodulator that does not significantly disrupt the epithelial homeostasis critical to inflammation control [61], and a genus that causes gastrointestinal disease [62]. We found a positive correlation between Sutterella spp. relative abundance and CD4 + T cells/µL. Similarly, in a study of MSM with HIV, Ceccarani et al.. found that a higher CD4+/CD8 + ratio was positively correlated with Sutterella [63]. Vesterbacka et al. found that elite controllers have significantly greater abundance of Sutterella compared to ART-naïve PLHIV who are not elite controllers [64]. Taken together, current evidence supports that higher relative abundance of Sutterella is linked to better immune function – as is also seen in the current research. Sutterella. Elucidating the clinical implications of these findings also requires additional research, including – but not limited to – the use of predictive functional profiling tools, e.g. PICRUSt, which would clarify if Sutterella is a marker of a healthier gut microbiome or a modulating influence on gut health and host immunity.

The Alcaligenaceae family has been identified as a commensal bacteria of the gut-associated lymphatic tissues (GALT), a critical area for HIV infection [65], and is normally contained to the underlying GALT. The presence of Alcaligenaceae elsewhere, however, may be pro-inflammatory in nature [66], as when seen, for example, in brain tissue and CSF from PLHIV [67, 68].

Our participants were receiving a broad spectrum antibiotic, co-trimoxazole, as a primary prophylaxis against opportunistic infections as was standard of care at the time of this study for all PLHIV – regardless of immune status [69]. As mentioned above, Nowak et al.. postulate that co-trimoxazole may be driving noted decreased alpha diversity in HIV-treated participants [54]. We agree that co-trimoxazole may contribute to the decreased diversity in microbial communities observed in persons who have initiated HIV therapy. Like Monaco et al.., we were unable to evaluate the relationship between co-trimoxazole and microbiome composition, as our entire cohort was receiving co-trimoxazole primary prophylaxis [24]. However, we note a consistent pattern of high immunosuppression and low alpha diversity in the literature and this study, conditional on co-trimoxazole receipt. Although research to further contextualize and parse microbiome studies where participants are receiving co-trimoxazole and other broad spectrum antibiotics could be useful, these analyses may have limited current clinical utility as WHO Guidelines no longer recommend reflexive co-trimoxazole therapy for PLHIV in low-resource settings, with the exception of patients with < 200 CD4 + T cells/µL. In 2018, Ugandan guidelines were updated to reflect that PLHIV receiving ART for at least one year could stop co-trimoxazole prophylaxis [69]. Other African countries have also changed their guidelines [70]. Two newer studies evaluating the gut microbiome in African PLHIV had lower rates of co-trimoxazole, only 34% in Chandiwana et al..27 and solely one individual in 55 had taken co-trimoxazole in the month prior to sample collection [28]. Others did not mention co-trimoxazole – although their data collection is similarly aged to the present study [23, 26]. Co-trimoxazole has broad antimicrobial activity against gram positive and gram negative bacteria [71], including against many gut flora; it can decrease microbial biodiversity [72, 73], and considering co-trimoxazole use is important when interpreting gut microbiome data in PLHIV.

The data presented herein represent a large cohort of Ugandan adults living with HIV and contributing to gut microbiome research, its composition, and the relationship between that composition and clinical correlates. Additionally, this study represents a mostly rural African population which is where half of Africans live [74]. Genetic and other regional differences notwithstanding [22], these participants are largely generalizable to other PLHIV seeking care across Uganda and many areas of Africa. Recently published data indicate that the overall immune status of this cohort may be higher than other areas of Sub-Saharan Africa, specifically West Africa, where the prevalence of CD4 + < 200 at ART initiation was 40% in 2019 compared to 30–34% in other African regions [75]. These differences in immunologic status, along with differences in diet and environment, should be considered when extrapolating these results to other African populations. Like data published by Dolo et al..20, these data also provide exploratory pathways for future investigation of how the gut microbiome composition may impact CD4 + T cell immune recovery while receiving ART. Furthermore, while patterns have emerged internationally in terms of gut profile, inflammation, and HIV [16, 19, 55, 76, 77], given the burden of HIV in Africa and context-specific factors impacting the gut microbiome, future research should prioritize African populations in rural and urban areas.

Comparing our microbiome data from an African, predominantly heterosexual population living with HIV with Western populations living with HIV such as from the United States, which have more varied diets, higher genetic diversity, and are predominantly homosexual communities is challenging. Indeed, a review by Tuddenham et al.. notes that men who have sex with men have very distinct microbiomes that likely confound most assessments of the gut microbiome [78]. They note that among studies from the US or Europe, three found increased alpha-diversity in MSM versus with non-MSM, while one found decreased alpha-diversity in MSM versus with non-MSM. Increases in the abundance of Prevotella spp. in MSM versus non-MSM is the most common finding. Further dietary, lifestyle, and frequency of other infections between African populations living with HIV and Western populations living with HIV make them distinct. Further research on the gut taking these important factors into account are needed to better understand the specifics of what HIV does to the gut outside of sexual orientation.

Limitations

This work only included baseline stool and blood collection with follow up CD4 + T cells/µL obtained via chart review. Because of this, we are missing follow-up CD4 + T cell concentrations for some patients and lack the ability to establish a CD4 + T cell trajectory for all patients. Although this is a large cohort, limitations of the study include the lack of a direct comparison group of HIV-uninfected persons from the same region and background. An additional limitation is the relatively small number of participants with < 100 CD4 + T cells/µL. Although we did subject our findings to multiple comparisons adjustment, and similar data are available in the literature, this small number of participants may not generate robust findings. Given the variation in dietary and regional impacts on the microbiome [79, 80], “isolating” the impact of HIV on fecal microbiome composition in Ugandan PLHIV would be well served by a similar comparison group without HIV. It would also be valuable to correlate inflammation markers, e.g. soluble CD14, with our microbiome data, and to analyze additional stool samples over time in this population to bolster longitudinal findings.

Data were analyzed via 16S sequencing, which allows identification to the genus and occasionally species [81]. Species identification can be important – particularly for clinical applications [82] – and it is possible that unique and imprecisely characterized species are impacting the results that we see here. And although these data were collected and analyzed per commonly used methods at the time of the study, the use of shotgun metagenomic sequencing would have offered superior insight by enabling more precise taxonomic classification, functional pathway analysis, and detection of strain‑level variation. Despite this limitation, these analyses still offer gut microbiome insights in the context of Ugandan PLHIV. Future studies using more specific methods, e.g. shotgun metagenomic sequencing, will provide species-specific and other contributions to the literature.

These samples were analyzed at the University of Minnesota Genomics Center via their standard protocols. However, additional potential limitations include that we did not perform specific contamination screening, and no controls beyond those provided by the sequencing center were included. Other limitations include not collecting potential environmental exposures, e.g., animal husbandry, health behaviors, e.g., smoking status, sexual practices, or dietary information. Although our anecdotal experience is that this clinical cohort has a very similar and typical rural Ugandan diet, we are unable to quantify how diet and nutritional status may have impacted our results; it is possible that our results, particularly the relationship between Anaerococcus [83] and CD4 + cells/µL, is confounded by diet and nutritional status, which is driving CD4 depletion.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, our results contribute to the characterization of the fecal microbiome and its relationship with immune status in rural and peri-urban Ugandan PLHIV. Severely immunocompromised PLHIV demonstrated higher fecal flora dysbiosis, including an inverse correlation between species of the Anaerococcus genus and CD4 + T cells/µL. (positive) and. Unclassified species of the Alcaligenaceae and Sutterella genera were correlated with increases in CD4 + T cells/µL over time. Future work exploring interventions that support gut microbiome recovery, conducted using more advanced sequencing modalities and are conducted in the context of less primary antibiotic prophylaxis and integrase strand transfer inhibitor regimens, is needed.
