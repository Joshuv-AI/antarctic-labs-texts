Site-directed mutagenesis and semi-rational design to enhance chitinolytic activity of bacterial exochitinase
Authors: Mati Ullah, Jianda Han, Xiaomei Zhu, Zhongjian Guo, Najumuddin, Muhammad Naeem, Vivian Andoh, Guoqiang Guan, Abebe Bogale, Esmael M Alyami, Ohoud A Alghamdi, Turki M Dawoud, Huayou Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChitin, a prevalent biopolymer found in arthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls, requires efficient depolymerization to provide bioactive derivatives suitable for agriculture, medicine, and wastewater treatment. Bacterial chitinases, such as the exochitinases produced by Paenibacillus barengoltzii, are potential biocatalysts for chitin recycling; however, they exhibit insufficient activity and stability for industrial use. In this study, Semi-rational engineering and site-directed mutagenesis were employed to enhance catalytic efficiency, resulting in five mutants: M1 (D344Y), M2 (D192Y), M3 (D144Y), M4 (D534G), and M5 (D192A). M3 and M5 significantly increased chitinolytic activity, boosting it by 338% and 276%, respectively, compared to the original enzyme. Further enhancement was achieved by incorporating a chitin-binding domain (CBD), resulting in a six-fold increase in activity with the M35-CBD chimaera. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed on wild-type, M3, and M5 variations to elucidate the structural basis for these enhancements. M3 (D144Y) exhibited greater structural stability, less RMSD fluctuations, and an improved hydrogen bond network in the substrate-binding region. Notably, enhanced π-stacking interactions facilitated by solvent-exposed aromatic residues, including tryptophan, improved substrate affinity. Unlike the wild-type enzyme, the produced mutants exhibited reduced conformational mobility alongside increased substrate interaction. The results demonstrate the efficacy of targeted mutagenesis and domain engineering in creating stable chitinases for industrial and environmental biotechnology applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-026-03031-9.Keywords: Chitin, Chitinase, Site directed mutagenesis, Chitin binding domain

Introduction

Chitin is a prevalent biopolymer in nature, functioning as a crucial structural component in specific arthropod exoskeletons, fungal cell walls, and marine organisms [1]. The efficient and selective hydrolysis of chitin to bioactive compounds, such as N-acetylglucosamine, is of great importance for several biotechnological applications, including agriculture, waste management, and pharmaceuticals. Chitinases, the degrading enzymes, have garnered significant interest from scientists owing to their extensive uses in the management of fungal infections, plant defense mechanisms, and involvement in bioconversion reactions [2].

Bacterial chitinases are a group of enzymes that facilitate the degradation and subsequent reutilization of chitin across ecosystems [3]. Bacterial chitinases exhibit significant structural diversity and are categorized into glycoside hydrolase families based on their amino acid sequences and catalytic mechanisms, with GH18 and GH195 being the most prevalent families [4]. Chitinases possess significant biological value and substantial potential for industrial and biomedical applications, including the production of bioactive oligosaccharides, plant biocontrol, and antifungal therapies. Their adaptability and efficacy make them the primary focus of research on the use of nature-derived biocatalysts in sustainable applications [5].

The Paenibacillus genus is known for its ability to synthesize a diverse array of enzymes and is a significant source of chitinases [6, 7]. The Paenibacillus barengoltzii exochitinases are distinguished by their activity and specificity in hydrolyzing the terminal units of chitin polymers, making them advantageous for research and industrial applications. Nonetheless, the molecular factors influencing their catalytic efficiency, substrate selectivity, and structure-function relationship remain ambiguous [7]. The intrinsic catalytic efficiency of bacterial chitinases often falls short of optimal activity levels for engineering applications, despite their significant potential for efficiency [8–10].

Protein engineering has become a versatile and rapidly advancing approach for improving enzyme performance, including catalytic efficiency, substrate specificity, thermostability, and industrial applicability. Key strategies such as rational design and site-directed mutagenesis enable precise modification of active-site and substrate-binding residues based on structural insights, while directed evolution and semi-rational design expand the search for beneficial mutations across wider sequence space. In parallel, technologies like surface display systems enhance enzyme stability and reusability, and approaches such as domain swapping and chimeric construction exploit modular protein architectures to introduce new functional properties. More recently, emerging methods including ancestral sequence reconstruction, computational redesign, and data-driven enzyme mining have further extended the capabilities of protein engineering, allowing the creation of biocatalysts with optimized and novel functionalities beyond the reach of traditional techniques [11–26].

These methodologies were employed to modify critical catalytic residues and substrate-binding domains (SBDs), thereby creating valuable tools to improve enzyme efficacy [27, 28]. Notably, changes to the CBD, in conjunction with alterations to the active site, can have substantial effects on substrate recognition, binding selectivity, and overall catalytic efficiency [29]. Although chitinases are well studied, the molecular mechanisms governing inter-domain synergy, heat resistance, and catalytic optimization remain incompletely elucidated, thereby restricting their translational potential across diverse industrial and biomedical sectors. Addressing these limitations necessitates well-designed research that combines structural, functional, and dynamic evaluations [30–33].

Despite significant advances in chitinase characterization, a clear research gap persists in the integrated engineering of both the catalytic core and substrate-binding interface of bacterial exochitinases through combined site-directed mutagenesis and chitin-binding domain (CBD) fusion strategies. This study utilized site-directed mutagenesis to produce five mutant versions of a heat-resistant bacterial exochitinase to improve chitinolytic activity and stability. All five mutants exhibited enhanced catalytic activity compared to the wild-type enzyme, with M3 and M5 demonstrating the greatest activity. To augment catalytic efficiency and substrate specificity, the chitin-binding domains of M3 and M5 were modified to maximize substrate affinity. This dual-alteration approach, aimed at both the catalytic core and the substrate-binding interface, led to markedly improved enzyme activity, demonstrating the synergistic benefit of engineering at both the active site and domain levels. Our research elucidates the correlation between the structure and function of chitinase and lays the groundwork for creating efficient chitin-degrading biocatalysts appropriate for commercial use.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains, gene, plasmids, and growth conditions

The specific bacterial strains, buffer, plasmid, and primers used in the present study are listed in Table S1. The exochitinase gene (GeneBank accession number KJ626399) from Paenibacillus barengoltzii CAU904, featuring a C-terminal histidine tag, was precisely optimized and synthesized in the pET-21a vector (designated pET-21a-Chi) by General Biological Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China. The host organism utilized for heterologous expression was E. coli BL21 (DE3), cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium augmented with ampicillin.

In silico molecular docking

The 3D structure of the exochitinase protein was constructed using SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org) and subsequently assessed for quality through the SAVESv6.0 server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu/). The molecular structure of the ligand chitin was acquired from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The exochitinase model was viewed and structurally analyzed in PyMOL. Molecular docking was performed via AutoDock Vina, with the receptor being the exochitinase model. The binding conformation exhibiting the minimal energy was chosen for investigation. Discovery Studio Visualizer was used to identify and annotate critical contact sites between exochitinase and chitin. Furthermore, active-site protein models were created in PyMOL, revealing structural intricacies and interaction modalities at the atomic scale.

Site-specific mutations and primer design

The molecular docking data indicated that amino acid residues within 4 Å of the ligand-binding region comprise the active site of the exochitinase protein. Site-directed mutagenesis and virtual screening of active-site residues were performed using the CHARMM (Chemistry at HARvard Macromolecular Mechanics) program. The alterations in binding free energy (ΔΔG) resulting from mutations were calculated to assess their impact on ligand affinity. The residues demonstrating considerably elevated binding energies upon mutation were identified as essential contributors to enhanced affinity. Site-specific primers for the exochitinase gene were created for experimental site-directed mutagenesis, and the appropriate nucleotide sequences were available for synthesis.

Plasmids construction and expression

Insertion mutations were generated using the Pet-22b-Chi plasmid template by circular PCR and ligation, or seamless cloning via multifragment PCR amplification. Recombinant plasmids were validated using Sanger sequencing. Validated mutant constructs were then introduced into E. coli BL21(DE3) cells for heterologous expression. Enzyme activity experiments were conducted utilizing colloidal chitin as the substrate. The mutant enzyme’s function and activity were verified according to previously established protocols [34]. The activity of the wild-type exochitinase enzyme was established as the reference standard, fixed at 100%. The relative activity of each mutant enzyme was determined as a percentage of the wild-type activity. To ensure precision and reproducibility, all experiments were performed in triplicate.

Structural analysis and molecular dynamics simulation

To examine the dynamic behavior, conformational alterations, and structural integrity of the exochitinase-ligand complexes, 100-nanosecond molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted on the wild-type enzyme and two specific mutants, M3 (ChitinaseASP144TYR) and M5 (ChitinaseASP192ALA), utilizing GROMACS 2019.4 with the CHARMM36m force field [35, 36]. Before simulation, the compounds were prepared using Maestro’s Protein Preparation Wizard to correct bond ordering, assign protonation states, remove steric clashes, and optimize ligand geometry. In GROMACS, systems were generated using the CHARMM-GUI solution builder, solvated in a cubic TIP3P water box with a 1.0 nm buffer, and neutralized with Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. After energy minimization, system equilibration was conducted in two stages: a 125 ps simulation under the NVT ensemble utilizing the Nosé–Hoover thermostat for temperature stabilization, followed by a 125 ps simulation under the NPT ensemble employing the Parrinello–Rahman barostat for pressure stabilization. The Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) approach was employed for long-range electrostatics, whereas the LINCS algorithm constrained all hydrogen-bonded atoms with a 2-fs integration time step. MD trajectories were documented for 100 ns each system, with PDB snapshots obtained every 10 ns. Structural investigations comprised calculations of root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen-bonding patterns, and total system energy, using GROMACS-integrated tools to evaluate the stability and flexibility of the complexes during the simulations.

Preparation of colloidal chitin substrate

Colloidal chitin was produced according to a previously established procedure with minor changes [37]. In summary, 5 g of chitin powder sourced from shrimp shells were added incrementally to 60 mL of 37% hydrochloric acid, stirred continuously on ice, and incubated overnight to ensure complete hydrolysis. The resultant viscous solution was subsequently transferred to 200 mL of ice-cold 95% ethanol and agitated for an additional 30 min on ice to promote the precipitation of colloidal chitin. The suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the pellet was washed several times with sterile distilled water until the pH was neutral. The resulting colloidal chitin suspension was stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Expression, purification, and concentration of recombinant chitinase

Mutant strains harboring the chitinase gene were cultivated in LB medium supplemented with ampicillin (100 µg/mL) at 37 °C and agitated at 200 rpm. Upon reaching an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 0.6 to 0.8, IPTG (1 mM) was administered to increase protein expression, followed by an additional incubation period of 10 h at 37 °C. The cells were subsequently harvested using centrifugation (10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C) and rinsed once with PBS (pH 7.4). The pellet was resuspended in PBS and incubated with lysozyme (1 mg/mL) at 27 °C for 75 min to induce cell lysis. The resultant lysate was centrifuged (12,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C), and the supernatant, containing the soluble His-tagged exochitinase, was collected. Purification was conducted using Ni-NTA affinity chromatography on an Inscinstech FPLC apparatus. The eluted protein was subsequently concentrated and desalted using Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore, USA), measured, and preserved at − 80 °C for future use.

SDS-PAGE, western blotting, and enzymatic activity assays

The activity of purified exochitinase was evaluated using a modified approach based on fundamental methods [38]. A 100 µL mixture of enzyme solution and 100 µL of reaction buffer containing 1% (w/v) colloidal chitin was incubated at 50 °C for 30 min. The resultant reducing sugars were measured utilizing the dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) assay, with standard N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (GlcNAc) as a reference [34]. One unit of chitinase activity is defined as the amount of enzyme that generates 1 µmol of reducing sugars per minute under the designated test conditions. The protein concentration was quantified utilizing the Lowry technique, with BSA as the standard [39]. The enzyme’s molecular weight and purity were assessed using SDS-PAGE, and the identity of the His-tagged exochitinase was verified using Western blotting with an anti-His monoclonal antibody.

Stability and activity of exochitinase in response to ph and temperature

The ideal pH for exochitinase activity was established by evaluating enzyme performance throughout a pH spectrum of 2.0 to 12.0, employing diverse buffer systems: glycine-HCl (pH 2–3), acetate (pH 4–5), phosphate (pH 6–8), Tris-HCl (pH 9–10), and glycine-NaOH (pH 11–12). The enzyme’s pH stability was assessed by pre-incubating it at each pH level for 30 min at 30 °C, followed by the evaluation of residual activity under standard assay conditions. The temperature dependence was examined by incubating reaction mixtures at 30–80 °C. Thermal stability was evaluated by pre-incubating enzyme samples at each temperature for 30 min, followed by activity assays. The enzyme underwent prolonged thermal inactivation when incubated in a 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5) at 65, 70, 75, and 80 °C for up to 4 h. Samples were collected at consistent intervals, cooled on ice, and analyzed to assess residual activity and thermal denaturation kinetics.

Analysis of the effects of chemical agents and metal ions on exochitinase activity

The impact of diverse chemical reagents (including EDTA, SDS, β-mercaptoethanol, and urea) and metal ions (such as Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, and Hg²⁺ at doses of 1 mM and 5 mM) on exochitinase activity was evaluated. Each reagent was added to the reaction mixtures, which were pre-incubated for a specified duration, following which the remaining enzymatic activity was measured under standard assay conditions. Control reactions without additives established a baseline for assessing 100% activity. The results were presented as percentages of activity relative to the control.

Engineering and functional evaluation of chitin-binding domains (CBD)

To enhance substrate affinity and catalytic efficiency, four synthetic chitin-binding domains (CBDs), BsFn-CBD, BsCBD, PbFn-CBD, and PbCBD, were engineered. The residues were selected based on their dynamic behavior and structural proximity to the chitin-binding pocket, and amplified by PCR using homologous recombination primers. The CBDs were later subcloned into expression vectors using a seamless cloning technique (Novartis, Nanjing, China) and verified in E. coli DH5α. The validated constructs were subsequently introduced into E. coli BL21 (DE3) for protein expression. Both individual CBDs and chimeric exochitinases were induced by IPTG and subsequently purified via affinity chromatography. Before conducting binding tests, protein concentrations were calibrated using the Bradford assay [40]. Binding affinity was assessed utilizing colloidal chitin, powdered chitin, and chitosan. The reactions (500 µL) comprised 200 µL of enzyme, 200 µL of 10% (w/v) substrate, and 100 µL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 5.5), and were incubated at ambient temperature with agitation. Samples were centrifuged every 2 h, and the unbound protein in the supernatant was quantified using the Bradford assay. The binding effectiveness was evaluated by measuring protein depletion from the supernatant. CBDs with enhanced binding capacities were reintroduced into the exochitinase framework to produce chimeric enzymes, which were subsequently re-expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3) and evaluated for catalytic activity and substrate selectivity using conventional enzyme assays.

Statical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three biological replicates, and each assay was independently repeated three times. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software. Differences among groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate Post Hoc multiple-comparison tests when applicable. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Confirmation and validation of exochitinase mutants expression

We performed SDS-PAGE and Western blot tests to confirm the effective expression of the exochitinase mutants. SDS-PAGE was utilized to evaluate the molecular weights and expression levels of the wild-type and mutant exochitinase proteins. Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining demonstrated clear protein bands at the expected molecular weight of exochitinase (74.3 kDa) (Fig. 1A). Compared with the control, all mutants showed bands of comparable intensity, signifying effective expression. Variations in band intensity among the mutations may indicate differences in expression efficiency or protein stability. To validate the identity and expression of the exochitinase mutants, Western blot analysis was conducted with an anti-rabbit goat and HRP-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG antibody. After electrophoretic transfer, immunoblotting confirmed the existence of the exochitinase protein in mutant samples. All examined mutant samples exhibited robust immunoreactive bands at the anticipated molecular weight, corroborating effective expression (Fig. 1B). The discrepancies in band intensity noted in Western Blotting corresponded with the SDS-PAGE findings, suggesting possible changes in protein production or post-translational modifications. In conclusion, these results confirm the effective expression of exochitinase mutants, establishing a basis for further enzymatic characterization and functional study.

Enhanced chitinase activity via mutagenesis and CBD fusion

Figure 2A shows that all five designed exochitinase mutant versions (M1–M5) exhibited superior chitin-degrading activity relative to the wild-type enzyme, which was set to 100% activity. Mutant M1 exhibited a mild rise to 162.2%, whilst M2 and M4 displayed greater increases, attaining 239.4% and 228.3%, respectively. The M3 and M5 mutant versions exhibited the highest catalytic activity levels, with 338.1% and 276.3%, respectively. The enhancements indicate that the specific point mutations incorporated into these variants markedly increased catalytic performance by altering substrate affinity or improving the shape of the active site [41].

Enzyme activity analysis of wild type and exochitinase mutants. A denotes the comparative relative activity of wild-type and mutant strains. B denoted the relative activity of wild type and mutants with and without the chitin binding domain (CBD). C denoted the efficiency of different chitin-binding domains at different time intervals

To assess the impacts of structural and functional engineering, we compared the performance of the wild-type enzyme with several engineered variants, including M3 and M5, their fusions with a chitin-binding domain (CBD), and a combinatorial mutant (M3,5) containing both mutation sets, with and without CBD fusion. The substitution of the wild-type CBD with a more optimized binding domain (designated as Wild Type CBD) increased enzymatic activity to 159.3% (Fig. 2B), indicating that enhanced substrate affinity via CBD fusion can augment catalytic performance.

Fusion of the optimized CBD with M3 and M5 yielded M3-CBD and M5-CBD, resulting in further significant increases in enzymatic activity of 537.42% and 439.48%, respectively. This synergistic effect underscores the complementary relationship between catalytic site optimization and enhanced substrate binding, consistent with prior results showing that dual methods can dramatically enhance enzyme-substrate interactions [42]. Interestingly, the combinatorial mutant M35 (harboring both M3 and M5 mutations) displayed even higher activity at 456.44%. When fused with the CBD, the resulting M35-CBD mutant reached the maximum observed activity of 616.45% (Fig. 2B), suggesting more than a sixfold increase over the wild-type enzyme. This significant improvement is probably due to additive or synergistic effects from the combination of mutations and CBD fusion, enhancing both catalytic efficiency and substrate affinity.

In support of this strategy, binding affinity experiments demonstrated that among the numerous CBDs tested, the CBD from Bacillus subtilis Fn3-like protein (BsFn-CBD) exhibited the highest binding effectiveness, particularly toward colloidal chitin, with a maximum of 27.72% (Table 1 and Fig. 2C). Consequently, the native CBD of B. subtilis chitinase BsChi (GenBank: AF069131.1) was substituted with BsFn-CBD, and the resulting recombinant enzyme was produced in E. coli BL21. Enzymatic experiments revealed a 1.89-fold enhancement in activity towards colloidal chitin compared to the wild-type and increased degradation efficiency of insoluble chitin substrates (Fig. 2C). These findings align with recent research that supports the significance of CBD engineering in augmenting chitinase function. For example, Su et al. showed that replacing the native CBD of SaChiA4 with that of Bacillus circulans ChiA1 dramatically boosted enzymatic activity by 54% on chitin powder and 49% on colloidal chitin, owing to improved substrate affinity mediated by hydrophobic interactions [43]. Similarly, Serratia proteamaculans-derived chitin-binding proteins (CBPs) have been shown to synergize with chitinases to enhance the hydrolysis of both α- and β-chitin, underscoring the value of substrate-binding domain insertion in enzyme design [44]. Xue et al. further demonstrated that fusing a CBD from Chitinolyticbacter meiyuanensis to a cutinase variant not only increased binding to PET films but also boosted degradation efficiency, indicating the broad applicability of this method to enzymes targeting refractory substrates [45].

Structural and functional analysis of chitin-substrate interactions

The RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation) measures structural deviation of the protein from its initial conformation over time. In this work, the wild type (ChitinaseWT), mutant M5 (ChitinaseASP192ALA), and mutant M3 (ChitinaseASP144TYR) showed stability at 50, 38, and 35 ns, respectively. These observations suggest that the mutant system equilibrated slightly earlier than the wild type under the simulation conditions. The RMSF analysis was performed to check the flexibility of wild-type and its mutant structures, and our result showed that wild-type and mutant M3 has larger RMSF values, indicating greater local fluctuations, suggestive of the higher the RMSF value, the more flexible the structure is, while the M5 mutant showed reduced fluctuations overall, but only the ALA619 residue was observed with higher fluctuations. This pattern is consistent with a comparatively stabilized backbone in M5 during the simulation time frame. Additionally, the radius of gyration (Rg), which measures the compactness of a protein structure, ranged from 4 to 5 Å across all complexes, indicating comparable global compactness throughout the simulation. Earlier, a lower Rg value with minimal volatility was observed, implying a more compact and stable structure during the simulation [41]. Overall, folding stability was similar across the systems, but minor variations in Rg may suggest subtle structural rearrangements. Hydrogen-bonding pattern investigation of all three complexes suggested that a maximum of 8 hydrogen bonds were observed over a 100-ns simulation. While the number of hydrogen bonds fluctuated over time, the overall patterns were comparable among the systems (Fig. 3). This is comparable to findings from another study, which evaluated the number of hydrogen bonds formed over 100 ns simulations between the ligand and chitinase, indicating the role of hydrogen bonding in maintaining protein-ligand interactions [46].

Further, to clarify the structural basis of the improved or altered substrate affinity in chitinase mutants, we conducted a rigorous comparative investigation of non-covalent interactions between chitin and the catalytic domains of wild-type, M3, and M5 chitinases (Fig. 4). The wild-type chitinase formed a relatively modest interaction network with the chitin substrate, comprising four hydrogen bonds, predominantly mediated by conserved residues (Fig. 4A). Glu176 formed a strong hydrogen bond (1.76 Å) via an O–H interaction, underscoring its crucial role in substrate activation. Thr137 formed two H–O bonds at 1.95 Å and 1.77 Å, respectively, indicating a dual-point anchoring that may improve substrate placement. Asn61 established a weaker hydrogen bond (2.39 Å), suggesting a peripheral stabilizing effect. Overall, these interactions provide a foundational basis for comparing mutant variants. In the M3, a substantial rearrangement in the binding interface was found, including five different residues (Fig. 4B). Asn61 returned as a hydrogen bond donor (1.89 Å), exhibiting a marginally closer distance than in the wild-type. Thr137 again revealed a crucial dual interaction (1.87 Å). Glu176 maintained a strong O–H bond at 1.70 Å, emphasizing its sustained enzymatic action. A novel interaction involving Tyr177 (1.98 Å) suggests extra substrate stabilization near the catalytic pocket. However, the anomalously distant Tyr251–chitin interaction (8.24 Å) showed a non-productive or transitory contact, which may occur from local conformational rearrangements. Overall, M5 shows a moderate increase in binding compactness, presumably improving catalytic efficiency by better positioning the substrate (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, Tyr144, introduced via the D144Y mutation, formed intramolecular interactions with Ala147 and His141, likely stabilizing the loop region and increasing active-site stiffness. Critically, three different tryptophan residues (Trp340, Trp414, and Trp240) formed eight distinct hydrogen bonds. This suggests an aromatic-rich milieu, amenable to π-stacking or to van der Waals stability of the polysaccharide substrate. Arg308, Tyr251, Asp252, and Asn227 also contributed tightly coordinated H–O or O–H bonds, producing a potent and multivalent interface. The M5 mutant, while demonstrating intermediate improvements (Fig. 4C). These interaction patterns indicate that M3 has a more advanced substrate-coordination network than the wild-type and M5 mutant. These structural findings show that tailored residue alterations, particularly those that increase aromatic clustering and hydrogen bonding, can significantly improve the substrate-binding properties of chitinases. Emphasized the importance of hydrogen bonding in maintaining the structural integrity of chitosanase, a similar enzyme, highlighting the role of such interactions in enzymatic stability [47]. This has obvious implications for rational enzyme design in applications involving chitin degradation, including bioconversion of chitinous waste and biomedical glycan processing.

To examine the effect of mutation in these complexes, as compared to the ChitinaseWT-N-acetyl Glucosamine complex, it was shown that the C-terminal region of ChitinaseASP144TYR and ChitinaseASP192ALA have angles of rotation of 125.280 and 131.080, respectively (Figure S2). Moreover, localized secondary structure transitions were observed in the N-acetylglucosamine binding site, specifically in ChitinaseASP144TYR, β3 and 4, together with α11, which exhibited loop-like transitions. Whereas, in ChitinaseASP192ALA β3, 4, 5, and 6 exhibited loop-like conformations (Fig. 5A). The amino acids comprising the β3, β4, β5, and β6 exhibit stability upon substrate binding, suggesting that these structural rearrangements may influence local flexibility near the binding pocket (Fig. 5B). Overall, these structural and conformational alterations suggest that mutations’ allosteric effects modulate local dynamics within the substrate-binding region. While, the MD simulation provide the structural insight into the observed enzymatic behavior [49, 50], the high flexibility (high RMSF) and altered loop dynamics identified in M3 are consistent with the experimentally measured improvements in catalytic performance, such as protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) and glycosyl hydrolase family 18 (GH18) chitinases indicates that loop flexibility improves substrate binding and catalytic efficiency [51].

Importantly, the M3 (ChitinaseASP144TYR) shows low RMSD values and altered loop dynamics, providing a structural basis for the experimental observation of its enhanced catalytic activity and improved thermal stability. While MD simulation is not a direct parameter to assess the enzymatic activity, the observed increase in structural stability and maintained hydrogen bonding pattern are consistent with the higher catalytic efficiency measured in vitro [52].

Effect of temperature and PH on exochitinase activity and stability

Figure 6A illustrates the ideal temperature profile for the enzymatic activity of both wild-type and mutant M3 exochitinase. Both enzymes demonstrate peak activity around 55 °C, with the M3 mutant sustaining a broader activity spectrum at higher temperatures (50–60 °C). In contrast, the wild-type enzyme exhibits a more pronounced decrease beyond its optimal range. The improved performance at elevated temperatures likely arises from strengthened intramolecular interactions and improved packing within the enzyme core, which collectively preserve active-site geometry under heat stress.

Comparison of optimal temperature and temperature stability of wild-type and M3 mutant exochitinase

Figure 6B illustrates the thermal stability of the wild-type enzyme at 50, 55, 60, 65, and 66 °C, over a 2-hour incubation period. A temporal decline in activity is apparent across all evaluated temperatures, with notable activity reduction at 65 °C and 66 °C. This verifies that the wild-type enzyme is thermolabile under prolonged exposure to heat. Conversely, Fig. 6C depicts the thermal stability of the M3 mutant enzyme under comparable conditions, including an additional data point at 70 °C. The mutant exhibits exceptional thermal stability, retaining over 60% activity at 60 °C for 120 min and maintaining significant activity at 65 °C and 70 °C. This enhanced thermostability is consistent with molecular dynamics findings demonstrating reduced structural fluctuations and improved hydrogen-bonding networks in M3. The D144Y mutation likely contributes to stabilization of loop regions proximal to the catalytic cleft, limiting heat-induced flexibility and preventing disruption of substrate-binding residues. The improved thermostability signifies structural alterations in M3 that reinforce the protein’s structure and safeguard it against heat-induced denaturation. Recent research has identified techniques to promote enzyme thermostability. Mutations introducing novel hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions have enhanced chitinases’ local structural stability and overall thermal resilience. Moreover, the increased quantity of α-helices and concealed hydrophobic residues is associated with improved thermostability in thermophilic chitinases [41]. The M3 mutant’s superior heat stability makes it a viable choice for industrial applications that require resilient enzymes. Thermostable chitinases have been effectively used to bioconvert chitinous waste into valuable chemicals, such as N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) [53]. Furthermore, chitinases exhibiting elevated heat stability are beneficial in activities requiring high temperatures, including specific agricultural and biotechnological applications.

The influence of the M3 mutation on the catalytic efficacy of exochitinase was evaluated by examining the ideal pH and pH stability profiles of both the wild-type and mutant enzymes (Fig. 7A and B). The ideal pH activity profile (Fig. 7A) demonstrated that both wild-type and M3 exochitinase displayed maximal activity at pH 6.0. However, the M3 mutant displayed a larger pH activity range, sustaining higher relative activity under both acidic (pH 2–4) and alkaline (pH 9–11) conditions than the wild-type enzyme. Notably, at pH 4 and 10, the M3 mutant retained around 10–15% greater activity than the wild type. This broadened pH tolerance suggests improved stabilization of ionizable residues within the active site, reducing sensitivity to protonation–deprotonation shifts that typically impair catalysis [54].

Comparison of optimal pH and pH stability of Wild-Type and M3 mutant exochitinase

The pH stability investigation during a 2-hour incubation across several pH levels (Fig. 7B) indicated that M3 demonstrated improved stability, especially at severe pH values. Although both enzymes exhibited optimal stability within the pH range of 5 to 8, the M3 mutant consistently maintained 10–20% more activity than the wild type at pH levels below 4 and above 9. This improvement likely arises from enhanced intramolecular hydrogen bonding and reduced solvent exposure of charged residues, which collectively buffer against pH-induced conformational perturbations. The D144Y substitution may contribute to a more hydrophobic microenvironment around flexible loop regions, thereby minimizing destabilizing electrostatic repulsions under extreme pH conditions. The results demonstrate that the M3 mutation enhances the functional pH tolerance and structural stability of exochitinase, indicating its potential for applications necessitating enzymatic resilience under fluctuating pH conditions. Guo et al. characterized a pH-stable chitinase derived from Micromonospora aurantiaca, which exhibited substantial activity over a broad pH range, underscoring its potential for chitin-degrading applications [55]. Research on a chitinase from Myxococcus xanthus revealed optimal activity at pH 7.0 and maintained over 70% activity across the pH range of 4.0–8.0, demonstrating significant stability across different pH settings [56]. Additionally, studies conducted by Guo et al. on a chitinase derived from M. aurantiaca showed that the enzyme displayed optimal activity at pH 6.0 and retained stability over a broad pH range, underscoring the importance of pH-resistant chitinases in biotechnological applications [55]. A study on a chitinase from Serratia marcescens demonstrated optimal activity at pH 6.0 and stability across a broad pH range, underscoring the importance of pH-resistant chitinases in biotechnological applications [57]. Additionally, a chitinase from Streptomyces sp. M-20 displayed maximal activity at pH 5.0 and maintained stability between pH 4.0 and 8.0, confirming the enzyme’s robustness across a wide s pH range [58].

Effects of chemical agents and metal ions on the activity of wild-type and mutant M3 exochitinase

Figure 8 offers a comparative investigation of the effects of several chemical agents (Fig. 8A and C) and metal ions (Fig. 8B and D) on the enzymatic activity of wild-type and mutant M3 exochitinase. The observations demonstrate that the M3 mutant exhibits greater tolerance and functional stability under chemically demanding conditions than the wild-type enzyme, underscoring the influence of the inserted mutations on structural and functional robustness.

Effects of chemical agents and metal ions on the activity of wild-type and mutant M3 exochitinase. A, B Relative enzymatic activity of wild-type exochitinase in the presence of several chemical agents (A) and metal ions (B). C, D Relative enzymatic activity of mutant M3 exochitinase in the presence of the same chemical agents (C) and metal ions (D). Enzymes were incubated with increasing concentrations (0.1, 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 M) of chemical agents and 0.1–2.0 mM of metal ions at 37 °C for 30 min. Residual activity was measured under standard assay conditions and expressed as a percentage of the activity in the absence of additives (set as 100%). Values are means ± SD of three independent experiments

In Fig. 8A, the wild-type exochitinase showed significant susceptibility to potent ionic detergents, such as SDS, with enzymatic activity declining markedly across all examined concentrations (0.1–2.0%). These chemicals likely interfere with the enzyme’s tertiary structure by interactions with hydrophobic or charged amino acid residues, resulting in protein denaturation or deformation of the active site. Moreover, solvents like ethanol and isopropanol induced mild inhibition, presumably due to dehydration effects on protein structure or disruption of substrate binding. Mild non-ionic surfactants (Tween-20, Tween-80, and Triton X-100) exerted reduced effects, showing partial tolerance of the wild-type enzyme to these compounds. Interestingly, reducing chemicals such as β-mercaptoethanol and dithiothreitol (DTT) modestly increased activity, potentially by lowering disulfide bonds and enhancing catalytic site flexibility or stability.

In contrast, the M3 mutant, as seen in Fig. 8C, retained substantially higher activity across nearly all chemical treatments. Although SDS continued to have inhibitory effects, the extent of activity reduction was markedly less than that observed in the wild type, even at elevated concentrations. M3 demonstrated enhanced tolerance to organic solvents, including ethanol, isopropanol, and DMSO, suggesting a more solvent-adapted or conformationally flexible enzyme structure. The favorable influence of reducing agents was more obvious in M3, with β-mercaptoethanol, DTT, and glycerol greatly enhancing enzyme activity. These data support the concept that M3 exhibits greater redox responsiveness and stability due to improved folding, tighter disulfide control, or reduced exposure of sensitive residues.

Figure 8B displays the metal ion sensitivity profile of the wild-type enzyme. Beneficial effects on activity were reported for divalent alkaline-earth metals, such as Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, which are commonly used as stabilizing cofactors for enzyme activity. In contrast, considerable inhibition was documented in the presence of heavy metals, including Cu²⁺, Ag⁺, Fe³⁺, and Al³⁺. These ions likely interfere with enzyme activity through oxidative stress, binding to catalytic residues, or displacing critical metal ions. Moderate inhibitory effects were also found with Zn²⁺ and Mn²⁺, particularly at higher doses, perhaps due to changed electrostatic interactions or conformational changes at the active site.

In contrast, Fig. 8D illustrates that the M3 mutant exhibited more activity in the presence of identical inhibitory metal ions. Despite elevated Cu²⁺, Ag⁺, and Fe³⁺ doses significantly inhibiting the wild-type enzyme, M3 retained substantial catalytic activity. This enhanced resistance suggests that the changes confer protection against metal-induced conformational destabilization or oxidative inactivation, possibly by stabilizing critical residues or by expanding the enzyme’s folding landscape. M3 also showed higher tolerance to Zn²⁺ and Mn²⁺, further validating its overall enhanced metal-ion resilience.

These results align with prior research. A chitinase from Stenotrophomonas rhizophila G22 showed considerable inhibition by Fe³⁺ and Cu²⁺ ions, but Mn²⁺ and Ca²⁺ functioned as activators at specific quantities [59]. Similarly, a study on a recombinant chitinase (CmChi3) indicated significant inhibition by Cu²⁺, Ni²⁺, and Fe³⁺, while Fe²⁺, Mg²⁺, Ba²⁺, and Na⁺ augmented its activity [60]. A study indicated that Cu2 + and EDTA reduce chitinase activity [61]. The similarities indicate that the M3 mutant’s increased tolerance to metal ions represents a notable advancement compared to naturally occurring enzymes.

Conclusion

In this work, we effectively utilized site-directed mutagenesis and semi-rational design to create five mutant variants of the bacterial exochitinase from Paenibacillus barengoltzii to improve chitinolytic activity and stability. Among mutants, M3 and M5 showed the greatest improvement, with enzymatic activities up to 338% and 276% higher than the wild-type enzyme, respectively. Further improvements were achieved by strategically fusing optimized chitin-binding domains (CBDs) to create the M35-CBD variant, which exhibited a staggering 616% increase in activity. These findings reveal the synergistic advantage of modifying both catalytic sites and substrate-binding interfaces to optimize enzyme efficiency. To shed light on the structural basis for these improvements, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for the wild-type, M3, and M5 variants. The RMSD and RMSF analysis indicated that the M3 mutant was the most structurally stable and had the least conformational fluctuations throughout the simulation. The results accentuate the role of single-residue substitutions in enhancing the binding affinity and thermal stability. Overall, the combination of computational modeling and experimental testing in this work illustrates an effective approach to the rational engineering of industrial enzymes. Despite the significant improvements in catalytic efficiency and stability, some limitations remain. Enzymatic characterization was performed under laboratory conditions using selected substrates, which may not fully reflect the complexity of industrial or environmental conditions. Additionally, large-scale expression and long-term operational performance were not evaluated. Further pilot-scale studies are required to confirm commercial applicability.
