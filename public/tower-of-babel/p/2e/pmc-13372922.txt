Parasites of fish and other aquatic organisms in the Anthropocene
Authors: Miguel Rubio-Godoy, Simonetta Mattiucci, Gerardo Pérez-Ponce de León
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Anthropocene is the most recent period in Earth’s history and is characterized by humankind-driven alterations to all ecosystems. Climate change, pollution, overfishing, invasive species and habitat degradation continue to reshape aquatic ecosystems, and like all living organisms in our changing world, parasites are likely to be affected at different levels. This Collection features research and review papers on some of these topics: parasites co-introduced with their fish hosts illustrate that biological invasions must be studied case-by-case, as some fish hosts lose their parasites upon translocation (enemy release), some pass them on to the invaded ecosystems (overspill) and some acquire –and sometimes amplify– native parasites (spillback), regardless of the time frame, as illustrated in studies of parasites co-introduced from 150 to 15 years ago. Biological information is a powerful tool to predict and prevent the spread of invasive/pathogenic species, and while this Collection provides baseline data for several introduced parasites, it also features practical proposals to control pathogenic/zoonotic species. Climate change and habitat alterations also exert a powerful influence on parasite populations – which likewise, must be studied individually, as some species decline and/or modify their distribution markedly (leading to hybridization and potentially speciation of previously separated taxa), while others benefit from the modified environment, are able to invade new hosts and/or become more infective or pathogenic. Parasites play different ecosystemic roles and are finely-tuned bioindicators of anthropogenic impact in freshwater ecosystems, and this Collection highlights the notion that they should be integrated in public health and conservation initiatives.

Introduction

This Collection, conceived during the XI International Symposium on Fish Parasites (Rubio-Godoy et al. 2025) aimed to compile research papers and reviews, focusing on the general and specific impacts of anthropogenic environmental changes on the biodiversity, ecology, and evolution of parasites of aquatic organisms. Climate change, pollution, overfishing, invasive species and habitat degradation continue to reshape aquatic ecosystems, and like all living organisms in our changing world, parasites are likely to be affected at different levels (Sures et al. 2023; Poulin et al. 2024, 2026; Wood 2025; Pyron et al. 2026; Butterworth et al. 2026) – from changes in ecological parameters like prevalence, abundance and distribution to new adaptive directions in view of increasing anthropogenic pressures.

We were interested in both basic and applied perspectives on the subject: Are host-parasite interactions shifting under environmental stressors? Are the modified distribution ranges of parasites and their hosts resulting in new hosts being infected and/or host switching events? What is the impact of human-translocated, exotic parasites in invaded ecosystems? Are zoonotic parasites and those affecting aquaculture changing – and what species should we keep an eye out for? The articles included in this Collection provide answers to some of these questions.

Co-introduced parasites

Fishes are among the most problematic introduced aquatic organisms given their adverse ecological impacts (Canonico et al. 2005; Ramírez-García et al. 2026) and recently, the impact of some parasites co-introduced with translocated fish has become apparent (e.g., Kuchta et al. 2018; Shinn et al. 2023). In this Collection, several papers involve the analysis of co-introduced parasites and provide both basic information to follow their fate and evaluate/propose methods to limit their impact/spread.

Some fish were translocated in the late XIXth century, like common carp introduced to Mexico and brown trout to Australia. Although both fish established stable populations, their parasitological impact contrasts markedly: while carp co-introduced the Asian fish tapeworm (AFT), Schyzocotyle acheilognathi and this cestode has spilled-over extensively to different fish hosts throughout Mexico (Casanova-Hernández et al. 2025), trout –apparently– did not co-introduce parasites from its ancestral distribution range into Australia – but in contrast carries potentially zoonotic nematodes previously only recorded in marine environments (Shamsi and Pearce 2025).

More recent cases further illustrate the widely different ecological outcomes biological invasions can have. Although channel catfish was introduced in 2010 from North America to Vietnam, it is now commonly infected by the Asian digenean Dollfustrema bagarii, which causes the “off-white grub” disease (Le et al. 2025) and is a clear example of spill-back (Díaz-Morales et al. 2025). Enemy release, where invasive hosts are favored by the absence of their common parasites, may be occurring in the case of twospot livebearer translocated from the Atlantic to the Pacific versant of Mexico (Sánchez-González et al. 2026) and of Gery’s piranha introduced to the Paraná River in Brazil (Michelan et al. 2026). In India, ornamental guppies carry the invasive nematode Camallanus cotti, which could spill over to native fishes (Dhinesh et al. 2026). Thus, these studies identify particular taxa that may be involved in host-parasite ecological interactions following biological invasions and provide “baseline” infection data to inform future studies – with the proviso that a “normal baseline” is probably unattainable and “pristine” ecosystems do not exist (Wood et al. 2026).

These contrasting ecological outcomes exemplify the complexity of biological invasions of hosts and their parasites, corroborate that parasite introductions must be studied case-by-case, and highlight the need for further research to understand factors favoring establishment and spread of invasive pathogens – for instance, use of ecological niche modeling to predict AFT’s future range expansions under climate change scenarios, and to propose priority areas for monitoring, managing, and containing this invasive parasite in Mexico (Casanova-Hernández et al. 2025).

Control of troublesome parasites

Treatments with ionic/nano silver were shown to affect the reproductive dynamics of the monogenean Macrogyrodactylus congolensis, one of the main pathogens of farmed North African catfish, suggesting these compounds could be evaluated to control aquaculture infections (Latief et al. 2025).

Anisakid nematodes are important zoonotic parasites, and papers in this Collection provide basic and applied information to control them. Experimental infections of rainbow trout with Anisakis simplex allowed detailed insights into fish immunity following invasion, suggesting the invading larva can regulate host defenses to promote its survival (Kumas et al. 2025). On the practical side, photodynamic inactivation is evaluated as a non-destructive method to control infective L3 stages in seafood destined for human consumption (Ramos et al. 2026).

In contrast to previous records, a parasitological survey of farmed Murray cod, Australia’s largest freshwater fish, showed that both the target species and co-habiting mosquitofish are only infected by Procamallanus (Spirocamallanus) nematodes (Grundy et al. 2025). This demonstrates that adequate farming and biosecurity practices can successfully limit parasite infections, and that reservoir species like mosquitofish represent a potential infection threat – more so if we consider that mosquitofish is a Neotropical fish which has co-translocated other parasites to areas where it has been introduced, like gyrodactylids (see Sánchez-González et al. 2026).

Shifting interactions

This Collection also features long-term studies in marine environments. Thus, one survey shows that parasite populations and assemblages associated with three stocks of anchovies off the Argentine coast changed significantly between the mid 1990’s and early 2020’s. Over these three decades, most metazoan parasite populations decreased, a trend observed over a broad geographic scale, suggesting large-scale processes like climate change are behind this decrease, rather than short-term, cyclical or random variability (Marcotegui et al. 2026).

In the Arctic, populations of anisakid nematodes infecting fish and seals have likewise fluctuated when contrasting the 1980’s to the 2020’s. An initial parasite population decline was followed by a period of demographic stability and increases in later years –probably reflecting demographic trends of both fish and pinniped hosts over the same period (Belli et al. 2025). A second study recorded adult Anisakis simplex infecting seals, an uncommon finding as this is considered a parasite of toothed whales – this may be an overlooked aspect of this parasite’s life cycle, or an opportunistic infection facilitated/resulting from the changing environmental conditions in the Anthropocene (Cipriani et al. 2025), which may be increasing as these findings confirm a recent report in Danish waters (Kumas et al. 2025).

Parasites play different ecosystemic roles and are finely-tuned bioindicators of anthropogenic impact in freshwater ecosystems, as illustrated by the various and sometimes far-reaching impacts habitat modification has on host-parasite interactions (Sures et al. 2026). Habitat alteration can shift parasites into becoming more transmissible pathogens (Lovy et al. 2026) or make them part of the impacted biodiversity (Carlson et al. 2017), highlighting the notion that parasites should be integrated in public health and conservation initiatives.

With parasite ecology increasingly recognized as a powerful tool to detect and characterize profound changes brought about by the Anthropocene (Poulin et al. 2024), as well as the growing perception that parasite conservation is relevant (Rubio-Godoy and Pérez-Ponce de León 2023; Rubio-Godoy et al. 2026), we hope the papers included in this Collection will contribute to both endeavors by providing examples at different scales, from the intimate and short-term interaction between a host’s immune system and a colonizing parasite, to the long-term modification of marine parasites’ geographical distributions or the genetic composition of discrete populations (Mattiucci et al. 2025), potentially showcasing speciation right before our eyes (Pyron et al. 2026). In this fluctuating world, it is important to be able to assess and characterize change and this Collection provides snapshots of parasite communities today (data baselines) which may inform tomorrow’s assessment of ecosystems, and also provide a reference framework for management/restoration/conservation efforts. Most importantly, we hope this Collection provides the inspiration to continue parasitological research of fish and other aquatic organisms.
