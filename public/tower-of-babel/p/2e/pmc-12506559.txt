Beyond the count: platelet dysfunction profile in pediatric trauma patients
Authors: Ronit Kar, Erin V Feeney, Jack R Killinger, Katrina M Morgan, Devin M Dishong, Katelin C Rahn, Rassam MG Rassam, Grace A Ballentine, Philip C Spinella, Matthew D Neal, Barbara A Gaines, Susan M Shea, Christine M Leeper
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting clinical coagulation assays are inadequate to evaluate posttraumatic platelet dysfunction in children.ObjectivesTo elucidate changes in flow-dependent platelet function after injury in children using a microfluidic assay.MethodsWe enrolled 18 children who presented with highest-acuity trauma activation and 10 healthy children at an academic pediatric center. For the control cohort, outpatient blood samples were collected, and for the trauma cohort, blood was collected in the trauma bay for conventional coagulation tests, thromboelastography, von Willebrand factor A1 domain activity, and a microfluidic assay using a stenotic channel (shear rate = 3500 s−1).ResultsThe trauma cohort characteristics were 67% male, median (IQR) age 6 years (4-12), median (IQR) injury severity score 17 (9-26), and 78% blunt mechanism. In total, 50% required blood transfusion and 11% (2/18) died. While thromboelastography maximum amplitude (59.9 ± 6.1 mm) and platelet counts (306.3 ± 111.1 × 103 cells/μL) were within normal limits, the microfluidic assay revealed significantly lower platelet deposition in the trauma cohort versus the control cohort (1.18 ± 0.25 vs 3.06 ± 0.82 mean fluorescence intensity fold change; P < .0001). Lower levels of platelet deposition correlated with receipt of blood transfusion within 6 hours of arrival (r = −0.44, P = .048). von Willebrand factor A1 domain activity was not different between groups and was not correlated with platelet deposition based on mean fluorescence intensity fold change (R2 < 0.0001).ConclusionPosttraumatic platelet dysfunction was observed by utilizing a microfluidic assay in a pediatric trauma cohort. Further study is needed to understand the underlying mechanisms and significance of posttraumatic platelet dysfunction in children.Keywords: Hemostasis, microfluidics, pediatrics, platelets, trauma-induced coagulopathy

Introduction

Injury is the leading cause of death for children in the United States [1]. Trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC) is the multifactorial postinjury hemostatic, inflammatory, and immune response to injury that is common in severely injured children and is associated with poor outcomes [2]. The pathophysiology of TIC remains poorly understood in this population, and effective clinical tools for diagnosis and treatment are lacking. TIC includes impaired platelet function [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Although studies involving injured adults have reported complex dysregulation in the postinjury platelet response [[8], [9], [10], [11]], investigation into platelet dysfunction specific to pediatric populations is limited. In comparison to adults, a reduction in vitamin K-dependent clotting factors, decreased platelet responsiveness, increased circulating von Willebrand factor (VWF), and impaired fibrinolysis mechanisms have been noted during childhood [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Distinct characterization of TIC in children is therefore necessary [18,19].

In the setting of severe injury and coagulopathy, standard hemostatic assays often fall within normal ranges in children and likely do not accurately reflect platelet function [13,20,21]. Microfluidic assays have simulated blood flow under various conditions and evaluated platelet-driven hemostasis in trauma studies with small numbers of adult patients, demonstrating heterogenous phenotypes and altered platelet function in injured subjects [4,22,23]. In this study, we aimed to assess differences in platelet function attributable to injury in children using a microfluidic model to quantify platelet function under flow. We hypothesized that the pediatric trauma cohort would exhibit altered platelet function compared to a healthy pediatric control cohort.

Methods

Enrollment and clinical data collection

Children (aged <18 years) who presented to a pediatric trauma center with the highest-acuity trauma activation were enrolled (“trauma cohort”). Exclusion criteria were known hemoglobin level <7 g/dL or weight <6.5 kg. Blood was drawn upon arrival to the trauma bay, concurrent with the standard trauma laboratory panel including complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, and international normalized ratio (INR). This cohort was further stratified by detection of any clinical or radiographic injury after a comprehensive trauma evaluation (“injured” vs “noninjured”). Clinical data were collected from the electronic health record, including demographics, injury mechanism, injury severity score (ISS), vital signs, blood product transfusion, and thromboelastography (TEG). Traumatic brain injury was defined as Head Abbreviated Injury Score >2. Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction 2 (PELOD-2) score characterized organ dysfunction [24]. Massive transfusion was defined by activation of the institutional massive transfusion protocol. Shock was defined by the Shock Index, Pediatric Age-Adjusted score for children aged ≥4 years and by age-adjusted hypotension by children <4 years [25]. Discharge disability was defined as discharge to acute care facility or dependence in any domain of the FIM score [26].

The injured and noninjured trauma cohorts were separately compared to a healthy control cohort (“controls”). Controls included children presenting for outpatient magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) who needed venipuncture in advance of planned moderate sedation. Indications for MRI included diagnosis or surveillance of a noninflammatory chronic neurologic condition and high-risk cancer surveillance due to family history. Subjects with pre-existing hypercoagulable conditions or taking antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications were excluded.

Microfluidic assay and VWF activity

Whole blood was collected into a heparin tube by venipuncture and transferred to the laboratory for immediate processing. Aliquoted blood samples were incubated with Human TruStain FcX (1:600; #422302, BioLegend) and anti-CD41 antibody conjugated to Janelia Fluor 646 (1:600; #NB100-2614AF646, Novus Biologicals) for 30 minutes. Blood was filtered and perfused through a collagen-coated (equine fibrillar type I collagen, 1 mg/mL; #P/N 385, Chrono-Log Corp), stenotic microfluidic channel for 5 minutes (stenotic region initial wall shear rate = 3500 s−1) (Figure 1A). Real-time fluorescence microscopy was performed at the region of stenosis to capture fluorescent signals reflective of platelet deposition (Figure 1B) using an AxioObserver 7 inverted microscope, Axiocam 506 camera, and Zen software version 3.6 (Zeiss). Microfluidic experiments were performed in duplicate, and mean values were reported. Platelet deposition was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) fold change over time, with greater MFI fold change corresponding to increased platelet deposition. From this, maximum MFI fold change, reflecting clot strength, and MFI fold change area under the curve, reflecting clot formation kinetics, were determined. VWF A1 domain activity (VWFAc% of norm; REF OPHL03, Siemens) was quantified using a STA Compact Max (Stago Diagnostica) assay specific for GP1ba. Inter- and intra-assay variability of this microfluidic model have been reported previously [27,28]. A small validation study of the effect of specimen transport and time to start of assay was performed on independent samples, which showed no significant impact on the assay result (Supplementary Figure 2).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD, median (IQR), counts, or percentages where indicated. Univariate comparisons were made using Mann–Whitney U-test for comparisons between 2 groups and Kruskal–Wallis test for comparison of >2 groups. For the microfluidics analysis, maximum MFI fold change and area under the curve for MFI are reported as mean ± SD. A subgroup analysis was performed for injured versus noninjured trauma subjects. A Spearman R coefficient assessed correlations between demographics, clinical outcomes, clinical laboratory data, and microfluidics results. Analyses were 2-sided and P < .05 was considered significant. Data handling and statistical analysis were performed using R [29] and GraphPad Prism [30].

Regulatory and ethics

This study was conducted under waiver of informed consent (IRB #22110047).

Results

Cohort characterization

In total, 18 subjects in the trauma cohort were compared to 10 healthy control subjects. The cohorts had similar baseline demographic characteristics (Table 1). Within the trauma cohort, 78% (14/18) had a documented injury after trauma evaluation (“injured” subgroup). The trauma cohort sustained predominantly blunt mechanism injuries (78%), with median (IQR) Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14 (4-15), median (IQR) ISS of 17 (9-26), and 50% (9/18) rate of severe traumatic brain injury (Table 1). On arrival to the trauma bay, 50% (9/18) had age-adjusted tachycardia, and 28% (5/18) met criteria for shock. Prehospital transfusion was noted in only 1 subject and consisted of solely red blood cells (RBCs). In-hospital transfusion within 6 hours (after laboratory blood draw) occurred at a rate of 50% (9/18). Within the cohort of subjects, 55% (5/9) received whole blood plus blood product components (RBCs [5/9], platelets [1/9], and/or fresh frozen plasma [3/9]), 33% (3/9) were transfused whole blood alone, and 11% (1/9) received RBCs alone. The mortality rate was 11% (2/18), and 63% (10/16) of survivors had functional disability. Median (IQR) PELOD-2 score within the first 24 hours was 3 (0-7) (Table 1).

Admission clinical laboratory testing: platelet count, INR, partial thromboplastin time, and TEG values

Platelet counts were within normal limits for all subjects on arrival at 306.3 ± 111.1 × 103 cells/μL (mean ± SD; laboratory normal range, 156-369). Hemoglobin was also within normal limits at 11.5 ± 1.9 (mean±SD, laboratory normal range variable based on age). INR values (mean ± SD) were 1.3 ± 0.4 and partial thromboplastin time values (mean ± SD) were not elevated at 34.9 ± 18.3 seconds. TEG values (mean ± SD) were also typically within normal limits reporting an activated clotting time (ACT) of 105.0 ± 12.4 seconds, R time of 0.85 ± 0.91 minutes, K time of 2.0 ± 0.9 min, an α-angle of 69.0 ± 6.9°, a maximum amplitude of 59.9 ± 6.1 mm, and a lysis at 30 minutes of 0.6 ± 1.0% (Table 2).

Microfluidic evaluation of platelet dysfunction

The microfluidic assay revealed significantly lower platelet deposition (mean ± SD maximum MFI fold change, 1.18 ± 0.25 vs 3.06 ± 0.82, P < .0001) and delayed platelet aggregation kinetics (Figure 1C, D) in the trauma cohort compared with controls. Real-time fluorescence microscopy revealed variation in platelet deposition phenotype, with many trauma subject samples exhibiting impaired platelet adhesion or aggregation. There was no significant difference in platelet deposition between the “injured” and “noninjured” subgroups (mean ± SD maximum MFI fold change, 1.20 ± 0.25 vs 1.08 ± 0.09, P > .99) (Figure 1E).

Platelet deposition in both the trauma and control cohorts showed no association with patient age, ISS, Glasgow Coma Scale score, Shock Index, Pediatric Age-Adjusted score, or other clinical characteristics. Hemoglobin concentration on arrival was not correlated with platelet deposition as measured by maximum MFI fold change (R2 = 0.472, P = .058). Regarding correlation between the various assays, platelet deposition was not associated with either platelet count (slope= 0.0005, R2 = 0.0094) or TEG maximum amplitude (slope = 0.0134, R2 = 0.0294) (Figure 2). A subgroup analysis of injured trauma subjects with ISS <17 compared to subjects with ISS ≥17 did not show a statistically significant difference in platelet deposition; subjects with ISS ≥17 had a mean ± SD maximum MFI fold change of 1.08 ± 0.11 compared with 1.22 ± 0.27 in subjects with ISS <17, P = .20 (Supplemental Figure 1). In terms of correlation with clinical outcomes, lower levels of platelet deposition were correlated with increased receipt of blood transfusion within 6 hours of arrival (r = −0.44, P = .048). No correlations with additional clinical outcomes, including mortality, hospital length of stay, or PELOD-2 score, reached statistical significance.

VWF activity

VWF A1 domain activity was not different between the trauma and control cohorts (P = .22) nor between the injured and noninjured subgroups (Figure 3A–C). VWF activity did not correlate with MFI fold change (R2 < 0.0001) or with any subject characteristic or outcome, including age, sex, blood product transfusion, and mortality (P > .05).

Discussion

In a severely injured pediatric cohort, there was significantly reduced platelet function compared with a healthy control cohort despite normal results on current clinically available hemostatic assays. Platelet dysfunction in our microfluidic model correlated with increased blood product transfusion, suggesting the potential for a clinically significant effect. Our finding that there is platelet dysfunction supports potential use of platelets in pediatric trauma and potential room for discussion regarding best indications for platelet transfusion. Future studies should explore the role of microfluidic models in the diagnosis and treatment of posttraumatic platelet dysfunction in children, as improved detection of platelet dysfunction on point-of-care assays could assist in guiding transfusion decision making.

Current hemostatic assays are often within normal ranges despite severe injury, suggesting a gap in clinically accurate platelet function testing in both children and adults with traumatic injury [31]. In this study, a flow-dependent microfluidic assay allowed for quantitative and qualitative assessment of platelet deposition and bulk platelet-rich clot formation. In addition to the quantitative difference in maximum MFI fold change between the trauma and control cohorts, there were notable qualitative changes in trauma patient samples, including reduced adhesion of platelets and an inability to accumulate larger platelet aggregates without being washed away. Furthermore, there was a correlation between platelet dysfunction and receipt of blood transfusion, although this analysis did not adjust for the many potential confounding characteristics and interventions. In the absence of additional mechanistic data and larger cohorts, the precise etiology of these changes and differentiation between a clinical or subclinical platelet dysfunction phenotype remains unclear. Understanding the significance of posttraumatic platelet dysfunction within the broader spectrum of pediatric TIC warrants further exploration.

More is known regarding posttraumatic platelet dysfunction in adults than in injured children, including prior studies utilizing a microfluidic platform to assess flow-dependent platelet function [4,22,23]. Proposed mechanisms of this observed posttraumatic platelet dysfunction include the dysregulation of the VWF–ADAMTS-13 axis [32], reduced platelet adhesion due to GPIbα/GPVI shedding, increased circulating platelet activation, and impaired aggregation [9]. In contrast to adult cohorts, there was no difference in our study between groups in VWF A1 domain activity based on injury status, nor did VWF A1 domain activity correlate with platelet deposition. While this may be attributable to the limited power of this analysis, it could indicate a difference in underlying TIC mechanisms between children and adults.

The primary limitations of this study are the single-center design and small sample size, which preclude robust adjusted or additional subgroup analyses and broad generalizability. While not all patients in the trauma cohort sustained major injury, this allows unique insight into the spectrum of platelet response via an intrinsic “noninjured” control in addition to the healthy control cohort. Since the healthy controls were patients with an indication for outpatient MRI, some degree of bias may persist. Further, this study was limited to a single time point; longitudinal studies could provide insights into the temporal dynamics of platelet function and coagulopathy.

Conclusion

In this cohort of severely injured children, a microfluidic assay showed significant platelet dysfunction compared to a healthy control cohort. This disparate platelet function in response to trauma was not detected on clinical coagulation assays. These findings highlight the potential for microfluidic assays to provide an informative functional platelet assessment in injured children. Future studies should expand upon these results and explore the unique mechanisms of platelet dysfunction in children. This research will ultimately enhance our understanding of pediatric TIC, with the goal of improving pediatric trauma care by developing effective and targeted interventions.
