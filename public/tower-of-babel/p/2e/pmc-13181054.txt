Factors associated with lapses in care among people living with HIV in South Carolina
Authors: Xueying Yang, Fanghui Shi, Shujie Chen, Gavi Samuel, Bankole Olatosi, Sharon Weissmen, Xiaoming Li, Jiajia Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Utilizing statewide electronic health records (EHR) data, this study aims to assess the factors determining the occurrence of lapses in HIV care among people with HIV (PWH) in South Carolina (SC). All adult (≥ 18 years old) PWH who were diagnosed with HIV between 2006 and 2018 with at least two HIV care encounters and at least 1-year follow-up record were included in the analysis. The outcome, a lapse in care, was defined as a repeated measure of HIV care encounter that occurs over a year following the previous visit. Generalized Estimation Equation models were employed. The study cohort had 8,856 PWH, of which 3,888 (43.9%) had a lapse in HIV care, and 1,405 (36.14%) PWH had multiple lapses. Background characteristics, such as longer duration of HIV diagnosis, being male, and being infected with HIV through injecting drug use or men who have sex with men were associated with a higher risk of a lapse in care. A variety of comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, liver disease, and renal disease, were associated with lower odds of a lapse in HIV care.

Introduction

Successful retention in HIV care is a critical step in the HIV care continuum to improve health outcomes among people with HIV (PWH) and end the HIV epidemic. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines successful retention in HIV care as having two or more viral loads/CD4 tests done at least three months apart in each calendar year1. According to the CDC, about 54% of PWH in the US were retained in care, and 65% were virally suppressed in 20222. In South Carolina (SC), there were approximately 20,000 prevalent cases of HIV, and 52% of them were retained in continuous care in 20222,3. A critical step in the HIV care continuum and decreasing HIV transmission is to reduce the number of PWH who are not in care (i.e., lapse in care), which is essential for achieving and maintaining viral suppression1,4. A lapse in care refers to the absence of a second HIV care encounter within one year of the previous encounter5. Studies have found that patients with viral suppression who are retained in care are less likely to observe a viral rebound and less likely to transmit HIV6,7. Identifying PWH who are at risk for lapse in care is a strategy to improve retention in care8.

Despite initiatives aimed at improving retention in HIV care for PWH, some individuals experience lapses in care due to patient-related factors like HIV stigma, substance abuse, burdens of taking care of family, mental health, transportation, or comorbid conditions. A 2023 study by Mason et al. aimed to design a predictive model that would assist in identifying patients at risk of lapses in HIV care5 using data from the Chicago Area Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Network9. In this predictive model study, they showed that Hispanic ethnicity was a top predictor of a lapse in HIV care compared to other ethnic groups; while age, type of health insurance coverage, and chronic comorbidities were other important predictors of lapses in HIV care5. A longitudinal study by Westergaard showed higher odds of lapses in HIV care among people who inject drugs (PWID) compared to non-PWID. However, their definition of lapse in HIV care was based on a six-month gap from the last care visit10. Other factors, such as higher CD4 cell count and younger age, were associated with increased risk of lapse in care in a study by Napravnik11. Studies in low and middle-income countries have found associations of sex, living in rural or difficult-to-reach areas, and HIV transmission category with lapse in care12–15.

Chronic comorbid conditions in PWH and their association with lapses in care have not been well studied. There remains a gap in knowledge on the importance and/or impact of other comorbidities like cardiovascular disorders, dementia, liver disease, peptic ulcer disease, renal disease, and rheumatic disease on lapses in HIV care. Our study aims to assess the association of lapses in care with demographic characteristics of PWH, their comorbidities, and HIV-associated characteristics.

Results

From 2006 to 2018, a total of 9,653 individuals were newly diagnosed with HIV in South Carolina, and 8,856 of them had at least two HIV care encounters. Of these, 3,888 (43.9%) had at least one lapse in care throughout the follow-up. The study population was mostly male (75.8%) and black race (69.9%). The proportion with lapse in care was highest among individuals diagnosed at younger ages (18–34, 50.5%) compared with those diagnosed at 35–54 (38.6%) and ≥ 55 (28.4%) (p < 0.0001). By race/ethnicity, black individuals had a higher prevalence of lapse in care (45.9%) than White individuals (40.7%), while Hispanic individuals had a lower prevalence (31.5%) (p < 0.0001). Among the PWH with a lapse in care, 24.90% had low baseline CD4 count records (< 200 cells/mm3), and 24.9% achieved suppressed VL (< 200 copeis/ml). There was a smaller proportion of PWH with a lapse in care with comorbid diagnosis at baseline (chronic pulmonary disease: 40.1% vs. 44.7%; diabetes: 35.0% vs. 44.5%; alcohol use: 40.2% vs. 44.3%; tobacco use: 41.1% vs. 45.4%) (Table 1).

Being diagnosed with HIV at an older age, 35–54 years (aOR = 0.72; 95% CI = 0.67–0.79) and 55 + years (aOR = 0.75; 95% CI = 0.64–0.88) was associated with lower odds for lapses in care compared to being diagnosed between 18 and 34 years of age. Significantly higher odds for lapses in care were observed to occur among males (aOR = 1.12; 95% CI = 1.01–1.26) compared to females, HIV risk category of MSM (aOR = 1.235; 95% CI = 1.09–1.40), IDU/MSM (aOR = 1.21; 95% CI = 1.01–1.46) compared to the heterosexual risk group (Table 2). Hispanics (aOR = 0.82; 95% CI = 0.67–0.99) had lower odds of lapse in care than the white population. Patients with a recent CD4 count greater than 200 cells/mm3 (200–350 cells/mm3; aOR = 0.77, 95% CI = 0.70–0.85) and (> 350 cells/mm3; aOR = 0.73, 95% CI = 0.67–0.79) and with suppressed VL (aOR = 0.21, 95% CI = 0.19–0.22) had lower odds of lapse in care. Patients with comorbidities such as cancer (aOR = 0.79; 95% CI = 0.64–0.97), cardiovascular disease (aOR = 0.77; 95% CI = 0.66–0.90), chronic pulmonary disease (aOR = 0.88; 95% CI = 0.81–0.96), diabetes (aOR = 0.81, 95% CI = 0.71–0.93), liver disease (aOR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.62–0.87), renal disease (aOR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.55–0.78) had lower odds of lapsing in HIV care. Substance use was not associated with lapses in care.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to examine lapse in HIV care status in a large statewide HIV cohort. We were able to define and measure lapse in care status in a cohort of PWH and longitudinally investigate the association between various factors associated with this lapse in care. Previous studies of lapse in HIV care showed similar risk factors, such as age and chronic comorbidities, were associated with increased risk for lapse in HIV care5,10,11. We found an association between demographic factors (age, gender, race), HIV-associated factors (HIV transmission mode, recent CD4 tests, viral suppression), comorbidities (cancer, cardiovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes, liver disease, and renal disease), and lapse in HIV care.

In this study population, age > 35 years at HIV diagnosis was a protective factor against lapse in HIV care; an observation also reported by other researchers13,14,16. Because HIV management is a lifetime commitment, it is essential that young PWH be carefully monitored and assisted to ensure continuous engagement in care. A study by Hong in Namibia had contrasting results that showed that the younger age group had lower odds of lapse in care12. This difference could be attributed to a short follow-up time of their cohort and differences in the study population.

We showed that being male was a risk factor for lapse in care, an observation that Hong et al. also found12. However, other studies showed conflicting results14,16. The lack of consistency between studies may be attributed to the population characteristics. In comparison to the white population, we showed that black people and Hispanics in SC had lower odds of experiencing lapse in care, an observation reported by Mason in other regions5. However, being a young black patient with HIV was shown to be a risk factor for lapses in care11. Instead of considering race alone as a risk factor, the interaction of age and race may be worth further investigation.

We also observed that PWH in SC who acquired HIV through MSM and IDU were more likely to have lapse in care compared to those whose HIV risk was due to heterosexual transmission. Similar results were reported by other studies that showed an increased risk for lapse from care among PWH who are MSM or IDUs17. It is possible that PWH who are IDUs and are MSM experience these lapses likely because of stigma and community perspective about their lifestyle and the intersection between these two characteristics; studies have shown that those who engage in MSM are also likely to be substance users/abusers18. The stigma and discrimination experienced by this population are potential barriers to HIV testing and access to HIV care.

Patient characteristics like alcohol consumption, tobacco, cannabis, cocaine, and opioid use were not significantly associated with lapses in care; Mason et al. showed that these were not significant predictors of lapses in care during their prediction model development for lapses in care5. Comorbidities like cancer, cardiovascular disease, cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, liver disease, and renal disease were protective factors against lapses in care. This could be explained by the fact that these patients received continuous care for both chronic diseases and HIV at the same time, thus reducing the likelihood of lapses in care.

Limitations

The diagnosis of comorbidities was defined based on EHR data; there was a potential for bias through underreporting of diagnoses. The definition of lapse in care was adapted to our data since HIV lab tests could not be identified for each healthcare visit in our data. The HIV healthcare encounter measure might not be accurate.

Conclusion

We utilized longitudinal electronic health record (EHR) data obtained from PWH receiving care in SC in 2006–2018, a strength of the analysis that reduces bias from limited follow-up time. This will assist clinicians and HIV care providers in implementing prevention strategies that ensure uninterrupted care among at-risk PWH. It is important to monitor other factors like recreational drug use and abuse, as there is a potential for significant interaction with other factors that affect lapse in care. Gaps still exist in our understanding of predictors significantly associated with lapse in care among PWH. Demographic and HIV-related patient factors are important factors to consider when managing PWH to prevent lapses in care. HIV-related factors like viral suppression, CD4 cell counts, and comorbidities still need to be researched more to fully explain the reasons behind lapses in care in the SC and US populations.

Methods

Study design and data sources

The current study is a statewide observational, retrospective cohort study using SC EHR data to examine factors associated with lapses in HIV care among people with HIV (PWH). We extracted data, including HIV diagnosis date, demographics information (age, gender, race, residence), HIV transmission category, laboratory tests, from SC Enhanced HIV/AIDS Reporting System (eHARS), and healthcare records from the uniform payer system. SC eHARS is a laboratory-based reporting system to which all statewide CD4 and VL tests have been mandatorily reported since January 1, 2004. The identifiable link from eHARS was sent to the SC Revenue and Fiscal Affairs Office (RFA), which served as the honest broker for the linkage of all diagnoses in the Uniform Billing form (UB-92) format from all emergency departments, hospital inpatient facilities, ambulatory-care facilities, and outpatient surgery facilities in SC. For data security, the RFA used de-identified system-generated ID numbers to ensure confidentiality but allowed the study to conduct data mining at both the individual and aggregated levels. Access to eHARS and UB-92 data required approval from South Carolina state agencies and the relevant data providers. This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies.

Participants

A total of 9,653 adult PWH were identified in SC between 2006 and 2018 from the SC eHARS. The final study cohort consisted of 8,856 PWH with at least two HIV care encounters after excluding 797 individuals who had no or only one HIV care encounter. HIV care encounters were defined as healthcare visits with an HIV provider or had a CD4 or VL lab test record. Encounters within 7 days were considered as a single encounter to avoid counting repeatedly. Our study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of South Carolina and relevant SC state agencies (#Pro00109797). Informed consent to participate is not required by US law when research uses secondary data under category 4 Federal Policy for the Protection of Human Subjects (45 CFR 46). This study was approved by the University of South Carolina IRB as not human subjects and declared non-exempt under category 4.

Variables

The outcome of interest was lapse in care status. Lapse of care was defined as a repeated HIV care encounter occurs over one year from the last visit. Regarding predictors of interest, we obtained and categorized age at HIV diagnosis (i.e. 18–34, 35–54, 55+), sex (i.e. male, female), race (i.e. White, Black, Hispanic, Other/Unknown), transmission mode (i.e. Heterosexual; men who have sex with men [MSM], injecting drug use [IDU], Other), and residence (i.e. Rural, Urban). Residence status is categorized as rural or urban based on the list of rural counties from the Office of Rural Health Policy19. Time varying factors, the most recent CD4 counts (< 200 cells/mm3, 200–350 cells/mm3, > 350cells/mm3, Unknown), the most recent viral load (VL) (> 200 copies/ml; or < = 200 copies/ml; Unknown), and comorbidities or substance uses, were considered before each lapse in care. Comorbidities and substance use were determined based on ICD-9/10 codes (Cancer, Cardiovascular disease, Chronic pulmonary disease, Dementia, Diabetes, Hemiplegia or paraplegia, Liver disease, Peptic ulcer disease, Renal disease, Rheumatic disease, Alcohol Use, Tobacco Use, Cannabis, Cocaine, Opioid, Other Substance use).

Statistical analysis

To describe the distribution of at least one lapse in care across the characteristics, we report the frequency and percent of at least one lapse in care by characteristics and use the chi-square test. A generalized estimation equation (GEE) was used to explore the factors associated with lapses in care. We fit the model with different correlation structures, first-order autoregressive (AR(1)), and compound symmetry (CS) structures, and then report the results with a smaller QIC. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 as the level of statistical significance. Data cleaning and analysis were performed by using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).
