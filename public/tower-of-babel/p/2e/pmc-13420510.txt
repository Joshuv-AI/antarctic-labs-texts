A Dynamic Prediction Study of Persistent Nine-valent Human Papillomavirus Infection Among HIV-negative Men Who Have Sex With Men in Urumqi, China: a Longitudinal and Time-to-event-based Joint Model
Authors: Guozhen Zhang, Juyuan Bian, Heng Yang, Wenjie Zhang, Wenhui Yu, Henry S Lynn, Jianghong Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPersistent infection with 9-valent human papillomavirus (9v-HPV) is a major risk factor for HPV-related cancers among men who have sex with men (MSM). This study aimed to develop a dynamic prediction model to assess whether longitudinal sexual-behavior-related and exposure-related indicators could predict persistent 9vHPV infection among human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1)-negative MSM.MethodBetween 2016 and 2023, HIV-negative MSM in Urumqi, China, were followed every 6 months. Baseline questionnaires and anal swabs were collected, and HPV genotyping was performed using an assay detecting 37 HPV genotypes. The primary outcome was persistent infection with 1 or more of the 9 genotypes covered by the 9-valent HPV vaccine. Persistent 9vHPV infection was defined as detection of at least one of these 9 genotypes on 2 or more consecutive visits after a negative baseline. Key indicators included same-sex partner number, anal intercourse frequency, condom use, commercial sex, club drug use, partner-seeking venues, and sexual role. A Bayesian joint model integrating longitudinal sexual-behavior-related indicators and time-to-event outcomes was developed. Predictive accuracy was assessed using the time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) with internal validation.ResultsAmong 704 MSM, factors including employment, commercial sex, receptive anal intercourse, and versatile roles were associated with increased persistence risk. Higher number of sexual partners (HR = 1.95) and more frequent anal intercourse (HR = 2.04) were strong predictors. The partner-based model demonstrated superior predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.727).ConclusionsThe Bayesian joint model provides accurate dynamic predictions of persistent 9v-HPV infection in MSM, supporting personalized prevention strategies.

Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection is a common sexually transmitted disease worldwide [1, 2]. Most infections are asymptomatic and can clear spontaneously, but persistent HPV infection may lead to cervical, oropharyngeal, anal, and vulvar cancers [3–6]. Men who have sex with men (MSM) represent an important population for HPV prevention because of the high burden of anal HPV DNA infection and HPV-related anal disease [7, 8]. Although some studies have reported comparable seroprevalence of quadrivalent HPV vaccine types (HPV-6/11/16/18) between MSM and women [9], seroprevalence reflects cumulative immune response and differs from site-specific HPV DNA detection and persistent infection. Therefore, longitudinal studies focusing on persistent anal HPV infection among MSM are needed to better understand infection dynamics, identify predictors of persistence, and inform targeted prevention strategies.

Despite many studies on the epidemiology and risk factors of HPV infection, predicting persistent infection in MSM remains challenging [10, 11]. The natural history of HPV infection is complex, with marked individual variation in infection duration and multiple influencing factors, including sexual behavior patterns, immune status, and viral subtypes [12]. Most current evidence comes from cross-sectional studies, which capture only a snapshot of infection and cannot fully reflect the dynamic process of persistent infection or its temporal determinants [13–15]. Limited understanding of HPV’s natural history constrains effective intervention, and traditional statistical methods struggle with longitudinal and time-to-event data. Joint modeling of longitudinal and survival data can account for censoring [16, 17], reduce bias, and improve prediction [18].

Therefore, this study constructed a joint model [19] based on longitudinal and time-to-event data to predict the risk of persistent HPV infection in MSM. By integrating behavioral characteristics, infection status, and viral subtypes and applying time-to-event analysis, the model aims to identify key predictors of persistent infection, provide new strategies for prevention and control of HPV among MSM, and ultimately reduce the burden of HPV-related diseases. The findings may also inform prediction of other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and contribute to optimizing public health strategies.

METHODS

Enrollment and Follow-Up of Study Participants

The snowballing method recruited MSM volunteers in Urumqi City from April 2016 to February 2023. Participants were recruited using snowball sampling through peer referral. Participants were not specifically instructed to recruit their sexual partners; however, because snowball sampling may recruit individuals from the same social or sexual networks, potential network clustering cannot be completely excluded. Recruitment-chain identifiers and sexual partner network information were not collected, and therefore network-level dependence could not be explicitly modeled. Inclusion criteria: 18–65 years old; same-sex sexual behavior within 6 months; signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria: HIV-positive; major diseases, hematopoietic dysfunction, bleeding tendency, or other bleeding disorders; suffering from autoimmune diseases; and chronic diseases such as malignant tumors. 1369 people were recruited for the cohort, and 704 people were included in the analysis; the specific process is shown in Figure 1.

Flowchart for analytic dataset selection.

Variable Measurement

After the study subjects signed the informed consent form, their socio-demographic information, sexual behavior-related information, and health-related information were collected through questionnaires. The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from a previously published version [20].

Anal Swab Collection and Human Papillomavirus DNA Typing Test

Specimens were collected 3–5 cm deep into the anus of the study subjects with a saline-moistened cotton swab and preserved in a cytostatic solution for subsequent HPV typing. Laboratory testing was performed using 37 typing HPV test kits (PCR + perfusion hybridization, Guangdong Compaq Biotechnology Co., Ltd) for infection determination and typing identification, with each specific probe membrane strip detecting 37 HPV genotypes simultaneously. Although the assay detected 37 HPV genotypes, the present analysis focused only on the 9 genotypes covered by the 9-valent HPV vaccine: HPV-6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58.

9-valent HPV infection: detection of positive DNA for ≥1 of HPV-6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58. Persistent 9-valent HPV infection: all negative HPV DNA tests at the previous follow-up visit, and detection of ≥1 DNA-positive of the 9-valent HPV types on at least 2 consecutive occasions during the subsequent follow-up visit.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed using R 4.2.2 software, describing the measurements with M (Q1, Q3) and the categorical variables with frequency counts and constitutive ratios.

Joint longitudinal and time-to-event models: consisted of linear mixed-effects models (longitudinal submodel) and Cox proportional hazards regression models (survival submodel). In particular, the linear mixed-effects model described the longitudinal trajectories of the number of same-sex partners in the last 6 months and the number of anal intercourse in the last 1 week and their relationships with time-dependent covariates, respectively. A Cox proportional hazards model was applied to assess factors associated with persistent 9vHPV infection, as defined above. Time-varying covariates: age, same-sex commercial sex, use of club drugs within 6 months, and seeking a sexual partner in a bath. Baseline covariates: marital status, pattern of sexual behavior, and history of sexually transmitted diseases. Longitudinal indicators: number of same-sex sexual partners in the past 6 months, number of anal sex sessions in the past week. Joint models were fitted using the JMbayes package in R (version 4.2.2) [10], and Bayesian estimation was used for model estimation. Two-sided tests were conducted at a test level of α = .05.

Model predictive efficacy: The area under the characteristic curve (AUC) of time-dependent subjects was used to evaluate the joint model's predictive ability. The joint model's accuracy in predicting the risk of 9-valent HPV infection at different times during the follow-up period was evaluated by calculating the AUC values of the study subjects at different follow-up time windows.

Individual dynamic prediction: One participant with persistent 9vHPV infection and 1 participant without persistent 9vHPV infection were randomly selected for individual dynamic prediction.

RESULTS

Baseline Demographic and Sexual Behavioral Characteristics of the Study Population

This study included 704 MSM, including 552 participants without persistent 9vHPV infection and 152 participants with persistent 9vHPV infection. The median age was 28 years in both groups. Overall, 51.7% were registered permanent residents of Urumqi and 87.2% were of Han ethnicity. The median age at first sexual experience was 20 years, and the median number of sexual partners in the past 6 months was 2 in both groups. Regarding sexual behavior, 52.1% were only or mainly insertive, 37.9% were only or mainly receptive, and 9.9% reported versatile roles. During the past week, 84.4% had anal intercourse, and 58.5% reported never using condoms during anal intercourse. Transactional sexual behavior, club drug use in the past 6 months, and seeking partners in public venues were reported by 8.0%, 26.0%, and 85.2% of participants, respectively. Longitudinal Trajectory of the Two Indicators

Longitudinal trajectories of MSM in terms of the number of same-sex partners in the last 6 months and the logarithm of the number of anal sex encounters in the last 1 week: the number of same-sex partners in the last 6 months in the group of persistently infected with 9-valent HPV was more volatile, with a decreasing and then increasing trend in the average trajectory, in contrast, the group of uninfected had less volatility, and the number of same-sex partners in the last 6 months of the majority of MSM was maintained at a relatively low level, with a relatively smooth trajectory of the change in the average value. The mean trajectory of MSM in the newly infected group was more volatile in terms of the number of anal intercourse in the last 1 week, showing an increasing, then decreasing, then increasing trend. The trajectory for the group with no persistent infection showed a steady decline, but not by much (Figure 2).

Longitudinal trajectory of sexual partner count in the past 6 months and frequency of sexual encounters in the past week among MSM.

Joint Model for Number of Same-Sex Partners

In the longitudinal submodel, older age (β = .016), club drug use in the past 6 months (β = .176), and occasional condom use (β = .095) were each associated with a higher number of same-sex partners, whereas frequent condom use was associated with fewer partners (β = −.247).

In the survival submodel, employment (HR = 1.758), commercial sex with male partners (HR = 3.500), sex of sexual partners: male versus female (ref) (HR = 1.441), receptive anal intercourse (HR = 1.914), and versatile role (insertive and receptive) (HR = 2.858) were independent risk factors for persistent 9-valent HPV infection.

The shared parameter linking the 2 submodels was significant (HR = 1.952), indicating that each 1-unit increase in the log number of same-sex partners (ie, multiplying the count by e≈2.72) was associated with a 95% higher risk of persistent infection.

Joint Model for Number of Anal Sex Encounters

No variables in the longitudinal submodel reached statistical significance (all P > .05).

In the survival submodel, employment (HR = 1.677), commercial sex with male partners (HR = 3.582), sex of sexual partners: male versus female (ref) (HR = 1.473), receptive anal intercourse (HR = 1.799), and versatile role (HR = 2.695) were associated with increased risk of persistent infection.

The shared parameter was also significant (HR = 2.039), suggesting that each 1-unit increase in the log number of anal sex encounters (ie, multiplying the count by e≈2.72) conferred a 104% higher risk of persistent infection (Table 2).

Evaluation of the Accuracy of Two Joint Prediction Models

Comparison of the prediction efficacy of the joint models based on different longitudinal indicators: The prediction accuracy of the longitudinal indicators for the risk of developing persistent 9-valent HPV infection was compared by calculating the time-dependent AUC values of the study subjects at months 18, 54, and 70, and the mean value of the AUC was calculated at the same time to evaluate the prediction ability of each model comprehensively. In the follow-up time of (t, t + Δt), the mean values of the time-dependent AUC of the joint model of the number of same-sex partners in the last 6 months and the joint model of the number of anal intercourse in the last week were calculated to be 0.727 and 0.699, respectively. The results showed that the joint model of the number of same-sex partners in the last 6 months had a high predictive efficacy (Table 3). It should be noted that the extremely high AUC values observed at 70 months with Δt = 24 may be partly due to the small sample size or limited number of events at that time window, and should be interpreted with caution.

Validation of Individual Dynamic Prediction Based on Joint Prediction Models

Based on the AUC evaluation results, we randomly selected 1 uninfected and 1 infected individual from the model's cohort based on the number of sexual partners for internal validation.

Individual 230: entered the cohort at age 33, initial sexual activity began at age 15, never married, reported club drug use in the past 6 months, and has been followed for 68.7 months with no persistent infection with any of the nine 9vHPV vaccine-covered genotypes. During the second follow-up, the risk of persistent 9-valent HPV infection sharply increased. If the number of sexual partners remains the same at that time, the predicted probability of a new 9-valent HPV infection by month 60 is 100%. However, in the third follow-up, as the number of sexual partners decreased, the risk of persistent 9-valent HPV infection decreased, which aligns with actual observations (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Individual 810: entered the cohort at age 34, initial sexual activity began at age 18, never married, reported club drug use in the past 6 months, and has been followed for 37.5 months with persistent infection with HPV-16. The prediction results indicate that Individual 810 has maintained a status of having multiple sexual partners for a prolonged period. Following the second follow-up, the prediction shows that their risk of new 9-valent HPV infection sharply increases, with an estimated probability of about 60% for new infection by month 40, corresponding with the sustained 9-valent HPV infection results at month 37 (Supplementary Fig. 2).

DISCUSSION

This study constructed a joint model based on 2 different longitudinal indicators to predict the risk of persistent 9v-HPV infection among MSM and to compare the predictive efficacy of different models. The results indicate that the longitudinal trajectories of the 2 indicators are associated with the risk of persistent 9v-HPV infection. The joint model based on the number of same-sex partners in the past 6 months showed strong predictive power, with a mean AUC over time of 0.727 (range 0.641 to 0.999), which is comparable to a predictive model based on a study that used a machine learning algorithm, Random Forest, to identify anal HPV infection status (AUC = 0.77) [21]. This model slightly outperformed some studies on the prediction of STIs in MSM [22, 23]. It may be because all of these studies used traditional statistical models, such as the COX regression model, Poisson regression, which uses only the last available observation, losing information, leading to insufficient predictive accuracy.

In the longitudinal submodel, we found that increasing age was significantly associated with increased same-sex partners in the past 6 months (β = .016, 95% CI: .008–.022). This finding contrasts with a study of African American MSM in Philadelphia, USA, which reported that the probability of having multiple sexual partners decreases with age, possibly due to sample selection bias and cultural differences [24]. Age was also found to be an influential factor for HPV infection in the earlier part of this study, with younger individuals being more susceptible [20]. These findings suggest that age may still play a role in HPV infection dynamics among MSM. Age was included in the present model as a demographic covariate rather than a behavioral factor. Its association with longitudinal behavioral trajectories may reflect differences in cumulative exposure, sexual network patterns, partnership characteristics, and potentially age-related immune susceptibility. However, detailed immunological markers and long-term partnership information were not available in this study; therefore, the mechanisms underlying age-related differences could not be fully explored.

Our study found that use of the club drugs mentioned by rush et al (β = .176, 95% CI: .134–.220) and occasional condom use (β=.095, 95% CI: .049–.140) significantly increased the number of same-sex partners. This is consistent with the results of a Meta-analysis of the association between substance use and the number of sexual partners among MSM, suggesting that substance use may lead to more permissive sexual behavior and an increase in the number of sexual partners, thereby increasing the risk of HPV infection [25]. A study in Taizhou City, China, showed that MSM substance abuse was positively associated with high-risk sexual behavior [26]. MSM who used club drugs were more likely to engage in unprotected sexual activity [27]. Multiple studies have shown that the use of club drugs is a high-risk factor for HPV and HIV infection in MSM [28, 29]. Therefore, promoting the elimination of club drug use is an effective measure to prevent HPV infection in MSM.

Our analysis indicated that employment may be associated with an increased risk of persistent 9v-HPV infection in MSM (HR = 1.758, 95% CI: 1.135–2.682). This result is consistent with findings from studies of HPV infection among MSM and transgender women in Southeast Asia, who identified employment status as a risk factor for persistent HPV infection among MSM [30]. Meanwhile, our study found that MSM engaging in commercial sex work had a higher risk of new infections (HR = 3.500, 95% CI: 1.827–6.402), which is consistent with the conclusions of Malagón et al [31]. that MSM engaging in commercial sex may have higher exposure to diverse sexual networks and repeated HPV exposure, thereby increasing the risk of persistent infection. Studies have shown that MSM who are keen to seek sexual partners in public places such as bathrooms tend to be more likely to engage in high-risk sexual behaviors, such as unprotected anal sex, and have a higher likelihood of using club drugs. This combination of risky sexual behavior and club drug use significantly increases their risk of contracting STIs) such as HIV and HPV [32, 33].

Additionally, our findings regarding types of sexual behavior are consistent with existing literature. Our study shows that receptive anal intercourse (HR = 1.914, 95% CI: 1.351–5.051) significantly increases the risk of persistent 9v-HPV infection. Multiple studies have indicated this risk factor; a repeated cross-sectional study in the Netherlands showed that receptive anal intercourse is the most substantial risk factor for MSM infection with HPV-16 and HPV-18 [34]. A study conducted in San Francisco indicated that, among male-male sexual behaviors, individuals engaging in receptive anal intercourse were more susceptible to perianal mucosal injuries, which significantly increased the risk of HPV infection. Due to the structural characteristics of the rectal mucosa, it is relatively fragile and has limited natural lubrication, making it prone to microtraumas during sexual activity. These subtle injuries allow the human papillomavirus and other STIs to invade [35].

Notably, the significance of the shared parameter in our joint model (P < .001) suggests a strong correlation between an increase in the number of same-sex partners in the past 6 months and the risk of 9v-HPV persistent infection. For each 1-unit increase in the log-transformed number of same-sex partners, corresponding to an approximately 2.72-fold increase in partner count, the risk of persistent HPV infection increased by 95.2%. (HR = 1.952, 95% CI: 1.392–2.671), which further elucidates the mechanism of HPV transmission. Many studies have emphasized this risk factor, suggesting that an increase in the number of MSM same-sex partners raises the probability of contact with an infected person, thereby increasing the risk of persistent HPV infection [10, 36–38]. Promoting safer sex practices, especially consistent condom use among individuals with multiple partners, is an important measure to reduce HPV transmission. Similarly, there was a strong correlation between the number of anal sex sessions in the last week and the risk of persistent 9v-HPV infection (P < .05). For each increase in anal sex sessions in the last week among MSM, the risk of persistent 9vHPV infection increased by 103.9% (HR = 2.039, 95% CI: 1.108–3.487). The rectal mucosa is relatively fragile and has limited natural lubrication, making it susceptible to microtrauma during anal sex. These microtraumas provide a portal of entry for HPV and other STIs [33]. Having anal sex multiple times a week significantly increases the risk of contracting HPV. Advising MSM to wear condoms during anal sex can be effective in preventing HPV infection.

Limitations and Strengths of the Study

This study leverages a Bayesian joint longitudinal–survival framework to link longitudinal behavioral trajectories with persistent 9vHPV infection, reducing information loss from last-observation analyses and enabling individualized dynamic prediction. The relatively long follow-up, clinically meaningful endpoint of persistent infection, and focus on an under-represented HIV-negative MSM population in Urumqi enhance the relevance of the study. In addition, time-dependent AUCs were used to characterize predictive performance across follow-up windows, and the identified predictors correspond to potentially actionable prevention strategies.

Several limitations should be considered. First, sexual behavior and substance use were self-reported and therefore prone to recall and social-desirability bias. Underreporting of stigmatized or high-risk behaviors, such as multiple sexual partners, commercial sex, club drug use, or inconsistent condom use, may have led to exposure misclassification and attenuation of the associations between exposure-related indicators and persistent 9vHPV infection. Overreporting, if present, could have inflated behavioral exposure levels and affected model calibration. However, the infection outcome was based on laboratory HPV DNA testing rather than self-report, reducing the likelihood of outcome misclassification.

Second, although behavioral and exposure-related indicators may contribute to repeated HPV exposure, reinfection, and local mucosal microtrauma, persistent HPV infection also depends on host immune clearance and viral biological characteristics. Detailed immunological and virological markers, such as CD4/CD8 profiles, cytokine levels, HPV viral load, and type-specific viral persistence patterns, were not available. Therefore, the model should be interpreted as a dynamic prediction model based mainly on demographic, sexual-behavior-related, and exposure-related indicators, rather than a mechanistic model fully explaining HPV persistence.

Third, the use of snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias and potential correlation among participants from the same social or sexual networks. Because recruitment-chain and sexual network identifiers were unavailable, we could not adjust for network clustering. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as cohort-based predictive associations rather than population-representative estimates.

Fourth, external generalizability is restricted by the single-region study setting. Model assumptions, including proportional hazards, mixed-model distributional forms, shared-parameter structure, and prior specifications, may influence estimates. Missingness and irregular measurement imply a missing-at-random assumption that may not fully hold. In addition, extremely high AUCs at late prediction windows likely reflect small sample sizes or limited numbers of events and should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, potential competing risks, such as incident HIV or other STIs, and limited events per parameter may raise concerns about overfitting.

Future studies should validate this model in external cohorts and incorporate immunological markers, virological indicators, partnership stability, educational background, and other lifestyle-related factors to improve prediction and clarify the mechanisms of persistent 9vHPV infection.

Conclusion

The Bayesian joint model based on longitudinal behavioral trajectories demonstrated high accuracy in predicting persistent 9v-HPV infection among MSM in Urumqi. Employment, same-sex commercial behavior, and specific sexual practices (receptive and versatile roles) were significantly associated with persistence risk. These findings highlight the importance of integrating behavioral and clinical factors into predictive modeling to support individualized risk assessment. They also provide a scientific basis for promoting regular HPV screening, timely vaccination, and targeted behavioral interventions in high-risk populations.
