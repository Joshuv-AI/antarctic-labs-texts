The utility of Ocimum basilicum, Perilla frutescens, and Mentha spicata essential oils to control larvae and engorged female Rhipicephalus sanguineus ticks on dogs
Authors: Nguyen Van VUI, Nguyen Thuy LINH, Nguyen Thi Kim QUYEN, Kim NANG, Ho Van VIET
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The study evaluated the larvicidal and reproductive effects of essential oils from Ocimum basilicum, Perilla frutescens, and Mentha spicata on engorged female Rhipicephalus sanguineus ticks. Adult immersion and larval packet assays were used to evaluate their larvicidal efficacy and impact on tick reproductive abilities. For tick larvae, Perilla frutescens essential oil had the highest 50% lethal dose of 1.89 mg/mL, followed by Ocimum basilicum at 1.58 mg/mL, and Mentha spicata at 1.07 mg/mL. Regarding adult ticks, although Mentha spicata essential oil had the lowest reduced oviposition value, it showed the highest product efficacy. Overall, all tested essential oils effectively killed tick larvae and influenced the reproductive capacity of engorged female ticks, with Mentha spicata essential oil being the most potent.

Rhipicephalus sanguineus, a brown tick prevalent in dogs, threatens the health of both canines and humans, through its action as a vector for a variety of blood-borne parasitic diseases in dogs, including Ehrlichia canis, Hepatozoon canis, and Babesia canis [7]. Additionally, it can transmit multiple bacteria to humans, including Rickettsia spp., which causes Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever in Europe (Rickettsia conorii) and the USA (Rickettsia rickettsii) [19]. To mitigate the harmful effects of Rhipicephalus sanguineus ticks, synthetic chemicals are often used on dogs and in their environment to mitigate the harmful effects of R. sanguineus ticks. However, these treatments are frequently misapplied, with incorrect dosages and improper treatment durations, which can adversely affect the environmental health of both humans and animals [6], and have led to the development of tick resistance to these chemicals. Numerous studies have previously documented tick resistance to a variety of drugs that are currently used worldwide [3, 9, 22]. Consequently, it is essential to discover new treatments to replace current ones, to ensure the control of tick damage while protecting the health of pets and humans. Essential oils derived from plants are effective against tick larvae and adult ticks [15, 24].

In Vietnam, various aromatic herbs, such as Ocimum basilicum, Perilla frutescens, and Mentha spicata are commonly used as food sources. These herbs are not only popular as spices in various household dishes, but are also widely used for essential oil extraction for medicinal purposes. The chemical composition of these essential oils primarily comprises bioactive compounds like Linalool, Cineole, Estragole, Eugenol, 1,8-Cineole, Perillaldehyde, Limonene, β-Caryophyllene, and Carvone [11, 23]. Numerous studies have further demonstrated that these bioactive compounds can inhibit bacterial growth [23], kill mites [26] and ticks [Rhipicephalus microplus [14], Rhipicephalus sanguineus [24]]. Therefore, the oils extracted from these aromatic herbs may potentially to kill ticks parasitizing dogs. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of essential oils extracted from Ocimum basilicum, Perilla frutescens, and Mentha spicata in eliminating Rhipicephalus sanguineus in dogs.

Ocimum basilicum, Perilla frutescens, and Mentha spicata plants were collected from an herb garden in Tra Vinh Province, Vietnam, in March 2023, where they were grown without pesticides or growth stimulants. All herbs were identified by the Department of Crop Science at Tra Vinh University, Vietnam before use. After harvesting, the leaves were dehydrated at 40°C until completely dry. The dried leaves were ground to a fine powder using a grinder. The essential oil was then extracted at room temperature by infusing 200 g of leaf powder in 1.5 L of ethanol (96%) for 5 days, with daily shaking. The resulting solution was filtered through a filter paper to remove all fine powder residues. Next, the mixture was vacuum-rotary evaporated at 40°C to eliminate all traces of ethanol. After the ethanol completely evaporated, the remaining product was the herb essential oil. Essential oils were stored in the dark and frozen at −20°C until use. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) was employed to analyze the composition and relative content of volatile compounds in the essential oils.

Solutions of essential oil were formulated at levels of 0.3125, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5, 10, and 20 mg/mL in 96% ethanol. Ninety six % ethanol was used as a negative control, while Hantox Spray containing 750 µg/mL the active ingredient Pyrethroid (Hanvet, Tra Vinh, Vietnam) was used as a positive control. Hantox Spray was also diluted with distilled water to levels of 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL to determine the lethal dose of 50% (LD50) of Hantox Spray against tick larvae.

Dogs owned by farmers in the suburban regions of Tra Vinh Province, Vietnam, were utilized for collecting parasitic ticks. These dogs had not received any tick control treatments before the collection. The study procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Tra Vinh University, under license number 272/2021/HD-HDKH&DT-DHTV.

Engorged female Rhipicephalus sanguineus ticks were gathered from dogs naturally infested with them. These female ticks were housed in jars covered with a cloth, at a temperature of 27°C and a minimum relative humidity of 80% until they finished laying eggs. The collected eggs were maintained under the same conditions until the larvae hatched. Five-day-old larvae were used to evaluate the effects of essential oils.

Five-day-old larvae were placed in cloth bags measuring 4–6 cm. Each bag contained approximately 100 larvae and the bag openings were securely fastened. The bags containing larvae were submerged in petri dishes containing 2 mL of different concentrations of essential oil solutions that had been previously prepared. After ensuring the essential oil had thoroughly soaked each bag, they were kept at 27°C with a relative humidity of at least 80% for 24 hr. After 24 hr, the larval survival rate was assessed under a microscope. This experiment was repeated eight times. The rate of larval mortality was calculated using the specified formula described by Castro [5].

Corrected mortality (%) = (Test mortality (%) - Control mortality (%))×100 100 - Control mortality (%)

Female ticks were chosen based on their ability to move, overall physical condition, and size (≥ 3.5 mm). Ticks were weighed and grouped based on weights, ranging from 120 to 170 mg. Each engorged female tick was individually placed in a small cloth bag measuring 2–4 cm and immersed in essential oil solutions at concentrations determined to cause 50% larval mortality, determined from the previous larval experiments, for 5 min. Following immersion, the ticks were dried with paper towels and kept at 27°C with a minimum relative humidity of 80% until egg laying was completed. After oviposition, the egg masses were weighed and stored until hatching. This experiment was repeated eight times. The reproductive ability of female ticks carrying eggs was evaluated according to the methodology of Drummond [8]. Metrics such as the index of egg production (EPI), reduction in oviposition (OR), efficiency of reproduction (RE), and product effectiveness (PE) were computed using the following formulas:

EPI=(weight of tick eggs/weight of ticks) × 100;

OR=((negative control EPI−treatment EPI)/negative control EPI) × 100;

RE=(tick egg mass weight × % of tick egg hatched/weight of tick mass);

PE=(negative control RE−treatment RE)/(negative control RE × 100).

The LD50 of the essential oil on the larvae was calculated through probit analysis using SPSS 22.0. Significant differences in essential oil concentrations were identified if the 95% confidence intervals of the LD50 did not overlap. ANOVA and Tukey’s test were employed to compare the mean values of various essential oil treatments on the reproductive capacity of engorged female ticks. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Dog-parasitizing tick species have been observed to develop resistance to ectoparasiticides in multiple regions [3, 9]. The utilization of plant-extracted essential oils as an alternative to synthetic parasiticides has previously been shown to effectively kill tick larvae and reduce the reproductive capacity of adult ticks [1]. In the present study, the abilities of essential oils from three herbs, Ocimum basilicum, Perilla frutescens, and Mentha spicata, to eradicate tick larvae and engorged female ticks were assessed. The results showed that all three essential oils killed larvae and reduced the reproductive capacity of adult ticks. The larvicidal activity and impact of these essential oils on the reproductive capacity of engorged female ticks may be caused by the chemical components present in the essential oils that possess larvicidal properties or the synergistic effect of bioactive compounds, which contribute to enhancing larvicidal activity and diminishing the reproductive ability of adult ticks. The essential oils of Ocimum basilicum and Perilla frutescens contain Estragole and Linalool, which can inhibit acetylcholinesterase in ticks [2]. Additionally, 1,8-Cineole, an active compound found in the essential oils of Ocimum basilicum and Mentha spicata, exerts larvicidal and adulticidal effects on Rhipicephalus (Boophilus) microplus [5]. Moreover, Carvone and D-Limonene, which are present in both Perilla frutescens and Mentha spicata essential oils, have been shown to kill both larvae and adult Rhipicephalus microplus ticks [18]. Eugenol, found in Ocimum basilicum essential oil, also exerts larvicidal and adulticidal effects on Rhipicephalus sanguineus ticks that parasitize dogs [15, 24].

Overall, the results of the present study revealed differences in the larvicidal efficacies of the three essential oils. Mentha spicata essential oil had higher larvicidal activity than Ocimum basilicum and Perilla frutescens essential oils. The differences in the larvicidal efficacy of these essential oils could be explained by their varying chemical components, which have different mechanisms of action on tick larvae. Additionally, synergistic or inhibitory interactions between the biologically active compounds of essential oils also affect their effects on tick larvae. Previous research has shown that the combined effects of essential oil components can effectively target both larvae and engorged female ticks [13, 14]. Conversely, the antagonistic effects of essential oil components have been shown to reduce their impact on ticks [25].

The larvicidal effects of these essential oils and their influence on the reproductive abilities of engorged female ticks may be attributed to the direct action of the constituent compounds. These oils initially interact with the cuticle of larvae and engorged females. Once they penetrate the tick’s outer layer, these substances can enter the hemolymph and impact internal organs [20]. Certain compounds may also influence the salivary glands, potentially altering blood concentration and impeding efficient nutrient absorption. Previous research has revealed a significant connection between the digestive and reproductive systems of ticks, indicating that digestive issues can impair reproductive capacity [21]. Tick ovaries comprise an epithelial cell layer and oocytes at various stages of development [16]. Oocyte damage, which can occur as a result of the effects of the essential oils on the ovaries of ticks, reduces the ability of females to produce viable eggs and offspring. The active compounds in these oils can alter the morphology of oocytes, leading to the development of vacuoles in the germinal vesicles and cytoplasm, as well as reducing or merging yolk granules, or causing oocyte rupture [13, 24].

Additionally, the active compounds in essential oils (Carvone, Eugenol, Carvacrol) can interact with the phospholipids of the ovarian membrane, affecting membrane permeability, consequently leading to its disruption. This results in a reduction in the number of produced oocytes. Moreover, the presence of essential oils inhibits the transfer of lipids from adipose tissue to the ovaries of ticks, resulting in eggs with lower cholesterol content and a consequent reduction in egg viability [12, 17]. Furthermore, the larvicidal ability and effectiveness of essential oils against engorged female ticks may be due to their potential to inhibit the nervous system of ticks. Damage to the nervous system of ticks can affect nerve impulse transmission, impairing the mechanisms that support movement and feeding. Some plant-derived essential oils have also been shown to cause neurological and morphological changes in ticks [5]. Compounds found in essential oils that block acetylcholinesterase, an enzyme crucial for transmitting neural action potentials, can also induce neurotoxic effects [10]. Estragole, Linalool, and 1,8-Cineole, which were the primary compounds in the essential oils investigated in this study, all also have the ability to inhibit acetylcholinesterase activity [2], thus showing efficacy against larvae and female ticks. These findings suggest that these essential oils may affect the nervous system of dog ticks. Therefore, studying the effects of these essential oils on the nervous system of ticks in future research is necessary to gain a better understanding of the mechanisms by which these oils impact ticks.

The results indicated that Mentha spicata essential oil treatment resulted in the highest egg laying rate, but the lowest hatching rate compared with treatments with Ocimum basilicum and Perilla frutescens essential oils. This result can be attributed to differences in the active compounds in Mentha spicata essential oils, such as Carvone, D-Limonene, and 1,8-Cineole, which synergistically interact to affect the reproductive capacity of ticks [5, 18]. These compounds can all penetrate the body of engorged female ticks and disrupt their lipid metabolism by inhibiting enzymes, disrupting hormonal regulation, inducing oxidative stress, impairing mitochondrial function, and altering gene expression [4]. These combined effects lead to reduced lipid storage, impaired reproduction, and decreased survival and growth in ticks. Lipids are essential for egg production in ticks. By inhibiting lipid metabolism, these compounds reduce the energy available for reproduction, resulting in fewer viable eggs [12, 17]. It is also crucial to extensively examine how these essential oils affect tick larvae and engorged female ticks. In particular, analyzing the anatomical changes in the reproductive organs of female ticks and their eggs after exposure to these oils could offer a robust scientific foundation to understand the lethal effects of oils on larvae and their influence on the reproductive abilities of female ticks. Moreover, investigating the synergistic, additive, and inhibitory interactions between the bioactive compounds in these essential oils is equally important. Such research is vital for the practical use of these essential oils and provides a scientific basis for discovering natural essential oil sources with similar chemical properties for parasite control in dogs.

This study concluded that the essential oils were effective in killing tick larvae and reducing the reproductive capacity of engorged female Rhipicephalus sanguineus ticks depending on the concentration. Among the oils tested, Mentha spicata was the most effective, followed by Ocimum basilicum and Perilla frutescens. Further studies are required to explore the mechanisms of action of these oils and their interactions with bioactive compounds. Additionally, in vivo and clinical trials are required to determine the optimal dose to control ticks while ensuring no negative effects on pets effectively. Overall, the findings of the present study support the practical application of these essential oils in production, offering an alternative to synthetic antiparasitic drugs that develop resistance.
