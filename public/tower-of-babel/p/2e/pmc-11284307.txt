Minimally Invasive Open Bilateral Total Thyroidectomy Using Unilateral Neck Incision in Thyroid Cancer: Preliminary Surgical and Quality of Life Outcomes
Authors: In A Lee, Minji Kim, Jin Kyong Kim, Cho Rok Lee, Sang-Wook Kang, Jong Ju Jeong, Kee-Hyun Nam, Woong Youn Chung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeThyroid cancer incidence has increased in recent decades, and thyroid surgery is continuously evolving in response to demands for postoperative comfort and cosmesis. This study aimed to introduce a new surgical method for minimally invasive open bilateral total thyroidectomy (MI-BTT) using a unilateral 2.5–3.0 cm neck incision. Furthermore, we reported the surgical outcomes and postoperative quality of life (QoL) using a validated Korean translated Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) questionnaire.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively evaluated 41 Asian patients who underwent MI-BTT for low-risk papillary thyroid cancer by a single surgeon from March 2019 to December 2021.ResultsA total of 4 male and 37 female patients were included. The mean age and body mass index were 46.2±10.1 years and 23.3±3.3 kg/m2, respectively. The average tumor size was 1.1±0.6 cm, and 36 patients (87.8%) had bilateral cancer. Twenty-three (56.1%) patients had occult central lymph node (CLN) metastasis in the final pathologic report, with the mean number of dissected CLNs being 7.2±6.5. Gross capsular extension was found in 6 patients (14.6%). Moreover, 28 patients (68.3%) received additional treatment after surgery with low or high doses of radioactive iodine. The average serum-stimulated thyroglobulin value identified during treatment was 1.57±2.30 ng/mL. The mean operation time was 78.0±13.9 minutes, and postoperative complications included transient hypocalcemia (36.6%), transient hoarseness (24.4%), and seroma (2.4%). The mean total DLQI score was 2.73±0.78, indicating a small effect on QoL.ConclusionMI-BTT is a novel, safe, and feasible technique to improve patient satisfaction for surgical scars and QoL.

INTRODUCTION

Background

The first thyroid surgery was performed by the Roman physician Aurelius Cornelius Celsus (25 BC–50 AD).1 After much trial and error, it was Emil Theodor Kocher (1841–1917) who was successful in lowering the mortality rate after thyroid surgery from approximately 40% to 1%.2 The principles of safe and efficient thyroidectomy were established by 1920, and conventional open thyroidectomy (COT) using a 6.0–8.0 cm long collar incision on the anterior neck (Kocher incision) remains the gold standard.23 However, a long transverse surgical scar on the neck may cause a lowering of self-esteem or affect the quality of life (QoL) of patients.

Rationale and knowledge gap

As a result, a multitude of surgical techniques have been introduced to reduce the scar-related morbidity associated with thyroid surgery, particularly by minimizing the incision or hiding it out of sight. Since Lirici, et al.4 performed the first completely endoscopic thyroidectomy, minimally invasive thyroidectomies (MIT) have continuously developed in various ways, including endoscopies and robot-assisted procedures via transcervical routes or remote access. Furthermore, over the past few years, several studies have reported the safety, efficacy, and surgical feasibility of MIT.567891011121314 Nevertheless, thyroid surgery through endoscopy or robot-assisted long-distance access requires time to create a surgical space before approaching the thyroid gland. Therefore, recent efforts have attempted thyroidectomies using small neck incisions. Park, et al.15 first reported a minimally invasive open thyroidectomy using a neck incision of 3.0–4.5 cm in 2001. MIT had a shorter operation time, less blood loss, shorter post-operative pain and hospitalization, and less drainage insertion compared to COT. Our institution has also continued to develop the MIT method and recently reported the surgical outcomes for an MIT using an even smaller incision length of 2.0–3.0 cm, which involved a hemithyroidectomy with or without contralateral partial thyroidectomy. However, the MIT method has since been applied to bilateral total thyroidectomy (BTT) by an expert surgeon using a same-sized unilateral neck incision.

Objective

The present study aimed to describe the surgical method for MI-BTT using a 2.5–3.0 cm unilateral neck incision (MI-BTT) and report on the early surgical outcomes and postoperative QoL using the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), one of the most frequently used QoL measure in dermatology.16 This manuscript was written following the TREND checklist.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and patients

We retrospectively evaluated the initial 41 Asian patients who underwent BTT with central compartment neck dissection (CCND) using a unilateral 2.5–3.0 cm neck incision for low-risk papillary thyroid cancer (PTC). The surgeries were performed by a surgeon at the Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine in Seoul, Korea, between March 2019 and December 2021. All patients were preoperatively diagnosed via physical examination, ultrasonography (US), and neck and chest computed tomography (CT). Additionally, the preoperative malignancy classification diagnosis of cancer was confirmed by the US-guided fine-needle aspiration (FNA). Clinical classification of cancer and lymph nodes (LNs) was performed according to the Korean Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System by radiologists at our institution.17 Patient selection from outpatient clinics included bilateral cancer or unilateral cancer with contralateral multiple nodules with clinical N0. Initially, the surgery was performed only on T1 cancers. However, the surgery was also later performed on T2 patients under 3 cm if the procedure was strongly desired by the patient. The final inclusion criteria were patients with the following: papillary thyroid carcinoma requiring total thyroidectomy, tumor measuring less than 3 cm in maximum diameter, and no evidence of LN metastasis on image studies. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) a cancer over 3 cm in size, 2) clinically, a high suspicion of cancer invasion to surrounding tissues (above T3b), 3) clinically or FNA proven metastatic central LNs (CLNs), and 4) clinically suspected distant metastasis. The extent of surgery and postoperative management were decided in accordance with the American Thyroid Association guidelines.18

Operative procedures

After general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the supine position with the neck extended. A 2.5–3.0 cm skin incision was made along the lower skin crease of the unilateral neck, with the size being determined by the patient’s body shape and the overall size of the thyroid gland. Incisions were made on the side of tumor location where CCND should generally be performed. In the case of bilateral cancers, incisions were made on the side where the larger tumor or imaging enlarged LNs were visible. In addition, in similarly sized sides, incisions were placed on the right side, in accordance with the surgeon’s preference. After cutting the platysma muscle with electrocautery, it was lifted with a Nam’s long Senn retractor, a modified Senn retractor by Dr. Nam, to make a skin flap from the subplatysmal layer (Fig. 1A). The original Senn retractor was short and slippery, causing great discomfort to the assistant in maintaining the pulling force in narrow spaces. In contrast, the Nam’s long Senn retractor had a longer body length and anti-slip protection, making it easier to use. After holding the sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM) with Allis forceps and dividing it from the lateral side of the strap muscle, the omohyoid muscle was identified. The omohyoid muscle and strap muscle were detached with electrocautery, after which the SCM and omohyoid muscle were pulled laterally using an Army-Navy retractor. After lifting the strap muscle with Allis forceps and detaching the internal jugular vein below it, the thyroid gland was exposed. The isthmus was resected in half over the trachea, and the superior thyroidal artery and vein were ligated using an energetic surgical instrument. The upper pole of thyroid gland was then completely resected, and the entire thyroid gland was pulled out of the incision using Allis forceps (Supplementary Video 1, only online). Additionally, exposing the lateral and inferior sides of the thyroid gland with Army-Navy and Nam’s long Senn retractors allowed confirmation and preservation of the inferior parathyroid gland and recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN). The CCND was performed in the same way as COT. Then, one side of the thyroid gland was resected in accordance with the procedure described in previous studies.19 Afterwards, holding the strap muscle up with Nam’s long Senn retractors, the other side of the thyroid gland was pulled with Allis forceps. After separating the opposite strap muscle from the thyroid gland with a surgical energy instrument, the thyroid gland was pulled medially and inferiorly to expose the upper pole. At this time, the patient’s head was turned toward the direction of incision in order to facilitate access. Using our modified Nam’s right-angle retractor, the larynx was pulled medially to secure the surgical field of view, allowing the superior thyroidal artery and vein to be ligated (Fig. 1B). When the upper pole of thyroid gland was dissected, the opposite side of the thyroid gland was pulled out of the skin incision (Supplementary Video 2, only online). Holding the thyroid gland medially with Allis forceps and pulling the strap muscle laterally and inferiorly with an Army-Navy and Nam’s long Senn retractor, the opposite inferior parathyroid gland and RLN were preserved. Afterwards, additional CCND was performed if necessary, and the opposite thyroid gland was removed. After controlling for bleeding, the platysma muscle was reapproximated with interrupted 4–0 Vicryl sutures, and the skin was closed using 5–0 Maxon subcuticular continuous sutures (Supplementary Video 3, only online). Video recordings of the entire procedure are attached.

Postoperative follow-up

The clinical parameters analyzed included patient characteristics, clinical imaging findings, operative variables, pathological findings, postoperative outcomes with surgical complications, and postoperative radioactive iodine (RAI) treatment, including serum-stimulated thyroglobulin (off-Tg). All the patients were followed up in the same manner, which included clinical examinations within 1 week of discharge and a 3-to-6-month follow-up comprising a physical examination, neck US or CT, an assay of tumor markers (serum thyroglobulin and thyroglobulin antibody concentration), and RAI treatment dependent on outcomes of surgery in the following 1–2 months. When visiting an outpatient clinic for 6 months after surgery, patients were asked to fill out a validated Korean translated DLQI questionnaire to check the degree of QoL reduction caused by surgical wounds. The DLQI consists of 10 questions that are classified into six subscales: symptoms and feelings (questions 1–2), daily activities (questions 3–4), leisure (questions 5–6), work and school (question 7), personal relationships (questions 8–9), and treatment (question 10). The total DLQI score was calculated by summing the scores of the 10 questions, with a maximum score of 30 and a minimum score of 0. The higher the score, the more the QoL is negatively affected.20 We retrospectively collected the clinical and pathological data and stored them in a dedicated database for analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013) and approved by the institutional review board of Yonsei University (IRB No: 4-2022-0417). The need for informed consent was waived due to the study’s retrospective design.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software for Windows version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous quantitative data were expressed as mean±standard deviation. Categorical qualitative data were expressed as percentages.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical data

A total of 41 patients (4 male and 37 female patients) underwent MI-BTT. The mean age was 46.2±10.1 years (range, 25–66), and the mean body mass index (BMI) was 23.3±3.3 kg/m2 (range, 16.0–31.1 kg/m2 ). Thirteen patients (31.7%) had a family history of thyroid cancer, with 5 patients (12.2%) having thyroid cancer in the first generation and 8 patients (19.5%) having cancers in other organs, including thyroid disease. There was no history of radiation exposure. According to preoperative imaging findings (US and CT) for clinical TNM staging, 34 patients (82.9%) had T1 stage cancer, while 7 patients (17.1%) had T2 stage cancer. All patients were clinically staged as N0 and M0. The indications for BTT were bilateral cancers and unilateral cancer with contralateral multiple nodules (61.0% and 39.0%, respectively) (Table 1).

Surgical outcomes

Table 2 shows postoperative pathology results. There were pathologic cancer subtypes including conventional PTC, follicular variant PTC, and tall cell variant PTC, each accounting for 85.4%, 12.2%, and 2.4% of the patients, respectively. The average tumor size was 1.1±0.6 cm (range, 0.3–2.8 cm). Twenty patients (48.8%) had multiplicity and 36 patients (87.8%) had bilateral cancer. There were 23 patients (56.1%) with pathology confirmed CLN metastasis. The mean number of dissected CLNs was 7.2±6.5, with CLN metastasis observed in 1.2±1.5 patients. The maximum metastatic LN diameter in 23 patients was 1.7±1.3 mm (range, 0.2–6.0 mm). Microscopic and gross capsular extensions were found in 24 (58.5%) and 6 (14.6%) patients, respectively. Additionally, 12 patients (29.3%) had lymphovascular invasion and 36 patients (87.8%) had BRAF mutation. TERT promoter mutation was not noted in the patients.

Of the patients, 36 were incised on the right side, while 5 were incised on the left. The mean operation time was 78.0±13.9 minutes (range, 49–108 minutes). Intraoperative estimated blood loss was minimal and no intraoperative complications were observed. Postoperative complications included transient hypocalcemia (36.6%), transient hoarseness (24.4%), and seroma (2.4%). In addition, the length of hospitalization was 2.8±0.4 days (range, 2–3) (Table 3).

In preoperative imaging, only T1 or T2 stages were evident. However, six cases of gross capsular extension without invasion of the strap muscles or RLN were noted during the surgeries, resulting in the shaving of the muscles and nerves. In pathologic N staging, 43.9% of patients were N0, and 56.1% were N1a. Of the total patients, 28 patients (68.3%) who were at intermediate risk (pathologic aggressive subtype, metastatic LNs >5, vascular invasion, and gross capsular extension with muscle or nerve shaving) according to the American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines received additional treatment after surgery with low or high doses of RAI.18 The average off-Tg value identified during treatment was 1.57±2.30 ng/mL (range, 0.04–8.00 ng/mL) (Table 4). Additionally, the average follow-up duration was 10.4±3.6 months (range, 6.0–22.0 months), and the median follow up was 9.0 months. No recurrence was noted during the follow-up period.

DLQI scores

The mean total DLQI score was 2.73±0.78 (range, 1–4). The mean score of question 4 included in the subscale 2 (daily activities), the most affected section among patients, was 1.56±0.55 (range, 1–3). Additionally, the mean score of question 2 included in the scale 1 (symptoms and feeling) was 1.17±0.50 (range, 0–2). According to the DLQI interpretation, 2–5 point scores indicate a small effect on QoL.20 All other questions included in the subscales, including leisure, personal relationship, work and school, and treatment, had 0 point for all patients, indicating no effect on QoL.

DISCUSSION

The incidence of thyroid cancer, predominantly PTC, has increased worldwide. With the recent development of diagnostic techniques, the detection of early thyroid cancer has also increased, and the age of patients at the time of diagnosis is decreasing.2122 With emphasis on the early stages of disease, many efforts to operate on thyroid cancer using minimal incisions have been made to meet the patients’ demands for postoperative comfort and cosmesis. Several experienced thyroid surgeons have performed thyroidectomy with incisions sized ≤3.0 cm without endoscopic assistance by shortening of the conventional open incision of 6 cm or longer.15232425 However, Ferzli, et al.23 and Cavicchi, et al.25 performed the surgery by placing an incision at the level of the cricoid cartilage, a significantly higher than COT level, and created a subplatysmal flap with resecting strap muscles for midline approach. Moreover, Park, et al.15 and Sackett, et al.24 used lateral lower neck incisions, with Park, et al.15 placing central lower neck incisions for BTT and Sackett, et al.24 stating that BTT was limited to minimal access surgeries. In most cases, the incision length was extended accordingly. Nevertheless, as the incision wound was smaller than COT, it had highly desirable cosmetic effects, as well as faster operation times, less postoperative pain, and shorter hospitalization compared with endoscopic or robot-assisted thyroidectomies and COT. However, when creating a subplatysmal flap with resecting of the strap muscles in the midline of the neck, postoperative adhesion causing swallowing disorder could not be avoided. Additionally, in the case of upper neck incisions, the position was visible, which may reduce the patient’s self-esteem and QoL.162627

In a recently published paper, Park’s team reported the results of less than total and total thyroidectomy using a unilateral lower neck small incision, similar to ours.28 While this modified MIT method approached between the strap muscles to expose the thyroid, we exposed the thyroid without damaging the strap muscle’s natural anatomy. To do so, our newly developed surgical instruments, Nam’s long Senn retractor and Nam’s right-angle retractor, were used. Through this, we succeeded in securing visibility by pulling the thick strap muscles. For male, who typically have thick strap muscles, BTT using small incision has historically been more difficult. However, we were also able to perform surgery on four male patients, and no restrictions on the patient’s age or BMI were implemented.

Regarding the surgical results, the number of harvested LNs was 7.2±6.5, more than the COT’s 5.5±4.0 reported in a previously published paper by our institution.19 Initially, we included only those that were clinically staged at N0 and below T3. However, pathologic N1a was found in 56.1% of patients, and there were six cases (14.6%) with gross capsular extension confirmed during operation. Except for one patient, the N1a patients generally had a low risk of recurrence, with metastatic LNs of less than 2 mm and five or less according to ATA guidelines.18 Considering the risk of recurrence, RAI treatment was performed together after surgery, and no recurrence has been noted to date. As the surgeries were performed with a small 2.5–3.0 cm-sized incision, there were transient hypocalcemia (36.6%) and transient hoarseness (24.4%); however, most cases improved during outpatient visits 3 months post-surgery. Moreover, the majority of these patients were early adopters of the minimally invasive surgery. Among the 19 patients treated after July 2021, excluding two cases for whom RLN shaving was performed, only one case (5.2%) reported transient hoarseness. The operation time was completed at 78.0±13.9 minutes, shorter than the 100.42±28.70 minutes reported by Park’s team. Two cases of drain insertion were included (0.8%) with Park’s team, while we did not have any cases.28 In addition, the postoperative hospitalization period was 2.8±0.4 days, which was significantly shorter than that of COT reported by us and Park’s team (p<0.001).1928

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), QoL is defined as physical, mental, and social well-being, as well as an ability to perform ordinary tasks in daily existence (WHO 1998). Thyroid cancer, particularly PTC, is of growing interest and special consideration pertaining to QoL due to the high survival rate and long-term follow-up. Choi, et al.27 first reported the QoL of patients with post-thyroidectomy scars being impaired after COT as much as that of patients with other chronic skin diseases, such as psoriasis, vitiligo, and severe atopic dermatitis. Compared to the results of Choi’s study on the QoL of patients using DLQI scores, our MI-BTT was high in all domains (Fig. 3). Most surgical wounds recover progressively over time. In this regard, considering that Choi, et al.27 surveyed an average of 12.9 months after surgery, the results of our survey 6 months after surgery were significantly better. Almost all patients responded that, although they had sometimes been self-conscious due to their scar, it had little impact on their QoL, except when choosing clothing.

This study had some limitations, including a small sample size, short follow-up period, and its retrospective nature. In addition, although we attempted to closely select patients with low-risk PTC to determine the surgical safety and feasibility of the new surgical treatment, six cases of advanced PTC identified during the operation were included. Considering indolent characteristics of PTC, further long-term prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the oncologic outcomes and to verify its technical feasibility.

In the surgery for thyroid cancer, surgical safety as well as patient’s perception of surgical scars and the associated impact on QoL are significant. All patients who underwent MI-BTT using our new surgical method showed high satisfaction with small incision wounds along the natural skin crease. In addition, the surgical outcomes showed improved results compared to the existing surgical methods that use similar small incisions. Therefore, our MI-BTT method provided feasible surgical outcomes and cosmetic effects for low-risk PTC patients, without adversely affecting their QoL.
