Household air pollution and association with heart disease among women in India: evidence from the nationally representative survey (NFHS-5)
Authors: Mriganka Dolui, Sanjit Sarkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBased on an increased prevalence of chronic respiratory conditions among women of reproductive age, understanding the risk factors of heart disease is crucial to inform policy and program interventions to address the problem. In this study, we empirically assessed the associations of behavioural factors such as the use of cooking fuels, smoking behaviour, household air pollution (HAP), and various demographic and socioeconomic characteristics with the prevalence of heart diseases in women.MethodsThe data were derived from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) conducted in 2019–2021. The effective sample size for the present study was 7,24,115 women aged 15–49 years in India. Descriptive statistics, along with bivariate analysis were conducted to find the preliminary results. Further, multivariable binary logistic regressions were conducted to find the relationship between heart disease and behavioural factors such as cooking fuel, smoking behaviour and HAP.ResultsIt was revealed that women aged more than 29 years are at higher risk for heart disease (p < 0.05), irrespective of using clean cooking fuel (CCF) or polluting cooking fuel (PCF). Besides, women who are overweight (PCF = 1.15%; p < 0.05), have diabetes (PCF = 5.33%; p < 0.05), and have hypertension (PCF = 1.44%; p < 0.05) tend to have higher prevalence of risk of heart disease as they were using PCF. Furthermore, the odds ratio of heart disease was higher among women having exposure to smoking (OR:1.227; CI:1.130–1.333; p < 0.05), aged 45–49 (OR:2.474; CI:2.258–2.710; p < 0.05), lower education (OR:1.144; CI:1.048–1.249; p < 0.05), poorer wealth status (OR: 1.103; CI:1.011–1.203; p < 0.05), diabetes (OR: 4.677; CI: 4.297–5.09; p < 0.05), hypertension (OR: 1.48; CI: 1.386–1.58; p < 0.05) and current smoker (OR: 1.428; CI:1.304–1.565; p < 0.05).ConclusionThe findings revealed that uses of PCF lead to HAP and is a contributing component to heart diseases. To address these challenges, the study suggests promoting alternative and CCF and raising awareness about the health hazards associated with HAP. Policy interventions focused on expanding access to clean fuels, and knowledge are crucial to achieving sustainable development goals and mitigating the impact of heart disease. Organizations dedicated to public health and environmental well-being should enhance efforts to promote the adoption of clean cooking alternatives, thereby reducing the burden of heart disease among women in India.Keywords: Household air pollution (HAP), Polluting cooking fuel (PCF), Clean cooking fuel (CCF), Heart disease, Women, And India

Introduction

Heart disease is the leading cause of global mortality, accounting for 32% of all global deaths [1]. Cardiovascular disease, congenital heart disease, ischemic heart disease, and heart disease caused by infection are among the various forms of heart disease. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) encompasses several conditions that impact the cardiovascular system, including coronary artery disease, heart failure, stroke, and peripheral artery disease [2]. Similarly, Congenital heart disease (CHD) refers to anomalies in the structural development or functioning of the heart that occur before birth [3]. Ischemic heart disease (IHD), commonly referred to as coronary heart disease, is characterized by reduced blood flow to the heart muscle due to narrowing or blockage of the coronary arteries, often caused by atherosclerosis [4]. Heart disease caused by infection refers to diseases in which an infectious agent leads to inflammation or harm to the heart, resulting in endocarditis (infection of the heart valves), myocarditis (inflammation of the heart muscle), or pericarditis [5]. However, lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) have a greater burden of diseases associated with adult respiratory ailments. In higher-income countries, smoking tobacco is the leading risk factor for heart disease [6, 7]. In contrast, India and other LMICs are characterized by a higher prevalence of heart disease risk factors among people with lower socioeconomic status (SES) [8, 9]. A prior study revealed that heart disease was responsible for 26.6% of total deaths in India and chronic respiratory infections among individuals [10, 11]. According to a report by NFHS-5 (2019), the prevalence of heart disease among women aged 15–19 was 0.3%, followed by 0.8% among 20–34 and 1.2% in the 35–49 years age group of women [12]. Moreover, earlier evidence indicates that smoke emanating from polluting fuel used for cooking and heating may be a significant determinant factor in LMICs [9, 13]. Concerning health outcomes, household cooking fuels are categorized as either polluting cooking fuel (PCF)- including kerosene, coal-lignite, charcoal, wood straw/shrubs/grass, agricultural crop residues, and animal dung, or clean cooking fuel (CCF) – encompassing electricity, LPG, and biogas [14].

The combustion of polluting fuels can adversely impact the respiratory health of women, given that their primary responsibility for cooking within households and other household members can also be harmed due to exposure to household air pollution (HAP) [15–17]. It can be hypothesized that HAP may contribute to local air pollution, affecting neighbours even if they use clean fuels [18–21]. This underscores a significant negative externality of HAP usage in the local community [22]. Interestingly, a study highlights a substantial link between neighbourhood externalities of polluting cooking fuel use and heart diseases [13]. Furthermore, the intensity of exposure, prolonged indoor time (often surpassing outdoor time), and the number of individuals exposed collectively contribute more to global particulate matter (PM) exposure via HAP than any other sources [14]. PCF produces the following emissions, consisting of harmful gases such as carbon monoxide and nitrogen oxides; suspended particulate matter that contains volatile organic chemicals, including methane, aldehydes, benzene, and its derivatives; and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons [23]. Therefore, HAP resulting from PCF comprises a combination of solid particles, liquid droplets, and gases present in the air, which can have negative effects on health and the surrounding household environments [24].

Significantly, the level of HAP in LMICs is higher in magnitude compared to outdoor pollution, with peak exposure to low-efficiency PCF combustion during cooking [14, 15, 20, 25]. Prior studies underscore the production of black carbon, a particulate matter (PM) component, during PCF-based cooking, positively correlating with health issues and potentially amplifying the risk of various conditions under the global burden of disease (GBD) spectrum, including hypertension and heart disease [26–28]. Moreover, household cooking that relies heavily on PCF or unclean cooking emits hazardous compounds, resulting in various health complications, including lung illnesses, respiratory ailments, and heart diseases [29–31]. In India, an exposure-response relationship exists between outdoor air pollutants, including PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, nitrogen oxide (NO₂), and sulphur dioxide (SO₂) (exposure) and Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and associated heart diseases (response) in urban settings, whereas households in rural areas rely on PCFs due to their accessibility and affordability constrains [32–35]. Furthermore, studies have established quantifiable exposure-response relationships, such as a 10 µg/m³ increase in PM₂. ₅ being linked to a 3–5% increase in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [36, 37]. Access to CCF significantly varies between rural and urban LMIC areas, with 52% of the global rural population depending on PCFs compared to 14% in urban regions [38]. LPG is a well-known CCF that reduces HAP, and 15% of Indian households in the country do not have access to LPG [39]. According to a study, only 14% of households in rural regions throughout the six states have reported CCF as their main cooking fuel [40]. A report by MoSPI India (Multiple Indicator Survey, 2020-21) revealed that only 49.8% of households in rural areas and 92% in urban areas use CCF as their primary source [41]. Previous literature highlighted that household income and education are significant factors in determining the adoption of LPG and its continued usage [42]. According to IRES, the reason for the inability to afford LPG was lower income, that 75% of the non-user households earn less than 10,000 Indian national rupees (INR) per month and live in Kachha or semi-kachha houses [43, 44]. Most households in LMICs burn PCF in rudimentary setups such as open fireplaces with basic arrangements like three rocks or U-shaped holes in clay blocks, often leading to incomplete combustion and high household pollution levels due to poor ventilation [25, 45, 46]. In rural households, exposure to outdoor air pollution from open burning practices is associated with agricultural residues and wood in residential cooking [24, 47].

The absence of availability of clean cooking fuels has several detrimental health effects, particularly for women and children residing in homes [30]. Therefore, most non-LPG users’ households are particularly affected by the use of solid fuels for cooking, as they are more exposed to them, which has a disproportionate impact on their health [29–31, 39]. A prior study from India revealed that households without LPG use were 1.39 times more prone to heart disease [48]. Another study explained that the prevalence of cardiovascular diseases was 22.4%, and the likelihood was 6.07 times more among households exposed to biomass fuel (without LPG connection households) [49]. In this regard, women are more exposed to hazardous pollutants generated by burning biomass or solid fuels because they are directly involved in cooking [29, 30]. Gender-specific concerns prevail regarding both exposures to smoking and cooking in India, where an individual faces subsequent respiratory issues due to PCF. A study illustrated that women spend over 24 h per week on cooking and related household activities, while men spend less than one hour [16]. As a result, women residing in households relying on polluting fuels are at a heightened risk of direct smoking exposure while cooking [13, 49–51]. Moreover, the inhalation of smoke from unclean cooking fuels collectively affects HAP and increases several chronic risk factors, including respiratory infections and heart diseases [52, 53]. A global study reported that the use of PCF for cooking was significantly associated with a greater risk of heart disease 1.2 times higher [54]. However, hypertension and diabetes are two significant risk factors that trigger chronic heart disease conditions, and hypertension contributes to 16% of other CVDs [55–57]. On the other hand, diabetes can be attributed to diabetes-related CVD complications such as coronary heart disease (CHD), stroke, peripheral artery disease (PAD), heart failure (HF), diabetic retinopathy (DR), renal disease, and cardiac autonomic neuropathy (CAN) [58, 59]. However, another source of HAP arises from exposure to smoking, including tobacco smoke, which poses a wide range of risks to various respiratory health outcomes, such as coronary heart and lung diseases [12, 60, 61].

Furthermore, research has linked the biological pathways to the development of heart diseases determined by Atherosclerosis (buildup of plaque in the arteries), Reactive oxygen species (ROS), inflammation, and immune response play important roles in the development and progression of chronic cardiovascular risk factors such as ischemic heart disease, angina (chest pain), myocardial infarction (heart attack) and other heart-related ailments [62–64]. Concentrating on the biological mechanisms, previous studies revealed that exposure to HAP from cookstove emissions contributes substantially to air-pollution-related mortality and identified a significant relationship between HAP and cardiovascular disease outcomes [65–67]. Another factor, including Endothelial dysfunction, may lead to endothelium’s capacity to control blood vessel dilation, blood flow, and hypertension caused by factors such as high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and smoking. However, broadly, biological risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension play a mediating role in heart disease [68]. Genetic susceptibility is a major factor in the development of heart disease if there is a family history of heart disease [69]. A previous study from India revealed, that individuals with a familial predisposition to heart disease are at a higher risk of developing heart disease compared to those without a familial history of heart disease [70].

Additionally, various stimulus factors, such as economic, demographic, geographical, locational accessibility, and reliability factors, also influence the choice of fuel [24, 71, 72]. Moreover, poor socioeconomic status, education, occupation, housing types, and access to electricity are the key determinants of HAP and the burden of heart disease [73, 74]. Moreover, communities with limited access to CCF, high rates of unemployment, and a large proportion of the population living in severe financial hardship are more likely to experience higher levels of HAP [24]. Notably, the significance of exposure to HAP and its growing impact on health, potentially leading to multimorbidity, has often been overlooked [25, 75, 76]. Despite the escalating prevalence of heart disease among Indian women, limited empirical evidence links HAP due to cooking fuels with adverse heart disease effects among women in India. Hence, this paper aims to probe the socioeconomic determinants that shape exposure to cooking fuel-related HAP and its potential ramifications on heart diseases among women in India.

Methodology

Data and sample

Recent comprehensive data available from the National Family Health Survey, round 5 (NFHS-5; 2019–2021) conducted by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) holds significant potential in dissecting and elucidating the aforementioned domains and their interconnections [12]. The primary objective of the study is to explore the association between HAP and heart disease exposure by PCF. Consequently, for the purpose of this study, the data from the NFHS-5 (2019–2021) have been used. The NFHS-5 (2019–2021) provides information on population, family planning, child and maternal health, nutrition, adult health, and domestic violence, among others, for India, each state/union territory (UT), and for 707 districts as of March 31st, 2017 [12].

In a meticulous approach, NFHS-5 has collected the data into two phases covering 28 states and 8 UTs and gathered information from 6,36,699 households, 7,24,115 women and 1,01,839 men [12]. The NFHS-5 sample was designed to provide national, state/union territory (UT), and district-level estimates of various indicators covered in the survey. The NFHS-5 sample is a stratified two-stage sample. The individual recodes (IR) file has been used for this study to show the prevalence of PCF, occurrence of HAP and its association with heart disease among women in India.

Outcome and Explanatory Variables

The outcome variable consists of heart disease to assess its prevalence among Indian women, where NFHS-5 collected information regarding heart disease from individuals. The question asked to the individual women is she “Has currently heart disease?” and responses are coded as “no = 0”, “yes = 1” and, “don’t know = 2” [12, 77].

The explanatory variables encompass the various types of cooking fuel (electricity, LPG, biogas, kerosene, coal/lignite, charcoal, wood, straw/shrubs/grass, agricultural crop, animal dung, and other) to which household women are exposed. Further, it converted in binary forms namely CCF (electricity, LPG, biogas) and PCF (kerosene, coal/lignite, charcoal, wood, straw/shrubs/grass, agricultural crop, animal dung, and other) [113]. Likewise, covariates, including socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of individual women, have been incorporated into the study’s framework. By meticulously considering the outcome variables, explanatory variables, and covariates, the study establishes a robust foundation for exploring the complex interplay between cooking fuel exposure, individual socioeconomic and demographic attributes, and the prevalence of heart disease among Indian women.

Covariates

A set of covariates related to heart diseases was included in the study based on prior literature [10, 19, 49, 76, 78, 79]. The covariates in the study include age (15–29, 30–44, 45–49 years), education (no education, primary, secondary, and higher), religion (Hindu, Muslim, and general), castes (scheduled caste, scheduled tribe, other backward class, and general), wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest), geographic region (north, central, east, northeast, west, and south), residence (urban/rural), Body Mass Index (BMI) (Underweight, Normal, Overweight), Diabetes, Hypertension, and current smoker (cigarettes/pipe full of tobacco/chews tobacco/smokes cigars, cheroots or cigarillos/water pipe or hookah/smokes or uses gutkha or paan masala with tobacco/smokes or uses khaini/smokes or uses paan with tobacco/uses other).

We assessed diabetes using binary indicators based on self-reported diabetes status or a glucose concentration level of ≥ 200 mg/dl [79]. Additionally, we evaluated hypertension using a binary indicator if at least one of four conditions were satisfied: respondents reported high blood pressure on two or more occasions by a doctor or other health professional, respondents were currently taking prescribed antihypertensive medication, respondents had an average systolic blood pressure greater than or equal to 140 mmHg, or respondents had an average diastolic blood pressure greater than or equal to 90 mmHg [79]. Furthermore, “exposure to smoking” served as an important predictor variable for heart disease. This involved determining whether, in the last 30 days, someone (other than the respondent) had smoked in the home or anywhere else while the respondent was present. However, exposure to smoking is a potential proxy measure for someone WHO regularly smokes in the house, contributing directly to a large proportion of HAP. A detailed explanation of all the variables is provided in Appendix 1.

Statistical approach

We restricted our analysis to individual women aged 15–49 who were eligible for biomarker measurements and asked about their health behaviour. The prevalence of cooking fuel, smoking exposure and HAP was estimated using exposure variables of women (15–49 years). The predefined PCF and exposure to smoking combined and generate HAP. The Bivariate analyses were carried out to show the sample distribution, the prevalence of HAP and heart diseases among women by their background variables. Further, to test the significance of the association between HAP and heart diseases among women, chi-squared tests (χ2-test) were performed. Further, GIS-based mapping tools were used to outline the geospatial pattern of HAP and the prevalence of heart disease among Indian women. Multivariable binary logistic regressions were applied to find out the odds of occurrence of heart disease and significant risk factors associated with HAP among women in India. The results of regression analyses were interpreted as an odds ratio (OR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI). All the statistical analyses were performed using STATA 17.0 (StataCorp), and the spatial Mapping was performed using ArcGIS 10.8.2 software.

Women exposed to household air pollution

HAP has occurred due to exposure to PCF and smoking. This study looks at the contribution of exposure to PCF, smoking, and HAP (Table 2). In the age group below 29 years, women had a higher prevalence of exposure to PCF (47.98%) and HAP (71.90%), whereas women from the 45–49 age category had a lower prevalence of PCF exposure and HAP at 39.81% and 67.58% respectively, but higher exposure to smoking with 48.70%. Similarly, those women who had a low level of education (81.51%), up to primary (76.11%) were more susceptible to HAP. The prevalence of PCF use, exposure to smoking, and HAP were found to be higher among the women WHO belong to the Hindu community at 44.87%, 48.13%, and 70.31%, respectively. Similarly, women from the poorest wealth status had more prevalent PCF, smoking, and HAP at 92.39%, 49.60%, and 95.94%, respectively. In the eastern region, people had a higher tendency to use polluting cooking fuel (64.47%) and HAP (80.16%), whereas the prevalence of exposure to smoking (57.93%) was highly concentrated in the northern region, where major Indigenous peoples reside. However, rural individuals also had a higher tendency to be exposed to polluting cooking fuel (59.37%), smoking (48.25%), and HAP (77.77%). Additionally, women who had smoking habits were more prone to PCF (65.15%), smoking (64.78%), and HAP (87.90%).

Table 5 shows the determinants and association between HAP and heart disease by the Binary logistic regression model at 95% confidence intervals (CI). The results revealed that the exposure to HAP, smoking, and PCF users are more vulnerable, and their likelihood is higher than others alone (unadjusted). However, HAP behaves as an insignificant factor in heart disease but has a higher OR. The adjusted OR shows that women WHO were exposed to smoking are 1.1227 times more likely (OR:1.227; CI:1.130–1.333; p < 0.05) to suffer from heart disease than those WHO were not exposed. Moreover, among different contributing factors, age is one of the significant predictors of heart disease; the likelihood of heart disease significantly increases with the age of women, and higher OR is identified among the 45–49 age group of women (OR:2.474; CI: 2.258–2.710; p < 0.05). The women who had a lower (primary) education level were 1.144 times more likely (OR:1.144; CI:1.048–1.249; p < 0.05) to suffer from heart disease, and higher educated women were less likely to suffer from heart diseases (OR: 0.820; CI: 0.724–0.928; p < 0.05). At the same time, the odds ratio of heart disease is more than one and a half times higher among the Muslim community (OR:1.703; CI:1.578–1.838; p < 0.05) than among Hindus. The likelihood of heart disease was significantly higher among women belonging to the general caste (OR: 1.118; CI: 1.024–1.221; p < 0.05) compared to SC. Nonetheless, women with poorer wealth status were 1.103 times more likely (OR: 1.103; CI: 1.011–1.203; p < 0.05) to suffer from heart diseases compared to those with poorer wealth status. Moreover, women with diabetes (OR: 4.677; CI: 4.297–5.09; p < 0.05) and hypertension (OR: 1.480; CI: 1.386–1.580; p < 0.05) are more likely to suffer from heart disease in India. Similarly, women WHO have currently experienced smoking were 1.42 times more likely to suffer from heart disease (OR: 1.428; CI: 1.304–1.565; p < 0.05) than non-current smokers.

Discussion

This ground-breaking study explores the association between exposure to HAP and heart disease with other socioeconomic factors among women in India. The findings revealed a positive association between the use of PCF in households and a significantly higher risk of heart disease compared to those utilizing CCF in their prevalence, but PCF does not determine heart disease alone [73]. This study further identifies the use of polluting cooking fuels and exposure to smoking as two major contributing factors to HAP, which in turn adversely affects heart disease prevalence among women in India. However, second hand or exposure to smoking would have a small contribution to household pollution levels if any proper ventilation system is provided, but our study explicitly identified the significant associations among them. The risk factor associated with HAP for heart disease emerges as a primary concern in this study, with polluting cooking fuels predicting an increased risk as HAP levels rise. Some literature suggests that the transition from polluting to clean cooking fuel leads to a progressive decline in HAP [82, 83]. Additionally, impairments associated with solid fuel use are akin to those linked to current smoking, which is a strong predictor and helps explain the significantly higher mortality risk associated with exposure to both solid fuel use and current smoking [54, 84, 85]. Earlier studies revealed that the incomplete combustion of polluting cooking fuels releases harmful substances like PM, SO2, NO2, and carbon monoxide (CO), which are major contributors to air pollution and detrimental to human health [86, 87]. This inclination towards polluting fuels can be attributed to limited access to alternatives, economic constraints, lack of awareness about air pollution hazards, and dependence on traditional cooking practices [88, 89].

Nevertheless, women who are experiencing passive smoking are addicted to smoking exposure within the household in improperly ventilated environments, which is one of the Major causes of heart disease. The findings of our study revealed that women who were exposed to smoking were 1.227 times more likely to be prone to heart disease. Smoking exposure is one of the major risk factors for heart disease, as stated by previous studies, and is significantly associated [6, 7]. The elderly (45–49) women were more prone to heart disease, with a 2.474 odds ratio determining the higher chances of detrimental health risks in the future. Previous literature also identified that the chances of heart disease were higher among individuals from the elderly category [90–92]. These have been connected with an increased risk of several health issues, including oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and degradation of the blood vessels and heart due to ageing among women [93]. Another possible factor might be the extended period of time spent in the house, resulting in increased exposure to HAP, which leads to heart disease.

Furthermore, results revealed that a lower educational level tends to have a higher risk ratio of heart disease at 1.144 times more. Previous studies also suggested that education is inversely associated with heart disease [94, 95]. Nevertheless, it is crucial to consider the potential that the associations between education and risk factors for heart disease result from shared underlying causes rather than the impact of education on these specific risk factors for heart disease [96, 97]. Similarly, caste is an important means of social stratification or a social indicator in India. Based on the caste stratification, it can easily be understand how they are vulnerable to exposure to different activities, including the use of PCF, exposure to HAP, and heart disease prevalence. Furthermore, our data clearly demonstrates a strong correlation between caste and cardiac disease. Women from the general castes had an 11.8% higher likelihood (OR: 1.118; CI: 1.024–1.221; p < 0.05) of experiencing heart disease compared to those from the SC caste. The potential causes might be the other factors associated, including food habits and the coexistence of other ailments in the prevalent group. In contrast, other studies showed, that socioeconomically disadvantaged groups (SC, ST) face greater levels of exposure to cardiovascular risk factors, including smoking, an increased incidence of atherosclerotic risk factors such as obesity, diabetes, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, poor working and living conditions, stress, lower rates of formal education, and limited access to healthcare and health education [98, 99]. Interestingly, even though exposure to polluting cooking fuels is more prominent among marginalized groups like STs and those with low wealth indices, the risk factor for heart disease among STs is significantly lower than in other social groups in India. Previous literature also found similar findings consistent with our results [100].

Additionally, women from a poor economic status had a 1.103 times higher likelihood of having risk factors for heart disease. The prolonged exposure to PCF and other poor conditions of the households may have a significant impact on developing heart disease [73, 74]. On the contrary, previous literature revealed that individuals from wealthy (rich) households were more likely to have a chance of heart disease compared to those from lower-wealth households [70]. Households with lower socio-economic status prefer to use solid fuels and biomass (PCF) due to their economic constraints. Furthermore, our study reveals regional disparities, indicating that women in northern India have a higher propensity to use polluting cooking fuels and experience a moderately elevated prevalence of heart disease. While the disparity in heart disease prevalence between rural and urban areas is substantial, with rural women being more vulnerable due to the widespread use of polluting cooking fuels, urban women are also affected by heart disease due to other risk factors like processed foods and lower dietary diversity [61, 101–103]. Consistent with previous literature, our study elucidated that risk factors like unhealthy weight, diabetes, and hypertension are more prevalent among women and contribute to a higher susceptibility to heart diseases. The result revealed that women WHO had diabetes were 4.677 times more likely to suffer from heart disease. Previous studies also highlighted the significant impact of diabetic risk factors on heart diseases, particularly as a consequence of diabetic cardiac disease [57]. Another finding suggests that the relative increase in the rate of heart disease among individuals with diabetes is greater for women than for men [56]. However, the findings of our study aligned with the existing literature that hypertension is a significant risk factor for heart disease in India [104]. Results showed that hypertension was 1.48 times more likely to be the risk of heart disease. A prior study also supports the findings, and they revealed that the mortality rate of heart disease is 2.3 times higher when individuals have hypertension, and the severity of hypertension influences the incidence of heart disease [55]. Moreover, hypertension has been linked to cognitive decline, indicating that changes in blood pressure have implications for cardiac risk [105–107]. Similarly, higher BMI or obesity tends to be a higher risk factor for heart disease, which is evident in some other studies, but in our study, BMI is not significantly associated with heart disease [108].

To address the burden of PCF use and reduction of HAP, the government of India has initiated various programs promoting alternate cooking solutions and clean cooking fuels, including schemes like the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY), the Unnat Chulah Abhiyan, and the National Biogas and Manure Management Programme [88, 109, 110]. While these programs have led to increased clean cooking fuel usage and new LPG connections, they have yet to achieve full adoption among individuals [111, 112]. Although these initiatives are making progress in reducing heart disease burden, universal adoption of clean cooking fuels in the social system remains a challenge. Other challenges to LPG distribution, supply, refuelling, and subsidy credits to the beneficiary still exist in India. Strategies such as enhancing LPG production, distribution, supply, and direct beneficiary transfer of subsidy, as well as focusing on rural electrification, are core approaches for mitigating HAP. Regarding health policies, the National Health Policy (2017), the National Programme for Prevention and Control of Cancer, Diabetes, Cardiovascular Diseases and Stroke (NPCDCS), and Ayushman Bharat - Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY) have been launched to provide comprehensive primary healthcare, including screening and management of heart diseases in India.

Limitations of the study

There are a few limitations that should be acknowledged in the study when conducting this research. First, the current study utilized cross-sectional data, which may not establish a definitive cause-and-effect relationship between the use of cooking fuel, HAP, and the occurrence of heart diseases. Second, the prevalence of heart diseases was estimated based on self-reported responses from participants, which could potentially lead to underreporting or bias, as some individuals might not be cognizant of their heart disease status until clinical screening occurs. Third, it’s important to note that the study’s scope was exclusively confined to the female population; hence, generalizing the findings to the entire population should be done cautiously. Fourth, ambient air pollution is a significant contributor to HAP, and exposure to pollution at the workplace might be affected at the individual level, which we could not consider in this study. Fifth, due to the data constraint in the preference for selecting cooking fuels as primary and secondary, it has not been considered, which might have a significant impact on the exposure level of HAP and is significantly associated with heart diseases. Sixth, after controlling for several sociodemographic variables, this analysis may not have captured the full effect of HAP on heart disease due to confounding and exposure limitations. Finally, findings presented in the district-level spatial map are obtained via mapping tools that offer ecological associations drawn from consolidated data at several geographical scales (such as neighbourhoods, PSUs, and regions). Recognizing the possibility of ecological fallacy is crucial, as it involves making assumptions about individual actions based on evidence collected at the group level. The patterns and interactions identified at the ecological level may not always be applicable to individual entities within those groupings. Readers and stakeholders should consider these limitations when applying the findings to individual-level situations. The results should primarily be used to gain insights into wider trends and patterns at the group level.

Conclusion

This study explicitly emphasized the factors that determine heart disease and its association with HAP, as well as the demographic and socioeconomic factors among women in India. The findings underscore the significance of transitioning away from polluting cooking fuels and raising awareness about the health hazards associated with HAP. Moreover, this research provides an outlook supporting the adverse effects of PCF such as wood, dung, coal, and agricultural waste on health, thereby emphasizing the need for cleaner fuel alternatives. While genetic factors and other epidemiological considerations may contribute to heart disease risk, HAP emerges as a significant driver of heart disease [104]. Furthermore, interventions aimed at improving household air quality must advocate for a transition to cleaner cooking energy across all households in the community. To accomplish the 2030 agenda for sustainable development and effectively address climate change and environmental sustainability (MDG 7), significant policy changes are imperative. These policies should focus on facilitating widespread access to clean fuel and technologies by 2030. Organizations with a vested interest in public health and environmental well-being must intensify their efforts to address this issue and promote the adoption of clean cooking alternatives. Shifting of households from the polluting cooking fuels to clean energy will minimize the risks of HAP and associated health hazards, including heart disease, which should be a top priority for the government. Additionally, an optimum diet, regular exercise for health, avoiding smoking, regular check of blood pressure and diabetes, use of a ventilated window during cooking, adaptation of CCF practices, and community-level awareness campaigns should be practised to control heart disease.
