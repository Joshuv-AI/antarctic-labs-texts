SERS-based spectral analysis of ITS1 PCR products for differentiation of three old world Leishmania species
Authors: Parisa Bakhshi, Ehsan Rakhshandehroo, Hassan Sharifiyazdi, Tina Yaghoobpour, Hamid Nadgaran, Amin Safaie, Marjan Jafari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeishmaniasis, a zoonotic disease caused by parasites of the genus Leishmania, poses a significant medical and veterinary importance worldwide. This study was designed to explore the potential of SERS-based plasmonic substrate combined with advanced multivariate statistical analysis for differentiation of three prevalent Old World Leishmania species. In the present study, we investigated the potential of Raman spectroscopy and surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) combined with machine-learning-based analytical approaches for differentiation of three prevalent Old World Leishmania species. Previously characterized ITS1 PCR products corresponding to Leishmania infantum, Leishmania tropica, and Leishmania major were used as target molecules for spectroscopic analysis. Species identity of the samples had been confirmed previously using ITS1 PCR, sequencing, and PCR-RFLP analysis. Raman and SERS spectra were acquired using a HORIBA Raman microspectrometer equipped with a He-Ne laser. Silver nanoparticle-based SERS substrates were synthesized using a modified Tollens’ method to enhance Raman signal intensity. Spectral datasets were preprocessed and analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA), combined with linear discriminant analysis (PCA-LDA). The PCA-LDA technique reduced data dimensionality and facilitated visualization of spectral separation between species. A support vector machine (SVM) classifier was also utilized as an exploratory approach to visualize potential decision boundaries among species based on the SERS data. SVM classifiers on the ITS1 product spectra suggested the potential ability of SERS-derived spectral features to distinguish among three prevalent Old World Leishmania species. The obtained classifications were fully consistent with the previously confirmed molecular identification results. The findings demonstrate that SERS coupled with multivariate statistical analysis and machine-learning approaches can provide a promising analytical framework for species-level differentiation of Leishmania parasites based on ITS1 PCR products. This preliminary study highlights the potential application of Raman/SERS-based molecular profiling for future diagnostic and epidemiological investigations. Broader sampling across geographically diverse isolates and independent validation datasets will be required in future investigations to determine the reproducibility, specificity, and potential translational value of this analytical approach.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10103-026-04940-2.Keywords: Leishmania, Dog, Raman spectroscopy, SERS

Introduction

Parasites of the genus Leishmania have become a priority in public health worldwide due to their great medical and veterinary significance, being reported in wide geographical territories [1]. Among domesticated animals, dogs may be infected with prevalent species of the parasite. Depending on the site of invasion, clinical infection is often categorized across a spectrum of manifestations, from cutaneous to disseminated visceral disease [2].

Canine leishmaniasis is rapidly diagnosed in biologic samples using the microscopic examination of stained smears, in-vitro culture of clinical samples, and serological tests [3]. However, these methods do not determine the species involved. Therefore, given the disease’s epidemiology and zoonotic nature, species identification is crucial, especially in endemic regions with visceral and cutaneous forms.

Sensitive detection techniques have developed utilizing molecular technologies, including assays based on the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) [4]. These techniques can be further enhanced by methods such as RFLP [5, 6] and Real-time PCR [7–9]. As an alternative molecular methodology, Raman spectroscopy offers a powerful and accurate tool to provide data on parasites, including Leishmania [10, 11]. This approach has been investigated recently but needs further expansion to develop a valid and rapid technique for species-level characterization in diverse biological specimens.

Raman spectroscopy is an advanced, non-destructive analytical method providing comprehensive insights into a substance’s chemical structure, phase, polymorphism, crystallinity, and molecular interactions [12, 13]. This technique relies on inelastic scattering of monochromatic light, typically provided by a laser, as it interacts with the vibrational states of molecules [12, 14]. Most of the incident light undergoes elastic scattering (Rayleigh scattering), but a small portion experiences inelastic scattering, known as Raman scattering, which shifts the energy of the scattered photons [15]. This energy shift is directly related to the vibrational modes of the molecule, producing a unique spectral fingerprint for the identification and characterization of different substances [16]. Surface-Enhanced Raman Scattering (SERS) is essential because traditional Raman spectroscopy has limitations in sensitivity, especially for detecting small quantities of biological molecules [14, 17, 18]. SERS can reduce fluorescence interference and improve Raman signal visibility. These capabilities make SERS particularly valuable for studying low-concentration biomolecules like DNA and RNA, facilitating detailed structural analysis and supporting enhanced sensitivity in various biomedical applications [12, 19, 20].

Because species identification is essential for epidemiological and clinical investigations of leishmaniasis, molecular targets with sufficient interspecific variability are particularly valuable. The internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) region has been widely used for this purpose because it combines conserved primer-binding regions with sequence polymorphism that supports species differentiation [5, 6]. In the present study, the ITS1 PCR products selected for Raman/SERS analysis had already been identified in our previous work by sequencing and ITS1 PCR-RFLP, and were therefore used here as previously confirmed molecular materials [6].

Based on this background, the present study aimed to explore whether Raman/SERS analysis of previously characterized ITS1 PCR products from L. infantum, L. major, and L. tropica could reveal distinguishable spectral patterns. To enhance signal detection, a SERS-active substrate composed of silver nanoparticles deposited on a glass surface was employed. The obtained spectral datasets were subsequently examined using multivariate analytical methods, including principal component analysis (PCA), principal component analysis–linear discriminant analysis (PCA-LDA), and support vector machine (SVM), to explore potential spectral differences among the species.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical considerations

This study was designed as an analytical investigation using previously collected and archived clinical specimens obtained from canine and human subjects with suspected leishmaniasis. The use of human-derived specimens and associated laboratory analyses was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (ethical code: IR.SUMS.REC.1403.103). All procedures involving human participants complied with institutional ethical regulations and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent had been obtained from all participants (or their legal guardians) at the time of the original specimen collection. Specifically, the consent form included provisions for the long-term storage of extracted DNA and its secondary use for supplementary and complementary diagnostic investigations. Consequently, the current study utilized archived, anonymized DNA samples for Raman spectroscopic analysis, and no new clinical interventions or additional sampling were performed. To ensure privacy, all samples were processed in a coded format to maintain patient confidentiality.

Animal-related procedures and the use of canine-derived DNA were approved by the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Shiraz University (letter No. 14023/46/2725) and were conducted in accordance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Sample origin

The DNA samples analyzed in the present study were obtained from previously collected clinical materials originating from dogs with suspected cutaneous or visceral leishmaniasis and from human patients presenting with ulcerative lesions consistent with cutaneous leishmaniasis. Details of sample collection procedures and clinical characteristics have been reported previously [6]. Briefly, lesion scrapings from affected dogs and human patients, as well as lymph node aspirates from a clinically suspected canine visceral case, were subjected to DNA extraction using a commercial tissue genomic DNA purification kit (Parstous®, Iran) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were evaluated using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The A260/A280 ratios of the extracted DNA samples ranged between 1.8 and 2.0, indicating acceptable purity for downstream PCR and spectroscopic analyses Extracted DNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

In the current investigation, these archived DNA samples were used for PCR amplification, and the resulting purified PCR products were subsequently analyzed via Raman spectroscopy as a complementary diagnostic approach.

ITS1 PCR amplification

Amplification of the internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) region of the ribosomal DNA was performed using previously described specific primers LSGITS1-F1 (5′-CATTTTCCGATGATTACAC-3′) and LSGITS1-R1 (5′-CGTTATGTGAGCCGTTATC-3′) following established protocols [21]. The specificity of the primers was verified via NCBI Primer-BLAST to ensure there is no cross-reactivity with host genomic DNA or other related protozoan sequences. The PCR reactions were carried out in a final volume of 20 µl containing PCR premix (Ampliqon®, Denmark), forward and reverse primers, template DNA, and nuclease-free water. Amplification was performed using a touchdown PCR program to improve specificity, as described previously. The amplified products were visualized by electrophoresis on 3% agarose gels stained with Safe DNA Gel Stain (Sinaclone®, Iran).

Species confirmation

The species identity of the ITS1 PCR products used in the present investigation had been previously confirmed through sequencing and ITS1 PCR-RFLP analysis in our earlier study [6]. Briefly, restriction digestion with HaeIII (BsuRI) enzyme was performed following previously described procedures for species-level characterization of Leishmania parasites [22–24]. Based on these analyses, representative ITS1 PCR products corresponding to L. infantum, L. tropica, and L. major were selected for further Raman spectroscopy and surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) analysis in the current study.

The published sequences—including L. major (accession number PP094785), L. tropica (OR237833), and L. infantum (PP784777 and PP848956)—were retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database. The nucleotide composition (A, T, C, and G content) of the ITS1 amplicons was calculated using NCBI and BioEdit software.

Raman spectroscopy and SERS methods

The confocal BXFM microscope, spectrometer, and CCD Syncerity OE from HORIBA (LabRAM HR Evolution, HORIBA Scientific Ltd.) were utilized to acquire Raman spectra in this experiment. The device in question was a Raman microspectrometer manufactured by HORIBA (LabRAM, HR Evolution). The CCD of this device operates at −60℃. In order to guarantee the accuracy and dependability of the spectrum, we conducted a comprehensive calibration prior to acquiring it by utilizing a pure silicon wafer and precise alignment of the spectrometer to the 520.7 cm⁻¹ silicon line. Experimental SERS measurements were carried out with a 17 mW He-Ne laser excited at 633 nm. To improve the settings for acquisition, a 10% neutral density filter and ×100 objective lens were used. The confocal microscope’s settings and fluorescence emission could be fine-tuned by positioning a pinhole 70 micrometers apart. In order to obtain the fingerprint area, which is crucial for biological materials and various DNA architectures, the Raman spectra of the DNA-SERS samples were recorded over a wavenumber range of 300 to 2000 cm⁻³. The acquisition time of measuring each spectrum in this spectral range lasted five seconds. To assess reproducibility, we collected five spectra from different locations on each sample and averaged them to reduce spatial variance. The resulting consistency supports the repeatability of the measurements. Further studies will address long-term substrate stability under storage conditions.

SERS Measurements

Ag-NPs SERS-based plasmonic substrate

In order to create silver nanoparticles, Li et al. (2013) utilized a modified Tollen’s method. The procedure involved continuously stirring a solution of potassium hydroxide (KOH) and silver nitrate (AgNO₃) in a reaction vessel at a concentration of 0.5 M. Thereafter, a 0.7 M sucrose solution was added as a reducing agent. As a result, for SERS experiments, we synthesized silver nanoparticles on a flawless glass substrate.

The size distribution of these NPs was mainly around 50 nm to 70 nm in diameter, which was measured through AFM. These nanoparticles were deposited uniformly onto a glass substrate. Moreover, morphology and surface profile characterization of the fabricated SERS substrate, such as roughness, were carried out across the AFM images. The 2D and 3D surface topography is demonstrated in Fig. 1. The average roughness of the substrate was measured at approximately 8.5 nm. To ensure signal reproducibility, SERS spectra were collected from five different points per sample, and the consistent resulting spectral profiles support the substrate’s reproducibility.

DNA-SERS spectra and spectral preprocessing

For this work, we purified the PCR products using the FavorPrepTM kit (Favorgen®, Taiwan). Aspects of the SERS for each group of Leishmania species: we used a calibrated sampler to transfer 10 µl of PCR products to the SERS-based substrate, where we measured the enhanced Raman spectra. Our approach used purified PCR products as input for the SERS target, resulting in sufficiently high DNA concentrations. Thus, the objective of this study is not to achieve low detection limits, but rather the purpose of this research is to investigate the species differentiation based on their unique molecular fingerprint. Five spectra were collected from various points on each sample to obtain the higher Raman spectra of the DNA molecules. This ensured that the results would be consistent and dependable. The chemical structure revealed by the observed variability in ITS1 sequences was better understood when the researchers averaged the spectra. This reduced the amount of regional variance and increased the statistical significance of the spectral data. Due to the limited number of samples for some species, certain PCR products were analyzed in two independent SERS measurements using separately prepared substrates. The resulting spectra were considered independent spectral observations for the subsequent statistical analyses. For preprocessing and to improve the spectra’s quality, we used LabSpec 6, which is made by Horiba Jobin-Yvon of Rue de Lille, France. Vector normalization, polynomial baseline correction, Savitzky-Golay smoothing, and noise and fluorescence background reduction were the last steps in the procedure. To ensure accuracy and uniformity across all samples, MATLAB (version R2024a, MathWorks Inc., MA, USA) was used to determine the average of the SERS data.

Results

Molecular confirmation of Leishmania samples

Species identity of the archived samples had been previously confirmed using sequencing and PCR-RFLP analysis [6], and representative ITS1 PCR products corresponding to L. infantum, L. tropica, and L. major were selected for Raman spectroscopy and SERS analyses.

Adjunct statistical analyses

PCA-LDA

Figure 4 displays the outcomes of dimensionality reduction and SERS spectrum classification for three separate Leishmania species (tropica, major, and infantum) using PCA and linear discriminant analysis (PCA-LDA). To select the principal components, we used Pareto chart analysis. As shown in Fig. 5, the first 10 components account for approximately 86% of the total variance. Therefore, these components were applied to retain most of the spectral information while avoiding unnecessary complexity.

Comparative (a) 2D and (b) 3D PCA-LDA visualization of SERS spectra for distinguishing Leishmania DNA Species

Pareto chart, derived from PCA, the first 10 components account for approximately 86% of the total variance. The green bars represent the variance explained by each individual component, while the red line indicates the cumulative variance. It reveals supporting dimensionality reduction while retaining most of the data’s variability

The 2D PCA-LDA plot differentiates the three species based on the first two linear discriminants (PCA-LDA 1 and 2). Each point represents a SERS spectrum, with distinct markers for each species. It should be noted that the ellipses, which encompass 95% confidence intervals, indicate a spread and overlap of the Raman spectral features. It is obvious that Leishmania infantum tends to gather along the positive PCA-LDA 1 and negative PCA-LDA 2 axes, while the Leishmania major species is placed mainly around negative values for both. This is while the Leishmania tropica species is inside the positive quadrant. Hence, we see a clear and acceptable distinction between species, using 2D PCA-LDA illustration.

We then added one more orthogonal principal component (PC3) and reapplied LDA on the SERS-DNA data. The 3D PCA-LDA result is illustrated in Fig. 4-b. This three-dimensional representation improves species classification and reveals how the PCA-LDA method simplifies SERS spectra data while simultaneously preserving the unique spectral features of each species. Considering the achieved classification results, an important diagnostic tool for correctly detecting pathogen DNA can be built using PCA-LDA since it shows a significant capacity to distinguish between the three Leishmania species with little overlap in both 2D and 3D plots.

Assessment of species separability in PCA-LDA space

Due to the limited sample data size available in the present study, the implementation of the support vector machine (SVM) was not intended to establish a fully trained or clinically validated classification framework. Instead, dimensional reduced SERS spectra were fed to SVM with the purpose of employing this method as an analytical and visualization tool in order to investigate the intrinsic separability of the SERS-derived DNA vibrational spectral features. The results were visualized in Fig. 6. As it is obvious in Fig. 6, the transformed spectral data occupied distinguishable regions within the reduced feature space. This finding enables the identification of separating hyperplanes according to the fundamental principles of SVM. Moreover, in the two-dimensional representation illustrated in Fig. 6a, these hyperplanes appear as linear decision boundaries together with the corresponding sample scatter distribution, so this provides an intuitive visualization of interspecies discrimination. Furthermore, the spatial arrangement of the three Leishmania species in Fig. 6a and b, and 6d demonstrates that the PCA LDA transformation effectively linearized the spectral data. It thereby allows the SVM algorithm to define clear and minimally overlapping decision regions.

The primary purpose of incorporating SVM in this study was therefore not to construct a finalized predictive machine-learning model, but rather to evaluate whether the molecular fingerprints that are generated by the proposed SERS platform possess sufficient discriminatory structure for further machine learning–assisted analysis. However, the observed clustering patterns indicate that the spectral signatures of L. tropica, L. major, and L. infantum exhibit relatively distinct distributions after PCA-LDA preprocessing.

Overall, the results of this study indicate that whenever the SERS-derived spectral data are represented in a reduced feature space, they exhibit a notable capacity for differentiating between Leishmania species. Although the limited number of samples does not allow the development and validation of a generalized machine-learning classifier at this stage, the observed distribution patterns suggest that the obtained molecular fingerprints provide a suitable basis for future machine-learning diagnostic approaches. Altogether, all these findings reveal the potential of the SERS-based approach as a promising platform for interspecies discrimination of Leishmania in larger and more comprehensive future studies.

Discussion

Molecular diagnostics based on the identification of parasite DNA has become an accurate and rapid alternative for the detection of Leishmania infections, offering improved sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional parasitological approaches. Species identification is essential for understanding disease epidemiology, detecting potential variants including drug resistance, improving diagnosis and treatment strategies, and guiding prognosis and control programs [25]. In the present study, we explored the feasibility of using Raman spectroscopy combined with multivariate analytical methods to differentiate Leishmania species using ITS1 PCR products. Prior molecular characterization of the isolates used in this investigation had been performed using the ITS1 PCR assay followed by HaeIII restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP) analysis, a well-established molecular approach for Leishmania species identification. The ITS1 PCR method was originally described by el Tai et al. (2000) and Schönian et al. (2003) [5, 26], and the internal transcribed spacer region has been widely applied in molecular epidemiological studies due to its conserved flanking regions and sufficient interspecific polymorphism. This ribosomal sequence region has also been used in real-time PCR approaches for both canine and human clinical samples, including visceral specimens such as blood, bone marrow, lymph nodes, as well as cutaneous lesions [27].

Digestion of amplified ITS1 products with the HaeIII enzyme has been shown to improve the ability to differentiate multiple Leishmania species from various human and canine biological samples and even from archived microscopic slides [5]. In Iran, where both cutaneous and visceral leishmaniasis are endemic in humans and dogs, several studies have successfully applied ITS1 PCR-RFLP for epidemiological and molecular characterization of circulating species [22–24, 28]. Because of its reliability and widespread use, this approach was employed in the present study as a reference molecular characterization step prior to Raman spectroscopic analysis.

Despite its usefulness, PCR-RFLP analysis can present certain technical limitations. The similarity in fragment sizes between some species may complicate gel-based interpretation, often requiring high-percentage agarose gels and optimized electrophoresis conditions for accurate band separation. In addition, variations in PCR conditions, primer contamination, or gel preparation may affect the reliability of band visualization and interpretation [29]. These limitations highlight the potential value of complementary analytical approaches that can interrogate molecular characteristics through alternative physical principles. In this context, Raman/SERS spectroscopy offers the possibility of detecting molecular vibrational signatures of nucleic acid fragments, providing a spectroscopic fingerprint that may support species discrimination when combined with appropriate multivariate analysis methods.

The key advantages of the synthesized SERS-based plasmonic substrate include label-free analysis, short acquisition time (about five seconds for each spectral measurement), relatively low cost, minimal preparation after PCR purification, and the ability to capture structural features of biomolecules with satisfactory spectral distinction. These features support its potential applicability in future point-of-care diagnostics when integrated with statistical or machine-learning tools. Moreover, this study explores the applicability of using plasmonic SERS-based substrate measurements along with advanced analytical techniques, such as PCA-LDA and SVM classification, to assess the spectral separability among the DNA sequences of three Leishmania species. The application of PCA-LDA not only reduced data dimensionality significantly but also facilitated visualization of separable spectral trends among species in two- and three-dimensional space. The subsequent use of SVM was employed to visualize potential class boundaries, illustrating its exploratory usefulness in distinguishing between the species based on their distinct spectral characteristics. In a recent related work on Leishmania detection in clinically infected dogs, the serum samples were used to detect the parasite [10]. In that study, the spectral ranges were evaluated corresponding to the Amide III, CH₂ torsion in leucine and isoleucine, and C–O–H and CH deformation in leucine. It was suggested that the frequency shift could reflect host-parasite interactions. This idea was because leucine is a structural protein of the parasite surface antigen in the L. infantum membrane. Also, there are leucine-rich repeats in the Leishmania amastigote aiming to defend against the host immune system [30]. In our study, variation in the Raman spectrum may be attributed to differences observed in the sequence compositions (A, T, C, G content) of the ITS1 region, which is polymorphic in these closely related pathogen species.

The interaction mechanism between DNA bases and silver nanoparticles is primarily driven by surface coordination through exocyclic nitrogen and oxygen atoms, both acting as preferred donor sites. According to Basu et al.‘s research (2008) [31], nucleobases such as cytosine, guanine, and adenine exhibit progressively stronger binding affinity to silver surfaces, resulting in increased aggregation, trapping in the activated hotspots, and enhanced SERS signals as well. It agrees well with our observed SERS signal trends and provides a plausible basis for molecular-level differentiation via SERS [31, 32].

The validity of a SERS-based method has previously been examined for antibody detection in human blood samples using citrate-capped gold nanoparticles conjugated with an immunogenic peptide derived from Leishmania promastigote surface antigens (PSA-dP) [33]. SERS measurement was paired with a partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) to analyze differences between sera of infected and noninfected specimens. The authors concluded that this methodology appears more effective for visceral-type Leishmania (L. infantum and L. donovani) compared to species leading to cutaneous form in Brazil, including L. braziliensis and L. amazonensis [33].

In the present study, we used purified ITS1 PCR products instead of serum samples. Our method enabled us to achieve consistent discrimination of the three species based on SERS spectra of ITS1 amplicons. Moreover, as we employed purified PCR products as a target molecule for SERS measurements, the resulting samples were free from interfering biological matrices (e.g., proteins, salts). Therefore, molecular interference was minimal, ensuring that the SERS spectra primarily reflected DNA structural features. This represents an advantage of employing PCR products for downstream spectroscopic analysis. In addition, the clear separation of species observed in the PCA-LDA visualizations and the distinct boundaries defined by the SVM classifier suggest species-specific spectral patterns as captured by SERS measurements. Hence, the combination of the SERS technique with the advanced multivariate statistical methods has demonstrated the potential feasibility of differentiating Leishmania species, offering preliminary insight toward enhancing diagnostic studies. However, the final goal of our study was not to develop a finalized AI model but to explore the potential of integrating SERS measurements with multivariate statistical techniques (PCA, LDA, SVM) for preliminary species differentiation.

Gathering more samples and data in future studies would enable us to develop more reliable machine-learning approaches for parasite DNA detection. Hence, the research should be extended toward increased sample size and diversity to support more robust and generalizable AI-based diagnostics. Also, as a direction for future studies, the diagnostic potential of this methodology may be further tested for detecting and distinguishing Leishmania species directly in unprocessed samples.

While the present findings support the potential of the SERS-based approach for Leishmania species differentiation, an important limitation of this study is that all samples were obtained from a single geographic region. This restricted sampling framework may not fully represent the broader genetic heterogeneity of Leishmania strains circulating in other endemic settings. Although the ITS1 region is relatively conserved, intraspecies polymorphisms associated with geographic variation may influence spectral features and affect classification performance. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as preliminary, and further validation using larger, geographically diverse, multicenter sample sets is required to confirm the robustness and wider applicability of this diagnostic platform.

Conclusion

This preliminary study demonstrated that Raman spectroscopy and SERS combined with PCA-LDA and SVM analytical approaches can successfully differentiate among Leishmania infantum, Leishmania tropica, and Leishmania major using ITS1 PCR products. The obtained classifications were fully consistent with previously confirmed molecular identification results. The findings support the potential application of Raman/SERS-based molecular profiling as a complementary analytical framework for species-level discrimination of Leishmania parasites. Future studies using larger and more samples and diverse datasets are warranted to further validate and expand the diagnostic applicability of this approach.
