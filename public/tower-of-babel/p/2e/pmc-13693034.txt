Beyond women’s burden: integrating male partners into contraceptive decision-making
Authors: Priyanka Garg, Mishu Mangla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContraceptive use and family planning have traditionally been viewed as women’s responsibility, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Despite growing evidence highlighting the positive impact of male involvement in improving contraceptive uptake, continuation, and reproductive health outcomes, the participation of male partners remains limited. This review explores the critical role of male partners in contraceptive decision-making and synthesizes current evidence on the barriers impeding their participation. It further identifies strategies and interventions to foster shared responsibility in family planning and promote equitable reproductive health practices.MethodsA narrative review approach was adopted to collate global and regional evidence from peer-reviewed literature, policy reports, and relevant case studies. The review is structured around five core themes: sociocultural and gender norms, knowledge and educational barriers, limitations within healthcare systems, misconceptions around male contraceptive methods, and systemic challenges to implementation. The review also presents innovative, context-sensitive strategies for male engagement and future directions to enhance male participation in family planning.ResultsThe analysis reveals that male involvement remains suboptimal due to persistent patriarchal norms, misinformation, stigma, and limited access to male-centric contraceptive options. Existing family planning programs often lack couple-focused counseling and fail to provide inclusive services for men. However, interventions such as couple-based education, male-friendly clinics, community advocacy, gamified digital tools, and AI-enabled reproductive health platforms show promise in shifting attitudes and improving engagement. Success stories from countries including Tanzania, Vietnam, and India provide evidence for scalable, culturally adaptive models that facilitate male involvement.ConclusionStrengthening male participation is pivotal for achieving equitable reproductive health outcomes. Multi-level strategies, such as spanning education, service delivery, community norms, and policy integration, are essential to promote shared responsibility. Addressing systemic barriers, expanding male contraceptive choices, and leveraging technology can catalyze behavioral change and contribute to more inclusive, effective, and sustainable family planning programs.Keywords: Male involvement, Family planning, Contraception, Shared decision-making, Reproductive health, Gender equity, Couple-based counseling, Health system barriers

Introduction

Enabling individuals and couples to realize their sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR), including the ability to decide freely and responsibly the number, timing, and spacing of children, relies fundamentally on equitable and informed family planning practices. Family planning practices help people manage their fertility, avoid unwanted pregnancies, and plan for the timing and spacing of children. Beyond demographic outcomes, family planning is central to reproductive autonomy, gender equity, and broader social and economic well-being. Effective family planning depends on informed, voluntary, and shared decision-making around fertility intentions and contraceptive choices.

Historically and programmatically, contraception has been positioned as women’s responsibility within both health systems and socio-cultural norms, while men’s role in fertility decision-making remains informal, indirect, and shaped by unequal gender power relations [1–3]. In low- and middle-income countries, women further encounter compounded barriers related to limited decision-making power, restrictive socio-cultural norms, entrenched taboos, and systemic health system constraints, which collectively undermine reproductive freedom. Importantly, men are often not formally engaged in family planning counseling or open contraceptive discussions; however, they are rarely absent from fertility and contraceptive decision-making. Their involvement is frequently informal, asymmetric, and power-laden, often occurring outside rights-affirming counseling frameworks. Such patterns of involvement consolidate male influence while limiting equitable participation, constraining genuine joint decision-making, and undermining women’s reproductive autonomy. This imbalance may adversely shape contraceptive uptake, continuation, and method choice [4]. Disparities in access to reproductive health-related information, economic marginalization, and religious beliefs restrict the continued and effective use of contraceptives [5]. These constraints underscore the need for interventions that promote shared responsibility while explicitly safeguarding women’s rights within family planning decision-making [6]. While shared decision-making is widely promoted, male engagement must be approached cautiously in contexts characterized by intimate partner violence, coercion, or covert contraceptive use, where women may not wish or be able to involve their partners safely.

The role of contraception practiced by the couple in significantly reducing maternal morbidity and mortality is universally recognized, yet responsibility for contraceptive use and engagement with services remains disproportionately borne by women [7]. Studies from developing nations like Ethiopia and Rwanda demonstrate that despite policy recognition, male engagement often remains programmatically peripheral and insufficiently integrated into routine services, even in settings with national strategies targeting men [8–12]. In India, the National Family Health Survey (NFHS) categorizes family planning practices into modern and traditional methods. However, family planning metrics and counseling practices give limited attention to male-controlled or male-dependent methods such as condoms, male sterilization (vasectomy), and coitus interruptus, with a primary focus on female contraceptive methods, thereby contributing to the under-documentation of men’s roles in fertility decision-making and contraception [13–15]. In alignment with Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 3.7 on universal access to sexual and reproductive health-care services, including family planning, and 5 on gender equality, recent international initiatives support shared decision-making about contraception. Aligned with the ICPD (International Conference on Population and Development) Program of Action and global gender equality commitments, contemporary approaches emphasize voluntary, supportive, and rights-affirming male engagement, rather than reinforcing male control over reproductive decisions. This framing is consistent with gender-transformative approaches that view male engagement not as increasing men’s control over family planning, but as promoting shared responsibility, critical reflection on masculinities, and more equitable reproductive decision-making. With this background, the present review examines the significance of involving male partners in contraceptive decision-making, conceptualizes male engagement through a gender and power lens, identifies key barriers and ethical considerations, and synthesizes best practices for promoting equitable, rights-based, and context-sensitive male participation in family planning.

Methods

Study design

This review was conducted as a narrative review, chosen to consolidate heterogeneous evidence on male engagement in contraceptive decision-making across diverse sociocultural, health system, and policy contexts. A narrative approach was considered appropriate given the conceptual nature of the research question and the diversity of study designs in the existing literature, including qualitative studies, observational analyses, intervention evaluations, policy reports, and programmatic case studies. This approach allowed integration of empirical evidence with rights-based and gender-analytic perspectives.

Search strategy and data sources

A structured literature search was undertaken to identify relevant peer-reviewed articles and grey literature published in English. Electronic databases searches included PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and Google Scholar. In addition, targeted searches of policy documents and technical reports from international organizations were conducted to capture programmatic and policy-level perspectives on male engagement in family planning. Key search terms and combinations included male involvement, male engagement, men and family planning, contraceptive decision-making, couple-based counseling, gender norms, shared decision-making, reproductive autonomy, and sexual and reproductive health and rights. Reference lists of selected articles were also screened to identify additional relevant studies.

Selection and inclusion criteria

Studies and reports were included if they addressed male involvement or engagement in family planning, contraception, or fertility decision-making and provided evidence on barriers, facilitators, interventions, outcomes, gender dynamics, couple-level decision-making, or service-delivery considerations. Eligible sources included quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods studies, as well as relevant policy documents, technical reports, and programmatic case studies. Evidence from low-, middle-, and high-income settings was considered to capture variation across sociocultural and health system contexts. Articles focusing exclusively on biological, pharmacological, or clinical aspects of contraception without reference to decision-making, gender dynamics, service delivery, or male engagement were excluded. No formal quality scoring or risk-of-bias assessment was undertaken, consistent with the narrative review design.

Data extraction and synthesis approach

Relevant information was extracted from included sources, focusing on study context, population, methodological approach, key findings related to male engagement, and implications for contraceptive decision-making. Findings were synthesized thematically rather than aggregated quantitatively. The synthesis was organized around five core thematic domains identified iteratively during review:

Results

The Results are organized into two sections to improve thematic clarity and reduce diffusion. The first section synthesizes key thematic areas describing how male engagement in contraceptive decision-making is shaped and constrained across sociocultural, relational, and health system contexts. The second section summarizes evidence-informed strategies reported in the literature for integrating male partners into family planning processes.

Section 1. Key thematic areas in male engagement in contraceptive decision-making

Patterns of male engagement in contraceptive decision-making

Studies examining male partner involvement in family planning counseling describe multiple patterns of engagement associated with contraceptive decision-making processes. Across settings, male engagement has been reported in the form of joint attendance at counseling sessions, informal consultation within couples, or male influence on method choice and continuation. Several studies report associations between male engagement and improved contraceptive uptake, continuation, and adherence, as well as increased communication around fertility intentions and spacing decisions [8, 16–18]. Male involvement has also been described as facilitating dialogue between partners, particularly in contexts where counseling by health workers reduces discomfort in initiating family planning discussions. Reported benefits include improved planning and spacing of pregnancies, which may have implications for maternal and child health outcomes [8, 16–18]. However, the nature and extent of male engagement vary widely, ranging from supportive participation to gatekeeping roles that influence women’s contraceptive choices. Male engagement is also shaped by the availability and acceptability of male-controlled contraceptive methods, particularly condoms and vasectomy, which are commonly cited in the literature. Men may also participate through male-dependent or couple-dependent practices such as withdrawal, periodic abstinence, or condom use during fertile days, although these roles are often less visible in routine family planning counseling and reporting. Condom use has additionally been discussed in relation to dual protection against pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV [15, 18].

Sociocultural and gender norms shaping male participation

In many societies, men’s participation in family planning is viewed as superfluous or inappropriate, and contraception is exclusively a woman’s responsibility. Across multiple settings, socio-cultural norms reinforce gendered expectations that position women as primary managers of contraception while limiting men’s engagement in reproductive decision-making. In many cultures, men are socialized to prioritize earning for their families while avoiding engagement in reproductive health issues, often viewed as inappropriate or emasculating [15]. Men are discouraged from having open conversations regarding contraception because of the stigma associated with such discussions, as well as their fear of being judged. Additionally, males may be hesitant to use or support contraceptive techniques like condoms or vasectomy because they believe that doing so diminishes their masculinity. These perceptions are frequently rooted in dominant constructions of masculinity that emphasize male authority, sexual potency, and control over reproductive outcomes. Patriarchal power dynamics further worsen such gender-specific barriers. Such an environment promotes men as the main decision-makers, including family planning and minimizing women’s say in Contraceptive choices. Previous studies report that contraception is seen as the sole responsibility of women, whereas men are allowed to evade such situations [8]. Together, these sociocultural norms contribute to constrained contraceptive choice, uneven responsibility for fertility regulation, and limited opportunities for shared decision-making within couples.

Knowledge, awareness, and educational barriers

Awareness of family planning, its implications, and a sound understanding of prevalent methods can significantly affect male involvement. While men are out of the house earning a living, they have limited exposure to information shared by frontline health workers, thereby affecting their health literacy and further weakening their ability to support their spouses in decision-making. This informational gap is particularly evident in settings where family planning communication is primarily directed toward women. A male-dominated workplace can also negatively impact literacy by fueling misconceptions and myths. Societal norms and insufficient outreach initiatives often reinforce educational gaps that exclude men from family planning discussions. Males may therefore feel unprepared or uninspired to make decisions about contraception due to a lack of accurate knowledge, which would sustain the cycle of exclusion. The literature describes a range of educational and informational approaches, including couple-based counseling, school-based sexual health education, and targeted communication campaigns, that have been explored to address knowledge gaps among men [19, 20].

Health system and service delivery constraints

The literature consistently describes family planning services as predominantly organized around women as primary clients, with men often positioned as peripheral or absent from routine service delivery [21]. As a result, men feel excluded or unwelcome as most reproductive health services focus primarily on women. This service orientation has been reported across diverse health system contexts and contributes to limited opportunities for male participation in contraceptive counseling and decision-making. In some settings, men are engaged indirectly as financial providers or informal permission-givers rather than as clients with their own reproductive health information and counseling needs. Male involvement in contraception decision-making is significantly hampered due to the limited availability of male contraceptive methods and their practical or perceived drawbacks [22]. Men primarily rely on condoms, withdrawal, and vasectomy; however, each has drawbacks that can discourage people from using them. Despite their widespread availability, condoms are occasionally viewed as inconvenient, less pleasurable, or less effective in long-term relationships, and withdrawal is notoriously unreliable. Vasectomy, a highly effective permanent solution, is often avoided due to misconceptions about its impact on manliness, sexual performance, and fertility [23]. These perceptions are reinforced in settings where counseling and information on male methods are limited or inconsistently provided. In contrast, women have access to a broader range of contraceptive options, including hormonal treatments, intrauterine devices (IUDs), and implants. The disparity in method availability and counseling emphasis has been described as reinforcing the perception of contraception as a predominantly female responsibility, thereby constraining men’s ability to assume a more active role in family planning [24].

Misconceptions and limitations of male contraceptive methods

Male contraception is surrounded by numerous myths and misconceptions that contribute to its underutilization. A commonly reported perception is that male contraceptive methods, particularly vasectomy, adversely affect physical or psychosexual health by impairing sexual performance or masculinity, despite evidence indicating no effect on testosterone levels, libido, or erectile function [25, 26]. Another misconception described in the literature is that male-controlled methods are unreliable or less effective than female contraceptive options, although condoms and vasectomy are highly effective when used correctly. Cultural and societal norms frequently reinforce the belief that men are not responsible for family planning, thereby placing the burden of contraception primarily on women [27]. In addition, skepticism and apprehension have been reported regarding emerging male contraceptive methods, such as hormonal injections or pills, often driven by misinformation about safety and potential side effects. These perceptions have been described as influencing men’s willingness to adopt male-controlled methods and to support their partners’ contraceptive use [26].

Addressing knowledge gaps through education and awareness

Informing men about contraceptive methods and reproductive health is crucial for informed participation in family planning and can be achieved through advocacy drives and community engagement initiatives like ‘nukkad natak’ (a type of street theatre in India), role plays in the community, workplaces, and health facilities [28]. Exposure to mass media and social media platforms can also help target myths and misconceptions. Curricula in school education can improve mindsets and shape positive attitudes from an early age. Previous studies from Malawi have demonstrated the effectiveness of educational interventions, where 78% of men in the intervention group reported using family planning methods, compared to 59% in the comparison group, highlighting the potential of educational programs in promoting shared responsibility [16]. However, implementing educational initiatives faces several challenges. There can be resistance due to cultural norms and traditional gender roles that can impact male participation, especially in societies with prevalent taboos [29]. There is also a scarcity of tailor-made, culturally sensitive educational materials that can cater to the varied needs of different communities, leading to ineffective communication. Moreover, inadequate access due to the digital divide and limited access to reliable internet sources pose challenges to reaching the masses and derail mass media campaigns [30]. In highly patriarchal societies, these initiatives may even face backlash as they challenge ongoing gender-specific roles, necessitating culturally sensitive approaches and engagement with community leaders [31].

Supporting voluntary couple-based counseling

Couple-based counseling can provide a structured space for partners to discuss contraception, available choices, medical eligibility, preferences, and concerns [32]. Where feasible and appropriate, it may be integrated into routine antenatal and reproductive health consultations or into community outreach sessions [33]. A previous study from Vietnam using a social-cognitive intervention involved the couple as a unit of study, reported an improvement in the usage of contraceptives and better communication with their spouses, and highlighted the real-world benefits of couple counseling in reaching a consensus on the choice of contraceptive methods to be adopted [34]. Despite the success, the study reported several bottlenecks in the implementation of such interventions involving couples, like cultural stigma about sex education and family planning that often limited couples from freely engaging in the discussions. In conservative societies with a higher prevalence of domestic/intimate partner violence, women may not be comfortable in openly sharing their choices due to fear of discordance, which further compromises their position in the family [35]. Couple-based counseling should be offered only with the voluntary and informed consent of the individual client; partner participation should never be assumed or required as a condition for receiving contraceptive counseling or services. Where covert contraceptive use reflects safety concerns or fear of partner retaliation, provider-client confidentiality should take precedence over couple-based engagement. Additionally, such interventions can be logistically challenging due to the need for resources to coordinate sessions or to find suitable times for male partners to attend counseling, as sessions during work hours may limit participation. There is also a dearth of trained, experienced counselors who can navigate intimate topics professionally and facilitate respectful conversation without making the situation embarrassing for the couple [36]. The inherent inhibitions of healthcare providers to engage in counseling with males due to personal biases or cultural beliefs further impede implementation. When effectively implemented, such counseling sessions increase uptake and adherence to contraceptive methods being offered in their community [37]. However, in strained relationships with significant power dynamics, such an approach should be used cautiously and requires context-specific counseling material that users find respectful.

Transforming sociocultural and gender norms

Prevalent societal norms that determine the gender dynamics significantly affect male participation in counseling sessions and determine the outcomes [38]. Strategies should also engage men and boys in critically examining harmful constructions of masculinity, including norms linking virility with high fertility, reluctance to use male contraceptive methods, and aversion to seeking reproductive health services. Anticipated results can be achieved through advocacy by involving stakeholders from the community, like the religious heads, community leaders, and administrators, who can help promote gender equality. Behavior change communication using social media influencers and community platforms can help challenge stereotypes and create safer spaces for men to discuss reproductive health. The CHAMPION Project in Tanzania successfully engaged men through community-based advocacy sessions and reported an increase in men’s support for contraception and a shift towards gender equity [39]. However, addressing societal norms is a complex and sensitive process due to several implementation challenges. Resistance from settings with strongly entrenched gender norms, having defined narratives regarding gender roles, can lead to backfire against women who demand freedom in decision-making and reproductive rights [40]. In male-dominated societies, local stakeholders may not favor such changes and may even attempt to resist, thereby negating the effects envisaged by advocacy. In addition, challenging societal norms without adequate cultural sensitivity can erode community trust, leading to aversion to such interventions. Such campaigns also require logistic support, finances, and long-term commitment to sustainability. Moreover, changing social norms is a gradual process that demands sustained community engagement and dialogue. Despite these challenges, previous studies demonstrate its effectiveness in increasing male involvement and shared decision-making, thereby minimizing disparities [41].

Expanding access to male-controlled contraceptive methods

The limited range of male-controlled contraceptive methods constrains opportunities for men to share contraceptive responsibility [24]. Despite the best advocacy efforts, the wider adoption of male contraceptive methods remains a cause of concern. An Ethiopian study found high awareness of family planning among men but low use, underscoring the need for improved access and health literacy. The available options are limited to condoms and vasectomy, which are underutilized. This limited male method mix reflects not only technological delay but also historical underinvestment and gendered assumptions in contraceptive research and development, which have prioritized female methods and left fewer reversible male-controlled options. In low-resource settings, inadequate healthcare infrastructure and supply chain challenges further hinder access to male contraceptives. There are prevalent misconceptions about vasectomy, such as that it interferes with masculinity or leads to infertility, which further restrain family planning programs. Likewise, the condom is attributed to less sexual pleasure, and a large proportion of users lack the basics of the appropriate method of use. Additionally, healthcare workers often lack the skills required to counsel men about contraceptive options, leading to biased or inadequate information for behavior change [42]. Current studies focusing on the newer male-specific contraceptives highlight certain hormonal gels and pills that have the potential to broaden the basket of choice [43]. The delayed availability of newer methods is attributed to ongoing research and regulatory approvals. When implemented effectively, expanding choices for males can improve the couple protection rates and minimize unintended pregnancies [24]. Having said that, addressing cultural resistance and low health literacy is crucial, underscoring the need for comprehensive educational campaigns to counter misinformation and address unmet needs among males.

Developing inclusive male-friendly health services

In many patriarchal ethnicities, reproductive health facilities are women-centric while discouraging men from seeking services. In addition, cultural resistance and stigma around male involvement in reproductive health can deter men from utilizing these services. Developing health services compliant with men’s concerns can improve their co-participation in reproductive health by creating an enabling environment targeting their unmet needs [44]. This can be realized through male-centric clinics with designated space within the maternal and child health centers, experienced male health workers to initiate discussions on intimate issues, and flexible timings to accommodate men’s work schedules. Active engagement of male health workers should be preceded by strategic recruitment and capacity-building programs, which may not be feasible in areas with distorted health workforce proportions.

The research indicates that such male-friendly services are beneficial in improving the utilization of reproductive health services by improving the health-seeking behavior among men and enhancing contraceptive use [45]. However, implementing such services faces several challenges, including the need for additional resources, infrastructure, and staff training, particularly in low-income settings. Male-friendly services should therefore be additive and integrated within existing SRHR systems, rather than diverting funding, staff, or programmatic attention from essential services for women and girls. Such services should also be implemented inclusively to avoid reinforcing gender segregation. At the same time, it is crucial to address cultural resistance and stigma in communities that may limit the effectiveness of such interventions, necessitating culturally sensitive outreach and advocacy.

Future directions and research needs

Improving male participation in family planning underscores the need for novel approaches to better engage men. Developing newer contraceptive techniques, such as non-hormonal pills and hormonal injections, could offer more options to men and improve their participation. Digital and AI-enabled tools may support personalized family planning information, reminders, and decision support; however, their use requires careful safeguards to protect data privacy and confidentiality, address digital exclusion, mitigate algorithmic bias, and ensure cultural sensitivity, particularly in low-resource and unequal settings [46]. Integrating gamification features, such as earning points for completing educational modules, watching videos, or contesting in the family planning challenges, could make learning about contraception engaging and rewarding [47]. The creation of ‘male-focused health hubs’ in community centers or mobile units designed specifically for men can increase access to contraceptive services, reproductive health education, and counseling. By combining cutting-edge technology with a safe, male-centric environment, such centers could transform the way men perceive and access contraception. Cultural gamification campaigns can also add to the benefits. In collaboration with local artists, musicians, and social media influencers, gamified campaigns could include social contests, interactive performances, or “family planning awareness days " celebrating shared decision-making. Such culturally acceptable interventions can closely align with family planning efforts and help decrease inhibitions in fun and creative ways. Such forward-thinking strategies can help modify societal attitudes while empowering men to participate in these uncharted domains.

Conclusions

Contraceptive decision-making should not be left to women alone. At the community level, peer support and shared experiences can complement formal counseling by improving access to information, normalizing contraceptive discussions, and reinforcing shared responsibility among partners. Through observation, couples learn the benefits of using contraceptives and are able to gauge the consequences of not using family planning methods. Promoting male participation in family planning enhances adherence to family planning methods and improves intimate relationships, thereby advancing gender equality. This can promote societal progression toward a more nuanced approach to contraception and family planning by involving males, addressing social barriers, and improving health literacy. Governments, healthcare providers, and community organizations need to collaborate to encourage male partners’ role in family planning and ensure the shared responsibility for reproductive decisions.
