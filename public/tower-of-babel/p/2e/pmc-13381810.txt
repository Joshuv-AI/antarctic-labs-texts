Distinct phosphorylation mechanisms as dynamic switches for Hsp90 regulation
Authors: Chanjuan Wan, Lei Zhu, Simin Wang, Xueqing Zhang, Jun Zhao, Xiaozhan Qu, Chengdong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phosphorylation is a central post-translational mechanism for on-demand regulation of protein function. In the ATP-dependent molecular chaperone Hsp90, multiple phosphorylation sites have been implicated in activity control, yet how individual sites encode regulatory instructions remains unclear. Here, using solution NMR spectroscopy, we delineate how site-specific phosphorylation distinctly reshapes the conformational energy landscape of Hsp90 across its ATPase cycle. The phospho-mimetic mutation T115E induces a global redistribution of the N-terminal domain energy landscape, flattening conformational barriers, pre-populating a lid-closed-like excited state in the apo form, weakening ATP-driven stabilization, and impairing ADP-mediated resetting. In contrast, T36E preserves the overall structure but selectively rewires dynamics at the ATP-bound step, where accelerated exchange and a reduced excited-state population bias the ensemble toward the ground state. Together, these findings reveal that phosphorylation sites engage different dynamic allosteric routes, yet converge on a common functional outcome by suppressing productive progression through the Hsp90 ATPase cycle.

Introduction

Molecular chaperones safeguard cellular proteostasis by ensuring proper protein folding and rescuing misfolded proteins under stress1. Among them, Heat shock protein 90 (Hsp90) is one of the most abundant and central molecular chaperones, governing the maturation and stability of over 1000 client proteins2. Dysregulated Hsp90 activity, particularly its overexpression, promotes oncogenic signaling and tumor cell survival, making Hsp90 a prominent target for cancer therapy3,4.

Hsp90 belongs to the GHKL ATPase superfamily and operates through an ATP-driven conformational cycle that underlies its chaperone function5. Structural snapshots from various Hsp90 species outline a cycle that alternates between a V-shaped open state and a compact, N-terminally closed state, marking its starting and ending points, respectively6 (Fig. 1a). However, the precise nucleotide-bound conformations of human Hsp90 remain debated7–11. Low-resolution small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) studies suggest that human Hsp90 retains an open conformation irrespective of nucleotides binding7–10, whereas kinetic analysis12, cross-linking combined with electron microscopy (EM)7, and single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) studies11 support a dynamic equilibrium between open and closed conformations in the ATP-bound form. More recently cryo-EM structures of human Hsp90, alone or in complex with co-chaperone/clients, show evidence of a closed conformation under certain conditions13–15. Within Hsp90’s N-terminal domain (NTD), a flexible segment known as the lid (residues 110–135 in human Hsp90α) functions as both an ATP sensor and a trigger for downstream conformational transitions16. Upon ATP binding, the lid shifts from an open to a closed conformation (Fig. 1b), initiating a cascade of structural rearrangements, including NTD rotation and interaction with the middle domain (MD), and swapping of the β1-H1 elements between dimeric NTDs11,17. These coordinated rearrangements enable N-terminal dimerization and ATP hydrolysis, coupling nucleotide chemistry to chaperone function. Despite extensive structural characterization, how these transitions are dynamically regulated remains incompletely understood.

Hsp90 ATPase activity is finely tuned by multiple regulatory inputs, among which co-chaperones and post-translational modifications (PTMs) are particularly prominent18. While co-chaperone-mediated regulation has been extensively characterized, the molecular mechanisms by which PTMs modulate Hsp90 activity remain poorly defined19–22. Notably, nearly half of the known PTMs in Hsp90α localize to the N-terminal domain, with phosphorylation accounting for approximately 65% of these sites18. Among them, T115 and T36 in human Hsp90α, or their equivalent residues in other orthologs, have attracted particular attention due to their strong effects on temperature-sensitivity viability23, in vivo Hsp90 function24–29, and pharmacologic response in cancer cells28. These key phosphorylation sites, both highly conserved across various eukaryotic Hsp90s, are located in the lid and the first helix H1 (residues 21–37) of the NTD, respectively, two regions central to ATP-induced conformational transitions6,17. Biochemical studies have shown that phosphorylation at either site markedly reduces ATPase activity by ~60%, perturbs interaction with co-chaperones and clients, and increases sensitivity to Hsp90 inhibitors25–28. However, how these modifications exert their regulatory effects at the molecular level remains unclear.

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy provides a powerful means to interrogate protein conformational dynamics at atomic resolution30. Recent advancements in NMR methodology and isotope-labeling strategies have extended its application to large, dynamic chaperones such as Hsp90s10,31,32.

Here, we apply solution NMR to dissect how phosphorylation at T115 and T36 distinctly modulates the conformational energy landscape of human Hsp90 across its ATPase cycle. Using phospho-mimetic mutants in both nucleotide-free and nucleotide-bound states, we show that these two modifications regulate Hsp90 dynamics through different allosteric routes, yet converge on a shared functional outcome by suppressing ATPase activity. Together, our findings establish an energy landscape-based framework for understanding site-specific phosphorylation as a mechanism for fine-tuning Hsp90 function.

Results

Nucleotide binding induces NTD-confined conformational changes in human Hsp90

We used solution NMR spectroscopy to investigate the conformational dynamics of human Hsp90α (hereafter referred to as Hsp90) throughout its ATP-driven cycle. 1H-13C-correlated methyl-TROSY (transverse relaxation optimized spectroscopy) NMR spectra were acquired for the ∼170 kDa dimeric Hsp90, selectively isotope-labeled at methyl-bearing residues (Met, Ile, Leu, Val, Ala, and Thr). The resulting spectra were of high quality (Supplementary Fig. 1). These six methyl-bearing residues, representing ∼34% of the protein sequence, are distributed across all three domains (Fig. 1a), enabling sensitive monitoring conformational changes in the full-length protein.

In the nucleotide-free state, dimeric Hsp90 exhibited a single set of resonance lines (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating that the two protomers sample the same conformational space on the fast-exchange NMR timescale (≤1 ms). Furthermore, the NMR spectra of isolated domains or domain combinations closely matched those of the full-length protein (Supplementary Fig. 2). These observations suggest that, in the absence of nucleotide, there are no extensive contacts between the individual domains, consistent with previous reports on other Hsp90 orthologs, including yeast Hsp8232 and E. coli HtpG33.

We next examined Hsp90’s conformational changes associated with nucleotide binding. In the presence of AMPPNP, a “non-hydrolysable” ATP analogue known to mimic the effects of ATP34, human Hsp90 exhibited a single set of signals (Supplementary Fig. 3), in stark contrast to yeast Hsp8232 or E. coli HtpG33, which exhibit extensive signal splitting indicative of co-existing conformational states. By comparison, ATPγS proved unsuitable for quantitative analysis in Hsp90 under our experimental conditions, as it induced heterogeneous and unstable spectra, along with evidence of slow hydrolysis (Supplementary Fig. 4). The absence of peak doubling indicates that AMPPNP-bound human Hsp90 adopts a structurally homogeneous ensemble on the NMR timescale. Chemical shift perturbation (CSP) analysis further revealed that nucleotide-induced changes are confined to the N-terminal domain (NTD), with minimal perturbations detected in the middle and C-terminal domains (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3). These data indicate that, unlike its yeast and bacterial counterparts, AMPPNP-bound human Hsp90 remains in an open, non-interdigitated conformation closely resembling the nucleotide-free state. Likewise, ADP binding induced CSPs that were largely restricted to the NTD, while the rest of Hsp90 remained largely unaffected (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 3). Negative stain EM data corroborate these observations, showing that both AMPPNP- and ADP-bound Hsp90 predominantly adopt an open, V-shaped conformation akin to the nucleotide-free state (Supplementary Fig. 5). Together, these results suggest that, unlike Hsp82 or HtpG, nucleotide binding in human Hsp90 induces localized NTD conformational changes without promoting global structural shifts or heterogeneity across the molecule.

To further characterize nucleotide-induced changes within the NTD, we completed NMR assignments for this domain. CSP analysis revealed highly overlapping perturbation patterns in the AMPPNP- and ADP-bound states (Fig. 1c, d, and Supplementary Figs. 3, 6, 7). Specifically, a substantial number of perturbated residues, as illustrated in Fig. 1e, are clustered around the binding pocket (e.g., residue L107 within helix 3 (H3)). Additionally, other regions, such as those within helix 1 (H1) (e.g., residues I26 and M30) and the lid region (e.g., residue L122), also exhibited significant CSPs, indicating that nucleotide binding elicits coordinated structural responses extending beyond the binding site itself. Notably, the CSP patterns observed in full-length Hsp90 closely mirror those detected in the isolated NTD and in prior structural studies of the NTD34. This consistency suggests that, in the absence of co-chaperone or client, the human Hsp90 NTD behaves as a largely autonomous structural unit with minimal coupling to the remaining domains.

In summary, our NMR analyses reveal a pronounced species dependence in the ATP-driven conformational cycle of Hsp90. Unlike its yeast and bacterial orthologs, nucleotide binding in human Hsp90 induces conformational changes that are confined to the N-terminal domain, without triggering global closure or conformational heterogeneity. These findings establish the NTD as the primary locus of nucleotide-responsive dynamics in human Hsp90 and provide a structural and dynamic framework for understanding its distinct regulatory behavior.

Phosphorylation at T115 and T36 exert divergent structural effects on Hsp90 NTD

We next assessed the structural effects of phosphorylation on Hsp90 by NMR, using the phospho-mimetic mutants T115E and T36E. Methyl-TROSY spectra show that these substitutions do not cause global conformational changes, as most resonances align closely with the wild-type (WT) protein (Supplementary Figs. 8–10). CSP analysis focusing on isoleucine residues indicates that the structural perturbations are confined to the NTD (e.g., I33, I110, and I128), with no noticeable impact on the other domains (Supplementary Figs. 8b and 10b). Comparative analysis of full-length phospho-mimetic mutants with their isolated NTDs and NTD-deleted constructs revealed highly similar spectra, indicating that the modified NTDs retain domain autonomy as in WT Hsp90 (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12).

Building on these observations, we determined solution structures of the WT and phosphor-mimetic NTDs. The NOE network of the WT NTD cannot be reconciled with a single conformation but instead requires at least two interconverting lid arrangements, consistent with previous NMR studies35. For example, we observe diagnostic long-range NOEs between the methyl groups of L64/T65 and M130, which are separated by more than 30 Å in the lid-open conformation (Supplementary Fig. 13). A similar open-closed pattern of NOEs is observed for T36E, indicating that it preserves the native two-state equilibrium (Supplementary Fig. 13). For T115E, lower spectral quality precluded identification of lid-closed-state-specific NOEs; therefore, only the open state was modeled. Ensemble calculations yielded well-defined structures for each construct (Supplementary Figs. 14a–c and 15). The mean pairwise heavy-atom r.m.s.d. values within the structured core are 1.45 Å and 1.83 Å for the WT open and closed states, respectively, 1.38 Å and 1.91 Å for T36E, and 1.75 Å for T115E. Structure-restraint statistics are provided in Supplementary Fig. 16 and Supplementary Table 1.

Our solution structures of the Hsp90 NTD adopt the canonical α/β two-layered sandwich fold and closely resemble the previously reported crystal and NMR models, with r.m.s.d. values of 2.2 Å for the lid-open form (PDB:5J2V) and 2.4 Å for the lid-closed form (PDB: 8B7J) (Supplementary Fig. 14d, e). The nucleotide-binding pocket in our NMR open-state structure is nearly identical to its crystal counterpart (Supplementary Fig. 14f). The largest deviations occur in the helix H1 and the lid segment, underscoring the pronounced conformational flexibility of these regions17,34.

Superimposition of the lowest energy structure of T115E and WT show that, although the overall fold is maintained, phosphorylation at T115 induces pronounced rearrangements in the lid (comprising the H4-loop-H5 motif) and periphery regions, as well as additional changes in distal areas of the domain (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 17). The introduction of a negative charged moiety at residue 115 creates electrostatic repulsion with E25 on the adjacent H1 (Supplementary Fig. 18), driving a ~30° clockwise rotation of H4 (viewed from its C-terminus) and partial unfolding at its N-terminus end (Fig. 2b). These rearrangements render the lid region more dynamic than in WT, as evidenced by attenuated resonance intensities likely due to conformational exchange-induced broadening (Supplementary Fig. 19a). In contrast, the T36E mutant exhibits minimal structural deviation from the WT in either lid states, apart from modest heterogeneity around the lid tip (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 20a). The limited variance is consistent with CSP profiles, which show that the largest CSPs are confined to residues surrounding T36, indicating that the substitution causes localized rather than global rearrangements (Fig. 2e, g, and Supplementary Fig. 20b, c). The limited effect is expected, as T36 engages only weak hydrophobic contacts with I128 and L143, making replacement with a negatively charged residue unlikely to induce major structural distortions (Supplementary Fig. 21a, b).

Comparative analysis of key residues within the nucleotide-binding pocket shows that its overall architecture is conserved among the WT and phospho-mimetic Hsp90 NTDs (Supplementary Fig. 21c), with only minor side-chain adjustments, e.g., E47 and N51 in T36E and E47 and V150 in T115E, indicating that phosphorylation does not substantially perturb the ATPase catalytic pocket. Moreover, CSP patterns between the free and nucleotide-bound states are nearly identical across all variants (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23), suggesting that the nucleotide-binding mode remains unchanged. Consistent with this, isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements reveal comparable binding affinities for ATP and AMPPNP in all constructs, whereas T115E showed an approximately tenfold reduction in ADP affinity (Supplementary Table 2). NMR titration corroborated this result: saturation required a markedly higher ADP-to-protein ratio for T115E, confirming reduced binding (Supplementary Fig. 24). We infer that, while the nucleotide pocket itself remains structurally intact, phosphorylation at T115 likely perturbs the dynamic organization of the lid and its adjacent solvent network that stabilizes ADP binding36. Although this reduction is notable, it cannot account for the decreased ATPase activity, as ADP release is not the rate-limiting step in the ATP hydrolysis cycle37,38. Indeed, weaker ADP binding would be expected to accelerate rather than slow turnover. These observations prompted us to examine whether phosphorylation alters the intrinsic dynamics of the NTD.

The intermediate-to-slow (μs–ms) timescale dynamics of the Hsp90 NTD were characterized using Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) relaxation dispersion experiments, a robust approach for detecting sparsely populated excited conformational states41–43. Global fitting of these dispersing residues to a two-state exchange model yields an exchange rate (kex) of 1947 ± 77 s−1 and a minor state population of ~2.9% ± 0.3% at 25 °C (Fig. 3b). By contrast, CPMG relaxation dispersion reveals that the ATPase-driven cycle imposes pronounced changes on the μs–ms conformational exchange landscape of the NTD. Thus, an ATP-like ligand markedly stabilizes and repopulates the excited basin while leaving the overall exchange barrier within a similar energetic regime. Although the present data do not unambiguously define the structure of this excited state, we tentatively interpret it as a lid-closed-like state, based on NOE cross-peaks consistent with contacts expected for a lid-closed arrangement (Supplementary Fig. 27), and consistent with previous studies showing that nucleotide binding promotes lid closure23.

By comparison, the ADP-bound state displays the opposite dynamic signature: intermediate-to-slow timescale exchange is largely extinguished. Only a small subset of residues (e.g., the H1 residue I34 and V136 around the lid) retains measurable dispersion, but global fitting fails to converge to stable parameters (Fig. 3b). This indicates that the ADP-bound NTD samples the excited basin only sparsely within the CPMG window, consistent with Hsp90 reverting to a predominantly open ground state at the end of the cycle. Together, these observations suggest that ADP suppresses sampling of the excited state, providing a mechanistic basis for its markedly faster association and dissociation kinetics relative to ATP38, as the absence of a closed-lid barrier leaves a largely unobstructed path for ADP entry and release.

Collectively, these results delineate a nuanced dynamic landscape of Hsp90’s NTD across the ATPase-driven cycle: ps–ns motions remain largely constant apart from local rigidification around the binding pocket, whereas μs–ms conformational exchange involving the lid-closed-like excited state is strongly modulated, being amplified by ATP and quenched by ADP. This differential tuning of fast and slow dynamics underscores how Hsp90 balances conformational flexibility with state-specific stabilization, a balance that may be essential for efficient progression through its chaperone cycle.

In summary, we show here that phosphorylation at T115 acts as a global reshaper of the NTD energy landscape. It flattens and desensitizes the μs–ms transition network by pre-populating the lid-closed-like excited basin in the apo state, dampening further ATP-induced stabilization, and preventing the near-complete ADP-driven reset seen in WT. These combined effects provide a structural and dynamic framework that may underlie the ~60% reduction in ATPase activity observed for T115E27,28. Thus, T115-dependent changes in structure and dynamics tune the balance between flexibility and stability in the NTD, thereby modulating Hsp90’s chaperone function.

In the ADP-bound state, T36E closely resembles WT: most dispersions disappear, and the small residual signals do not converge robustly, consistent with a return to a predominantly ground-state ensemble. Thus, phosphorylation at T36 perturbs only the ATP-bound step while leaving the apo and ADP landscapes largely intact.

Taken together, these results indicate that T36E imposes a step-specific brake on the chaperone cycle: it selectively suppresses ATP-induced stabilization of the excited basin while leaving the apo and ADP equilibria largely intact. By greatly diminishing access to the lid-closed-like excited state in the ATP-bound step, T36E compromises the productive conformational progression required for efficient hydrolysis, providing a dynamic framework that may underlie ~60% reduction in ATPase activity observed upon phosphorylation25. This mode of inhibition differs fundamentally from the global landscape reshaping caused by T115E, underscoring that distinct phosphorylation sites modulate different control points within the NTD transition network.

Discussion

The conformational landscape of Hsp90 has long been a research focus, especially in understanding how its intrinsically low ATPase activity facilitates its chaperone function. Here, we used solution NMR to monitor the conformational dynamics of human Hsp90 throughout its ATPase-driven chaperone cycle and investigated how phosphorylation at two representative sites, T36 and T115, impacts this landscape.

Species-dependent conformational dynamics of Hsp90 reflect evolutionary adaptation

While there is consensus that ATP-bound Hsp90 can adopt a closed conformation6,16,22, studies have reported conflicting results across different Hsp90 species using various techniques7,32. Our findings, combined with recent NMR studies and other existing literatures32,33,35, help reconcile these discrepancies and suggest an evolutionary trajectory for Hsp90’s conformational dynamics. This trajectory shows marked species-dependent differences, likely reflecting adaptations to diverse cellular environments and regulatory needs. We propose that as Hsp90 evolves from prokaryotic to eukaryotic systems, its ATP-bound conformational states became increasingly restricted and finely tuned by selective modulation.

Our NMR analysis of E. coli HtpG revealed that the ATP-bound form of HtpG can sample multiple conformational states, including the open, NTD-rotated intermediate, and fully closed conformations33. This coexistence of multiple conformational states indicates a highly flexible chaperone system capable of broad conformational sampling without co-chaperones. Such flexibility suggests a self-sufficient chaperone mechanism capable of managing client proteins independently, potentially explaining the lack of identified co-chaperones in prokaryotic systems.

Recent NMR studies on yeast Hsp90 (Hsp82) have shown reduced flexibility compared to HtpG. ATP-bound Hsp82 primarily samples only the open and NTD-rotated states, with no evidence of closed state being stably populated32. This suggests an evolutionary shift toward more regulated conformational transitions, likely driven by the presence of co-chaperones like Sti119,20,46 and Sba121,22, which fine-tune its activity and reduce the need for intrinsic conformational flexibility.

Human Hsp90 displays an even more restricted conformational profile. Our data show that AMPPNP-bound human Hsp90 remains in an open conformation, with structural changes confined to the NTD and no NMR-detectable population of the lid-closed-like states under our experimental conditions (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 3). This implies that human Hsp90 evolved to function with a higher energetic barrier for state transitions, relying on co-chaperones (such as p2321,22 and Aha117) and client proteins to facilitate these changes.

These observations suggest an evolutionary trajectory where the intrinsic conformational flexibility of Hsp90 becomes more controlled and reliant on external modulators. In prokaryotic systems, the broad conformational sampling allows HtpG to navigate various structural transitions independently. In contrast, eukaryotic Hsp90, especially in humans, has evolved to minimize the sampling of non-productive conformations and to rely on a network of co-chaperones and cofactors, including PTMs, for precise regulation. This evolution from self-sufficiency in simpler organisms to a co-regulated system in more advanced ones highlights a shift toward a more refined and controlled conformational landscape, reflecting the increased complexity of eukaryotic cellular environments.

Phosphorylation impact on Hsp90

Eukaryotes PTMs are pervasive and exert profound effects on protein function, regulating essential processes such as signaling, localization, stability, and interactions. Decades of studies has revealed that phosphorylation, one of the most common and versatile PTMs, primarily modulates protein activity through mechanisms such as inducing structural rearrangements47, altering protein-protein interactions48, modifying dynamic equilibria49, contributing to the formation or dissolution of membrane-less compartments50, and engaging in intricate crosstalk with other PTMs51.

In the context of Hsp90, both co-chaperones and PTMs play significant roles in regulating its activity. Co-chaperones stabilize different Hsp90 conformational states and intermediates19–22,46, regulate its conformational cycle52, and facilitate client protein loading19, providing a multi-layered regulatory network. However, the mechanistic understanding of PTMs, particularly phosphorylation, remains limited, especially for the N-terminal domain (NTD). Current studies provide only fragmented insights into Hsp90 phosphorylation effects. Phosphorylation at Y313 within the MD has been shown to induce extensive structural and dynamic changes, as demonstrated using hydrogen-deuterium exchange (HDX) and NMR signal width analysis53. Another study on multi-site phosphorylations within the MD and CTD using FRET reported conformational and inter-subunit effects, but with limited resolution54, while molecular dynamics (MD) simulations suggest potential phosphorylation-induced conformational changes yet lack experimental validation.

Given that nearly half of identified PTMs on Hsp90 are phosphorylations, and ~65% of these are located in the NTD, there is a significant gap in understanding of how phosphorylation affects the dynamics and function of this critical domain. Our findings provide high-resolution insights into the conformational dynamics of Hsp90 upon phosphorylation at T36 and T115, positioned within H1 and the lid—two structural elements that initiate and propagate NTD transitions. Using solution NMR, we uncover two mechanistically divergent but functionally convergent regulatory modes (Fig. 6). Phosphorylation at T115E appears to act as a global energetic landscape modifier, reshaping the NTD transition network by (i) pre-populating the lid-closed-like excited basin in the apo form, (ii) blunting ATP-induced conformational transition, and (iii) preventing the ADP-driven return to the open ground state. The net outcome is consistent with a flattened and desensitized landscape, in which the NTD samples multiple conformational states more readily but with reduced nucleotide-specific, tightly coordinated redistributions that normally drive efficient cycling. Consistent with this view, the persistent μs–ms conformational exchange in the ADP-bound state of T115E correlates with a marked reduction in ADP affinity, linking the altered dynamic landscape to its thermodynamic consequence. In contrast, phosphorylation at T36 acts as a selective regulator of the ATP-bound step. By nearly eliminating the lid-closed–like excited population, it constrains the ATP-bound NTD to a predominantly ground-state ensemble, reducing the ATP-driven conformational progression required for efficient hydrolysis while leaving the apo and ADP energetic landscapes largely intact. Our results may help explain prior findings that phosphorylation at T36 or T115 alters interactions with co-chaperones like Aha125,28. Specifically, we show that phosphorylation at T36 or T115 reshapes the dynamics of the H1 and lid regions, which are key for binding the C-terminal domain of Aha1 to Hsp9055. These phosphorylation-induced dynamic shifts could disrupt or even abolish Aha1’s interactions with Hsp90, potentially impacting the regulatory chaperone network.

These findings illustrate how phosphorylation acts as a precise modulator of Hsp90 dynamics, analogous to its role in kinase regulation where conformational flexibility is finely tuned for activity control49,56. They reinforce that Hsp90’s functional core lies in its conformational dynamics—an emerging paradigm across many chaperone systems57,58. Phosphorylation leverages this dynamic sensitivity with striking specificity: T115 reshapes the entire transition network, whereas T36 selectively alters the ATP-bound step. Despite operating through distinct mechanistic routes, both sites ultimately regulate the progression of the ATP-driven cycle. We speculate that phosphorylation at other lid-proximal sites such as T65 and T109 may exert similar dynamic effects18. T65 contributes to the hydrophobic interface stabilizing the lid-closed-like state, so introducing a negative charge would be expected to weaken closure. T109 contacts the H6 hinge that governs lid opening and closing, and its phosphorylation could perturb this coupling. Future studies may reveal whether modifications at other sites produce comparable regulatory influences.

The insights gained here underscore the potential of targeting Hsp90 dynamic and regulatory mechanisms in drug design. Most current anti-cancer Hsp90 inhibitors, designed as ATP-competitive compounds, target the NTD but have shown limited clinical success due to toxicity, poor bioavailability, and acquired resistance4. Our findings suggest an alternative strategy: targeting the dynamic allosteric regulation of Hsp90. Drugs designed to mimic or inhibit dynamic changes could offer a more selective and effective approach to modulating Hsp90 activity in cancer therapy.

Methods

Protein expression and purification

The gene encoding full-length Hsp90α (residues 1–732, UniProt No. P07900) and the NTD-deletion protein (Hsp90α ∆N, residues 238-732) were cloned into a pET15 vector with an N-terminal 6×His tag, followed by a tobacco etch virus (TEV) protease cleavage site. The NTD of Hsp90α (residues 1–237) was obtained via PCR amplification and inserted into a modified pET15 vector containing a maltose binding protein (MBP) tag, and a TEV cleavage site after an N-terminal 6×His tag. Mutants, including Hsp90αFL T36E, Hsp90αFL T115E, Hsp90αNTD T36E, Hsp90αNTD T115E, and Hsp90αNTD T115E/I110V were generated using Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (NEB, catalogue number: E0554S). All constructs were verified by DNA sequencing. Oligonucleotide sequences used for PCR amplification and mutagenesis are listed in Supplementary Table 3. All plasmids generated in this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Recombinant proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli (E. coli) BL21 (DE3) cells induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) at OD600 ~ 0.5, followed by 21 h of incubation at 18 °C. For ITC measurements and negative staining electron microscopy studies, unlabeled protein samples were grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium with 100 μg/mL ampicillin. For NMR studies, uniformly isotopically labeled proteins were produced in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells grown in M9 minimal medium supplemented with 15N-NH4Cl (1 g/L) and 13C-glucose (4 g/L) (CIL and Isotec). For methyl labeling, samples were selectively labeled at the methyl groups (13C1H3) of Ile-δ1, Leu-δ1/δ2, Val-γ1/γ2, Ala-β, Thr-γ2, and Met-ε in an otherwise perdeuterated (2H) and 12C background. For 13CH3-ILVMAT labeling, the following precursors (from CIL) were added to M9/D2O medium supplemented with deuterated glucose (2 g/L) 30 min before induction: [13CH3]-α-ketobutyric acid-methyl-13C, 3,3-D2 (46 mg/L), [13CH3]-L-Methyonine-methyl-13C (50 mg/L), [2H2, 13CH3]-L-Alanine-3-13C, 2-D (50 mg/L), [2H2, 13CH3]-L-Threonine-4-13C,2,3-D2 (50 mg/L), [13CH3]-α-ketoisovaleric acid-dimethyl-13C2 (85 mg/L), and [2H5]-Glycine-D5 (100 mg/L). For 13CH3-ILVM labeling, only [13CH3]-α-ketobutyric acid-methyl-13C, 3,3-D2, [13CH3]-L-Methyonine-methyl-13C, and [13CH3]-α-ketoisovaleric acid-dimethyl-13C2 were added to the culture at identical concentrations.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, 500 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 8.0). Lysis was performed with 1 mM PMSF using a JN-Mini Pro Low-temperature Ultra-high pressure cell disrupter (JNBIO) at 4 °C. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 50,000 × g for 30 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatants were loaded onto a Ni2+-chelating column (GE Healthcare) preequilibrated with the lysis buffer. After washing with 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 500 mM NaCl, and 25 mM imidazole, the target proteins were eluted with 25 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, and 300 mM imidazole. The eluted proteins were subjected to TEV protease cleavage and dialyzed overnight against 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 100 mM NaCl, and 1.5 mM β-mercaptoethanol (βMe) at 4 °C. A second Ni2+-affinity purification removed the tags and TEV protease. Target proteins were then further purified using size exclusion chromatography on a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 75 column (GE Healthcare) for NTD proteins or a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 column (GE Healthcare) for full-length and NTD-deletion proteins. Fractions were identified by SDS-PAGE, and protein concentrations were determined by absorbance at 280 nm using calculated extinction coefficients.

ITC measurement

Calorimetric titrations of full-length or NTD of Hsp90α and their respective mutants with ADP, ATP, or AMPPNP were performed using a MicroCal ITC200 microcalorimeter at 25 °C. Protein samples were dialyzed against the ITC buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 75 mM NaCl, 6 mM MgCl2, and 1 mM Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP). Nucleotides (ADP, ATP, and AMPPNP) were dissolved in the same ITC buffer and adjusted to pH 7.4. For wild-type protein and the T36E mutant, 50–100 µM full-length protein or 100–200 µM NTD protein samples were added to a 200 µl cell, and the 40 µl syringe was filled with 1–2 mM ADP, AMPPNP, or ATP. For Hsp90α T115E and its isolated NTD protein, 20–50 µM full-length protein or 70–500 µM NTD-only protein samples were loaded into the sample cell, while the syringe was filled with 1–2 mM ADP, 2 mM AMPPNP, or 10 mM ATP. Each titration consisted of an initial 1-µl injection followed by 19 subsequent 2-µl injections, with a 120-s equilibration period between injections. Data were analyzed using a one-site binding model and processed with Origin 7.0 software (MicroCal).

Negative stain electron microscopy

All samples were incubated for 1 h at 0.12 µM in an incubation buffer (PBS, pH 7.0) on ice, either with or without AMPPNP/MgCl2 or ADP/MgCl2. To ensure saturation of the Hsp90α-nucleotide complexes, a 10-fold molar excess of AMPPNP or ADP and a 20-fold molar excess of MgCl2 were added to the nucleotide-bound samples. A 4 µl aliquot of each Hsp90α sample (with or without nucleotide) was applied to glow-discharged carbon-film 400-mesh copper grids, then stained using 2% uranyl acetate for 1.5 min. Negative-stained samples were imaged using an FEI TF20 electron microscope operated at 200 keV with a nominal magnification of 62,000×. Micrographs were collected at a defocus of −1.5 µm. Data processing was performed using CyoSPARC (v3.3.2), starting with contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation, followed by manual particle picking to generate a template. Template-based particle picking was subsequently used for 2D classification, 3D reconstruction, and refinement, yielding final electron micrograph maps of Hsp90α in various nucleotide-bound states. All micrograph images were visualized and analyzed using Chimera software.

NMR spectroscopy

All NMR data were recorded on Bruker 850 MHz or 600 MHz spectrometers equipped with cryogenic probes at 25 °C (298 K) or 37 °C (310 K). Data acquisition was performed using Bruker TopSpin Software (version 3.6.2). Data processing was performed using NMRPipe59, with analysis conducted using either NMRFAM-Sparky60 or CcpNmr software (v2.4.2)61. NMR samples were prepared in 50 mM PBS buffer at pH 7.0 containing 7% (V/V) D2O, with protein concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mM. For nucleotide-bound states, a 10-fold molar excess of AMPPNP or ADP and a 20-fold molar excess of MgCl2 were added to 300 µl of 300 µM protein samples.

Backbone chemical shift assignments for apo wild-type Hsp90αNTD was adopted from the reported literature34 and verified by 3D NNH-edited NOESY experiment. For the apo T36E mutant, backbone assignments were primarily transferred from the wild-type Hsp90αNTD based on the high spectral similarity, with 3D HNH- and NNH-edited NOESY experiments used to confirm assignments for residues exhibiting chemical shift perturbations or ambiguity. For the apo T115E mutant, backbone assignments were derived from standard triple-resonance experiments (3D HNCA, HNCOCA, HNCACB and HN(CO)CACB experiments) on a 15N, 13C, 2H labeled sample, due to the presence of more extensive chemical shift perturbations relative to the wild-type protein that precluded reliable assignment transfer. These assignments were further supported by 2D SOFAST-aromatic 1H−13C HMQC and 3D HNH-edited aromatic NOESY spectra. Assignments of the AMPPNP- and ADP-bound states of all constructs were obtained from 3D HNH- and NNH-edited NOESY experiments recorded on uniformly 1H, 15N labeled samples and were further confirmed by nucleotide titration (Supplementary Fig. 24). This workflow yielded backbone assignments for 202, 208, and 193 out of 231 non-proline residues in the apo states of Hsp90αNTD, Hsp90αNTD T36E, and Hsp90αNTD T115E, respectively, corresponding to an assignment yield of approximately 87%. In the nucleotide-bound states, increased conformational dynamics led to a moderate reduction in assignment yield to approximately 80% for wild-type Hsp90αNTD and T36E mutant, and to about 70% for the T115E mutant.

Side-chain methyl assignments for both apo and nucleotide-bound Hsp90αNTD and its mutants (T115E, T36E) were derived from 3D CCH-edited HMQC-NOESY-HMQC and CNH-edited HMQC-NOESY-HMQC experiments. Samples were prepared in a U-[2H, 15N, 12C] background with specific labeling of methyl groups in Ile (δ1), Thr (γ2), Met (ε), Ala (β), Leu (δ1/δ2), Val (γ1/γ2), yielding an average assignment completeness of ~97% for methyl 1H−13C resonances. All NOESY experiments were performed using the SOFAST acquisition schemes with a mixing time of 300 ms in D2O buffer. Typical acquisition parameters included ~80 scans per increment, resulting in an average experiment time of approximately 4 days. A detailed list of acquisition parameters for all NMR experiments is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Structure calculations

Distance restraints were derived from 13C- or 15N-edited NOESY experiments. Dihedral angle restraints (φ and Ψ) were derived using the TALOS+ program based on backbone chemical shift assignments. To assess the presence of multiple conformational states, multi-state structure calculations were first performed using CYANA 3.98, following previously described procedures62–65. Calculations were carried out assuming 1-, 2-, 3-, 4-, or 5-state models. In the multi-state calculations, each restraint could be satisfied by one or more conformers in the ensemble (20 lowest-energy structures out of 100). The resulting target functions were compared to identify the minimal model sufficient to account for the experimental data (e.g., for WT Hsp90αNTD, the target function decreases from 10.26 in the 1-state model to 6.77 in the 2-state model, while models with additional states do not further improve the results). Based on this analysis, a two-state model was selected.

For structural interpretation, NOE restraints were subsequently grouped according to whether they were predominantly satisfied by the lid-open or lid-closed state, and state-specific structure refinements were then performed using the XPLOR-NIH simulated annealing protocol. Hydrogen bond restraints, inferred from secondary structure information derived from backbone chemical shift assignments, were included for structure calculation. These restraints reflected characteristic patterns typical of helices and β-sheets and were incorporated to enhance structural resolution. An initial set of 100 structures was generated and the 20 lowest-energy structures were further refined using a water refinement protocol in CNS. Finally, the 10 lowest-energy structures without violations were selected for the final structure ensemble. The quality of the calculated structures was evaluated using the PROCHECK-NMR program66.

The selection criteria for inclusion in the global fits required three conditions: (i) clear, non-flat dispersion across both static fields, (ii) reproducible profiles between independent replicates, and (iii) stable two-state behavior in single-residue fits.
