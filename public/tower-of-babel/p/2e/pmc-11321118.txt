Inhibition of RAN attenuates influenza a virus replication and nucleoprotein nuclear export
Authors: Lei Cao, Ziwei She, Ya Zhao, Chuxing Cheng, Yaqin Li, Ting Xu, Haiying Mao, Yumei Zhang, Xianfeng Hui, Xian Lin, Ting Wang, Xiaomei Sun, Kun Huang, Lianzhong Zhao, Meilin Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTNuclear export of the viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) is a critical step in the influenza A virus (IAV) life cycle and may be an effective target for the development of anti-IAV drugs. The host factor ras-related nuclear protein (RAN) is known to participate in the life cycle of several viruses, but its role in influenza virus replication remains unknown. In the present study, we aimed to determine the function of RAN in influenza virus replication using different cell lines and subtype strains. We found that RAN is essential for the nuclear export of vRNP, as it enhances the binding affinity of XPO1 toward the viral nuclear export protein NS2. Depletion of RAN constrained the vRNP complex in the nucleus and attenuated the replication of various subtypes of influenza virus. Using in silico compound screening, we identified that bepotastine could dissociate the RAN-XPO1-vRNP trimeric complex and exhibit potent antiviral activity against influenza virus both in vitro and in vivo. This study demonstrates the important role of RAN in IAV replication and suggests its potential use as an antiviral target.KEYWORDS: Influenza A virus, nuclear export, viral ribonucleoprotein, RAN, bepotastine

Introduction

The influenza A virus (IAV) is a major virus that primarily causes acute infections in the upper respiratory tract and less frequently in the lungs, resulting in thousands of deaths worldwide every year [1,2]. The IAV genome is fragmented and comprises eight single-stranded negative-sense RNAs. Each viral RNA molecule is encapsulated by nucleoproteins (NP) and associated with viral polymerases (PA, PB1 and PB2) to form a viral ribonucleoprotein (vRNP) complex [3]. After infection, unlike most other RNA viruses, influenza vRNPs are transported into the nucleus, where transcription and replication of the viral genome ensues. Newly generated PB1, PB2, PA and NP enter the nucleus and assemble with vRNAs to construct progeny vRNPs. Nuclear export of the progeny vRNPs is organized elaborately and is a critical step in the late phase of infection [4]. A “daisy-chain” model proposed that vRNP nuclear export depends on the cellular export receptor XPO1-mediated pathway (also known as CRM1), wherein the viral M1 protein directly binds to vRNP and NS2 acts as a bridge by tethering M1 and XPO1. The nuclear export of vRNP is an active and energy-dependent process that is heavily dependent on the Ras-related nuclear protein RAN [5].

RAN is one of the most abundant and conserved small GTP-bound and hydrolyzed eukaryotic proteins implicated in multiple nuclear processes, including the maintenance of nuclear structure, mRNA processing, protein import and export and cell cycle regulation [6–8]. During the course of host components nuclear export, the transformation between RAN-GTP and RAN-GDP powers the traffic of the XPO1-RanGTP-cargo complex across the NPC [9,10]. Unsurprisingly, RAN has been extensively involved in regulating the life cycles of various viruses. For instance, the L protein of encephalomyocarditis virus (EMCV) interacts with RAN to promote nuclear efflux of proteins required for its infection [11]. Nuclear trafficking of host proteins involved in enterovirus 71 (EV71) replication is performed in an RAN-dependent manner [12]. Human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HSV-1) Rev protein-activated RNA export is linked to the RAN-GTP cycle [13]. Moreover, previous studies have indicated that RAN is involved in the nuclear export of influenza vRNP [5,14]. However, the exact action mechanism of RAN during influenza virus infection and its significance in combating influenza infections remain unclear.

To date, vaccination remains the most effective way to prevent influenza infection, which has remarkably reduced the number of infections each year [15,16]. However, influenza viruses can produce new strains owing to genetic mutations and recombination, which may render the vaccine ineffective [17]. Accordingly, in the case of infection, the use of antiviral drugs has become necessary. Currently, several anti-flu drugs targeting viral components have been approved by the FDA and the regulatory authorities of other countries such as China, Russia, and Japan [18]. Various drug-resistant viruses have emerged owing to long-term drug exposure and have raised widespread concerns [19,20]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop new anti-influenza drugs. It is universally known that IAV must usurp the host factors and processes throughout its life cycle to complete its replication. Indeed, we agree that inhibiting cellular factors or processes essential for viral replication could not only yield a broad-spectrum antiviral effect but also reduce the emergence of resistant virus variants [21,22]. In recent years, many researchers have developed antiviral drugs based on this principle. For example, trifluoromethyl tuberculin (TFMT) inhibits the association of host cap RNAs with IAV PB2 protein by inhibiting MTR1 activity [23]. The host MEK1/2 inhibitor ATR-002 shows broad efficacy against IAV and SARS-CoV-2 by inhibiting viral replication and modulating inflammation [24]. Furthermore, many compounds have been discovered to inhibit the vRNP nuclear export process to block the replication of viruses, such as the XPO1 antagonist KPT-335 (Verdinexor) and the NP inhibitor Naproxen [25,26]. These newly identified anti-influenza drugs have shown promise for the treatment of influenza infections. Considering that RAN-GTP is indispensable for the binding of XPO1 with its cargo NS2 and the ensuing nuclear export of vRNP, the development of RAN inhibitors would be a plausible strategy to block this process.

In this study, we showed that silencing RAN expression heavily attenuated the replication of influenza virus in transformed and primary cells as well as in a mouse model. Mechanistically, knockdown of RAN caused the retention of vRNP in the nucleus and prevented the nuclear transport of vRNP. Furthermore, we demonstrated that RAN enhanced the binding affinity of XPO1 for NS2, thereby promoting the nuclear export of the vRNP complex. More importantly, based on structural modelling, we discovered bepotastine acts as an RAN inhibitor and exhibits potent antiviral activity against influenza infection both in vitro and in vivo. Collectively, our findings improve the understanding of the role of RAN in the nuclear export of influenza vRNP and suggest the potential utility of bepotastine as an antiviral agent.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and virus

DF-1, MDCK, 293 T, Hela cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Thermofisher, #11965092), A549 cells were cultured in Ham’s F12 medium (Thermofisher, #11765054). All these transformed cell lines were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The primary human type II alveolar epithelial and NK cells were provided by Pricella (Pricella, # CP-H209, # CP-H168) and were cultured in a specific complete culture medium (Pricella, # CM-H209, # CM-H168). The primary chicken embryo fibroblast (CEF) cells were isolated from 9-day-old embryonated eggs and were cultured in DMEM. All basic culture media were supplemented with 10% FBS (PAN-Biotech, #P30–3306) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2. The IAV strains used were A/duck/Hubei/WH18/2015 (H5N6-JX) (GenBank accession number KX652135), A/chicken/Guangxi/97/2017 (H7N9-GX), A/Hunan/Hubei/3/2005 (H3N2-HB), A/chicken/Hubei/327/2004 (H5N1-DW), A/Puerto Rico/8/34 (H1N1-PR8), A/Swine /Henan/05/2018 (H1N1-HN) and the luciferase-expressing reporter influenza virus (PR8-Gluc). A/WSN/1933 (H1N1-WSN) virus was rescued from the pHW2000 plasmids system and the H5N6-GFP recombinant virus was constructed based on the H5N6-JX strain. All viruses were amplified using 10-day-old embryonated eggs and stored in our laboratory.

In vivo experiments

For siRNA treatment in vivo, two cholesterol-conjugated and 2′ - OME modified RAN-siRNA (siRAN-1, target sequence 5′-GAAUAAAGUUGUAUUUCAAAU-3′ and siRAN-2, target sequence 5′-GCAACAAAGUGGAUAUUAAAG-3′, synthesized by AuGCT), were nasally instilled to 4- to 6-week-old BALB/c female SPF mice at days – 2 and 0. On day 2, mice in each group (n = 3) were anesthetized and sacrificed, and mice lungs were homogenized (1:10 dilution) for western blotting with anti-RAN antibody. Next, the BALB/c mice were nasally instilled with the siRAN-1 (5 nmol) or siNC at days 0, 2 and 4, while at 6 h after the first treatment, the mice were challenged with 10 LD50 of PR8 or mock-infected as control. Body weight and survival of the mice were recorded daily for 1 week before infection and 2 more weeks post-infection (n = 7).

For bepotastine treatment in vivo, 4 to 6-week-old BALB/c female SPF mice were randomly selected for intraperitoneal injection with 50 μL bepotastine (2, 10 or 50 mg/kg, dissolved in 1% DMSO), 50 μL oseltamivir (10 mg/kg, dissolved in 1% DMSO) or 50 μL 1% DMSO from day – 5 to 10. At day 0, mice were intranasally inoculated with 10 LD50 of PR8 50 μL, or with 50 μL PBS as non-infection control. Body weight and survival of the mice were recorded daily for 1 week before infection and 2 more weeks post-infection (n = 7). At day 5 post-infection, 3 mice in each group were anesthetized and sacrificed, and the lungs were collected for homogenate in 1 mL PBS (contains 5% penicillin–streptomycin) or fixed in 4% formaldehyde. The homogenized samples were centrifuged at 4°C 15,000 g for 10 min. Then the supernatant was used for measurement of viral titre on MDCK cells. The fixed mice lungs were used for H&E staining and immunohistochemistry analysis.

This study was conducted in strict accordance with the recommendations provided in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China. All animal experiments were approved by the Research Ethics Committee, Huazhong Agricultural University, Hubei, China (approval no. HZAUMO-2024–0044 and HZAUMO-2024-0049).

Plasmids

The chicken RAN and XPO1 genes were inserted into the eukaryotic expression vector pCMV-3×Flag and pCAGGS-HA respectively, and the final recombinant vector was referred to as Flag-RAN and HA-XPO1. The Flag-RAN-M with the synonymous mutation was generated from Flag-RAN. The human RAN and XPO1 genes were cloned into the pCMV-3xFlag, pGEX-KG, and pCAGGS-HA plasmids and designated as Flag-huRAN, GST-huRAN and HA-huXPO1, respectively. The H5N6-NS2 was cloned into the pEGFP-N1 vector and termed EGFP-NS2. The PCR primers were designed by Primer 5, and all plasmid constructs were confirmed by sequencing. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting chicken and human RAN were purchased from Tsingke (Beijing, China). The knockdown efficiency was examined by western blotting. The primer and siRNA sequences are listed in Table S2 in the supplemental material.

Antibodies and reagents

The antibodies used were anti-RAN rabbit monoclonal antibody (Proteintech, #10469-1-AP); anti-HA, anti-GFP, anti-GAPDH, anti-β actin and anti-GST mouse monoclonal antibodies (Proteintech, #66006-2-Ig, #66002-1-Ig, #60004-1-Ig, #66009-1-Ig and #66001-2-Ig respectively); anti-Flag mouse monoclonal antibody (Sigma, #F1804); anti-XPO1 and anti-laminB1 rabbit polyclonal antibody (Abclonal, #A0299 and #A11495); anti-IAV PB1, NP, NS2 and M1 rabbit polyclonal antibodies (GeneTex, #GTX125923, #GTX125989, #GTX125953 and #GTX125928 respectively); and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-mouse (Proteintech, #SA00013-1) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-rabbit (Proteintech, #SA00013-4) secondary antibodies. The small molecule compounds used in this study were: DAPI (DAPI dihydrochloride; 1:1,000) (Beyotime, #C1002) and leptomycin B (10 ng/mL) (MCE, #HY-16909); Baf-A1 (Bafilomycin A1; 100 nM) (MCE, #HY-100558); CHX (cycloheximide; 100 μg/mL) (Sigma, #66819); Sialidase (5 units/mL) (Sigma, #10269611001); TPCK (Tosylsulfonyl phenylalanyl chloromethyl ketone-treated trypsin; 2.5 μg/mL) (Worthington, #LS003740); Bepotastine (TargetMol, #T7893); Oseltamivir phosphate (TargetMol, #T1486).

Virus manipulation

After reaching 80% confluence, the cells were washed with PBS twice, followed by incubation with the diluted viruses for 1 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. The viral diluent was removed and replaced with a medium containing 1% penicillin–streptomycin without FBS. All experiments with the H5N1-DW, H5N6-JX and H7N9-GX viruses were performed in a biosafety level 3 laboratory (BSL3). This study was carried out per the recommendations of the BSL3 laboratory at Huazhong Agricultural University (HZAU). All procedures were approved by the Intuitional Biosafety Committee of HZAU.

Virus binding and internalization

Virus binding and internalization analyses were performed as described previously [27]. As a control, DF-1 cells were pretreated with sialidase at 37°C for 12 h. The DF-1 cells transfected with siRNAs were then incubated with H5N6-JX virus at an MOI of 10 at 4°C for 1 h and then washed with ice-cold PBS. Cell lysates were prepared for western blotting. For virus internalization, cells were warmed to 37°C for 30 min after being incubated with the virus at 4°C for 1 h, followed by cold PBS-HCl (pH = 1.3) washing to remove the attached virus and detect only internalized virus particles. Cell lysates were also prepared to determine influenza virus matrix protein 1 (M1) levels by western blotting.

Transfection and virus titration

Plasmids and siRNA transfections were done using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, # 11668500) in all cells maintained in Opti-MEM. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with 10% FBS after 6 h post-transfection. For virus titration of IAV, viral supernatants were harvested at the indicated time points, diluted serially in DMEM, and adsorbed onto MDCK cell monolayers seeded in 96-well plates. After 1 h of adsorption, the inoculum was removed, and cells were washed with PBS and overlaid with DMEM containing trypsin-TPCK (Worthington) or not. The plates were incubated at 37°C for 72 h. Virus titres were determined by calculating log10 TCID50/milliliter on MDCK cells using the Spearman-Karber method [28].

Fluorescence and indirect immunofluorescence imaging

For fluorescence imaging, siNC and siRAN DF-1 cells were infected with the H5N6-GFP (MOI = 0.01) virus. At 24 hpi, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then were permeabilized with 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 prepared in PBS, followed by nucleus staining with Hoechst (blue). Fluorescence images (GFP and Hoechst) were taken by immunofluorescence microscopy (EVOS FL Auto; ThermoFisher).

For indirect immunofluorescence imaging, the cells were grown on coverslips and transfected with the indicated plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 or treated with the indicated compounds. Then the cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized using 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 15 min, and blocked with 1% (v/w) BSA for 30 min. Coverslips were incubated with primary antibodies for 2 h and then with the appropriate Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h before the nuclei were stained with DAPI (Beyotime) for 15 min at room temperature. The stained samples were observed using a confocal microscope (LSM 880; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

FISH for influenza virus vRNA was performed as described previously [29]. In brief, FISH probes labelled with the Quasar 570 fluorophore for detecting H5N6-JX M segment vRNA were designed by using the online probe designer (Stellaris Probe Designer version 4.2). The probes were purchased from Biosearch Technologies (Novato, CA, USA). DF-1 cells were infected with the H5N6-JX virus at an MOI of 10 in a 12-well plate and were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Then the samples were permeabilized with 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 and washed thrice with PBS. Next, the cells were incubated with 200 μl of wash buffer A (SMF-WA1-60, Biosearch Technologies, USA) for 5 min. After that, 300 μl of hybridization buffer (SMF-HB1-10, Biosearch Technologies, USA) containing 3 μl of FISH probes (final concentration, 12.5 nM) was added and incubated for 16 h at 37°C in the dark. The hybridization buffer was removed, and the cells were then incubated with wash buffer A for 5 min at 37°C. Images were obtained with a confocal microscope (SIM/STORM, Nikon, Japan).

Structure-based RAN inhibitor screening

Structure-based virtual screening for RAN inhibitors was conducted using molecular docking against 4,655 bioactive compounds from the DrugBank database. Docking simulations were performed using the LibDock programme (Discovery Studio), setting a grid box within the binding region of the RAN protein and XPO1 complex crystal structure (PDB ID: 4HAT). Only FDA-approved drugs were considered in the analysis, and compounds with a LibDock Score exceeding 100 were prioritized for subsequent in vitro studies.

Molecular dynamics simulations

XPO1 binds to cargo proteins at the nuclear export signal (NES) region, specifically the H11-H12 interval [30]. Using Zdock, we docked the NS2 and XPO1 proteins, with XPO1 structures selected under conditions of RAN presence (PDB: 3GJX) [31] and absence of RAN (PDB: 4FGV) [32], thereby simulating scenarios with and without RAN. After examining the docking results, conformations with reasonable binding sites were chosen for molecular dynamics simulations. The CHARMM36 force field was applied to the systems, and the systems were set to Explicit Periodic Boundary conditions. A 0.145 M sodium chloride solution was added to equilibrate the system. The specific simulation steps were as follows: first, a preliminary minimization of 1000 steps were conducted using the Steepest Descent algorithm, with an RMS Gradient of 1.0; followed by a secondary minimization of 2000 steps using the Adopted Basis NR algorithm, with an RMS Gradient of 0.1. Subsequently, a 100ps heating simulation was performed from 0 to 300 K, followed by equilibration for 100 ps. Simulations were then conducted for 600 ps at 300 K and constant pressure, with a sampling interval of 2 fs. RMSD and binding energy of the NS2 and XPO1 binding region were calculated, and the binding surface area (BSA) was computed using PyMOL scripts.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

The harvested lungs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then paraffin-embedded. Each paraffin-embedded tissue sample was sectioned into 5-μm-thick sections. The tissue section was deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE) for the examination for histopathological analysis. The lung sections were assessed using an AxioVert (200 M; Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) optical microscope. Stained slides were scanned using a Pannoramic slide scanner (Pannoramic 250/MIDI) and the morphological changes were assessed by a semi-quantitative score. For the scoring, a dual histopathology scoring system was used to assess pathological severity [33].

For immunohistochemistry experiments. Lung sections were washed three times in PBS, permeabilized using 0.3% (v/v) Triton X-100 for 30 min at room temperature, and then washed with PBS three more times. Sections were blocked in 5% normal goat serum in 0.05% (v/v) PBS-Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature. Primary anti-IAV NP antibodies (GeneTex, 1:500) were added and the samples were incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS five more times, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated AffiniPure goat anti-rabbit (Proteintech, 1:400) at room temperature for 1 h. The sections were washed with PBS and mounted onto slides. Images were obtained from a Pannoramic slide scanner (Pannoramic 250/MIDI).

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in Western and IP Cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013J) after two PBS washes. Proteins were quantified by standardization. Each protein sample was separated using 10-12% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. Subsequently, the membrane was immersed in 2% (v/w) BSA for 1 h at ambient temperatures and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies successively. Finally, images were captured using an ECL detection system (Tannon, Beijing, China). All experiments were repeated at least three times, and a representative result was shown.

Immunoprecipitation assay

DF-1 and HEK293 T cells were transfected with the indicated plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 and/or treated with the indicated compounds. At 24 h post-transfection, the cells were lysed in Western and IP Cell lysis buffer (Beyotime) with the addition of a protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE, #HY-K0010) on ice. Subsequently, a portion of the cell lysate was used in the immunoprecipitation experiment by incubating it with anti-HA (MCE, #HY-K0201), anti-Flag (MCE, #HY-K0207) and anti-GFP (Beyotime, #P2132) magnetic beads at 4°C overnight while the remaining cell lysate was used as immunoprecipitation input.

The endogenous immunoprecipitation was conducted using anti-RAN, anti-XPO1 or anti-NS2 antibodies with rabbit IgG antibody as a control. The cell lysate and antibody mixtures were incubated at 4°C overnight. On the next day, pretreated protein A/G agarose (Santa Cruz, #SC-2003) was added to the mixtures and incubated at 4°C for another 2 h with rotation. The immunoprecipitated proteins and input cell lysates were analyzed by western blotting as described above.

GST/Streptavidin pulldown and immunoprecipitation assay

The biotinylated bepotastine (Biotin-bepotastine) was synthesized from QIYUE-Bio (Xi’an, China). The Glutathione S-transferase (GST)-fused huRAN or GST proteins were expressed from Escherichia coli and purified by glutathione-Sepharose beads (GE Healthcare, #17075601). Purified GST and GST-RAN proteins were analyzed by SDS-PAGE using Coomassie blue staining. For the GST pulldown experiments, HEK293 T cells in six 10-cm dishes were transfected with HA-XPO1 plasmids (10 μg per dish). At 36 h post-transfection, the cells in each dish were harvested and lysed with 500 μl of IP lysis buffer. The purified GST-RAN proteins were mixed with 50 μl of glutathione-Sepharose beads and rotated for 3 h at 4°C. After five washes with the buffer, the lysates of HEK293 T cells with or without bepotastine treatment (10 mM) were added to GST-bound beads and incubated with rotation for 2 h at 4°C. After five washes, the bound proteins were eluted and analyzed by western blotting. For the streptavidin pulldown and endogenous immunoprecipitation experiments, the biotin or biotin-bepotastine was incubated with streptavidin magnetic beads (MCE, #HY-K0208) by gently rocking at 4°C for 3 h. After five more washes with TBS buffer, the biotin or biotin-bepotastine-bound streptavidin magnetic beads were added to reaction buffer containing 5 μg purified GST-RAN or GST proteins or HEK293 T lysates followed by another 2 h incubation at 4°C. After five washes, the immunoprecipitated proteins and input cell lysates were subjected to western blotting analysis.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractionation

A549 cells were harvested in ice-cold PBS and collected by centrifugation at 1,500 rpm for 5 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in hypotonic lysis buffer (10 mM HEPES-NaOH [pH = 7.9], 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2 and 0.5 mM beta-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (MCE), vortexed, and then incubated on ice for 15 min followed by the addition of 3 μl 10% NP-40 for another 5 min. Samples were centrifuged at 16,000 g for 10 min, the supernatants were cytoplasmic proteins, and the pellets were for nuclear protein extraction. Pellets were washed with cold PBS twice with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer added (Beyotime, #P0013B) as nuclear lysis buffer, and they were then dispersed and incubated on ice for 20 min. The extractions were centrifuged at 16,000 g for 10 min for nuclear proteins. The proteins were further subjected to western blotting analysis.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using a colorimetric-based cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (MCE, #HY-K0301). In brief, cells in 96-well plates were treated with siRNAs or compounds, and cell viability was measured at 24 h or 36 h after treatment. We added 10 μl of CCK-8 reagent to each well of the plates, and cells were incubated at 37°C for 4 h. The absorbance of the reaction product in each well was measured using a microplate reader at 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the means ± SD of at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed by determining p values using the unpaired two-tailed Student t-test or two-way ANOVA (ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001).

Results

Knockdown of RAN inhibits the proliferation of IAV

The GTPase RAN plays an essential role in the nucleocytoplasmic shuttle of the protein and is closely involved in influenza vRNP nuclear export [5,34]. Therefore, it would be interesting to investigate the effects of RAN on influenza virus replication. Firstly, we designed two small interfering RNA (siRNAs) to knock down avian RAN expression levels and tested their efficiency in avian DF-1 cells. As shown in Figure 1A, both siRNAs caused a satisfactory reduction of RAN protein, and siRAN-1, which showed no apparent cytotoxicity by a CCK8 assay (Figure 1A), was selected for the subsequent single-group knockdown experiments. Next, we demonstrated that RAN knockdown significantly attenuated the proliferation of avian H5N6-JX virus. This result was consistent with the reduction in viral PB1 and NP protein levels in RAN-knockdown cells (Figure 1B and C). We obtained similar results in freshly isolated primary chicken embryo fibroblasts (CEF), which showed reduced expression of RAN, hampering H5N6-JX viral production (Figure 1D and E). Two other avian strains, H5N1-DW and H7N9-GX, were also used to evaluate the effect of RAN on influenza virus replication, and the results exclusively supported our conclusion (Figure 1F-I). These results indicate a broad proviral role of RAN in IAV. However, in the setting of RAN overexpression, we found that the ectopic introduction of RAN had no expected proviral effect on H5N6-JX replication (Figure 1J and K), probably because endogenous RAN was saturated to support the life cycle of influenza. To address this, we restored RAN expression in siRAN-transfected cells by introducing an RAN-expressing vector mismatched with the siRAN sequence (Figure 1L). In this rescue model, we found that the impaired replication of H5N6-JX due to RAN depletion could be reversed by supplementation with exogenous RAN (Figure 1M and N), indicating an on-target effect of RAN on viral replication. Finally, we infected RAN-knockdown DF-1 cells with a recombinant GFP-expressing H5N6 virus to evaluate the proviral effect of RAN. The results showed that knockdown of RAN resulted in a significant reduction in the GFP signal of the H5N6-GFP virus compared to that in the control cells (Figure 1O). These data suggest that RAN knockdown inhibits the proliferation of various subtypes of influenza viruses in DF-1 cells.

RAN facilitates the nuclear export of vRNP and vRNA

RAN is involved in nucleocytoplasmic transport and participates in both the import and export from the nucleus of host proteins and RNAs as well as influenza vRNP [7,35]. Next, we aimed to assess which process was responsible for RAN-mediated regulation of vRNP transportation using immunofluorescence assays. Cycloheximide (CHX) is a protein synthesis inhibitor used to restore the production of progeny NP or vRNP. At 2 h post infection (hpi), NP appeared to accumulate in the nuclei of both siRAN and siNC cells, in contrast to its retention in the cytoplasm by the nuclear import inhibitor Baf-A1 (Figure 2A) [36]. To examine NP nuclear export, we used the nuclear export inhibitor leptomycin B as a positive control [37]. At 6 hpi, most NP shifted to the cytoplasm, as observed in the control cells. In the RAN-deficient or leptomycin-treated cells, NP appeared to be confined to the nucleus and accumulated near the nuclear membrane (Figure 2B). Therefore, it appears unlikely that RAN participates in the early nuclear import of NP but rather in their later nuclear export. Lastly, using viral binding and internalization assays, we showed that the entry of viral M protein was not influenced by the knockdown of RAN but by sialidase treatment, indicating that RAN was not involved in the membrane binding or internalization of the virus (Figure 2C).

To better understand the effects of RAN on the nuclear export of vRNP, we dynamically monitored the distribution of NP in siRAN- or siNC- transfected and virus-infected cells. At the early infection stage of 2 hpi, NP accumulated in the nucleus, regardless of the introduction of siRAN (Figure 2D). At 4 hpi, NP translocated from the nucleus into the cytoplasm, resulting in both nuclear and cytoplasmic distribution in the control infected cells; however, NP nucleus retention was observed in 76% of the RAN-deficient DF-1 cells (Figure 2D and F). As time elapsed, NP accumulated in the cytoplasm, and its proportion in the nucleus decreased in the control cells. Contrastingly, NP nuclear retention was still observed in RAN-deficient cells, even at 10 hpi (Figure 2D). The distribution of viral M RNA was evaluated using an in situ hybridization assay. At 4 hpi, M vRNAs were located in the nuclei of both negative control and RAN-silenced cells (Figure 2E). At 6 and 8 hpi, it was apparent that the M vRNAs entered the cytoplasm in most of the control cells (91%) but were confined to the nucleus in RAN-silenced and leptomycin B-treated cells (Figure 2E and G). Taken together, these data demonstrate that the knockdown of RAN significantly inhibits the nuclear export of vRNP and vRNA, leading to remarkable retardation of the influenza virus life cycle.

RAN interacts with XPO1 and strengthens the stability of the XPO1 and NS2 complex

Previously, nuclear export of influenza vRNP was achieved using the XPO1 export machinery and its co-factor RAN [9,38]. In agreement with previous findings, we showed that RAN could be immunoprecipitated using an anti-XPO1 antibody and vice versa (Figure 3A-D). Moreover, RAN colocalized with XPO1 in the nucleus (Figure 3E). These results confirmed the interaction between RAN and XPO1 in cells. Viral NS2 acts as an adaptor linking XPO1 and M1/vRNP, which is essential for vRNP trafficking between the cytoplasm and nucleus [5,39]. We investigated whether RAN influences the association between XPO1 and NS2. To this end, we performed endogenous co-IP experiments on RAN-knockdown DF-1 cells infected with IAV. The results showed that when XPO1 was used as a bait, the amount of immunoprecipitated NS2 was largely reduced by silencing RAN expression (Figure 3F). We obtained similar results upon co-immunoprecipitation with an anti-NS2 antibody (Figure 3G). In the overexpression system, we also demonstrated that RAN knockdown weakened the interaction between XPO1 and NS2, regardless of the bait protein (Figure 3H and I). These results indicate that apart from energy supply, RAN can stabilize the XPO1-cargo complex and facilitate vRNP nuclear export. To support our conclusion, we selected the XPO1-NS2 complex conformation with higher docking scores and performed a 600ps molecular dynamics simulation for both RAN-containing and non-RAN-containing complexes; the information of binding region between NS2 and XPO1 in the results was mainly obtained. Results showed that the binding energy between NS2 and XPO1 was – 374.565 kcal/mol in the presence of RAN, much lower than in the absence of RAN (-267.665 kcal/mol; Figure 3J). In addition, the root mean square deviation (RMSD) value was also lower in the presence of RAN (Figure 3K). These data indicate that RAN contributes to the stable interaction between XPO1 and NS2. We noticed that the absence of RAN increased the contact area between NS2 and XPO1 (Figure 3L). We inferred that the addition of RAN likely caused conformational changes in the XPO1 binding region. Taken together, our results suggest that RAN promotes the binding of XPO1 and NS2 and plays a critical role in influenza vRNP nuclear translocation.

Characterization of various influenza virus replication mechanisms in RAN-deficient human cells and mice

RAN is highly conserved with a 99.5% homology between avian and mammalian species (Figure S1A). We investigated whether RAN exerts a pro-influenza effect on human cells. First, three different siRNAs targeting human RAN were designed to knockdown their expression in A549 cells, and the best one (sihuRAN-3) was selected for further experiments (Figure S1B). A549 cells were transfected with siRNAs for 36 h, followed by infection with various IAV subtypes, including H1N1-PR8, H1N1-WSN, H3N2-HB, H5N1-DW, H5N6-JX and H7N9-GX. All the tested viruses showed nuclear retention of vRNPs and an apparent reduction in progeny viral titres in the context of RAN deficiency (Figure 4A-F). Further, a Eurasian bird-like H1N1 swine influenza virus (H1N1-HN) [40] showed significantly reduced virus production in RAN knockdown A549 cells (Figure S1C and D). In addition, we found that reduced RAN expression in human type II alveolar epithelial cells (PAEC) also led to impaired replication of the influenza virus (Figure S1E and F).

Next, we investigated whether RAN knockdown has an antiviral effect on IAV infection in vivo. We obtained a mouse RAN-targeting siRNA with approximately 46.3% (simRAN-2) knockdown efficiency in vivo (Figure 4G and H) and used it for subsequent experiments. Next, BALB/c mice were nasally instilled with simRAN-2 or siNCs. On days 0, 2 and 4 after the first treatment, the mice were challenged with or without 10 LD50 of PR8 (Figure 4I). Body weight loss was alleviated upon simRAN administration (Figure 4J). Moreover, RAN suppression prolonged the survival period by approximately 2 days and protected 28.5% of the mice from death at such a high influenza dose (Figure 4K). Together, these data suggest that blocking RAN expression yields broad anti-flu activities, both in vitro and in vivo, which could be harnessed to develop antiviral drugs against influenza infections.

Identification of bepotastine as an effective RAN inhibitor for IAV replication

Given that the association of RAN with XPO1 is involved in the initiation of vRNP nuclear export, the entire vRNP nuclear transposition is paralyzed by disrupting the conformation of the RAN-XPO1 complex. We hypothesized that occupation of the RAN-binding pocket by a compound other than XPO1 could hinder vRNP nuclear export, thereby attenuating viral replication. To identify such a compound, we performed in silico screening of 4655 compounds in a chemical database (DrugBank; https://go.drugbank.com) (Figure 5A and B), wherein we used the crystal structure of RAN and XPO1 [Protein Data Bank identifier (PDB ID): 4HAT] (Figure 5A) [41] for molecular docking studies. The output results included those for 1367 compounds, 140 of which had affinity scores >100. Subsequently, 72 commercially available compounds were selected for anti-IAV activity testing in A549 and MDCK cells using a Gluc-expressing PR8 recombinant virus. In this small-scale screening, nine drugs, namely tannic acid, montelukast, vitamin K2, methotrexate, aprepitant, nelfinavir, salmeterol, bepotastine and saquinavir, exhibited steadily increased anti-influenza activities at concentrations of 5, 10 and 20 μM but without detectable cytotoxicity (Table S1 and Figure S2A). Among these, nelfinavir, salmeterol, tannic acid and saquinavir have been reported to have anti-IAV activity [42–44]. Furthermore, we examined the effects of the nine candidates on vRNP translocation using confocal microscopy. Based on the results, we concluded that bepotastine treatment resulted in the best nuclear retention of vRNP compared with the other compounds (Figure S2B) and was selected for further detailed studies.

To obtain additional evidence that bepotastine targets the RAN-binding site, we used ligand–target modelling to obtain a detailed view of the predicted interactions between the compound and this site. We compared the predicted binding energies of the compound to the XPO1 active site based on the ligand-RAN crystal structure [(PDB ID): (4HAT)]. Bepotastine is predicted to interact extensively with several amino acid residues in RAN. It can be observed that Arg76 interacts with bepotastine through its side chain donor to form a hydrogen bond; certain hydrophobic groups on the bepotastine molecule (such as aromatic rings) closely interact with the hydrophobic groove region on the surface of the RAN. Tyr80, Ala67 and Trp104 bind primarily to bepotastine via hydrophobic interactions, whereas Asp77 and Asp107 interact with bepotastine via electrostatic interactions. The predicted binding positions in the 4HAT structure closely matched the crystallographic binding modes (Figure 5B and C). Subsequently, using the PR8-Gluc tool virus, we determined that the median inhibitory concentration (IC50) of bepotastine for IAV infection was 9.255 μM in A549 cells and the half-cytotoxicity concentration (CC50) was 605.95 μM, yielding a selectivity index of 65.5 (Figure 5D). Bepotastine demonstrated consistently considerable antiviral activity in MDCK cells, with an IC50 of 70.44 μM (Figure 5E). The CC50 was higher than 562.6 μM, yielding a selectivity index of 7.99. In both A549 and MDCK cells, bepotastine effectively inhibited PR8 replication, and the effect was comparable to that of oseltamivir; the inhibitory effect was dose-dependent (Figure 5F-I). We evaluated the antiviral role of bepotastine in human type II alveolar epithelial cells and cord blood NK cells. We infected both cell lines with PR8 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1 for 24 h, followed by measurement of viral titre and NP protein levels, showing that the replication of influenza virus was inhibited by bepotastine (Figure 5J and K). Importantly, in A549 cells, the anti-IAV activity of bepotastine was observed in response to infection with H1N1-PR8, H1N1-WSN, H3N2-HB, H5N1-DW, H5N6-JX and H7N9-GX (Figure S3A-F). These data suggest that bepotastine has a broad anti-IAV activity with non-cellular specificity.

Bepotastine is associated with RAN and impedes its binding affinity for XPO1

Next, we examined whether bepotastine inhibits the replication of influenza virus as a consequence of RAN-XPO1 on-target dependency. First, A549 cells were infected with PR8 in the absence or presence of bepotastine for 6 h, and the cellular distribution of NP was detected using an immunofluorescence assay. As expected, bepotastine treatment led to the apparent retention of NP in the nucleus (Figure 6A). Statistical analysis showed that the nucleus-only cells accounted for approximately 15% of control cells, much less than the 72.3% of bepotastine-treated cells and 95% of the positive control leptomycin B-treated cells (Figure 6B). After nuclear/cytosolic fractionation, immunoblotting indicated higher NP protein levels in the nuclei of bepotastine-treated cells than in those of the control cells (Figure 6C). Nuclear retention of NP by bepotastine was reproduced in human type II alveolar epithelial cells (Figure 6D and E). This indicated that bepotastine can impair vRNP nuclear export during IAV infection, prompting us to investigate whether the action of bepotastine affects the RAN-XPO1 directed pathway. Accordingly, we prepared biotinylated bepotastine and performed an immunoprecipitate assay using GST-huRAN. In a buffer system, purified GST-huRAN could be pulled down by biotinylated bepotastine but not by free biotin or by GST tag peptides (Figure 6F). Furthermore, in the cell transfection model, endogenously expressed RAN was precipitated by biotin-bepotastine (Figure 6G). These results indicated a direct interaction between RAN and bepotastine. To verify whether bepotastine can prevent the formation of the RAN/XPO1 complex, HEK293 T cells were co-transfected with HA-huXPO1 and Flag-huRAN in the absence or presence of bepotastine, followed by a co-IP assay. The amount of RAN immunoprecipitated with XPO1 gradually decreased with increasing bepotastine doses, and vice versa (Figure 6H and I), suggesting a weakened interaction between RAN and XPO1 following bepotastine treatment. To find out which amino acid in RAN was responsible for bepotastine binding, we generated four RAN variants by A67P, Y80P or W104P single mutation and A67P-Y80P-W104P tri-mutation, followed by a GST-pull down assay. The results showed that the bepotastine severely impaired the pulled down efficacy of HA-XPO1 by wild type RAN, A67P and W104P variants but not by Y80P and A67P-Y80P-W104P variants (Figure 6J), indicating the Tyr80 might be a key amino acid for RAN binding to bepotastine. These results demonstrate that bepotatine inhibits influenza vRNP nuclear export by disrupting the XPO1-RAN interactions in transformed and primary human cells.

Bepotastine protects mice against lethal influenza infection

In vivo studies were conducted using the PR8 infection model to evaluate the antiviral effects of bepotastine therapy. Mice were treated according to the protocol shown in Figure 7A. Three different concentrations of bepotastine (2, 10 and 50 mg/kg, respectively) were used to treat the mice and oseltamivir treatment was used as a positive control. We observed that the weight loss of all bepotastine-treated mice was alleviated as compared with the DMSO-treated mice (Figure 7B). In addition, all mice in the vehicle treatment group succumbed to the infection on day 8. As a comparison, 2 mg/kg bepotastine treatment prolonged mice survival for 2 to 3 days, however high dose of bepotastine injection (10 and 50 mg/kg) had protected 28.6%-42.9% of mice from death (Figure 7C). In line with these observations, bepotastine significantly reduced the influenza viral load in mouse lungs, as determined by TCID50 measurements and NP protein immunofluorescence staining (Figure 7D and E), and resulted in attenuated inflammatory cell infiltration, alveolar atrophy, and fibrosis (Figure 7F). The lung pathological score was also significantly lower in the bepotastine-treated mice than in the control group (Figure 7G). However, oseltamivir treatment conferred better protection to the infected mice and more effectively prevented weight loss than the bepotastine and DMSO treatments, achieving a survival rate of 100%. Collectively, these results indicate that bepotastine treatment restricts influenza replication in mice.

Discussion

Currently, there are several FDA-approved drugs, such as oseltamivir, zanamivir, peramivir and baloxavir marboxil, for IAV treatment against viral proteins; however, treatment with drugs targeting viral components could result in the generation of drug-resistant strains during clinical usage [19,20,45]. Host-directed antiviral drugs are designed to target IAV replication dependence and are less likely to induce resistance. In this study, we demonstrated that RAN is indispensable for the nuclear export of IAV vRNP and that inhibition of RAN yields broad antiviral effects. We showed that RAN promoted the association of the XPO1-vRNP complex by strengthening the XPO1-NS2 interaction, thereby promoting vRNP nuclear export. We discovered that a compound, bepotastine, acts as a barrier between RAN and XPO1, which effectively inhibits viral replication both in vivo and in vitro.

The influenza virus hijacks the host nuclear trafficking machinery, which is precisely controlled by host factors and viral components. Our earlier studies demonstrated that host CHD3 interacts with NS2 and contributes to vRNP assembly and subsequent nuclear export [46]. PRC2 promotes the association of M1 with vRNP by directly binding to M1, which in turn favours the nuclear export of vRNP [47]. Generally, host factors facilitate vRNP assembly by recruiting NS2 or M1 proteins. RAN, a small GTPase of 216 amino acids, belongs to the ras superfamily and is highly conserved in animal genomes, including those of chickens, humans and mice [48]. It is well documented that RAN provides energy for the nucleocytoplasmic transport of cellular proteins and RNAs as well as for invading viral proteins [35,49]. Previous studies have shown that RAN participates in the life cycle of several other viruses, including EMCV [11] and EV71 [12], however, the role of RAN in influenza virus replication has not been studied. In the present study, we extensively evaluated the function of RAN in influenza virus replication in different cell lines and subtype strains, showing that RAN knockdown heavily compromised virus replication. According to our data, this can be explained by the nuclear retention of vRNP caused by RAN depletion. Nuclear export of the progeny vRNP is indispensable for IAV life cycle completion, which is regulated by the XPO1 export machinery. In the CRM1-vRNP complex, the viral M1 protein interacts directly with vRNP, and NS2 acts as an adaptor that tethers M1-vRNP and XPO1. It is widely believed that RAN combines with XPO1 to provide an impetus for vRNP trafficking into the cytoplasm of progeny. However, it remains unclear whether RAN plays other roles in vRNP trafficking. Our study indicates that RAN is neither involved in the early steps of IAV binding and internalization nor in vRNP nuclear import. Instead, we demonstrated that RAN depletion resulted in the accumulation of NP or M1 vRNA in the nucleus, consistent with previous findings [5]. More importantly, we found that reduced RAN expression attenuated the association of XPO1 with NS2, suggesting an important role of RAN in stabilizing the XPO1-NS2 interaction and the XPO1-cargo complex. Additionally, a molecular dynamics simulation underpinned our finding that the binding affinity between free XPO1 and NS2 was enhanced by the addition of RAN, resulting in a more centralized distribution of the binding patterns. Previous studies have demonstrated that abolishing the protein–protein interactions between XPO1 and NS2 leads to severe growth arrest in the influenza virus [50]. Therefore, we propose that RAN-GTP contributes to the nuclear export most likely via two aspects, probably by providing energy and strengthening the XPO1-cargo complex.

Given that influenza vRNP nuclear export of influenza vRNP is an essential part of the viral replication circuit, blocking the formation of the XPO1/RAN-cargo complex is a promising strategy to combat IAV infection. Recently, virtual screening coupled with functional analysis has shown tremendous promise in the discovery of antivirals [23,51,52]. Therefore, we screened in silico compounds that could stack on the binding pocket of XPO1 for RAN. After antiviral testing, nine compounds with effective anti-flu activities were obtained. However, only bepotastine was found to have the potential to inhibit vRNP nuclear export, suggesting a more complicated anti-flu mechanism for these compounds than expected. Follow-up experiments confirmed that bepotastine led to the nuclear retention of vRNP and had strong antiviral activity against multiple subtypes of influenza viruses, in line with observations in the setting of RAN silencing. Molecular docking studies have shown that bepotastine binds to several amino acid residues of RAN and matches the key interaction region in the crystallization binding mode of RAN and XPO1 [41]. Our pull-down assays showed that bepotastine might directly interacted with RAN Tyr80 amino acid residue and further impaired its interaction with XPO1. Thus, our results suggested that blocking the XPO1/RAN-mediated nuclear export pathway is effective in combating IAV infection. In recent years, several compounds have been reported to inhibit the export of NP and vRNP [53–55]. However, the antiviral mechanisms of these compounds, except naproxen, are not fully understood. Naproxen impedes the nuclear export of vRNP mainly by targeting the RNA groove of the NP and dissociating the XPO1-NP complex [26]. Theoretically, XPO1/RAN targeting excludes viral components, which potentially confers bepotastine a more broad-spectrum antiviral activity, not limited to influenza viruses. Nonetheless, the effects of bepotastine on the replication of other viruses need to be assessed in the future.

Bepotastine is a second-generation histamine H1 receptor antagonist with strong affinity and high selectivity. It has been approved for the treatment of allergic diseases such as allergic rhinitis, urticaria, and allergic conjunctivitis in Japan and the U. S [56–58]. To the best of our knowledge, bepotastine has not previously been implicated in viral infections. We reported that bepotastine exhibited a broad and highly efficient anti-flu activity with an IC50 of 9.255 μM in human cells. Importantly, the corresponding selectivity index of bepotastine is 65.5, which is much higher than the recommended criterion of SI ≥ 10 [59]. In the mice experiments, bepotastine achieved approximately 50% protection against influenza virus infection at 10 mg/kg. However, a higher concentration of bepotastine (50 mg/kg) did not significantly improve the protective efficiency, we suspected that bepotastine might have saturated at 10 mg/kg. In addition, no significant difference was observed in body weight and serum toxic effects indicators of mice treated with bepotastine at 50 mg/kg for 21 days as compared with the control group (Figure S4 A and B), suggesting that the dose of 10 mg/kg bepotastine is within the safe range in vivo. Unlike those drugs that target viral proteins, bepotastine treatment is less likely to generate a drug-resistant virus because RAN is encoded by the host genome. Thus, the virus needs to adapt to other host pathways or factors to develop resistance. Moreover, bepotastine can be used as a lead compound to generate its derivatives, which may exhibit enhanced antiviral performance. Notably, bepotastine showed prominent anti-IAV activity in human primary alveolar epithelial cells and NK cells, suggesting its potential clinical efficacy. Therefore, as an approved drug, bepotastine is much faster and easier to bring to the market for influenza infections.

In summary, our results indicated that the host protein RAN supports the proliferation of various subtypes of influenza viruses by facilitating the nuclear export of vRNP. Importantly, RAN promotes the formation of the vRNP nuclear export complex by increasing the binding affinity of XPO1-NS2. We found that the RAN-targeted drug bepotastine had significant antiviral activity both in vitro and in vivo, further revealing that bepotastine causes the nuclear accumulation of vRNP by disrupting the interaction between RAN and XPO1 (Figure 8). These findings provide new insights into the mechanism of influenza vRNP nuclear export as well as ideas for the development of host-directed antiviral drugs.

Model for RAN-regulated vRNP nuclear export during influenza virus infection. Late in infection, the mature vRNPs are transported from the nucleus to the cytoplasm for the assembly of progeny virus. Naturally, RAN promotes the nuclear export of vRNP by enhancing the interaction between XPO1 and NS2. When RAN is absent or is inhibited by a small molecule like bepotastine, the conformation of XPO1 is intolerable for NS2-vRNP binding, which largely blocks the vRNP nuclear export.
