Maternal histories of depression and childhood maltreatment, authoritarian parenting, and daughter error-related negativity: Developmental model of adolescent daughter depression risk☆
Authors: Katie J Paige, Katie L Burkhouse, Jennifer H Suor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal depression, maltreatment history, and authoritarian parenting styles are associated with adolescent depression, especially among girls. Individual characteristics, such as altered error-related negativity (ΔERN), an event-related potential biomarker implicated in internalizing disorders, may potentiate depression vulnerability among adolescent girls exposed to these risk factors. Yet, prior work has not evaluated the interplay among contextual and biological risk mechanisms in the development of girls’ depression. We conducted a longitudinal study of biological mother-daughter dyads (N = 69; ages 11–16 years) assessed across three timepoints. We recruited high (n = 29) and low risk groups (n = 40) of daughters based on maternal depression history. Participants completed symptom and parenting assessments, and a flanker task to elicit the ΔERN via electroencephalogram recordings. We found that maternal depression history predicted increased daughters’ depressive symptoms. Maternal maltreatment history indirectly predicted increases in daughters’ depressive symptoms across 1-year via authoritarian parenting. This mediation effect was moderated by daughter ΔERN. Only daughters who exhibited an enhanced ΔERN and experienced greater authoritarian parenting styles reported increased depressive symptoms. Findings suggest authoritarian parenting styles and ΔERN may be important targets for depression prevention for this vulnerable population of adolescent girls and mothers.Keywords: Adolescent Depression, Event-Related Negativity, Event-Related Potentials, Maternal depression history, Maternal maltreatment history, Authoritarian Parenting

Introduction

Adolescence represents a period of development in which depressive symptoms skyrocket [1], especially for girls [2], [3]. Adolescent depressive symptoms have been associated with serious long-term sequelae, including failure to complete secondary school, unemployment, and enduring mental health problems. Enhancing our understanding of risk pathways to adolescent depressive symptoms could potentially identify novel, objective targets for prevention. A substantial body of research has identified a number of underlying mechanisms of depressive symptoms, including maternal histories of depression [4] and maltreatment [5], and authoritarian parenting styles [6]. Additionally, growing evidence suggests that altered daughter error-related negativity (ERN) may be an important individual difference factor in moderating the impact of psychosocial stressors on depression risk [7], [8]. However, we still lack a full understanding of how multiple levels of influence interact to increase depressive symptoms during adolescence, a developmental period associated with initial onset of symptoms [3]. To this end, the current study sought to examine how well-known risk factors may associate and interact with each other over time to predict increases in daughter depressive symptoms. To investigate multilevel mechanisms of depression risk, we recruited a sample of girls and their biological mothers who either had a history of major depression or were lifetime free of psychiatric illnesses.

Maternal risk characteristics, parenting styles, and daughter depression

Maladaptive parenting practices have been linked to adolescent depression risk across studies [9]. One such constellation of maladaptive parenting practices that has been well-described is authoritarian parenting, which is characterized by parents who are high in control (i.e., controlling, intrusive, and hold high expectations for conformity and compliance with parental authority) and low in responsiveness to their children’s emotional needs [6], [10], [11]. There is robust empirical evidence that authoritarian parenting practices are a key risk factor in increasing daughter depression risk. Indeed, in a national survey of 17,399 youth, King and colleagues [6] found that those who reported experiencing more authoritarian parenting styles endorsed higher depression symptoms compared to those who experienced authoritative parenting styles. Additionally, girls who experienced authoritarian parenting practices exhibited the highest levels of depressive symptoms compared to boys.

Guided by parenting process models and child development [12], [13], it is thought that parenting practices and styles are determined by parental characteristics. Maternal depression and mother’s own maltreatment history are key predictors of dysfunctional parenting. Research has shown that mothers who have histories of depression exhibit less responsive and more controlling parenting styles with their children [14]. Additionally, mothers are likely to engage in more maladaptive parenting styles when they report adverse childhood experiences of their own, which in turns predict mental health risk in their offspring [12], [13], [15]. Thus, evidence indicates that maternal characteristics contribute to depressive symptom outcomes in offspring, which often begin to emerge and further increase over adolescence, and thus are important psychosocial mechanisms to consider in preventive interventions for adolescent girls. Given robust links between authoritarian parenting practices and offspring depression [6], research that aims to clarify risk pathways to daughter depressive symptoms must consider the broader context of parental characteristics, namely maternal histories of depression and maltreatment, that may predispose authoritarian parenting.

Role of daughter error-related negativity

However, not all adolescents who experience challenging parenting environments develop depressive symptomatology, which is consistent with diathesis-stress frameworks of psychopathology where some individuals have biological vulnerabilities that increase their sensitivity to psychosocial stresses. For example, biological factors that impact how an individual perceives and experiences their parents may modify her proneness to develop depression. One individual difference factor that is involved in how someone experiences negative environmental cues of criticism, threat, and making mistakes and errors is the ERN neural marker, which is an event-related potential (ERP) component assessed via electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings during cognitive tasks. Specifically, the ERN is a negative deflection in the ERP waveform that usually peaks 100 ms after an individual makes an error, and is generated by the anterior cingulate cortex, a key region in the brain involved in conflict monitoring and management of emotions elicited in response motivationally significant stimuli [16], [17].

Altered ERN patterns has been implicated as a risk biomarkers for internalizing syndromes characterized by heightened levels of negative affect, such as multiple forms of psychopathology, particularly those characterized by high negative affect, self-criticism, and worry [18]. Heightened and blunted ERN been observed among adolescents and adults with depression symptoms [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24]. One explanation for this mixed pattern of findings is that individuals with moderate levels of depression are more likely to demonstrated enhanced ERN relative to healthy controls [19], [22], [24]. This is consistent with the negative information biases (greater attention to failures or errors compared to correct performance) that characterize some individuals with depression. Conversely, individuals with severe depressive symptoms or with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) comorbidity may demonstrate more blunted ERN compared to healthy controls [21], [25], [26], [27], which some argue is reflective of the motivational deficits that some depressed individuals exhibit [8]. However, some work has also found no associations between ERN and depression [18]. Inconsistent findings may be due to sampling differences and that more limited work has evaluated the role of ERN in adolescent depression [28]. Accordingly, the present study sought to evaluate how the ERN might operate as a susceptibility factor in multilevel models of adolescent female depression risk.

The present study

Toward advancing a multilevel developmental model of depression risk we tested a moderated mediation of the association among maternal depression and maltreatment history, ERN, and depressive symptoms over 12-months in a sample of adolescent girls enriched for depression risk due to maternal history. First, we evaluated the direct and indirect roles of maternal maltreatment experiences and depression history might have in predicting increased depressive symptoms in their biological daughters, and whether some of this risk occurred through increased experiences of authoritarian parenting styles. Second, we examined whether daughter ERN moderated the association between authoritarian parenting styles and depressive symptoms. Given the mixed literature on ERN and depression, our moderating analyses were exploratory and we did not have specific predictions whether enlarged or reduced ERN might increase risk for depression among those who experienced greater authoritarian parenting styles. The study drew from a larger investigation of the intergenerational transmission of maternal depression to their adolescent daughters, which employed a two-group study design. A high risk (HR) group was recruited which consisted of mother-daughter dyads where mothers had histories of MDD. A second low risk (LR) comparison group of mother-daughter dyads were recruited which included mothers who had no history of any psychiatric illness during their lifetimes. Across the two groups, daughters were only eligible if they had never experienced a depressive disorder in their lifetime, as the goal of the larger study was to identify developmental pathways underlying depression onset over the course of adolescence.

Methods

Participants and procedures

Participants were N = 69 adolescent girls and their mothers between the ages of 11 and 16 (M=14.11, SD=1.73). Daughters identified as White (56.79 %), African American (16.05 %), other (13.58 %), Asian (8.64 %), and multiracial (4.94 %), and 35.80 % identified as Hispanic/Latinx. Similarly, mothers identified as White (58.75 %), African American (17.50 %), other (11.25 %), Asian (7.50 %), and multiracial (5.00 %), and 28.75 % identified as Hispanic/Latinx. On average, mothers reported 16.22 years of education (SD=2.44; range=11–24). Participants were recruited in a large urban metropolitan city in the Midwest from flyers, community events, and social media postings. Recruitment focused on obtaining a sample of daughters whose biological mothers had experienced a major depressive episode (n = 29; HR group), and a comparison group of daughters whose biological mothers were lifetime free of any psychiatric disorder (n = 40; LR group), based on DSM-5 criteria. Interested dyads were invited to a laboratory visit where mothers completed diagnostic interviews, and childhood maltreatment history, parenting, and clinical symptom questionnaires. Daughters completed a computerized Flanker task while EEG was recorded as well as mental health questionnaires and interviews during their visit. Additionally, daughters completed clinical symptom questionnaires through online surveys at 6- and 12-month follow-ups. n = 56 (81 %) and n = 54 (78 %) daughters provided data on their depressive symptoms at Times 2 and 3, respectively. At Times 2 and 3 the mean age of daughters was 15.35-years-old (SD=1.51) and 15.80-years-old (SD=1.34), respectively. Study exclusions for mother-child dyads included substance/alcohol dependence within the past 6 months or histories of bipolar spectrum disorder or schizophrenia, intellectual disability, serious medical conditions, and pervasive developmental disorders.

Trained research assistants and master’s- or doctoral-level clinicians administered diagnostic interviews to assess psychiatric histories in mothers and daughters. The Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5) [29] was administered to mothers and the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children [30] was administered to daughters. In both the high- and low-risk groups, daughters could not meet criteria for current or past MDD. Separate staff administered the KSADS and SCID-5 to daughters and mothers respectively to reduce bias that could occur if the same interviewer administered diagnostic interviews to the mother-daughter dyad. Weekly supervision meetings were conducted to discuss individual interviews and create consensus ratings.

The BLINDED FOR PEER REVIEW Institutional Review Board approved study procedures before the start of data collection and informed consent and assent were obtained from mothers and their daughters, respectively.

Measures

Maternal depression history

At time 1 maternal depression history was confirmed via the SCID-5 (First, 2015). Of the mothers with MDD (n = 29), 2 met criteria for current MDD while 27 had lifetime histories of recurrent MDD. Maternal depression history was dichotomized into a single variable, such that 0 =no depression history (low-risk, LR; n = 40) and 1 =history of depression (high-risk, HR; n = 29).1

Maternal childhood maltreatment history

At time 1, mothers completed the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire (CTQ) [31]. The CTQ is a 28-item self-report inventory that assesses history of childhood maltreatment. Mothers responded to 25 statements about experiencing various forms of maltreatment during their childhood (e.g., physical neglect and abuse, emotional neglect and abuse, and sexual abuse), using a five-point Likert scale (1 =Never true to 5 =Often true). Scores that fall above the following thresholds on the individual subscales indicate history of moderate to extreme abuse: emotional abuse > 12, physical abuse > 9, sexual abuse > 8, physical neglect > 9, and emotional neglect > 14. To test study hypotheses, the current study used the total CTQ score which is the sum of all the subscales. Higher total CTQ scores indicated greater experiences of childhood maltreatment. The CTQ total score evidenced excellent internal reliability (α=0.94).

Authoritarian parenting styles

At time 1, daughters reported on their mothers’ parenting styles using Parental Bonding Instrument (PBI) [32] in order to assess daughter’s perceptions of their mother’s parenting styles. This study focused on the authoritarianism scale which includes four items to measure authoritarian parenting style. The 4 items included, “Likes me to make my own decisions,” “Lets me decide things for myself,” “Lets me dress in any way I please,” and “Gives me as much freedom as I want.” Daughters responded to these statements using a four-point Likert scale (0 =very likely to 3 =very unlikely), such that higher scores indicate higher levels of authoritarian parenting style. Internal consistency at time 1 was acceptable (α=0.71).

Daughter withdrawn/depressed symptoms

Daughters completed the Youth Self-Report (YSR) [33] at time 1, six-month follow-up (time 2), and 12-month follow-up assessments (time 3). The YSR assesses a wide range of behavior problems that have occurred in the past six months. The withdrawn/depressed subscale (8 items) was used to assess depressive symptoms. Daughters responded to each item using a three-point Likert scale (0 =not true to 2=very true or often true). Sum scores were calculated for the withdrawn/depressed subscale, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptoms. Internal consistency reliabilities ranged from acceptable to good across time (αs = 0.67–.80). Sum scores were calculated and were included as a time-varying covariate across times 1–3. Internal consistency reliabilities were acceptable (α range=0.81–0.87) across the three repeated measures. Given a robust literature indicating that parents are less reliable reporters of internalizing symptoms in their children compared to behavioral disorders, which often accounts for why adolescents with depressive symptoms are misdiagnosed and go on untreated [34], [35], [36], we used daughter-reports of their own depressive symptoms. Still, we examined differences in our model using mother-reports of authoritarian parenting and daughter depressive symptoms, and results from those analyses are reported in Supplemental Material 1.

We also conducted post hoc sensitivity analysis to determine the specificity of findings to depression by assessing anxiety symptoms (anxious/depressed subscale (8 items) from the YSR) rather than depressive symptoms as the outcome variable (Supplemental Material 2). The internal consistencies of anxious/depressed subscale were acceptable (α range=0.81–0.87).

Error monitoring

Flanker task

At time 1, daughters completed a modified computerized Flanker task [37], which measures performance monitoring in response to errors. The flanker task reliably elicits the ERN in youth and adults and demonstrates excellent retest reliability [38], [39]. See Supplemental Material 3 for a description of the flanker task.

Participants’ data were retained if they had accuracy rates ≥ 75 %, ≥ 6 total errors, errors response, and ≥ 6 usable EEG error and correct segments after artifact rejection [40]. Ten participants had accuracy rates below 75 %, two participants made fewer than 6 errors, and four additional participants had fewer than 6 usable EEG error and correct segments after artifact rejection. After these participants were removed, EEG data from n = 49 daughters remained (LR=29; HR=20). In this final subsample, on average, daughters committed 40.69 errors (SD=16.31), which corresponded to an overall percent accuracy of 87.68 % (SD=4.94). LR and HR daughters did not differ in missing ERN data (Χ2=0.26, p = 0.61) or behavioral performance (t(49) = 1.84, p = 0.07).

EEG data acquisition and processing

Continuous EEG recordings were collected while participants completed the Flanker Task using the BioSemi (Amsterdam, Netherlands) 34-channel cap (32 channels plus FCz and lz). EEG data was process offline using Brain Vision Analyzer software. See Supplemental Material 3 for EEG data collection and preprocessing and artifact rejection procedures. To calculate the ERN, ERPs to errors and correct responses were averaged separately and baseline corrected −500 – −300 ms before the response. Inspection of the topographic maps indicated that ΔERN was maximal at frontocentral sites (Fz, FCz, Cz, see Fig. 2). The ΔERN was scored by pooling frontocentral sites, Fz, FCz and Cz as mean activity 0–100 ms after the response for correct and error segments (Fig. 2). The ERP response was more negative following errors versus correct responses (t(49) = -6.20, p < .001). We employed a residualizing scoring approach by regressing the pooled correct response variable on pooled error response variable and saved the unstandardized residual values to use in our analyses. Since the ΔERN is negative ERP component, more negative amplitudes indicate enhanced response to self-initiated errors relative to correct responses (i.e., increased error monitoring).

Data analytic strategy

Descriptive analyses and correlations were conducted for all study variables. Assumptions for multiple regression (i.e., linearity, homoscedasticity, independence) were assessed, and non-normal data were winsorized at three standard deviations above the mean. To evaluate the proposed developmental model of depression risk, first, we tested the a path (see Fig. 1) using a linear regression model estimated in PROC REG in SAS 9.4, which uses listwise deletion to handle missing data. This test retained n = 69 who had data on all included variables (maternal depression history, maternal maltreatment history, daughter age, and authoritarian parenting). The model was arranged such that maternal depression and childhood maltreatment history at time 1 predicted daughter-reported authoritarian parenting at time 1, controlling for daughter age. While the predictors and the dependent variable were all assessed at time 1, the predictors are retrospective reports of experiences that temporally precede the dependent variable. Thus, although measured concurrently, they nevertheless reflect earlier exposures.

Conceptual figure of developmental model of depression risk. Note. The proposed developmental model of depression risk depicts time 1 maternal maltreatment history and maternal depression history associating with authoritarian parenting, which in turn, interact with daughter Δ error-related negativity to predict increases in daughter depressive symptoms across time. The Δ error-related negativity residual was used to measure error monitoring. To test prospective associations in daughter depressive symptoms, multilevel models were estimated to test the moderated b path. Repeated measures (level 1) were nested within participants (level 2), and data were arranged to test prospective associations (e.g., depressive symptoms at time 1 predicts depressive symptoms at time 2, and depressive symptoms at time 2 predicts depressive symptoms at time 3).

Next, to test prospective associations in daughter depressive symptoms, multilevel models (MLM) were estimated in PROC MIXED procedure in SAS 9.4 with maximum likelihood estimation (ML). MLM allows for varying times of assessments between participants and accommodates missing data on dependent variables [41]. MLM with ML deletes an observation if a predictor variable is missing, but retains an observation if predictors are valid but the outcome is missing. This process for handling missing data uses the assumption that the data are missing completely at random (MCAR) and does not lead to biased interference when deleting the instances with missing data [41]. Because SAS cannot estimate missing data on predictor variables the second model only included daughters who had complete EEG data (n = 49). Daughters had missing EEG data when the quality of their data did not meet established standards for useability (insufficient number of segments, significant interference in signal). There were no systematic differences in which daughters had complete or missing EEG data across all the predictor variables (e.g., authoritarian parenting, maternal maltreatment, MDD group) and depressive symptoms (ps > .65). Repeated measures (level 1) were nested within participants (level 2), and data were arranged to test prospective associations (e.g., depressive symptoms at time 1 predicts depressive symptoms at time 2, and depressive symptoms at time 2 predicts depressive symptoms at time 3). Maternal depression history, maternal maltreatment history, authoritarian parenting styles, and daughter ΔERN at Time 1 were included as level 2 independent variables to examine main effects on daughter depressive symptoms. Next, to test moderation, the interaction term for authoritarian parenting style (level 2) and ΔERN (level 2) was added. All models included a random intercept and daughter age as a level 1 covariate. All independent variables were mean centered at zero to aid in the interpretation of the results [42]. If the interaction effect was significant, we explored the simple slopes by determining the effects on daughter depressive symptoms at one standard deviation above and below the mean of the moderator for descriptive purposes [43]. Significant interactions were also probed using the Johnson-Neyman technique to better understand the nature of the interaction effects by estimating regions of significance [44], [45]. Finally, estimates (e.g., A and B paths) were entered into a Monte Carlo simulation with 20,000 samples to test indirect effects [46].

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations for study variables are reported in Table 1. On average, daughter depressive symptoms remained relatively stable across our three repeated measures and were in the normal range (M range=3.58–3.82; T-score=53). The proportion of daughters who fell above the subclinical threshold (T-scores>65) at times 1, 2, and 3 was 2.60 %, 12.28 %, and 10.90 %, respectively. ΔERN were modestly negatively correlated with depressive symptoms across time, indicating that daughters who had more negative ΔERN amplitudes (enhanced error-monitoring) at time 1 reported increased depressive symptoms across the 12-month follow-up period.

C’ path

Maternal depression history had a significant main effect on daughter depressive symptoms (b=1.38, p < 0.05; Table 3, Model 1), such that daughters whose mothers reported a history of depression reported increases in their own depressive symptoms across time.3 The association between maternal childhood maltreatment history and daughter depressive symptoms was not significant (b=0.01, p = 0.71).

Moderated mediation

The pattern of main effects suggested an indirect pathway from maternal childhood maltreatment history through the interaction term for authoritarian parenting styles and daughter ΔERN to daughter depressive symptoms. The overall model was supported [a*b= -0.002, 95 % CI= -0.0061, −0.00001]. Zero was not within the CI, indicating a significant moderating effect of daughter ΔERN on authoritarian parenting styles on the indirect effect on daughter depressive symptoms from maternal childhood maltreatment history.4

In summary, results demonstrated maternal depression history directly predicted increases in daughter depressive symptoms over time. Maternal histories of childhood maltreatment predicted higher levels of authoritarian parenting, which in turn predicted increases in daughter depressive symptoms for daughters who exhibited a heightened ΔERN.

Discussion

The goal of our study was to increase understanding of adolescent depression risk by synergistically integrating risk factors across social and biological levels of influence and how their interplay could contribute to the onset of depressive symptoms over time. A strength of our study was the longitudinal design and multilevel modeling approach, which provided temporal precedence and allowed us to predict changes in daughters’ depression levels across three repeated measures. Additionally, we utilized a sample enriched for daughters’ depression risk. Our hypothesis that maternal depression and childhood maltreatment histories would associate with high levels of authoritarian parenting, which in turn, would interact with altered daughter ΔERN to predict increases in daughter depressive symptoms was partially supported. Findings provide some preliminary evidence there is likely a complex interplay among maternal childhood maltreatment history, authoritarian parenting, and enhanced daughter ΔERN in increasing daughter depressive symptoms across time [19], [47].

Maternal depression history

Our hypothesis that maternal depression history would associate with high levels of authoritarian parenting was not supported. While this is somewhat inconsistent with the notion that mothers with histories of depression display lower levels of warmth and higher levels of behavioral control [48], meta-analytic work suggests that associations between maternal depression and parenting styles may be small in magnitude [14]. In addition to genetic risk and maladaptive approaches to parenting, theories of intergenerational transmission of depression emphasize blunted neural socioemotional processing styles as an intergenerational mechanism of risk [7]. Given the present findings, future studies should consider other mechanistic processes when examining indirect pathways in intergenerational transmission of risk.

Notably, our developmental risk model suggested a direct association between the presence of maternal depression history and increases in daughter depressive symptoms across time. This is perhaps unsurprising, as research suggests moderate genetic influences on risk for depression, with a heritability estimate of 37 % [49]. Moreover, one tentative interpretation of the findings is that contribution of genetic factors on depression might increase from childhood to adolescence [50], [51]. Interestingly, in our sensitivity analysis, we did not find an association between maternal current depressive symptoms at time 1 and changes in daughter depression symptoms across time. At time 1 mothers were reporting moderating subclinical levels of current depressive symptoms. Current symptomatology may not be as strong of a predictor compared to MDD history in predicting longitudinal increases in offspring depressive symptoms. The current results are perhaps consistent with research that finds a strong predictive relationship between parental history of meeting full diagnostic criteria for MDD and a four-fold increase in risk of offspring experiencing an MDD by adulthood compared to offspring of parents with no histories of depression [52]. Nonetheless, future work may consider other psychosocial risk mechanisms, including alternative caregiving practices, that maternal depression history might act through in predicting depression vulnerability.

Maternal maltreatment history

Our hypothesis that maternal childhood maltreatment history would associate with high levels of authoritarian parenting styles, which in turn, would interact with altered daughter ΔERN to predict increases in daughter depressive symptoms was supported. This finding is consistent with human and animal research which has found links between maternal maltreatment history and harsher parenting approaches [13], [53]. Childhood maltreatment history is an extreme stressor that adversely affects mothers’ abilities to cope and regulate their emotions and behavior, in turn increasing their propensity to rely on parenting strategies that may be maladaptive. This finding is consistent with prior research demonstrating that adults who experience multiple forms of maltreatment during their childhood exhibit higher levels of interpersonal problems [54], including being less supportive towards their children [55]. The significant relationship between maternal maltreatment history and maladaptive authoritarian parenting styles points to a broader range of negative parenting styles, beyond maltreatment, that might contribute to depressive symptom pathways in adolescent girls.

Daughter error-related negativity

A strength of integrating multiple systems of risk in our developmental model is that we were able to identify that authoritarian parenting styles may represent an especially risky context for daughters who have enhanced ΔERN. We found a significant interaction for authoritarian parenting styles and daughter ΔERN predicting increases in daughter depressive symptoms across time, accounting for prior daughter depressive symptoms. Specifically, daughters who exhibited enhanced ΔERN and perceived that their mothers engaged in high levels of authoritarian parenting styles reported increases in depressive symptoms over the 12-month follow-up period. An enhanced ERN represents an overactive performance monitoring system [56], [57] and increased traits of defensive reactivity in response to errors (e.g., perception of errors as threatening to the self) [8]. Interpreted within the context of the current study, daughters with enhanced ΔERN could be more likely to monitor and internalize negative feedback or cues from their parents. When negative interactions between authoritarian mothers and daughters with enhanced ΔERN occur, these daughters may over-monitor their own behavior for errors and more strongly, negatively internalize corrective feedback. Both these predispositions to increased sensitivity to criticism, in turn, could increase their risk for experiencing depression across development. This stands in contrast to daughter who have blunted ΔERN patterns, and could be less attuned or sensitive to authoritarian parenting styles which could buffer them against experiencing depressive symptoms. Given the small sample size, future replication with larger samples is needed to bolster these interpretations. Another strength of our study is that we used daughter-reports of authoritarian parenting, suggesting that daughter perceptions of their mothers’ parenting styles may be particularly important when considering how daughters’ sensitivity to interpersonal threats (i.e., their ΔERN) may modify their depression risk. Interestingly, results were not consistent when we used mother-reports of authoritarian parenting and daughter depressive symptoms (see Supplemental Material 1). One possible explanation is that mothers are less likely to endorse engaging in authoritarian parenting behaviors due to high face validity of items and social desirability. Additionally, our conflicting findings raise concerns about mothers’ ability to accurately report on daughters’ internal experiences of depressive symptoms, which is supported by extant literature suggesting that parents are less reliable reporters of internalizing symptoms in their children [34], [35], [36]. Future research should continue to consider these measurement issues carefully and may elect to utilize daughter reports of parenting and depressive symptoms when deemed developmentally appropriate. To our knowledge, this study was the first to examine how enhanced ΔERN and authoritarian parenting styles may operate synergistically to predict longitudinal daughter depressive symptoms in a high-risk, depression naïve sample.

These findings provide initial support that in some cases, an altered ERN may be a risk factor for depressive symptoms. Some past work has reported significant associations between enhanced ERN and moderate levels of depressive symptoms, others report no relationship, and, some have documented links between blunted ERN and severe depression symptoms [8]. Importantly, depression is a very heterogenous disorder given that individuals can either meet diagnostic criteria based on negative mood or anhedonia [58], which might shed some light on why studies have observed both heightened and blunted ERN patterns and depression [59]. Prior work has suggested that individuals who endorse more moderate symptoms of depression demonstrate an enhanced ERN profiles [22], [24] while those with severe depressive symptoms demonstrate blunted ERN response to errors [25], [60]. In the present study, enlarged daughter ΔERN at time 1 was significantly correlated with daughter depression at times 2 and 3, such that high error-monitoring was related to high levels of depressive symptoms over time. As daughters in our sample were depression naïve at time 1 and endorsed low levels of average depressive symptoms across times 2 and 3, these correlations align with previous studies that report associations between enhanced ERN and less severe depressive symptom clinical presentations [19], [21]. Further, given high levels of comorbidity between anxiety and depression, it is likely they share some of the same neural characteristics. Indeed, it has been proposed that heightened ERN may be more reflective of core personality traits of negative affect and shared characteristics among internalizing psychopathologies rather than being disorder specific [60].

Despite bivariate correlations between ΔERN and depressive symptoms, when examined within our development model, it was the two-way interaction effect between daughter ΔERN and authoritarian parenting, but not the main effect of daughter ΔERN, that predicted increases in depressive symptoms across time. Evidence from the developmental literature has shown that youth with greater traits of negative emotionality are more sensitive to unsupportive caregiving, which can be predictive of internalizing symptoms across childhood and adolescence [61], [62], [63]. Interpreted from this perspective, our findings are consistent with diathesis-stress theories and broader research on the interplay among unsupportive parenting and biological indicators of negative affectivity, and depression [64]. Findings make two promising contributions to the literature that could have the potential to guide future advancements in adolescent depression risk field. First, results highlight the importance of utilizing a longitudinal design and accounting for previous levels of depressive symptoms when aiming to elucidate risk factors that elevate risk for increases in depression across development. Second, our developmental model of depression risk emphasizes that enhanced daughter ΔERN should be considered within a broader context of family risk processes, such as authoritarian parenting styles, when examining its role in increasing depressive symptoms. Indeed, while enhanced ΔERN may not have direct links to depression in isolation, it is possible that this ΔERN pattern confers risk when daughters grow up in risky family environments, such as having mothers who experienced childhood maltreatment and thus are more likely to parent in maladaptive ways.

Limitations

Findings from the current study should be interpreted within the context of certain limitations. First, our study was limited by a single assessment of daughter ΔERN at time 1, and due to the hypothesized within-time association between ΔERN and authoritarian parenting styles, we were also only able to include one assessment of authoritarian parenting styles. Furthermore, the PBI authoritarianism subscale includes only 4 items, which may not capture the full breadth of characteristics that define authoritarian parenting styles. Given this measurement limitation, future studies should use a more comprehensive measure of authoritarian parenting styles to increase confidence in the present findings. Second, our sample size was small, raising concerns about low power, and there were fewer mothers in the sample with histories of depression than without. These are common limitations in the field [56], [65], as employing EEG methodology is expensive and time-intensive. Still, these limitations precluded our study from examining bidirectional and transactional associations that might exist among study variables. Concurrent assessment of variables in our developmental model limits our ability to draw causal inferences. Taken together, even though the current findings offer novel insights and could serve as a guide for future investigations, they are still preliminary in nature due to our small sample size. Although there were no systematic differences among daughters who had completed versus missing EEG data, the number of participants that were included in the moderated mediation analysis was reduced. Considering sample size limitations, future replication studies are needed to increase confidence in findings from the study. One important methodological step is to recruit a larger sample size with equal number of mothers with and without histories of depression and include repeated assessments of daughter ΔERN and authoritarian parenting styles, which would allow for a rigorous evaluation of how these social and biological levels of influence impact adolescent girls’ depressive symptoms across adolescence. Such a study may even elect to employ latent trajectory approaches which could test a formal developmental cascade model of depression risk by examining transactions among multiple risk factors identified in the current study. Third, the study only included mothers and daughters. Although our goal was to examine these processes in girls who often show initial onset of depression in adolescence [2] and may be more sensitive to interpersonal stressors within family relationships [66], [67], future work should include boys. Relatedly, research supports the notion that fathers also play critical and unique roles in shaping offspring mental health [68], [69]. Recent evidence suggests fathers are more likely than mothers to engage in authoritarian parenting practices [70], emphasizing the importance of including fathers in future studies. Fourth, the internal consistency reliability for daughter withdrawn/depressed scores fell just below acceptable at time 1, perhaps due to eligibility criteria which required all daughters regardless of maternal MDD history to be depression naïve at enrollment and likely restricted variability in initial daughter depressive symptoms. Conversely, increasing levels of daughter depressive symptoms and acceptable reliabilities across times 2 and 3 strengthen confidence in our interpretation that we were able to capture a developmental model of depression across a 12-month period of adolescence. Lastly, our results suggest that it may be beneficial for future work to examine how daughter ΔERN interacts with peer stressors, which in turn could contribute to the emergence of depressive symptoms since peer relationships have a critical impact on mental health during adolescence [71].

Conclusion

Our developmental model of depression risk identified several promising pathways that could underlie increases in daughter depressive symptoms across adolescence. While maternal depression and maltreatment histories were moderately correlated with each other, our results suggest that they each uniquely predicted daughter depressive symptoms through different mechanisms, consistent with the principle of equifinality [72]. Our findings demonstrated that a presence of maternal depression history likely confers risk for daughter depression directly, perhaps through a genetic mechanism of risk or other psychosocial risk factors [49]. Interestingly, our model provides some evidence that maternal maltreatment history indirectly portends risk for daughter depression through mothers’ use of more authoritarian parenting styles, which in turn, elevates daughter depressive symptoms across time for daughters who exhibited enhanced ΔERN, consistent with the principles of multifinality and differential susceptibility [72]. This developmental model of daughter depression risk highlights the importance of considering various pathways across multiple systems of development.

In sum, our findings underscore the importance of enhancing access to therapy and parenting interventions for mothers with traumatic childhood experiences to promote positive parenting styles and adolescent mental health outcomes. Two-generation early intervention programs (i.e., programs with interventions for both parents and children), like Head Start and Early Head Start, focus on increasing families’ self-sufficiency as well as enhancing children’s cognitive, social, and emotional development [73]. Notably, research supports the efficacy of these early childhood intervention programs with parents who report childhood maltreatment history [74], and predicts positive long-term outcomes for adolescent mental health [75]. Additionally, our risk pathway identified multiple modifiable factors that may be useful intervention targets. Namely, enhanced daughter ΔERN increased risk for depressive symptoms across time in the context of authoritarian parenting styles. Recent research has found that brief cognitive training programs can reduce enhanced ERN [76], [77], which may protect against increases in depressive symptoms in the context of interpersonal stressors. Relatedly, there is some evidence that those with enhanced ERN might benefit more so from cognitive-behavioral interventions compared to adults with blunted ERN profiles for reducing internalizing symptoms. However, there has yet to be pediatric clinical research studies that have evaluated these potential treatment mechanisms for depression prevention. Future research is needed to clarify for whom and under what conditions targeting enhanced ΔERN may be the most therapeutically beneficial for youth at-risk for depression.
