Development of a Screening Model for Exercise-Induced Desaturation by Machine Learning Method
Authors: Seung Ju Kim, Jae Ha Lee, Ji-Yong Moon, Chang Youl Lee, Soo-Jung Um, Seong Yong Lim, Hyoung Kyu Yoon, Kwang Ha Yoo, Chin Kook Rhee, Won-Yeon Lee, on Behalf of the KOCOSS Cohort and the Korean Pulmonary Rehabilitation Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background Exercise-induced desaturation (EID) during the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) is an established marker of adverse outcomes in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Therefore, we aimed to develop a machine learning approach focused on screening to identify patients at increased risk of EID.Methods We analyzed data from the nationwide, multicenter Korea COPD Subgroup Study. EID was defined as a peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) <90% with a decrease of ≥4%p. The cohort was divided into training (80%) and test (20%) sets. Candidate predictors were selected using the Boruta algorithm, and models were developed using multivariable logistic regression, extreme gradient boosting (XGB), random forest, and support vector classification, employing a screening-oriented threshold strategy that prioritized sensitivity.Results Among 1,788 patients with COPD, 185 (10.3%) exhibited EID. All models demonstrated a high precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) during internal validation. The predictors selected by the Boruta algorithm included body mass index, COPD Assessment Test, St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire for COPD patients, mental health indicators, pulmonary function parameters, X-ray-identified bronchiectasis, and hemoglobin level. In the independent test set, PR-AUC decreased across models, while calibration metrics showed modest differences between datasets. The XGB model achieved the highest sensitivity during internal validation, and both its sensitivity and specificity remained relatively stable in the test set. Baseline SpO2 and diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide were the most influential predictors.Conclusion A screening-oriented machine learning approach utilizing routinely available variables may facilitate targeted referral for the 6MWT in COPD patients.

Introduction

Exercise-induced desaturation (EID) during the 6-minute walk test (6MWT) has been recognized as an important clinical marker in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Previous studies consistently show that patients experiencing EID have poorer clinical outcomes, including higher rates of acute exacerbations (AEs) [1,2] and increased mortality [1-6].

Emerging evidence suggests that EID may be a potentially modifiable risk marker. A recent study involving patients with COPD and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis found that supplemental oxygen therapy for those with EID was associated with improvements in exercise capacity and dyspnea, albeit with an intermediate effect size [7]. In addition, in a recent post hoc analysis, the prescription of supplemental oxygen was associated with improved survival among patients with early EID, defined as a peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) value of ≤ 88% within 1 minute during the 6MWT [8].

Despite its clinical relevance, the routine assessment of EID using the 6MWT is challenging in real-world practice. Time, space, and personnel constraints limit the feasibility of conducting exercise testing for all patients with COPD, particularly in high-volume outpatient settings. Consequently, there is a need for practical screening methods to identify patients at high risk for EID. In this study, we developed a machine learning approach focused on screening, utilizing readily available clinical, functional, and patient-reported variables to identify patients at increased risk of EID.

Materials and Methods

1. Study population and data collection

All patients included in this study were selected from the Korea COPD Subgroup Study (KOCOSS) [9,10]. KOCOSS is a nationwide, multicenter, prospective cohort study established in April 2012, enrolling patients with COPD who visit 60 referral hospitals across South Korea.

Eligible participants were aged ≥40 years, had a smoking history of ≥10 pack-years, and exhibited a post-bronchodilator (post-BD) forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio <0.70, had a documented 6MWT result in the registry and did not receive supplemental oxygen during the 6MWT. Patients enrolled in KOCOSS through 2022 were included in this analysis. All participating hospitals obtained approval from their respective institutional review board committees, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

2. 6-minute walk test

The 6MWT was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the European Respiratory Society and the American Thoracic Society [11]. EID during the 6MWT was defined as a post-6MWT SpO2 <90% with a decrease of ≥4%p from the pre-6MWT value [12].

3. Clinical and functional assessments

All patients underwent a comprehensive baseline assessment at enrollment. The history of AEs in the year prior to enrollment was classified according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) report. The severity of respiratory symptoms was evaluated using the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale and the COPD Assessment Test (CAT).

Quality of life was assessed across various dimensions. Respiratory symptom-related quality of life was measured using the St. George’s Respiratory Questionnaire for COPD patients (SGRQc). Depression and anxiety levels were evaluated with the Beck depression inventory (BDI) and the Beck anxiety inventory (BAI), respectively.

Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) included measurements of FEV1, FVC, FEV1/FVC ratio, diffusion capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), and the ratio of residual volume (RV) to total lung capacity (TLC), with additional parameters assessed as clinically indicated. All PFT parameters analyzed were obtained under post-BD conditions.

4. Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.5.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value <0.05 unless otherwise specified. Baseline characteristics, including 6MWT results, were compared between patients with and without EID. Continuous variables were analyzed using Student's t-test, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test.

The study population was randomly divided into training (80%) and test (20%) sets through stratified sampling based on EID status. After data splitting, the balance between the training and test sets was evaluated using standardized differences for all candidate variables. A standardized difference of less than 0.10 was considered indicative of adequate distributional balance.

Missing data were addressed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), performed separately in the training and test sets to prevent information leakage. All variable selection procedures were restricted to the training set. Variable selection was conducted in the training set using the Boruta algorithm, retaining predictors classified as confirmed.

To address class imbalance, the synthetic minority over-sampling technique for nominal and continuous features (SMOTENC) was applied within the resampling folds of the training set only. Resampling was limited to the training folds to ensure that test set evaluation and out-of-fold (OoF) predictions remained fully independent of the over-sampling process.

A multivariable logistic regression (MLR) model was established as a linear benchmark. Concurrently, machine learning models, including extreme gradient boosting (XGB), random forest (RF), and support vector classification (SVC), were trained. Model performance was primarily evaluated using threshold-independent metrics, with a particular emphasis on the precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC) due to the low prevalence of EID. Additional performance metrics included the Brier score, calibration intercept and slope, and graphical calibration assessment.

In the absence of an external validation cohort, repeated stratified cross-validation was performed within the training set, and OoF predicted probabilities were collected. These predictions were used to calculate sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the F1 score across a range of decision thresholds. Threshold selection was guided by a predefined screening-oriented criterion: among thresholds that met the requirements of specificity ≥0.50 and PPV ≥0.20, the threshold yielding the highest sensitivity was chosen. If multiple thresholds provided identical sensitivity, the final operating point was determined by sequentially prioritizing NPV, specificity, and PPV. Model performance was evaluated in the test set by applying the pre-specified model and fixed threshold established during training.

The model demonstrating the highest sensitivity under these criteria was selected as the final screening model. Feature importance for this model was assessed using permutation-based methods.

Results

1. Study population

A total of 3,476 patients were initially identified from the KOCOSS cohort (Figure 1). Among these, 803 were excluded due to the absence of baseline PFT results (n=112), a baseline post-BD FEV1/FVC ≥0.70 (n=205), age <40 years (n=5), or a smoking history of <10 pack-years (n=481). The remaining 2,673 patients constituted the eligible COPD cohort.

Among these patients, those who did not undergo a 6MWT (n=846) or required supplemental oxygen during the 6MWT (n=39) were further excluded. A total of 1,788 patients were included in the final analysis. Of these, 185 patients (10.3%) met the criteria for EID and 1,603 patients were classified as non-EID.

2. Baseline characteristics

Compared to patients without EID, those with EID were slightly older (EID, 70.0±7.7; non-EID, 68.5±7.5), had a lower body mass index (BMI) (22.1±3.4; 23.1±3.3), and a higher proportion of former smokers (84.3%; 69.1%) (Table 1). Patients with EID exhibited more severe respiratory symptoms, as indicated by higher mMRC (1.8±1.0; 1.3±0.9) and CAT scores (18.2±8.1; 14.0±7.7), along with poorer health-related quality of life, reflected in higher SGRQc scores (42.8±22.8; 29.8±20.4).

Pulmonary function was significantly more impaired in the EID group, with lower FEV1 (1.4±0.5 L; 1.8±0.6 L), FEV1/FVC (42.6%±12.0%; 51.0%±11.3%), and DLCO (46.9%±17.3%; 65.7%±19.8%), as well as higher RV/TLC (49.2%±13.9%; 42.3±11.6%). The distribution of GOLD grades differed significantly between groups, with a higher proportion of patients in the EID group classified as GOLD 3 (41.1%; 25.5%) and GOLD 4 (16.2%; 2.9%).

Patients with EID were more likely to have experienced at least one AE (32.1%; 19.6%) in the year prior to enrollment, particularly severe AEs (18.2%; 7.2%). However, there were no significant differences between groups in asthma history (21.7%; 24.1%), blood eosinophil count (249.6±284.6 cells/μL; 235.7±272.9 cells/μL), or fractional exhaled nitric oxide (31.9±19.3 ppb; 29.8±24.4 ppb). Tuberculosis history (30.3%; 22.9%) showed a statistically significant but modest difference between groups, while no significant difference was observed in computed tomography-identified bronchiectasis (BE) (19.0%; 15.0%).

Pre-6MWT SpO2 (94.4%±2.9%; 96.4%±2.2%) was lower in the EID group, whereas the 6-minute walk distance (382.6±131.0 m; 390.6±113.4 m) did not differ significantly between groups. As expected, based on the definition of EID, post-6MWT SpO2 (82.6%±11.5%; 95.2%±2.6%) was markedly lower in patients with EID.

3. Data splitting for model development

The training set comprised 1,430 patients (EID, n=148; non-EID, n=1,282), and the test set comprised 358 patients (EID, n=37; non-EID, n=321). Most baseline characteristics demonstrated acceptable balance between the two datasets, with the majority showing standardized differences below 0.10 (Supplementary Table S1). Missingness rates of candidate predictors are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Missing data were handled using MICE, as described in the Materials and Methods section.

4. Candidate predictor selection

Boruta identified a subset of variables that are crucial for discriminating EID, retaining a total of 13 predictors. These include BMI, respiratory symptom burden measured by the CAT score, health-related quality of life assessed by the SGRQc score, mental health status indicated by the BDI and BAI scores, and baseline SpO2 prior to the 6MWT, which reflects resting SpO2. Additionally, PFT parameters were retained, including FEV1 (% predicted), FVC (% predicted), FEV1/FVC (%), DLCO (% predicted), and RV/TLC (%). In addition, X-ray identified BE and hemoglobin level (g/dL) were selected as important predictors.

5. Model performance evaluation

All models exhibited strong discriminative performance based on PR-AUC in the training dataset (Table 2), with overall patterns of PR-AUC largely overlapping (Supplementary Figure S1). Among the evaluated models, the RF model displayed the most favorable calibration characteristics, showing the lowest overall probabilistic prediction error as assessed by the Brier score. Additionally, the calibration intercept and slope of the RF model were closest to their ideal values (0 for the intercept and 1 for the slope). Consistent with these quantitative findings, graphical calibration assessments based on OoF predictions indicated that the RF model had the closest agreement between predicted probabilities and observed event rates within the range of low-to-intermediate predicted risks (Supplementary Figure S2).

In the independent test dataset, however, PR-AUC was consistently lower than that observed in the training dataset across all models (Table 2). In contrast, calibration- related metrics showed only minimal differences between the training and test datasets. Brier scores in the test dataset were slightly higher than those derived from OoF predictions in the training dataset, with the RF model continuing to demonstrate the lowest prediction error. Similarly, calibration intercepts and slopes in the test dataset were modestly lower than the corresponding estimates in the training dataset.

6. Threshold-based operating point selection

Among the evaluated models, the XGB model demonstrated the highest sensitivity while meeting the specified constraints (specificity ≥0.50 and PPV ≥0.20) (Table 3 and Supplementary Figure S3). Consequently, the XGB model was chosen as the final screening model. When the selected operating thresholds were applied to the independent test set, the sensitivity of the XGB model was comparable to that observed in the OoF evaluation.

7. Permutation-based feature importance of the XGB model

Permutation-based feature importance analysis revealed that the predictive performance of the XGB model was primarily driven by a limited number of key clinical variables (Figure 2 and Supplementary Table S3). Among all predictors, pre-6MWT SpO2 had the largest contribution to model performance, followed by DLCO, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, SGRQc score, FVC, BAI score, and BMI. For these variables, the 95% confidence intervals of PR-AUC reduction did not cross zero.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to apply machine learning approaches to assess EID risk during the 6MWT in patients with COPD. In this cohort, the prevalence of EID was lower than reported in several prior studies focused on moderate-to-severe COPD [6,13,14], likely due to the high proportion of patients with milder disease. Importantly, EID was not limited to advanced COPD; a significant proportion of patients with GOLD stages 1 or 2 also exhibited EID in this cohort.

Consistent with previous reports, patients with EID exhibited a more adverse clinical profile. Individuals with EID tended to be older [15], have greater cumulative smoking exposure [16], exhibit more severe airflow limitation, as reflected by lower FEV1 [15,17-23], and resting oxygen saturation has been reported to be lower [15,17,18,21,23]. EID has also been associated with worse respiratory symptoms [24] and poorer health-related quality of life [25], as well as a higher frequency of AEs [1,6] and increased mortality [1-6,26].

The primary objective of this study was to rule out EID among low-risk patients rather than to definitively establish its presence. In this context, conventional metrics like the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve provide limited clinical insight, especially considering the low prevalence of EID [27,28]. We therefore emphasized precision-recall-based evaluation and prioritized sensitivity to minimize missed cases of EID. At the same time, lower bounds for specificity (≥0.50) and PPV (≥0.20) were imposed to ensure that threshold selection remained clinically feasible and did not result in excessive downstream testing.

The substantial reduction in PR-AUC observed in the test dataset (Table 2) is likely due, at least in part, to optimism introduced during model development through imbalance correction techniques, such as SMOTENC. Synthetic over-sampling can inflate apparent PR performance by artificially increasing the density and separability of minority class observations in the training data. This leads to an overestimation of PR-based metrics that do not generalize to data maintaining the original low-prevalence distribution [29]. The simultaneous increase in Brier scores, along with the downward shift of calibration intercepts and slopes across all models, further supports the presence of probability-scale distortion following rebalanced training [29].

Notably, the XGB model displayed significantly higher calibration slopes in both the training and test datasets. This may be attributed to the inherent probability compression of tree-based ensemble methods, which results in predicted probabilities being distributed within a relatively narrow range, even while maintaining risk ranking [30]. This pattern of under-dispersion is consistent with its higher Brier scores compared to other models. Collectively, these findings emphasize that both imbalance correction strategies and model structure can affect discrimination and calibration, highlighting the importance of comprehensive performance assessment when developing machine learning models for screening-oriented clinical use.

Despite the reduction in PR-AUC in the test dataset and the relatively unfavorable calibration metrics of the XGB model, threshold-based measures such as sensitivity and specificity remained stable between the training and test sets, with particularly consistent results observed for the XGB and RF models (Table 3). Although the PPV declined to below 0.20 for the MLR and SVC models in the test dataset, the magnitude of this decrease stayed within an acceptable range.

These findings suggest that, although the overall distribution of predicted probability rankings weakened in the test dataset, model performance at the selected operating threshold remained relatively stable. Therefore, discrimination metrics such as PR-AUC may not adequately reflect screening performance in this context [31,32]. While these results should not be generalized without caution, the findings indicate that machine learning-based models have the potential to serve as practical screening tools in real-world clinical settings. In cohorts with varying outcome prevalence, however, recalibration or other adjustment methods may be necessary to ensure appropriate probability scaling and threshold performance [33].

Based on the XGB feature importance analysis, the predictive signal for EID was distributed across multiple clinical domains. Pre-6MWT SpO₂ and DLCO exhibited significantly higher feature importance than other variables, suggesting that these measures provided the most informative signals for identifying patients at risk of desaturation. A lower pre-6MWT SpO₂ may indicate reduced baseline oxygen reserve, making patients more susceptible to reaching the EID threshold during exertion. In contrast, a reduced DLCO likely reflects impaired pulmonary gas exchange and diffusion capacity during exercise [34]. Notably, measures of mental health status, particularly the BAI score, demonstrated relatively high feature importance, despite receiving limited attention in previous studies of EID. These measures may reflect broader aspects of disease burden, symptom perception, or reduced functional and psychological reserve, all of which can influence an individual's tolerance to exertion [35,36].

From a clinical perspective, the proposed model is not intended to replace the 6MWT or serve as a definitive diagnostic tool for EID. Rather, it is designed to function as a screening aid that helps prioritize patients who may benefit most from the 6MWT. Given the constraints associated with routinely performing the 6MWT in all patients with COPD, a pre-test risk stratification approach based on readily available variables may facilitate a more efficient allocation of testing resources in real-world practice.

The strengths of this study include the use of a large, nationwide, multicenter cohort that reflects routine clinical practice, as well as a screening-oriented modeling framework explicitly aligned with its intended clinical use. Model interpretability was supported through OoF permutation-based feature importance, and performance evaluation emphasized metrics appropriate for low-prevalence outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, external validation in independent cohorts was not conducted, and the study population was derived from a single country, which may limit generalizability. Additionally, some baseline characteristics were imbalanced between the training and test sets following data splitting, which may have introduced potential bias in model performance estimation and affected the robustness and generalizability of the model. Second, the KOCOSS cohort is characterized by a markedly high proportion of male patients (97.7%), which may limit the applicability of the findings to female patients with COPD. Third, several predictor variables contained substantial missing data; specifically, the BDI and BAI scores were missing in approximately half of the patients. Although missing values were addressed using MICE, the high degree of missingness may still have introduced uncertainty and potential bias into the model development process. Fourth, the analysis focused on cross-sectional prediction of EID during a single 6MWT and did not incorporate longitudinal outcomes or repeated measurements over time. Finally, while internal validation strategies were carefully implemented, prospective evaluation in real-world clinical workflows will be necessary to confirm the practical utility of this screening approach.

In conclusion, this study suggests that machine learning approaches can be effective in identifying patients with COPD who are at high risk of EID. For screening purposes, model evaluation should not depend on a single performance metric; instead, it should consider discrimination, calibration, and threshold-dependent measures collectively to select the most appropriate model. In patients suspected of having EID, assessing resting SpO2 and DLCO may provide particularly valuable information.
