Harnessing probiotics to combat nonylphenol toxicity: a multiomics approach of gut microbiome remodelling in Silurus meridionalis
Authors: Deqin Luo, Fanglian Lu, Lian Yang, Zhenbo Gan, Xianbo Zhang, Zhenxin Zhao, Ranran Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs a ubiquitous environmental endocrine disruptor, nonylphenol (NP) threatens aquatic organisms, driving the need for sustainable mitigation strategies. While probiotics represent promising eco-friendly supplements, their molecular mechanisms against NP toxicity remain unclear. In this study, S. meridionalis received 7-week of probiotic (Bacillus subtilis and Lactobacillus acidophilus) pretreatment followed by 15 days of NP exposure. Integrated metagenomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics analyses, with Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) and Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) validation, were performed to elucidate microbial, genetic and metabolic responses. Growth performance, including the specific growth rate (SGR) and weight gain rate (WGR), was concurrently assessed.ResultsNP exposure significantly suppressed WGR and SGR, and induced gut microbiota dysbiosis alongside and lipid metabolism disorders in S. meridionalis. Probiotic pretreatment effectively reversed these toxic effects and restored the inhibited WGR and SGR. Multiomics integration revealed that the protective effects of probiotics were mediated by a coherent “microbe-host” co-metabolism network across 3 progressive layers: (1) Microbial Remodelling: in which beneficial taxa (e.g., Bacteroides eggerthii and Cetobacterium sp.) were enriched, and the functional capacity for short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) synthesis and ethanolamine metabolism was enhanced; (2) Host Gene Regulation: in which key lipid metabolism genes (ek1, cept1, ept1, mogat2, and abcg2a) were upregulated, and lipase activity was restored; and (3) Metabolic Pathway Activation and Physiological Repair: in which the activity of the NP-suppressed Kennedy pathway was reactivated, thereby promoting phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) and phosphatidylcholine (PC) synthesis and ultimately restoring gut barrier function. These results were further were corroborated by RT‒qPCR and ELISA.ConclusionThis study systematically elucidated that probiotics alleviated NP toxicity by remodelling a “microbiota-host Kennedy pathway gene–metabolite (PE and PC)-growth performance” regulatory network. The key mechanism is the beneficial microbiota activating the host Kennedy pathway and restoring gut phospholipid homeostasis and barrier function. These findings provide a theoretical basis for developing targeted, lipid metabolism focused probiotic feed additives for use in sustainable aquaculture.Keywords: Growth performance, Silurus meridionalis, Bacillus subtilis, Lactobacillus acidophilus, Kennedy pathway, Lipid metabolism

Introduction

NP, a widely used industrial compound in surfactants and lubricants [1, 2], poses a significant environmental threat. Its hydrophobicity leads to its accumulation in organic-rich sediments, making it a persistent aquatic pollutant [3, 4]. As a typical environmental endocrine disruptor, NP induces both acute and chronic toxicity in aquatic organisms. In fish, crustaceans, and molluscs, NP disrupts reproductive hormone synthesis, leading to gonadal abnormalities and imbalanced population sex ratios [5, 6].NP also induced intestinal toxicity, damage the gut epithelium, compromise barrier integrity, and perturb microbiome homeostasis [7]. Its long alkyl chain resistance leads to persistent accumulation in gut tissues, exacerbating barrier dysfunction and microbial dysbiosis [8].

Within antibiotic-free aquaculture, probiotics have been redefined as strategic microbial modulators [9, 10]. Their function now extends beyond growth promotion to include the enhancement of environmental adaptability through dynamic microbiota–host interactions [11]. Probiotics transiently colonize the gut mucosa, directly or indirectly affecting host health through the secretion of metabolites, competition for ecological niches, and modulation of the local microenvironment [12]. Evidence from various models supports their multifunctional roles. For example, Lactobacillus enhances gut barrier function by activating the hypoxia-inducible factor signalling pathway through lactic acid production [13]. In Oncorhynchus mykiss, dietary probiotics improve gut morphology and absorptive capacity [14]. Studies have also shown that B. subtilis (including its spore form) promotes beneficial lactic acid bacteria, whereas L. acidophilus affects the microbiota composition, improves the gut structure, and increases digestive enzyme activity [15–17]. Furthermore, in Oreochromis niloticus, supplementation with multistrain probiotics (e.g., B. subtilis or L. acidophilus) alters the gut microbiota to increase nutrient absorption [18]. In Litopenaeus vannamei, a supplement containing B. subtilis and L. acidophilus strengthens the gut barrier, enhances the utilization of carbon by the gut microbiota, and increases beneficial probiotic populations [19]. Similarly, in Sus scrofa, the same combination strengthens the mucosal barrier by increasing the abundance of beneficial microbiota and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) [20]. These findings highlight the dual role of probiotics in improving nutrient utilization and enhancing resilience by maintaining a diverse and functional gut microbiota. Although the role of probiotics in promoting gut homeostasis is well documented, their ability to reduce environmental pollutant toxicity and the related molecular mechanisms is not yet fully known.

S. meridionalis, an economically important species of the family Siluridae, is valued for its high intake ability, strong disease resistance, rapid growth, and nutritional value, making it a vital source of high-quality protein [21, 22]. Due to its benthic habit, S. meridionalis is consistently exposed to NP in sediments [23]. It is unclear whether this affects the gut balance and whether probiotics can counteract the toxicity, as well as the molecular processes involved. On the basis of the aforementioned background, this study employed S. meridionalis as a model organism. By utilizing a “probiotic-pretreatment followed by NP-exposure” experimental design, we sought not only to evaluate the mitigation of NP‑induced intestinal toxicity by a B. subtilis and L. acidophilus complex but also to mechanistically decipher its protective effects. To achieve this goal, an integrated multi‑omics approach was used: metagenomics was used to analyse microbial community and functional pathway changes, transcriptomics was used to examine host genes related to lipid metabolism and intestinal barrier function, and untargeted metabolomics was used to track global shifts in the gut metabolome. Finally, through correlating and integrating of these data, a “microbial function – host gene – metabolite –phenotype” regulatory network was constructed, thereby providing a system‑level perspective on how the probiotic formulation alleviates NP‑induced toxicity.

Materials and methods

Experimental feed and design

B. subtilis and L. acidophilus used in this study were purchased from Shandong Linyi Yi Antibiotic-Free Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). with viable counts of 1.0 × 10¹¹ CFU/g and 1.0 × 10¹⁰ CFU/g, respectively. In accordance with the experimental protocol, a stock solution of NP was initially prepared in ethanol and subsequently diluted to achieve the desired working concentrations of 0.1 mg/L and 0.25 mg/L. The two NP concentrations were chosen on the basis of a previous study in juvenile African catfish (Clarias gariepinus), in which exposure to 0.1–0.3 mg/L NP for 15 days induced marked reproductive toxicity [24]. Accordingly, the lower concentration (0.1 mg/L) was set to the minimum effective dose identified in that study, while the higher concentration (0.25 mg/L) was chosen as an intermediate point within the same range. Simultaneously, equal quantities of the two probiotic types were combined to create compound probiotic powder preparations, adhering to a mass ratio of 1.00% relative to the basal feed. These preparations were then dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4), and the buffer solution volume (mL) was maintained at a feed mass (g) ratio of 1:10 [25]. The probiotic compounds were uniformly sprayed onto the basal feed, thoroughly mixed, and air-dried under ventilated conditions. Fresh feed was prepared daily, portioned appropriately, and stored at − 20 °C until required for use. After acclimation to the rearing conditions, the experimental fish were randomly assigned to six groups (20 fish per group, with three replicates per group) as follows: Group C: fed with basal feed only; Group NP1: fed with basal feed for 7 weeks, followed by exposure to 0.1 mg/L NP for 15 days; Group NP2: fed with basal feed for 7 weeks, followed by exposure to 0.25 mg/L NP for 15 days; Group NP1 + PB: fed with compound probiotic-supplemented feed for 7 weeks, followed by exposure to 0.1 mg/L NP for 15 days; Group NP2 + PB: fed with compound probiotic-supplemented feed for 7 weeks, followed by exposure to 0.25 mg/L NP for 15 days; Group PB: fed with compound probiotic-supplemented feed for 7 weeks without NP exposure [26, 27].

Experimental fish

The healthy S. meridionalis juveniles (4 months post-hatching, sexually immature) used in this study were obtained from a local aquaculture farm in Huishui County, Guizhou, China. Prior to the experiment, the fish were acclimated to laboratory conditions for 2 weeks. After acclimation, their total length and body weight were measured (mean total length: 20.56 ± 1.37 cm; body weight: 53.65 ± 4.02 g, mean ± SD), and they were randomly allocated to the different treatment groups. Feeding was performed twice daily (8:00 and 17:30) until apparent satiation. During the experimental period, the water quality was strictly controlled: the water temperature was maintained at 25 ± 2 °C, the dissolved oxygen concentration was maintained kept above 7.0 mg/L, the pH was 7.0 ± 0.5, and the ammonia nitrogen concentration was maintained below 0.08 mg/L [28–30].

Enzyme activity measurement and differentially expressed metabolite validation

The levels of ethanolamine kinase (EK1) and lipase were measured according to the protocols provided by Jiangsu Saiyan Biotechnology Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). EK1 and lipase levels were measured according to the protocols provided by Jiangsu Saiyan Biotechnology Technology Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). To validate the differentially abundant metabolite results, the contents of phosphocholine (PC), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), and total phospholipids (PL) were measured using commercial enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from the same manufacturer. PC was quantified using a sandwich ELISA kit, which employed an anti-PC antibody for specific recognition, a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labelled detection antibody, and a TMB colorimetric reaction. PC concentrations were derived from a standard curve (0–480 pg/mL). PE was quantified using a competitive ELISA kit, where an anti-PE antibody coated on the microplate and an HRP-labelled PE antigen that competed with sample PE for antibody binding were used. PE concentrations were determined from a standard curve (15–240 pg/mL) on the basis of the absorbance at 450 nm, which was inversely correlated with the PE content. PL was quantified using an oxidase-based colorimetric kit: phospholipids were enzymatically hydrolyzed to choline and then oxidized to generate hydrogen peroxide, resulting in the formation of a blue dye at 600 nm, with the absorbance proportional to the PL content. All procedures were performed strictly in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metagenomic analysis

After a 24-hour fasting period, the gut contents were collected from each fish for metagenomic sequencing. Genomic DNA was extracted was extracted using a commercial extraction kit provided by Guizhou Saiyan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guizhou, China). The integrity of the extracted DNA was assessed via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and only samples with a primary band length of ≥ 10 kb were retained to ensure high sequencing quality. After confirming DNA quantity and purity, sequencing was performed on the Illumina Nova Seq high-throughput platform. Independent libraries were constructed for each sample, employing a paired-end sequencing strategy (PE150bp). Raw sequencing reads were initially evaluated using FastQC (v0.12.1) to assess the base quality distribution, mean sequence quality, and nucleotide composition. Quality control was subsequently conducted using Cutadapt (v1.17) and fastp (v0.20.0) to remove low-quality reads (length < 50 bp, average quality score < 20, or reads containing ambiguous bases) [31]. High-quality clean reads were then assembled using MEGAHIT with the “meta-large” preset parameter. A “single-sample preassembly-merging of unmapped reads−reassembly” strategy was applied to achieve efficient large-scale metagenomic assembly and optimization, thereby generating high-quality contigs for subsequent taxonomic annotation and functional analysis. Nonredundant gene sets were constructed using MMseqs2. The predicted sequences were aligned against the GenBank nonredundant (NR) database using BLAST, and hits with E values ≤ 1e − 5 were retained for species annotation [32]. Functional annotation was then performed by comparing the predicted genes against the GO, KEGG, eggNOG, Pfam, SwissProt, CAZy, VFDB, CYPED, and additional reference databases [33]. For downstream differential analysis, metagenomic features (taxonomic and functional) were retained if present in more than 60% of the samples, and their abundances were normalized to reads per kilobase million (RPKM). Differential features between groups were identified using the Kruskal‒Wallis test, with p < 0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance.

Transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the gut samples of S. meridionalis using a commercial kit (Guizhou Saiyan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). RNA quality was rigorously assessed: purity (OD260/280 ratio of 1.8–2.0) and concentration were determined spectrophotometrically, and integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (all samples an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0). Sequencing libraries were constructed from qualified RNA and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were processed using fastp (v0.23.0) to remove adapters, poly-N sequences, and low-quality bases, yielding high-quality clean reads [34]. These reads were aligned to the S. meridionalis reference genome (GCA_014805685.1) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1). Transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie (v1.3.0). For functional annotation, the predicted protein sequences were aligned against the NR, Swiss-Prot, and KOG databases using DIAMOND (v2.0.15) with a significance threshold of an E value < 1e − 5. The top-hit protein (highest alignment score) for each transcript was used to assign functional annotations. Gene expression levels were quantified as read counts and FPKM using featureCounts (v2.0.9) and Salmon (v1.10.0), respectively. Prior to differential expression analysis, genes with CPM (counts per million) > 1 in more than 50% of the samples were retained and log2-transformed. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with DESeq2 (v1.46.0), applying a threshold of adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂FC| > 1) [35]. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of the DEGs were conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.14.0), with the results visualized via ggplot2 (v3.5.1) [36].

Validation of DEGs by reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR)

Five differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (ek1, gpd1l, gpd1b, pgs1, and abcg2a) were selected for validation using RT‒qPCR. The total RNA utilized for validation was identical to that used in the transcriptomic analysis and was reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). RT‒qPCR assays were conducted employing the SYBR Green method. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. The β-actin gene served as an internal reference, and relative gene expression levels were determined using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Each gene was analysed using three biological samples, with each sample assessed in triplicate technical replicates. The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance among groups was evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post‑hoc test [37].

Metabolomic analysis

Gut samples stored at − 80 °C were used for metabolomic analysis. All the experimental instruments (such as steel beads and tweezers) were sterilized at high temperature or disinfected with ethanol prior to use to ensure experimental consistency and prevent cross-contamination. Approximately 50 mg of each sample was weighed and mixed with 1mL of prechilled extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile: water = 2:2:1, v/v/v) containing internal standards. Stainless steel beads were added, and the samples were mechanically disrupted at 65 Hz for 90 s using a tissue grinder. This process was followed by intermittent ultrasonic extraction for 10 min to enhance metabolite recovery. The homogenates were incubated at − 20 °C for 1 h to precipitate proteins and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. A 50 µL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred into a 2 mL centrifuge tube and stored overnight at − 20 °C. The samples were centrifuged again under identical conditions to remove residual impurities, and 120 µL of the final supernatant was transferred into pretreated amber vials for subsequent analysis. Metabolomic profiling was performed using a Waters ACQUITY I-Class PLUS ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system coupled with a Xevo G2-XS QTOF high-resolution mass spectrometer. The chromatographic separation conditions were as follows: 98% A (0–0.25 min), 2% A (10.0–13.0 min), and 98%A (13.1–14.1 min), where mobile phase A was water containing 0.1% formic acid, and phase B was an acetonitrile/methanol mixture. The raw mass spectrometry data were processed using Progenesis QI software for peak extraction, alignment, denoising, and quantification. Prior to analysis, metabolomic features with < 80% missing values and a CV < 30% were retained and log2-transformed. Differentially abundant metabolites were identified using OPLS-DA (VIP > 1) combined with the Student’s t-test (p < 0.05). Metabolites were annotated through multiple databases, including HMDB, KEGG, and Lipid Maps, to identify their chemical classes and potential metabolic pathways. The, significantly altered metabolites were subsequently subjected to statistical and pathway enrichment analyses using Python-based bioinformatics toolkits (e.g., pandas, scipy, bioinfokit), enabling elucidation of biologically relevant changes at the metabolic pathway level.

Statistical analysis

The research data were analysed using OriginPro 2026 and the Windows version of SPSS v27.0 software, and are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The statistical assessment of differences was conducted by applying one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. The growth parameters, included WGR (%) = (final weight – initial weight)/initial weight × 100 and SGR (%/d) = (Ln final weight − Ln initial weight) × 100/day.

Utilizing the scripy.stats.spearmanr function in Python (version 3.10.9), we performed Spearman’s rank correlation analysis to evaluate all pairwise cross-omics interactions, including microbe‒gene, gene‒metabolite, and metabolite‒microbe relationships. To minimize spurious associations arising from the omics data, significant correlations were defined as |r| > 0.7 and raw p < 0.05, followed by Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction applied to all pairwise tests (FDR < 0.05). A conditional filter was then applied for integration: a “microbe–gene–metabolite” triplet was retained only if all three pairwise correlations (microbe–gene, gene–metabolite, metabolite–microbe) simultaneously satisfied |r| > 0.7 and FDR < 0.05. This strict criterion ensured that only closed-loop association architectures with mutual reinforcement across all three omics layers were considered for downstream analysis. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the genes and metabolites present in the identified triplets using the hypergeometric test, with an FDR < 0.05 as the significance threshold. All correlation heatmaps and network visualizations were generated using the Majorbio Cloud Platform (https://www.majorbio.com/tools). Pathway figures were generated employing Figdraw 2.0.

Results

Gut biochemical indicators and growth performance

Exposure to NP significantly inhibited the growth and enzymatic activity in S. meridionalis (Fig. 1), with the key enzymes, EK1 and lipase showing a decrease in activity compared with the control group (p < 0.05). The NP1 and NP2 groups also experienced lower SGR and WGR (p < 0.05). However, the introduction of probiotics significantly alleviated these effects, with the NP1+PB and NP2+PB groups showing increased growth and enzyme activity compared with the NP group (p < 0.05), they did not fully reach control group levels. The PB group showed no significant differences compared with the control group (p > 0.05).

Probiotic supplementation and the restoration of microbiota‒gene association patterns

Probiotic (PB) supplementation effectively reversed the negative impact of NP on the gut microbiota and host gene expression, as shown by integrated metagenomic and transcriptomic data. NP exposure (NP1/NP2 groups) significantly reduced in the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as B. eggerthii, Bacteroides acidifaciens, Cetobacterium sp., and Parabacteroides merdae, but significantly increased the abundance of harmful bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Clostridium. PB supplementation (NP1+PB/NP2+PB groups) markedly restored the bacterial balance and abundance (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, transcriptomic analysis revealed that PB supplementation reversed NP-induced gene downregulation and significantly upregulated key lipid metabolism genes (e.g., mogat2, gpd1l, ept1, ocln, ek1, and abcg2a) (Fig. 2B). Correlation network analysis further demonstrated that PB reshaped the disruption of the “microbiota–gene” interaction network caused by NP. Specifically, the restored beneficial bacteria (especially B. acidifaciens, B.eggertthii, and P. merdae) showed significant positive synergistic correlations with upregulated lipid metabolism genes (mogat2, gpd1l, ept1, ocln, ek1, abcg2a) (Fig. 2C). In summary, PB alleviated NP-induced toxicity by synergistically restoring the composition of the gut microbiota and host lipid metabolism gene expression.

Probiotic supplementation and the restoration of microbiota‒metabolite association patterns

Among the twenty most significantly enriched gut bacterial taxa, the NP group had more harmful genera (e.g., Clostridium and Escherichia; p < 0.05), whereas the NP+PB group contained more beneficial genera (e.g., Parabacteroides, Bacteroides and Cetobacterium; p < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). Metabolites in the NP2+PB group were significantly enriched in the phospholipase D signalling pathway, regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, and glycerophospholipid metabolism, which indicated that PB might regulate host energy metabolism (Fig. 3B). Correlation analysis revealed that NP exposure weakened the associations between beneficial bacteria (e.g., P. merdae, B. acidifaciens, B. eggerthii and Cetobacterium sp.) and key metabolites (e.g., PC, PE, phosphatidic acid, and phosphocholine), whereas PB supplementation strengthened these associations (Fig. 3C). In summary, PB alleviated NP-induced toxicity by restructuring the gut microbiota and modulating host lipid metabolism.

Probiotic supplementation and the restoration of transcription‒metabolite association patterns

Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic data demonstrated that PB supplementation helped restore host genes and metabolic homeostasis disrupted by NP. Compared with the NP group, the NP+PB group showed increased enrichment of metabolic pathways, particularly in lipid metabolism (Fig. 4A), with the glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway being central (Fig. 4B). In this group, key lipid metabolism genes such as mogat2 and ept1, along with lipid metabolites such as PC and PE, were significantly increased (p<0.05) (Fig. 4C-D). Network analysis revealed distinct “gene‑metabolite” interactions between the NP2 and NP2+PB groups. The NP2+PB group exhibited a strengthened positive‑correlation network with the upregulation of key genes (including mogat2, ept1, ek1, and ocln), and phospholipid metabolites (PC, PE and Phosphocholine) compared to with the NP2 group (Fig. 4E-F). In summary, the NP2+PB group showed reconstructed synergistic interactions centred on mogat2 and ept1 connecting host genes and metabolites, thereby reversing the suppression of NP-induced glycerophospholipid metabolism suppression.

Integrated multiomics analysis: PB alleviates NP-induced gut barrier damage by activating the gut microbiota–Kennedy pathway axis to promote host phospholipid synthesis

Integrated analyses of metagenomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data, supported by mechanistic investigations, revealed that PB restored gut barrier function compromised by NP exposure by remodelling the “microbiota–substrate–host synthesis” axis, thereby driving gut phospholipid synthesis (Fig. 5). Specifically, PB increased beneficial bacteria such as B. eggerthii and Cetobacterium sp. The SCFAs produced by these bacteria facilitate triglyceride (TG) hydrolysis, increasing 2-monoacylglycerol (2-MAG) levels. Within enterocytes, 2-MAG is converted to 1,2-diacylglycerol (1,2-DAG) by MOGAT2, which together with Etn, is a precursor for phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) synthesis. PB supplementation co-ordinately upregulated the expression of core enzymatic genes expression in the Kennedy pathway, driving PE synthesis through three sequential steps: 1) Phosphorylation: Etn is phosphorylated to phosphoethanolamine (P-Etn) by EK1 (encoded by the significantly upregulated ek1 gene). 2) Cytidylylation: P-Etn is converted to CDP-ethanolamine by the key regulatory enzyme P-Etn cytidylyltransferase (CEPT1; the encoding gene cept1 also exhibited elevated expression). 3) Transfer: CDP-Etn reacts with 1,2-DAG to produce PE, a reaction catalysed by diacylglycerol ethanolamine phosphotransferase (EPT1; concomitantly, ept1 showed increased transcription). Enhanced flux through this pathway significantly increased the PE and PC levels, thereby promoting the integration of newly synthesized phospholipids into the enterocyte membrane. This finding supports the anchoring of tight-junction proteins such as ocln to strengthen the physical barrier of the gut. In summary, PB alleviates NP-induced metabolic and barrier dysfunction by reconstructing a complete metabolic cascade, from beneficial bacteria and substrate provision to the coordinated upregulation of the core Kennedy pathway enzymes, thus systemically promoting the endogenous synthesis of PE and PC.

Discussion

Probiotics as potential in aquaculture: modulating the gut microbiome to counteract np‑induced stress

Sustainable aquaculture calls for eco-friendly strategies to mitigate the impact of pervasive environmental pollutants such as NP. Modulating the gut microbiota to improve host health has been suggested as a promising alternative to direct chemical interventions[38–40]. Our integrated multiomics analysis (metagenomics, transcriptomics, and metabolomics) provided insights into the probiotic-mediated effects: while NP exposure induced gut dysbiosis, PB supplementation appeared to restored microbial homeostasis [41]. This restoration was characterized by the enrichment of beneficial commensals, including B. eggerthii and Cetobacterium sp., which may be associated with increased production of SCFAs such as acetate and propionate [42, 43]. Taken together, our findings suggest that probiotics may serve as potential “green bioregulators”, that support a protective microbiome, potentially contributing to host resilience against NP‑induced stress. These observations point to the possible utility of microbiota-modulated approaches in the context of sustainable aquaculture management.

Probiotic supplementation and the nutrient assimilation axis: genomic insight into feed efficiency

Our results indicated that NP exposure was associated with inhibited activity of gut lipase, a critical enzyme for lipid digestion in carnivorous fish [44]. This impairment appeared to coincide with an altered hydrolysis of dietary TG into 2-MAG, the primary form for intestinal fat absorption. Consequently, the uptake of energy and essential fatty acids seemed to be affected, which may represent a contributing factor restricting growth and poses a potential bottleneck for sustainable production [45]. Notably, transcriptomic and enzymatic analyses suggested that PB supplementation correlated with the recovery of lipase expression and activity, potentially promoting efficient luminal lipid digestion and supporting a sustained supply of 2-MAG [46]. By potentially counteracting this digestive bottleneck [47, 48], PB supplementation appeared to be associated with the re‑establishment of nutrient assimilation axis, which may supply essential substrates for downstream anabolic pathways and could be linked to the recovery of growth performance (WGR and SGR). In China, lakes, reservoirs, and rivers collectively account for 48% of the total freshwater aquaculture area and are mostly connected to major river systems, where NP concentrations have been reported to range from 60 to 1000 ng/L [49, 50]. Moreover, NP has been detected in the muscle tissues of farmed freshwater fish [51]. Together, these observations offer genomic insights into how probiotics may help alleviate NP-induced toxicity and imply a possible strategy for safeguarding nutrient absorption and feed efficiency in aquaculture under pollutant stress.

Multiomics insights into probiotic-mediated gut barrier repair via a CDP-Etn+ 1,2-MAG→PE metabolic axis

Our results suggested that NP was associated with inhibition of the CDP-Etn + 1,2-MAG→PE axis, with reduced PE and PC synthesis. As phospholipids constitute the fundamental matrix of cellular membranes, their reduction may impair the integrity and function of gut epithelial cells [52, 53]. Notably, our integrated multiomics approach provided insights into the potential probiotic counteraction: transcriptomic data indicated the upregulation of key genes within this axis (e.g., ek1, cept1, ept1, mogat2). Critically, enzymatic assays suggested a restoration of EK1 activity, which appeared consistent with a PB-associated reprogramming of the host metabolism. This metabolic change was accompanied by a rebound in PL, PE and PC levels. The reconstituted PL pool could serve a dual purpose: primarily, it may provide the necessary biochemical substrate for the repair of cellular membranes and organelles; furthermore, it may help establish a lipid microenvironment conducive to the assembly of tight junction complexes [54]. The concurrent upregulation of the tight junction protein genes (ocln) in the NP+PB group offered a molecular link between this metabolic repair and enhanced barrier function, which is consistent with previous reports on probiotics [55–57]. Taken together, these observations suggest that PB may alleviate NP‑induced damage via a complementary dual-axis mechanism: it appears to restore the “Lipase→2-MAG” axis for substrate and energy acquisition and may also influence the “CDP-Etn+ 1,2-MAG→PE” axis for membrane biosynthesis. This model offers a potential explanation for the observed growth recovery (WGR, SGR) and offers a case for considering metabolic modulation in the context of chemical-resilient aquaculture. Nevertheless, as our current findings are primarily derived from correlative multiomics and enzymatic data, direct causal validation through knockout of key genes is still needed. In addition, the experiments were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions, and further studies are needed to assess the alleviating effects of probiotics in more complex field settings. Moreover, it remains unclear whether these effects are generalizable across species.

Conclusion

In summary, our multiomics analysis offers a preliminary suggestion that probiotic supplementation may operate through a microbiota-triggered, host-centred metabolic reprogramming. The enrichment of SCFA-producing taxa (e.g., B. eggerthii and Cetobacterium sp.) appeared to be correlated with a repair cascade that concurrently influenced luminal lipid digestion and was associated with reactivation of the host’s Kennedy pathway for PE synthesis. This dual response was accompanied by the coordinated upregulation of core genes (e.g., ek1, cept1, ept1, mogat2), which may contribute to functional recovery ranging from nutrient assimilation to membrane assembly. Thus, by potentially re-establishing this critical metabolic flow, probiotics could provide a physiological basis that may support growth recovery under pollutant stress. Collectively, these insights provide a preliminary framework for employing genomics to understand microbe‒host interactions, thereby offering perspectives that may inform the development of resilient and sustainable approaches to green aquaculture.
