Co-targeting CDK4/6 enhances anti-cancer activity and alleviates immune-related adverse events of anti-PD-1 antibody for breast cancer
Authors: Shu-Wei Hu, Ming-Hsin Yeh, Yu-Hao He, Ya-Ling Wei, Fang-Ju Cheng, Yi-Lun Yeh, Thanh Kieu Huynh, Pei-Tong Liu, Dai-Wei Hu, Tzu-Hsuan Chang, Bo-Wei Wang, Bo-Rong Chen, Mei-Chun Lin, Der-Yen Lee, Yi-Hsien Hsieh, Yuan-Man Hsu, Chih-Hsin Tang, Ping-Chih Ho, Mien-Chie Hung, Wei-Chien Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryEarly-onset breast cancer (EOBC) increases annually with unclear mechanisms and shows poor therapeutic responses and survival rates. We identify environmental exposure to the common plasticizer di-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (DEHP) as a contributing factor to breast tumor initiation by impairing cancer immune surveillance. DEHP exposure upregulates the immune checkpoint molecule programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) through estrogen receptor beta (ERβ) activation in human breast cancer cells and mouse models. Transcriptomic analysis reveals cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) as a key mediator of DEHP-induced immunosuppressive signaling. The CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib suppresses ERβ, programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), and PD-L1 expression, enhances anti-tumor responses, and reduces immune-related adverse events caused by anti-PD-1 antibody treatment in mice. While the strongest immune alterations appear in DEHP-exposed EOBC patients, the mechanisms extend to DEHP-associated breast cancer regardless of age. This study highlights immune dysregulation as a hallmark of DEHP-related breast carcinogenesis and supports co-targeting CDK4/6 and PD-L1 as a therapeutic strategy.

Introduction

Early-onset breast cancer (EOBC), commonly considered as breast cancer diagnosed under the age of 45, accounts for 10%–12% of new breast cancer cases1 and shows approximately 50% recurrence2 and 15% 5-year mortality.3 Most EOBC patients are diagnosed at a more malignant stage, with larger and denser tumors, and are less responsive to standard therapies, contributing to a poorer prognosis.4 The therapeutic response, relapse-free survival, and overall survival of patients with EOBC are significantly worse than those of their elder counterparts.5 A family history of breast cancer and genetic defects, such as BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene mutations, account for only 10%–15% of EOBCs6 and are found to have relatively low significance in this disease.7 Young age has been identified as an independent prognostic risk factor for breast cancer8 and is associated with lower disease-free and 5-year survival rates.9

The immune system plays a crucial role in eliminating transformed cells through the cytotoxicity of macrophages, dendritic cells, and natural killer T cells, along with cytokine production.10 A tumor-associated immune response is determined by the level of tumor-specific antigens, such as HER2, which are expressed on many solid tumors and serve as targets for immune effector T cells.11 Nevertheless, immune surveillance against cancer cells is defective due to the expression of negative co-stimulatory molecules, such as immune checkpoint programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4, for the repression of cytotoxic functions of T cells via interaction with their counter receptors programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) and co-stimulatory molecules like CD86, respectively.12 Lymphoid infiltration has been reported as a prognostic variable for early-onset breast carcinomas.13,14 Despite the association of higher tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) with young age in patients with invasive lobular15 and ductal16 breast cancer, the association of PD-L1+ TILs with young age16 may reduce the immune surveillance of TILs against cancerous cells in younger patients. However, the involvement of immune checkpoint and TIL dysregulation in the early onset of breast cancer remains unclear.

Plasticizers are widely used in the production of many products, including medical consumables, food wraps, plastic coatings, cosmetic formulations, and children’s toys. As endocrine-disrupting chemicals,17 plasticizers interfere with the secretion, binding, synthesis, action, elimination, or transport of natural hormones.18 In addition to causing birth defects and developmental disorders, overexposure to plasticizers increases the risk of breast cancer by enhancing estrogen receptor-dependent cell proliferation19 and has been implicated in breast tumorigenesis in young women in an epidemiological study.20 Polypropylene microplastics, which contain phthalate plasticizers such as di-2-ethylhexyl phthalate (DEHP), have demonstrated the potential to exacerbate breast cancer progression by inducing the expression of metastasis-related genes.21 Furthermore, phthalates have been shown to enhance the proliferation, migration, and invasion of breast cancer cells.22 The higher daily phthalate intake of schoolchildren in East Asia than in Western countries23 may explain the higher incidence of EOBC in East Asia.24 Nearly 70% of EOBC tumor tissues express hormone receptors,25 suggesting that endocrine disruption by environmental toxins may be an important risk factor for this disease. However, it remains unclear whether and how exposure to plasticizers causes the early onset of breast cancer.

In this study, we investigated how exposure to plasticizers alters immune regulatory pathways relevant to breast cancer development. Plasticizer exposure suppressed T cell cytotoxicity by upregulating PD-L1 and PD-1 through cyclin-dependent kinase 4 (CDK4) and estrogen receptor beta (ERβ) signal pathways to facilitate tumorigenesis and progression. Despite the significant suppression of tumor growth, monotherapy with an anti-PD-l antibody also elicited immune-related adverse events (irAEs), the hurdle of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatments in breast cancer treatment,26,27 in a DEHP-exposed immunocompetent mouse model. Interestingly, co-targeting CDK4/6 not only increased tumor-specific CD8+ T cell activation to enhance the anti-tumor activity of an anti-PD-1 antibody but also alleviated ICI-induced irAEs. These findings highlight a potential immunosuppressive mechanism triggered by environmental toxins and offer therapeutic insights that may be relevant to EOBC patients. While the most pronounced immune alterations were observed in DEHP-exposed EOBC patients, the mechanistic findings may broadly apply to DEHP-associated breast cancer regardless of age at onset.

Results

Exposure to DEHP was associated with early tumorigenesis and low tumor infiltration of lymphocytes of human breast tumors

First, we observed that tumor infiltration of CD3+, cytotoxic CD8+, and cytotoxic granzyme B+ (GZMB+) lymphocytes showed a trend in positive correlation with diagnosis age (Figure S1A), suggesting the involvement of immune dysfunction in certain populations of EOBC patients. Exposure to DEHP, the most common plasticizer used in making daily products, has been reported as a risk factor for overall breast cancer.20 Since plasticizer metabolites are excreted primarily through the urinary system within 24–48 h but can accumulate in hair for several months to years,28 hair rather than urinary levels of DEHP were therefore measured to represent the long-term exposure history of patients to this plasticizer. The average hair DEHP level was 0.19 ± 0.27 fmol/mg in healthy donors and 5.22 ± 10.63 fmol/mg in patients with breast cancer (Figure 1A). DEHP-positive cases, in which hair DEHP levels were over 10-fold of the average in healthy donors (1.9 fmol/mg), were found in 23 of 70 (33%) overall patients, in 9 of 22 (40%) EOBC patients, and in 14 of 48 (29%) non-EOBC patients but in none of the healthy donors (Figure 1B). In overall breast cancer patients, the hair DEHP level showed a trend in negative correlation with age at diagnosis of breast cancer (Figure S1B) and was significantly associated with lower levels of CD8+ and GZMB+ but not CD3+ TILs (Figure S1C; Tables S1–S3). Moreover, patients with higher hair DEHP content had a 9.24- and 4.94-fold increased odds ratio (OR) of lower CD8 and GZMB expression, respectively (Table S4). A significant reduction in CD3+ and cytotoxic CD8+ TILs was observed in DEHP-exposed EOBC patients (Figure 1C), while GZMB+ reductions were evident in both EOBC and non-EOBC groups (Figure 1D). DEHP suppressed T cell-mediated cytotoxicity and promoted tumor occurrence and progression

To further confirm the involvement of immune dysregulation in DEHP-associated tumorigenesis and tumor progression in breast cancers, two immune-competent syngeneic mouse models, MMTV-HER2/neu-transgenic and 4T1 breast tumor allograft mouse models, were employed. A dose of 1.73 mg/kg, equivalent to 8.4 mg for a 60 kg individual, represents the highest tolerable exposure level of DEHP defined across various countries.29 This dosage has been associated with toxic effects in preclinical models and lies near the upper threshold of exposure levels commonly used in regulatory toxicology studies.30,31,32,33 Administration of DEHP accelerated tumorigenesis, as it reduced the average time to tumorigenesis from 46 to 31 weeks (Figure 2A, left) and shortened the tumor-free survival rate (Figure 2A, right) in the MMTV-HER2/neu transgenic mouse model. To further investigate the promoting effect of DEHP on tumor formation of breast cancer, BALB/c mice were injected with 4T1 mouse breast cancer cells into the mammary fat pad followed by daily DEHP treatment (1.73 mg/kg) for 1 month. Exposure to DEHP reduced the average time to form palpable tumors from 16.3 to 10.4 days (Figure 2B) and increased tumor weight (Figure 2C) and growth rate (Figure 2D) of 4T1 tumors in a BALB/c mouse model. We next examined the effects of DEHP on the immune cell population in peripheral blood and the tumor microenvironment. The populations of T cells with CD4+ and CD8+ markers, as well as CD69+, an early activation marker that plays a crucial role in T cell activation and tissue retention,34 were more significantly reduced by DEHP in tumor tissues (Figure 2E) compared to peripheral blood (Figure S2A) in 4T1 allograft mice, as determined by flow cytometry analysis. The reduction of the CD8+GZMB+ population in DEHP-exposed 4T1 allograft tumors further demonstrated the suppressive effect of plasticizers on the cytotoxicity of TILs (Figure S2B). Lower tumor infiltration of CD3+, CD8+, and GZMB+ lymphocytes in the tumor tissues of both MMTV-HER2/neu (Figure 2F) and 4T1 allograft mice (Figure 2G) was observed by immunohistochemical (IHC) staining. DEHP promotes immune evasion in breast cancer cells by inducing PD-L1 expression via ERβ-dependent transcriptional activation

To further investigate how DEHP may contribute to immune dysregulation in breast cancer, we performed RNA sequencing on MCF-10A human breast epithelial cells treated with DEHP. Hallmark pathway analysis revealed a significant downregulation of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) signaling following DEHP exposure (red in Figures 3A and S3A). Gene Ontology analysis similarly showed that DEHP repressed pathways related to T cell-mediated cytotoxicity (Figure 3B), suggesting the potential for impaired immune surveillance. These transcriptomic changes may be accompanied by, and potentially linked to, increased expression of multiple immune checkpoint molecules.35,36 Using an immune checkpoint antibody array, we found that DEHP enhanced the expression of CD200, HVEM, PD-L1, and PD-L2 in MCF-10A cells (Figure S3B), with concordant increases in mRNA levels of PD-L1, PD-L2, and CD200 (Figure S3C). The upregulation of PD-L1, but not PD-L2 and CD200, was further validated in DEHP-treated tumors from the 4T1/BALB/c allograft model in vivo (Figure S3D). These data further support that DEHP selectively induces PD-L1 expression as part of its immunomodulatory effect in both normal and malignant mammary epithelial contexts. Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining confirmed that DEHP dose-dependently increased PD-L1 protein expression (Figures 3C and 3D), and flow cytometry showed elevated surface PD-L1 levels (Figures 3E and S3E). Consistent with a previous report,37 HER2 overexpression increased PD-L1 expression in MCF-10A cells, and this effect was further enhanced by DEHP treatment (Figure S3F). Since DEHP increased PD-L1 mRNA levels (Figure S3B), we next examined whether this regulation occurred at the transcriptional level. Luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that DEHP dose-dependently enhanced PD-L1 promoter activity in MCF-7 cells (Figure 3F). Using the JASPAR database, we identified ERβ (ESR2), STAT3, and RELA (nuclear factor κB) as potential transcriptional regulators of PD-L1 (Figure 3G). Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) further revealed that the expression of ERβ, STAT3, and RELA but not ERα positively correlated with PD-L1 expression across breast cancer subtypes (Figures 3H and S4A). Moreover, high expression of ERβ and STAT3 was associated with a trend toward poorer overall survival in breast cancer patients (Figure S4B). Importantly, high CD8+ T cell infiltration was significantly associated with better overall survival only in patients with low ERβ expression (Figure S4C), supporting a role for ERβ in suppressing anti-tumor immune surveillance.

In parallel, transcriptomic hallmark analysis indicated activation of the estrogen response pathway by DEHP in MCF-10A cells (Figure 3A, blue), suggesting that DEHP may act through estrogen receptors to regulate PD-L1 expression. Supporting this, DEHP induced estrogen response element-dependent transcriptional activity (Figure S4D) and upregulated both ERα and ERβ protein levels and activity in MCF-7 and T47D cells (Figure 3I). DEHP also increased both ERβ activity and PD-L1 expression in triple-negative MDA-MB-231 and 4T1 cells (Figure 3J). Silencing ERβ abolished DEHP-induced PD-L1 protein expression (Figure 3K), mRNA levels (Figure 3L), and promoter activity (Figure 3M). In contrast, silencing ERα only modestly reduced PD-L1 protein expression (Figure S4E), highlighting a predominant role for ERβ. Additionally, mutation of ERβ binding sites #2 and #3 on the PD-L1 promoter markedly reduced DEHP-induced promoter activation in both triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and luminal A cells (Figures 3N, 3O, and S4F). Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays further confirmed DEHP-enhanced ERβ binding to these promoter regions (Figure 3P). Collectively, these findings identify ERβ as a central transcriptional regulator mediating DEHP-induced PD-L1 expression in breast cancer cells. This mechanism likely contributes to impaired CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity across multiple breast cancer subtypes, underscoring ERβ′s key role in environmental endocrine disruptor-driven immune evasion.

PD-L1 and ERβ are upregulated by DEHP exposure in tumor tissues and correlate with impaired immune surveillance in EOBC

In vivo analysis using the 4T1 allograft mouse model revealed that DEHP exposure significantly increased PD-L1 expression in tumor cells (Figure 4A) but not in peripheral lymphocytes (Figure S5A), as determined by flow cytometry. Importantly, PD-L1 protein levels were significantly higher in tumor tissues from patients with DEHP-associated breast cancer, both in the total cohort (Figure S5B; Table S5) and in EOBC cases (Figure 4B) but not in non-EOBC patients (Figure 4C), regardless of the molecular subtype. A strong association was observed between elevated PD-L1 levels and higher DEHP content in hair, with a 13.07-fold increase in the odds of PD-L1 upregulation among highly exposed individuals (Table S6). Additionally, PD-L1 levels demonstrated a trend toward negative correlation with age at diagnosis (Figure S5C) and were inversely associated with the infiltration of CD3+, CD8+, and GZMB+ lymphocytes in tumor tissues (Figure 4D), suggesting immune suppression in DEHP-associated tumors. Consistent with these findings, nuclear ERβ levels were positively correlated with DEHP content in patient hair (Table S7) and were associated with a 3.57-fold increased risk of breast cancer (Table S8). Nuclear ERβ expression was significantly elevated in tumor tissues from DEHP-associated overall patients and EOBC patients (Figures 4E and 4F) but not in non-EOBC cases (Figure 4G). Nuclear ERβ levels were also positively correlated with PD-L1 expression (OR: 4.47, p = 0.006; Figure 4H; Table S9), and higher ERβ-expressing tumors exhibited reduced infiltration of CD3+, CD8+, and GZMB+ lymphocytes (Figure 4I). ERβ expression also showed a trend toward negative association with age at diagnosis (Figure S5D). In the 4T1 tumor model, DEHP exposure similarly induced nuclear ERβ expression (Figure 4J), which correlated with increased PD-L1 levels and decreased lymphocyte infiltration (Figure 4K), further supporting the immunosuppressive role of ERβ in vivo. To investigate whether blocking ERβ could reverse this immune suppression, we treated DEHP-exposed 4T1 tumor-bearing mice with fulvestrant, a selective estrogen receptor degrader approved by the Food and Drug Administration for HR+ breast cancer patients. Although no ERβ-specific inhibitors are currently available for clinical application, fulvestrant has been shown to antagonize ERβ activity in certain contexts. Remarkably, fulvestrant treatment in the DEHP-exposed 4T1 model not only significantly increased the expression of CD69 and CD8, indicating enhanced T cell activation and infiltration (Figure S5E), but also reduced PD-L1 and PD-1 expression in tumor cells (Figure S5F), suggesting a reversal of DEHP-induced immune evasion. DEHP induces PD-L1/PD-1-mediated T cell exhaustion and impairs cytotoxic function beyond checkpoint blockade

To investigate whether PD-L1 mediates DEHP-induced suppression of T cell activity, CD69 was assessed to serve as a sensitive indicator of T cell activation status and functional responsiveness.38 Flow cytometry revealed the gating strategy and activation profile of CD45+CD69+ T cells (Figure S6A) expanded from αCD3/interleukin (IL)-2-stimulated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs).39 Co-culture with MCF-10A or MCF-7 cells for 2 days reduced this activated T cell population, and DEHP exposure further exacerbated the reduction (Figure 5A). DEHP treatment increased the binding of recombinant PD-1 protein to MCF-10A cells, indicating elevated PD-L1 expression (Figure 5B). To evaluate the functional impact of DEHP-induced PD-L1, DEHP-treated MCF-10A, MCF-7, and MDA-MB-231 cells were co-cultured with αCD3/IL-2-activated PBMCs in the presence or absence of the anti-PD-L1 antibody. PD-L1 blockade significantly restored the CD45+CD69+ T cell population suppressed by DEHP exposure (Figure S6B). In addition, DEHP increased PD-1 expression in PBMCs, both with (Figure S6C) and without (Figure S6D) co-culture with cancer cells, indicating direct induction of PD-1 by DEHP. Treatment with the anti-PD-1 antibody partially rescued the CD45+CD69+ PBMC population from DEHP-induced suppression when co-cultured with MCF-10A and cancer cells (Figure 5C). To determine the functional relevance of these findings, we performed an in vitro killing assay using the OT-I/E0771-OVA system. DEHP significantly impaired OT-I T cell-mediated killing of E0771-OVA cancer cells at both effector-to-target ratios of 0.5:1 and 1:1 (Figure 5D). Importantly, treatment with the anti-PD-1 antibody restored the cytotoxic activity of DEHP-exposed OT-I T cells (Figure 5E). Together, these results demonstrate that DEHP suppresses T cell cytotoxic function by upregulating PD-L1 in epithelial and cancer cells and inducing PD-1 in T cells. Targeting CDK4 rescued the activation of T cells in response to DEHP exposure

Using L1000FWD analysis,40 exemestane, mitomycin-C, and palbociclib were identified as the top three clinically available drugs for breast cancer that reversed the DEHP-altered transcriptomic profile (Figure 6A). Among these compounds, palbociclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor, is the only drug associated with immune-cytotoxic responses.41 We found that DEHP treatment significantly upregulated CDK4 mRNA (Figure 6B) and protein levels (Figure 6C) in both MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. While CCND1 was also induced by DEHP (Figure S7A), CDK6 expression remained unchanged (Figure S7B). CDK4/6 inhibitors, palbociclib (Figure 6D), abemaciclib, and ribociclib (Figure S7C), effectively suppressed the colony-forming ability of DEHP-exposed MCF-7 cells. ERβ, previously shown to repress cell cycle progression via CCND1 downregulation,42,43 was suppressed upon CDK4/6 inhibition with palbociclib, alongside reduced PD-L1 expression in DEHP-treated MCF-7 cells (Figure 6E). These results suggest that DEHP may activate a CCND1/CDK4 axis to drive proliferation and simultaneously induce a CDK4/ERβ/PD-L1 axis contributing to immune evasion. Interestingly, co-treatment with the ERβ antagonist PHTPP further amplified DEHP-induced CCND1 expression (Figure S7B), implying that ERβ may exert context-dependent negative feedback on cyclin D1, which is disrupted by DEHP. Beyond tumor-intrinsic effects, DEHP also increased CDK4, ERβ, and PD-1 expression in PBMCs, which was repressed by palbociclib, while STAT3 remained unaffected (Figure 6F). Flow cytometry confirmed that the PD-1+ T cell population was elevated by DEHP and reduced by palbociclib (Figure 6G). Conversely, the suppressed population of CD45+CD69+ T cells was restored by palbociclib treatment (Figure 6H). Consistently, in the OT-I/E0771-OVA in vitro killing assay, DEHP significantly impaired T cell-mediated tumor cell cytotoxicity. However, treatment with palbociclib alone or in combination with an anti-PD-1 antibody effectively restored T cell-killing activity (Figure 6I). Furthermore, the expression of the effector cytokine GZMB was upregulated by the combination treatment, indicating enhanced cytotoxic function (Figure S7D). Similar rescue effects were observed in co-culture systems with cancer cells (Figures 6J and S7E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DEHP induces immune evasion via a CDK4/ERβ/PD-L1 signaling axis, contributing to T cell exhaustion and suppressed cytotoxicity. Inhibition of CDK4/6 not only blocks this oncogenic loop but also restores T cell activation and function. Co-treatments with CDK4/6 inhibitors enhanced the anti-tumor activity and reduced the irAEs of the anti-PD-1 antibody

We next examined the therapeutic effects of co-treatment with an anti-PD-1 antibody and palbociclib on DEHP-enhanced tumor progression. The combination treatment showed a better suppressive effect on DEHP-elevated tumor growth (Figure 7A) and tumor weight (Figure 7B) of 4T1 tumors in BALB/c mice than the treatment alone without affecting the body weight (Figure S8A). Co-treatment with palbociclib not only diminished tumor growth in four mice but also eradicated the tumor in one mouse (Figure 7B). Furthermore, DEHP-elicited suppression of CD8+ and CD45+CD69+ T cells, downregulation of GZMB and TNF-α expression, and induction of CDK4, nuclear ERβ, and PD-L1 expression in the tumor microenvironment were reversed by the combination treatment in IHC staining (Figure 7C) and flow cytometry analysis (Figure S8B), indicating that co-targeting CDK4 and PD-1 enhances the reactivation of CD8+ T cells to combat cancer cells. To further validate the therapeutic efficacy of this strategy in a different immunocompetent background, we employed the E0771 murine breast cancer model in C57BL/6 mice. Similar to the BALB/c/4T1 model, DEHP exposure promoted tumor growth (Figures S9A and S9B) and suppressed T cell activity (Figures S9C–S9E) in this model as well. Combination treatment with palbociclib and the anti-PD-1 antibody significantly inhibited tumor progression (Figures S9A and S9B), increased TILs, and restored cytotoxic T cell activity (Figures S9C–S9E). This was accompanied by reduced expression of PD-L1, ERβ, and CDK4 as shown by both flow cytometry (Figure S9C) and IHC (Figures S9D and S9E). Fatal hypersensitivity reactions caused by anti-PD-1 antibodies have been reported to lead to mortality in a murine model of breast cancer.44 In the BALB/c/4T1 model, treatment with the anti-PD-1 antibody alone resulted in mortality at the fifth dose on day 28 (Figures 7B and 7D), accompanied by severe tail skin rashes in all mice (Figure 7E) and markedly elevated serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase, key indicators of hepatocellular toxicity (Figure S10A). The frequency of grade 3/4 liver damage was reported as 1.1%–1.8% during treatment with anti-PD-1 monotherapies.45 Co-treatment with palbociclib not only showed better anti-cancer activity but also prevented anti-PD-1 antibody-induced skin rash (Figure 7E) without alterations in the blood biochemical tests (Figure S10A). Additionally, the serum level of granulocyte colony stimulating factor (G-CSF), a key cytokine regulator of neutrophil production,46 was elevated by anti-PD-1 antibody treatment but downregulated upon co-treatment with palbociclib in cytokine array analysis (Figures 7F and S10B). The inhibitory effect of palbociclib on anti-PD-1-induced serum G-CSF levels was further confirmed by ELISA (Figure 7G). Importantly, an increased neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio is associated with a higher incidence of liver injury in cancer patients treated with ICIs.47 The peripheral population of CD11b+Ly-6G+ neutrophils was increased by DEHP exposure and further enhanced by monotherapy with an anti-PD-1 antibody. Co-treatment with palbociclib reduced the neutrophil population to basal levels (Figure 7H). These findings suggest that combination therapy with palbociclib not only enhances the anti-cancer activity of anti-PD-1 antibodies but also mitigates anti-PD-1 antibody-induced irAEs through the modulation of G-CSF and neutrophil levels.

In conclusion, our results indicate that the plasticizer DEHP contributes to the early onset of breast cancer through the upregulation of the CDK4/ERβ/PD-L1 regulatory axis in cancer cells and the CDK4/PD-1 pathway in T cells to reduce immune surveillance. Monotherapy with the anti-PD-1 antibody partially rescued T cell exhaustion and reduced tumor growth but elicited irAEs and liver injury in DEHP-exposed 4T1 allograft mice. Co-targeting CDK4/6 with palbociclib potentiates anti-tumor activity and also reduces the irAEs of the anti-PD-1 antibody, suggesting a favorable therapeutic strategy for patients with plasticizer-associated EOBC.

Discussion

Suffering from EOBC significantly affects the lives of young women, who may face the challenges of fertility, pregnancy, economics, and other aspects.48 The incidence of EOBC varies worldwide depending on a combination of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors.1 The early detection and appropriate treatment of EOBC are crucial for the long-term health and well-being of these patients. Higher PD-L1 expression is associated with poor prognosis in both early-onset and pregnancy-related breast cancers.49 In this study, exposure to DEHP was further demonstrated to be a key factor in accelerating tumor incidence in mouse models in association with the induction of both PD-L1 and PD-1 and the reduction of TILs in EOBC patients, indicating that plasticizers enhance the early onset of breast cancers via immune dysregulation.

High PD-L1 expression and TILs are associated with better complete response to ICIs in patients with breast cancer.50 Although aging-related diminishment of interferon signaling is associated with poorer therapeutic efficacy of ICIs in TNBC in mice,51 younger patients, using the 50-year-old threshold, in a cohort of 1,661 patients with various cancers52 and lung cancer53 had shorter overall survival and poor efficacy of immunotherapy compared to elderly patients. ICI-based regimens also show poor efficacy in young patients with gastrointestinal cancers.54 The survival rate of patients with EOBC, but not elderly patients, is strongly correlated with the levels of TILs.14 These findings suggest that the level of TILs affected by other factors is more critical for the overall survival and therapeutic responses to ICIs in young patients than in elderly patients. Tumors with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations exhibit enhanced rather than defective TILs,55 excluding the involvement of BRCA mutations in the suppression of immune surveillance in EOBC. Phthalates have been implicated as immune modulators, potentially contributing to the development of allergic symptoms,56 and showed more obvious effects in juveniles than in adult animals.57 Our results demonstrated that administration of DEHP reduced TILs and increased PD-L1 and PD-1 expression, which may contribute to the early onset and partial response of TNBC tumors to the anti-PD-1 antibody in mice. The association between DEHP exposure and reduced TILs (Figure 1), along with elevated PD-L1 expression (Figure 4) predominantly observed in EOBC, further supports the notion that plasticizer-induced immune dysregulation, characterized by PD-L1 and PD-1 upregulation, not only accelerates breast tumorigenesis but may also contribute to the diminished efficacy of ICIs.

When stratifying by breast cancer subtype, the reduction of CD8+ and the induction of ERβ were associated with DEHP exposure in all subtypes though the limited numbers of HER2+ and TNBC patients in this study. This broader immunosuppressive effect could be due to the involvement of ERβ, which is expressed in all subtypes not limited to ER+ tumors, in DEHP-induced PD-L1 expression. Although elevation of ERβ mRNA expression has also been linked to poorer disease-free survival, lymph node metastasis, and recurrence,58 the role of ERβ in regulating cancer cell proliferation varies across different cancer types and contexts and remains controversial.59 Beyond its role in promoting PD-L1 expression, our data also revealed an anti-proliferative function of ERβ in DEHP-treated cells, as evidenced by the enhanced cyclin D expression by the ERβ inhibitor (Figure S7B). In addition, ERβ plays an essential role in the activation and augmentation of TCR signaling to boost anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.60 These observations suggest that targeting ERβ is not an ideal strategy for plasticizer-associated breast cancer.

Our data further discovered that targeting CDK4/6 reversed the DEHP-elicited transcriptomic profile and cancer cell growth (Figure 6). The crosstalk between estrogen-independent estrogen receptor signaling and CDK461 may account for the growth of DEHP-exposed cancer cells. Interestingly, although palbociclib is classically known to inhibit CDK4/6 kinase activity, our data revealed that palbociclib also repressed DEHP-induced CDK4 protein levels (Figures 6E and 6F), which was consistent with the previous report,62 and pointed to a potential additional mechanism of drug action beyond kinase inhibition. More importantly, targeting CDK4/6 also repressed the DEHP-induced PD-L1, PD-1, ERβ, and cyclin D1 expression (Figures 6E and 6F), placing CDK4 as an upstream regulator of ERβ/cyclin D1 and ERβ/PD-L1 signaling axes. These findings suggest CDK4 as a more ideal therapeutic target for DEHP-associated breast cancer than ERβ.

In addition to cell-cycle arrest in cancer cells, CDK4/6 inhibitors also promote anti-tumor immunity by suppressing the proliferation of regulatory T cells,63 downregulating PD-L1 expression,64 and inducing the DNA damage-mediated STING/IFN axis,65 supporting the enhancing effect of CDK4/6 inhibitors on the efficacy of immunotherapies in colorectal cancer. Our results further demonstrated the rescue effect of CDK4/6 inhibitors on T cell activity in DEHP-treated tumors. The protection of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells by CDK4/6 inhibitors from chemotherapy-induced exhaustion66 also explains the augmentation and extension of the duration of the anti-tumor response of the combination of chemotherapy/ICI regimen with CDK4/6 inhibitors in small-cell lung cancer.67 These findings provide a rationale for the development of combination regimens comprising CDK4/6 inhibitors, chemotherapy, and immunotherapies for patients with plasticizer-associated breast cancer in future clinical trials.

Although ICIs have led to significant advancements in patient outcomes across various malignancies, their clinical efficacy is often hindered by the occurrence of irAEs.68 Of particular concern are hypersensitivity reactions triggered by anti-PD-1 antibody therapy, which have been associated with cytokine storms and the release of pro-inflammatory chemokines, culminating in the recruitment of neutrophils to inflammatory sites.69 Interestingly, the irAEs were found in the BALB/c/4T1 but not in the C57BL/6/E0771 models. The Th2-skewed immune profile and enhanced humoral responses in BALB/c mice, compared to the Th1-dominant and cellular immunity-oriented phenotype of C57BL/6 mice,70 may underlie the strain-specific susceptibility to irAEs. Aligning with the role of G-CSF in stimulating the production of neutrophils46 and liver injury through autoimmune hepatitis,71 our findings demonstrated a significant increase in peripheral G-CSF and neutrophil levels and hepatocytic toxicity following anti-PD-1 antibody treatment in the BALB/c/4T1 model, supporting the link between anti-PD-1 antibody treatment and liver damage in patients with various cancer types.72 Interestingly, CDK4/6 inhibitors reduce G-CSF and neutrophils, similar to the side effects of neutropenia,73 and may thereby prevent anti-PD-1 antibody-induced liver injury. However, elucidation of the molecular mechanism underlying the downregulation of G-CSF by CDK4/6 inhibitors requires further investigation.

In conclusion, we reveal a critical role for DEHP-induced CDK4/ERβ/PD-L1 signaling in impairing T cell-mediated immune surveillance, contributing to early breast cancer onset. Our preclinical findings provide a mechanistic basis for targeting CDK4/6 in combination with PD-1 blockade, offering a promising therapeutic strategy for patients with plasticizer-associated breast cancers while minimizing the risk of irAEs. While this study focuses on EOBC, our results showed that DEHP-associated immune suppression may not be restricted to age-defined subgroups. Given that CDK4/6 inhibitors are standard therapies for advanced HR+ breast cancer, our findings underscore the need for further investigation into their broader clinical application in modulating cancer immunity and the therapeutic response of plasticizer-associated breast cancer.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, the clinical analysis was based on a relatively small cohort, particularly within the HER2+ and TNBC subtypes, limiting the statistical power to detect subtype-specific associations with DEHP exposure and immune infiltration. Larger, more diverse patient cohorts will be needed to confirm these findings and to establish robust correlations across all breast cancer subtypes. Second, while ERβ was identified as a key mediator of DEHP-induced PD-L1 expression, current clinical datasets primarily assess ERα status, which may overlook the contribution of ERβ signaling in immune regulation. Third, although our in vivo findings were supported by two independent murine models (BALB/c and C57BL/6 with 4T1 and E0771 tumors, respectively), the translational relevance to human EOBC patients remains to be validated in larger preclinical or clinical settings. Fourth, while we demonstrated that DEHP upregulates CCND1 and CDK4, the precise molecular mechanisms linking ERβ to the CDK4/6 pathway require further investigation, including possible transcriptional and post-translational regulatory mechanisms. Finally, although combination therapy with CDK4/6 inhibition and PD-1 blockade showed promising results in reversing DEHP-induced immunosuppression and tumor progression, the long-term efficacy and potential toxicities, particularly in the context of environmental exposure, warrant more comprehensive evaluation.

Experimental model and study participant details

Clinical specimens

A total of 70 residual breast tumor tissue specimens were collected with written informed consent from patients with different breast cancer subtypes who underwent surgery at Chung Shan Medical University Hospital (Taichung, Taiwan) following the protocol (CS1-22107) approved by the Institute of Research Board Committee. The patient inclusion criteria were as follows: diagnosis between 2018 and 2019 at a single tertiary care institution, histologically confirmed invasive ductal carcinoma breast cancer, randomly collected and non-selected samples from each molecular subtype, and tumor grade. Correlations among multiple variables, including gender, molecular subtypes, tumor grade, and tumor type, with the protein expression were analyzed. G∗Power software (RRID: SCR_013726) was utilized for power analysis, employing an error probability of 0.05 and a power level of 0.8 to determine the sample size for data collection.

Allograft tumor model and FACs analyses of peripheral blood and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

All animals in this study were approved by the IACUC of China Medical University (CMUIACUC-2022-051-2) in accordance with the NIH guidelines. MMTV-HER2/neu transgenic mice (FVB/N-Tg(MMTVneu)202Mul/J, RRID: IMSR_JAX:002376) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. 4T1 cells (1×104) were subcutaneously injected into the fat pad of Balb/c female mice (6–8 weeks old), and were treated with DEHP 1.73 mg/kg every day when the tumor was palpable followed. Tumor volume was measured every 3–4 days. For the C57BL/6 model, E0771 cells (1×105) were orthotopically injected into the mammary fat pad of 6- to 8-week-old female C57BL/6 mice. Mice were treated with DEHP (1.73 mg/kg/day) starting when tumors were palpable. For drug treatment, mice were intraperitoneally injected with anti-PD-1 antibody (RMP1-14, BioLegend) at a dose of 200 μg per mouse every four days, along with control PBS injections. Additionally, mice received oral treatment with the CDK4/6 inhibitor palbociclib at a dose of 100 mg/kg, administered for 3 days, followed by 4 days off, until reaching the experimental endpoint. Fulvestrant was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 5 mg per mouse every three days. For FACs, peripheral blood and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were collected after sacrifice with CO2. The tumors were digested, weighed, and minced into cell clumps at the bottom of the syringe. Cell clumps were removed through a 70 μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions followed by centrifugation, and the cell pellets were washed twice with PBS. Cells were stained in incubation buffer (containing 0.5% BSA) for 30 min, washed, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Method details

Immunohistochemical staining (IHC)

Tumor tissues were dewaxed with xylene and rehydrated with 100%, 95%, 80%, and, 75% ethanol, followed by incubation with antibodies overnight and a polymer HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 10 min according to the protocol of the UltraVision Quanto detection system (TL-060-QHD; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The tissue slides were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min and dehydrated. The staining was visualized and quantitated in the TissueFAXS system (TissueGnostics, Los Angeles, CA, USA). The primary antibodies used for IHC were anti-CD8 (Proteintech, #66868; 1:20000), anti-GZMB (Abcam; #ab4059; 1:100), anti-CD3 (Abcam; #ab16669; 1:100), anti-PD-L1 (Abcam; # ab205921; 1:100), anti-CDK4 (Antibodies, #A35439; 1:100), and anti-ERβ (Invitrogen; #PA1-310B; 1:200).

Hair DEHP extraction

Two separate bundles of hair from the patients were collected and cleaned with ddH2O, dried, cut into small pieces, and weighed. DEHP was extracted by incubating the cut hair (25 mg/patient) with 1 mL of methanol/TFA (8.5:1.5) in glass bottles overnight and was dried in a chemical hood, followed by dissolution in 30% methanol and quantitation in a liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system 28.

Cell lines and cell culture

Human breast epithelial cells MCF-10A (RRID: CVCL_0598), breast cancer cell lines MCF-7 (RRID: CVCL_0031) and MDA-MB-231 (RRID: CVCL_0062), and human embryonic kidney cell lines (HEK-293T, CVCL_0063) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/F12 (DME/F-12) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (GeneDriex), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (P/S) (Thermo) and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. Mouse mammary carcinoma cell lines 4T1 (RRID: CVCL_0125) and E0771 (RRID: CVCL_GR23) were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium and DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. All cells were cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC).

RNA sequencing and dataset analysis

Total RNA extracted from either parental or DEHP-treated MCF-10A cell lines was processed using TRIzol reagent. Subsequently, library generation was performed using the KAPA mRNA HyperPrep Kit (KAPA Biosystems, Roche, Basel, Switzerland), following the manufacturer’s instructions, with the inclusion of index codes for sample attribution. To ensure sequencing quality, the libraries were validated using a Qubit@ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Finally, library sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform, producing 150 bp paired-end reads (dataset available at GEO, GSE307413).

The transcription factor-binding elements within the PD-L1 promoter (1k) were predicted using the JASPAR database (RRID: SCR_003030). The association between the transcription factor and PD-L1, as well as survival rates, in TCGA database was assessed using TIMER2.0 (http://timer.cistrome.org.).

Immunostaining

MCF-10A breast epithelial cells cultured on gelatin-coated cover slides in 24-well plates were treated with DEHP (0, 1, and 10 μM), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and blocked with 1%BSA (TBS, with or without 0.1% Triton X-100) for immunostaining with an anti-PD-L1 antibody (Novus; #NBP2:15791) and secondary rabbit antibody.

Reverse-transcription and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

After total RNA extraction, reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction was performed. First-strand cDNA synthesis was performed with 1 μg RNA, oligo-dT, dNTP, and distilled DEPC H2O at 65°C for 5 min, followed by incubation with 5X First-Strand Buffer, DTT, M-MLV RT enzyme at 37°C for 50 min, and 70°C for 15 min. The cDNA products were amplified by quantitative real-time PCR (q-PCR) using cDNA, forward primer, reverse primer, and SYBR Green Master Mix (KAPA).

Transfection

Cells were transfected with plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) or TransIT- X2 (Mirus) transfection reagent, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, cell lysates were harvested and subjected to analysis.

Luciferase reporter assay

The cells were seeded at a density of 2×104 cells/well in a 12-well plate. PD-L1 promoter-luciferase and β-galactosidase plasmids were co-transfected into cells for 24 h. After 24 h of transfection, luciferase activity was measured using the Secrete-PairTM Gaussia Luciferase Assay Kit (GeneCopoeiaTM), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. β-galactosidase activity was used as an internal control to normalize transfection efficiency.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were washed with PBS, trypsinized, and suspended cells were incubated with control IgG (PE-mouse IgG) or PE-anti-human CD274 for 1 h at RT. The labeled cells were subjected to a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

shRNA knockdown assay

Lentiviruses containing shRNA were used to knock down the target genes. Cancer cells at a density of 2 × 105–3×105 cells/well were infected with lentivirus at an MOI of 250–500 with 1 mg/mL polybrene. After 16–18 h of infection, 2 μg/mL of puromycin was added for selection, followed by treatment with DEHP for 24 h. The validated shRNAs for the negative control (pVoid), ERα, and ERβ were purchased from the National RNAi Core Facility at Academia Sinica.

PBMCs cells isolation and co-culture with breast epithelial or cancer cell lines

PBMCs were isolated from whole human blood samples using Ficoll-Paque PLUSTM (GE Healthcare) and activated by treatment with an anti-CD3 antibody and IL-12. MCF-10A, MCF-7, or MDA-MB-231 cells were pretreated with DEHP 10 μM for 24 h and subsequently cocultured with activated PBMCs at a cancer cell to PBMC ratio of 2:1 for 48 h. To block PD-L1/PD-1, 10 μg/mL of anti-PD-L1 or anti-PD-1 antibody was added to the coculture medium for 48 h.

OT-I CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity assay

Naive CD8+ T cells were isolated from spleens of OT-I transgenic mice using a negative selection CD8+ T cell isolation kit (e.g., Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Isolated T cells were activated by culturing with Dynabeads Mouse T-Activator CD3/CD28 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio in the presence of recombinant mouse IL-2 (20 U/mL) for 3 days. On Day 3, magnetic beads were removed, and cells were split and rested in fresh IL-2-containing media for an additional 3 days to allow for recovery and expansion. On Day 6, viable T cells were enriched by density gradient centrifugation using Lymphoprep (STEMCELL Technologies) to remove dead cells prior to use in cytotoxicity assays. For target cell preparation, EO771 and EO771-OVA cells were differentially labeled with CellTrace CFSE or CellTrace Violet (CTV) dyes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 1 × 105 cells of each line were seeded into 24-well plates and allowed to adhere for 2 h. Subsequently, CD8+ OT-I T cells were added to the wells at the desired effector-to-target (E:T) ratio and co-cultured for 24 h. After co-culture, cells were harvested and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess specific lysis of target cells, determined by the differential loss of CFSE- or CTV-labeled tumor cells.

PD-L1/PD-1 binding assay

To measure the interaction between PD-1 and PD-L1 proteins, cells were fixed and incubated with PD-1 Fc protein, followed by Alexa Fluor 488 secondary antibody staining at RT for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342. The fluorescence intensity of Alexa Fluor 488 was measured by flow cytometry.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

After DEHP treatment, the cells were harvested and processed for the ChIP assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions (HighCell# ChIP Kit). Chromatin fragmentation was performed using a Bioruptor chromatin shearing sonicator. Subsequently, the binding sites for the transcription factors were analyzed by quantitative real-time PCR using specific primer sets. The primer sequences (5′ to 3′) for the PD-L1 promoter were as follows: F: ACTGAAAGCTTCCGCCGATT; R: CCCAAGGCAGCAAATCCAGT for ESR2#1+RELA (−67 to −149), F: AAATGTGGATTTGCTTTAATCTTCG; R: AGTGAAACATGTCAGTCCAGT for STAT3#1 (−202 to −282), F: TGAGGAAGTCACAGAATCCACG; R: CCCATATGGCTTTGGTTTTTATTCT for ESR2#2+STAT3#2 (−490 to −593), and F: ACACAGGCACGGTGGCTCAA; R’: TCCCGCGCTGAACTTCTAGGTG for ESR2#3 (−821 to −921).

Statistical analysis

The analysis of differences between two categorical variables was performed using either Student’s t test or Welch’s two-sample t-test using the two-tailed test, while differences among more than two categorical variables were assessed using one-way ANOVA. Pearson’s chi-square test was used to analyze the characteristics of breast cancer patients, while unconditional logistic regression was used to calculate the odds ratio. The results, with a sample size (n) greater than or equal to 3, are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (RRID: SCR_002798) or SPSS 21 software (RRID: SCR_002865). Statistical significance was set at ∗p ≤ 0.05; ∗∗p ≤ 0.01; ∗∗∗p ≤ 0.001; ∗∗∗∗p ≤ 0.0001.
