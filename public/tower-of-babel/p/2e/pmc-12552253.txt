Elucidating zerumbone’s low-efficacy agonism at the μ-opioid receptor via molecular dynamics simulation and Markov state modeling
Authors: Wan Mardhiyana Wan Ayub, Nurul Amirah Marjohan, Mohamed Haneif Khalid, Enoch Kumar Perimal, Muhamad Arif Mohamad Jamali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractZerumbone is a natural sesquiterpene compound from Zingiber zerumbet plant. While it significantly exhibits analgesic properties through the μ-opioid receptor (μOR) found in animal models, its precise molecular mechanism at the receptor level remains poorly investigated. The present work involves 1-µs molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, MM/PBSA binding-free energy analyses, principal component analysis (PCA) as well as Markov state modeling (MSM) to address how the dynamic basis of zerumbone-μOR interactions compared to morphine, which is a known full agonist. MD trajectories reported greater receptor backbone fluctuations, improved loop mobility, reduced stable hydrogen bonds, and moderate receptor compaction in the zerumbone-bound state in contrast to morphine. MM/PBSA calculations indicated similar total binding affinity and the driven for zerumbone affinity was primarily hydrophobic interaction. PCA recognized notable intermediate conformational substates that were stabilized by zerumbone. In the interim, highly stabilized intermediate-activation macrostate with high-kinetic barriers (~ 8–16 k_BT) and millisecond-scale residency was also revealed through MSM analysis. In agreement with analgesic activities reported previously, these computational insights identify zerumbone as a low-efficacy partial agonist, providing comprehensive molecular explanation to its analgesic profile to serve as a source of safer and more opioid-like drugs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10822-025-00677-2.Keywords: μ-opioid receptor (μOR), Analgesia, Docking simulations, Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, MMPBSA analysis, Safer opioid alternatives

Introduction

According to the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), pain is defined as “an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience that correlates with or resembles in which correlates with actual or potential tissue damage where in cases when this process exceeds three months, it is considered to be chronic [11, 27]. Recent meta-analyses estimate that around 20% of adults across the world live with chronic pain, and this record continues to rise with ageing populations and multimorbidity [23]. This has eventually led to the increment in opioid prescribing which has also raised concerns about the unwanted effects of the drugs. Opioids exert its analgesic activity or pain-relieving effect primarily through the μ-opioid receptor (μOR), a class A G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR). Basically, this receptor modulates neuronal signaling through the inhibition of adenylate cyclase and downstream reduction of cAMP levels [26]. Nevertheless, conventional μOR agonists including morphine, fentanyl, and oxycodone, cause severe adverse effects such as respiratory depression, tolerance, addiction, and gastrointestinal dysfunction. The long-term use and safety profiles of these functional organs will be limited and therefore, the urgent need to discover novel opioid receptor modulators with safer pharmacological profiles and a distinct receptor engagement mechanism should not be neglected [22].

There are natural products, particularly phytochemicals that are derived from medicinal plants which have historically provided structural templates and pharmacophores invaluable for analgesic drug discovery [15]. Among these is zerumbone, a monocyclic sesquiterpene which was extracted from the rhizome of Zingiber zerumbet (L.) Smith. Zerumbone has received a lot of interest due to its extensive bioactivities including anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and analgesic effects [7]. It is revealed through preclinical models that zerumbone demonstrated significant antinociceptive activity mediated through opioid receptors, which was proven by complete blockade of zerumbone analgesic effects by both the non-selective opioid antagonist naloxone and the μ-selective antagonist β-funaltrexamine [7]. Although it is known that zerumbone requires an intact opioid system for analgesia and could be antagonized by μ-selective compounds, its efficacy class (full, partial, or biased agonist) has not been tested at a molecular level hence this study was initiated.

The most recent computational method such as the long-timescale molecular dynamics (MD) computational simulation, complemented with the calculations of Molecular Mechanics/Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MM/PBSA) have also played an important role in our understanding on the interaction between GPCR and ligand [5]. Computational methods offer atomic-level information on conformational shifts induced by ligand binding, binding affinities and receptor activation dynamics [8]. A different computational methodology, the Markov state modeling (MSM) has also become a highly effective technique to systematically explore receptor dynamics, construct detailed free-energy landscapes, and quantify kinetic barriers between receptor conformational states [20, 24]. Therefore, the integration of MD, MM/PBSA, and MSM methods provides a solid framework to elucidate ligand efficacy, receptor activation states, and kinetics of conformational transitions and this method can be especially useful in characterizing partial or biased agonists which preferentially stabilize intermediate receptor conformations [28].

Therefore, in the present work, we use all-atom MD simulations, MM/PBSA free-energy analyses, principal component analysis (PCA) and MSM, in direct comparison to a morphine-bound control derived from a recent μOR structure (PDB ID: 8EF6) to comprehensively investigate the binding interactions and activation mechanisms of zerumbone at the μOR. We found that zerumbone binds favorably to different intermediate receptor conformations, that is, increased conformational flexibility, moderate global compaction, fewer hydrogen bonds, and significant kinetic trapping in partially active conformations. These computational results are useful in augmenting the existing pharmacological data and provide a detailed mechanistic basis for zerumbone’s analgesic activities hence paving the way toward the rational design of safer painkiller targeting the μOR (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Molecular docking simulations

The μ-opioid receptor (μOR) structure was obtained from PDB ID 8EF6, which includes the receptor, the Gi heterotrimer, and an scFv16 antibody fragment. For docking and MD simulations, only the receptor chain (chain A) was retained, while all non-receptor components (Gi proteins, scFv16, crystallographic ligands, and water molecules) were removed. Missing side chains and short unresolved loops were automatically modeled and minimized using CHARMM-GUI, ensuring a complete and stable receptor structure for subsequent simulations.

The state of protonation of residues at physiological pH (7.4) were determined using PROPKA calculation [16, 25]. Meanwhile, zerumbone was prepared, energy-reduced and characterized using suitable parameters for the CHARMM36m force field using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF) [27]. After that, the docking of Zerumbone into the μOR binding pocket was conducted using AutoDock Vina version 1.2 [28]. Subsequently the receptor and ligand structures were converted to PDBQT mode and the docking was focused on the orthosteric site. Ligand topology and parameters were generated using the CHARMM General Force Field (CGenFF) under CHARMM36m.

Molecular docking was performed with AutoDock Vina v1.2 [28]. The docking grid was centered on the co-crystallized morphine binding site with coordinates x = 138.45, y = 117.10, z = 132.76 and box dimensions of 49.90 × 60.56 × 62.88 Å, with a grid spacing of 1 Å. The docking parameters included exhaustiveness = 8, modes = 9, and energy range = 3. For validation, morphine was re-docked into the μOR binding site under identical settings, reproducing the crystallographic pose with an RMSD of ~ 1.2 Å. Zerumbone docking poses were ranked by Vina scores, and the top-ranked pose was selected based on affinity and consistency with canonical μOR orthosteric contacts (Asp149^3.32, Tyr150^3.33, Trp295^6.48). The final docking orientation of zerumbone is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Molecular dynamics simulations

These simulations were carried out using CHARMM36m force field and GROMACS 2024 [1]. The complex between each receptor–ligand system was integrated into a POPC lipid bilayer and solvated in TIP3P water molecules via CHARMM-GUI Membrane Builder [12]. Systems were neutralized and ion levels were also adjusted to physiological levels (150 mM NaCl). POPC was chosen as a widely used simplified bilayer model for GPCR simulations, ensuring reproducibility and direct comparison between ligand systems. However, we acknowledge that neuronal membranes are enriched in cholesterol, sphingomyelin, and phosphatidylethanolamine, which have been shown to significantly influence μOR conformational equilibria and activation pathways [9]. These lipids were not included in the present model, which may underestimate certain lipid–protein allosteric effects, but future work will address this limitation by employing multi-component bilayers that more closely mimic the native neuronal environment. Protonation states were assigned at physiological pH (7.0–7.4): Asp/Glu were modeled deprotonated, Lys/Arg protonated, histidines neutral (His6.52 as HIE), morphine protonated at its tertiary amine (+ 1), and zerumbone neutral (enone tautomer), with zwitterionic termini, the conserved TM3–ECL2 disulfide retained, and no crystallographic waters or sodium ions explicitly included.

Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest-descent method and convergence (< 1000 kJ/mol/nm). Meanwhile, the equilibration was done on 100 ps in an NVT ensemble (303 K, V-rescale thermostat) followed by 10 ns NPT equilibration (303 K, 1 bar, Parrinello-Rahman barostat). Production simulations were run for 1 µs per system, to make use of periodic boundary conditions with a 2 fs timestep. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using particle mesh Ewald (PME) and van der Waals interactions used a cutoff of 1.2 nm. RMSD, RMSF, radius of Gyration and Hydrogen bonding structural analyses were run by built-in tools via Gromacs and the hydrogen bonds were geometrically defined with distance ≤ 3.5 Å and angle ≤ 30°. For each ligand–receptor complex, three independent 1 μs production runs were performed, starting from randomized initial velocities. Structural stability metrics (RMSD and Rg) were summarized as mean ± SD across replicas (n = 3), calculated over the last one-third of each trajectory.

MM/PBSA binding-free energy calculations

Binding free energies were estimated with gmx_MMPBSA v1.5.0 [29]. Snapshots were uniformly sampled across each 1000-ns trajectory, yielding 65 frames (≈ 15.4-ns spacing) for the MM/PBSA calculations. We used a solute dielectric of 1, solvent dielectric of 80, ionic strength 0.15 M NaCl, probe radius 1.4 Å, and radiopt = 0 to take radii from the CHARMM topology; the nonpolar term was treated via a SASA model. Although simulations were performed in an explicit POPC bilayer, the PB step used a continuum (implicit) dielectric representation for the membrane environment, consistent with typical gmx_MMPBSA practice. Reported ΔG values were assessed for stability by inspecting the running average across snapshots.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

MD trajectory calculated covariance matrices of receptor backbone atom coordinates (Cα atoms) that were analyzed through principal component analysis (PCA). The MDTraj [14] and Matplotlib [10] were used to visualize PC1 and PC2 projections.

Markov state modeling (MSM)

The analysis of MSM was conducted to determine receptor dynamics and conformational changes. Time-lagged independent component analysis (tICA) was performed on backbone torsions with a lag of 5 frames to denote dimensionality. Conformations were clustered into 50 discrete microstates employing k-means clustering, with MSM being calculated using PyEMMA [21]. From the MSM transition matrices, implied timescales (ITS), free-energy landscapes, and mean first-passage times (MFPTs) were calculated. A lag time of 10 frames was selected based on ITS analysis, at which most timescales stabilized,although the slowest timescale of the morphine system did not fully plateau, this choice provided a balance between statistical reliability and model resolution. The calculation of macrostates was done by PCCA++, which identified three metastable basins, whereas transition pathways were quantified by MFPTs. Free energy was mapped on the tIC1/tIC2 planes and presented as landscapes weighted by Boltzmann factors. The structures of macrostate units retrieved on MSM were rendered using PyMOL (Schrödinger, LLC). Distance measurements that occurred between residues Tyr326 (NPxxY motif) and Asp148 (DRY motif) were under evaluation as an indication of conformational changes attributable to activation.

Results and discussion

Molecular dynamics reveal differential structural dynamics of μOR upon zerumbone and morphine binding

From extensive 1-µs MD simulations, it was found that there were significant differences in receptor conformational flexibility when bound to zerumbone compared to morphine (Fig. 2). Mean values of the backbone RMSD values for zerumbone-bound μOR were shifted by higher average fluctuations (~ 0.30 nm) than the morphine-bound receptor (~ 0.20 nm; Fig. 2A). High RMSD means that the protein is less stable in its conformations, which is often seen with the partial agonists, which do not stabilize a single, rigid active form [8].

Stability metrics of MD-derived for μOR bound to morphine and zerumbone over a 1 µs simulation, black dashed and purple solid respectively. A Morphine–μOR stabilizing at ~ 0.18–0.22 nm post-100 ns is shown in backbone RMSD, while zerumbone–μOR exhibits higher deviations (~ 0.28–0.32 nm) which indicates increased flexibility. B Morphine-induced fluctuations (~ 0.5 nm) mainly in loops are revealed through RMSF analysis, whereas zerumbone enhances flexibility (~ 0.75 nm) near residue 200 (TM5/ICL3), which reflects partial activation. C The radius of gyration for morphine remains stable (~ 2.12–2.14 nm) but shows gradual compaction (~ 2.11 to 2.07 nm) with zerumbone. D Persistent 1–2 H-bonds for morphine (occasional spikes to 3) determined through hydrogen bond analysis while zerumbone forms 0–1 transient H-bonds, consistent with reduced binding strength

Additional investigation by RMSF allowed better understanding of the dynamics of the receptors revealing that receptors in the zerumbone-bound system are particularly flexible around the segment of intracellular loop 3 (ICL3) and transmembrane helix 5 (TM5), experiencing maximum fluctuation of ~ 0.75 nm (Fig. 2B). Such receptor domains play an essential role in the coupling of G-proteins and arrestins, and their higher mobility indicates less and more immediate activating signals than morphine (smaller RMSF peaks of approximately 0.50 nm). Similar partial agonist behaviors, indicated by increased loop flexibility without full receptor activation, were observed previously in other GPCR modulators [28].

The radius profiles of gyration (Fig. 2C) exhibited a slight structural compaction of zerumbone-bound μOR during incubation time that decreased from ~ 2.11 nm to ~ 2.07 nm. On the other hand, the spacing of morphine-bound μOR maintained consistently (~ 2.13 nm). It was reported previously that partial agonists can induce subtle conformational rearrangements that are neither fully active nor non-active and that align with properties of partial efficacy and atypical receptor dynamics [29].

Hydrogen-bond analysis is graphed in Fig. 2D which further supported distinct interaction profiles. Most zerumbone–μOR complexes formed at best confined contacts (0–1 hydrogen bonds), when compared to morphine’s stable network (1–2 hydrogen bonds). The sustained interactions are important to the ligand-mediated stabilization of fully active GPCRs conformations, so the non-permanent nature of interactions of zerumbone is directly associated with its low-efficacy profile [2, 3].

To confirm the robustness of these observations, we performed three independent replicas for each ligand. Replica analyses yielded consistent RMSD and Rg values (Supplementary Fig. S2), and group statistics are reported in Supplementary Table S1. The RMSD of the μOR backbone stabilized after ~ 200 ns for morphine and showed higher fluctuations for zerumbone (Fig. 2). Similarly, the Rg of morphine-bound μOR was slightly more compact than in the zerumbone system. Across three independent replicas, morphine exhibited a lower RMSD (0.219 ± 0.015 nm) compared to zerumbone (0.292 ± 0.036 nm), while Rg values were similar (1.822 ± 0.008 vs. 1.807 ± 0.019 nm) (Supplementary Table S1).

Zerumbone and morphine display similar binding affinities but distinct interaction profiles

In the present work, MM/PBSA calculations were conducted to quantify the ligand affinities and are summarized in Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 4. The running averages of ΔG computed over 65 uniformly spaced snapshots reached stable plateaus for both systems (morphine and zerumbone), indicating converged MM/PBSA estimates (Supplementary Fig. S3). The means of binding-free energies (ΔG_bind) were similar in the case of zerumbone (–11.8 kJ/mol) as well as morphine (–10.8 kJ/mol). The interaction profiles were, however, different as indicated when the energy component was broken down. Hydrophobic (van der Waals) effects (–31.2 kJ/mol) contributed most to Zerumbones binding energy and a significant polar solvation penalty (ca. + 16.6 kJ/mol) was observed. The affinity of morphine was supported with a similar electrostatic interaction (13.8 kJ/mol) and hydrophobic one (–26.9 kJ/mol), superseded by an even stronger polar solvation cost (+ 33.1 kJ/mol). This agrees with the earlier reports emphasizing the stabilization of partially active GPCR conformations by hydrophobic ligands.

MM/PBSA binding‐free energy decomposition for Zerumbone versus Control (Morphine)

The ligand receptor interaction also contributes to the action of zerumbone as shown in Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 1. The π–stacking between the cyclopentadiene ring of zerumbone and Trp320, combined with polar interactions with Lys235 and Tyr150, binds zerumbone to the orthosteric pocket, reinforcing intermediate receptor states identified in partial agonists [28]. Analysis of the MM/PBSA energy decomposition revealed distinct contributions of hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions to morphine and zerumbone binding. For morphine, binding was dominated by strong electrostatic stabilization arising from the salt bridge between its protonated tertiary amine and the conserved Asp3.32, in agreement with the established pharmacophore model of μOR agonists. Additional hydrogen-bonding and polar interactions with residues in transmembrane helices 3 and 6 further stabilized morphine within the orthosteric pocket. By contrast, zerumbone, which lacks a protonatable amine, relied predominantly on van der Waals and hydrophobic contacts with residues such as Ile, Val, Leu, and Phe in the transmembrane bundle. This difference is consistent with prior structure–activity relationship studies indicating that nitrogen-free ligands engage the μOR primarily via hydrophobic packing rather than canonical electrostatic interactions.

B Polar contacts with Lys235^5.39: The carbonyl oxygen of zerumbone forms two long‐range polar interactions to the ε-amino group of Lys235^5.39 at 4.98 Å and 4.67 Å (dashed lines), anchoring the ligand’s enone moiety near TM5. From a functional perspective, these divergent interaction profiles suggest distinct receptor conformational outcomes. Morphine, through its classical Asp3.32 salt bridge, favors stabilization of fully active receptor states associated with robust G protein signaling. In contrast, zerumbone’s weaker electrostatic engagement and stronger reliance on hydrophobic contacts may preferentially stabilize intermediate conformations, a property observed in other biased or partial agonists. Such intermediate states have been linked to reduced β-arrestin recruitment while maintaining G protein activation, a signaling profile associated with improved therapeutic safety in μOR pharmacology. Although experimental validation will be required, these computational findings raise the possibility that zerumbone could act as a functionally selective μOR modulator with reduced arrestin-mediated side effects.

MSM analysis reveals zerumbone-induced intermediate-state stabilization with unique kinetics

Possible limited sampling of intermediately located receptor conformations was observed and indicated by principal component analysis (PCA) of multiple loading of zerumbone-bound μOR as in Fig. 4. This restricted ability to change conformations is reconciled with previous observations that partial agonists stabilize receptor substates instead of fully active ones [29].

Representation of conformational sampling of μOR–zerumbone over a 1 µs trajectory via principal component analysis. Following the first two principal components PC1 and PC2, it reveals three well-separated clusters of receptor conformations stabilized by zerumbone (purple circles)

MSM analyses were done in order to describe conformational transitions and kinetics accurately. MSM-derived free-energy surfaces disclosed striking distinctions between morphine and zerumbone-bound μOR conformational landscapes as shown in Fig. 5. The morphine-bound receptor assumed a low-energy conformation that is a global-minimum active-like state, separated by no more than (~ 1–2 kBT). Furthermore, rapid interconversions among these states took place within hundreds of nanoseconds (462–596 ns). The kinetics of morphine, therefore, represented those of a full-agonist: rapid low-barrier transitions in order to maintain an active signaling state [19].

Comparative MSM free-energy landscapes and transition kinetics for morphine (control) and zerumbone-bound μOR. A Morphine-bound μOR shows four basins across the first two tICs, with a global active-like minimum at (–0.6, 0.6; 0 k_BT), intermediate states ~ 1–2 k_BT above, and an inactive basin ~ 5–6 k_BT higher. B Zerumbone–μOR features a single deep intermediate minimum at (0.6, –0.6; 0 k_BT) and three sparsely populated inactive states elevated by 8–16 k_BT. C MFPT for morphine indicates fast transitions (462–596 ns) among active/intermediate states and ~ 2 µs to inactive-like. D Zerumbone exhibits slow escape (~ 9.8 ms) from the intermediate state and faster transitions (266–586 ns) among higher-energy basins

However, binding with zerumbone has altered the free-energy landscape of the receptor quite extensively to form a strong global-minimum intermediate basin. This intermediate was separated, by high free-energy barriers (~ 8–16 kBT) from fully active and inactive receptor states. More importantly, mean first-passage times (MFPTs) of ligand diffusion determined in MSM indicated kinetic trapping of the receptor within the millisecond time range (~ 9.8 ms escape time). This trapping in an intermediate conformation is an effective mechanism to inhibit the transition to an activated state, and this is characteristic of a limited efficacy of partial agonist [18].

This kinetic mechanism was also elucidated by representative structural snapshots as in Fig. 6. The distance between the activation markers Tyr326 (NPxxY motif, TM7) and Asp148 (DRY motif, TM3) was mined to show differences in the displacement state of TM6: zerumbone-bound intermediate (18.4 Å) and morphine-bound fully active (19.5 Å). Partial outward rearrangement of TM6 expressed by partial agonists does not allow the receptor to completely activate G-protein signaling cascades, which is also precisely align with MSM kinetic and thermodynamic understanding [28].

The snapshots of the three MSM macrostates for both zerumbone‐bound (A–C) and morphine‐bound control (D–F) μOR with emphasis on the Cα–Cα distance between Tyr326^7.53 (the NPxxY motif on TM7) and Asp148^3.49 (the DRY motif on TM3) as a measure of TM6 movement. In the zerumbone system (panels A–C), State 1 (the global minimum) shows a Tyr326–Asp148 separation of 18.4 Å, while the higher‐energy States 2 and 3 both measure 19.4 Å compared to the active‐like State 1 in the morphine control (panels D–F) exhibits a slightly increased distance of 19.5 Å, with States 2 at 19.4 Å and 3 at 19.0 Å. These minor changes in NPxxY–DRY spacing show that, although zerumbone engages the receptor and promotes partial TM6 outward displacement, it does not stabilize the fully open, active‐like conformation as effectively as the full agonist morphine

One limitation of this work is the use of a simplified POPC bilayer to model the receptor environment. Although POPC is widely employed in GPCR simulations and allows reproducible comparisons across ligand systems, it does not fully replicate the lipid heterogeneity of neuronal membranes. In particular, cholesterol, sphingomyelin, and phosphatidylethanolamine are known to significantly modulate μOR conformational equilibria and activation pathways [9]. Cholesterol, for example, interacts with conserved GPCR motifs and can stabilize active-like states, while sphingomyelin and PE lipids alter membrane thickness and receptor packing. The absence of these sterols and lipids in our current model may therefore underestimate certain allosteric effects. Nonetheless, because both zerumbone- and morphine-bound receptors were simulated under identical POPC conditions, the comparative conclusions remain internally consistent. Future work incorporating multi-component lipid mixtures more reflective of native membranes will be important to validate and extend these findings.

MSM robustness was further validated by the implied-timescale analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5) where convergence of the slowest-relaxation processes was observed beyond lag times of ~ 10 frames. This convergence can be used to justify that the sampling herein has been adequately performed, thus showing the validity of conclusions drawn using MSM, which affirms the kinetic fingerprints of morphine and zerumbone at hand [13].

Integrated computational findings align strongly with experimental pharmacological data

Our extensive molecular simulations that resulted in terms of MD simulations, MM/PBSA binding affinities, PCA-based structure characterization, and MSM kinetic modeling, are in concurrence with known pharmacological properties of zerumbone. Previously performed in vivo experiments also proved the analgesia induced by zerumbone to be sensitive to opioid receptor blockade since they were effectively blocked by naloxone (non-selective opioid antagonist) and beta-funaltrexamine (mora-selective opioid antagonist) thereby incontrovertibly associating with μOR-mediated analgesic pathways [7].

The accompanying computational studies present a molecular explanation to this pharmacological pattern, that is, zerumbone is a partial agonist of the μOR that locks the receptor in an intermediate active-state. This intermediate form of receptor activation initiates analgesia without manifesting the fully activated conformational state that is usually accompanied by major side effects of full agonists like morphine (e.g., respiratory arrest and constancy). This sets up zerumbone as a potentially safe drug alternative when it comes to the analgesic effect as it can show the unique manner of partial activation of the receptors through a kinetic and structural level.

This array of computation points towards assessing the importance of combining structure based and dynamics-based analysis in interpreting pharmacological outcomes, with specific mechanistic explanations to efficacy profiles of natural products. This level of molecular insight can be used to form iterative rational drug design and development of safer μOR-targeted analgesics to apply partial agonist properties of the protein to realize safer analgesics [18].

Conclusion

This study used Markov state modeling and all-atom molecular dynamics simulation in order to further explain the effect of the conformations of zerumbone bound to the μ-opioid receptor (μOR). Relative to morphine, the control agonist, zerumbone caused a larger structural flexibility, fewer ligand–receptor hydrogen bonds, as well as kinetic trapping in a deep intermediate macrostate. These data indicate that zerumbone maintains a partly active or intermediate-like conformation of μOR, which is consistent with experimental reports of its partial agonist activity, rather than implying receptor selectivity. While in vivo studies have reported that zerumbone can also interact with κ- and δ-opioid receptors, our computational analysis here was restricted to μOR and does not allow conclusions regarding receptor selectivity or signaling bias. The combination of thermodynamic (MM/PBSA) and kinetic (MSM) analyses highlights the possibility of zerumbone acting as a partial agonist, where binding does not necessarily result in full activation of canonical G-protein signaling. Collectively, these findings offer a mechanism-based validation of the pharmacological data and underline the relevance of computational biophysics in natural product-based drug discovery toward GPCRs.
