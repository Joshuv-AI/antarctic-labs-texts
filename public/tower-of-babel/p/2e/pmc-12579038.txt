Examining the Relationship Between Weight Stigma, Diabetes Stigma, and HbA1c in Adults with Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Tracy J Sims, Chanadda Chinthammit, Melissa L Constantine, Donald M Bushnell, Erik Spaepen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aims to examine the extent to which experienced and/or internalized weight stigma and diabetes stigma may be associated with HbA1c level in adults with type 2 diabetes mellitus.MethodsA total of 857 participants completed a web-based survey including self-reported demographics, weight, HbA1c, and measures of weight stigma and diabetes stigma, including the Modified Weight Bias Internalization Scale (WBIS-M), Weight Self-Stigma Questionnaire (WSSQ), and the Type 2 Diabetes Stigma Assessment Scale (DSAS-2).ResultsParticipants with elevated HbA1c reported greater weight stigma and diabetes stigma than those with an HbA1c level within the standard-of-care range. Exploratory subgroup analysis of participants who did not provide an HbA1c level reported experiencing and internalizing weight stigma and diabetes stigma at similarly high levels as those with elevated HbA1c. Compared to Black non-Hispanic participant’s mean WBIS-M and WSSQ-Total scores, Hispanic participants and White non-Hispanic participants reported greater weight stigma. Hispanic participants endorsed higher DSAS-2 Self-Stigma scores than Black non-Hispanic participants.ConclusionsWeight stigma and diabetes stigma may be associated with suboptimal diabetes care outcomes measured as elevated HbA1c or inability to report an HbA1c level.Keywords: Obesity, Diabetes, HbA1c, Bias, Stigma

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Previous research indicates that approximately 50% of people with diabetes achieved an HbA1c level of ≤7% and 20% achieved standard-of-care HbA1c, blood pressure, and cholesterol levels, exposing them to poor health outcomes.Weight stigma and diabetes stigma have been identified as contributors to psychological distress and negative health behaviors.Using self-reported data, validated instruments, and de novo questions, this study examines the relationship between self-reported weight and diabetes stigma and HbA1c. What was learned from the study? The results indicated that participants who reported greater weight stigma and diabetes stigma reported elevated HbA1c levels. Exploratory subgroup analysis of those unable to provide an HbA1c level reported experiencing weight stigma and diabetes stigma at similar levels as those with higher HbA1c.Our study adds to the evidence base, reinforcing the need to prioritize the identification and management of weight and diabetes bias and stigma as factors that may be associated with suboptimal diabetes management and care outcomes.

Introduction

The nearly 12% prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in the U.S. population [1] and its frequent co-occurrence with obesity [2] underscore the importance of improving the quality of life of people with T2DM. Despite significant advances in the treatment of T2DM [3], health outcomes for people with T2DM continue to lag behind treatment standard recommendations, with approximately 50% meeting HbA1c outcome recommendations [4]. Suboptimal HbA1c outcomes, combined with excess adiposity, high blood pressure, and/or lipid measures, contribute to the increased risk and incidence of T2DM-related complications and poorer health-related quality of life [5, 6].

Despite considerable research evidence of these medical consequences of diabetes, few empirical studies have focused on the stigma that people with T2DM face because of their diabetes and/or obesity and how these forms of stigma may affect their health status. Both obesity and T2DM are stigmatized diseases in which the people who have them are inaccurately assumed to be blameworthy and personally at fault for their weight and/or diabetes due to “personal failings” such as lack of willpower or discipline, being lazy, and being unmotivated to improve their health [7, 8]. A substantial body of literature has established the prevalence and harmful impact of weight stigma for individuals with obesity [8, 9]. This evidence underscores weight stigma and discrimination as prevalent problems affecting as many as 40% of adults with obesity [10]. Weight stigma contributes to numerous negative health and psychological consequences [11–16]. Although the health and psychological implications of weight stigma have been well documented, few studies have examined weight stigma in individuals with T2DM, despite the high comorbidity of these diseases. Furthermore, compared to several decades of research on weight stigma, comparatively little research has studied the nature of diabetes stigma or its implications for patient health. Emerging domestic and global studies suggest that people with T2DM commonly face diabetes societal and self-stigma [17–19].

Initial evidence suggests that both weight stigma and diabetes stigma are commonly experienced and internalized among adults with T2DM [17]. Both weight stigma and diabetes stigma were associated with greater diabetes-related distress, unhealthy eating behaviors, and poorer quality interactions with healthcare providers [20, 21]. These findings suggest that many people with T2DM face weight stigma and diabetes stigma and blame themselves for stigmatizing experiences, and that both forms of stigma contribute to adverse health behaviors and outcomes.

The limited amount of empirical research on the health implications of stigma for people with T2DM is surprising, particularly given several studies suggesting an association between stigma and glycemic management [22–25]. While this evidence suggests that links between stigma and glycemic control may be present, there are significant knowledge gaps. It is unknown whether blood glucose levels are related to weight stigma and/or diabetes stigma in adults with T2DM. Furthermore, stigma can be experienced and/or internalized, all of which negatively affect health behaviors and health indices [12, 26]; yet, links between glycemic management and experienced versus internalized forms of these stigmas have not been studied. Implications of these relationships are also important to consider in the context of demographic factors; initial evidence has documented mixed findings regarding the patterns of weight stigma and diabetes stigma across race/ethnicity and sex [17], highlighting the need for increased research on these constructs in diverse samples of adults with T2DM.

Examining whether, and the extent to which, experienced and/or internalized weight stigma and diabetes stigma may be associated with blood glucose levels is key in understanding these neglected psychosocial contributors to health among people with T2DM. Pursuing these empirical questions also aligns with increasing international calls for research focused on the health effects of diabetes stigma for people with T2DM. Most recently, a 2024 international consensus statement published in The Lancet Diabetes and Endocrinology concluded that diabetes stigma is a widespread problem in need of research to determine its consequences on health, including glycemic outcomes [27]. Thus, establishing what relationship may exist between stigma and glycemic management is a key priority, with implications for informing both research and clinical care of people with T2DM. In response to these clear research gaps, the present study assessed associations between patient-reported HbA1c levels and experienced and internalized weight stigma and diabetes stigma among a national panel of adults with T2DM. Within this exploratory examination, it was predicted that individuals reporting greater weight stigma and diabetes stigma would report higher HbA1c levels. Associations between these forms of stigma and glycemic management were additionally examined across participants’ race/ethnicity and sex to examine demographic patterns and differences.

Methods

Participants and Procedure

Self-reported data for this study were drawn from a nationally representative U.S.-based panel of adults living with T2DM, who were invited to participate in a cross-sectional, self-administered online survey created for this project. Participants were recruited through the National Opinion Research Center’s (NORC) AmeriSpeak panel. The AmeriSpeak panel is a probability-based sample designed to be representative of the U.S. household population. Randomly selected U.S. households are sampled using area probability and address-based sampling with a known, non-zero, probability of selection from the NORC National Sample Frame. These sampled households are then contacted by U.S. mail, telephone, and field interviewers (face-to-face). The panel provides sample coverage of approximately 97% of the U.S. household population. As a U.S. representative sample, the survey sample is expected to be racially and ethnically proportional to the U.S. general population. Those excluded from the sample include people with P.O. Box-only addresses, some addresses not listed in the USPS Delivery Sequence File, and some newly constructed dwellings.

The target population for this study was English-speaking adults aged 18 + residing in the U.S. with a self-reported diagnosis of T2DM. Respondents who self-reported as currently participating in a weight-loss program or in a T2DM clinical trial were excluded from study participation. Also excluded were respondents who self-reported as currently pregnant or experiencing end-stage renal disease. The survey was only provided in English language and was available for electronic completion only. Eligible panel members were informed that the survey’s objective was to learn more about people’s experience with T2DM. Panel members who completed the survey received the equivalent of $10.00 in the form of AmeriPoints. The Western-Copernicus Group institutional review board (tracking number: 20231182) approved the study protocols and informed consent forms. The study was conducted in accordance with ethical principles originating in the Declaration of Helsinki and consistent with Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices. Each participant provided informed consent at study entry before they responded to survey questions. NORC collected the survey data, and members of the research team received only aggregate data; no personal identifying information was included. The survey was designed to be completed in 20–30 min. Participant recruitment and data collection occurred between April 10, 2023, and April 20, 2023.

Measures

The web-based survey for this study included healthcare utilization questions, existing patient-reported outcome measures of experienced and internalized stigma associated with obesity and T2DM, as well as self-reported height and weight, and the most recent HbA1c value. Copyright permissions for the use of survey instruments were obtained. The survey items and measures, including the Cronbach’s alpha values from their use in this study, are detailed below.

Demographics

Participant responses to standard demographics questions were provided by the AmeriSpeak panel, including sex (male, female, unknown), age, race/ethnicity (reported as White non-Hispanic, Black non-Hispanic, Other non-Hispanic, Hispanic, two or more races non-Hispanic, and Asian non-Hispanic), education, marital status, employment status, household income, and geographic area of residence.

Weight Status

The body mass index (BMI) was calculated using the generally accepted formula BMI = weight (kg) / [height (m)]2 [28] and based on participant self-reported weight and height.

HbA1c Level

Participants were asked to report their most recent HbA1c level, with the explanation that HbA1c is a metric of average glucose ordered by their healthcare provider. The allowable survey report values of HbA1c report were constrained to 0.0 to 30.9. The response option of “I do not know” was also included. Self-reported health data, including HbA1c, may be subject to survey question misinterpretation, recall bias, and/or incorrect reporting of measurement quantity.

Internalized Weight Bias

Weight bias internalization was assessed with two validated self-report measures. First, the 10-item Modified Weight Bias Internalization Scale (WBIS-M) (α = 0.94) measured the extent to which participants engage in self-devaluation and self-application of weight-related stereotypes because of their weight [29, 30]. Using a 7-point Likert-type scale (1 = “strongly disagree”’ to 7 = “strongly agree”), participants indicated their level of agreement with statements such as “My weight is a major way that I judge my value as a person” and “I don’t feel that I deserve to have a really fulfilling social life, because of my weight.” Higher WBIS-M scores reflect higher levels of internalized weight bias. Participants completed the Weight Self-Stigma Questionnaire (WSSQ) [31], a 12-item measure that includes two six-item subscales: the Self-Devaluation subscale (α = 0.87), which measures personal blame for one’s body weight (e.g., “I don’t have enough self-control to maintain a healthy weight”), and the Fear of Enacted Stigma subscale (α = 0.92) which assesses the extent that one is concerned with being the target of bias or discrimination because of one’s weight (e.g., “Others are ashamed to be around me because of my weight”). Participants rate their extent of agreement to each item on a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = “completely disagree” to 5 = “completely agree”), with higher scores indicative of greater self-stigma because of weight.

Experienced Weight Stigma

Using three closed-ended (yes/no) questions, participants were asked whether they had ever been teased, treated unfairly, or discriminated against because of their weight [21, 32]. Those who responded “no” to each question were coded as “0” and having no history of experiencing weight stigma. To create a dichotomous variable of experienced stigma, those who responded “yes” to at least one of the three questions were coded as “1” and categorized as having experienced weight stigma. These questions have been previously used in national studies [33] and in samples of adults with obesity and T2DM [17, 34].

Type 2 Diabetes Stigma

The Type 2 Diabetes Stigma Assessment Scale (DSAS-2) was used to measure experienced and internalized stigma related to having T2DM [7]. This validated measure contains 19 items across three subscales including the Treated Differently subscale (six items, α = 0.94; example item: “I have been rejected by others because of my type 2 diabetes”); the Blame and Judgment subscale (seven items, α = 0.92; example item: “Because of my type 2 diabetes, health professionals have made negative judgments about me”); and the Self-Stigma subscale (six items, α = 0.92; example item: “I blame myself for having type 2 diabetes”). Using a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = “strongly disagree” to 5 = “strongly agree”), participants reported their agreement on each item. Item responses were summed within each subscale, and higher scores indicated greater diabetes stigma.

Statistical Analysis

Participants self-reported their most recent HbA1c level, which was coded as follows: HbA1c ≥ 4–20%, HbA1c ≥ 4–8%, HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20%, and “did not report HbA1c.” We chose an HbA1c of 4.0% for the lower boundary of the HbA1c ≥ 4–20% and HbA1c ≥ 4–8% groups as it is consistent with the established laboratory HbA1c low normal. We acknowledge this is an HbA1c seldom recorded for a person with diagnosed T2DM. We chose 8% for the upper boundary of the ≥ 4–8% group as it is consistent with HbA1c considered “controlled” in the Healthcare Effectiveness Data Information System (HEDIS) Comprehensive Diabetes Care data set. We acknowledge the American Diabetes Association Standard of Care recommendation to achieve an HbA1c of ≤ 7%. We chose 20% as the upper limit for the HbA1c ≥ 4–20% and HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% groups. Reported HbA1c levels < 4.0% and > 20.0% were considered out of range for analysis. Participants unable to report an HbA1c were retained for exploratory subgroup analysis. We used data from the 857 completed surveys and report basic descriptive statistics. Sex, race, BMI and stigma scores were compared across HbA1c categories. Similarly, stigma scores, BMI, and HbA1c was compared across sex and race. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test (for 2 categories) or the Kruskal–Wallis test (for > 2 categories). Binary categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, whereas categorical data with more than 2 outcome categories were evaluated using the 2xN Cochran-Mantel–Haenszel test. Although demographic survey questions allowed participants to report multiple racial/ethnic categories, participants who identified as Other non-Hispanic, Asian non-Hispanic, or two or more races non-Hispanic were excluded from the analytic sample due to inadequate subsample sizes for meaningful statistical comparisons. The target sample size of n = 800 allows a margin of error of ± 3.4 with a 95% confidence level. We performed all statistical analyses using SAS© 9.4.

Results

Response and Demographics

A total of 27,590 AmeriSpeak Panel members were notified of the survey by e-mail or telephone, and 6785 (24.6%) completed the screener questions to determine eligibility. A total of 875 screened eligible (eligibility rate = 12.9%); 857 of those screened eligible completed the electronic survey, a survey completion rate of 97.9%. The median time to complete the survey was 20 min.

Most participants were male (61.6%); 61.8% of participants were White non-Hispanic, 14.4% Black, non-Hispanic, and 15.4% Hispanic (Table 1). The mean age of participants was 57.4 years (SD = 15.7). Nearly 87% of the participants reported weight and height equating to a BMI indicating having either overweight or obesity. Among participants with a valid HbA1c level between 4 and 20% (n = 575), the mean BMI was 33.4 (SD = 8.44) (Table 2). Overall, participants reported an HbA1c level indicating well-managed blood glucose with 88% reporting an HbA1c within the range of 4.0% to 8.0%. Over 29% of the participants, however, did not report an HbA1c. Past studies reported non-response to self-reporting of HbA1c of 30% to 50% [35, 36]. An additional n = 32 participants reported an HbA1c coded as out of range to be reliably included in the analysis of HbA1c as an outcome, with n = 24 reporting a value < 4.0% and n = 8 reporting a value > 20.0%.

In total, 47.8% of the sample reported experienced weight stigma while those with HbA1c ≥ 4–8% experienced the least weight stigma (46.7%) and had the lowest mean BMI (33.1), whereas those with an HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% experienced the highest weight stigma (55.7%) and had the highest mean BMI (35.9) (Table 2).

WBIS-M

The mean WBIS-M score for those reporting an HbA1c ≥ 4–20% was 3.2 (SD = 1.49) (Table 2). When segmented, those with an HbA1c in the higher group (≥ 8.01–20%) reported a significantly higher WBIS-M score than those in the lower group (≥ 4–8%) (p = 0.0153). There was no difference between those with an HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% and those who did not report an HbA1c (p = 0.9145).

Weight Self-Stigma Questionnaire Total (WSSQ-T), WSSQ-Self-Devaluation (WSSQ-SD), and WSSQ-Fear of Enacted Stigma (WSSQ-FES)

The mean WSSQ-T and subscale WSSQ-SD and WSSQ-FES scores were 29.1 (SD = 10.91), 15.0 (SD = 5.51), and 14.1 (SD = 6.10), respectively (Table 2). Those with an HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% reported significantly higher WSSQ-T, WSSQ-SD, and WSSQ-FES scores (p = 0.0037, 0.0187, 0.0030) than those with an HbA1c ≥ 4–8%. There was no significant difference between those with an HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% and those who did not report an HbA1c (p = 0.3973, 0.5852, 0.4632).

Type 2 Diabetes Stigma Assessment Scale Total (DSAS-2-T), DSAS-2-Treated Differently (DSAS-2-TD), DSAS-2 Blame and Judgement (DSAS-2-BJ), and DSAS-2 Self-Stigma (DSAS-2-SS)

The mean DSAS-2-T and subscale DSAS-2-TD, DSAS-2-BJ, and DSAS-2-SS scores were 38.3 (SD = 15.42), 10.2 (SD = 4.80), 16.3 (SD = 6.99), and 11.8 (SD = 5.68), respectively (Table 2). Those with an HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% reported significantly higher DSAS-2-T, DSAS-2-TD, DSAS-2-BJ, DSAS-2- SS scores (p = 0.0019, 0.0014, 0.0151, 0.0047) when compared to those with an HbA1c ≥ 4–8%. There was no difference between those with an HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% and those who did not report an HbA1c (p = 0.3381, 0.4109, 0.1434, 0.8800).

Weight and Diabetes Stigma across Race and Ethnic Groups

We additionally compared the relationship of weight and diabetes stigma and HbA1c in White non-Hispanic, Black non-Hispanic, and Hispanic participants. Hispanic participants reported the highest experienced weight stigma (53.0%), followed by White (48.3%) and Black non-Hispanic participants (42.3%) (Table 3). The mean BMI was similar for White non-Hispanic (33.7), Black-non-Hispanic (34.2), and Hispanic (33.9) participants. The mean HbA1c was also consistent across the three groups with 6.9 (SD = 1.32) for White non-Hispanic participants, 7.0 (SD = 1.77) for Black non-Hispanic participants, and 7.1 (SD = 1.94) for Hispanic participants. Hispanic participants reported the highest mean HbA1c in the HbA1c ≥ 8.01–20% group (10.9, SD = 2.67) and the highest “did not report HbA1c” (35.6%); White and Black non-Hispanic participants reported 9.7 (SD = 1.62), 27% and 10.4 (SD = 2.12), 27.6%, respectively.

WBIS-M

Hispanic participants reported the highest mean WBIS-M score and Black non-Hispanic participants the lowest (Table 3). Hispanic participants reported WBIS-M scores that were significantly higher than those of Black non-Hispanic participants (p = 0.0003) but not significantly different from those of White non-Hispanic participants (p = 0.1091). WBIS-M scores reported by White non-Hispanic participants were significantly higher than scores of Black non-Hispanic participants (p = 0.0117).

WSSQ-T, WSSQ-SD, and WSSQ-FES

The mean WSSQ-T scores were 26.4 (SD = 11.28), 29.8 (SD = 10.63), and 31.9 (SD = 10.59) for Black non-Hispanic, White non-Hispanic, and Hispanic participants, respectively (Table 3). Total scores were significantly higher in Hispanic participants compared to Black non-Hispanic participants (p = 0.0005), as were WSSQ-SD and WSSQ-FES scores (p = 0.0004, 0.0065). WSSQ-T scores were significantly higher in White non-Hispanic participants than in Black non-Hispanic participants (p = 0.0085) as were WSSQ-SD and WSSQ-FES scores (p = 0.0033, 0.0437). There were no significant differences between White non-Hispanic and Hispanic participants.

DSAS-2-T, DSAS-2-TD, DSAS-2-BJ, and DSAS-2-SS

The mean DSAS-2-T scores were 36.9 (SD = 16.91), 38.6 (SD = 15.19), and 41.7 (SD = 17.01) for Black non-Hispanic, White non-Hispanic, and Hispanic participants, respectively, but the differences were not significant (Table 3). Similarly, there were no significant differences between these groups across the DSAS-2-TD, DSAS-2-BJ, and DSAS-2-SS subscales of the DSAS-2, except for the DSAS-2-SS subscale for which Hispanic participants reported higher scores (p = 0.0037) than Black non-Hispanic participants.

These findings suggest that weight and diabetes stigma are experienced across race and ethnic groups, with Hispanic participants reporting the highest scores for weight stigma. The lack of statistically significant differences in diabetes-related stigma across race and ethnicity may indicate parity of experience with the disease and associated bias and stigma.

Discussion

We examined the association between weight stigma, diabetes stigma, and HbA1c in adults with T2DM. Diabetes care in the U.S. continues to be suboptimal, with approximately 50% of people with the disease achieving an HbA1c ≤ 7% and approximately 20% achieving standard-of-care HbA1c, blood pressure, and cholesterol levels [4]. These results persist despite the disease being well-articulated, with widespread availability of evidence-based treatment guidelines and efficacious therapies [37]. Such outcomes subject those with T2DM to greater risk for and realization of associated complications [38] and cause significant costs to the U.S. health system [39].

Therefore, it is critical to examine factors that may contribute to health management challenges and suboptimal diabetes care outcomes. Weight stigma and diabetes stigma have been identified as psychosocial correlates of psychological distress and adverse health behaviors [11, 12, 20, 40]. Our study adds to the evidence base, reinforcing the need to prioritize the identification and management of bias and stigma as social drivers of suboptimal health outcomes for people with T2DM affected by bias and stigma. Approximately half of the participants in our study reported having experienced weight stigma, suggesting that these are common experiences for adults with T2DM. The dual burden of internalized and experienced weight stigma and diabetes stigma may have compounding negative effects on people with T2DM, contributing to their health-seeking and health management behaviors, resulting in poorer health outcomes. Our findings parallel recent evidence of similarly high rates of weight and diabetes stigma reported by adults with T2DM both domestically and internationally [17, 41] and highlight the need for more research to examine the nature and prevalence of both weight stigma and diabetes stigma, what contexts these forms of stigma are most prevalent in this population, and how to reduce the presence and impact of both weight and diabetes stigma. Such efforts may build upon domestic and international calls to reduce bias and stigma against people with diabetes [27, 42]. In particular, evidence has documented the presence of weight bias and stigmatizing attitudes among medical professionals toward people with obesity [43, 44], and it will be important for future work to determine how diabetes stigma and weight stigma in the healthcare setting are associated with patient health outcomes in this population and to collaborate on optimizing current and future efforts to reduce such stigma in the clinical setting [45, 46].

Notably, participants in our study who reported elevated HbA1c levels experienced greater weight stigma and diabetes stigma. This was particularly evident on self-reported stigma measures indicating fear of enacted weight stigma (WSSQ – Fear of Enacted Stigma subscale) and being stigmatized by others for their diabetes (DSAS-2 – Treated Differently subscale). Furthermore, exploratory subgroup analysis of participants who did not report an HbA1c level reported experiencing weight stigma and diabetes stigma at similar levels as those with higher HbA1c. Though previous research found nearly 61% of persons with above standard of care HbA1c had not received HbA1c testing per ADA guidelines [47], we cannot draw conclusions about the causes or their directionality for non-reporting nor the HbA1c level itself as our survey questions did not assess reasons why a participant did not report their HbA1c. We propose, however, five possibilities: 1) the participant had not recently engaged a healthcare provider enabling an HbA1c order; 2) an HbA1c was not ordered for the participant when engaged with their healthcare provider; 3) the participant was not told their HbA1c; 4) the participant did not understand the meaning of the HbA1c, and 5) the participant did not remember the HbA1c. Future studies should examine these and other potential reasons why people with T2DM might not know or disclose their HbA1c and whether experienced or internalized stigma may be contributing factors. For example, it could be that internalized diabetes stigma (e.g., personal shame and/or blame for one’s diabetes) contributes to reluctance to disclose a high HbA1c that could lead to negative judgment from others and being perceived as having poor diabetes self-management.

Similarly, anticipated weight stigmatization may cause a person with T2DM to avoid visits to their healthcare professional for diabetes care. Finally, an HbA1c could be interpreted by a person with T2DM as a measure of performance in their T2DM self-care. A person with overweight or obesity and T2DM may avoid such performance assessment risk as a higher HbA1c may be negatively interpreted in the context of behaviors associated with their weight status, influencing their T2DM self-management. As the relationship between HbA1c and stigma is poorly understood, research in this area should be prioritized. Our study also identified that Hispanic participants with obesity and diabetes reported higher degrees of bias and stigma and were least likely to report an HbA1c, compared with White non-Hispanic and Black non-Hispanic participants. While examining the reasons for this result was beyond the scope of our study, these findings are similar to National Healthcare Quality and Disparities Reports and Centers for Disease Control data indicating lower rates of HbA1c testing and higher rates of diabetes-related complications in the Hispanic population compared to the White non-Hispanic population with diabetes [48]. Our study provides additional insights highlighting weight stigma and diabetes stigma as potential contributors to elevated HbA1c levels that warrant empirical attention in this population.

The commonality of weight stigma and diabetes stigma reported by participants in our sample and their links with self-reported HbA1c levels underscore the need for future research to improve the understanding of what healthcare management linkages are most affected by bias and stigma. These linkages may include those that are patient-managed (e.g., meeting with a healthcare professional, therapy management, subjective norm management) and those that are healthcare professional-managed (e.g., lab testing, patient-centric care practices, therapeutic inertia, referrals). More immediately, emphasis should be placed on action around previous calls in the diabetes field for efforts to reduce bias and stigma at both structural and patient care levels [27, 49]. Such emphasis supports advances in health equity, more efficient use of healthcare resources, and improved quality of life for people currently burdened by the detrimental and compounding effects of serious chronic disease and bias and stigma. Similar efforts have been initiated in the obesity field to educate healthcare providers about weight stigma and reduce weight bias in the medical setting [50]; there may be opportunities for collective efforts to target both diabetes stigma and weight stigma in the medical community.

The present study is the first to examine links between HbA1c levels and both weight stigma and diabetes stigma in people with T2DM. The racial/ethnic diversity of the participant sample and assessment of both internalized and experienced weight stigma and diabetes stigma using validated measures are strengths of the present study and enhance its contribution to the emerging literature in this field. Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional descriptive data prevent conclusions about causality or directionality of associations between stigma and glycemic management. Causal and reverse causal factors associated with weight and diabetes bias and stigma associated with diabetes management and its outcomes, should be additionally considered. The reliance on self-reported data, including measures of stigma and HbA1c levels may be subject to survey question misinterpretation, recall bias, and/or incorrect reporting of measurement quantity. Additionally, the role of BMI, or, more specifically, the role of fat mass and its distribution on HbA1c outcomes cannot be discounted as a contributor to suboptimal HbA1c outcomes regardless of the degree of bias and stigma. Longitudinal research examining weight stigma, diabetes stigma, and objective measurements of blood glucose levels at multiple time points in people with T2DM are needed to determine the nature and temporality of these relationships over time. Additionally, the percentage of participants in the study sample who reported HbA1c levels above treatment standard recommendations was relatively low; future work is needed to replicate the present findings in people with T2DM to compare links with stigma and HbA1c levels above versus within treatment standard recommendations. As one-third of the study participants did not report their most recent HbA1c level, it will also be important to examine reasons for not reporting blood glucose among people with T2DM and whether stigma plays a contributing role. Finally, future work should include assessment of additional forms of stigma experienced by people with T2DM (e.g., racial/ethnic discrimination), as intersecting stigmatizing identities may compound health consequences arising from stigma that people experience due to their weight and/or diabetes.

Conclusions

The findings of this study provide additional insights into the relationships between weight stigma, diabetes stigma, and elevated HbA1c values in people with T2DM and their inability to report an HbA1c. As improving T2DM care outcomes across populations is of paramount importance, our findings suggest that these efforts should consider the contributing roles and impacts of weight stigma and diabetes stigma. Advancing efforts to reduce social, health system, and self-stigmatization may aid in improved health management agency and outcomes for people with T2DM. Further research is warranted to clarify the role of stigma in HbA1c, to examine factors perpetuating stigmatization of people with T2DM, and to identify effective strategies for reducing stigma and its harmful health consequences.
