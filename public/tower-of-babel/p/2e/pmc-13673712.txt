Social networks and intergenerational support among rural left-behind older adults in China: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Zhongsu Shi, Chunhui Yang, Yue Zhang, Jia Wang, Yali Mao, Junnan Song, Huan Liu, Lin Zheng, Yuqin Jiang, Annuo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify latent classes of social networks among rural left-behind older adults in China, examine their influencing factors, and explore their associations with different dimensions of intergenerational support.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted in July–August 2022 across seven counties in Anhui Province, China, using village-based convenience sampling combined with a random-walk household procedure, using a general information questionnaire, the Lubben Social Network Scale, and an intergenerational support scale. Latent profile analysis (LPA) identified network types; multinomial logistic regression examined covariate predictors; and ordinal models including quadratic terms characterized the associations of intergenerational support dimensions with network type, with inflection-point estimation and sensitivity analyses.ResultsLPA revealed three distinct social network profiles: Family-Friend Weak-Tie (56.5%), Family-Friend Moderate-Maintenance (31.3%), and Family-Friend Stable -Active (12.3%). Age, primary economic source, social participation, vision impairment, and intergenerational support were significant influencing factors (all p < 0.05). Emotional support was consistently and positively associated with network level (β = 0.312, p < 0.001), whereas financial support showed a significant inverted-U association (linear β = 1.07, p = 0.002; quadratic β = − 0.105, p = 0.003), with an inflection point at a financial support score of 5.11; instrumental care(daily care) showed no significant association. The inverted-U pattern was robust across sensitivity analyses.ConclusionThe social networks of rural left-behind older adults in China are heterogeneous, with over half characterized by weak ties and a high prevalence of social isolation. Dimensions of intergenerational support are differentially associated with network type: emotional support is consistently positive, financial support follows an inverted-U pattern (suggesting moderate rather than maximal support), and instrumental care shows no significant association. Interventions should prioritize the weak-tie subgroup, emphasize emotional connection, and promote moderate levels of financial support.

Population aging is a global phenomenon, and older adults' social relationships are increasingly recognized as an important factor for their health and well-being in later life [14, 34]. Among the various features of social relationships, the structural condition of social networks and the risk of social isolation are particularly important: older adults with sparse social ties and infrequent contact face higher risks to their physical and mental health and quality of life [31].

A social network refers to the structural features of the social ties an individual forms and maintains through ongoing interaction, reflected mainly in network size and the frequency of contact with family and friends [5, 19]. Social isolation is an objective state characterized by a small social network and sparse contact; it is conceptually distinct from loneliness (a subjective feeling of social deficiency) and from social support (the functional resources exchanged within relationships) [6, 31]. This study focuses on the structural dimension of social networks and the corresponding risk of social isolation, measured using the Lubben Social Network Scale-6 (LSNS-6) [28].

Regarding the structure of older adults' social networks, the convoy model provides an overarching perspective: each person's social convoy changes continuously over the life course and is shaped by personal characteristics and life circumstances [2]. Building on this, the social network typology approach further proposes that older adults' social networks do not vary along a single continuum but can be grouped into qualitatively distinct structural types—commonly including diverse, family-focused, friend-focused, and restricted/isolated types—differentiated mainly by structural features such as network size and the frequency of contact with family and friends, and associated differently with well-being and the risk of social isolation [9, 13]. In recent years, researchers have increasingly used person-centered methods such as latent profile analysis to identify these network types [26].

The composition of social networks varies across cultural contexts. In Western individualist societies, voluntary ties such as friendships occupy a larger proportion of older adults' networks; by contrast, in East Asian collectivist societies, older adults' networks are more family-centered, with relatively fewer but closer friendships [10, 13]. This means that in the East Asian context the condition of family ties has a more decisive influence on older adults' overall network structure, while older adults' maintenance of ties beyond the family is relatively weak [12].

Parallel to these cultural differences is another layer of context at the geographic and developmental level. Globally, older adults in rural and remote areas tend to face higher social network vulnerability: particularly in low- and middle-income countries, the internal migration of young adults from rural to urban areas has become an important feature of demographic change, leaving large numbers of older parents behind in the countryside, with their social networks weakened and the risk of social isolation rising [1]. This "left-behind" phenomenon is not unique—in South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, China and other developing regions, the out-migration of younger people likewise leaves older adults increasingly isolated in resource-poor rural and peripheral areas [7, 18].

Rural China embodies both the cultural and developmental features described above. On the one hand, Chinese family relationships have long followed the "differential mode of association" (chaxugeju) proposed by Fei Xiaotong, in which social ties are arranged outward from close to distant, with the parent–child tie at the core of the individual's relational network [8], making Chinese older adults' networks highly family-centered. On the other hand, as population aging and urbanization accelerate, large-scale rural-to-urban labor migration has continuously expanded the rural left-behind older population, with more than half of rural older adults aged 60 and above living in empty-nest households( [27]. The out-migration of children directly alters the family core of these older adults' networks, leaving their social networks fragmented [38] and potentially shifting them toward family-focused or even restricted/isolated network types, thereby increasing the risk of social isolation [23]. According to the convoy model, an individual's social network is a dynamic structure that is reshaped over the life course and by life circumstances; the out-migration of children, as a situational factor, may reshape the network structure of rural left-behind older adults. This study adopts a network typology perspective and uses latent profile analysis to identify the distinct network types present in this population in a cross-sectional sample.

Among the many factors shaping the network types of left-behind older adults, the intergenerational support provided by out-migrated children warrants particular attention, as it is an important link between distant children and their left-behind parents. Intergenerational support typically comprises three distinct dimensions—emotional support, financial support, and daily care—and can be measured using an intergenerational support scale [16]; existing research indicates that its different dimensions may be associated in different ways with older adults' social participation and psychological states [22, 29]. Among these, emotional and financial support are often the most prominent [17]. However, regarding the relationship between the dimensions of intergenerational support and social network structure—particularly whether "more is always better"—the existing evidence remains divided: moderate intergenerational support is generally beneficial, whereas excessive support may be counterproductive [22].

Although academic attention to the well-being of rural left-behind older adults continues to grow [24], research on the heterogeneity of their social network structure, and on the associations between the dimensions of intergenerational support and this heterogeneity, remains limited. Latent profile analysis is a person-centered method that can identify latent subgroups differing in social network structure without imposing predefined categories [32]. This study is exploratory and does not posit hypotheses to be tested; its aims are to (1) identify the social network types of rural left-behind older adults in China, and (2) examine the associations between the dimensions of intergenerational support (emotional, instrumental, financial) and network type membership. The specific research questions are: (a) What distinct social network types can be identified among rural left-behind older adults? (b) Are the dimensions of intergenerational support associated with these types, and if so, in what way?

Participants and methods

Study participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted in July–August 2022 by a university summer social-practice team (the 'Yixing Shougen' Rural Health Service Team of Anhui Medical University). A community-based convenience sampling strategy was used to access rural communities: the team members collected data in their respective home counties. Within each county, eligible rural left-behind older adults were recruited using a random-walk household sampling procedure. Rural left-behind older adults were selected according to the following criteria: ① Aged 60 years or older; ② Registered as rural residents; ③ Children have been living away from home for at least six months; ④ Diagnosed with at least one chronic disease; ➄ Provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. Exclusion criteria included: ① Diagnosis of severe mental illness or Alzheimer’s disease; ② Severe communication barriers (e.g., hearing or speech impairments preventing normal interaction); ③ Inability to complete the questionnaire, even with caregiver assistance, due to refusal, poor compliance, or cognitive limitations. Study instruments

General information questionnaire

Based on a comprehensive literature review and the specific circumstances of the target population, a self-designed general demographic questionnaire was developed. It collected information on age, gender, living arrangements, educational level, and other relevant sociodemographic characteristics(The specific classification characteristics are shown in Table 3).

Lubben social network scale (LSNS-6)

The Lubben Social Network Scale (LSNS-6), developed by Lubben et al. (2006), was used to assess participants' social networks [28]. As one of the most widely used tools in gerontological research, the LSNS-6 employs concise items to efficiently evaluate older adults' social connectivity. The scale consists of 6 items covering two dimensions: family networks (3 items) and friend networks (3 items). Each item is scored from 0 to 5 points, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 30. A total score below 12 indicates social isolation. In this study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α coefficient of 0.831.

Intergenerational support scale

The Intergenerational Support Scale, developed by the Institute of Population Research at Xi'an Jiaotong University in China [16], was used to assess three dimensions of intergenerational support: emotional support (3 items), daily care support (3 items), and financial support (2 items), totaling 8 items.Emotional support items were rated on a 3-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 3 to 9. Daily care and financial support items were rated on 5-point Likert scales, with score ranges of 3–15 and 2–10, respectively. The total score ranged from 8 to 34, with higher scores indicating higher levels of intergenerational support. In this study, the scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.731. Intergenerational support was examined at two complementary levels. In the multivariable model (Table 4), a dichotomized global indicator of intergenerational support (poor vs. good, split at 21) was included [42]. The three support dimensions (emotional, instrumental, financial) were then examined separately (Tables 5, 6 and 7) to characterize their distinct and potentially non-linear associations.

Data collection

Following the acquisition of informed consent, data were collected through two primary approaches: home visits and on-site surveys conducted at local village service centers. Trained investigators administered the questionnaires using a standardized face-to-face interview format, with uniform instructions provided to all participants. All questionnaires were collected immediately upon completion.

Following data collection, all responses were systematically entered into a database. The dataset underwent a rigorous verification process, including a comprehensive review and secondary validation, to ensure accuracy. The finalized data were subsequently stored under secure conditions by designated personnel. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Approval No. 81220209).

The robustness of the inverted-U was assessed through sensitivity analyses: excluding extreme financial support scores, using non-imputed data, comparing model fit (AIC) with and without the quadratic term, and re-estimating multinomial models that impose no ordering assumption. Multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors. A sensitivity analysis restricted to participants aged ≥ 65 years was also conducted.

Results

Common method bias test

Common method bias was assessed using Harman's single-factor test. The results indicated that the first factor accounted for 29.916% of the total variance, which is below the critical threshold of 50% [33]. This suggests that common method bias does not pose a substantial threat to the interpretation of the findings in this study.

Latent profile analysis of social networks among rural left-behind older adults

Latent profile analysis was conducted in Mplus 8.7 using robust maximum likelihood estimation (MLR). The analysis was performed on participants with complete LSNS-6 data (n = 458). To guard against convergence on local maxima, 500 random start values were specified, with 100 retained for final-stage optimization; the best log-likelihood value was replicated across start values, confirming a stable solution. One- to four-class models were estimated and compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and sample-size-adjusted BIC (lower values indicating better fit), entropy (higher values indicating clearer classification), the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood-ratio test (LMR-LRT) and the bootstrap likelihood-ratio test (BLRT) (significant p-values indicating that a k-class model fit better than a (k–1)-class model), together with class size and theoretical interpretability [32]. Although the AIC and BIC continued to decrease for the four-class solution, both the LMR-LRT (p = 0.104) and BLRT (p = 0.109) were non-significant, indicating no significant improvement over the three-class model; solutions with more than four classes were therefore not considered. The three-class solution was retained, balancing statistical fit, class size, interpretability, and theoretical meaning. Entropy was high (0.971), and average latent class assignment probabilities ranged from 97.1% to 99.2%, supporting the reliability of the identified structure.

Characteristics and naming of the social network latent classes

The optimal 3-class model revealed distinct social network patterns among rural left-behind older adults: Class 1 (n = 257) is designated as "Family-Friend Weak-Tie Type," this group demonstrated low scores across both family and friend dimensions, with particularly minimal friend network engagement. This profile reflects limited social interaction and weak relational strength in both family and friend contexts; Class 2 (n = 144) is identified as "Family-Friend Moderate-Maintenance Type," these individuals showed balanced, intermediate levels of engagement in both family and friend networks, indicating consistent but moderate social connectivity across relationships; Class 3 (n = 57) is categorized as "Family-Friend Stable-Active Type," this group maintained adequate family network involvement while demonstrating notably high friend network engagement, characterizing a socially active profile with strong peer connections. Multivariate analysis of social network latent profiles

Multinomial logistic regression was performed with the three-category social network variable as the dependent variable, using the "Family-Friend Weak-Tie Type" as the reference group. The multinomial logistic regression model demonstrated adequate fit (model χ2(30) = 137.99, p < 0.001; Cox & Snell R2 = 0.278, Nagelkerke R2 = 0.329, McFadden R2 = 0.174). Likelihood-ratio tests indicated that age (p < 0.001), primary economic source (p = 0.021), social participation (p < 0.001), vision impairment (p = 0.003), and intergenerational support (p = 0.030) were significant predictors, whereas education level (p = 0.517) and pain (p = 0.152) were not. All variance inflation factors were below 2.6, indicating no problematic multicollinearity (Supplementary Table S2). After adjusting for covariates, age, social participation, vision-related daily life impairment, intergenerational support and primary economic source remained statistically significant predictors of latent profile membership (all p-values < 0.05). Model validation of intergenerational support dimensions on social network latent classes

Given that the social network classes demonstrated an overall ordered progression from c1 to c3, characterized by increasing scores in family and friend dimensions and decreasing isolation levels (Table 2), the latent profiles were treated as ordered categories reflecting underlying social connectivity levels. Theoretically, although latent profile analysis yields qualitatively distinct classes, the LSNS-6 operationalises social connectivity as a continuous construct (total score 0–30), with social isolation defined at its lower end (a score below 12). The three profiles differ primarily in their position along this underlying continuum of social connectivity—from the socially isolated Weak-Tie profile, through the intermediate Moderate-Maintenance profile, to the well-connected Stable-Active profile—providing a theoretical basis for treating them as ordered with respect to overall connectivity. This ordinal coding was applied only to the trend analyses; covariate associations were examined using multinomial regression that imposes no ordering (Table 4), and a multinomial model without any ordering assumption reproduced the same inverted-U finding, confirming the result is not an artefact of the ordinal coding.

To quantitatively verify the trends observed in Fig. 2, we constructed generalized linear models for statistical inference, with specific attention to testing nonlinear relationships. Model 1 examined the effect of emotional support on social network classes. Results indicated that emotional support was a significant positive predictor of network level (β = 0.312, P < 0.001), confirming that higher emotional support corresponds to a greater likelihood of belonging to more socially connected classes.

Model 2 simultaneously incorporated instrumental care and financial support, including quadratic terms to test for nonlinear effects. Instrumental care showed neither significant linear nor quadratic effects (both P > 0.05), suggesting no substantial association with social network classification. In contrast, financial support demonstrated a significant inverted U-shaped relationship (linear term: β = 1.070, P = 0.002; quadratic term: β = −0.105, P = 0.003).This indicates that social network levels are higher connected with increasing financial support up to an optimal point, beyond which further support is associated with lower network connectivity. The inverted-U association was robust across sensitivity analyses: it remained significant after excluding high financial support scores (p = 0.033), when using non-imputed data (p = 0.002). The equation model exhibits good fit (ΔAIC ≈ 9) (Supplementary Table S1). We conducted repeated analyses on the sample of older adult aged ≥ 65 years(n = 385), which similarly supported this conclusion(linear β = 1.32, p = 0.002; quadratic β = − 0.137, p = 0.002).

Discussion

This study departs from a view of social networks as a single, undifferentiated whole and identifies three ordered network types among rural left-behind older adults in China, finding that the dimensions of intergenerational support show different, and even opposing, associations with social network type: emotional support is consistently and positively associated with network level, whereas financial support shows an inverted-U association.

Identification of social network types

Using latent profile analysis, this study identified three types of social networks: the Family-Friend Weak-Tie type, the Moderate-Maintenance type, and the Stable-Active type. The model fit well, supporting the heterogeneity of this population's social networks. In terms of the established network-typology literature, the Weak-Tie profile corresponds closely to the 'restricted' (socially isolated) type, whereas the Stable-Active profile resembles a more friend-oriented type, with the Moderate-Maintenance profile occupying an intermediate position [9, 13]. Consistent with the rural left-behind context, however, all three profiles were comparatively family-centred and were distinguished primarily by the extent of friend ties rather than family ties.

More than half of the participants were classified into the Weak-Tie type and were almost all in a state of social isolation, indicating that over half of rural left-behind older adults have small, sparsely connected networks and are doubly vulnerable in both family and friend networks—consistent with reports by Li [21] and Chen [4] on the contraction of social networks among rural left-behind older adults—suggesting that this population is at high risk of social isolation and requires policy and intervention attention.

About one-third of older adults were classified into the Moderate-Maintenance type, a profile that may represent a transitional state between the Weak-Tie and Stable-Active types. Compared with the Moderate-Maintenance type, the Stable-Active type had similar family network scores but a lower rate of social isolation, with friend network scores being the key factor distinguishing the two groups (Fig. 1). This suggests that, when family ties are relatively limited, stronger friend ties may be associated with a lower risk of social isolation [35]. At the same time, the Stable-Active type scored lowest on family contact frequency (item 1), suggesting that higher family contact frequency is not necessarily accompanied by a larger or more diverse network. And the greater variability in friend networks suggests that friend ties may be a more sensitive marker for distinguishing network types, and that interventions targeting friend ties may help some older adults move toward more connected types [3].

Individual characteristics of the latent classes

The covariates that were significant in the multivariate analysis together delineate a profile of older adults in the Weak-Tie type: advanced age, vision impairment, economic dependence on children, poorer intergenerational support, and a gap between willingness to participate socially and actual behavior. These features are not isolated but point to a situation of cumulative disadvantage: declines in physical and sensory function narrow the range of activity and contact needed to maintain a network [39], consistent with the convoy model's view that convoy structure is shaped by individual health and life circumstances; while the gap between participation willingness and behavior further hinders the translation of the network from intention into actual ties. Consistent with prior research, the rural left-behind older adults in this study were mainly engaged in migrant-worker or agriculture-related occupations and largely depended on their children in later life [40], and were less likely to obtain abundant friend support than older adults who held higher social status before retirement [36]. Poorer intergenerational support was associated with lower life satisfaction and higher risk of social isolation [4, 30]. In other words, those who fall into the restricted network type are often older adults of advanced age who both lack the daily presence of family and find it difficult to translate social intentions into actual ties. This identifiable profile provides a basis for screening priority intervention targets.

The relationship between social networks and intergenerational support

Intergenerational support and its dimensions were significantly associated with social network type, consistent overall with prior studies reporting positive correlations [43]. However, this association did not hold once networks reached moderate-to-high levels, suggesting that higher intergenerational support does not necessarily correspond to higher network levels.

Emotional support was significantly and positively associated with social network type, and this positive association was stable across the three types. Relying solely on the total intergenerational support score may obscure the independent role of emotional support. This suggests that the emotional connection in intergenerational relationships may be the dimension most stably associated with network level among rural left-behind older adults. A plausible reason is that, among Chinese older adults, emotional exchanges are concentrated within close family ties [17, 41]; emotional support may therefore track overall network connectedness more stably than the financial or instrumental dimensions.

Financial support showed a significant inverted-U association with social network type: moderate financial support was associated with higher network levels, whereas beyond the inflection point (a financial support score of 5.11) higher financial support was associated with lower network levels. In this study, the mean financial support in each class was below the inflection point, reflecting generally low financial support among rural left-behind older adults. As for the negative association beyond the inflection point, existing research provides clues: compared with those in balanced or over-benefited positions, "under-benefited" Chinese older adults report better self-rated health [25], and a Korean study likewise found a nonlinear association between intergenerational financial exchange and psychological well-being [20]; one possible explanation is that excessively high and difficult-to-reciprocate financial support places older adults in an imbalanced "over-benefited" state, bringing feelings of indebtedness and impaired autonomy. Thus, excessively high financial support from children is not necessarily beneficial and may instead weaken older adults' psychological and social functioning through reciprocity imbalance [15, 25].

Instrumental care was not significantly associated with social network type in the model (linear p = 0.394; quadratic p = 0.866, Table 7). This may be related to the characteristics of this study's population: left-behind older adults live apart from their children for long periods, and geographic distance may limit the provision of daily instrumental care, thereby possibly weakening its association with network type.

Implications for intervention and policy

The above findings have several implications for intervention and policy. First, precisely identifying high-risk subgroups: the Weak-Tie type accounts for more than half of the sample and is almost entirely in a state of isolation; in the rural left-behind context that lacks daily family care, it should be a priority target for screening and intervention. Second, the need to expand non-family ties: given that friend ties are key to distinguishing more connected types, interventions can focus on helping older adults rebuild connections beyond the family, such as community group activities, peer support, and regular volunteer visits; systematic reviews suggest that group interventions in which older adults are active participants and that precisely reach them in community and public settings are more effective in improving structural social ties [11]. Third, prioritizing low-cost emotional connection: emotional support is the dimension most stably associated with network level, suggesting that low-cost strategies such as regular remote emotional contact are worth incorporating; given the geographic separation between left-behind older adults and their out-migrated children, maintaining emotional contact through telephone and video may be a realistic and low-cost avenue [37]. Fourth, advocating "moderate rather than maximal" financial support: given the inverted-U association of financial support, policy should not simply encourage more remittances but should also consider older adults' autonomy and psychological experience.

Conclusion

Rural left-behind older adults in China face a substantial risk of social isolation, and their social networks show marked population heterogeneity; the Weak-Tie subgroup should be prioritized for identification and intervention. Intergenerational support and its dimensions are significantly and differentially associated with social network type: the positive association of emotional support is the most stable; financial support shows an inverted-U association, with current levels generally below the inflection point; and instrumental care is not significant in the model, its role possibly affected by this population's long-term living apart from children. These findings suggest that family intergenerational support—especially emotional connection—should be incorporated into intervention strategies, while advocating moderate rather than maximal financial support, and exploring the role of intergenerational ties in promoting active aging.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the survey was conducted only in Anhui Province, with participants mainly from northern Anhui; although the sample size was adequate for preliminary analysis, it may not fully reflect the diverse circumstances of rural left-behind older adults in Anhui or other regions, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic prevention-and-control period. It should be noted that at the time of collection, rural livelihoods in the local area have attained a relatively stable status. Pandemic control may have to some extent raised the absolute level of older adults' social isolation; however, because this effect was broadly similar across all participants, the core relationships of interest in this study—the classification of network types and the associations between the dimensions of intergenerational support and network type (including the inverted-U association of financial support)—are unlikely to have been systematically distorted. Therefore, the pandemic context may affect the absolute estimates of isolation rates but should not alter the main conclusions of this study; future research could replicate this under normal conditions to further confirm the robustness of the results. In addition, when examining the influence of intergenerational support dimensions on social networks, this study did not explore in depth the gender differences in the provision and need for intergenerational support. Furthermore, because the study focused on left-behind older adults living apart from their children for long periods, the observed effect of instrumental care may be attenuated; future research should include non-left-behind older adults to more clearly clarify the relationship between instrumental care and social networks.
