Optimizing camera placement in multi-view markerless motion capture: a validation study against gold-standard kinematics for static and dynamic tasks
Authors: Ali Sahraneshin Samani, Mohsen Razeghi, Mohammad Abdoli-Eramaki, Alireza Choobineh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the optimal camera configuration for a markerless motion capture system, which may offer a lower-cost alternative to laboratory based systems. It validated the system’s performance against a gold standard during both static postures and a dynamic lifting task, addressing a key gap regarding the impact of camera arrangement on measurement validity. Six unique stereo camera pairs were calibrated for 3D reconstruction using MediaPipe. Joint angles were computed, and agreement was assessed using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), and Bland-Altman analysis.ResultsSystem performance was highly sensitive to camera geometry. The optimal configuration (approximately 45 degrees separation) demonstrated excellent agreement for both static (ICC = 0.851, MAE = 9.28 degrees) and dynamic (ICC = 0.823, MAE = 12.92 degrees) tasks. Major peripheral joints (hips, knees, shoulders) showed good to excellent reliability (ICC = 0.73–0.92), while neck showed only moderate agreement (ICC = 0.56–0.66). Configurations with very wide baselines (greater than 90 degrees) performed poorly (ICC < 0.40). A two-camera setup with a narrow baseline can yield clinically useful joint angle estimates for major joints, providing practical guidance for affordable motion-capture deployment in biomechanics, ergonomics, and rehabilitation.

Introduction

Accurate 3D human motion measurement is essential in biomechanics, ergonomics, rehabilitation, and sports science [1–7]. The gold standard remains marker-based optical motion capture (e.g., Qualisys, Vicon), which tracks reflective markers with infrared cameras [8–10]. However, its high cost and limited portability restrict its use in large-scale or field studies [9, 11–15]. These practical limits have renewed interest in markerless approaches that exploit advances in computer vision and deep learning to estimate anatomical keypoints directly from video. These approaches promise more cost-effective, simpler, and faster data collection with reduced participant burden [8, 12, 16]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis underscore this potential: while markerless systems show strong validity and reliability for spatiotemporal parameters and major joints (e.g., hip, knee), there is greater variability in accuracy, especially for joints subject to occlusion or depth estimation errors [17].

Markerless methods are commonly implemented either as single-camera (monocular) pose estimators or as multi-camera systems that triangulate 3D points from synchronized 2D detections. Frameworks like MediaPipe and OpenPose estimate human pose from video; for example, MediaPipe’s BlazePose infers 33 landmarks in real time, suiting it to low-resource hardware [18, 19]. Single-view systems remain fundamentally limited by depth ambiguity and occlusion [20, 21]. Synchronized multi-view setups reduce these limitations by leveraging stereophotogrammetry principles [22, 23], yet they introduce new practical choices for camera number, baseline, height and orientation that directly affect both geometric triangulation quality and the robustness of 2D detections across views. Several recent studies have begun validating multi-camera markerless systems for 3D joint kinematics. Notably, direct validation efforts (from multi-camera AI frameworks demonstrating high accuracy and real-time performance to clinical dual-camera evaluations for lower-limb kinematics) report mixed results that depend strongly on camera geometry, joint of interest, and movement speed [16, 17, 24–28], reported accuracy therefore varies across joints and movement types and key questions remain about how camera arrangement affects performance for both static and dynamic tasks.

Although camera placement and calibration are mature topics in the stereophotogrammetry literature, those results assume reliable, precise detection of correspondences across views (e.g., reflective markers or easily trackable features) an assumption that does not hold for learned keypoint detectors whose performance varies with viewpoint. Markerless systems based on learned keypoint detectors introduce a different practical constraint: 2D detections are view-dependent and can be missed or systematically shifted depending on viewpoint, occlusion, illumination, and subject appearance. Calibration and field-of-view simulation tools ensure correct geometric relations between cameras, but they do not guarantee that a pose-estimation model will produce consistent, accurate 2D points in each view. Consequently, optimal camera configurations for markerless multi-camera systems must balance classical triangulation geometry with robustness of per-view keypoint detection, a trade-off we refer to as the detection constraint. Unlike marker-based systems where field-of-view simulators can predict marker visibility, no existing tool can predict whether a pose estimation model will detect landmarks reliably from a given camera angle, as this depends on the model’s training distribution. Previous studies on camera placement largely overlooked this trade-off and were constrained by narrow scope, small sample sizes, and a limited range of postures [29, 30]. The objective of this study was to empirically evaluate the triangulation–detection trade-off in accessible two-camera configurations, and accordingly identify configurations that maximize joint-angle agreement with a laboratory gold standard, and translate these findings into actionable guidance for practical, field-friendly motion capture.

Main text

Method

A concurrent-validity study was performed to compare joint angles computed by a custom multi-camera markerless system against a gold-standard, marker-based system (ProReflex, Qualisys Track Manager® Ltd., Gothenburg, Sweden). An experienced operator placed passive reflective markers on participants according to a standard full-body marker set protocol.

In order to test six different stereo pair configurations systematically, an eight-camera experimental apparatus array was designed. The system comprised of eight identical consumer-grade HD cameras (Dahua Technology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China), each recording at 15 frames per second (fps). The cameras were arranged in a fixed configuration around the participant (Fig. 1), mounted on tripods and oriented toward the center of the capture volume at a distance of approximately 3 m. For a comprehensive view, seven cameras were positioned at waist height (~ 1 m), while one camera (Camera 2) was elevated to eye level (~ 1.6 m) to assess the effect of height variation on the measurement parameters. This methodological approach allowed direct comparison of camera geometries to identify the optimal configuration for a practical two-camera system.

The choice of 15 frames per second (fps) was deliberate and based on a conservative “worst-case scenario” validation approach. We intentionally selected the lowest-cost hardware tier potentially available in resource-limited settings including rural clinics, workplace ergonomic assessments using existing security cameras, and consumer smartphone video (which often defaults to 15–30 fps). If the system performs acceptably at 15 Hz, users can be confident that upgrading to higher frame rates will only improve performance. Given that frame rate directly influences processing demands on pose estimation algorithms like Mediapipe, this conservative choice also establishes a more robust setting that eliminates the need for high-performance systems.

For the functional movements examined (lifting, bending, reaching), biomechanical studies indicate that the dominant frequency content for functional movements such as lifting, bending, and reaching is below 10 Hz [31–33]. This aligns with standard practice in observational ergonomics, where methods like REBA and RULA are routinely applied to standard video at 15–30 fps. For applications requiring analysis of high-velocity movements (e.g., sports biomechanics), we recommend higher frame rates (≥ 60 Hz) while maintaining the optimal camera configuration validated in this study.

From the eight cameras, six stereo pairs were defined for 3D reconstruction; Each pair was calibrated individually: first, using images of a planar checkerboard pattern moved throughout the field of view [34]. Next, stereo calibration was performed to determine their relative extrinsic parameters (rotation and translation). We repeated this calibration process for every subject’s setup to account for any changes in camera placement or orientation.

We used Google’s MediaPipe Pose Landmarker to extract body landmarks. It was selected for several reasons. First, it is designed for real-time inference on consumer hardware, aligning with our goal of developing a practical, deployable system for field applications. Second, it is open-source and freely available, promoting accessibility and reproducibility. Third, its underlying BlazePose model is pre-trained on diverse datasets encompassing varied body shapes, clothing, and poses, enhancing robustness. Fourth, it provides 33 body landmarks sufficient to compute all joint angles of interest. Fifth, recent studies have validated MediaPipe for ergonomic and biomechanical applications, reporting accuracy levels suitable for such tasks [29, 30, 35]. Finally, compared to alternatives like OpenPose, MediaPipe offers a favorable balance of landmark accuracy and computational efficiency, which is advantageous in multi-camera setups. We used the default model complexity (Complexity 1), which provides an appropriate trade-off between speed and precision for the functional movements examined. We acknowledge that algorithm choice can influence detection robustness and kinematic accuracy; therefore, comparing multiple pose-estimation backends (e.g., OpenPose and others) under identical camera geometries is an important direction for future work.

Finally, nine joint angles (left/right elbows and shoulders, left/right hips and knees, and neck) were calculated in 3D space as the vector angle between adjacent body segments (e.g., the shoulder joint angle from shoulder, elbow and hip landmarks).

Data processing and analysis

Six stereo pairs were selected from the eight cameras for triangulation. Camera pairing was constrained by the calibration requirement, which necessitated an overlapping field of view between cameras to simultaneously image the checkerboard target. Consequently, opposing camera pairs (e.g., Cameras 4 & 7 in Fig. 1) were excluded, leaving only six viable pairs.

The Qualisys marker-based data were downsampled to 15 Hz to match the video frame rate. Because the two systems operated on independent hardware clocks, synchronization was performed, after which nine joint angles were computed.

The system demonstrated excellent tracking for major lower and upper limb joints. During static poses, knee and hip joints were measured with high precision (MAE: 3.1–7.9°) and good-to-excellent reliability (ICC: 0.73–0.91). Shoulder joints also showed excellent ICC values (> 0.91). Joints critical to the lifting task, such as the hips and shoulders, maintained very good to excellent reliability during dynamic motion (ICC: 0.85–0.91), confirming the system’s validity for assessing compound, functional motions. The neck joint angle proved challenging in both tasks, showing only moderate agreement (Static ICC = 0.56; Dynamic ICC = 0.66). This indicates a current limitation in precisely estimating cervical kinematics from the implemented pose estimation model under these testing conditions.

Discussion

Camera Pair 1–3 achieved good-to-excellent agreement with marker-based kinematics for major joints, yielding the highest ICC and lowest MAE (ICC: 0.823–0.851; MAE: 9.28°–12.92°). However, performance was highly dependent on camera configuration.

This performance aligns with prior markerless validation studies, which report joint angle errors of 5°–15° for depth cameras [37, 38], and ICCs of 0.17–0.92 for OpenPose-based systems [22]. For key lower-limb joints (e.g., hip ICC: 0.90; knee ICC: 0.91), our results meet or exceed those of consumer-grade depth sensors, demonstrating that a calibrated two-camera setup can rival more expensive proprietary solutions.

The strong performance in dynamic tasks for lifting-critical joints—hips (ICC: 0.90–0.91) and shoulders (ICC: 0.85–0.87)—is particularly noteworthy. This indicates that the optimal camera configuration reliably tracks the complex, multi-joint coordination required for functional activities, validating its potential for real-world applications in ergonomics and rehabilitation.

The findings demonstrate that simply using multiple cameras is insufficient; their placement must be strategically optimized. The ~ 2.5x increase in MAE between the best- and worst-performing configurations provides a crucial design guideline: camera pairs should be positioned to minimize their angle to the primary planes of motion. For example, for sagittal-plane–dominant activities like lifting, a moderate, front-to-parallel camera separation (~ 45°) is superior to a very wide baseline. This evidence-based guideline for configuration is a key contribution to the methodology of affordable, multi-camera motion capture.

The joint-level analysis reveals a consistent pattern: the system excels at tracking major peripheral joints but shows only moderate reliability for axial skeleton segments. High-Reliability Joints were the knees, hips, shoulders, and elbows. The good-to-excellent reliability (ICC > 0.75) for these joints can be attributed to two factors. First, the anatomical landmarks defining these joints are relatively superficial and well-defined, making them reliably detected by the pose estimation model. Second, their motion primarily occurs in planes well-captured by the optimal camera setup.

The lower agreement for the neck (static ICC = 0.56) is primarily attributable to an anatomical definition discrepancy. The markerless system estimates the “neck” as the midpoint between the shoulder landmarks, while the gold-standard system uses the physical C7 marker. This mismatch, combined with the cervical spine’s limited range of motion during lifting, resulted in higher relative errors compared to high-excursion limb joints.

The “neck” keypoint used here approximates the cervical spine base and is inherently limited for calculating angles relative to the torso, especially during complex movements. This finding aligns with existing literature reporting similar difficulties in estimating detailed ankle kinematics from sparse keypoint data [22, 39]. Both joints present substantial challenges due to their anatomical complexity and multiple degrees of freedom. Consequently, while the current system provides utility for gross postural assessment, its moderate cervical spine reliability precludes its recommendation for applications requiring high-fidelity measurement.

A key novel finding of this study is the breakdown in performance at wide baselines. Classic stereophotogrammetry dictates that wider baselines (approaching 90°) minimize the geometric dilution of precision (GDOP), theoretically improving depth accuracy [40]. However, our data show the opposite: Pair 3–7 (~ 135°) failed significantly. This suggests a fundamental constraint unique to markerless motion capture using computer vision and machine-learning methods: the Detection Constraint. For triangulation to work, the neural network must identify the same anatomical landmark in both camera views. If cameras are placed too far apart, one inevitably views the subject from a lateral or obscured angle where the pose estimation model fails to detect the keypoint reliably. We hypothesize that this failure occurs because the models are predominantly trained on frontal-view images, causing their accuracy to dwindle as the viewpoint diverges. Consequently, the optimal ~ 45° configuration represents a “sweet spot”—wide enough to provide meaningful depth data, yet narrow enough to ensure robust keypoint detection in both views.

A key conceptual contribution of this work is to highlight the detection constraint that differentiates markerless ML-based multi-camera systems from classical stereophotogrammetry. Traditional camera-placement recommendations prioritize triangulation geometry (e.g., larger baselines to reduce depth uncertainty) under the assumption that image features or markers are reliably detected across views. In markerless systems, however, 2D keypoint detectors can fail or shift depending on viewpoint, self-occlusion, and local appearance changes. As a result, camera geometries that improve triangulation can simultaneously increase the likelihood of asymmetric or missing detections, thereby degrading overall 3D reconstruction accuracy. Our empirical results demonstrate this trade-off: some wide-baseline configurations produced better theoretical triangulation metrics but worse joint-angle accuracy due to reduced cross-view keypoint consistency.

This interaction has practical implications. For practitioners aiming to deploy markerless multi-camera systems outside well-controlled laboratory settings, we recommend choosing camera placements that balance geometric triangulation with robust, overlapping fields-of-view that preserve consistent 2D detections across cameras. The configurations evaluated here provide actionable guidance for accessible setups: moderate baseline angles deliver reliable joint-angle estimates in typical ergonomic tasks while avoiding the detection failures observed at more extreme baselines.

A practical two-camera deployment using consumer-grade hardware can substantially reduce equipment and infrastructure requirements compared with laboratory marker-based systems. We therefore describe the approach as potentially more accessible and lower-cost rather than asserting strict cost parity. A formal cost–benefit analysis (hardware, setup time, licensing, and training) was beyond this study’s scope and is recommended for future work.

While camera-placement and calibration are well established for marker-based stereophotogrammetry, our study addresses a different, application-oriented problem: how to configure consumer-grade, two-camera markerless systems so they deliver practically useful 3D joint angles in real and resource-limited settings. Unlike prior work that focused on laboratory hardware or simulations assuming perfect correspondences, we systematically evaluated six stereo geometries using inexpensive cameras, validated results against a laboratory gold standard (Qualisys), and quantified per-landmark cross-view detection consistency. This combination (accessible hardware, systematic geometry comparison, gold-standard validation, and empirical detection-rate analysis) is what differentiates our contribution from previous camera-placement and calibration studies.

Laboratory marker-based systems (e.g., Qualisys) remain the reference standard for high-precision kinematic measurement and detailed biomechanical modelling. While marker-based laboratory systems can technically be paired with machine-learning algorithms to perform markerless tracking, such implementations still rely on fixed infrared camera arrays, proprietary hardware, dedicated laboratory space, and trained technical operators. In contrast, the present system was designed specifically for portability and deployability using consumer-grade cameras and open-source software. The practical benefit therefore lies not in outperforming laboratory gold standards, but in enabling motion analysis in environments where such infrastructure is unavailable. This distinction is critical for applications in workplace ergonomics, community-based rehabilitation, rural clinics, and large-scale field research. Our study is aimed at this application-oriented scenario validating accessible, two-camera markerless setups for practical use rather than replacing laboratory pipelines for high-fidelity biomechanical modelling.

We acknowledge that reducing hardware count or improving algorithmic performance could also be a cost-effective route to accessible motion capture. Single-camera and algorithm-centric strategies reduce hardware cost and setup complexity, but they face fundamental limitations (depth ambiguity, increased sensitivity to occlusion) that multi-camera triangulation can mitigate for true 3D joint-angle estimation [20, 21]. Conversely, multi-camera consumer solutions increase hardware needs but can provide robust stereo cues and redundancy across views when configured correctly. A formal cost–benefit and accuracy comparison (including time-to-setup, operator training, licensing, maintenance, and algorithm development costs) between single-camera, multi-camera, and hybrid approaches was beyond the present study’s scope and is recommended for future work. Importantly, our findings furthers that future comparison by identifying camera geometries that allow a minimal (two-camera) consumer setup to produce practically useful joint-angle estimates.

Conclusion

The results demonstrate that with an optimal camera configuration specifically, a narrow baseline of ~ 45 and targeted post-processing, the system achieves measurement agreement with a gold-standard system suitable for assessing major peripheral joints in a wide range of applications. While not a replacement for high-end laboratory systems in all scenarios, it provides a validated, accessible alternative for group-level research, clinical screening, and ergonomic assessment.

Limitations

First, the 15 Hz frame rate limits capture of high-velocity movements. This was a deliberate ‘worst-case scenario’ validation using the lowest-cost hardware tier potentially available in resource-limited settings. For the functional movements examined (lifting, bending, reaching), the dominant frequency content is below 10 Hz, making 15 Hz theoretically sufficient. For applications requiring analysis of rapid movements, we recommend higher frame rates. Second, the study included only male participants. Differences in anthropometry, body composition, or clothing in female populations could influence keypoint detection reliability. Future studies should validate these findings across diverse demographic groups including women, children, older adults, and individuals with varied body morphologies. Moreover, this validation was performed in a controlled laboratory environment. Future work should assess system performance in cluttered, real-world conditions.

We note that the pose-estimation algorithm influences detection robustness and 3D accuracy; our results are specific to the MediaPipe backend used here and future studies should compare multiple algorithms to quantify how algorithmic differences interact with camera geometry.

Pose estimation systems rely on AI-trained models, whose performance in multi-camera configurations can be compromised if they are not adequately trained on non-frontal views. Since these systems depend on images from varied perspectives to achieve spatial accuracy through triangulation, enhancing their robustness in multi-view settings requires training models with datasets that include diverse non-frontal viewpoints. This focus could substantially improve 3D reconstruction accuracy.
