Nonpharmacologic interventions for managing distress, anxiety, and depression for patients with cancer and their family caregivers: A systematic review and meta‐analysis
Authors: Lisa C. Schiess, Lixin L. Song, Sabine Schädelin, Thomas Fürst, Corinne Urech, Christopher R. Friese, Maria C. Katapodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs more cancer treatments take place in outpatient settings, family caregivers provide essential care and emotional support over long periods. Unaddressed patient and caregiver psychological distress can lead to worse outcomes, reflecting the challenges of managing complex care demands in the home setting. This systematic review and meta‐analysis examined how well nonpharmacologic interventions (NPIs) reduce distress, anxiety, and depression in adult patients with solid tumors and their family caregivers. The authors included 68 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with a total of 11,987 participants. NPIs were characterized as psychoeducation, therapeutic counseling, skills training, or behavior modification. By using random‐effects models (Hedges g), they observed that NPIs significantly reduced patient distress at both 0.0–3.0 months (g = 0.13) and 3.1–6.0 months (g = 0.18), but NPIs did not significantly reduce caregiver distress. In the short term (0.0–3.0 months), NPIs also significantly reduced anxiety (g = 0.31 for patients; g = 0.15 for caregivers) and depression (g = 0.28 for patients; g = 0.25 for caregivers). Subgroup analyses examined the impact of patient and caregiver characteristics along with NPI type, delivery format, dose, and duration. NPIs delivered jointly to patients and caregivers yielded significant effects that were higher compared with NPIs delivered separately. NPIs can help manage distress in patients and reduce anxiety and depression in both patients and caregivers. However, the lack of long‐term follow‐up limits our understanding of their impact on patients and caregivers with prolonged or delayed psychological symptoms (PROSPERO registration number CRD42024536629).

INTRODUCTION

Cancer carries a substantial societal burden, with the World Health Organization reporting approximately 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths worldwide in 2022. 1 By 2050, the annual incidence of cancer is estimated to exceed 35 million new cases. 1 The rising global cancer incidence coincides with an expanding role of outpatient settings for care delivery, with family caregivers assuming essential responsibilities for an average 8 hours per day for 14 months. 2 , 3 Family caregivers frequently face emotional, physical, and social challenges, such as uncertainty about the future, feelings of loss, increased role demands, 4 , 5 fatigue, sleep disturbance, and social isolation. 6 , 7 , 8 Prolonged and unmanaged psychological and physical burden may lead to psychological distress 9 , 10 and symptoms of anxiety and/or depression 11 that often co‐occur with a cancer diagnosis. 12 , 13 , 14 Given the high prevalence of distress, 5 anxiety, and depression 15 among patients and family caregivers, the American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer recommended that cancer programs should screen patients for distress and include family caregivers as stakeholders in cancer care. 16 However, resources to address distress, anxiety, and depression in patients with cancer and family caregivers are often inadequate, perpetuating adverse effects of the disease and its treatments 5 and increasing the estimated annual €199 billion global economic burden of cancer. 17 , 18

Nonpharmacologic interventions (NPIs) have been tested for distress, anxiety, and depression in patients with cancer and their caregivers. 9 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Although the number of randomized clinical trials (RCTs) has increased notably over the past 20 years, findings have been inconsistent because heterogeneity of NPIs, modes of delivery, and assessment time points. In 2019, Northouse et al. 19 reported that NPIs showed small but significant effects on caregiver distress and anxiety beyond 6 months, a finding that was partially confirmed by Pang et al. 21 in 2022 for patients, but not for caregivers, and was not replicated by Dhumal et al. 9 in 2023. Northouse et al. 19 did not find any effect of NPIs on caregiver depression, a finding that was confirmed by Pang et al. 21 The meta‐analyses by Pang et al. 21 and Dhumal et al. 9 did not report the timeframe for assessing NPI effects, which may partially explain these inconsistencies.

As the number of NPIs for managing distress, anxiety, and depression for patients with cancer and family caregivers continues to grow, with contemporary trends favoring self‐directed technologies (e.g., mobile applications) compared with in‐person or clinic‐based approaches, 20 ongoing assessment is essential to guide the allocation of resources. The objective of this systematic review and meta‐analysis is to provide actionable insights to clinicians and policymakers on optimizing the management of distress, anxiety, and depression for patients with cancer and their family caregivers and to examine NPI types and patient and caregiver characteristics that may moderate outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Overview

The study was conducted according to current guidelines 26 , 27 following the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses statement. 28 The research protocol was registered with the international Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO identifier CRD42024536629) and provides a detailed description of the project.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were established based on the PICOS (population, interventions, control, outcomes) format. The population included both adult patients with cancer (any type of solid tumor, any stage) and their adult family caregivers. Interventions included only nonpharmacologic modalities, addressing both patients with cancer and their family caregivers, either as dyads (patient and caregiver together) or separately. The control group received either active control, attention control, usual care, or was wait‐listed. Outcomes were patient and caregiver psychological distress, anxiety, and depression. Only RCTs were included to ensure similar methodological rigor and the reliability of conclusions. Studies targeting children with cancer were excluded to avoid heterogeneity because of parent–child dynamics. Studies conducted on patients with hematologic malignancies were also excluded given the variability in treatment and prognosis compared with solid tumors. 29 No other restrictions were applied.

Search strategy and study selection process

An information specialist systematically searched Medline (Ovid), Cochrane CENTRAL, APA PsycInfo (Ovid), and CINAHL (EBSCOhost) using text words and database‐specific subject headings from inception to December 11, 2023 (CINAHL) and January 27, 2025 (all other databases). ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/) and the World Health Organization's International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (WHO ICTRP; https://www.who.int/tools/clinical‐trials‐registry‐platform) were also searched to January 27, 2025. Full search details are provided in the Supporting Methods. References were imported into Covidence (Covidence Systematic Review Software, Veritas Health Innovation; available at www.covidence.org), deduplicated, and screened by two independent reviewers; conflicts were resolved by a senior team member.

Data extraction and risk‐of‐bias assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted study characteristics and outcomes by intervention group and follow‐up period for patients and caregivers. For each outcome, we used validated measures reported in the RCT. When multiple measures reported on the same outcome, we chose the most specific measure or the global score for measures that had multiple subscales. In RCTs with several experimental arms, we selected the arm with the most effective intervention; if no control existed, we selected the intervention most similar to usual care as the control. Outcomes were grouped by follow‐up: time 1 (≤3 months), time 2 (3–6 months), and time 3 (>6 months). Missing information was requested from authors (30‐day response window). When multiple publications reported outcomes from the same RCT, data were extracted as a single study. The risk of bias (RoB) was assessed by two reviewers with the Cochrane RoB tool (version 2). 30

Synthesis and statistical analyses

Data were analyzed using meta‐analytic techniques. 31 , 32 Standardized mean differences, representing the effect size between treatment and control groups, were calculated using the Hedges g unbiased approach 31 from means, standard deviations, sample sizes, and differences in mean scores, standardized by the postscore standard deviation. Data were pooled using a random‐effects model, with studies weighted by the inverse of their sampling variance, to account for heterogeneity in NPI content, delivery format, and sample characteristics. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q statistic, with a significant Q indicating variability in effect sizes beyond chance.

Subgroup analyses for categorical variables and meta‐regression models that assess continuous variables as covariates explored potential sources of heterogeneity, including NPI type (see Results, below), delivery format (dyad together or separate; face‐to‐face, remote, hybrid), dose and duration (number of sessions, overall minutes, number of weeks), rates of attrition and enrollment, patient and caregiver characteristics (cancer stage, cancer type, sex), study design factors (multicenter/single‐center; RoB), time trends (years since publication), and type of control condition (active control, attention control, usual care, waitlist).

To assess the robustness of pooled effect sizes, sensitivity analyses used the leave‐one‐study‐out approach 33 for each outcome and follow‐up time point. Publication bias was assessed with funnel plots and Egger t‐test, with significance based on one‐tailed p values. Analyses were conducted in Comprehensive Meta‐Analysis version 4 software (BioStat, Inc.) 34 and R 4.5.0 (meta package; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Effect sizes were categorized according to the Cohen d as small (g = 0.20), medium (g = 0.50), and large (g = 0.80). 35 Certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach. 36 Ratings did not influence study inclusion but were considered in the interpretation of findings.

RESULTS

An initial search retrieved 16,550 records from databases and registers (Figure 1). After removing 4780 duplicates and 2726 non‐RCTs identified by automation tools, 37 9044 titles and abstracts were screened. Six publications reporting outcomes from three different RCTs 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 were considered as single studies. Twelve studies (13 reports because of one pair of publications from the same RCT 42 , 43 ) lacked sufficient data or had incompatible designs (e.g., re‐randomization, multiple overlapping intervention arms) and were included only in the narrative synthesis (see Supporting Methods, Tables S1 and S2 for full details of studies excluded from the meta‐analysis). Additional data requested from six authors were obtained for two studies. This meta‐analysis includes 68 RCTs (70 reports because of two pairs of reports from two different RCTs) published between 1983 and 2025, all with complete data for calculating effect sizes.

Study characteristics

Table 1 outlines the intervention types, components, and delivery formats. Most RCTs were conducted in the United States (n = 40), followed by China (n = 9) and Canada (n = 5), and were published in the past 5 years (n = 39). Most RCTs assessed outcomes within the first 3 months (n = 59), whereas fewer evaluated intermediate (3.1–6.0 months) and long‐term (>6 months) outcomes (n = 22 and n = 14, respectively). Information on participant compensation was inconsistently reported. Among RCTs that reported compensation (n = 25), the median amount was $40 per assessment (range, $20–$50 per assessment; n = 18) and $100 total for completing all assessments (range, $25–$700; n = 24), typically as cash or gift cards. One trial reported nonmonetary compensation (prepaid long‐distance telephone access), and three provided gifts (e.g., application subscriptions or wearables) in addition to monetary compensation.

Nonpharmacologic intervention characteristics

Classification of nonpharmacologic interventions

The 68 RCTs included 74 primary NPIs as their experimental arms. The number of NPIs (k = 74) exceeds the number of reports (N = 68) because five included more than one experimental arm. NPIs were categorized into four primary types based on their core therapeutic approach and delivery focus.

Psychoeducation (k = 9) focused primarily on increasing knowledge through structured information about cancer, treatment, symptom management, and psychosocial impacts. Delivery was primarily didactic and unidirectional, aiming to foster understanding and, to a lesser extent, emotional engagement or behavior change.

Therapeutic counseling (k = 33) emphasized active dialogue between the counselor (e.g., psychologist, nurse, social worker) and participants to address the psychosocial impact of cancer, build a supportive relationship, and encourage reflection, expression, and meaning‐making around illness and caregiving.

Skills training (k = 20) was interactive and aimed at strengthening practical abilities, such as improving symptom management, and/or psychological skills, including coping, problem‐solving, stress management, communication, and adaptability to make caregiving more effective.

Behavior modification (k = 6) included NPIs that targeted sustained lifestyle changes, including physical activity, nutrition, or sleep, using behavior change strategies, such as goal‐setting and self‐monitoring.

The most common combination of NPI types involved both psychoeducation and skills training and was identified in 26 RCTs. The majority of NPIs (k = 58) were developed based on a theoretical framework, most commonly cognitive behavioral theory (k = 8), social cognitive theory (k = 6), and theory of stress and coping (k = 5).

Content of nonpharmacologic interventions

The content of NPIs focused on three areas:

Patient caregiving was found in k = 51 protocols and provided practical information and skills for caregiving tasks, such as changing dressings or managing ostomy bags.

Marital/family care was found in k = 57 protocols and provided patients and caregivers with tools to handle family and marital/relationship concerns, including effective communication, teamwork, and maintaining an intimate relationship.

Self‐care was found in k = 65 protocols for patients and k = 64 protocols for caregivers and provided strategies to manage one’s well‐being, maintain social support, and access resources.

Dose and duration

The dose and duration of each NPI were calculated as the total number of hours (median, 6.0 hours; range, 0.43–30.0 hours), the total number of sessions (median, 6.0 sessions; range, 1.0–56.0 sessions), and the duration in weeks (median, 6.0 weeks; range, 0.14–27.0 weeks).

Mode of delivery

Most protocols were delivered to patients and caregivers together simultaneously (k = 50). When delivered separately (k = 10), content was identical in one half of the studies (k = 5) or was tailored by duration (k = 4) or by both content and duration (k = 1). In protocols that combined joint and separate delivery (k = 8), the content was usually identical (k = 5) or was tailored by duration (k = 2) or by both content and duration (k = 1). Most NPIs were delivered through guided communication technology (phone or videocall conference; k = 43), followed by face‐to‐face visits (k = 39). Among protocols that were delivered face‐to‐face, the largest proportion took place in an unspecified location (k = 23), followed by clinical settings (k = 18), or at patients' homes (k = 6). Self‐directed communication technology, such as mobile applications, was used in k = 18 protocols. Group meetings were the least common delivery format (k = 6). Approximately one in three protocols (k = 21) combined face‐to‐face sessions with additional guided communication technology contact.

Interveners and fidelity

Most protocols were delivered by psychologists (k = 21) or nurses (k = 21), followed by social workers (k = 17). Assessment of fidelity was mentioned in k = 47 protocols, e.g., through NPI manuals and checklists, recording and random review of sessions, or regular supervision with feedback to the interventionists.

Participant characteristics

Enrollment rates varied from 10.0% to 94.0%, with a median of 55.0%, although this information was not reported for all RCTs (Table 2). Caregivers were identified by the patient and were subsequently invited to participate in most RCTs (n = 29). Alternatively, both individuals of the dyad were approached e.g., during clinic visits (n = 25). Attrition rates at 3‐month follow‐up ranged from 1.0% to 52.0% (median, 13.0%). For the RCTs that included follow‐up assessments at 3.1–6.0 months and >6.0 months, median attrition rates were 22.0% and 21.0%, respectively. Outcomes assessed after the patient's death were not included in this meta‐analysis.

Patients with cancer

The 68 RCTs involved a total of 5795 patients (median, 64 patients per study; range, 14–302 patients per study). Among the RCTs reporting sex (n = 64), 53.6% of patients were female. Among the RCTs reporting race/ethnicity (n = 32), patients were predominantly Caucasian (72.6%), followed by Hispanic (13.6%), African American (7.1%), and Asian (1.6%). Most common cancer types were breast (27.7%), colorectal (18.1%), and lung (16.3%) cancers. Most RCTs included patients with one type of cancer (64.7% homogeneous), whereas 35.3% included multiple types of cancer. Cancer stage was reported in 41 RCTs: 62.1% included patients with stage 0–III disease; 32.5% included those with stage IV disease; and, for 5.4% of RCTs, this information was missing.

Family caregivers

The 68 RCTs included a total of 6192 family caregivers (median, 61 family caregivers per study; range, 14–441 family caregivers per study). Caregiver sex was reported in 64 RCTs. Most were caregivers were female (57.6%) and spouses (79.7%). The median age of caregivers was 51.7 years (range, 39.5–66.6 years). Among the studies reporting race/ethnicity (n = 35), caregivers were predominantly Caucasian (81.4%), followed by African American (6.3%), Hispanic (5.6%), and Asian (1.4%).

Publication bias

The Egger t‐test detected no funnel plot asymmetry for all outcomes at 0.0–3.0 and 3.1–6.0 months (Table 3). Possible publication bias was observed for patient distress and for caregiver anxiety and depression at >6.0 months, although this may reflect small‐study effects or heterogeneity, both of which can also contribute to funnel plot asymmetry detected the an Egger t‐test. 26 The certainty of evidence (GRADE [Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation] assessments; see Table S3) was generally moderate for short‐term outcomes and was low to very low for longer term effects, mainly because of RoB, heterogeneity, and imprecision (see Table S4).

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

We also explored whether the effects varied by NPI and participant characteristics. The main findings are summarized here, with detailed results in Table 4. The leave‐one‐study‐out sensitivity analyses confirmed that no single study changed the overall pattern of results at the three time points.

Therapeutic counseling appeared to be associated with significant reductions in anxiety among patients and in depression among both patients and caregivers. Other types of NPIs did not produce significant effects. A higher number of sessions was not linked to better outcomes. When delivered to patients and caregivers together, NPIs significantly reduced distress in patients and anxiety and depression in both groups. NPIs that combined face‐to‐face and remote delivery (mixed formats) significantly reduced patient distress, anxiety, and depression. For caregivers, however, mixed‐format NPIs reduced only depression. Remote interventions significantly reduced patient anxiety and depression, whereas face‐to‐face NPIs reduced caregiver depression. Subgroup analyses by control type did not meaningfully moderate NPI effects. Effect sizes varied across outcomes without a clear pattern, and differences compared with usual care or attention control were inconsistent for patients and caregivers.

Effects did not differ significantly by patient or caregiver sex (female vs. male). Regarding cancer type, patients with colorectal cancer and their caregivers experienced significant reductions in anxiety and depression, and patients with breast cancer experienced significant reductions in anxiety. Stage‐specific analyses indicated benefits among patients with stage 0–III disease for anxiety and caregiver depression, but not among patients with stage IV disease and their caregivers. Meta‐regression indicated that higher attrition was associated with smaller effects for patient and caregiver anxiety and for caregiver depression. The enrollment rate was generally unrelated to NPI effects, except for patient depression.

Both multicenter and single‐center RCTs showed significant reductions in patient and caregiver anxiety and depression. However, single‐center RCTs consistently yielded larger effects than multicenter RCTs, possibly because of more homogeneous samples. NPI effects did not differ based on the year of publication. Finally, there was no consistent association between RoB category and effect magnitude (Figure 8; Figure S2 and Table S5).

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta‐analysis synthesized evidence from 68 RCTs on the effects of NPIs on distress, anxiety, and depression in adult patients with solid tumors and their family caregivers. We focused on NPIs that targeted both patients and caregivers, reflecting the growing body of literature regarding interdependence between patient and caregiver distress. 110 , 111 , 112 , 113 , 141 High caregiver distress is associated with poorer quality of care, 16 greater unplanned health care use, 114 , 115 and higher caregiver symptom burden. 116 We hypothesized that dyadic NPIs may improve psychological outcomes for both parties and may be more feasible to implement. Key findings and implications for clinicians and researchers are summarized below.

Differential effects in patients with cancer and their family caregivers

Across outcomes and timepoints, NPIs showed more consistent benefits for patients than for caregivers, with the strongest effects observed in the short‐term for anxiety and depression. NPIs designed for both patients and caregivers often focus primarily on patients' needs, whereas caregivers receive less attention. 25 Caregivers would continue to have unmet needs without explicit strategies to address their symptoms.

Durability and timing of effects

NPIs showed the strongest effects within 0.0–3.0 months postintervention and generally became nonsignificant afterward, except for patient distress and caregiver depression, which remained significant up to 3.1–6.0 months. The median contact time was approximately 6 hours, likely explaining the short‐term benefits and the limited durability. Although short‐term ΝPI benefits for patients are frequently reported, 24 , 117 , 118 this modest dose may be insufficient to sustain psychological improvements because distress can (re‐)emerge in patients and caregivers during prolonged treatment. 119 Nonetheless, early benefits may remain clinically meaningful despite attenuation over time given the substantial time burden of cancer care for patients and caregivers. 120 Because extended treatment periods may increase delayed psychosocial challenges, NPIs that provide longer‐term support for patients and caregivers are needed to strengthen evidence and improve care.

Moderators of intervention effects

Intervention characteristics

Therapeutic counseling was the most common NPI and produced the largest, statistically significant reductions in patient and caregiver anxiety and depression. However, this finding was not observed for distress. Therapeutic counseling may work by fostering emotional exploration and patient–caregiver dialogue. 121 , 122 In contrast, psychoeducational NPIs are more informational, involving less active dialogue, whereas behavioral change and skills‐based NPIs are usually prescriptive or focus on applied techniques. However, these findings warrant caution because therapeutic counseling is a heterogeneous NPI type and is overrepresented in the current meta‐analysis compared with earlier reviews, which emphasized psychoeducation. 19 , 20

Dose and duration were not associated with outcomes, but delivery format emerged as an important moderator. Hybrid models combining face‐to‐face sessions with remote guidance yielded larger patient effects than either format alone. Caregiver outcomes were less format‐dependent. Participant retention was also an important moderator. Higher attrition was associated with smaller effects, suggesting that retention may also indicate NPI feasibility and effectiveness. Hybrid approaches may combine the benefits of in‐person therapeutic engagement with delivery flexibility. 117 , 123

Cancer types and stages

NPIs produced strong effects in patients with colorectal cancer for almost all outcomes, particularly when colorectal cancer was among the three most common diagnoses in the sample. Effects were also stronger in patients with stage 0–III disease, especially for patient anxiety and caregiver depression, possibly reflecting better prognosis and shorter treatment.

Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data from 2013 to 2022 124 show stage IV diagnoses in 6.0% of patients with breast cancer, 21.8% of those with colorectal cancer, and 47.4% of those with lung cancer. Compared with SEER data, early stage breast, colorectal, and lung cancers were underrepresented in this meta‐analysis. Among patients with stage IV disease, breast cancer was overrepresented, colorectal cancer was aligned with SEER data, and lung cancer was underrepresented. However, this interpretation is limited by underreporting because as 27 RCTs (39.7%) did not report disease stage. Future studies should carefully consider tumor type and stage when setting eligibility criteria.

Strengths and limitations

This review synthesizes NPIs that target patients and family caregivers using rigorous meta‐analytic methods, including prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses across all outcomes and time points. The inclusion of diverse NPI types, delivery formats, and cancer populations enhances generalizability.

Limitations include RoB assessment only for primary outcomes of each RCT. Twelve studies were excluded because of incomplete or incompatible data. Caregivers in excluded studies were younger (see Figure S1), but this likely had minimal impact given their smaller sample (1443 vs. 6192 included in the meta‐analysis). Certainty of evidence ranged from moderate for short‐term to low or very low for longer term outcomes, driven by limited long‐term data and RCT quality.

Generalizability of the current results is limited by the concentration of RCTs in high‐income countries, predominantly female and Caucasian samples, and poor reporting of race/ethnicity outside the United States, which is critical information for promoting cultural relevance in psychosocial oncology. Additional limitations include the exclusion of patients with hematologic malignancies and recruitment of dyads in which both members were willing and able to participate, limiting generalizability to vulnerable patients without caregiver support. The use of pharmacologic treatments for psychological symptoms was addressed only in two RCTs and could not be systematically examined in this analysis. Outcomes assessed after patient death, including bereavement‐related distress, were outside the scope of this analysis. Heterogeneity across studies, including variation in NPI characteristics and duration, reflects a limitation of the current literature that influences the precision of pooled effect estimates and warrants cautious interpretation.

CONCLUSION

Clinical implications

NPI effects for patients were strongest at 0–3 months, with little evidence of sustained benefit thereafter. NPI duration was generally brief, and greater dose was not associated with better outcomes. Effect attenuation may reflect unmeasured factors, such as timing, continuity, or context, rather than the number of sessions and duration. Low‐burden maintenance strategies (e.g., booster contacts or digital follow‐up) may help sustain benefits without adding burden and time toxicity, although these hypotheses should be tested in future studies. Adaptive models with periodic symptom reassessment may allow for tailored care and/or may help allocate resources to patients experiencing more severe symptoms, 125 particularly during prolonged treatment, transition periods, or advanced disease. Psychosocial support is limited by resource constraints. 126 A realistic approach regarding the resources required for NPI implementation would facilitate scaling up in routine practice. 24 Hybrid models combining therapist‐led support with guided or self‐directed technologies may be more feasible options for routine care.

Evidence for scalable, caregiver‐focused NPIs remains limited. Caregiver effects were weaker, in which distress did not improve, and only depression showed sustained benefit beyond 3 months. Although effects were not statistically significant, NPIs may help achieve clinically meaningful outcomes. Given the growing evidence regarding the interdependence of patient and caregiver well‐being 110 , 111 , 112 and caregivers' central role in cancer care, especially in low‐resource settings in the United States and parts of Europe and in low‐income to middle‐income countries, 127 , 128 , 129 , 130 , 131 , 132 , 133 caregivers should be formally recognized as part of the oncology workforce 134 and supported through hybrid, technology‐assisted strategies. Policy‐level strategies (e.g., paid caregiver leave, reimbursement for caregiver education) may also improve patient and caregiver outcomes. Rigorous RCTs are needed to inform reimbursement and scale‐up of patient–caregiver programs.

Research implications

The reach of NPIs must expand to underserved populations, including underrepresented cancer types, patients receiving newer treatments with limited documentation of late‐onset or persistent psychosocial symptoms, male and nonspousal caregivers, and racially, culturally, and socioeconomically diverse populations. Implementation frameworks can guide adaptation and dissemination by addressing contextual, structural, and resource‐related factors.

To address heterogeneous and evolving symptoms of distress, anxiety, and depression, scalable and responsive designs, such as SMART (stepped care, ecological momentary assessments, sequential multiple assignment randomized trials) or hybrid effectiveness‐implementation models, may extend NPI benefits and improve resource allocation. 76 , 135 , 136 , 137 Such models, already successful in other chronic conditions, 138 adjust support dynamically based on need and timing and are well suited to dyadic care, in which patient and caregiver needs may diverge over time. 139 , 140

In summary, structured NPIs can provide short‐term benefits for patients with cancer and modest effects for family caregivers. Given limited resources and uncertain durability beyond 3 months, priority should be given to tailored, low‐burden support and possibly should include maintenance strategies (e.g., boosters, hybrid delivery). Collaboration among the clinician, patient, and family caregiver triad can help integrate these approaches into routine care and achieve the best possible outcomes.
