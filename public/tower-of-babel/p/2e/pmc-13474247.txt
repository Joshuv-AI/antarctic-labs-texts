Ethosuximide-induced lupus erythematosus: Unveiling a rare adverse reaction through systematic review
Authors: Sara Najafi, Mohaddeseh Sadat Alavi, Elham Nejati, Saba Abbasnia, Hamid Reza Sadeghnia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEthosuximide (ESM) is a first-line anti-seizure medication (ASM) for absence seizures and is generally well-tolerated. However, rare immune-mediated adverse reactions like drug-induced lupus erythematosus (DILE) have been reported. This study aimed to systematically review published reports of ESM-induced lupus erythematosus (LE).MethodsA comprehensive search of databases and sources was conducted up to June 2025 to identify eligible case reports and case series. Inclusion was based on predefined diagnostic criteria for DILE. The methodological quality of included studies was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklists. Extracted data included publication characteristics, patient demographics, clinical features, laboratory findings, management, and outcomes.ResultsTwenty-eight publications encompassing 47 patients met inclusion criteria. The median age was 9 years, and 70.4% were female. Most patients presented with systemic manifestations such as arthralgia, fever, rash, and arthritis, with occasional renal and central nervous system involvements. Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were positive in 95% of cases, typically at high titers with a homogeneous pattern. Anti-histone antibodies, characteristic of DILE, were detected in over 85% of tested patients, while anti-dsDNA antibodies were also reported in a notable subset. Discontinuation of ESM led to marked clinical improvement in 80% of patients, with or without adjunctive corticosteroids.ConclusionESM-induced LE, though exceedingly rare, should be suspected in patients on ESM presenting with systemic autoimmune features. Its clinical and serological profiles overlap those of idiopathic systemic lupus erythematosus and classic DILE. Early recognition and prompt discontinuation of ESM typically result in rapid remission and may prevent unnecessary prolonged immunosuppression.

Introduction

Drug-induced lupus erythematosus (DILE) is a rare autoimmune condition triggered by acute or chronic exposure to specific medications. While DILE shares several clinical and serological features with idiopathic systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), important distinctions exist. SLE is a chronic, relapsing-remitting autoimmune disease with multi-organ involvement, whereas DILE typically arises after prolonged drug exposure, follows a milder course, and often resolves after discontinuation of the offending agent [1], [2]. Clinically, DILE most often presents with constitutional and musculoskeletal symptoms such as fever, arthralgia, and arthritis, while major organ involvement (e.g., renal or central nervous system (CNS) manifestations) is relatively rare compared to SLE [3], [4].

Serologically, both conditions are characterized by positive antinuclear antibodies (ANA); however, anti-histone antibodies are found in up to 95% of DILE cases but only in approximately 50–70% of SLE cases [5]. In contrast, anti-dsDNA antibodies, hypocomplementemia, persistently elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, and low complement levels are more characteristic of idiopathic SLE [6]. These distinctions aid in clinical differentiation between the two conditions, although overlap may occur, particularly in anticonvulsant-induced lupus, which can present with broader systemic features.

Diagnosis of DILE generally requires fulfillment of four major criteria: [7] sustained exposure to a suspected medication, [1] at least one clinical feature consistent with SLE, [2] absence of lupus manifestations prior to drug exposure, and [3] improvement or resolution of symptoms following discontinuation of the suspected medication [4]. However, distinguishing DILE from idiopathic SLE remains challenging due to overlapping clinical and serological findings [8].

For the purpose of the present review, three operational diagnostic categories were adopted. Probable DILE refers to cases fulfilling all four standard DILE criteria with anti-histone antibody positivity and resolution following ESM discontinuation [4], [5], [6]. DILE with atypical features denotes cases meeting DILE criteria but exhibiting features unusual for classic drug-induced lupus, such as anti-dsDNA positivity, renal or neurological involvement, or low complement levels [6], [8]. Suspected ESM-triggered SLE describes cases in which ESM exposure may have unmasked or precipitated underlying idiopathic SLE, evidenced by persistent autoantibodies or disease activity after drug withdrawal, or by a clinical and serological profile indistinguishable from juvenile SLE [8]. These categories are applied descriptively throughout the analysis and are not mutually exclusive, reflecting the inherent diagnostic continuum of this condition [4], [8].

The true prevalence of DILE is likely underestimated, as many cases are mild or subclinical and thus under recognized. Nevertheless, DILE is estimated to account for 6–12% of all lupus cases [8], with approximately 15,000–30,000 new cases annually reported in the United States [4]. More than 100 drugs have been implicated in DILE pathogenesis, with hydralazine, isoniazid, and procainamide representing high-risk agents for classic DILE. In pediatric populations, anti-seizure medications (ASMs) are also linked to DILE, although they are generally considered low- or very low-risk [1]. Among these, carbamazepine, phenytoin, ethosuximide, primidone, and valproate have been most frequently reported [6], [9].

Ethosuximide (ESM), a succinimide derivative introduced in 1960, remains a mainstay for the treatment of absence seizures [10]. Its therapeutic effect arises from selective inhibition of low-threshold (T-type) calcium currents in thalamic relay neurons, thereby suppressing thalamocortical oscillations responsible for absence seizure activity. Unlike many other ASMs, ESM has minimal effects on sodium or potassium channels, contributing to its selectivity for absence epilepsy. ESM is primarily metabolized hepatically via cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP3A, with approximately 10–20% excreted unchanged and the remainder eliminated as inactive urinary metabolites [11].

ESM is generally well tolerated, with common adverse effects including gastrointestinal discomfort and drowsiness. Less frequent adverse events include headache, ataxia, and behavioral or mood changes, particularly in pediatric populations. Rare but serious idiosyncratic reactions have also been reported, such as hepatotoxicity, hematologic abnormalities (e.g., agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia), Stevens-Johnson syndrome, and systemic autoimmune reactions such as lupus erythematosus [12]. Although the precise mechanism of ESM-induced lupus erythematosus (LE) remains unclear, one hypothesis proposes that ESM acts as a hapten, binding to host proteins and modifying their antigenicity, thereby triggering autoantibody production and subsequent autoimmune responses [13].

ESM-induced LE was first described in 1968 in the United States, involving three pediatric patients [14]. Since then, numerous case reports have described lupus-like manifestations in patients on ESM therapy, typically emerging several months after treatment initiation. The most common symptoms include fever, rash, and arthralgia, though renal involvement has also been reported [3], [15]. For example, a 10-year-old girl developed nephrotic syndrome and acute tubulointerstitial nephritis after three months of ESM therapy, with complete resolution following drug withdrawal [13]. Other reports have described cardiovascular and pulmonary manifestations such as coronary artery ectasia and pleuritis [16], [17]. Notably, several cases have occurred in children under 12 years of age [7], [18]. A recent pediatric DILE review identified ESM and minocycline as the most frequently implicated agents, accounting for 13 and 12 of 65 reported cases, respectively [19].

Given the rarity of ESM-induced LE and the limited literature available, the clinical spectrum, diagnostic characteristics, and outcomes remain poorly defined. Because its presentation can closely mimic idiopathic SLE, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for drug-induced etiologies in patients receiving anticonvulsants who present with lupus-like symptoms. Most current knowledge is derived from isolated case reports, and no comprehensive systematic synthesis of the literature has yet been published. Therefore, this systematic review aimed to collect and analyze all reported cases of ESM-induced LE to characterize its demographic, clinical, serological, and therapeutic features. By identifying consistent patterns and potential diagnostic clues, this study seeks to enhance early recognition and improve clinical management of ESM-associated autoimmune complications.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The objective of this systematic review was to comprehensively characterize ESM-induced LE, with emphasis on its prevalence, clinical presentation, and laboratory features. The review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [1]. A predefined study protocol was developed and registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251078834. Ethical approval was obtained from Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (MUMS), registration code IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1404.228.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was constructed using two major keyword clusters: “Ethosuximide” and “Lupus”, including their synonyms and related terms, combined with the Boolean operator “AND.” Controlled vocabulary terms, including Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Emtree terms, were used to enhance both sensitivity and specificity. Searches were performed across major electronic databases, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science without any language or publication date restrictions. To identify additional sources, we screened the first thirty pages of Google Scholar and manually reviewed the reference lists of all eligible studies. The final database search was completed in June 2025.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

All retrieved citations were imported into EndNote X10 (Thomson Reuters, New York, NY) for reference management, and duplicates were removed. Title and abstract screening was initially performed by one reviewer (SN), followed by independent full-text assessment by two reviewers (SN and EN). Studies eligible for inclusion were case reports or case series describing lupus erythematosus as an adverse reaction to ethosuximide, meeting the four established diagnostic criteria for drug-induced lupus erythematosus (DILE). Abstract-only publications were also considered if they provided sufficient clinical information.

Studies were excluded if they lacked individual patient-level data, including observational or interventional studies, reviews, letters, dissertations, book chapters, comments, conference abstracts without sufficient details, animal experiments, and mechanistic investigations. Any discrepancies or uncertainties during the selection process were resolved through discussion with an additional expert reviewer.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently evaluated by two reviewers (EN and SA) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklists for case reports and case series. Each checklist item was scored as 1 (fully addressed), 0.5 (partially addressed), or 0 (not addressed). Studies achieving ≥50% of the maximum possible score were considered methodologically acceptable. Any discrepancies in assessment were discussed and resolved in consultation with a third reviewer.

For abstract-only publications, quality appraisal was adapted to account for the inherent reporting limitations of this format. Items relating to detailed methodology, follow-up duration, or laboratory confirmation that could not be assessed from an abstract alone were scored as “unclear” rather than “not applicable,” and a lower completeness threshold was accepted for inclusion, provided the core diagnostic elements (drug exposure, lupus manifestations, and clinical outcome) were reported.

Data extraction

After confirming the eligibility of publications, data were systematically extracted and entered into a pre-designed Microsoft Excel spreadsheet by two reviewers (SN and EN). To ensure accuracy and reliability, all entries were double-checked, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion among the reviewers.

Extracted information included: (i) Publication details: first author, year of publication, country, and study design. (ii) Patient demographics and baseline characteristics related to ESM therapy: age, gender, nationality, underlying diseases, concurrent medications, ESM dosage, serum ESM levels (if reported), treatment duration prior to symptom onset, family history, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) status, and relevant past medical history. (iii) Clinical and paraclinical features of ESM-induced LE: presenting symptoms, vital signs, and laboratory findings including hemoglobin, white blood cell and platelet counts, direct Coombs test, coagulation profile, ESR, CRP, liver function tests (LFTs), thyroid function tests (TFTs), urinalysis (UA), serum creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), albumin, cholesterol, urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, creatinine clearance, and other relevant biochemical markers. Immunological data encompassed ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-histone, anti-Sm, anti-ENA, ANCA, complement components (C3, C4), immunoglobulin levels, lupus erythematosus (LE) cells, and other rheumatologic parameters. Imaging and biopsy findings were also extracted, including chest, abdominal, and brain imaging; echocardiography; and skin or bone marrow biopsy results when available. (iv) Treatment and outcomes: final diagnosis, therapeutic interventions, clinical and laboratory response, resolution following ESM discontinuation, and recurrence of symptoms upon drug withdrawal or rechallenge.

Results

Search and study selection

Following the implementation of the final search strategy and removal of duplicates, a total of 224 records were retrieved from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science. Titles and abstracts were screened for relevance according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Additionally, grey literature sources were searched, and the reference lists of relevant articles and reviews were manually examined to identify any additional eligible studies. After the initial screening, 61 articles were selected for full-text review. Study characteristics

The included studies were published between 1968 and 2025, with the first report of ESM-induced LE appearing in 1968. Geographically, the studies originated from the United States (n = 11), Japan (n = 5), the United Kingdom (n = 3), France (n = 2), and several other countries. Regarding study design, 19 were case reports and 9 were case series, with 9 studies available only in abstract form. Patient demographics and ESM therapy

A total of 47 patients were included in this review, showing a female predominance (33 patients, 70.2%). Among the 31 patients with available age data, the median age was 9 years (range: 1–24 years). Nationality was reported for 17 patients, including 11 Hispanic, 3 African or African American, and one each of Chinese, Japanese, and Lebanese origin.

Underlying medical conditions were documented in 37 patients, all of whom received ESM for seizure management. Of these, 30 were treated for absence seizures, while the remainder had other seizure types, including myoclonic, generalized tonic-clonic, or partial seizures. Two patients had additional comorbidities, obsessive–compulsive disorder (OCD) and chromosomal abnormalities.

Regarding medication history, 27 patients received ESM monotherapy, while 10 were treated with combination therapy alongside other antiseizure medications (ASMs) such as valproic acid (VPA), phenobarbital (PHB), topiramate, hydantoins, or phenytoin. In six patients, prior ASMs were replaced with ESM, whereas in four cases, ESM was added to an existing regimen.

The median ESM dose was 500 mg/day (range: 250–1250 mg/day) (Fig. 2A). Primary clinical manifestations

Among 44 patients with available clinical data, the most common manifestations of ESM-induced LE were arthralgia (n = 28, 63.6%), fever (n = 23, 52.3%), arthritis (n = 15, 34.1%), and malar rash (n = 14, 31.8%). Other cutaneous lesions, excluding malar rash, were also reported in 14 patients (31.8%). Lymphadenopathy was observed in 12 patients (27.3%). Constitutional symptoms, including fatigue, malaise, or generalized weakness, were present in 8 patients (18.2%).

Less frequent findings included gastrointestinal symptoms (n = 7), hepatosplenomegaly (n = 5), weight loss, headache, and edema. Rare manifestations comprised photosensitivity, oral ulcers, alopecia, myalgia, chest pain, and scleroderma-like changes such as Raynaud's phenomenon and digital ulcers. Laboratory and paraclinical manifestation

Among 18 patients with reported complete blood counts (CBCs), 7 (38.9%) demonstrated pancytopenia, 5 (27.8%) exhibited bicytopenia (anemia and leukopenia), 2 had isolated leukopenia (one with accompanying lymphopenia), 1 showed isolated anemia, 1 revealed eosinophilia, and 2 had normal findings. The indirect Coombs test was performed in 10 patients, with 8 (80%) yielding positive results suggestive of autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Bone marrow biopsy was conducted in 7 patients: 3 showed normal findings, 2 demonstrated hypocellularity and hypoplasia, and 2 were consistent with autoimmune aplastic anemia. No cases of coagulopathy were reported.

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was reported in 20 patients and was elevated in 19 (95%), with values ranging from 40 to 186 mm/h. C-reactive protein (CRP) was measured in 7 patients, of whom 4 (57.1%) had elevated levels.

Urinalysis (UA) was performed in 15 patients, showing a wide range of abnormalities. Seven (46.7%) had normal findings, while eight demonstrated proteinuria, four had hematuria, two exhibited granular casts, and one had epithelial cell casts. These abnormalities were indicative of renal involvement, which was confirmed in six cases overall (12.8% of all patients). Serum creatinine (Cr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels were reported in 16 patients; 14 were within normal limits, while one patient each was diagnosed with lupus nephritis and with a combination of nephrotic syndrome and tubulointerstitial nephritis.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels were measured in three patients, all of whom had elevated values. Liver function tests (LFTs) were reported in eight patients, with one case showing elevated transaminases. Thyroid function tests (TFTs) were available for three patients, one of whom demonstrated elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) consistent with autoimmune thyroiditis.

Chest imaging was performed in 15 patients. Eight showed normal findings, while seven (46.7%) had abnormalities, including pleuritis (n = 4), pleural effusion (n = 3), and pneumonia (n = 2). Electroencephalograms (EEGs) were reported in eight patients; five were consistent with absence seizures, and three were normal.

Cardiac evaluations, including echocardiography and electrocardiography, were conducted in 14 patients. Nine showed no abnormalities, while five (35.7%) exhibited cardiac involvement: one with coronary ectasia and carditis, one with aortic incompetence, one with pericardial effusion, one with combined myocarditis and pericarditis, and one with isolated pericarditis.

Histopathologic examinations included lymph node biopsy, which revealed vasculitis consistent with suspected ESM-triggered SLE, and several skin biopsies. One skin biopsy was indicative of scleroderma, another demonstrated IgG deposition at the dermo-epidermal junction consistent with lupus, and one showed normal histology. Immunological profile

Autoantibody testing and immunologic parameters were reported in most cases, demonstrating a mixed immunologic profile spanning probable DILE and DILE with atypical features. Antinuclear antibodies (ANA) were assessed in 40 patients, of whom 38 (95%) tested positive, predominantly showing a homogeneous staining pattern. Reported ANA titers ranged from 1:160 to 1:10240. Anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies were positive in 26 patients (78.8%) and negative in 7. Anti-histone antibodies, typically associated with DILE, were detected in 25 of 29 cases (86.2%). Anti-Smith (anti-Sm) and anti-extractable nuclear antigen (anti-ENA) antibodies were evaluated in 5 and 8 patients, respectively, and were uniformly negative. Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were assessed in one case and found to be negative.

Complement levels were measured in 24 patients; 12 (50%) demonstrated decreased C3 and/or C4 levels, while the remaining 12 had normal complement values. LE cells were identified in 12 of 13 tested patients (92.3%). Immunoglobulin profiles were reported in four cases, revealing elevated IgG in two, elevated IgE in two, elevated IgM in one, and one case of IgA deficiency; the remaining immunoglobulin levels were within normal limits.

Additional immunological findings included two negative rheumatoid factor (RF) results, one positive and one negative anti-streptolysin O (ASO) test, two positive anticardiolipin antibody tests, and one positive lupus anticoagulant. Final diagnosis and therapeutic approach

In addition to the diagnosis of probable DILE, DILE with atypical features, or suspected ESM-triggered SLE, several comorbid or overlapping conditions were reported. Renal involvement was documented in six patients; two cases were diagnosed with lupus-scleroderma overlap syndrome; two with confirmed aplastic anemia; one patient had cerebral involvement; one was diagnosed with autoimmune thyroiditis; and one case of celiac disease was reported.

Discontinuation of ESM was the primary therapeutic intervention and was implemented in all 47 cases. Among ASMs, 15 patients were either continued on or transitioned to alternative ASMs. Supportive and symptomatic treatments were also reported, including acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) in four patients, and single cases of ibuprofen, acne medications, gluten-free diet with folic acid supplementation, and antibiotic therapy. One patient required therapeutic pericardiocentesis for evacuation of serosanguinous pericardial fluid. Additional interventions included albumin and diuretic infusions combined with antihypertensive therapy (amlodipine), as well as blood transfusion for anemia-related complications.

Following ESM discontinuation and initiation of appropriate therapy, clinical improvement was achieved in 37 patients (80.4%), while 2 patients (4.4%) experienced partial improvement. Seven patients (15.2%) showed no clinical response and suffered recurrent disease flares despite treatment, most commonly among those classified as suspected ESM-triggered SLE (Table 1, Table 6; Fig. 3).

The time to symptom resolution ranged from immediate improvement following ESM withdrawal to up to 6 months, with most patients improving within the first two months (median = 2 months; IQR: 0.5–2 months). Immunological resolution, defined as normalization or marked improvement of autoantibody titers and complement levels, was observed between 2 and 30 months, typically within the first year post-treatment (median = 6 months; IQR: 3–12 months) (Fig. 2C).

Despite overall favorable outcomes, persistent immunological abnormalities were reported in several patients. ANA remained positive in 8 patients, anti-dsDNA antibodies in 3, anti-histone antibodies in 3, low complement levels in 2, and positive LE cells in 2. Notably, one case exhibited persistent cardiac involvement 4 years after diagnosis, underscoring the potential for long-term sequelae in select patients despite appropriate management.

Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive overview of 47 patients with ESM-induced LE, encompassing probable DILE, DILE with atypical features, and suspected ESM-triggered SLE, a rare but clinically significant adverse drug reaction. Given the widespread use of ESM as a first-line therapy for pediatric absence epilepsy, recognizing this uncommon complication is essential for timely diagnosis and management. Our findings underscore ESM's potential to elicit lupus-like syndromes and delineate the broad clinical, laboratory, and immunologic spectrum associated with this reaction.

The exact pathogenesis of DILE remains incompletely understood but is believed to involve interactions among genetic predisposition, drug metabolism, and immune dysregulation. ESM, a succinimide compound (3-ethyl-3-methyl-pyrrolidine-2,5-dione), undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism via CYP3A4 (∼80%) to largely inactive hydroxylated metabolites, with only 10–20% excreted unchanged (30). Unlike classic aryl-amine DILE-inducing agents such as hydralazine and procainamide, where slow N-acetylation via N-acetyltransferase 2 (NAT2) markedly increases risk, ESM lacks an aryl-amine moiety; thus, acetylator phenotype is unlikely to play a major pathogenic role (31). Instead, CYP3A-mediated biotransformation and immune recognition pathways likely represent more relevant contributors (31).

In DILE more broadly, autoimmunity may arise from drug- or metabolite-induced modification of nuclear proteins (e.g., histones) and epigenetic dysregulation, leading to anti-histone antibody production and lupus-like inflammation. Specific HLA class II genotypes (e.g., HLA-DRB104:01, DRB103:01/DR0301) and complement C4 null alleles have been implicated in susceptibility to several drugs and could theoretically confer risk with ESM, although our review identified no evidence of a consistent HLA association (16, 31–33). Given that imide scaffolds can undergo oxidative ring opening to yield succinamate intermediates capable of covalently modifying proteins, we hypothesize that rare ESM-derived adducts may alter histone or nucleoprotein antigenicity in genetically susceptible hosts, a testable prediction through adductomic and HLA-immunopeptidomic analyses (31). Supporting this, patients who develop DILE may exhibit reduced metabolic clearance of ESM compared with tolerant individuals, potentially increasing exposure to these immunogenic intermediates (34). Because CYP3A4/5 allele frequencies vary across populations, particularly within Hispanic/Latino groups, pharmacogenetic variability in ESM metabolism may partly account for the demographic patterns observed, and targeted CYP3A and HLA genotyping in future ESM-LE cohorts is warranted to clarify these associations (35).

The demographic profile of affected individuals in this review aligns with known patterns of autoimmune disease, characterized by a marked female predominance (70.2%). Although this trend reflects general autoimmune susceptibility, the female-to-male ratio observed here differs from that of idiopathic SLE (9:1) and classic DILE [7] (36). Interestingly, a prior case-based systematic review on carbamazepine-induced LE reported a female-to-male ratio of 3:1, which more closely parallels our findings (36). The median age of 9 years underscores the particular vulnerability of the pediatric population, likely reflecting the widespread use of ESM as a first-line agent for childhood absence seizures [16]. In contrast, DILE more commonly occurs in adults aged 50–70 years, likely due to the higher prevalence of anti-arrhythmic and anti-hypertensive drug exposure in that age group, whereas idiopathic SLE typically presents during the second and third decades of life (37). Notably, in a previous review of pediatric DILE, ESM was identified as the most frequently implicated agent, accounting for 13 of 65 reported cases [16].

Regarding ethnicity, Hispanic patients constituted the majority (64.7%) among the 17 cases for whom nationality data were available, representing only a subset of the 47 total patients included in this review. This observation should be interpreted with considerable caution, as it may reflect reporting bias, geographic clustering of published cases, or differential access to medical literature in English-language databases rather than a true biological predisposition. With these limitations in mind, the finding may be hypothesis-generating: Hispanic populations carry higher frequencies of HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR15 alleles associated with SLE susceptibility, and ethnic variability in NAT2 acetylation status is well documented, both of which could theoretically contribute to differential risk (1, 38, 39). However, these remain speculative associations requiring prospective, population-level investigation. Clinicians should maintain awareness of this possible pattern while recognizing its current evidentiary limitations [7].

The latency between ESM initiation and lupus onset varied markedly, with a median duration of 7 months and symptom onset reported as late as 7.5 years after exposure. This broad latency range underscores the potential for delayed immunologic responses to ESM [3], [9]. Moreover, ESM-induced LE was observed across a wide dosage spectrum, showing no consistent dose-response relationship and thereby suggesting that individual immunologic susceptibility is a key determinant of disease onset [9], [12]. Contributing factors likely include genetic predisposition, female sex, and pre-existing immune dysregulation (1, 40). The absence of pharmacokinetic and immunologic profiling, such as serum drug levels or immune activation markers, in most case reports limits mechanistic insight and hinders risk stratification. Importantly, the development of lupus in patients receiving both ESM monotherapy and combination therapy supports a direct etiologic role of ESM rather than one mediated by drug-drug interactions [20], [22]. Notably, a few reports have suggested that higher serum ESM concentrations may predispose to more severe or systemic lupus manifestations [8], [12], [14]; however, the limited number of cases with available drug-level data precludes definitive conclusions. Collectively, these findings point toward an idiosyncratic, immune-mediated mechanism rather than a dose-dependent toxic effect (41).

Clinically, the predominant manifestations were characteristic of DILE, most notably musculoskeletal symptoms such as arthralgia and arthritis, along with constitutional features including fever and fatigue (42). Although myalgia has been reported in up to 50% of DILE cases in previous systematic reviews, it was comparatively less frequent in our cohort (43). Serosal involvement, manifesting as synovitis, pleural effusion, pleuritis, pericardial effusion, and pericarditis, was observed in several cases with pulmonary and cardiac manifestations, resembling the clinical pattern seen in procainamide-induced lupus (44).

While cutaneous involvement is typically less prominent in classic DILE than in idiopathic SLE, our findings demonstrated a relatively high frequency of dermatologic manifestations, including malar rash, photosensitivity, and other cutaneous lesions (42, 43). Notably, renal involvement, most often presenting as proteinuria and hematuria, and central nervous system symptoms such as headache and transient loss of consciousness were also reported, features that are uncommon in conventional DILE. These findings suggest that a substantial subset of cases represent DILE with atypical features rather than classic probable DILE, reflecting a broader and potentially more severe clinical spectrum (42, 43).

The presence of atypical features, including scleroderma-like changes and Raynaud's phenomenon, further indicates that DILE with atypical features can occasionally mimic or overlap with other connective tissue diseases or vasculopathies (27, 29, 45). Moreover, the occurrence of lupus nephritis, myocarditis, and vasculitis in several cases underscores that ESM may precipitate severe organ involvement, warranting careful diagnostic evaluation and ongoing monitoring in suspected cases. The coexistence of autoimmune thyroiditis and celiac disease in a few patients suggests a possible underlying autoimmune predisposition [6], [20].

Interestingly, one case initially misdiagnosed as reticulum cell sarcoma was later reinterpreted as benign lymphoid hyperplasia, a finding consistent with reactive lymphadenopathy previously reported in systemic autoimmune diseases and among patients receiving anticonvulsant therapy. Such presentations can closely mimic lymphoma, emphasizing the importance of considering drug-induced etiologies, including ESM, in atypical lymphoproliferative or autoimmune scenarios [10]. These diverse and sometimes overlapping manifestations can complicate diagnosis, particularly in pediatric patients where autoimmune diseases are less readily suspected.

Laboratory and paraclinical findings in the included cases revealed prominent hematologic and inflammatory abnormalities. Pancytopenia was reported in approximately 40% of patients, while bicytopenia occurred in about 30% of those with available data. Additionally, 80% of patients tested positive on the indirect Coombs test, indicating a high prevalence of autoimmune hemolytic anemia, with rare instances of autoimmune aplastic anemia also observed. These hematologic manifestations contrast with classic DILE, in which severe cytopenias are typically less frequent (43). Nevertheless, the predominance of hematologic abnormalities, particularly pancytopenia and autoimmune aplastic anemia, over renal or neurologic complications aligns with recognized trends in drug-induced lupus. Inflammatory markers further supported ongoing disease activity. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated in 95% of cases, whereas C-reactive protein (CRP) was measured less frequently and found to be elevated in only 57%. This pattern suggests that ESR may serve as a more sensitive indicator of systemic inflammation in DILE (42). In contrast, CRP elevation is more characteristic of idiopathic SLE than drug-induced forms [23].

The immunologic profile observed in our study demonstrated overlapping characteristics spanning all three operational categories, from probable DILE to suspected ESM-triggered SLE. ANA were positive in 95% of patients, most frequently showing high titers with a homogeneous staining pattern, findings consistent with previous reports in both DILE and SLE (37, 42). Anti-histone antibodies, a hallmark of DILE rather than SLE, were detected in over 85% of tested patients (43). Unexpectedly, anti-dsDNA antibodies, typically absent or rare in probable DILE, were positive in 79% of cases, a finding more consistent with DILE with atypical features or suspected ESM-triggered SLE, blurring the immunologic distinction between drug-induced and idiopathic forms of lupus (42, 43). In contrast, anti-ENA antibodies were uniformly negative across all reported patients, in accordance with expectations for DILE (43). The frequent presence of LE cells and low complement levels further complicated the diagnostic landscape, suggesting a hybrid immunopathogenesis, as complement levels are usually normal in classic DILE (43). Moreover, prior reports have proposed that ESM may unmask subclinical or latent SLE in genetically predisposed individuals, which corresponds clinically to our category of suspected ESM-triggered SLE [12]. Collectively, these findings underscore the diagnostic challenges in distinguishing DILE with atypical features and suspected ESM-triggered SLE from juvenile SLE, particularly in the presence of significant serologic overlap.

The timeline for recovery varied considerably, ranging from rapid improvement to prolonged immunologic normalization, underscoring the need for individualized follow-up. In most cases, clinical symptoms resolved within two months, whereas immunologic abnormalities often persisted for more than a year. These findings suggest that although ESM-induced LE is largely reversible, extended monitoring may be necessary to detect disease flares or progression to chronic autoimmune conditions (42). Notably, the persistence of autoantibodies, such as ANA and anti-dsDNA, years after ESM discontinuation indicates incomplete immunologic resolution and a potential for sustained autoimmune activity. One case even reported persistent cardiac involvement four years after drug withdrawal, highlighting the possibility of long-term sequelae [13]. Overall, while prognosis is generally favorable, patients with severe or systemic manifestations may warrant prolonged clinical and serologic surveillance.

Compared with other anticonvulsant-induced lupus cases, ESM-induced LE appears to exhibit a broader and more systemic clinical spectrum. Carbamazepine (CBZ)-induced LE typically lacks renal and neurologic involvement; although ANA positivity is common, anti-dsDNA antibodies are detected less frequently (47.6%) compared with 79% in ESM-induced LE (36). In contrast, valproic acid (VPA)-induced LE generally presents with milder manifestations, a male predominance, and earlier onset. Pleural effusion is a notable feature, whereas central nervous system and renal involvement are rare. Immunologically, anti-histone and anti-dsDNA antibodies are less consistently present in VPA-induced cases than in those induced by ESM (46).

Beyond CBZ and VPA, other ASM such as phenytoin (PHT) and primidone (PRM) have been occasionally implicated in DILE, though with far lower frequency. PHT-induced lupus often manifests with constitutional symptoms, rash, arthralgia, and serositis, but renal and CNS complications are uncommon; serologically, ANA and anti-histone antibodies predominate, while anti-dsDNA positivity is unusual (31, 47). PRM-induced lupus is exceedingly rare, with only sporadic reports describing fever, rash, and arthritis, typically without major organ involvement (31).

Taken together, ESM-induced LE demonstrates a broader systemic involvement and higher rates of renal and neurologic manifestations compared with other ASM-associated DILE syndromes. The consistent presence of anti-dsDNA antibodies further distinguishes ESM-induced LE, rendering its immunologic and clinical phenotype more closely aligned with suspected ESM-triggered SLE or juvenile SLE in a notable proportion of cases [16].

Given the diagnostic complexity of ESM-induced LE, particularly in pediatric populations, a structured clinical approach is essential for timely recognition and management. When a child receiving ESM presents with systemic or autoimmune symptoms, such as fever, rash, arthralgia, fatigue, or lymphadenopathy, clinicians should initiate a basic laboratory workup, including CBC, ESR, ANA, and anti-histone antibody testing, to assess for systemic inflammation and autoimmunity. Infections and malignancies, which represent important mimickers in this age group, should be excluded through appropriate serologic testing, imaging, and, when indicated, biopsy. If these differentials are ruled out and both anti-histone antibodies and lupus-like clinical features are present, probable DILE should be strongly considered. Conversely, the absence of anti-histone antibodies in the presence of a positive ANA may suggest an alternative etiology; however, DILE remains possible even with negative anti-histone results. Prompt discontinuation of ESM constitutes the cornerstone of management and is typically followed by rapid clinical improvement. Corticosteroids are recommended in moderate to severe cases, especially when organ involvement is suspected.

For patients who continue to require antiseizure therapy following ESM withdrawal, alternative ASM with lower immunogenic potential should be selected cautiously, as several agents are also capable of inducing lupus-like reactions. Prior to initiating any ASM therapy, assessment of family history for SLE, DILE, or other autoimmune diseases is prudent. In patients with such a history, avoidance of ESM, mephenytoin, phenytoin, primidone, trimethadione, valproic acid, and carbamazepine, may be advisable [22]. Longitudinal monitoring of both clinical symptoms and serologic markers (e.g., ANA, anti-dsDNA, ESR) is essential to evaluate recovery and identify persistent or evolving autoimmune activity. Our systematic review possesses several notable strengths, including a rigorous and transparent methodology, comprehensive inclusion of both full-text and abstract-only publications spanning a wide temporal range, and an in-depth synthesis of clinical, paraclinical, and therapeutic data. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the evidence base consisted primarily of case reports and case series, which are methodologically limited and inherently susceptible to publication bias. Second, the absence of standardized diagnostic criteria for pediatric DILE, together with the variable latency between drug exposure and symptom onset, likely contributes to under recognition and diagnostic inconsistency. Third, incomplete reporting of laboratory data across cases may have led to underestimation of certain clinical or immunologic findings.

Furthermore, some patients may have had unrecognized pre-existing autoimmune conditions, corresponding to our category of suspected ESM-triggered SLE, blurring the distinction between drug-induced and idiopathic lupus. This issue is particularly relevant given the unexpectedly high prevalence of anti-dsDNA positivity, which complicates direct comparisons across studies and may obscure overlap with idiopathic SLE. Finally, approximately one-third of the included studies (15/47) were published before 2000, potentially limiting the applicability of their findings to current clinical practice standards and diagnostic frameworks.

Further studies employing case-control or cohort designs are needed to distinguish patients who develop LE from those who tolerate ESM, thereby helping to identify potential risk factors. Longitudinal investigations assessing clinical and immunologic outcomes following drug withdrawal are also warranted to elucidate the persistence of autoantibodies and the likelihood of progression to chronic autoimmune disease. In addition, mechanistic studies are required to clarify the immunopathogenic pathways through which ESM triggers autoimmunity, particularly in light of its distinct structural and metabolic profile compared to other DILE-inducing agents. Specifically, prospective studies designed to address the apparent overrepresentation of Hispanic female children among affected patients are needed. These should include systematic pharmacogenomic profiling (CYP3A4/5 and HLA typing), recruitment from geographically diverse populations, and standardized reporting of ethnicity and nationality. In parallel, risk stratification models integrating demographic, genetic, and clinical variables could guide individualized monitoring protocols for children initiating ESM therapy, potentially informing evidence-based screening recommendations before and during treatment. Finally, the lack of standardized diagnostic and management guidelines for pediatric DILE highlights the urgent need for collaborative efforts to develop age-specific diagnostic criteria and therapeutic protocols.

In conclusion, ESM-induced LE, though rare, represents an important differential diagnosis in children and adolescents receiving ESM who develop systemic or autoimmune manifestations. Prompt recognition, drug withdrawal, and tailored immunomodulatory therapy are key to favorable outcomes. Unlike hydralazine- or procainamide-induced lupus, no consistent dose-response relationship has been identified for ESM, supporting an idiosyncratic, host-dependent mechanism. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for ESM-induced LE, particularly in female and Hispanic patients presenting with evolving constitutional or autoimmune symptoms.
