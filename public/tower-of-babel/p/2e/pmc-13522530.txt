Development and validation of a machine learning-based risk prediction model for hyperkalemia in patients with chronic kidney disease
Authors: Wei Han, Bing Liu, Jie Li, Lu Gong, Meixu Jiang, Yuying Jing, Jiazhen Shang, Shouyu Chai, Zhitao Zeng, Xincong Lv, Xiaotian Han, Zhimei Lv, Rong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To develop an optimal predictive model for hyperkalemia in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Clinical data of CKD patients were collected from Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University between January 2017 and December 2023, including 343 hyperkalemia cases and 713 cases with normal potassium levels. The data were divided into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio. Important features were screened via univariate analysis, collinearity diagnosis, and LASSO regression, identifying 20 feature variables for hyperkalemia. Five machine learning models were established: logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), gradient boosting machine (GBM), support vector machine (SVM), and K-nearest neighbors (KNN). Models were compared using the area under the curve (AUC), Brier score, calibration curve, decision curve analysis (DCA), and overfitting control. The LR model was identified as the optimal model, showing excellent performance in predicting hyperkalemia. The AUCs were 0.899 (training set) and 0.868 (test set), with corresponding F1 scores of 0.819 and 0.725. Calibration and DCA curves demonstrated high predictive accuracy and clinical benefit. Additionally, the nomogram based on the LR model could assist in clinical decision-making. Our findings suggest that the LR model is the optimal predictive model for the risk of hyperkalemia in CKD patients. Introduction

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) has emerged as a critical public health issue and one of the fastest-growing causes of mortality worldwide1. The causes of death in CKD patients are multifactorial2, among which hyperkalemia stands out as a common and life-threatening electrolyte disorder. The prevalence of hyperkalemia in CKD populations is notably high, ranging from 14% to 20%3, and the associated mortality risk escalates with advancing CKD stages, reaching 20.12% to 35.58% in stages 4–54.

Although studies indicate a U-shaped relationship between serum potassium levels and mortality, with the lowest risk within the 4.0–5.0 mmol/L range5, hyperkalemia is particularly prevalent and dangerous in the CKD setting due to impaired potassium excretion. It is a silent threat that can precipitate fatal cardiac arrhythmias with minimal premonitory symptoms6,7. Given its high prevalence, severe consequences, and often asymptomatic nature, the early identification of CKD patients at high risk for hyperkalemia is of paramount clinical importance.

The prevention and treatment of hyperkalemia in China is even more severe. The prevalence of CKD among people aged 18 and above in China is 8.2%8, and the incidence of hyperkalemia in CKD patients can be as high as 22.89%. More notably, the diagnosis rate, overall treatment rate, and 7-day serum potassium re-examination rate of hyperkalemia are only 2.53%, 8.69%, and 11.41%, respectively9, and the awareness rate and treatment rate of hyperkalemia among Chinese people are both at a low level. It is worth noting that newly diagnosed CKD patients with hyperkalemia will face higher medical costs and longer hospital stays10, which further highlights the urgency of hyperkalemia prevention and control.

Currently, risk assessment in clinical practice often relies on static thresholds and physician experience, which may lack precision and timeliness. Therefore, developing a robust predictive model for hyperkalemia holds significant value. Such a tool could facilitate early detection, guide personalized monitoring, and prompt timely interventions, ultimately reducing the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events and mortality in this vulnerable population. This study aims to develop and validate a machine learning-based prediction model for hyperkalemia risk in patients with CKD.

Methods

Data source

The data for this study were derived from the clinical records of inpatients diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (CKD) who received treatment at Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University between January 2017 and December 2023. We collected detailed basic information, vital sign data, diagnostic information, laboratory test results, and treatment-related information of the enrolled patients.

We confirm that all methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and all procedures involving human participants complied with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (Approval No.: SWYX: NO. 2021 − 324). Owing to the retrospective nature of this study, the requirement for written informed consent from participants was waived by the above-mentioned ethics committee.

Participants

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) definite diagnosis of CKD; (2) age ≥ 18 years; (3) patients with available baseline serum potassium test data and serum potassium test data during hospitalization; and (4) complete medical records.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with acute kidney disease; (2) patients with severe infections; (3) patients with hypokalemia during hospitalization (serum potassium < 3.5 mmol/L); (4) patients with incomplete demographic data and relevant laboratory indicators; and (5) patients with baseline serum potassium ≥ 5.5 mmol/L.

A total of 1056 CKD patients were finally included, consisting of 343 cases with hyperkalemia and 713 cases with normal serum potassium.

Relevant definitions

The baseline was defined as the first measurement time point after admission where the patient’s serum potassium was < 5.5 mmol/L. The window for collecting baseline clinical data was within 12 h before or after this time point. If the same indicator was measured multiple times within this window, the most complete measurement value was prioritized; if completeness was comparable, the record closest to the baseline time point was selected.

Hyperkalemia was defined as a serum potassium concentration ≥ 5.5 mmol/L11. Patients whose first serum potassium measurement after the baseline reached ≥ 5.5 mmol/L during the hospitalization period were included in the hyperkalemia group. Patients whose serum potassium levels remained < 5.5 mmol/L throughout the treatment observation period were included in the normal serum potassium group.

Research variables

Data were collected based on the associations between the variables and outcomes. The following candidate variables were retained:

Demographic and clinical characteristics at baseline were collected, including age, gender, body weight, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), presence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), coronary atherosclerotic heart disease (CHD), malignant tumors; history of dialysis, smoking, or drinking; and use of RAAS inhibitors (RAASi), β-blockers, or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA).

Baseline laboratory indicators were collected, including the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA), cystatin C (Cysc), uric acid (URIC), retinol-binding protein (RBP), BUN/CREA ratio, hemoglobin (Hb), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), serum carbon dioxide (CO₂), prealbumin, anion gap (AG), serum calcium (CA), serum phosphorus (PHOS), serum sodium (NA), serum magnesium (MG), and serum chloride (CL). For patients receiving dialysis treatment, all laboratory indicators were collected during the current hospitalization period prior to the first dialysis session.

The dataset was randomly split into a training set and a test set. Missing data in both sets were then imputed using the multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE) algorithm via the “mice” package in R.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.3. Qualitative data were expressed as counts and percentages [n (%)], with intergroup comparisons by χ² test.

With hyperkalemia as the outcome, data were split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets via stratified sampling. Key variables were selected on the training set through univariate analysis, collinearity diagnosis, and LASSO regression to ensure the independence of the test set. Undersampling was then performed on the training set to address class imbalance.

Logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), gradient boosting machine (GBM), support vector machine (SVM), and K-nearest neighbors (KNN) models were built on the balanced training set, with five-fold cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning.

Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, Brier score, and F1 score. The optimal model was selected based on these metrics. Two-tailed P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Variable selection

Important features were screened using univariate analysis, collinearity diagnosis, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression in the training set.

After three-level screening in the hyperkalemia group, 20 variables met the criteria of significant univariate association (P < 0.05), no severe collinearity (VIF ≤ 10), and non-zero coefficients in LASSO regression simultaneously (λmin = 0.008894). These variables included: weight, BUN/CREA ratio, hemoglobin, triglycerides, serum carbon dioxide, BUN, creatinine (CREA), serum phosphorus, serum sodium, serum magnesium, smoking, alcohol consumption, coronary heart disease, RAAS inhibitors, β-blockers, MRA antagonists, dialysis, hyperphosphatemia, hypermagnesemia, and hyperchloremia. These were used as core features for subsequent modeling in the hyperkalemia group (Fig. 1ab).

ROC analysis: The LR, KNN, and SVM models showed strong discriminative performance with AUC > 0.8 in both the training and test sets, whereas DT and GBM performed weaker in the test set (Fig. 2ab). Calibration curves: The DT and GBM models exhibited poor calibration with a large predictive bias or concentrated distribution, whereas the LR and SVM models had calibration curves close to the diagonal, indicating high predictive accuracy (Fig. 3ab). DCA analysis: GBM, LR, and DT demonstrated high clinical utility in the training set, while the LR model achieved the highest net benefit and good stability in the test set (Fig. 4ab, Table 3). Overfitting analysis: The LR model showed minimal performance differences between the training and test sets, with the lowest risk of overfitting (Supplementary Fig. 1). Brier scores: The LR and SVM models had lower scores, indicating high and stable predictive precision (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In summary, the logistic regression (LR) model exhibited a well-balanced performance across all evaluation metrics. Given the modest performance differences between the LR model and other models, the model is preferred not for its predictive superiority but primarily for its superior interpretability and stability, which aligns with the requirements of clinical practice for model interpretability. Therefore, the LR model was ultimately designated as the optimal choice for clinical implementation, in line with the stringent requirements for model interpretability in real-world clinical settings.

Logistic Regression (LR) Model Analysis and Visualization

Based on the LR model, we further quantified the associations between each feature and the risk of hyperkalemia in patients with CKD using coefficients (β), odds ratios (OR), and corresponding P-values, ultimately retaining variables with P < 0.05. The final model identified seven significant predictors: serum sodium levels, RAAS inhibitor (RAASi) use, hyperchloremia, coronary heart disease (CHD), hyperphosphatemia, alcohol consumption status, and dialysis treatment (Table 4). To enable rapid and intuitive clinical assessment of individual hyperkalemia risk, a nomogram was developed to visualize the final LR model, facilitating quantitative estimation of disease probability for clinical practice (Fig. 5).

Figure 7 further demonstrates that sensitivity and specificity stayed above 0.75 across all subgroups, with strong alignment between training and test sets. Collectively, these findings confirm that the model maintains reliable and balanced predictive performance across diverse patient subgroups.

Bootstrap validation confirmed the robustness and generalizability of the hyperkalemia prediction model. The bootstrap stability analysis of AUC (Fig. 8), based on 1000 stratified samples, yielded a mean AUC of 0.867, with a narrow 95% confidence interval (0.822–0.910) and a coefficient of variation (CV) of 0.027, demonstrating extremely stable predictive performance. Further bootstrap analysis of core metrics (Fig. 9) showed all metrics to be highly stable across 1000 samples, with CV values ranging from 0.027 to 0.053. Accuracy and AUC exhibited the lowest variability (CV = 0.027). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the prediction model delivers excellent and stable performance overall and is robust to repeated sampling, supporting its potential clinical applicability.

Discussion

Patients with CKD are susceptible to electrolyte disturbances. Hyperkalemia can induce arrhythmias or even cardiac arrest, significantly increasing the risks of hospitalization and mortality5,12. This study developed five machine learning-based models to predict abnormal serum potassium. Through a multidimensional evaluation, we identified the most clinically suitable model.

In terms of model performance, the LR model demonstrated an AUC greater than 0.85 in both the training and test sets for predicting hyperkalemia, outperforming the other four models (Table 2). It also achieved the best predictive accuracy in the test set, with a Brier score of 0.141 (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2). Furthermore, the LR model showed the smallest performance variance and a significantly lower risk of overfitting than the other models (Supplementary Fig. 1), indicating superior generalizability.

Regarding model selection, although the SVM exhibited excellent predictive performance for hyperkalemia, its reliance on complex feature mapping limits its clinical interpretability13. In contrast, the key advantage of the LR model lies in its ability to directly interpret the strength and direction of feature-outcome associations through regression coefficients and odds ratios (ORs)14. This enhances its practicality and operability in clinical settings, making it the preferred choice for real-world applications.

This study identified seven predictors associated with hyperkalemia in patients with CKD (Table 4). Analysis of the results revealed that each unit increase in serum sodium level was associated with a 13.5% reduction in hyperkalemia risk (OR = 0.865), which may be related to the physiological mechanism whereby sodium reabsorption promotes potassium excretion. Low serum sodium levels can impair the driving force for sodium-potassium exchange, leading to impaired potassium excretion15. The risk of hyperkalemia in RAASi users was 3.900 times that of non-users (OR = 3.900). Previous studies have also indicated a higher prevalence of hyperkalemia in patients receiving RAASi16, as RAASi inhibits aldosterone activity, reducing renal potassium excretion and consequently elevating the serum potassium levels17. Hyperchloremia (OR = 2.663) may affect potassium excretion by inhibiting the K⁺-Cl⁻ cotransporter (KCC) pathway18,19, whereas hyperphosphatemia (OR = 2.118) may indirectly lead to elevated serum potassium levels by exacerbating renal impairment20.The risk in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD) was 2.090 times that of those without CHD (OR = 2.090), and the risk in alcohol drinkers was 3.366 times that of non-drinkers (OR = 3.366), both potentially related to increased heart failure risk or interference with renal function21,22. Dialysis treatment conferred the highest risk (OR = 13.307). This therapy influences serum potassium levels indirectly through factors such as dialysate potassium concentration, dialysis frequency, and residual renal function. First, selecting an appropriate dialysate potassium concentration is crucial for maintaining potassium homeostasis in hemodialysis patients. An excessively low concentration (e.g., < 2 mmol/L) increases the risk of hypokalemia and cardiac arrest23, while a concentration that is too high (e.g., ≥ 3 mmol/L) may lead to inadequate potassium removal24. Second, an appropriate dialysis frequency helps achieve more stable potassium control and reduces fluctuations in serum potassium levels25. Finally, as the kidneys are the primary organ for potassium excretion, responsible for approximately 90% of potassium elimination26, the degree of residual renal function directly impacts a patient’s potassium homeostasis27. Therefore, for patients undergoing dialysis therapy, we recommend implementing a comprehensive management strategy: prescribing individualized dialysate potassium concentrations, adopting an appropriate dialysis frequency to minimize serum potassium fluctuations, and placing high importance on the protection and monitoring of residual renal function to improve patient outcomes.

To translate the risk assessment of predictive factors into a clinically operable tool, we developed a nomogram based on the LR model (Fig. 5). This tool converts key predictor variables into an intuitive visual scoring system, allowing clinicians to quickly estimate the probability of hyperkalemia based on patient characteristics, such as age, sex, dialysis status, and medication use. Previous studies28–30 have confirmed that nomograms derived from LR models exhibit both clinical acceptability and predictive accuracy in the field of chronic disease risk prediction. The nomogram constructed in this study for hyperkalemia in CKD patients can assist in the early identification of high-risk individuals during initial diagnosis and treatment, enabling early detection and intervention in the future.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, the performance of the developed model needs to be validated using multi-center, prospective, external data to further enhance its generalizability. Second, the wide confidence intervals for the odds ratios (ORs) of some variables (e.g., dialysis) indicate uncertainty in the effect estimates, necessitating more precise evaluation with a larger sample size in future research. Furthermore, this study did not explore the associations between hyperkalemia risk and specific treatment parameters such as dialysate potassium concentration, dialysis frequency, and residual renal function. Finally, due to limitations in the completeness of medical records, information such as dietary potassium intake was not analyzed. Future research should focus on expanding the sample size, incorporating multi-center data, collecting detailed dialysis parameters and lifestyle information in a prospective design, and further optimizing the model through external validation.

Conclusions

In summary, this study systematically compared multiple machine learning models and identified the LR model that exhibited both strong predictive performance and clinical interpretability. We delineated the core risk factors for hyperkalemia in patients with CKD and subsequently developed a nomogram as a predictive tool, providing an evidence-based foundation and practical support for the early prevention and management of hyperkalemia. Future efforts should focus on model refinement, mechanistic exploration, and clinical translation to further improve the management of electrolyte disturbances in CKD patients.
