Social media use for general and oral health information-seeking: a qualitative study in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Rahaf Hamdan BinHamdan, Maram Ali Alwadi, Salwa Abdulrahman Alsadhan, AlBandary Hassan AlJameel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial media has become a widely used source of general and oral health information worldwide. While several studies have examined the prevalence and influence of health-related content on social media, concerns remain regarding misleading information and the varying credibility of online sources. In Saudi Arabia, existing research has primarily focused on quantitative assessments of social media use for general and oral health information. However, limited qualitative research has explored how individuals seek health information encountered on these platforms.ObjectiveThis qualitative study aimed to explore how adults in Saudi Arabia use social media to seek, evaluate, and act on both general and oral health information.MethodsNineteen adults aged 18–50 were interviewed between November 2024 and January 2025 using in-depth, semi-structured interviews. Data were analyzed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis.ResultsTwo major themes emerged: (1) social media engagement and information-seeking behavior, and (2) the use of social media in health and oral health decision-making. Participants used platforms selectively based on content type, ease of use, and social norms. Information-seeking was deliberate and included cross-checking information with healthcare professionals, online tools, and peers. Trust in content was influenced by perceived transparency, professional identity of the source, verification cues, and emotional resonance. While social media offered convenience and motivation, participants expressed concerns about misinformation, time-wasting, and digital fatigue.ConclusionsAdults in Saudi Arabia actively and critically navigate social media for health and oral health information. Their behaviors reflect both a sense of empowerment and the challenges of engaging with unregulated content. These findings underscore the importance of culturally tailored, professionally guided strategies to support digital health literacy and informed decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27951-8.Keywords: Social media, Health information, Saudi Arabia, Health misinformation

Background

Over the past decade, the popularity of social media platforms such as Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, and YouTube has grown exponentially, transforming how people access and interact with health-related content [1–3]. These platforms allow individuals to quickly obtain health information, exchange personal stories, and engage in health-related discussions beyond traditional healthcare settings [4].

While social media offers opportunities for peer support and public health engagement [5, 6], the absence of rigorous content regulation on social media platforms contributes to the spread of health misinformation [7]. Misinformation in this context refers to inaccurate health information that contradicts scientific evidence, spreads rapidly via social media, blogs, and information communities [8, 9]. Misinformation is often distinguished from disinformation, which refers to false information deliberately created or shared with the intent to deceive or cause harm [10].

In the context of oral health, which is often underrepresented in traditional health promotion campaigns, social media provides a novel avenue for raising awareness about preventive practices and treatment options [11]. However, the quality and credibility of oral health information online vary widely, and the individuals’ ability to critically assess such information is influenced by factors including digital literacy, which is defined as “the ability to use digital technology, communication tools or networks to locate, evaluate, use and create information,” [12] trust in online sources, and prior health beliefs [13].

Given these dynamics, frameworks like the Online Misinformation Engagement Framework [14], the Social Media Infodemic Listening (SoMeIL) framework [15], and Wilson’s Model of Information Behavior [16], can help explain how users engage with and evaluate online health content by highlighting the iterative, emotionally influenced, and context-driven nature of their information-seeking, evaluation, and decision-making processes.

Saudi Arabia represents a unique setting for exploring these dynamics due to its high rate of social media use and growing emphasis on digital health transformation [17]. Several quantitative studies have examined general and oral health-related content and social media use among people in Saudi Arabia. For example, AlMuammar et al. (2021) found that health information accessed through social media has a considerable influence on people, reinforcing the need for credible sources [18]. Additionally, a study showed that most adults in Riyadh use social media to find health information and to get direct medical consultations online [19]. Similarly, previous studies have shown that social media and online platforms are increasingly used as sources of dental information among Saudi populations [20–22]. Research examining online media sources related to dentistry has also demonstrated that digital media play an important role in shaping public narratives around oral health and the dental profession, influencing how oral health issues are understood within society [23]. Furthermore, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia indicates that social media can influence patients’ decisions when selecting healthcare providers, with many dentists reporting that social media platforms play an active role in shaping patients’ healthcare choices [24].

While these studies provide valuable insights into the prevalence and patterns of social media use for health information, they primarily rely on survey approaches and therefore offer a limited understanding of how individuals interpret, evaluate, and act upon the health information they encounter online. To date, limited qualitative research has explored how individuals in Saudi Arabia engage with oral and general health content on social media and how they evaluate and act on this information. Qualitative research is essential for gaining an in-depth understanding of the underlying motivations, perceptions, and decision-making processes of users, which is crucial for tailoring effective health interventions [25]. Addressing this gap is important for informing culturally appropriate public health communication strategies in an increasingly digital health environment.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore how adults in Saudi Arabia use social media to seek, evaluate, and act upon general and oral health information. To guide this exploration, the study addressed the following research questions:

How do adults in Saudi Arabia use social media platforms to seek general and oral health information?

How do adults in Saudi Arabia use social media platforms to seek general and oral health information?

How do individuals evaluate the credibility of general and oral health-related information encountered on social media?

How do individuals evaluate the credibility of general and oral health-related information encountered on social media?

How does social media information influence participants’ general and oral health-related decision-making?

How does social media information influence participants’ general and oral health-related decision-making?

Methods

Study design

This study employed an explanatory sequential design, in which the qualitative phase used in-depth, semi-structured interviews to explore how adults in Saudi Arabia seek, evaluate, and act upon general and oral health information on social media. A total of 19 interviews were conducted with adult participants aged 18–50 years residing in Saudi Arabia. The qualitative design and analytical approach were informed by established methodological literature on qualitative research and thematic analysis, particularly Braun and Clarke’s framework for Reflexive Thematic Analysis [26]. Principles of qualitative rigor and trustworthiness were guided by Lincoln and Guba [27], and reporting transparency followed the COREQ checklist developed by Tong et al. [28].

Participants

A combination of purposive and self-selection sampling methods was employed to recruit participants for this study. Purposive sampling was utilized to ensure representation across key variables, including sex, age, and region of residence. In addition, self-selection played a role, as participants who completed the survey in the first phase of the study and expressed interest in further participation were invited to take part in the qualitative interviews. The age range was limited to 18–50 years to capture a wide range of perspectives from adult participants who had consented to be recontacted, enabling the exploration of potential differences in views and experiences across age groups. These individuals provided their contact details and were individually approached by the primary researcher, who explained the purpose of the study and confirmed their willingness to participate. Those who consented to participate were sent an information sheet and a consent form, and interviews were arranged at times convenient to them. Recruitment was guided by the concept of information power [29] guided by the study’s objective, participant relevance, data depth, and the overall quality of interviews. This approach ensured an adequate sample size for thematic analysis. Transcription occurred alongside data collection, allowing for ongoing assessment of data sufficiency. As no new themes emerged in later interviews, data collection was concluded at 19 participants. Participants did not receive direct compensation; however, all interviewees were entered into a lottery to win Jarir Bookstore coupons as a token of appreciation for their time and participation. Participants represented diverse educational and occupational backgrounds, including university students, employed professionals, homemakers, and some retired participants. Most participants had completed at least a university-level education. The characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

Data collection

Data collection was carried out through in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted by the primary researcher (RB) between November 25, 2024, and January 30, 2025. Each session ranged from 30 to 50 min in duration. Interviews were held in Arabic, the participants’ native language, and were audio-recorded with their clear consent. Alongside the recordings, the researcher also documented field notes to capture contextual elements such as tone of voice, emotional expressions, and non-verbal cues, which contributed to a more comprehensive interpretation of the data. To accommodate participants’ preferences and ensure ease of participation, interviews were conducted either in person or virtually via Zoom. In-person sessions occurred in a designated classroom at the Dental University Hospital, King Saud University. Current literature supports the view that video interviews differ from face-to-face interviews primarily in technical and privacy aspects, and when appropriately conducted, they offer an inclusive, efficient, and secure alternative [30]. Both formats allowed for open, comfortable conversations and yielded rich, detailed responses. No one else was present during the interviews aside from the participant and the researcher. Upon completion, all interviews were transcribed verbatim. Transcripts were reviewed and cross-checked with audio recordings to ensure accuracy.

Interview structure

The findings from the quantitative phase, relevant literature [19, 22, 31, 32], and collaboration with research team members informed the development of the interview guide. The guide consisted of open-ended questions designed to explore social media use and preferred platforms, types of content consumed, perceived credibility of sources, decision-making processes, and oral health misconceptions. Probing questions were included to gather deeper insights into participant responses (See supplementary file 1: Topic Guide). The present manuscript focuses specifically on participants’ experiences seeking, evaluating, and acting upon general and oral health information on social media.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA), following the six-phase framework developed by Braun and Clarke [26]. This analytical method was selected for its flexibility and its recognition of the researcher’s active role in interpreting qualitative data. RTA emphasizes that themes are not passively found but are constructed through deep engagement with the data, informed by the researcher’s positionality, context, and reflexivity [26]. To ensure close interaction with the material, all interviews were manually transcribed verbatim by the primary researcher. The initial familiarization phase involved reading and re-reading the transcripts, accompanied by documentation of early reflections and analytical observations. Coding was then conducted manually on a line-by-line basis, identifying meaningful units of data and assigning descriptive labels that captured both explicit content and underlying meanings. Following the generation of initial codes, related codes were grouped to construct preliminary themes. This phase was iterative and involved multiple rounds of reviewing and refining, during which themes were examined for internal consistency across the dataset. Discussions with the research team, who were experienced in qualitative methodology, were integral to ensuring the analytical rigor and conceptual clarity of the themes. The team has prior experience in qualitative health research, including thematic analysis and ethnographic approaches [33–35]. Once finalized, themes were clearly defined and named to represent their central meanings and relevance to the study’s aims. A final narrative was developed to present the results, supported by illustrative quotations from participants to ensure that their voices remained central to the interpretation of findings.

Quality indicators

To enhance transparency and rigor, this study adhered to the 32-item COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist developed by Tong et al. (2007). This checklist guided the reporting of essential elements across three domains: the research team and reflexivity, study design, and data analysis and reporting [28]. In addition, Guba and Lincoln’s criteria for establishing trustworthiness in qualitative research- credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability- were addressed throughout the research process [27].

Credibility was supported through prolonged engagement, as the primary researcher (RB) ensured sustained involvement with the data by personally transcribing interviews, performing in-depth coding, building rapport with participants, and incorporating participants’ direct quotations to represent their perspectives. Reflexivity was actively embedded in all stages of the research. As a native Arabic speaker sharing a cultural and social background with participants, the researcher (RB) brought an insider perspective that supported rapport-building and enriched the interview process. Her familiarity with social media platforms further enabled a nuanced understanding of participants’ experiences. At the same time, awareness of her professional background as a postgraduate doctoral student in dental public health informed a conscious effort to avoid influencing responses. To address this, she used accessible, non-technical language during interviews and emphasized participants’ autonomy and right to withdraw at any point. No prior relationship was established with participants, though they were informed that the researcher was conducting the study as part of her doctoral research. Theory triangulation was employed to enhance analytical depth by interpreting the findings through multiple theoretical lenses. This approach allowed for a more comprehensive understanding of participant behaviours by validating findings across different conceptual perspectives. A diverse sample in terms of age, gender, and region was intentionally recruited to reflect a broad spectrum of experiences to support the transferability of findings to similar settings in Saudi Arabia. Throughout the analytical process, an audit trail of key decisions and interpretations was collaboratively refined within the research team, and thorough documentation of research procedures was provided to address dependability. Confirmability was strengthened through reflexive journaling and peer debriefing, which helped minimize researcher bias and grounded the findings in illustrative, verbatim data quotations. Journaling captured analytic reflections during coding, while debriefing helped validate interpretations.

Results

A total of 19 in-depth interviews were conducted. RTA revealed two main themes: 1- social media engagement and health information-seeking behavior, and 2- social media as a source of general and oral health information and decision-making. These themes are summarized in Table 2.

Theme 1: Social media engagement and health information-seeking behavior

This theme describes participants’ patterns of social media use, their reasons for platform preferences, and their approaches to seeking and evaluating general and oral health-related information. It also reflects the perceived benefits and drawbacks of using social media for health purposes, highlighting both positive roles and potential negative impacts.

Subtheme 1.1: Social media platforms use and preferences

Participants reported long-term use of social media, with platform preferences shifting over time based on life stage, content needs, and evolving interests. WhatsApp was mainly used for communication, while Instagram, Twitter, Snapchat, and TikTok served varied purposes such as entertainment, news, and health-related information.

It all started with Twitter back in 2012 or 2013…Then came Instagram, followed by Snapchat. WhatsApp was always there, but it wasn’t that important at the time. (P5).

Together, these accounts show that participants did not replace one platform with another. Instead, they added new platforms over time for different purposes, while WhatsApp remained their main tool for communication. This suggests that participants chose and continued using platforms based on how useful they were in meeting their changing needs and daily routines.

I’ve been using social media since it first started, around 2012… WhatsApp is probably the number one app I use the most, but X (formerly Twitter) is my favorite and the platform I’m most active on. (P19).

Many selected apps are based on ease of use, content clarity, social norms, and family context. For example, Instagram was often preferred for educational content due to its visual appeal.

“I feel like Instagram is clearer, and the images and videos are more visually accessible…I mostly use it for educational content.” (P4).

P4 emphasized Instagram’s visual accessibility, while P2 highlighted its relevance and quality of content. Together, these accounts suggest that participants preferred Instagram because they viewed it as a clear and useful platform for educational information.

I like Instagram…I started using it and liked it. Also, I feel that its content offerings are better for me….(P2).

Others prioritized platforms for their perceived safety or relevance to family life.

I use WhatsApp and Snapchat just for friends and family….(P5).

These views show platform choice was influenced by social and family ties. WhatsApp and Snapchat were valued more for familiarity than info, while P6 sharing Instagram with her daughter shows family shaped platform use. This indicates platform selection is tied to social relationships.

WhatsApp might be my preference because everyone on it is familiar… I choose Instagram especially since I share it with my daughter. (P6)

Some platforms were chosen to fulfil specific needs, such as academic use, staying updated with news, or exploring general or oral health-related updates. Platform functionality also influenced preferences. P19 described Twitter as a multi-purpose platform, saying, “…Twitter’s variety brings together features of all other apps in one place.” This reflects how participants often repurpose platforms, transforming entertainment apps into general and oral health information hubs or search tools.

Subtheme 1.2: Health information-seeking behavior in social media

Participants demonstrated diverse, often intentional approaches to seeking and evaluating general and oral health information on social media. For many, information-seeking began with curiosity or exposure, such as seeing a trending video. However, rather than accepting the content, participants sought clarification from trusted sources. These actions reflected an awareness of the risks of general or oral health-related misinformation and a desire to protect themselves from harmful claims.

First, I check the source of the information, where it comes from. Was it based on a study or issued by the Ministry of Health? If it’s from the Ministry of Health, I trust it. I might also ask a doctor or even check the comments to see if someone credible, like a doctor, has verified the information….(P1).

These two accounts illustrate complementary verification strategies. P1 described an approach in which institutional authority (the Ministry of Health) and professional opinion served as decisive anchors, whereas P2 reflected an exploratory, comparative orientation that placed value on encountering multiple perspectives before forming a judgment. Despite their differences, participants did not accept content at face value but verified it first. This suggests that social media information-seeking was active and deliberate, not passive.

I am the type of person who loves to search. Even if I come across any piece of information (whether on Snapchat, other platforms, or anywhere else) I always like to see different perspectives and viewpoints from various sources….(P2).

Others demonstrated a more exploratory style of verification, turning to Google or YouTube for deeper context and to compare multiple health-related viewpoints. Some participants verified oral health claims with healthcare professionals during routine visits, relied on instinct or logic when evaluating claims or engaged in discussions with family and peers.

The information I come across on social media, I always verify during my next dental appointment. I ask the dentist whether it’s actually good or not. (P4).

Both quotations show that verification was part of participants’ routine behavior, whether by asking healthcare professionals during appointments or comparing information across online sources. This reflects a normalized, critical approach to online health content and supports the active stance mentioned.

“I might watch a few videos about a specific topic I’m interested in. I’ll watch several clips, compare the information, and try to figure out what’s useful and what’s not…I search Google to read about specific condition… I try to learn from others…”. (P14).

Still, a few acknowledged that verifying information could be overwhelming, especially with abundant contradictory online health content.

Subtheme 1.3: Perceived benefits of social media

Participants shared positive views on social media’s role in accessing health information, emotional well-being, learning, and social connections.

First of all, it made access to information much easier, regardless of whether the information is true or false, it’s now easier to get it. It’s up to the person to verify whether what they find is correct or not…. (P15).

These accounts view social media as an access infrastructure actively constructed by participants, challenging the idea that exposure to online health content is inherently beneficial.

…entertainment, staying connected with people who are far from me, and also getting information. There are aspects like self-development, useful knowledge, and keeping up with the news….(P17).

Participants also described the emotional and mental relief social media could provide. Many participants also appreciated the communication and connectivity that social media enabled.

The positives of social media are that it makes communication much easier. Instead of having to go out and find people ….(P8).

Participants suggest that they experienced social media as more than an information channel, it functioned as a space supporting both social and emotional needs. Recognizing this dual role is analytically important because it complicates accounts that frame social media use purely in informational terms, and it foregrounds the affective work that platforms perform in everyday life.

I see more positives, maybe. If I’m feeling stressed or overwhelmed, I find that social media can help me relax. (P1).

These benefits reinforced participants’ sense of empowerment, access, and being informed, especially during times when traditional sources of information or support were less accessible.

Subtheme 1.4: Perceived drawbacks of social media

Despite the positives, participants expressed concerns about misinformation, time-wasting, emotional fatigue, and ethical risks. Some mentioned addiction-like behaviors or disconnection from real-life interactions.

It’s a double-edged sword. One of the downsides is addiction, as it keeps you constantly on your phone….(P4).

Both quotations discuss social media use as a matter of self-regulation but focus on different risks. P4 emphasized compulsive engagement, describing it as “a double-edged sword,” while P8 highlighted the loss of productive time when use is not carefully managed. The common theme is that participants see the platforms as needing intentional management, indicating an awareness of behavioral risks.

…it can be a huge time-waster if someone isn’t mindful and doesn’t regulate their usage….(P8).

A key drawback was the rapid spread of unverified health information, which could lead to harmful behaviours.

…when someone takes incorrect information without knowing whether it’s true or it can have harmful effects. (P10).

These two reflections highlight a common uncertainty. Participants understood that misinformation spreads online and that its negative effects can emerge later and be hard to identify initially. P18’s statement that absolute certainty is impossible is especially revealing, it shows that participants approach health content with a general sense of provisional trust instead of full confidence.

One of the negatives is that we can never be 100% sure the information is accurate. There’s always a chance that what we’re seeing or reading might not be true….(P18).

These reflections revealed that participants were not passive users but were actively recognizing the risks and consequences of social media on general and oral health.

Theme 2: Social media as a source of general and oral health information and decision-making

This theme explores participants’ sources of general and oral health-related information and their perceptions of social media’s reliability and credibility in this context. It also examines how individuals assess and decide whether to follow health trends, the role of social media in addressing health concerns, and the factors that shape their trust in online health content.

Subtheme 2.1: Health information sources

Participants reported relying on various sources to obtain general and oral health-related information. These included social media platforms, government websites, search engines, health professionals, and family members. Official institutions such as the Ministry of Health were frequently mentioned as a reliable reference.

…I rely only on official sources…I refer to the Ministry of Health, the clinics I visit, or I consult a doctor I’ve seen before. (P3).

A few participants mentioned turning to AI-powered tools to help clarify health topics they had seen on social media. P8 said, “…And maybe even from ChatGPT too! I sometimes ask it for reliable information and tell it to provide details from doctors’ studies…” However, this use was generally described as supplementary; they preferred validation from professional guidance and personal judgment. P11 noted, “Doctors, any piece of information I come across, I go back and ask the doctor about it.”

Subtheme 2.2: Opinions on social media as a health information source

For many participants, the perceived reliability of health content on social media depended on who was sharing the information. Some viewed social media as a useful starting point, particularly for building health awareness or prompting curiosity about health topics.

If it’s from a specialist doctor or an official website, then it’s reliable and trustworthy. But if it’s from random websites or non-specialists, then it could be inaccurate or false information….(P11).

Both participants viewed reliability as stemming from the source’s professional identity rather than the platform, indicating that the key question for many was not if social media was trustworthy, but who on social media deserved trust.

.It’s right if the person sharing the information is a doctor, like a dentist who specializes in the field. In that case, the information is more reliable.(P5).

However, some participants did not consider social media a reliable source of health information. P8 stated, “I don’t consider it a reliable source because anyone can share information and present themselves as an expert…” Yet even among those with reservations, there was an appreciation for social media’s educational potential. P1 stated, “Platforms like ‘Live Healthy’ from the Ministry of Health on Twitter is honestly really beneficial.”

Subtheme 2.3: Decision-making process on health trends

Participants described how they assessed and responded to health trends shared on social media. Low-risk health trends (e.g., natural remedies, brushing habit changes) were more frequently adopted if participants believed the information was harmless, even if ineffective. Some participants stressed verifying with health professionals before trying new trends, particularly during dental visits.

The last thing I saw was people saying you can brush your teeth with coconut oil…The idea intrigued me, and I thought, ‘Okay, coconut oil is beneficial and harmless in many ways, so why not try it?’ (P2).

Together, these quotations suggest that participants approached low-risk health trends thoughtfully. They were more willing to try trends they believed were harmless, but their decisions were shaped by previous experiences and personal judgment rather than impulse.

…on Snapchat, you come across different accounts, but I’ve asked about some things before and didn’t really benefit….(P5).

Participants also relied on their logic and judgment to weigh the relevance and safety of trends. Many distinguished between adopting general lifestyle advice and following specific medical treatment trends. For example, P13 explained, “Healthy eating tips? Okay But when it comes to medical treatments, absolutely not.”

In some cases, negative past experiences with health trends shaped more cautious future behaviour. P1 said, “.I used to follow diet trends on Instagram and WhatsApp… then I regained the weight, becoming heavier than before.”

Family influence or cultural norms and habits also played a role in participants’ decisions making around health-related trends. P15 stated, “My daughters said, ‘There’s a doctor talking about this new thing,’ and I said, ‘That’s up to him! I prefer sticking to the old knowledge I’m used to.”

Subtheme 2.4: Role of social media in addressing health issues

Participants often turned to social media during periods of health concerns to search for explanations, alternative perspectives, emotional reassurance, and practical solutions. Other participants turned to social media to manage mild symptoms or everyday health concerns, such as oral ulcers, bloating, or skin conditions. P14 noted, “For example, if I have symptoms like fever or inflammation, I might use the social media to check basic information—like how to relieve certain symptoms or reduce discomfort”.

P5, who was diagnosed with a sleep disorder, stated, “The doctor really exaggerated the situation… But when I looked into it… After reading more information, I actually felt a bit relieved.”

Participants also used social media to find online communities for hope and support during family health struggles. P2, who was supporting her mother through a health issue,

.I rely entirely on searching through social media and learning from others’ experiences. Why? Because when I consulted multiple doctors, they all directed me toward the same approach. But why should I follow that one path when I have the option to explore alternative ways to treat the condition safely? (P2).

Subtheme 2.5: Factors influencing trust in online health information and accounts

Participants’ trust in general and oral health information shared on social media was shaped by several factors such as source credibility, posts’ tone, certification visibility, platform verification, and content clarity. A dominant factor was the importance of source verification. Participants preferred accounts by verified health professionals or official institutions, especially for oral health and medical issues advice.

What makes me trust the information more is when I see that the account is run by a group of doctors… it’s better than taking the opinion of just one individual. (P19).

Participants distinguished between institutional credibility and commercial self-presentation, treating verification as a necessary but not always sufficient condition for trust. Marketing intent, even from credentialed sources, was perceived as compromising reliability.

…The most important thing is that they are a verified doctor and well-known….Even many clinics accounts claim certain things about themselves, but their information isn’t always reliable. Most of the time, they have marketing goals. (P4).

Some participants described relying on their own instincts and emotional reactions to assess whether a platform account felt genuine or not.

First of all, it depends on whether the person convinces me or not, if what they’re saying makes sense to me and aligns with how I feel or think, then that’s one factor….(P16).

Both participants used affective and perceptual cues such as conviction, sincerity, presentation style, and physical appearance to evaluate credibility, demonstrating that trust assessments are not solely cognitive but also embodied.

…If I feel that the person is speaking genuinely, not just for commercial purposes or exaggerating things, then honestly, I tend to trust them more. Also, the way the person talks, how they present themselves, and even their overall appearance….(P17).

Others rely on public reputation and social proof by checking clinic reviews and account verification.

Google Maps reviews, I check the comments …The doctor’s reputation also matters…Whenever you meet someone, they’ll tell you. (P6).

The combined use of Google Maps reviews and account verification indicates that participants triangulated across multiple credibility cues rather than relying on a single signal. Taken together with the credentialing and affective cues described above, this indicates that trust is built from multiple sources.

…I would check its rating on Google Maps. I would read the reviews to see what people were saying. I would also do the same when searching on Twitter or Instagram, I would check if the account was verified or fake….(P7)

Discussion

This study aimed to explore how adults in Saudi Arabia use social media to seek general and oral health information, and how they evaluate, trust, and act upon the content they encounter. Participants described not only the platforms they preferred but also how they differentiated between credible and misleading content based on social cues, emotional appeal, and professional identity. The findings suggest that participants are not passive recipients of online health content; rather, they are active users exploring confusing and unregulated digital health information. This behavior was observed in oral health information, where participants frequently reported verifying dental advice through professional consultation or personal judgment. This aligned with findings from Sillence et al. (2007), where women were observed to actively interrogate websites to form their own conclusions about menopause and hormone replacement therapy [36].

Participants reported long-term and multiple platform engagement, often assigning different social, professional, or health educational purposes to different platforms. This reflects the growing evidence that users approach social media as a multi-use environment where platforms serve specialized functions [37, 38]. For example, WhatsApp was primarily used for interpersonal communication, while platforms like Instagram and TikTok were associated with visual general and oral health learning or entertainment. Others adopted X (Twitter) as a hybrid tool for news, health knowledge-seeking, and social discussions. This observation aligned with previous study results indicating that users actively choose platforms based on their ability to fulfill specific informational or social needs [39].

These platforms also served as spaces where participants encountered oral health advice, dental hygiene trends, and preventive care information, highlighting the growing role of social media in shaping oral health awareness. Previous research has demonstrated that social media can enhance knowledge of dental care and oral hygiene practices by providing accessible educational content and enabling patients to access information beyond traditional clinical settings [11]. In addition, research has reported that many individuals actively seek oral health advice through social media platforms, which can influence their knowledge, attitudes, and oral health behaviors [40].

The patterns observed align with the four stages outlined in the Online Misinformation Engagement Framework: source selection, information selection, evaluation, and reaction [14]. Participants demonstrated intentional source selection, often selecting their information environments by favoring platforms and accounts they perceived as credible, such as government health pages or verified professionals. This was followed by selective information consumption, where users ignored irrelevant content and prioritized information aligned with their goals, consistent with the framework’s idea of content filtering through visual appeal, novelty, or social norms. The evaluation stage was particularly evident, as participants frequently assessed the credibility of health information through comparing sources, cross-verification with professionals, or discussions within their social circles, illustrating a proactive stance against potential misinformation. Finally, participants’ reactions (e.g., sharing, ignoring, or discussing content) were shaped by emotional responses, trust in sources, and perceived personal relevance. These behaviors reflect the multi-layered engagement with online general and oral health information described by the framework.

While the Online Misinformation Engagement Framework captures the stages of user interaction with online misleading content, Wilson’s model of information behavior offers insight into the motivational and contextual drivers behind such interactions [16]. Participants’ behaviors were often triggered by a perceived information need (e.g., an individual health concern or curiosity sparked by trending content), influenced by personal, social, and environmental factors. According to Wilson’s model, individuals engage in either active search, passive attention, or ongoing scanning, depending on their motivation and the perceived reliability of sources. Participants who mentioned using AI tools described them as fast and convenient, although some expressed uncertainty or discomfort due to unfamiliarity with how they work. This highlights a growing need to examine how generative AI may shape users’ trust, decision-making, and exposure to misinformation [41]. This illustrates Wilson’s concept of information acquisition and processing. However, concerns raised by participants about the emotional strain of navigating conflicting or overwhelming health content aligned with previous research documenting the cognitive fatigue and stress resulting from digital health information overload [42]. This can be explained by the absence of clear authority or reliable filters that can create anxiety as users struggle to distinguish credible general and oral health information from misinformation.

Perhaps most interestingly, this study found that trust in social media general and oral health content is not solid but rather constructed through a complex interplay of credibility factors. Participants emphasized the importance of transparency, professional identity, and verification cues (e.g., blue checkmarks and institutional affiliations). Participants also frequently described relying on official institutions, specifically the Ministry of Health, as trusted sources when verifying health information encountered online. It suggests that institutional credibility continues to play an important role in shaping trust within digital health environments. These observations are consistent with previous findings on predictors of trust in online health information, which demonstrate that hospital physicians are regarded as the most reliable sources, alongside content from various platforms [43, 44]. However, trust was also described as both intuitive and socially constructed, influenced by emotional resonance, peer recommendations, and public perception reputation. This might be explained by the role of cultural context and shared norms in shaping trust judgments in health sources. In collectivistic cultures, like many in the Middle East, social relationships and community opinions play a significant role in determining trustworthiness [45, 46].

Decision-making around health trends was also highly contextual. Participants weighed perceived risks, trend popularity, alignment with their values, and practical experiences. This aligns with the Social Media Infodemic Listening (SoMeIL) framework by Tsao et al. (2024), which highlights that public health behavior online is shaped not only by rational thought but also by emotions, perceptions, and algorithms that reinforce users’ existing beliefs [15]. Similarly, participants of the study were more willing to adopt low-risk or lifestyle-related health advice, while expressing caution about treatments, medications, or advice lacking clinical backing. This pattern was also reflected in oral health behaviors, where participants described trying trends such as natural remedies or brushing practices only when they perceived them to be harmless. This filtering process shows how online content and real-life decisions are connected, supporting SoMeIL’s view that emotional and contextual factors guide engagement. Additionally, the influence of social norms and trend popularity parallels findings in social psychology, demonstrating how social influence can shape general and oral health-related behaviors [47].

The study has several strengths and limitations. A key strength lies in its in-depth exploration of diverse adult perspectives on social media use and general and oral health information-seeking behaviors, grounded in a culturally specific context that is often underrepresented in digital health research. The study captured diverse and rich experiences using an RTA and drawing on a broad age and gender representation. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the purposive and self-selected sampling approach enabled the inclusion of diverse perspectives, the study was limited to participants who were willing to share their experiences in depth. This may have resulted in the underrepresentation of individuals who hold views they may feel hesitant to share, even in confidential settings. Second, the findings are grounded in the specific sociocultural context of Saudi Arabia. Therefore, the transferability of results to other populations or cultural contexts may be limited. To mitigate this limitation, descriptions were provided to support contextual understanding and allow for judging the applicability of findings to similar settings. Third, gender-related patterns in health information-seeking behavior may have contributed to differences in how participants evaluated and engaged with content.

The study’s findings have practical implications for digital health communication, especially in public health and oral health education. Participants’ trust in the Ministry of Health presents an important opportunity for official health authorities to actively engage on social media by providing verified, evidence-based information and addressing misleading content. Professional bodies should consider engaging more actively in social media platforms using verified accounts, culturally appropriate messaging, and easy content formats to improve information uptake. Dental professionals and oral health organizations may benefit from engaging more actively on social media platforms to provide credible and evidence-based oral health information. Given the emotional and cognitive burden participants described, public health campaigns should also integrate digital health awareness components by teaching users how to evaluate sources and manage information overload. Finally, the role of community influence and peer validation suggests that social media health strategies in collectivistic societies might benefit from utilizing trusted community figures, such as local healthcare professionals, religious leaders, teachers, and respected public figures. These individuals often hold social authority and moral credibility within their communities, making them powerful agents for promoting accurate general and oral health information. Future research could explore how these social media engagement patterns with general and oral health information vary across different demographic or regional populations within Saudi Arabia. Additionally, experimental studies are needed to test how different types of content creators shape users’ trust and behaviors related to general and oral health on social media.

This study contributes to the growing body of research on social media and health communication by providing qualitative insights into how adults in Saudi Arabia seek, evaluate, and act upon both general and oral health information encountered on social media. Moving beyond the descriptive nature of existing quantitative surveys, this study explored the internal motivations and trust dynamics that drive user engagement. The findings extend existing literature and provide practical insights for public health strategies in digitally connected societies by highlighting the roles of credibility cues, institutional trust, and cultural context in shaping information evaluation.

Conclusion

This study provides important insights into how adults in Saudi Arabia use social media to access and evaluate general and oral health information. Rather than passively consuming content, participants demonstrated intentional and critical behaviors when navigating digital platforms for both general and oral health topics. Their information-seeking practices were shaped by platform functionality, perceived credibility of sources, and the influence of personal and social experiences. The findings underscore the need for public health and oral health professionals to adopt strategic and culturally sensitive approaches to digital engagement.
