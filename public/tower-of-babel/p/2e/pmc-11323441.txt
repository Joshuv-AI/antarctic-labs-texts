Transcriptomics and gut microbiome analysis of the edible herb Bidens pilosa as a functional feed additive to promote growth and metabolism in tilapia (Oreochromis spp.)
Authors: Che-Chun Chen, Chung-Yen Lin, Hsin-Yun Lu, Chyng-Hwa Liou, Ying-Ning Ho, Chang-Wen Huang, Zhong-Fu Zhang, Chih-Hsin Kao, Wen-Chin Yang, Hong-Yi Gong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo reduce the use of antibiotics and chemicals in aquaculture, an edible herb, Bidens pilosa, has been selected as a multifunctional feed additive. Although there has been considerable research into the effects of B. pilosa on poultry, the wider effects of B. pilosa, particularly on the growth and gut microbiota of fish, remain largely unexplored. We aimed to investigate the interactive effects between the host on growth and the gut microbiota using transcriptomics and the gut microbiota in B. pilosa-fed tilapia. In this study, we added 0.5% and 1% B. pilosa to the diet and observed that the growth performance of tilapia significantly increased over 8 weeks of feeding. Comparative transcriptome analysis was performed on RNA sequence profiles obtained from liver and muscle tissues. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that B. pilosa regulates several pathways and genes involved in amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, endocrine system, signal transduction, and metabolism of other amino acids. The expression of the selected growth-associated genes was validated by qRT–PCR. The qRT–PCR results indicated that B. pilosa may enhance growth performance by activating the expression of the liver igf1 and muscle igf1rb genes and inhibiting the expression of the muscle negative regulator mstnb. Both the enhancement of liver endocrine IGF1/IGF1Rb signaling and the suppression of muscle autocrine/paracrine MSTN signaling induced the expression of myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs), myod1, myog and mrf4 in muscle to promote muscle growth in tilapia. The predicted function of the gut microbiota showed several significantly different pathways that overlapped with the KEGG enrichment results of differentially expressed genes in the liver transcriptomes. This finding suggested that the gut microbiota may influence liver metabolism through the gut–liver axis in B. pilosa-fed tilapia. In conclusion, dietary B. pilosa can regulate endocrine IGF1 signaling and autocrine/paracrine MSTN signaling to activate the expression of MRFs to promote muscle growth and alter the composition of gut bacteria, which can then affect liver amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, endocrine system, lipid metabolism, metabolism of other amino acids, and signal transduction in the host, ultimately enhancing growth performance. Our results suggest that B. pilosa has the potential to be a functional additive that can be used as an alternative to reduce antibiotic use as a growth promoter in aquaculture.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-024-10674-8.Keywords: Tilapia, Bidens pilosa, Phytogenics, Transcriptomics, Gut microbiome, Growth, Metabolism, Antioxidation, Oxford nanopore technologies

Introduction

The widespread use of antibiotics and chemicals in aquaculture for disease prevention and growth promotion has raised concerns about drug residues, super bacteria resistant to antibiotics, and environmental contamination [1]. As a result, their use has been banned in aquaculture [2]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop safe and effective alternatives. As consumers place greater importance on the quality of aquatic products, there is a growing trend to explore natural substances as replacements for antibiotics and chemicals in aquaculture. This shift has generated significant interest in both academia and industry [3].

Recent studies have investigated various feed additives and their effects on the growth, immune response, disease resistance, and microbiota of aquaculture species [4–8]. Herbal additives have emerged as promising alternatives for shrimp and fish farming. They have demonstrated the ability to promote growth, boost immunity, and act as appetite stimulants [9, 10]. These natural substances offer several benefits, such as increased feed consumption, induction of early maturation, control of microbes, and reduction of stress [9, 11].

Asteraceae plants have traditionally been used for their medicinal properties. For example, Bidens pilosa, Crassocephalum crepidioides, and Elephantopus scaber are frequently used to treat stomach disorders, malaria, and liver diseases [12–14]. B. pilosa is an herbaceous plant that grows easily and is widely distributed worldwide. The Taiwan Ministry of Health and Welfare has approved B. pilosa as a legal herb for human and animal consumption [15, 16]. Polyacetylenes and flavonoids are the most common compounds extracted from B. pilosa. Its phytoflavonoids have been shown to have anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [17]. Polyacetylenes extracted from B. pilosa also possess antibacterial, antiprotozoal, and antiangiogenic properties [18, 19]. In animal studies, B. pilosa has been shown to be a disease preventive agent [17, 20, 21] and a growth promoter [21, 22].

Recent evidence has revealed a regulatory role of herbal medicines in the intestinal bacteria of fish [23]. Research indicates that the immune response in carp can be enhanced by manipulating the diversity and structure of the gut microbiota [24]. In addition, promoting the growth of zebrafish can be achieved by improving the intestinal microbiota associated with sugar metabolism and amino acid metabolism [25]. However, the effects of traditional Chinese herbal medicine supplementation on fish, including its impact on the gut microbiota and gene expression, remain largely unexplored.

Tilapia is one of the most important freshwater aquaculture species globally, including in Taiwan [26]. Recent advances in omics technologies have enabled accurate study of the effects of different additives on the gut microbiota and gene expression levels in fish. The aim of this study was to explore the effects of B. pilosa on growth performance, gene expression, and the gut microbiota in tilapia. Additionally, we investigated the interactive effects between the gut microbiota and the host on growth using transcriptomics and gut microbiome analysis.

Materials and methods

Diet preparation

The standard diet of tilapia was mixed with 0.5% or 1% B. pilosa, which replaced an equivalent amount of wheat flour. The B. pilosa extract powder, which was prepared and processed as previously described [20], was purchased from Altruism Co., Ltd., Taiwan. For the B. pilosa extract contents, please refer to [27]. Briefly, B. pilosa was authenticated and then processed as follows. The dried plants were ground into a homogenized powder. The powder was boiled in water (1:10, w/v) for 2 h twice. The water extract was filtered, concentrated with rotary evaporators, dried by lyophilization and ground into powder. The water extract of B. pilosa was prepared after shade drying the whole plant of B. pilosa. Different batches of B. pilosa were chemically characterized by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis. Chlorogenic acid is used as an index compound for quality control of B. pilosa. Each batch of B. pilosa contained 120 ppm or more chlorogenic acid. The extract powder of B. pilosa was then mixed with tilapia feed. Three diets, without B. pilosa and with 0.5% B. pilosa or 1% B. pilosa were prepared using fish meal, soybean meal, poultry meal, corn gluten, corn grain meal, rice bran, and wheat flour as the main nutrition sources. The formulation and compositions of the experimental diets are shown in Supplementary Information Table S1. All dry ingredients were ground and filtered through a 60-mesh screen. Tilapia diets were individually mixed by a planet mixer (Chusheng Food Machinery Works Co., Ltd.) and then homogenized after the soybean oil and fish oil were added. Next, distilled water was added to achieve the proper granulation concentration. Each diet was then extruded in a feed extruder (Ming Seng Machinery Co., Ltd.) with a 2 mm die. Then, the diets were dried in an air-forced oven (Risen Instruments Co., Ltd.) at 70 °C for 2 h and 40 °C for 24 h. All of the experimental diets were then stored at 4 °C. Proximate analyses (dry matter %) were performed for the experimental diets according to AOAC standard methods [28] for moisture, crude protein, ether extract, crude fiber, ash, and nitrogen-free extract.

Fish and experimental design

The tilapia (Oreochromis spp.) used in the experiment (weight of 3.7 ± 0.8 g) were obtained from a tilapia breeding farm in southern Taiwan and transferred to a fish room in the Department of Aquaculture, National Taiwan Ocean University. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approved the animal use protocol for this research (IACUC Approval No. 109070). This study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). The tilapia fish were maintained in round net cages (diameter × depth: 45 cm × 30 cm; mesh size: 0.4 cm) in a 10-ton (width × length × depth: 5 m × 2 m × 100 m) cement pool and fed a control diet at 09:00 and 21:00 for two weeks before we started the experiment. The water temperature was 26 ± 1.5 °C. The pH ranged from 7.2 to 7.8, and the dissolved oxygen concentration was not less than 5 mg/L. After acclimatization, the tilapia were divided into three groups (control, 0.5% BP, and 1% BP) in triplicate (45 fish in 3 cages for each group, for a total of 135 fish in 9 net cages). Each net cage had 15 fish (initial weight of 7.5 ± 1.0 g). The experiment was conducted for eight weeks.

Blood biochemistry and antioxidative response analysis

Tilapia were starved for 24 h before blood sampling. Three fish from each net cage (nine fish from each treatment) were collected. Next, 100 µL of blood was transferred to a 1 mL tube and mixed with 900 µL of anticoagulant solution (heparin sodium, 30 IU/mL). The mixture was kept at 4 °C. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured using a Ransod Kit (Randox, Crumlin, UK). GPx activity was measured using a Ransel RS-5004 kit (Randox). The measurement details were described in a previous study. Glucose, cholesterol, and triglycerides in the serum were analyzed by a clinical chemistry analyzer (Artax Menarini Diagnostics, Firenze, Italy) using a Human GmbH test kit (Wiesbaden, Germany) [29, 30].

Transcriptome profiling

The transcriptome was established for 15 tilapia (number of control = 5, 0.5% BP = 5, 1% BP = 5), and their liver and muscle tissues were used for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted from the tilapia tissues using Invitrogen™ TRIzol® Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Then, total RNA was purified using an Invitrogen™ PureLink® RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the genomic DNA was removed using an RNase-Free DNase Kit (QIAGEN, Germany). The RNA concentration and quality were measured with a Nanodrop™ 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The extracted RNA was sequenced by Genomics BioSci & Tech Co., Ltd. Raw data were submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) short-read archive database (accession numbers: SRR21869238-SRR21869243).

Transcriptome analysis was performed via the docexpress workflow set on galaxy platform (https://hub.docker.com/r/lsbnb/docexpress), which was created for more convenient RNA-seq analysis. In the docexpress workflow, the adapters and low-quality raw reads were trimmed with TrimGalore (ver 0.4.3.1) and then aligned to the reference genome (NCBI Assembly Oreochromis niloticus: GCF_000188235.2_Orenil1.1_genomic) [31] through HISAT2 (ver 2.1). Later, Stringtie (ver. 1.3.3) was used to determine the fragments per kilobase per million (FPKM) values. Groups Control and 0.5% BP (0.5/C) and Control and 1% BP (1/C) were compared to each other to gene select differential genes, and the conditions were an FPKM fold change ≥ 2 and a PoolA and PoolB ≥ 1. Then, the two sets of intersections were analyzed by Cytoscape 3.9.1 (Institute for Genomics and Bioinformatics, Graz University of Technology, Graz, Austria). Biological Processes Ontology (BPO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis were performed with the ClueGO v2.5.9 plugin. Gene Ontology (GO) levels were set from 3 to 5, the minimum number of genes per cluster was set to 3, and GO term fusion was used. The two-sided hypergeometric test was used for enrichment/depletion. The p value of minimum clustering was ≤ 0.05, and the minimum κ-score was 0.3.

Gene expression analysis

RNA was extracted using the above steps and the products were purified and reverse transcribed. One microgram of RNA was used for 20 µL reverse transcription reactions. The Applied Biosystems™ High Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for RT reactions following the manufacturer’s manual. The cDNA was stored at -20 °C until further qRT–PCR analysis. A QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Fast SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were used for gene expression analysis. The sequences of the gene-specific primer sets are provided in Supplementary Information Table S2. The reaction mixture consisted of 10 µL of 2× Fast SYBR® Green PCR Master Mix, 1 µL of each primer (4 µM), 5 µL of cDNA (1 ng) and DEPC-treated ddH2O to 20 µL. The thermocycling conditions for the reaction were 2 min at 50 °C and 10 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, 60 s at 60 °C, and 15 s at 95 °C. Gene expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method, and elongation factor 1-alpha (ef1α) was used as the reference gene.

Gut microbiome analysis

After dietary supplementation with B. pilosa for 8 weeks, DNA from the fish gut was extracted using a Quick-DNA™ HMW MagBead Kit (Zymo Research Corporation, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Q5® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, UK) was used for polymerase chain reaction, while the universal primers 27F and 1492R (Supplementary Information Table S2) were used for 16S rRNA amplification. PCR barcodes (length: 24 bp) were added to the 5’ cap of the 27 F and 1492R primers [32] before amplification, and the sequences were provided by Oxford (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK). The amplified region was V1-V9 (full length; ~1.5 kbp) of 16 S rRNA, and the PCR products were confirmed by electrophoresis. Next, purification was performed using the AMPure XP Purification kit (Beckman Coulter, USA) to remove substances that would interfere with the sequencing library. A Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used to measure the DNA concentration of all of the purified samples, and all of the samples were combined.

The library was constructed according to the SQK-LSK110 kit user manual (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK). The complete DNA was sampled onto a MinION with a FLO-FLG001 Flongle Flow Cell (R9.4.1) and sequenced using MinKNOW software (version 20.10.3), and the sequencing time was set to 96 h. Next, the fast5 file was converted using the basecaller Guppy (version 4.2.2, https://github.com/timkahlke/LongRead_tutorials). To remove nontarget amplicons and sequences with poor sequencing quality, the fastq files were filtered by NanoFilt (version 2.7.1, https://github.com/wdecoster/nanofilt) for sequences less than 1450 bp, larger than 1650 bp, and with q-values less than 7. Then, the sequences were sorted using Porechop (version 0.2.4, https://github.com/rrwick/porechop), and adapters were removed from the sequences. Minimap2 [33] was used for data alignment and microbial species taxonomy. The 16 S rRNA sequence database was obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (released on 17th February 2022).

Then, statistical analysis was performed using the R programming language (version 4.1.3). All samples were first calibrated to the minimum number of reads using the rrarefy function in the vegan package so that all samples had the same number of reads. Then, the MicrobiotaProcess package was used to calculate the alpha diversity of all samples, including diversity, richness, and evenness indices (Observe, Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson, and Pielou indices). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to calculate the variability in richness and diversity among the samples. The Bray-Curtis similarity was used to calculate the species differences between groups. The results of the analysis of variability were presented on a Nonmetric multidimensional analysis (NMDS). The different compositions of the gut microbiota between the groups were analyzed using the permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) package. The Bray-Curtis similarity and NMDS analyses were performed using the phyloseq package. PERMANOVA was performed using the microbiome package. The composition of the gut microbiota is presented in the bar plot using the MicrobiotaProcess and ggplot2 packages.

The PICRUSt 2.0 package [34] for the prediction of metagenome functions and the prediction results of KEGG level 2 and 3 pathways are presented in the extended error bar plot using STAMP v2.1.3 software [35]. The 0.5% BP and Control (0.5/C), 1% BP and Control (1/C), and 1% and 0.5% BP (1/0.5) were calculated by Student’s t test.

Statistical analysis

The growth performance, body indices, body blood biochemical variables, and gene expression were analyzed using a quadratic regression model relative to dietary B. pilosa and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to analyze the data. The values are expressed as the means ± SEs. Gene expression was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method, and ef1α was used as the reference gene.

Results

Growth performance and blood biochemical variables

In this experiment, a total of 135 fish were divided into three groups, including a control group fed a diet without B. pilosa, a 0.5% BP group fed a diet with 0.5% B. pilosa, and a 1% BP group fed a diet with 1% B. pilosa. Figure 1 shows the weekly body weights of the tilapia. The IBW of the three groups ranged from 7.3 to 7.4 g. Weight differences began to appear from the fourth week in the 1% BP treatment group and the sixth week in the 0.5% BP treatment group after feeding with the B. pilosa diet. In the last week (the eighth week), the FBW of the 1% BP group was significantly different from that of the control group (p < 0.05), while that of the 0.5% BP group was not significantly different from that of the control group (control: 39.56 ± 1.33; 0.5% BP: 46.66 ± 3.35; 1% BP: 53.69 ± 2.23).

The growth performance, body indices, blood biochemical variables, and antioxidant responses are shown in Tables 1, 2 and 3 and Supplementary Information Fig. S1. Both quadratic regression and ANOVA revealed statistically significant relationships (p < 0.05) between FBW, PWG, SGR, FCR, survival rates, CF, triglycerides, and cholesterol and B. pilosa. The growth performance had significant differences (p < 0.05) after eight weeks of feeding with the 1% B. pilosa diet (Table 1). Table 2 shows an increasing trend in the CF after eight weeks of feeding, with a significant difference in the 1% BP treatment. Table 3 shows that although quadratic regression did not find a significant effect on SOD activity, ANOVA revealed a significant increase in SOD activity with increasing feeding dose of B. pilosa. Blood glucose, cholesterol, and triglyceride concentrations had similar performance patterns, but cholesterol and triglycerides showed significant differences (p < 0.05).

Transcriptome analysis of liver and muscle tissue

To analyze the overall impact of B. pilosa feeding on tilapia gene expression, we collected five samples from each group for transcriptome analysis. The analysis was divided into liver and muscle sections. A total of 1,228 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the 0.5/C (0.5% BP/control) group and 1,431 DEGs in the 1/C (1% BP/control) group were identified in the liver (log2fold change ≥ 1 or ≤ -1; summation of expression of two groups ≥ 1). In the muscle tissue, a total of 1,138 DEGs in the 0.5/C group and 845 DEGs in the 1/C group were identified.

Functional enrichment analysis was performed using Cytoscape software and ClueGo application. In the liver section (Fig. 2), KEGG level 3 pathways belonging to six major KEGG level 2 pathways (amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, endocrine system, signal transduction, and metabolism of other amino acids) were enriched and associated with GO biological processes (BPs). The 0.5/C group showed enrichment in three pathways belonging to amino acid metabolism: valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation; glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; and tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 2A). Glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and fatty acid degradation were enriched pathways belonging to lipid metabolism. Glyoxylate and decarboxylate metabolism were enriched pathways belonging to carbohydrate metabolism. The insulin signaling pathway, GnRH signaling pathway, and PPAR signaling pathway were enriched pathways belonging to endocrine system. The MAPK signaling pathway, which belongs to signal transduction, was also enriched. Furthermore, the GO BPs that had strong interactions with the aforementioned pathways belonging to amino acid metabolism, lipid metabolism, and endocrine system was carboxylic acid catabolic process cluster. The results for the 1/C group were similar to those for the 0.5/C group. The 1/C group showed enrichment in two pathways belonging to amino acid metabolism: glycine, serine and threonine metabolism and arginine and proline metabolism (Fig. 2B). Glycerophospholipid metabolism, fatty acid degradation, and fatty acid biosynthesis were enriched pathways belonging to lipid metabolism. Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism, glyoxylate and decarboxylate metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and fructose and mannose metabolism were enriched pathways belonging to carbohydrate metabolism. The insulin signaling pathway, adipocytokine signaling pathway, GnRH signaling pathway, and PPAR signaling pathway were enriched pathways belonging to endocrine system. The MAPK signaling pathway and FoxO signaling pathway were enriched pathways belonging to signal transduction. Beta-alanine metabolism, which belongs to metabolism of other amino acids, was also enriched. In addition, the GO BPs that had strong interactions with the aforementioned pathways belonging to carbohydrate metabolism were cellular carbohydrate metabolic process cluster and cellular glucan metabolic process cluster. Pathways belonging to lipid metabolism had strong interactions with carboxylic acid catabolic process cluster and fatty acid metabolic process cluster.

Gene expression in liver and muscle tissue

After eight weeks of B. pilosa diet feeding, significant differences in growth performance and blood biochemical parameters were observed between the tilapia in the B. pilosa-supplemented group and the control group. To understand the underlying DEGs responsible for this phenomenon, we identified relevant genes from the transcriptome and validated them by qRT–PCR.

qRT–PCR was used to measure the expression levels of representative genes related to growth, lipogenesis, and antioxidation in the liver and muscle of tilapia. In the liver, only the expression of insulin-like growth factor 1 (igf1) was significantly increased, although the expression of growth receptor b (ghrb) and insulin-like growth factor 2 (igf2) trended to increase (Fig. 4A). The expression levels of igf1 in the 0.5% and 1% BP groups were 2.13 and 3.36 times greater than those in the control group, respectively. The expression levels of ghrb in the 0.5% and 1% BP groups were 1.65 and 1.64 times greater than those in the control group, respectively. The expression levels of igf2 in the 0.5% and 1% BP groups were 1.09 and 4.34 times greater than those in the control group, respectively.

Gene expression in the liver and muscle of tilapia was analyzed using qRT–PCR. The expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with ef1α serving as the reference gene. Each bar represents the mean ± standard error (SE) of five independent fish. An asterisk (*) indicates statistical significance (p < 0.05)

In muscle, the gene expression levels of myostatin b (mstnb), growth hormone (gh), and ghrb were significantly reduced (Fig. 4B). The expression levels of mstnb in the 0.5% and 1% BP groups were 0.72 and 0.22 times greater than those in the control group, respectively. The expression levels of gh were 0.81 and 0.44 times those of the control group in the 0.5% and 1% BP groups, respectively. Discussion

Several studies have shown that phytogenic feed additives can improve animal health and growth performance [36, 37]. It has been suggested that these additives exert antioxidant and antibacterial effects and support the intestinal microbiota, which may influence animal growth performance [38]. B. pilosa is a phytogenic feed additive that has been demonstrated to have suppressive effects on protozoan infection and positive effects on growth performance in chickens. These effects are likely due to the modulation of gut bacteria [21, 22]. However, it was unclear whether B. pilosa affected growth performance, gene expression, or the gut microbiota in tilapia.

In this study, to investigate the effect of B. pilosa on tilapia,0.5% and 1% B. pilosa extracts were fed to tilapia for eight weeks. Both quadratic regression and ANOVA showed that tilapia fed B. pilosa had significant differences in FBW, PWG, SGR, and FCR but not in FI (Table 1). These results suggest that the differences in growth may not be due to differences in food intake or appetite. The body index results indicated that the CF increased significantly after B. pilosa feeding, suggesting that the difference in body weight was due to muscle protein accretion (Table 2). Table 3 shows that the levels of triacylglycerol and cholesterol significantly decreased with B. pilosa treatment. These findings suggest that dietary B. pilosa may affect the synthesis of triacylglycerol and cholesterol, as well as the release of cholesterol from adipose tissue (the storage site) to muscle (the utilization site). Previous studies have shown that B. pilosa inhibits adipogenesis and lipid accumulation in mice by inhibiting the Egr2, C/EBPs, and PPARγ pathways, resulting in a significant reduction in body fat and an increase in protein levels [39]. Additionally, adding MP (60% B. pilosa, 30% Urena lobata, and 10% Ramulus cinnamomi) to pig diets may improve the sensory qualities of the meat while decreasing backfat thickness and cholesterol levels, without negatively impacting pig growth rate or pork processing properties [40]. As mentioned above, B. pilosa appears to have a positive effect on both growth performance and protozoan infection rates in chickens, probably by modulating the gut microbiota [22].

Several reports have shown a positive correlation between growth and muscle development (myogenesis). Myogenesis is mainly controlled through the positive regulation of the GH–IGF axis and the negative regulation of myostatin (MSTN). The hypothalamic–pituitary–somatotropic (HPS) axis of GH and IGFs is an essential endocrine mechanism for organism growth that is conserved in all vertebrates with few variations [41, 42]. First, GH secreted from the pituitary gland enters the circulatory system where it binds to its receptors (GHR) on the membranes of target tissues, including the liver and muscle, in teleost fish. This stimulation leads to the expression and secretion of IGFs (IGF1 and IGF2) [42, 43]. Previous studies have described two receptor clades of GH in fish, namely, GHRa (or GHR1) and GHRb (or GHR2), both of which are highly expressed in the liver. However, in tilapia, GH stimulates the expression of ghrb but not ghra, both in vitro and in vivo [44]. Myf5, Myod, Myog, and Mrf4 are myogenic regulatory factors (MRFs) that are positively regulated by GH, IGFs, and AKIRIN1 [45, 46]. They are basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factors that regulate myogenic lineage (Myod and Myf5) and differentiation (Myog and Mrf4) in skeletal muscle myogenesis [35, 36]. In addition, MRFs [47, 48], AKIRIN1 [45], and IGFs [49] are inhibited by myostatin b (MSTNb). MSTN, a member of the transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) superfamily, plays a major role as a negative regulator of myogenesis and muscle growth [50, 51]. In mammals, mstn is specifically expressed in skeletal muscle, while it is widely distributed in teleosts [52]. Most teleosts possess two mstn genes, mstna (also named as mstn-2) and mstnb (also named as mstn-1), due to gene duplication [52]. In tilapia, mstnb, the orthologue of mammalian mstn gene, was found to be expressed not only in the skeletal muscle but also in the brain, eyes, gut, gill, and ovary [53].

Transcriptome analysis revealed significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway and FoxO signaling pathway, which include the important growth-related gene igf1, in the liver (Fig. 2). These findings suggest that B. pilosa may regulate multiple signal transduction mechanisms related to muscle development. To validate these results, we selected growth-related genes from the transcriptome and assessed their expression levels by qRT–PCR. The results indicated that B. pilosa feeding significantly upregulated igf1 in the liver (Fig. 4A). Moreover, igf1rb expression in muscle increased, which enhanced the expression of myod1, myog, and mrf4, although the expression of myf5 and myod2 was not significantly affected (Fig. 4B and C). Additionally, the expression of mstnb was significantly decreased in muscle. Previous studies have shown that somatic growth and growth performance increase when mstnb is mutated [48, 51, 54]. Therefore, we suggest that mstnb may be an important regulator affecting muscle growth in tilapia after B. pilosa feeding. No significant differences were found in igf1, igf2, akirin1, and akirin2 expression in muscle after B. pilosa feeding.

The evidence presented does not support the notion that the increase in muscle growth is caused by the akirin1 and muscle autocrine/paracrine igf genes. Furthermore, our observations indicated that neither the mstnb nor the gh gene in the brain were affected by B. pilosa feeding (Supplementary Information Fig. S3). Overall, we suggest that B. pilosa may enhance muscle growth via liver IGF1 to promote IGF1/IGF1Rb signaling and inhibit MSTNb signaling to cooperatively enhance myogenesis in tilapia. Additionally, gh and ghrb were significantly downregulated in muscle (Fig. 4B), suggesting that rapid growth might negatively regulate their expression in muscle. However, confirmation of the assumption that muscle GH operates through a negative feedback mechanism requires further experimentation.

Supplementary Information Fig. S2 shows that Cluster 1 had the greatest number of genes according to quadratic regression, indicating an optimum level range of 0.5-1%. Although the growth performance regression results showed an increase in growth with the addition of B. pilosa, the effect on growth-related genes suggested that a dose response was already present at 0.5-1% B. pilosa. The aim of this study was to investigate the molecular mechanism by which B. pilosa promotes growth in tilapia. It is crucial to consider the cost of incorporating functional additives into commercially produced tilapia feeds. The tilapia industry requires an effective minimum dose rather than an effective maximum dose. According to our results, a feeding rate of 0.61–0.68% B. pilosa may be the best addition ratio for promoting myogenesis.

Several reports have shown that SOD (detoxification of superoxide anions) and GPx (reduction of H2O2 and organic peroxides) are important antioxidant enzymes for the first line of defense against oxidation [55]. In mammals, there are three types of SOD: SOD1 (i.e., copper–zinc SOD, CuZn-SOD), which is found in the intracellular cytoplasmic compartment; SOD2 (i.e., manganese SOD, Mn-SOD), which is found in the mitochondria; and SOD3 (i.e., extracellular SOD, EC-SOD), which is localized in extracellular elements. Thus, to detect oxidative stress from the cytosol (exogenous) and mitochondria (endogenous), the mRNA expression of sod1 and sod2, respectively, was used [56]. Additionally, a study has shown the importance of SOD2 in protecting cells from cytotoxicity and glutamate-induced oxidative stress [57]. While mammals have one copy of GPx1, fish have two different types of GPx1 (GPx1a and GPx1b) [58]. In addition to GPx, CAT can also reduce H2O2 levels.

The blood biochemical analysis results indicated a significant increase in antioxidative responses, specifically superoxide dismutase, with B. pilosa (Table 3). Like igf1, sod2 is another crucial gene involved in signal transduction, including the MAPK signaling pathway and FoxO signaling pathway. To confirm the regulation of the expression of genes related to antioxidant responses in response to the diet of B. pilosa, we identified antioxidant-related genes from the transcriptome and evaluated their expression levels using qRT–PCR. The expression levels of gpx1b in the liver and gpx1a in the muscle were significantly increased after B. pilosa feeding, indicating an increase in antioxidant capacity (Fig. 4F and G). Notably, gpx1a and gpx1b may be tissue-specific and are predominantly expressed in different tissues. Furthermore, the expression of gpx4 showed a strong upward trend in the liver, although the difference was not statistically significant. A recent study showed that GPx4 plays a crucial role in ferroptosis inhibition, while lipid metabolism may determine ferroptosis sensitivity [59]. Thus, the high expression level of gpx4 may indicate intense lipid metabolism in the liver. Additionally, the expression of sod2 was significantly enhanced in muscle, while that in the liver was not affected. The high expression of sod2 may be attributed to significant mitochondrial energy metabolism in muscle, which generates a large amount of reactive oxygen species (ROS). It is unlikely that individual injury or environmental stress caused this effect, as all three groups were kept in the same environment. Based on the natural mortality rate, the control group experienced the greatest environmental stress. CAT is only active at high concentrations of H2O2 [60]. It plays a relatively minor role in H2O2 catabolism at lower H2O2 levels [61]. These findings may explain why the expression of cat was not affected by dietary B. pilosa.

The liver is responsible for absorbing, storing, synthesizing, and catabolizing fatty acids and triglycerides. Studies have shown that LPL can hydrolyze core triglycerides in circulating chylomicrons [62]. Additionally, inhibiting LPL- and hepatic lipase (HL)-dependent hepatic cholesterol uptake can lead to an increase in hepatic cholesterol synthesis [63]. The regulation of lpl is tissue-specific and controls the uptake of lipids, lipoproteins, and free fatty acids (FFAs), which affects the distribution of nutrients and lipids in tissues [64]. In other words, the expression level of lpl is mainly determined by dietary lipid intake and metabolic pathways, such as energy metabolism or lipid deposition [65]. FAS is the primary enzyme responsible for the de novo synthesis of fatty acids and is secreted as a lipoprotein triglyceride. Fas directly regulates fat synthesis, affecting body fat content [66]. This effect may decrease due to the high levels of dietary fat and energy in fish [66, 67]. Additionally, fas acts as a negative feedback regulator of fat deposition, with no tissue-specific regulation [68].

Table 3 shows a significant reduction in triglyceride and cholesterol levels. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that lpl is a critical gene involved in glycerolipid metabolism pathways in lipid metabolism and the PPAR signaling pathway in endocrine system (Fig. 2 and 3). The expression of lpl was only significantly upregulated in muscle, while that of fas showed a downward trend in both liver and muscle (Fig. 4D and E). However, B. pilosa in the diet did not provide a high level of fat (approximately 7% crude lipid) for the fas gene. This may indicate that B. pilosa enhances food efficiency in tilapia, leading to increased energy production. The increased expression of lpl in muscle indicates that more energetic metabolism had occurred in muscle. Moreover, there were no differences in the proximate analysis (Supplementary Information Table S1) or FI (Table 1) between the control group and the 0.5% and 1% BP groups, indicating that the observed changes were not due to increased lipid intake.

An increasing number of studies have found that the gut microbiota plays a crucial role in regulating host metabolism, including amino acid, carbohydrate, and lipid metabolism [69]. Similarly, studies in fish have shown the potential involvement of the gut microbiota in lipid metabolism and hepatotoxicity [70]. Portune et al. reported that gut bacteria synthesize proteinaceous amino acids and produce metabolites with physiological effects on the host [71]. Amino acids can function as antioxidants and regulate the host’s antioxidant capacity. For instance, arginine induces an antioxidant response by stimulating glutathione (GSH) synthesis [72], while histidine can act as an antioxidant or free radical scavenger [73]. Other amino acids, such as beta-alanine and taurine, act as antioxidants by preventing the production of mitochondrial oxidants [74]. Dietary glycine supplementation has been shown to significantly improve the growth, immune performance, and antioxidant capacity of tilapia [75]. Furthermore, the bacterial metabolites of aromatic amino acid derivatives, such as phenylalanine, tryptophan, and tyrosine, may have protective effects on hepatic metabolism and inflammation (indole and indole-3-acetic acid) and promote steatosis (phenylacetic acid) via the gut–liver axis [76].

In addition to the antioxidant capacity provided by amino acids, the enhanced antioxidant response might be caused by massive lipid metabolism. Lipid metabolism in animals involves the oxidation of FFAs from triglycerides in mitochondria or peroxisomes, which are then converted to acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) and enter the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle/oxidative phosphorylation to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP). This process, also known as β-oxidation of fatty acids, is an important lipid metabolism process in animals [63]. In addition, PPAR signaling is a crucial pathway for the regulation of lipid homeostasis, particularly in the liver, where it regulates lipid storage in hepatocytes [64].

Figure 7 shows the results of bacterial functional prediction after 8 weeks of B. pilosa feeding. There were 43 significantly different KEGG level 3 pathways that are significantly different, belonging to amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, endocrine system, lipid metabolism, metabolism of other amino acids, and signal transduction. The functional analysis of the transcriptome and the functional prediction of intestinal bacteria showed remarkable similarity (Fig. 2 and 7). Liver transcriptome analysis revealed enrichment of three pathways of amino acid metabolism in the 0.5/C group (glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; tryptophan metabolism; and valine, leucine and isoleucine degradation) and two pathways in the 1/C group (glycine, serine and threonine metabolism; arginine and proline metabolism). The predictive function results for the gut microbiota showed similar pathways and were significantly different. Therefore, we suggest that dietary B. pilosa may lead to changes in the gut microbiota, which in turn produces amino acids and affects host metabolism.

In addition to the antioxidant capacity provided by amino acids, the enhanced antioxidant response in this study might have been caused by massive lipid metabolism. Similarly, we observed that several pathways related to lipid metabolism, such as fatty acid degradation, the PPAR signaling pathway, and glycerophospholipid metabolism, were significantly different in both the liver transcriptome and gut functional prediction. Figure 8 summarizes our speculations on how B. pilosa promotes growth in tilapia based on the results of functional analysis of the transcriptome and gut microbiota. Our research suggested that feeding B. pilosa affects the composition of gut bacteria, which in turn affects hepatic energy metabolism through the gut–liver axis and promotes growth.

Conclusion

This study examined the ability of the edible herb B. pilosa as a functional feed additive to promote growth and metabolism in tilapia. The functional gene network was investigated through transcriptome analysis, and the molecular regulation of growth was revealed by qRT–PCR after feeding B. pilosa to tilapia. Additionally, the potential functions and roles of the gut microbiota were predicted through full-length 16 S rRNA sequencing. This study showed that feeding tilapia B. pilosa significantly enhanced their growth performance. The analysis of gut microbiota function revealed several pathways overlapping with the KEGG enrichment results of DEGs in the transcriptome, suggesting that dietary B. pilosa may alter the composition of the gut microbiota. Subsequently, the gut microbiota influences various metabolic processes, including amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, endocrine system, lipid metabolism, metabolism of other amino acids, and signal transduction in the host via the gut–liver axis, ultimately promoting growth. It is believed that B. pilosa may promote muscle growth in tilapia by activating endocrine IGF1/IGF1Rb signaling and repressing autocrine/paracrine MSTNb signaling to increase the expression of genes encoding MRFs, such as Myod1, Myog, and Mrf4. The communication between microbes and hosts is likely the cause of the growth-promoting effect. This study provides information to support the potential use of B. pilosa as an alternative to antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs). Understanding the molecular mechanisms of herbal extracts that contain effective phytogenics is valuable. Additionally, analysis of the gut microbiota revealed how B. pilosa may promote growth.
