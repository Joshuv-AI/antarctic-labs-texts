Influence of sponsorship on betting intentions: from linear to deep learning models
Authors: Manuel Alonso Dos Santos, Rodrigo Fuentes-Solis, Ana María Gómez-Tafalla, Eduardo Torres-Moraga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sports sponsorship by commercial betting providers is an essential source of income for sports entities worldwide. This study examines the formation of betting behavior in sporting events using predictor variables, namely, self-congruence, perceived exposure, subjective norms, goodwill, and involvement, to predict betting intention and actual gambling behavior, comparing linear (PLS-SEM) and nonlinear (artificial neural networks—ANN) methods. Data from 661 Spanish subjects were analyzed. The results of the PLS-SEM model identified subjective norms and self-congruence as the main predictors of betting intention, whereas goodwill showed limited effects. However, the ANN analysis revealed complex patterns not detected by linear methods, where variables such as goodwill acquire greater relative importance when considering complex interactions between variables. The research contributes theoretically by highlighting the need to adopt conceptual frameworks that consider the complex interaction between psychosocial variables, overcoming traditional models that analyze the determinants of gambling behavior in isolation. Additionally, it provides new knowledge about the differentiated and complementary influence of self-congruence, perceived exposure, goodwill, and involvement in predicting gambling behavior, constructs that had not been systematically integrated into a joint predictive model. Methodologically, it contributes by demonstrating that the integration of SEM and ANN approaches improves the robustness and validity of the analysis, offering a more complete view of the factors that influence the intention to gamble. The findings have practical implications for the design of more effective problem gambling prevention strategies and regulations.

Introduction

The economic survival of sports organizations depends largely on sponsorship, as it represents an essential source of income that allows them to finance their activities and maintain their operations [1]. Among all sponsors, sponsorship by commercial gambling providers (CGP) is of notable economic importance because it constitutes a growing source of income for clubs, leagues, and event organizers. Recent official data confirm that advertising expenditure in the gambling and sports betting sector remains substantial in Europe and Latin America, with continued growth in regulated markets and sustained investment in sports sponsorship exceeding USD 700 million annually worldwide [2, 3].

This flow of capital strengthens the financial viability of sports entities and drives consumption and fan participation. Sponsorships allow betting companies to segment and target their marketing strategies more effectively, maximizing return on investment through personalized campaigns and integrating the betting experience into the sporting narrative [4].

From a social perspective, CGP sponsorship in sporting events contributes to the normalization of gambling by associating betting with positive values such as entertainment, excitement, and fan engagement, especially among young people and vulnerable groups [5]. Several studies and recent systematic reviews provide convergent evidence that constant exposure to gambling advertising and sponsorship in sporting contexts is associated with more favorable attitudes toward gambling, stronger gambling intentions, engagement with incentives, gambling-related harms, and measurable gambling behavior, particularly among vulnerable populations and highly involved consumers [6–9]. This convergence between sports and gambling has transformed the fan experience by integrating betting into sports consumption, creating new challenges in terms of public health, regulation, and social responsibility [7]. This process is consistent with the concept of the “gamblification” of sport, whereby gambling becomes embedded within sports consumption and fandom, reinforcing social acceptance of betting, particularly among young men [10, 11].

In recent literature on CGP sponsorship of sporting events, linear statistical methods, especially multiple linear regression analysis, t tests for group comparison, and analysis of variance including ANOVA and MANOVA, are predominant [12–14]. Nevertheless, several studies have also applied structural equation modelling approaches, including SEM and PLS SEM, to examine the relationships between sponsorship exposure, psychosocial variables, and gambling intentions [15, 16]. These approaches allow the estimation of latent constructs and direct effects, but they remain constrained by assumptions of linearity, which may limit their ability to capture complex interactions among psychosocial variables in gambling related decision making. Accordingly, the present study positions artificial neural networks not as a replacement of SEM based approaches, but as a complementary method capable of modelling nonlinear and noncompensatory relationships that are particularly relevant in the context of sports betting sponsorship.

However, the use of nonlinear methods, such as artificial neural networks (ANN), can provide additional advantages because they allow the modelling of complex, nonlinear relationships between multiple variables, capturing patterns and dependencies that linear models cannot identify [17]. Artificial neural networks differ from traditional linear and structural models in that they do not impose assumptions of linearity or additivity and are capable of capturing complex nonlinear relationships and interaction effects among predictors. These characteristics make ANN particularly suitable for behavioural contexts characterised by heterogeneous decision rules and threshold effects, such as gambling-related behaviours, where psychological, social, and marketing variables interact dynamically rather than independently. Consequently, ANN offer more accurate predictions and a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms through which betting sponsorship influences consumer behaviour.

Therefore, our first objective is to examine the association between sponsorship-related psychosocial variables and betting behaviour in sporting events, from both linear and nonlinear perspectives, using predictor variables related to the sponsor and the characteristics of the subjects.

Additionally, the use of variables such as self-congruence, perceived exposure, perceived goodwill, and involvement to predict gambling intention and actual behavior represents a significant contribution to the study of sports betting sponsorship, as the literature has so far focused mainly on models based on planned behavior theory and traditional variables such as attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived control [6, 7]. No previous study has systematically integrated these four constructs into a single predictive model, additionally adding perceived exposure, despite evidence suggesting that these factors may influence the formation of intentions and their translation into actual gambling behavior in a differentiated and complementary manner [4]. Incorporating this multidimensional approach allows us to better capture the complexity of the psychological and social processes that mediate the impact of sponsorship on consumer behavior, opening up new avenues for the design of more effective interventions and regulations in the field of responsible gambling. Therefore, our second objective is to examine the influence and complementarity of the variables self-congruence, perceived exposure, goodwill, and involvement to explain the intention to gamble and gambling behavior.

Both objectives lead to two contributions to the literature: (1) The integration of the SEM and ANN methodological approaches improves the robustness and validity of the analysis, offering a more complete and accurate view of the factors that influence the intention to gamble, which contributes to enriching theory and practical applications in the field of sports betting behavior; and (2) new knowledge about the interaction of the use of self-congruence, perceived exposure, goodwill, and involvement to predict the intention and actual gambling behavior.

Hypothesis development

The visible presence of a sponsor in promotional materials for a sporting event, such as including their logo on a poster, can significantly influence perceptions of goodwill toward the brand [18]. Attendees and fans may interpret the logo's presence as a sign of the sponsor's commitment to and endorsement of the sporting activity and the community. This positive association generates feelings of appreciation and gratitude toward the brand, as it is perceived as contributing to the development of the event and the well-being of participants and spectators [19, 20]. Consequently, visual exposure of the sponsor in key communication elements can strengthen the perception of goodwill, thereby increasing sympathy and positive brand valuation among the target audience [21]. Therefore, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1

Perceived exposure is positively associated with perceived goodwill.

Perceived goodwill refers to the positive attitude, reciprocity, and moral-affective evaluation that fans develop toward sponsors when corporate support is perceived as genuine, appropriate, and beneficial to the event, team, or sport [22, 23]. Fan involvement has a significant influence on the development of goodwill in sports sponsorships because highly involved fans are more attentive to sponsors’ actions and more likely to recognize their contribution to the sport [24]. Involvement is defined as the level of emotional and cognitive engagement that fans maintain with a sports event, team, or activity [25]. Accordingly, involvement not only increases fans’ sensitivity to sponsors’ actions but also facilitates the generation of goodwill when corporate support is perceived as genuine and beneficial [22].

Recent evidence in sports betting contexts shows that higher levels of involvement are associated not only with attitudinal responses, but also with stronger behavioral intentions and actual engagement with betting products [4].

Vance et al. [26] showed that highly involved fans develop stronger moral judgments about sponsors, associating their support with the viability and ethics of the sport, which intensifies goodwill. Involvement also acts as a catalyst by increasing awareness of mutual dependence between the event and the sponsor. This mechanism is consistent with sponsorship theory, which posits that higher involvement increases emotional engagement, perceived reciprocity, and goodwill toward sponsors [24]. According to Gwinner and Swanson [27], involvement fosters social identification, making fans internalize the team’s success as their own and perceive the sponsor’s support as a personal benefit, generating reciprocity in the form of goodwill.

H2

Involvement is positively associated with perceived goodwill.

Self-congruence refers to the perceived similarity between an individual’s self-concept, actual (how one sees oneself) or ideal (how one wishes to be), and the personality or image of a brand, organization, or entity [28]. This construct is central to understanding how individuals form attachments and loyalty toward brands or organizations because it reflects the alignment between personal identity and external symbols. Prior research shows that self-congruence emerges through processes of identification and internalization that are strengthened in highly involving contexts [29]. In sponsorship contexts, higher involvement strengthens processes of identification and internalization, increasing the alignment between the self and the sponsoring brand [27].

When individuals exhibit high involvement, the relevance of self-verification (actual self-congruence) increases, as people seek consistency between their current self and the personality of the organization or brand. This is supported by self-verification theory, which posits that people are motivated to maintain consistency with their self-concept, especially in contexts of high personal relevance [30]. Conversely, high involvement reduces the relevance of self-enhancement motives associated with ideal self-congruence, since individuals tend to avoid situations that highlight discrepancies between their actual and ideal selves, as these can provoke negative emotions and threaten self-evaluation [31]. Thus, issue involvement amplifies the impact of actual self-congruence and attenuates the impact of ideal self-congruence on attitudes and behaviors [32].

H3

Involvement is positively associated with self-congruence.

Several studies have shown that when consumers perceive congruence between a sponsoring brand’s image and their own self-concept, whether real or ideal, symbolic and emotional identification processes are activated, strengthening evaluative responses toward the sponsor [30]. In sponsorship contexts, this perceived alignment enhances personal relevance and perceived fit, which translates primarily into more favorable affective attitudes toward the sponsoring brand [32]. Although prior research has also linked self-congruence to longer-term outcomes such as brand loyalty and enduring relational bonds, these effects are typically conceptualized as downstream consequences that emerge after initial attitudinal evaluations have been formed [30]. More recent evidence suggests that self-congruence also contributes to behavioral intentions and usage-related outcomes through its impact on attitudes, reinforcing the role of attitude as a key mediating mechanism in sponsorship effectiveness [28]. Importantly, contemporary sponsorship research emphasizes that in event-based and episodic consumption contexts, such as sports betting sponsorships, self-congruence exerts its most direct and robust influence on attitudes toward the sponsor, even when long-term loyalty or repeated consumption are less salient or unlikely [33].

H4

Self-congruence is positively associated with attitude toward the sponsor.

Beyond its antecedents, goodwill is also expected to shape downstream evaluative responses toward the sponsoring brand. Unlike purely functional or performance-based brand evaluations, goodwill reflects judgments about the sponsor’s motives, ethical standing, and perceived contribution to the sport or event [22, 23].

Prior research conceptualizes sponsor goodwill as a multidimensional construct. Core components include perceived authenticity of the sponsorship, perceptions of social responsibility, moral appropriateness, and the absence of opportunistic or purely profit-driven motives. When these dimensions are present, consumers are more likely to interpret the sponsorship as legitimate and socially acceptable, which fosters positive affective responses toward the sponsor [26, 34].

The role of goodwill becomes especially salient in sponsorship contexts involving morally sensitive or controversial industries, such as sports betting. In these settings, consumer responses are strongly shaped by moral judgments and perceptions of social responsibility, making goodwill a central mechanism through which sponsorship effects operate. Rather than functioning as a secondary attitudinal variable, goodwill conditions the perceived legitimacy of the sponsor’s presence in sport, thereby influencing subsequent attitudes and behavioral intentions [26].

Recent literature confirms that the goodwill generated by the sponsor directly influences the positive attitude of the public toward the sponsoring brand. When participants perceive that the sponsor acts with goodwill, this perception translates into a more favorable assessment and a greater willingness to support or prefer the brand associated with the sponsored event or cause [26, 34]. This effect is observed in both sporting contexts and social events, where the transfer of goodwill from the sponsored activity to the sponsor is a determining factor in consolidating positive attitudes and strengthening the emotional bond with the brand [23]. Thus, goodwill is positioned as a central element in the effectiveness of sponsorship because it clearly and directly influences the public’s perception and attitude toward the sponsor.

H5

Perceived goodwill is positively associated with an attitude toward the sponsor.

Empirical evidence indicates that sponsor goodwill not only shapes attitudes, but also translates into stronger behavioral intentions and supportive consumption behaviors toward the sponsor, particularly in morally sensitive or high-risk categories. [26]. Goodwill’s ability to generate trust and perceived legitimacy explains this effect in risk-evaluating environments such as sports betting.

The connection between goodwill and behavioral intention is based on documented psychological mechanisms. According to Smith et al. [34], when consumers perceive authenticity and social responsibility in a sponsoring brand (key dimensions of goodwill), the brand’s credibility is strengthened, reducing the perception of risk associated with activities such as betting [16]. This mechanism operates through cognitive and affective evaluation processes in which positive emotional responses influence complex consumer decisions [6, 23].

In addition, goodwill facilitates an image transfer effect that directly influences the intention to gamble. When a bookmaker or sponsored event is supported by brands with high goodwill, consumers project positive associations onto the platform, perceiving it as more trustworthy [35, 36]. This phenomenon is particularly relevant in sports betting, where the transfer manifests itself as a greater willingness to participate in bets associated with events or brands with perceived goodwill [26].

Therefore, goodwill generates reciprocity and gratitude among consumers who are more likely to favor a brand when making risky consumption decisions [34]. This effect is explained by the consolidation of perceived legitimacy and the reduction of psychological barriers, thereby extending their influence to complex areas such as betting decisions [23].

H6

Goodwill is positively associated with bet intention.

The variable subjective norms significantly influence the intention to bet on sporting events, as evidenced by its role in shaping perceived social approval and behavioral expectations. According to the theory of reasoned action and theory of planned behavior, which underpin multiple studies, individuals’ betting intentions are strongly mediated by their perceptions of whether significant others (e.g., peers, family, or societal groups) endorse gambling behavior [7, 15]. For instance, young adults’ exposure to pervasive gambling advertising and sponsorship in sports cultivates normative beliefs that betting is socially acceptable, thereby increasing behavioral intention [37, 38]. This effect is amplified in communities where gambling is embedded in cultural practices, as social networks reinforce the normalization of betting through shared engagement and implicit endorsement [39, 40].

Empirical studies further demonstrate that problem gamblers exhibit heightened sensitivity to subjective norms, where perceived social pressures significantly predict higher betting frequency and riskier gambling patterns [41, 42]. For example, sports bettors who perceive strong social approval for gambling are more likely to translate intentions into actual betting behavior, particularly when advertisements and sponsorships amplify these norms [10, 43]. Conversely, restrictive norms or negative social perceptions can mitigate intention, highlighting the variable’s dual role in both facilitating and inhibiting gambling behavior [7, 44]. Thus, subjective norms function as a critical sociocognitive mechanism that links environmental stimuli—such as marketing and peer influence—to individual betting intentions.

H7

Subjective norm is positively associated with bet intention.

Several studies have demonstrated that a positive attitude toward CGP increases the likelihood that individuals will express a greater intention to participate in sports betting. In this context, attitude functions as a direct antecedent of behavioral intention [15, 16]. Sponsors' promotional strategies, such as sign-up bonuses, “risk-free” bets, and micro-bets, reinforce positive attitudes by emphasizing perceived benefits, such as the ease of winning and immediate excitement, which reduces behavioral barriers and increases the intention to participate [42, 43]. This association is further strengthened when sponsors are integrated into sporting contexts, as their visibility on items such as shirts, broadcasts, posters, and stadiums normalizes gambling by linking it to positive elements such as competitiveness and entertainment. This creates a symbolic connection that reinforces the social acceptability of gambling [10, 45].

H8

Attitude toward the sponsor is positively associated with bet intention.

When consumers perceive that a sponsor's values reflect their own, they are more likely to use the sponsor's services [29]. Several studies have shown that exposure to sports betting sponsors and advertising is associated with an increased intention to gamble, particularly when individuals perceive that sponsors have influence over them [6, 46]. This influence is enhanced when the sponsor is integrated into the sporting environment, making it easier for consumers to associate the brand with their sporting experience and increase the likelihood that they will bet during events [6]. Furthermore, research indicates that regular bettors process information from betting platforms more quickly and with greater familiarity, reflecting a deeper integration of the sponsor into their sporting experience [7]. Consequently, self-congruence with the sponsor reduces the perceived distance between consumer identity and the brand, facilitating the adoption of betting behaviors in a sporting context [4, 6, 7].

H9

Self-congruence is positively associated with bet intention.

Gambling behavior is understood as the observable manifestation of gambling, i.e. how frequently and in what manner a person participates in gambling activities [47]. According to the Theory of Planned Behavior [48], intention to gamble is the main immediate antecedent of gambling behavior. This is consistent with the literature, which has shown that intention translates attitudes, which in turn predict actual gambling behavior [15, 49, 50]. Therefore, the intention to gamble is the central cognitive mechanism through which marketing stimuli and individual perceptions influence the act of placing bets, thus consolidating its role as a key predictor of gambling behavior in sports and sponsorship contexts [5, 48, 51]. Method

To perform the analysis, a hybrid methodology was adopted, integrating both linear and nonlinear techniques: Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). This combined strategy leverages the strengths of each method: SEM is effective for confirming theoretical frameworks, examining causal links, working with smaller datasets, and modeling latent variables [52]. On the other hand, ANNs excel at detecting complex nonlinear patterns, deliver strong predictive performance, and assess variable significance without relying on predefined data distributions [21].

Data collection and sample characteristics

The sample was obtained through a Qualtrics survey administered via Prolific in 2023. Eligible participants were adults over 18 years old who had gambled at least once and provided informed consent. The survey was anonymous, conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and approved by the University Ethics Committee. The database was filtered based on response time, and multivariate outliers were removed using the Mahalanobis distance criterion. After eliminating invalid responses and multivariate outliers, the final analytical sample consisted of 661 Spanish participants, representing 94% of the valid responses. The mean age was 37.3 years (SD = 9.59), 450 participants were male, representing 68.1% of the final sample. Regarding education, 83% of the sample had at least basic or higher education, including 62% with high school education. In terms of income, 60% earned between $25,000 and $50,000 per year, while 22% earned less than $25,000. Finally, 62.3% of participants were employed full-time.

Scales

In order to ensure the validity and reliability of the measurement instruments, all scales employed in this study were adapted from previously validated sources in the academic literature. All data were collected in Spanish. To ensure cross-cultural equivalence [53], the scales were translated by native Spanish speakers, back-translated into English by a native speaker, and compared for conceptual and linguistic consistency. A group of colleagues and students then reviewed the final version for clarity and adequacy.

Gambling behavior was measured using a single self-report item adapted from Moore and Ohtsuka [49], which captures the frequency of respondents’ sports betting behaviour. Thus, the dependent variable reflects how often participants place bets on sports events, rather than general gambling in other domains.

Perceived exposure, goodwill, attitude toward the sponsor, self-congruence and subjective norms are all explicitly framed with respect to betting brands that sponsor sports. Event involvement items capture the degree of emotional and cognitive engagement with the sports event associated with the sponsor. Table 3 presents the wording of the items and their sources. All independent variables were measured using a 5-point Likert scale to ensure methodological consistency. The items were drawn from previous studies when they represented the same conceptual domain and could be meaningfully transferred to betting brands sponsoring sports events. Attitude toward the sponsor and sponsor goodwill were adapted from sponsorship effectiveness research [22, 23], while sponsorship exposure and event involvement were based on prior sport sponsorship studies addressing sponsor visibility, event engagement, and sponsorship responses [54, 55]. Self-congruence and subjective norms were incorporated because they capture identity-based and social-normative mechanisms relevant to betting intentions in sport contexts [28, 48, 49]. This approach ensured that the indicators were conceptually consistent with prior literature while being adapted to the empirical setting of betting brands sponsoring sports events.

Common method bias

Several procedural and statistical precautions were implemented to minimise potential common method bias (CMB). At the procedural level, the questionnaire was administered through the Prolific panel, which provides access to a heterogeneous pool of adult respondents with gambling experience, thereby reducing the likelihood that the sample was driven by a single socio-demographic or behavioural profile. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and respondents were informed that there were no right or wrong answers, helping to mitigate social desirability effects. In addition, the items were presented in randomised order and were drawn from previously validated multi-item scales in the sponsorship and consumer-behaviour literature.

At the statistical level, we assessed the presence of common method variance following the recommendations of Hair et al. [56] and Henseler et al. [57]. Harman’s single-factor test indicated that no single factor accounted for the majority of the explained variance, with the first factor explaining less than 50% of total variance. We also examined full-collinearity VIF values [58], all of which were below 3.3. These results suggest that common method bias is unlikely to pose a substantive threat to the validity of the findings.

Neural network analysis

Conventional statistical approaches like PLS-SEM rely on the premise that relationships between variables are linear, which restricts their effectiveness in detecting more complex, nonlinear interactions [62]. Evidence from ANOVA indicated that nonlinear patterns exist, supporting the adoption of artificial neural networks (ANN). ANNs are well-suited to model these nonlinear dynamics and noncompensatory decision processes, offering improved accuracy and predictive performance compared to linear methods [52, 63–65].

The methodological combination of PLS-SEM and ANN reinforces the validity and reliability of the results, highlighting the advantage of using hybrid techniques for nonlinear multivariate analysis and ensuring an optimal balance between model performance and simplicity, in line with the specialized literature and empirical evidence [66–68]. To address the complexity of the model and the identified nonlinearities relationships, a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) analysis with a sigmoid algorithm was implemented, consisting of one input layer, two hidden layers, and one output layer [69, 70].

The sample size of 661 subjects provides sufficient statistical power for the effective training of neural architectures, considering that previous studies have shown satisfactory results with similar or smaller samples [21, 64, 71]. The number of observations exceeds the minimum requirements to prevent overfitting and ensure model generalization, which complies with the optimal relationship between the number of parameters and the amount of available data recommended by the specialized literature on machine learning [69].

The complexity of the prediction model influences the minimum dataset size required to train the ANN. The ANN model included 43 adjustable parameters distributed across weights and biases (Fig. 2). Following Widrow’s rule of thumb, the required number of training examples can be estimated as: number of training examples = number of adjustable parameters / permitted network error [72]. With 43 adjustable parameters, this corresponds to 430 cases for a 10% permitted error and 860 cases for a 5% permitted error. Therefore, the available sample satisfies the 10% criterion, although it does not meet the more conservative 5% criterion. Accordingly, the ANN results are interpreted as a complementary predictive analysis to the PLS-SEM findings, supported by tenfold cross-validation, rather than as evidence of optimal generalization under the strictest sample-size assumption.Fig. 2Estimated deep learning ANN model A

The comparison between partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) and artificial neural networks (ANN) revealed critical differences in the identification of key predictors of bet intention and gambling behavior. In the previously reported PLS-SEM analysis, subjective norms and self-congruence emerged as significant predictors of Bet intention (β = 0.594 and β = 0.351, respectively), while perception of goodwill showed no relevant influence (β = − 0.011). However, the ANN sensitivity analysis for bet intention shows a different hierarchy: Subjective norms maintain its dominance (100% normalized importance), but self-congruence (40.72%) and goodwill (24.66%) take on substantial roles, followed by involvement (29.33%) and perceived exposure (19.56%). This divergence highlights the ability of neural networks to detect nonlinear contributions that linear models overlook.

The PLS-SEM model identified bet intention as the main predictor (β = 0.681). The ANN results for model B with dependent variable gambling behavior confirm this pre-eminence (100% normalized significance), but reveal additional contributions not captured linearly: Subjective norms (40.67%), goodwill (22.85%), and self-congruence (26.43%) show nontrivial influences. This methodological contrast is reflected in the predictive superiority of ANNs, whose nonlinear relationships explain substantially larger variances than PLS-SEM models.

Conclusions

The literature on sports sponsorship by CGP has consistently documented that exposure to sponsorship and advertising influences perceptions of gambling and can increase the intention to gamble [5, 6, 10]. However, less is known about how sponsorship-related psychosocial variables operate jointly when both linear and nonlinear analytical approaches are considered.

In our analysis using the PLS-SEM model, we observed that subjective norms and self-congruence are the main predictors of gambling intention, which is consistent with the findings of Moore and Ohtsuka [49] and Miller and Howell [50], who highlighted the importance of social influences and personal identification with the sponsor in this type of behavior. Likewise, goodwill showed a limited effect in the PLS-SEM model, confirming previous studies in which its relevance was secondary to other psychosocial factors [7, 43].

On the other hand, perceived exposure to sponsorship, although less explored in previous studies, emerges in our analysis as a factor that can intensify the relationship between psychosocial variables and the bet intention, in line with recent observations [6, 10]. Regarding involvement, our results show an influence that remains stable and consistent with previous research, where its impact may vary depending on the context and nature of the sponsorship [5, 50].

When applying artificial neural networks (ANN), significant differences were observed with respect to the results obtained using PLS-SEM and those reported in the traditional literature. The ANN analysis identified that variables such as goodwill and perceived exposure, which play a secondary or insignificant role in the linear model, acquire greater relative importance when considering possible interactions and complex relationships between variables. Specifically, goodwill, which was not significant in the PLS-SEM model, showed considerable weight in the ANN model, as evidenced by the existence of threshold effects or combinations of factors that enhance its influence. This result is consistent with the findings of Dwyer et al. [4], who highlighted the ability of ANNs to detect patterns and relationships that are not evident in conventional models.

The joint analysis of subjective norms, self-consistency, and goodwill using artificial neural networks (ANN) allows us to identify complex interactions and patterns that are not apparent in traditional linear models, showing that the impact of goodwill on the bet intention depends on its combination with other psychosocial variables and not on its isolated effect, which helps explain why previous literature found inconsistent results regarding its relevance [4, 7, 43, 49].

The differences observed between the SEM and ANN results can be explained by the distinct analytical logic of both techniques. SEM estimates linear and additive relationships based on theoretically specified causal paths, which makes it suitable for confirming stable structural relationships among constructs [56]. In contrast, ANN is able to capture complex and nonlinear interaction patterns that may not be detected in a linear framework, prioritising variables according to their contribution to overall predictive performance [52]. As a result, the variables that appear as significant in SEM reflect consistent linear effects aligned with the proposed theoretical model, whereas the variables that obtain higher relevance in the ANN analysis reflect their influence under nonlinear combinations of inputs. This complementarity supports the value of adopting a hybrid SEM–ANN approach, as it offers a more comprehensive interpretation of how sponsorship-related perceptions influence betting intention and behaviour.

Theoretical and practical implications

The results of this study have important implications for both theory and practice. From a theoretical perspective, they highlight the need to move beyond traditional models that analyze the determinants of betting behavior in isolation and advocate for the adoption of frameworks that consider the complex interaction between psychosocial variables such as subjective norms, self-congruence, goodwill, involvement, and perceived exposure. This integrative approach provides a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying the influence of sports sponsorship on betting intentions and explains why the results of previous literature have been inconsistent or fragmented.

In practical terms, these findings suggest that gambling prevention and regulation strategies should pay particular attention to not only reducing advertising exposure but also identifying risk profiles defined by a combination of psychosocial and contextual factors. Thus, public policies and interventions in the field of responsible gambling can benefit from models that allow for the anticipation of situations in which the interaction of these variables significantly increases the probability of gambling, facilitating the design of more effective campaigns and regulations that are adapted to the real complexity of the phenomenon. Some actions that could be implemented to reduce the risk of harmful sports betting behavior could be: (1) implementing ‘branding-free windows’ during critical moments of sporting events to interrupt emotional associations. Measures such as the partial reduction of exposure or the introduction of branding-free windows during emotionally intense moments of the event imply a rebalancing of sponsorship objectives. While sponsors typically seek not only short-term commercial returns but also brand recognition, favourable attitudes and long-term image effects, these measures may temporarily limit brand presence at peak-engagement moments. However, they also contribute to reinforcing reputational legitimacy and socially responsible positioning, which can become part of the broader value proposition of the sponsorship for both sponsors and sport organisations. (2) developing educational campaigns that dismantle the self-congruence between sporting identity and betting brands through role models; and (3) creating predictive systems with ANN that identify risk configurations in real time and activate personalized messages that counteract cognitive biases during peaks of perceived exposure [4, 6].

Limitations and future research lines

First, because the sample consists of adults who had gambled at least once, and data were collected using a cross-sectional self-report survey, we cannot establish strong causal inferences or rule out the influence of other determinants of gambling behaviour, such as the general popularity and availability of betting services. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of the incremental association of sponsorship-related exposure, involvement and psychosocial variables with betting intention and behaviour, rather than as proof that sports sponsorship is the sole or primary cause of gambling.

Other limitation of this study lies in the restriction of the sample to Spanish participants, which hinders the generalization of the results to other cultural and geographical contexts. Furthermore, the use of a cross-sectional design based on self-reported surveys prevents the establishment of robust causal relationships and may be subject to social desirability or recall bias. Although the combination of linear (PLS-SEM) and nonlinear (ANN) models enhances analytical robustness, the complexity of neural networks complicates the interpretation of underlying mechanisms and may affect the replicability of findings in other settings. Additionally, emotional variables and local regulations were not incorporated, which could limit a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon under study.

While age and gender differences are highly relevant, the present study was not designed to perform multi-group comparisons, as its primary objective was to model the psychosocial determinants of betting intention and behaviour. We have now acknowledged this issue as a limitation and an avenue for future research, given the documented vulnerability of young people and men to gambling advertising [5, 76].

In future research, it is recommended to adopt longitudinal and experimental designs that allow for the analysis of the real impact of advertising and marketing strategies on gambling behavior and the emergence of associated problems. Segmenting users through psychographic and personality models is also pertinent for identifying risk profiles and adapting preventive and communication strategies. Finally, it is suggested to explore the effects of new advertising formats, such as social media and gamification, and to expand studies to different legal and cultural contexts in order to achieve a more global and applicable perspective.
