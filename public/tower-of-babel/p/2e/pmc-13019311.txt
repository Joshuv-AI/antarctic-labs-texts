The birth, death, and evolutionary compensation of uORFs in Drosophila
Authors: Mengze Xu, Chenlu Liu, Wanting Jin, Yuanqiang Sun, Yuange Duan, Xiaolu Tang, Jian Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpstream open reading frames (uORFs) are critical regulators of messenger RNA translation, yet their evolutionary dynamics remain poorly understood. Here, we analyze uORF evolution across Drosophila species and uncover pervasive birth–death turnover. This process is characterized by a persistent excess of upstream start codon (uATG) gains over losses, shaped by the interplay of mutational input and natural selection. We find that the evolutionary conservation of uATGs is strongly associated with translational evidence, indicating a tight coupling between uORF retention and translational output. Lineage-specific uATGs are linked to reduced translation of downstream coding sequences, revealing lineage-dependent regulatory effects. We further identify evolutionary compensation between uATG gain and loss events within genes, supported by functional assays demonstrating frequent and condition-dependent effects on translation. At the population level, canonical uORF variants show signatures of population-specific selection, suggesting a role for uORF turnover in local adaptation. Together, our results reveal how natural selection, translational regulation, and evolutionary turnover jointly shape the uORF landscape in Drosophila.

Introduction

Organisms have evolved mechanisms to regulate gene expression at multiple levels, including the transcriptional, post-transcriptional, and translational stages [1, 2]. In eukaryotes, the translation of messenger RNAs (mRNAs) is controlled by a variety of regulatory mechanisms, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), RNA-binding proteins, and cis-regulatory elements [3–6]. Among these, upstream open reading frames (uORFs) are among the most prevalent post-transcriptional regulatory elements in eukaryotic genomes [7–11]. uORFs are small ORFs whose start codons are located in the 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR) of eukaryotic mRNAs and typically decrease the translation of downstream coding sequences (CDSs) by sequestering or competing for ribosomes. uORFs play crucial roles in organismal development [12–15], and disruptions of uORF-mediated translational regulation have been implicated in human disease [16–22], including cancer [23, 24]. Some uORFs are responsive to environmental stresses, providing a mechanism for organisms to adapt to changes in their environment swiftly [25–29]. Additionally, some uORFs encode functional peptides that have regulatory roles, such as acting as signaling molecules or modulating the translational activities of downstream CDSs [30–33].

As a class of crucial translational regulators, uORFs are widespread across eukaryotic genomes, but their repressive effects on downstream CDSs are heterogeneous. Some uORFs cause leaky scanning of small ribosome subunits during translation and do not inhibit CDS translation, whereas others have repressive effects that vary and are often correlated with the Kozak context surrounding the ATG start codons of uORFs (uATGs), the conservation levels of uATGs, and the positions of uORFs in 5′ UTRs [12, 34, 35]. uORFs with more conserved uATGs tend to have stronger repressive effects on the translation of downstream CDSs [12, 20]. In addition to their heterogeneous effects, uORFs also present considerable variability in their evolutionary trajectories [9]. Some uORFs are conserved across species, suggesting that they may play essential roles in cellular functions or regulatory mechanisms. In contrast, others are species-specific or lineage-specific, indicating recent origins and ongoing evolution. Novel uORFs can arise through various mechanisms, such as mutations, insertions, or rearrangements in the 5′ UTRs. Notably, newly emerged uORFs are often subject to negative selection at the population level, as they tend to suppress translation of downstream CDSs and disrupt normal translational regulation [36–43]. However, positive selection can also promote the fixation of newly arising uORFs with adaptive functions, as demonstrated in both flies and primates [12, 34]. Once an adaptive uORF is fixed within a population, it is usually maintained by natural selection due to functional constraints [9]. Consequently, polymorphic uORFs—those arising from novel mutations or disruptions of existing uORFs—often have deleterious effects in populations of humans [16–22, 43] and flies [12]. Despite these insights, the functional importance and evolutionary dynamics of uORFs remain complex and incompletely understood. Fine-scale patterns of uORF gain and loss events, their evolutionary trajectories, and their roles in the evolution of translational regulation warrant further investigation.

In this study, we investigate the evolutionary dynamics of uORFs in Drosophila, with a particular focus on their turnover and potential compensatory mechanisms. By integrating comparative genomics, ribosome profiling, and population genetic analyses, we examine how uORF gain and loss events are shaped by translational constraints and natural selection across evolutionary timescales. Together, this work provides a systematic framework for understanding how regulatory elements embedded within 5′ UTRs evolve and contribute to gene regulatory variation.

Materials and methods

Library construction, PacBio sequencing, and genome assembly of D. simulans

We used the Drosophila simulans sim4 strain (UCSD_14021–0251.216) for genome sequencing and assembly. DNA was isolated from virgin females. For next-generation sequencing, short-insert paired-end (100 bp) library construction and sequencing with an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform were performed by Beijing Nuohe Zhiyuan Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). For PacBio sequencing, DNA extraction, SMRTbell library (20-kb library size) construction, and sequencing on the PacBio Sequel platform with one single-molecule real-time (SMRT) cell were performed by Wuhan NextOmics (https://www.grandomics.com/) (Wuhan, China). We assembled the PacBio data alone with two independent de novo assemblers, Falcon [44] and Canu v1.7 [45], and error-corrected the sequence contigs with Arrow [46] and Pilon v1.22 [47], using PacBio reads and paired-end short reads (Illumina HiSeq) separately, and then merged the two assemblies using Quickmerge [48]. Afterward, we scaffolded the assemblies with the software mscaffolder [49], using previously published D. simulans assemblies as the reference. Finally, we filtered the Wolbachia and Acetobacter genomes using Kraken v1 [50] and filtered short redundant contigs on the basis of Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) v5.4.3 [51] and self-aligned Mummer v3.23 [52] results. The assembly quality was assessed using BUSCO with OrthoDB diptera_odb10 dataset.

Identification and inference of uORF gain and loss events

We downloaded the whole-genome sequence alignment (maf) of D. melanogaster (dm6) along with 26 other insect species from the University of California Santa Cruz (UCSC) genome browser (genome.ucsc.edu) [53]. We set repetitive sequences to lowercase (soft-masked) via RepeatMasker v4.1.1 [54] for the PacBio D. simulans genome and aligned it to the D. melanogaster genome dm6 following UCSC guidelines with Lastz v1.04.33 [55] in runLastzChain.sh. The Lastz alignment parameters and the scoring matrix were the same as the dm6 and droSim1 parameters used in the UCSC Genome Browser. Chained alignments were processed into nets using the chainNet and netSyntenic programs. This alignment was integrated into the multiple alignments of 27 species with Multiz v11.2 [56]. On the basis of the genome annotation of D. melanogaster (FlyBase r6.04, https://flybase.org/), the 5′ UTR sequence of each annotated transcript of D. melanogaster and its corresponding sequences in the other 26 insects were extracted from the multiple alignments via Biopython v1.78 tools [57]. The C/G content, the number of each nucleotide, and occurrences of dinucleotide for each sequence were calculated by faCount v362 [58]. We identified all the ATG triplets in the 5′ UTRs of each species and then removed the ATG triplets that overlapped with any annotated CDS region. For the filtered ATG triplets, we used the GLOOME program v1.266 [59] with default parameters to infer the occurrence of gain and loss for each ATG based on stochastic mapping and maximum parsimony. For each gene of D. melanogaster, we first included all the uATGs in any 5′ UTR sequence annotated in FlyBase for that gene and then removed redundant uATGs on the basis of their genomic coordinates.

mRNA-seq and Ribo-seq data processing and translational efficiencies of uORFs

We analyzed Ribo-seq and matched mRNA-seq data of 21 samples of D. melanogaster from previous studies [12, 60–63], including 10 samples from different developmental stages and tissues (0–2, 2–6, 6–12, and 12–24 h embryos, larvae, pupae, female and male bodies, and heads) from our previous studies [12], mature oocytes and activated eggs from Kronja et al. [60], germline stem cells at different differentiation stages from Samuels et al. [61], S2 cells from Douka et al. [62], and 0–2 h embryos from Dunn et al. [63] (Supplementary Table S1). The 3′ adapters were trimmed via Cutadapt v3.0 [64], and the next-generation sequencing reads were mapped to the genomes of yeast, Wolbachia, virus, and the sequences of transfer RNAs, ribosomal RNAs, small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), or small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) of D. melanogaster (FlyBase r6.04) via Bowtie2 version v2.2.3 [65] with the parameters “-p8 –local -k1.” The mapped reads on these genomes/sequences were further removed from the downstream analysis.

The remaining Ribo-seq and matched mRNA-seq reads were mapped to the reference genome of D. melanogaster (FlyBase, r6.04) using STAR v2.4.2a [66]. For Ribo-seq reads, we assigned a primarily mapped ribosome-protected fragment (RPF), which is 27–34 nucleotides (nt) in length, to its P-site with PSite [67]. We calculated the mRNA coverage of each nucleotide site following the methods of our previous study [12, 63]. We then summed the mRNA or P-site coverage values of all the nucleotide positions within each uORF or CDS region to obtain the overall coverage [12, 63]. For each codon, P-sites corresponding to the first nucleotide position of the codon and its flanking 1-nt regions (both upstream and downstream) were assigned to calculate the P-site coverage for that codon. For uORFs overlapping with CDSs, only the non-overlapping regions were used when the mRNA or P-site coverage was calculated. The translational efficiency (TE) of the uORF was calculated as the ratio of P-site RPF reads per kilobase per million reads (RPKM) to mRNA RPKM (RPKMP-site/RPKMmRNA). For each gene, the transcript with the highest mRNA RPKM was used. In all analyses related to the TEs of the uORFs, only the most highly expressed transcripts with an mRNA RPKM_CDS > 1 in a specific sample were used, and only the uORFs with an mRNA RPKM_uORF > 1 in a specific sample were used.

The mRNA-seq and Ribo-seq data with harringtonine treatment of S2 cells from our previous study [12] were used to identify the translation initiation sites (TISs) and estimate the initiation signal. We identified the ribosome occupancy peaks by requiring the P site coverage of the +1 codon to be larger than that at the +2 codon and the summed coverage of the −1 and −2 triplets, according to the previous study [12, 68]. The codon that shows the ribosome occupancy peak and has a P-site coverage of no <3 and an mRNA RPKM >1 was regarded as the predicted TIS codon. Then, we calculated the TIS signal for each predicted TIS codon as RPKMP-site/RPKMmRNA in the window centering the predicted TIS codon (−1, +4) [34, 69].

To complement our findings, we re-analyzed Ribo-seq and matched mRNA-seq data for nine developmental stages of D. simulans (embryos 2–6, 6–12, and 12–24 h; larvae; pupae; female and male bodies and heads) that we previously generated [70]. Reads were mapped to our newly assembled D. simulans genome, and RPKM values and TEs for CDSs and uORFs were calculated using the same pipeline employed for D. melanogaster, ensuring cross-species comparability. All TE values were normalized in subsequent comparisons between different samples of D. melanogaster and/or D. simulans.

Branch length scores and Kozak scores of uATGs

Branch length scores (BLSs) and Kozak scores of uATGs were calculated following previously described methods [12, 34]. Briefly, BLSs were computed using multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic trees of 27 insect species (23 Drosophila species and 4 outgroup species) obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser. For each uATG, the BLS was defined as the ratio of the summed branch lengths of the subtree comprising species in which the uATG is present to the total branch length of the full 27-species phylogeny.The Kozak sequence was defined as the nucleotide region spanning positions −6 to +4 relative to the ATG, with positions +1 to +3 corresponding to the start codon itself. A position weight matrix (PWM) was constructed using the −6 to −1 and +4 positions of ATGs from annotated main CDSs. For each uATG, the Kozak score was calculated as the log-odds score of its surrounding sequence based on the PWM. Kozak scores were calculated separately for D. melanogaster and D. simulans.

Matched random-sampling test for compensatory uATG gain and loss

We used a matched random-sampling approach to test whether the observed numbers of genes exhibiting loss–gain or gain–loss uATG compensation differed significantly from random expectations while controlling for 5′ UTR length and C/G content. For example, to evaluate loss–gain compensation, we first identified 203 genes with ancestral uATG loss on branch b3. For each such gene, we randomly sampled a gene from all protein-coding genes in D. melanogaster that matched the focal gene in 5′ UTR length and nucleotide composition. Specifically, the sampled gene was required to differ in 5′ UTR length by <20 nt and to fall within the same C/G content stratum, defined using 20 equal-width bins. We then counted how many of the sampled genes overlapped with the set of 1604 genes exhibiting uATG gain on branch b1. This random sampling procedure was repeated 1000 times to generate an empirical null distribution, which was compared with the observed number of genes showing loss–gain compensation. Gain–loss compensation was assessed using the same matched sampling strategy. We additionally restricted the analysis to uATGs located within 300 nt upstream of the CDS and repeated the procedure.

Mcdonald–kreitman test for newly fixed uATG gain and loss events in D. melanogaster

We applied McDonald–Kreitman (MK) tests to assess selection acting on newly fixed uATG gain and loss events in D. melanogaster, using uATG-altering variants in short introns as neutral controls. Specifically, newly fixed and polymorphic uATG gain- and loss-associated Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) located within the 8–30 nt region of short introns (<65 nt in length) were used to represent neutrally evolving sites. We first extracted 5′ UTR and short intron sequences from the D. melanogaster genome based on the reference genome annotation. Orthologous sequences from D. simulans and D. yakuba were obtained using multiple sequence alignments. ATG triplets within 5′ UTRs and within the 8–30 nt interval of short introns were identified, and uATG gain and loss events were inferred using a parsimony-based approach with D. yakuba as the outgroup. We next identified all SNPs within 5′ UTRs and short introns that resulted in uATG gain or loss across 1 356 worldwide D. melanogaster strains [71]. These SNPs were mapped to their orthologous genomic positions in D. simulans and D. yakuba using liftOver v377 [53]. uATGs located within repeat regions identified by RepeatMasker [54] or overlapping annotated CDS regions were excluded. Only polymorphic sites with a minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05 were retained. For newly fixed substitutions, corrections for multiple hits were applied using the Kimura two-parameter model [72]. Asymptotic MK tests were performed using the asymptoticMK framework [73]. Polymorphic sites in the neutral control regions were partitioned into frequency bins of equal size based on increasing derived allele frequency (DAF), and polymorphic sites in the test regions were binned using the same frequency breakpoints. MK tests were conducted separately for different classes of uATG gain and loss events.

Functional validation by the dual-luciferase reporter assay

To improve the expression efficiency in the D. melanogaster Schneider line 2 (S2) cell line, we used a reporter plasmid modified from the psiCHECK-2 vector (C8021, Promega). This modified reporter plasmid contains the promoter of the actin5C gene (−522 to −1 region) [74] for both the Renilla and firefly CDSs, and the Renilla and firefly CDSs were optimized according to codon usage in D. melanogaster. For Abd-B-J, alleles of the 5′ UTR from three species (D. melanogaster: ISO1 strain; D. simulans: w501 strain; D. yakuba: Tai18E2 strain) were amplified from 4 to 6 h embryo complementary DNA (cDNA) via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using PlatinumTM SuperFiTM II DNA polymerase (12361010, Invitrogen). To obtain the cDNA, we extracted the total RNA with the TRIzol reagent (15596018, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then synthesized the cDNA using the PrimeScript First-strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (6110A, TaKaRa). For other selected genes with uATG gains or losses, only the D. melanogaster allele of the 5′ UTR was amplified from cDNA. The 5′ UTR sequence was subsequently ligated into the plasmid between the promoter and the CDS of Renilla luciferase. Mutations were introduced via primers with mutated regions to construct plasmids with the anc1 or anc2 allele. The PCR products were purified via an EasyPure Quick Gel Extraction Kit (EG101-01, TransGen Biotech), and ligation was performed via a pEASY-Basic Seamless Cloning and Assembly Kit (CU201-02, TransGen Biotech). Finally, all the plasmids were validated via Sanger sequencing. All the primers or synthesized DNA fragments used in the plasmid vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

We transfected S2 cells with different 5′ UTR sequences for Renilla luciferase with a LipofectamineTM 3000 Transfection Kit (L3000008, Invitrogen). For each sample, six biological replicates were performed for luciferase activity measurement, and six biological replicates were performed to measure the mRNA expression for each luciferase. The samples were treated with either water (normal conditions) or 200 μM NaAsO2 (stress conditions) at 21 h after transfection, and the activities of the Renilla luciferase enzyme and firefly luciferase enzyme were measured via the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1960, Promega) at 24 h after transfection. Moreover, RNA extraction from cells and reverse transcription were performed via a HieffTM Fast Cell Direct SYBR Green RT-qPCR Kit (11172ES60, Yeasen), and qPCR was performed via PowerUpTM SYBRTM Green Master Mix (A25742, Applied Biosystems) to measure the mRNA expression of Renilla luciferase and firefly luciferase. All reagents and reaction times were used as specified in the manufacturer’s instructions. The primers used for qPCR are also listed in Supplementary Table S2. Real-time qPCR was performed on the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). The comparative cycle threshold method was used to quantify gene expression changes. Firefly luciferase was used as the internal control to calculate the relative enzyme activity and relative mRNA expression of Renilla luciferase. The protein/mRNA ratio of Renilla luciferase was subsequently calculated by dividing the relative enzyme activity by the mean relative mRNA expression. The normalized protein/mRNA ratio was calculated by dividing the protein/mRNA ratio of each sample by the average protein/mRNA ratio of the D. melanogaster allele under each condition.

Identification of uORF-altering variants and signatures of selection in D. melanogaster natural populations

We analyzed genetic variants (SNPs and indels) from a worldwide D. melanogaster population genomic dataset comprising 1356 genomes from our previous study [71]. Variants affecting uORFs were identified using UTRannotator [75]. Gain or loss events of uATGs or uSTOP codons were determined based on inferred ancestral alleles reconstructed from orthologous sequences in D. simulans and D. yakuba. To identify recent signals of positive selection in the China (CN), France (FR), North America (RAL), Ethiopia (EF), South Africa (SD), and Zambia (ZI) populations, we calculated pairwise FST values for each variant using VCFtools v0.1.16 [76] and then computed population branch statistics (PBSs) [77]. PBS analyses were conducted based on predefined population tree topologies following a previous study [71]. Briefly, branch-specific selection in each of the three non-African populations (CN, FR, and RAL) was inferred using the subtree [CN, (FR, RAL), EF], whereas selection in each of the three sub-Saharan populations (ZI, SD, and EF) was inferred using the subtree [(CN, EF), (SD, ZI)]. As an example, CN-specific candidate adaptive variants were defined as variants that (i) ranked within the top 1% of PBS values for CN in both the [(CN, FR), EF] and [(CN, RAL), EF] trio tests, (ii) did not rank within the top 1% of PBS values for FR or RAL in their respective trio tests, and (iii) exhibited a higher DAF in CN than in any other population. The same strategy was applied to identify population-specific selection in the other focal populations.

Identification of uATG gain and loss compensations within D. melanogaster natural populations

In each population, we calculated the linkage disequilibrium coefficient D for each pair of uATG gain and loss mutations located on the same transcript. Consider the ancestral allele as ab, where a undergoes uATG gain to become A, and b undergoes uATG loss to become B. We calculated counts and frequencies (P) of the haplotypes ab, aB, Ab, and AB in each population, using only individuals who were homozygous at both loci. Then, we computed the D using the formula D = PABPab − PaBPAb. We performed two-sided Fisher’s exact test using the counts of these four haplotypes to determine whether the two loci were significantly associated. The false discovery rate (FDR) was calculated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Modeling the co-occurrence of canonical and non-canonical uORFs using a poisson framework

To test whether non-canonical uORFs co-occur with high-confidence canonical uORFs within the same 5′ UTRs more or less frequently than expected by chance, we modeled uORF occurrence along 5′ UTRs using a Poisson process. We assumed that the number of high-confidence canonical or non-canonical uORFs per unit length of 5′ UTR follows a Poisson distribution with rate μ, such that a 5′ UTR of length L has an expected uORF count of μ × L. Using all annotated coding genes in D. melanogaster, we calculated the total 5′ UTR length and the total numbers of high-confidence canonical uORFs (n = 7068) and non-canonical uORFs (n = 19 280), from which the corresponding rates μhigh and μnon-canonical were estimated. For a gene with 5′ UTR length L, the probability of harboring at least one uORF of each type was calculated as P = 1 − exp(−μ × L). Based on these probabilities, each gene was assigned expected weights for belonging to one of three categories (both uORF types, canonical only, or non-canonical only), and expected counts across the 7002 genes were obtained by summing these weights.

Results

Identification of candidate uORFs and their translational evidence in D. melanogaster

We identified canonical uORFs in all annotated transcripts of protein-coding genes in D. melanogaster, defining them as sequences that initiate at an upstream ATG codon (uATG) within the 5′ UTR and terminate at an in-frame TAA, TAG, or TGA stop codon, which may reside within or extend beyond the 5′ UTR. Because the start codon is the defining and most evolutionarily conserved feature of a canonical uORF [9, 34], we focused on uATGs as the primary units in subsequent analyses (Fig. 1A). To avoid redundancy, each unique uATG was counted only once when multiple uORFs shared the same start codon but differed in stop codons across transcript isoforms of the same gene. In contrast, uORFs initiated from distinct in-frame uATGs within the same transcript (e.g. ATGNNNATGNNN…) were treated as separate entities, as each start codon independently initiates translation and can independently repress downstream CDS translation. In total, we identified 36 565 distinct uATGs that potentially function as uORF start codons in D. melanogaster, of which ~2% (n = 747) corresponded to N-terminal extensions of annotated CDSs; these were also retained as uATGs in subsequent analyses.

To assess uORF translation, we analyzed previously published mRNA-seq and matched Ribo-seq datasets spanning multiple developmental stages and tissues, including germline stem cells [61], mature oocytes, and activated eggs [60], S2 cells [62], and embryos, larvae, pupae, and adult tissues [12, 63] of D. melanogaster (Supplementary Table S1). Ribosome P-site positions were assigned using PSite [67], and all datasets exhibited clear three-nucleotide periodicity in both CDS and uORF regions despite differences in experimental protocols (Supplementary Fig. S1). TE was quantified as the ratio of P-site RPF RPKM to mRNA RPKM, considering only transcripts with mRNA RPKM > 1. Using these criteria, 29 844 candidate uORFs exhibited detectable ribosome occupancy (TE > 0.1) in at least one D. melanogaster sample (Supplementary Table S3). Among these, 25 265 candidate uORFs contained at least one mapped P-site RPF covering the start codon (uATG) (Supplementary Table S4) and were retained for downstream analyses.

To obtain higher-confidence evidence of active translation, we further applied three independent translation detection algorithms—ribORF [78], Ribo-TISH [79], RiboCode [80]—which identify translated ORFs based on the three-nucleotide periodicity of ribosome-protected footprints. Across samples, 6408–11 190 translated CDSs were detected by at least one method, whereas 1348–6948 translated uORFs were identified per sample (Supplementary Table S5), consistent with the weaker translation, shorter length, and lower ribosome occupancy of uORFs compared with canonical CDSs. In parallel, we analyzed our previously published mRNA-seq and Ribo-seq datasets from Drosophila S2 cells treated with harringtonine, a translation elongation inhibitor that stalls ribosomes at TISs and thereby enables precise mapping of initiating ribosomes [12]. This analysis identified 1602 ATGs located in 5′ UTRs that exhibited distinct ribosome occupancy peaks after filtering for sites with P-site coverage ≥ 3 and mRNA RPKM > 1.

Based on these complementary lines of translational evidence, the 36 565 computationally identified canonical uATGs gave rise to 25 265 uORFs with detectable translation, which were classified into three confidence categories. High-confidence uORFs (n = 7068) exhibited robust translational signatures, defined by significant translation detected by at least one of the three algorithms in at least three tissues and/or support as TISs in harringtonine-treated S2 cells. Moderate-confidence uORFs (n = 5 718) were detected as significantly translated in one or two tissues by at least one algorithm, whereas low-confidence uORFs (n = 12 479) displayed weak or marginal translational signals that did not meet stringent criteria for confident translation. The remaining 11 300 uORFs lacked detectable translation under the conditions examined and were therefore classified as putative uORFs.

In addition to canonical uORFs, non-canonical uORFs initiated by near-cognate codons (e.g. ACG, CTG) can also function as TISs, albeit with substantially lower initiation efficiency depending on sequence context [34, 81]. Analysis of harringtonine-treated Ribo-seq data [12] identified 19 280 near-cognate initiation codons in 5′ UTRs that exhibited ribosome occupancy peaks; however, TIS signals associated with them were significantly weaker than those of uATGs (Supplementary Fig. S2). Together, these canonical and non-canonical uORFs provided a comprehensive foundation for subsequent comparative and evolutionary analyses.

Global birth-and-death dynamics of uATGs in Drosophila

We utilized uATGs as unambiguous mutational units for tracking canonical uORF birth–death turnover via comparative genomics. This focus avoids the context-dependent complexities of near-cognate initiation codons, which were instead examined separately as a downstream functional layer. Using this uATG-centric framework, we analyzed whole-genome sequence alignments from 27 insect species obtained from the UCSC Genome Browser [53], including 23 Drosophila species and 4 outgroup insects. We identified a critical limitation in the widely used D. simulans genome assembly (droSim1) [82], which exhibited extensive fragmentation and alignment gaps in untranslated regions, thereby hindering accurate inference of uATG evolution. To overcome this limitation, we generated a high-quality female D. simulans genome assembly using SMRT (PacBio) sequencing [83], achieving over 80-fold coverage and a BUSCO completeness score [51] of 99.1% (Supplementary Table S6). Replacing droSim1 with this assembly substantially improved 5′ UTR alignment quality and increased the number of inferred uATGs from 31 534 to 35 727 (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Using the improved alignments, we developed a bioinformatics workflow to infer the evolutionary trajectories of uATGs across the 27 insect species, anchored on the 5′ UTR annotations of D. melanogaster (Supplementary Fig. S3). In total, we identified 668 375 uATGs across these genomes (Supplementary Table S7), with counts ranging from 2178 in Apis mellifera to 36 565 in D. melanogaster. As expected, the number of detected uATGs was strongly correlated with the length of aligned 5′ UTRs across species (Spearman’s ρ = 0.99, P = 4.91 × 10−9, Supplementary Fig. S4). This strong dependence indicates that interspecies variation in uATG numbers largely reflects ascertainment bias arising from D. melanogaster-based annotations rather than true biological differences.

From orthologous sequence alignments, we inferred 302 849 uATG gain events and 32 837 loss events, with substantial variation across evolutionary branches (Supplementary Fig. S5). Overall, 11 775 protein-coding genes experienced at least one uATG gain or loss event. The total number of uATG turnover events per gene correlated positively with 5′ UTR length (ρ = 0.82), with gain events showing a stronger correlation (ρ = 0.81) than loss events (ρ = 0.65) (Supplementary Fig. S6). Although uATG gains and losses were positively correlated within genes (ρ = 0.72), gains exceeded losses in 99.07% of genes (11 666 of 11 775). This excess of gains was consistently observed across 5′ UTRs stratified by distance from the CDS start site (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Gene Ontology analysis of the top 1000 genes with the highest total number of uATG gain and loss events revealed significant enrichment for developmental pathways, including “neuron differentiation,” “imaginal disc development,” and “tissue development” (Supplementary Fig. S8). Genes with the highest net uATG gains showed similar functional enrichment. Given prior evidence that uORFs proximal to the CDS start codon are more likely to exert regulatory effects on translation [12], we further examined uATG turnover within 300 nt upstream of the CDS. Among the top 1000 genes with the highest uATG turnover (Supplementary Fig. S9A) or net gains (Supplementary Fig. S9B) in this proximal region, developmental pathways remained significantly enriched after multiple-testing correction. Together, these results suggest that dynamic uATG gain and loss has contributed to the evolutionary fine-tuning of translational regulation during Drosophila development.

The role of nucleotide composition in uATG evolutionary dynamics

Comparative genomic analysis revealed substantial variation in uATG density across the 27 insect species, with an average of 7.74 uATGs per 1000 nt, ranging from 5.88 in Musca domestica to 9.46 in Apis mellifera (Supplementary Fig. S4). Because nucleotide composition varies across Drosophila species and is known to influence genomic features and evolutionary trajectories [84–87], we next examined how sequence composition contributes to uATG evolutionary dynamics. Specifically, we analyzed the relationship between uATG density and nucleotide composition within 5′ UTRs across the 23 Drosophila species. uATG density showed a positive correlation with thymine (T) content (ρ = 0.43, P = .04), whereas no significant associations were observed with adenine (A), cytosine (C), or guanine (G) content individually (Supplementary Fig. S10). In addition, uATG density exhibited a marginally significant negative correlation with overall C/G content (ρ = −0.38, P = .07; Supplementary Fig. S11). Analysis of dinucleotide composition further revealed positive associations between uATG density and several dinucleotides, including GpT, TpG, ApC, CpA, ApT, and TpA, whereas CpC, GpG, CpT, and GpA were negatively correlated with uATG density (FDR < 0.05; Supplementary Fig. S12).

Together, these results indicate that variation in mono- and dinucleotide composition can bias local sequence context toward or against the formation of ATG triplets, thereby modulating the propensity for uATG gain or loss. This compositional bias provides a mechanistic link between background sequence evolution and lineage-specific differences in uORF turnover.

Gains and losses of uATGs in D. melanogaster and D. simulans lineages

A potential limitation of uATG turnover analyses based on genome-wide multiple sequence alignments is sensitivity to misalignment artifacts, particularly when distantly related species are included. To minimize biases arising from alignment uncertainty and incomplete sequence coverage, we restricted our analysis to uATG gain and loss events within the D. melanogaster–D. simulans clade, using D. yakuba or D. ananassae as outgroups (Fig. 1B). These species diverged from D. melanogaster ~5.4, 12.8, and 44.2 million years (Myr) ago, respectively [88], providing a phylogenetic framework well suited for robust inference of lineage-specific uATG birth and death events.

Comparative analysis of 5′ UTR alignments revealed extensive uATG turnover despite substantial sequence conservation. While 30 975 uATGs are shared, we identified thousands of lineage-specific uATGs, including 5590 in D. melanogaster and 4752 in D. simulans. Because reliable inference of uATG gain and loss requires continuous sequence coverage across species, subsequent phylogenetic analyses were restricted to 5′ UTR regions with unambiguous alignment, excluding regions containing gaps that may reflect missing sequence rather than true evolutionary events. Within these reliably aligned regions, we inferred 2719 uATG gains and 994 losses along the D. melanogaster lineage, and 2226 gains and 813 losses along the D. simulans lineage (Fig. 1B). Thus, the majority of inferred uATG gain and loss events in both lineages were attributable to point mutations, as they involved nucleotide substitutions within reliably aligned sequence blocks. Although indel-mediated gain/loss events are more sensitive to incomplete sequence coverage, they may nevertheless play a secondary but non-negligible role in shaping uORF evolution (Supplementary Table S8).

Using branch-length-normalized estimates, we quantified uATG gain and loss rates across major branches across these Drosophila species. Along the D. melanogaster lineage (branch b1), uATGs accumulated at a rate of ∼504 gains per Myr, compared with ∼184 losses per Myr, yielding a net positive turnover (Fig. 1B and Table 1). A similar pattern was observed along the D. simulans lineage (b2), with gain and loss rates of ∼412 and ∼151 per Myr, respectively. In deeper branches, both gain and loss rates declined: the ancestral branch (b3) showed ∼268 gains and ∼42 losses per Myr, whereas older branches exhibited even lower loss rates (e.g. ∼34 losses per Myr in b4). Similarly, for the high-confidence and moderate-confidence uATGs combined in a whole, the gain rate was 151.7 per Myr in b1, while the loss rate was 45.6 uATGs per Myr in b2 (which has a similar length with b1). Together, these results suggest that while continuous mutational input generates a persistent pool of new uATGs, they are subject to rigorous filtering by purifying selection over time. This process manifests as a high net excess of gains in terminal lineages that becomes significantly attenuated in deeper branches, consistent with the cumulative impact of long-term selection on uORF retention.

Highly translated uORFs are more conserved and less likely to be lost during Drosophila evolution

We next examined the relationship between translational evidence and the evolutionary conservation of uATGs. Among uATGs identified in D. melanogaster, the fraction that was conserved across all four examined species (D. melanogaster, D. simulans, D. yakuba, and D. ananassae) declined progressively with decreasing translational confidence, from 57.9% for high-confidence uORFs to 50.4%, 44.2%, and 39.8% for moderate-confidence, low-confidence, and putative uORFs, respectively (P < .001, χ2 test, Fig. 1B). Consistent with this pattern, uATGs that originated after the divergence of D. ananassae and D. melanogaster were significantly enriched among uATGs with weak or undetectable translational evidence. This trend was robust across multiple phylogenetic contexts, including uATGs present in D. melanogaster, D. simulans, and D. yakuba but absent in D. ananassae, as well as uATGs shared only between D. melanogaster and D. simulans or unique to D. melanogaster (P < .001 in all cases, χ2 test; Fig. 1B).

We next asked whether translational evidence also predicts the likelihood of uATG loss. Focusing on uATGs identified in D. melanogaster and tracing their fate in the D. simulans and D. yakuba lineages using D. ananassae as an outgroup, we observed progressively increasing loss rates with decreasing translational confidence. For ancient uATGs that originated prior to the divergence of all four species, loss rates in the D. yakuba lineage were 1.2%, 1.6%, 1.9%, and 2.3% for high-, moderate-, low-confidence, and putative uORFs, respectively (P < .001, χ2 test). Similarly, among uATGs that arose before the split of D. melanogaster and D. yakuba, loss rates in the D. simulans lineage increased from 2.6% to 3.2%, 3.4%, and 4.3% across the same categories (P < .001; Fig. 1B).

To further quantify evolutionary conservation across a broader phylogenetic context, we calculated BLSs, which measure the phylogenetic conservation of uATGs across 27 insect species [12, 34]. Consistent with the above analyses, BLS values declined monotonically with decreasing levels of translational evidence for canonical uORFs (Fig. 1C). Non-canonical uORFs exhibited overall lower BLS values than high- and moderate-confidence canonical uORFs, a pattern that is examined in detail below. A similar trend was observed for uATGs located within 300 nt upstream of annotated CDSs (Supplementary Fig. S13).

Together, these results demonstrate a strong positive association between translational evidence and uATG evolutionary conservation across Drosophila. uATGs with stronger translational signatures tend to be more ancient and evolutionarily conserved, whereas uATGs with weak or undetectable translational evidence are predominantly younger, lineage-specific, and more prone to loss.

uATG conservation is associated with Kozak context strength and translational output

To examine whether evolutionary conservation of uATGs is linked to functional translational output, we first assessed the relationship between uATG conservation and TE of the corresponding uORF. Across 20 of 21 D. melanogaster samples, uATG BLS were significantly positively correlated with uORF TE (Fig. 2A and B), indicating that highly conserved uATGs are more likely to be efficiently translated. A similar pattern was observed for uORFs located within 300 nt upstream of annotated CDSs (Supplementary Fig. S14), supporting a general association between uORF evolutionary retention and translational activity.

Because translation initiation efficiency is strongly influenced by the nucleotide context surrounding the start codon (−6 to +1), known as the Kozak sequence [9], we next asked whether conserved uATGs are associated with optimized initiation contexts. We calculated Kozak scores for all canonical uORFs in D. melanogaster and D. simulans as previously described [12]. Kozak scores were highly correlated between orthologous uATGs shared by the two species (ρ = 0.616; Fig. 2C), indicating strong evolutionary conservation of initiation contexts. Moreover, Kozak scores increased progressively with translational confidence, with high-confidence uORFs exhibiting the strongest initiation contexts within canonical uORFs in both species (Supplementary Fig. S15). Consistently, uORFs with conserved uATGs showed significantly higher Kozak scores than species-specific uORFs in both species (two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test; Fig. 2D).

In D. melanogaster, TE of canonical uORFs was positively correlated with Kozak score across most of the analyzed samples (Fig. 2E). Concordantly, uORFs with conserved uATGs displayed significantly higher TE than species-specific uORFs in almost all samples (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. S16), indicating that optimized initiation contexts contribute to enhanced uORF translation. To further dissect these relationships, we performed three-way conditional correlation analyses in each of the 21 D. melanogaster samples. We found that Kozak score remained positively associated with TE after controlling for BLS, and BLS retained a strong positive association with Kozak score after controlling for TE. Notably, TE and BLS also showed a positive conditional correlation after controlling for Kozak score in 19 of 21 samples (Supplementary Fig. S17).

We next examined the evolutionary stability of uORF translation across species. Analysis of ribosome profiling data spanning nine developmental stages in D. simulans [70] revealed that, among uORFs translated in D. melanogaster, the fraction retaining detectable translation in D. simulans declined progressively from high-confidence to moderate-confidence, low-confidence, and putative uORFs (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, uORFs with conserved uATGs exhibited significantly higher TE than other uORFs in D. simulans (Supplementary Fig. S18).

Together, these results indicate that uATG conservation is tightly coupled to translational output across species. uORFs with conserved uATGs tend to harbor optimized Kozak contexts, exhibit higher TE, and maintain more stable translation across Drosophila lineages, whereas recently emerged uORFs typically show weaker initiation contexts, reduced translation, and increased lineage specificity. These findings suggest that selection progressively refines translation initiation contexts, thereby promoting the evolutionary retention and functional integration of uORFs.

Association between lineage-specific uATGs and reduced CDS translation in D. melanogaster

To examine lineage-specific translational regulation mediated by uORFs, we focused on uATGs present in D. melanogaster but absent in D. simulans. Analyses were restricted to non-putative uORFs with detectable transcription and translation in each tissue (uORF TE ≥ 0.1), and paired samples were further required to show evidence of both mRNA expression (RPKM ≥ 1) and active CDS translation (CDS TE ≥ 0.5) in both species. Across tissues, between 13 and 839 genes met these criteria. Drosophila melanogaster-specific uORFs were preferentially translated in adult tissues, particularly in heads and bodies, whereas substantially fewer such uORFs were detected in embryos and larvae. Gene Ontology analysis of genes harboring D. melanogaster-specific uORFs in adult tissues (1047 genes in total) revealed significant enrichment for development-related pathways, including organ morphogenesis, regulation of developmental processes, and neuron differentiation (Supplementary Fig. S19).

To quantify the regulatory impact of D. melanogaster-specific uATGs on downstream CDS translation, we calculated interspecific differences in CDS TE between D. melanogaster and D. simulans. Across the nine paired samples, genes harboring confident D. melanogaster-specific uATGs (high-, moderate-, or low-confidence categories pooled) exhibited overall lower CDS TE in D. melanogaster than in D. simulans, with the majority of samples showing consistent effect directions (Fig. 3C). Together, these results support a repressive role of lineage-specific uATGs on downstream CDS translation.

Drosophila melanogaster-biased uORFs are associated with lineage-specific reduction in CDS translation

To assess lineage-biased translational repression mediated by uORFs, we compared TE of orthologous uORFs with conserved uATGs between D. melanogaster and D. simulans. In each paired sample, uORFs were classified as D. melanogaster-biased if they were robustly translated in D. melanogaster but not in D. simulans, or if they were translated in both species but exhibited at least a two-fold higher TE in D. melanogaster.

To evaluate gene-level effects, we further restricted the analysis to genes whose translated uORFs were exclusively biased toward a single lineage. Genes harboring D. melanogaster–biased, confidently translated uORFs exhibited lower CDS TE in D. melanogaster than in D. simulans across all nine paired samples, with seven showing statistically significant differences (Fig. 3D). Notably, similar trends were also observed when confidently translated uORFs and putative uORFs were analyzed jointly (Supplementary Fig. S20). Together, these results indicate that lineage-biased translated uORFs are associated with reduced CDS TE in the corresponding lineage.

Abd-B exemplifies lineage-specific translational repression driven by uORF turnover

To illustrate how the evolutionary and functional principles described above manifest at a key developmental locus, we examined the Hox gene Abdominal B (Abd-B), which exhibits exceptionally extensive uORF turnover. Across 23 Drosophila species and 4 outgroup insects, Abd-B harbors 112 uATGs in D. melanogaster and shows 923 inferred uATG gains and 188 losses, indicating rapid and ongoing remodeling of uORFs within its 5′ UTRs (Supplementary Fig. S21). This turnover is highly concentrated in the Abd-B-J transcript isoform (GenBank accession NM_001316542.1), which contains an unusually long 5′ UTR (5649 nt) with 96 uATGs, whereas other Abd-B isoforms harbor far fewer uATGs (Supplementary Figs S22 and S23). RNA-seq data from the modENCODE project [89, 90] revealed robust expression of Abd-B-J during early embryogenesis in D. melanogaster, peaking at 4–6 h post-fertilization and declining thereafter. Ribosome profiling further provided translational evidence for 43 of the 96 uORFs (TE > 0.1) in at least one of 21 D. melanogaster samples (Supplementary Fig. S24), indicating substantial uORF translation from this transcript.

Comparative analysis identified 70 uATGs conserved across the Abd-B-J 5′ UTRs of D. melanogaster, D. simulans, and D. yakuba, alongside several lineage-specific uATGs (Fig. 4A). To test whether divergence within the Abd-B-J 5′ UTR contributes to lineage-specific translational regulation, we partitioned the UTR into distal and proximal regions (Fig. 4B) and assessed their regulatory effects using dual-luciferase reporter assays in S2 cells (Supplementary Fig. S25), under both basal conditions and sodium arsenite (NaAsO2)–induced stress, which triggers eIF2α phosphorylation and translational reprogramming [91, 92]. The distal region (2572 nt in length) exhibited significant interspecies differences in protein/mRNA ratios, whereas the proximal region adjacent to the CDS (3077 nt) showed comparable translational output across species (Fig. 4C and D), indicating that regulatory divergence is localized to a specific segment of the 5′ UTR.

This case highlights how uORF evolution can drive lineage-specific translational regulation at individual developmental loci.

Evolutionary compensation between uATG gain and loss events

To investigate whether uATG gain and loss events are evolutionarily coupled, we examined two complementary scenarios of uATG turnover in the D. melanogaster and D. simulans lineages (Fig. 5A). In the first scenario, genes experienced uATG loss along the b3 branch (from the most recent common ancestor [MRCA] of D. melanogaster and D. yakuba to the MRCA of D. melanogaster and D. simulans), followed by uATG gain in other regions of the 5′ UTR along the D. melanogaster (b1) branch (Fig. 5A). We identified 82 genes exhibiting this loss–gain pattern, significantly more than expected by chance (P < .001) based on matched random-sampling test that accounted for differences in 5′ UTR length and GC content (expected mean = 61 genes; 95% CI: 52–70; Fig. 5B). A similar enrichment was observed when the analysis was restricted to uATGs in D. melanogaster with translational evidence (Supplementary Fig. S27). In addition, uATGs exhibiting loss–gain patterns showed significantly higher Kozak scores in D. melanogaster than in D. simulans (P = .014; Supplementary Fig. S28), indicating that compensatory uORF gains are often accompanied by optimization of the initiation context. Together, these results indicate that a subset of uORFs gained in the D. melanogaster lineage may functionally compensate for prior uATG losses.

In the second scenario, genes gained uATGs along the b3 branch and subsequently lost uATGs along the b1 branch (Fig. 5A). We identified 231 genes displaying this gain–loss pattern, which again exceeded random expectations (P < .001; expected mean = 160 genes; 95% CI: 144–176; Fig. 5C), consistent with a reciprocal compensation mechanism.

In contrast, no comparable patterns were detected when the same analyses were applied to ATGs in short introns (Supplementary Fig. S29). Moreover, these patterns remained significant when analyses were restricted to uATG events occurring within 300 nt upstream of the CDS start codon. Specifically, 24 genes showed loss–gain compensation in this proximal region, significantly more than expected by chance (P = .01; expected mean = 16; 95% CI: 10–23; Supplementary Fig. S30A), while 107 genes exhibited gain–loss compensation (P < .001; expected mean = 64; 95% CI: 52–77; Supplementary Fig. S30B).

Selection shapes uATG gain and loss dynamics in D. melanogaster

To test whether uATG gain and loss in 5′ UTRs deviate from neutral expectations, we compared ATG density and turnover in 5′ UTRs with those in short intronic regions (positions 8–30 of introns <65 nt), which served as neutral controls [93]. In D. melanogaster, ATG triplets were significantly depleted in 5′ UTRs relative to short introns (8.07 versus 16.36 per 1000 nt; P < 2.2 × 10−16, Fisher’s exact test). Consistently, comparative genomic analysis showed both ATG gain and loss rates were markedly lower in 5′ UTRs (0.997 gains and 0.268 losses per 1000 nt) than in short introns (4.66 gains and 0.919 losses per 1000 nt; P < 2.2 × 10−16). Moreover, the ATG gain-to-loss ratio was significantly reduced in 5′ UTRs compared with short introns (3.72 versus 5.07; P = 2.02 × 10−6), indicating that uATG emergence—and thus uORF formation—is constrained by purifying selection. In addition, we shuffled 5′ UTR nucleotide sequences while preserving C/G content and dinucleotide composition. The shuffled sequences were observed with significantly higher ATG density than original 5′ UTRs (P = .008, Supplementary Fig. S31), demonstrating that the depletion of ATGs in authentic 5′ UTRs is not an artifact of base composition.

Because short introns have lower C/G content than 5′ UTRs (∼34% versus ∼44%), we further controlled for nucleotide composition by stratifying both regions into bins of comparable C/G content. Even after this correction, ATG density and gain-to-loss ratios remained significantly lower in 5′ UTRs (Supplementary Fig. S32), indicating that the constraint on uATG emergence cannot be explained solely by compositional differences. Together, these analyses show that although uATG gains outnumber losses over evolutionary time, both processes are globally suppressed in 5′ UTRs relative to neutral regions.

While purifying selection constrains uATG emergence, previous studies have shown that a substantial fraction of newly fixed uORFs in D. melanogaster are driven by positive selection [12, 34]. To quantify the contribution of adaptive evolution to uATG gain and loss, we applied the AsymptoticMK test [73], comparing newly fixed uATG-altering mutations in the D. melanogaster lineage with polymorphic variants in natural populations. We analyzed whole-genome population data from 1356 D. melanogaster individuals [71], using uATG-altering mutations in short introns as neutral controls. Consistent with previous observations [12, 34], the estimated fraction of adaptive substitutions (α) was 51.84% (95% CI: 39.11%–64.57%) for newly fixed uATG-gain mutations in D. melanogaster (Fig. 5D). Notably, α was higher for high- and moderate-confidence uATG gains (62.46% and 64.61%, respectively) than for low-confidence events (45.82%) or putative uORF gains (45.31%). Moreover, for the 82 genes exhibiting loss–gain compensation, α for uATG gains was 10.56% (95% CI: −35.66%–56.78%), likely reflecting limited statistical power.

Strikingly, the newly fixed uATG-loss mutations also showed strong signatures of positive selection, with α = 82.07% (95% CI: 72.68–91.46%) genome-wide and a similar estimate (84.86%, 95% CI: 72.12%–97.59%) among the 231 genes exhibiting gain–loss compensation (Fig. 5D). Comparable results were obtained when restricting the analysis to events within 300 nt upstream of the CDS start codon (Supplementary Fig. S33). Together, these findings indicate that while uATG emergence is generally constrained, both uATG gain and loss can be targets of positive selection after fixation, including in the context of compensatory uORF turnover.

Functional validation of uATG gain and loss events and evolutionary compensation

To experimentally assess the translational consequences of uATG gain and loss events, we performed dual-luciferase reporter assays in Drosophila S2 cells in both basal and stress (3 h NaAsO₂ treatment) conditions. We selected 11 genes representing four evolutionary scenarios: lineage-specific uATG gains, lineage-specific uATG losses, uORF loss–gain compensation, and uORF gain–loss compensation. As illustrative examples, we present ribosome profiling data showing robust ribosome coverage and clear 3-nt periodicity for representative genes from each scenario in tissues of D. melanogaster or D. simulans (Supplementary Fig. S34). For each gene, the D. melanogaster 5′ UTR was cloned upstream of a reporter, and ancestral alleles corresponding to inferred uATG states of the most recent common ancestors of D. melanogaster-D. simulans (anc1 allele) and, where applicable, D. melanogaster-D. yakuba (anc2 allele) were reconstructed. Translational output was quantified as the protein-to-mRNA ratio and normalized to the D. melanogaster allele under each condition (Figs 6 and 7).

Among genes with lineage-specific uATG gains, Mtpalpha and Manf each harbored a single uATG gain, whereas CG17163 contained five uATG gains, all specific to the D. melanogaster lineage. Compared with the D. melanogaster allele, the ancestral anc1 alleles of Mtpalpha and CG17163 exhibited significantly higher protein/mRNA ratios under both basal and stress conditions (Fig. 6A and B). A similar trend was observed for Manf under stress conditions, although the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6C). Together, these results are consistent with translational repression mediated by newly gained uORFs.

We next examined genes that experienced lineage-specific uATG losses. In smg, grp and Sdc, a uATG was lost specifically along the D. melanogaster lineage. For smg, the D. melanogaster allele showed a significantly higher protein/mRNA ratio than the anc1 allele under basal conditions, with a similar but non-significant trend under stress (Fig. 6D). For grp, the D. melanogaster allele consistently exhibited higher translational output than the anc1 allele under both conditions (Fig. 6E). No significant difference was detected for Sdc (Fig. 6F), indicating that the functional impact of uATG loss can be gene- and context-dependent.

Together, these results demonstrate that lineage-specific uATG gains and losses can exert predictable, often repressive, effects on downstream CDS translation. Building on this observation, we next asked whether uORF turnover also shows evidence of evolutionary compensation, whereby uORF loss and gain events occur sequentially within the same 5′ UTR to rebalance translational output.

In genes exhibiting uATG loss–gain compensation, Art4 and CG9933 each experienced a single uATG loss along the b3 branch followed by a subsequent uATG gain in the D. melanogaster lineage (b1), whereas nrv3 underwent one ancestral loss followed by two independent gains (Fig. 7A–C). For all three genes, the D. melanogaster allele exhibited significantly lower protein/mRNA ratios than both anc1 and anc2 alleles under basal and stress conditions, indicating stronger translational repression in the derived lineage. Although differences between anc1 and anc2 were significant only for nrv3 under stress, the consistently reduced translational output of the D. melanogaster alleles supports a model in which newly gained uORFs impose translational repression that functionally offsets earlier uORF losses.

We also examined genes showing the opposite trajectory, in which ancient uATG gains were followed by subsequent losses. In fz, one ancient uATG gain was followed by two losses in the D. melanogaster lineage, whereas Not1 experienced two gains and two losses (Fig. 7D and E). Under basal conditions, no significant differences were observed between the D. melanogaster and anc1 alleles for either gene; however, the anc1 allele exhibited significantly lower protein/mRNA ratios than the anc2 allele. Under stress conditions, the anc1 allele of fz showed significantly reduced translational output compared with both the D. melanogaster and anc2 alleles, whereas for Not1, the anc1 allele showed lower output than the D. melanogaster allele but was not significantly different from anc2. These results suggest that ancient uORF gains are frequently associated with translational repression, whereas the functional impact of subsequent uORF losses may be context-dependent and less readily detectable.

Overall, 75% (12 of 16) of the tested uATG gain or loss events significantly altered CDS protein/mRNA ratios under at least one condition. In all cases showing significant effects, uORF alterations were associated with repression of downstream CDS translation, and no instances of enhanced CDS translation were observed. Together, these functional assays support an important role for uORF turnover in shaping translational output during evolution.

Identification of canonical uORF variants in D. melanogaster populations

In addition to species-level analyses, we examined uORF variation at the population level using whole-genome sequencing data from 1356 D. melanogaster strains analyzed in our previous study [71]. Variants located in 5′ UTRs were annotated using UTRannotator [75] to identify mutations predicted to alter canonical uORFs. These variants were classified according to their predicted effects, including creation (uATG_gained) or disruption (uATG_lost) of uATG start codons, gain (uSTOP_gained) or loss (uSTOP_lost) of uORF stop codons, frameshift-disrupting indels (uFrameshift), and compound variants affecting multiple uORF features (Fig. 8A).

In total, we identified 56 671 variants predicted to alter uORFs across 8 434 genes (Supplementary Table S9; Supplementary Fig. S35). The vast majority of these variants were rare, with 92.08% exhibiting minor allele frequencies (MAF) below 5% (92.46% of SNPs and 91.55% of indels; Fig. 8B), consistent with pervasive purifying selection acting on uORF-associated regulatory elements. Among variant classes, uATG-gained variants were the most abundant (18 459; 32.57%) and were strongly enriched among very rare alleles (DAF < 0.01; Fig. 8C). These variants also exhibited the lowest Tajima’s D values (Supplementary Table S10), indicating particularly strong selective constraint against the emergence of newly introduced uORFs in natural populations.

We next asked whether compensatory uATG gain and loss events could be detected at the population level. To this end, we examined linkage disequilibrium between pairs of uATG gain and loss variants co-localized within the same transcript, performing analyses separately for each population to minimize confounding effects of population structure. We identified 24, 32, 61, 47, 24, and 78 compensatory events in the CN (China), FR (France), RAL (Raleigh, USA), EF (Ethiopia), SD (South Africa), and ZI (Zambia) populations, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S36A and Supplementary Table S11). Notably, three compensatory events (in CAP, CG9313, and CG9518) were detected across all six populations (Supplementary Fig. S36B–D). Overall, compensatory uATG gain and loss events were rare at the population level, suggesting that while such regulatory adjustments can arise within populations, they likely accumulate over longer evolutionary timescales and are constrained by the functional importance of uORF-mediated translational regulation.

Population-specific selection and local adaptation of canonical uORF variants in D. melanogaster

Although most uORF-altering variants segregate at low frequencies and are subject to purifying selection, a subset may rise to higher frequency within specific populations due to local adaptation. To identify such cases, we analyzed D. melanogaster strains from six populations (CN, FR, RAL, EF, SD, and ZI) and applied PBS analysis [77] based on established population phylogenies [71]. Variants were classified as population-specific if they ranked within the top 1% of PBS values for a given population across multiple trio comparisons, were not similarly enriched in other populations, and exhibited higher DAFs in the focal population.

Using this approach, we identified 115 uORF-altering variants across 105 genes showing evidence of population-specific selection (Supplementary Table S12). These variants encompassed multiple functional classes, including 25 uATG_gained variants, 33 uATG_lost variants, 19 uSTOP_gained variants, 10 uSTOP_lost variants, 21 uFrameshift variants, and 7 compound variants. Notably, uATG-loss variants were significantly enriched among population-specific candidate adaptive variants relative to their genome-wide frequency (33/115 versus 10 629/56 671; P = .008, Fisher’s exact test), whereas uATG-gain variants were significantly underrepresented (25/115 versus 18 459/56 671; P = .013, Fisher’s exact test). This asymmetry suggests that loss of uORFs is more likely than uORF gain to contribute to adaptive regulatory divergence at the population level.

Consistent with this interpretation, 17 population-specific uORF variants overlapped genomic regions previously identified as targets of positive selection in D. melanogaster populations [71] (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S13). These included both uATG-gained and uATG-lost variants showing markedly elevated DAFs in specific populations relative to global averages. Genes harboring these variants span multiple functional categories, including neural function (gry, sbb, TBPH, Cirl), metabolism (Octβ3R), DNA repair (tefu), and cell-cycle regulation (cmet), suggesting that uORF-altering mutations may contribute to local adaptation by modulating CDS translation in diverse biological pathways. Representative examples include a uATG-gained variant in gry and a uATG-lost variant in SCCRO3 in the CN population, as well as population-enriched uATG-altering variants in Octβ3R, TBPH, tefu, and cmet (Fig. 8D and E). Together, these results indicate that while uORF-altering variants are generally constrained within populations, a limited subset may be selectively favored in specific environments, highlighting a role for uORF turnover in population-specific regulatory evolution.

Non-canonical uORFs exhibit distinct evolutionary and translational properties

In addition to canonical uATGs, analysis of mRNA-seq and Ribo-seq data from harringtonine-treated S2 cells identified 19 280 candidate non-canonical start codons, which differ from ATG by a single nucleotide (Supplementary Fig. S2). We therefore examined how these non-canonical uORFs are distributed across transcripts and how their evolutionary and translational properties compare with those of canonical uATGs.

We first asked whether non-canonical uORFs co-occur with high-confidence canonical uORFs within the same 5′ UTRs. Across 7002 genes containing at least one high-confidence canonical or non-canonical uORF, significantly fewer genes harbored both uORF types than expected under a Poisson process that accounts for 5′ UTR length and uORF density (P < .001). In contrast, non-canonical uORFs frequently clustered within the same UTRs, whereas high-confidence canonical uATGs were more evenly distributed across genes (Supplementary Tables S14 and S15). These patterns suggest that non-canonical uORFs preferentially occur in genes lacking strongly translated canonical uATGs, rather than acting as additive regulatory elements.

We next assessed the evolutionary stability of non-canonical uORF start codons. Compared with canonical uATGs that showed translation evidence, non-canonical start codons showed significantly lower conservation across four Drosophila species and elevated loss rates in both the D. yakuba and D. simulans lineages (P < .01, Fig. 1B). Also, transitions from non-canonical start codons to canonical ATGs were rare: among 2016 divergent non-canonical sites between D. melanogaster and D. simulans, only 43 corresponded to ATGs in D. simulans. BLS was lower for non-canonical uORF start codons than high-confidence and moderate confidence uATGs, but higher than low-confidence and putative uATGs (Fig. 1C), suggesting they are overall under selective pressure. Interestingly, non-canonical start codons often exhibited strong Kozak contexts, in many cases exceeding those of canonical uATGs (Supplementary Fig. S15), indicating that favorable local sequence context alone is insufficient to ensure long-term evolutionary retention.

Finally, we examined the translational output and regulatory impact of non-canonical uORFs. Ribosome profiling analyses of S2 cells from Douka et al. [62] of D. melanogaster revealed that non-canonical uORFs were translated at significantly lower efficiency than canonical uORFs (Supplementary Fig. S37A). Consistent with this reduced translational activity, genes regulated exclusively by non-canonical uORFs exhibited higher CDS TE than genes regulated by canonical uORFs (Supplementary Fig. S37B), indicating a weaker repressive effect on downstream translation.

Together, these results indicate that non-canonical uORFs constitute a flexible but evolutionarily transient regulatory layer with weak translational activity, limited repression of CDS translation, and reduced long-term conservation compared with canonical uATGs.

Discussion

In this study, we combined phylogenetic, population genetic, and experimental approaches to investigate the evolution and functional consequences of uORFs across the Drosophila phylogeny. Our analyses reveal that uORFs constitute a highly dynamic regulatory layer shaped by frequent gain, loss, and compensatory turnover events, with natural selection acting at multiple evolutionary scales. Together, these results establish uORF turnover as an important mechanism contributing to the evolution of translational regulation.

By integrating comparative translational profiling between D. melanogaster and its closely related sibling species D. simulans, our study advances the functional and evolutionary understanding of uORFs beyond single-species analyses. This comparative framework allows us to distinguish conserved, actively translated uORFs from lineage-specific uORFs whose translational activity and regulatory impact differ across species, thereby providing direct evolutionary context for uORF function. The majority of lineage-specific and experimentally validated uORFs are associated with reduced translation of downstream CDSs, supporting a predominant repressive role for uORFs in translational regulation. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that deletion of a uORF in bicoid increases translation and perturbs developmental patterning [70], and that start-codon mutations in circadian clock transcripts enhance translation and alter physiological rhythms [94] in Drosophila. However, both the cross-species analyses and targeted mutagenesis experiments reveal that this effect is not universal: a subset of uORFs exhibits weak, context-dependent, or undetectable effects on CDS translation. These exceptions underscore that uORF-mediated regulation operates within a broader regulatory landscape rather than acting as a simple on–off switch, with outcomes shaped by local sequence context, cellular state, and evolutionary history. Together, these comparative analyses illustrate how uORF gain and loss can be selectively constrained, tolerated, or occasionally favored as part of an evolving translational regulatory repertoire.

Previous studies have consistently shown that uORFs are generally depleted from 5′ UTRs because newly introduced uORFs are often deleterious and are removed by purifying selection at the population level [36–43]. While our observation—that uATG gains outnumber losses across the Drosophila phylogeny—may initially appear paradoxical, this discrepancy primarily reflects the distinct evolutionary scales under consideration. Our data strongly support the presence of pervasive purifying selection acting on uATGs. This is evidenced by an ATG density in 5′ UTRs that is significantly lower than that observed in neutrally evolving short introns (8.07 versus 16.36 per 1000 nt). Furthermore, the uATG gain-to-loss ratio is markedly reduced in 5′ UTRs relative to short introns (3.72 versus 5.07), and 92.08% of uORF-altering variants in D. melanogaster segregate at minor allele frequencies below 5%, with uATG-gained variants being particularly rare. These observations indicate that the vast majority of de novo uATGs are deleterious and are efficiently purged from the population. In contrast, our phylogenetic analysis captures the long-term dynamics of the small fraction of uATGs that survive this evolutionary filtering to reach fixation along specific lineages. Because our inference of gain and loss events was restricted to orthologous positions that remain continuously aligned across species, the observed asymmetry does not arise from simple differences in total UTR length or alignment coverage. Our ribosome profiling data further characterize the functional properties of these retained uATGs: conserved uATGs exhibit more optimized Kozak contexts, higher TE, and consistent translational activity across species. This suggests they have been selectively retained for specialized regulatory functions. Importantly, these translational data do not demonstrate gain–loss asymmetry, but instead provide functional context for why a subset of newly introduced uATGs may escape elimination. Overall, the uORF landscape is shaped by the interplay between continuous mutational input, stringent purifying selection, and the long-term preservation of a functional minority.

Our results further suggest that uORFs follow evolutionary trajectories analogous to those of protein-coding genes. Newly emerged uORFs tend to exhibit weak translation and limited regulatory impact, whereas conserved uORFs display stronger Kozak contexts, higher TE, and more pronounced repression of downstream CDS translation. This pattern parallels observations that young protein-coding genes are typically expressed at low levels and gradually acquire optimized regulatory and coding features [95, 96]. We propose that weakly repressive uORFs are more likely to persist initially, with subsequent mutations that enhance initiation context and TE being favored over time, progressively strengthening their regulatory effects.

In a broader context, uORFs constitute a fundamental regulatory architecture of the eukaryotic transcriptome, having diversified extensively throughout eukaryotic evolution. Our results indicate that the uORF landscape in Drosophila is far from static; rather, it is characterized by ongoing innovation and turnover that suggests the system has not yet reached a state of strict evolutionary equilibrium. If the uORF landscape were at strict evolutionary equilibrium, gains and losses might be expected to occur at comparable long-term rates. Here, the observed dominance of gains points toward a continued evolutionary exploration of the 5′ UTR sequence space to expand regulatory diversity. Several biological and technical factors likely contribute to this pronounced pattern of uORF turnover in Drosophila. The characteristically large effective population sizes (Ne) of Drosophila species enhance the efficiency of natural selection [97], potentially allowing for the precise fine-tuning of nascent regulatory elements. Furthermore, the substantial variation in genome size, nucleotide composition, and evolutionary rates across the Drosophila phylogeny may create unique lineage-specific opportunities for regulatory innovation. These patterns were revealed by integrating high-quality genome assemblies—including our new D. simulans assembly—with comprehensive ribosome profiling datasets. This high-resolution framework provides a significantly more detailed characterization of uORF turnover than was previously achievable. Extending these integrative strategies to diverse eukaryotic lineages will be essential to determine whether this pattern of ongoing regulatory expansion is a general feature of eukaryotic genome evolution.

Although most uORF-altering variants in D. melanogaster are deleterious at the population level, a substantial fraction of both uORF gains and losses among fixed differences show signatures of positive selection. While compensatory loss following earlier uORF gains likely explains some adaptive losses, additional mechanisms may also contribute. Weakly deleterious or conditionally neutral uORFs may initially become fixed under relaxed constraint and subsequently be selectively removed as environmental or physiological demands change. In this context, uORF loss may provide an efficient route to rapidly derepress translation when increased protein output becomes advantageous. Consistent with this idea, our population-specific analyses suggest that uORF turnover may contribute to local adaptation. The Ethiopian (EF) population exhibited a disproportionate number of population-specific uORF gain and loss events, consistent with previous reports of strong selection associated with high-altitude environments [71]. For instance, loss of a uORF in the DNA repair gene tefu could plausibly enhance translational output and improve UV tolerance [98, 99]. Although most uORF changes likely have modest or context-dependent effects, such variants may serve as a reservoir of regulatory diversity that can be selectively leveraged under particular environmental conditions. At present, only a limited number of studies have directly linked uORF-altering variants to phenotypic outcomes [100–102], underscoring the need for future work integrating translational profiling, functional assays, and fitness measurements.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, uATG gain and loss rates were inferred using parsimony-based ancestral reconstructions and, therefore, likely underestimate true event frequencies. This is particularly true for the uORF birth–death process, which remains largely understudied due to the differing timescales of evolutionary detection. At the population level, many newly arisen uATGs are highly transient; these “infant” uORFs are typically deleterious and eliminated by purifying selection before they can spread, making them largely unobservable in cross-species comparisons. Consequently, our phylogenetic rate estimates are restricted to “fixed” events that have already passed through this initial selective filter. Furthermore, our inference of uORF “death” relies on phylogenetic signaling, where a previously fixed uATG is subsequently lost along a specific lineage. Because multiple hidden events may occur at the same site—especially along deeper branches—the true volatility of the uORF landscape may be higher than reported here. Additionally, our evolutionary analyses treat uATGs as discrete events and do not explicitly model quantitative differences in translational strength or regulatory impact among uORFs. Second, because 5′ UTR annotations and multiple sequence alignments are anchored on D. melanogaster, inference of uATG dynamics across deeply diverged species may be affected by alignment erosion and incomplete sequence coverage. In principle, such reference-anchored alignments could influence the apparent balance of inferred gain and loss events if alignment coverage differs among lineages. To minimize this potential bias, all quantitative birth–death analyses were restricted to closely related species within the D. melanogaster–D. simulans clade and to continuously aligned regions where orthology and alignment confidence remain high. Importantly, because gains and losses were inferred only at positions that remain alignable across all focal species, reference anchoring alone cannot generate a systematic bias toward inferred gains. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that some events occurring in poorly aligned or lineage-specific UTR regions remain undetected. Third, although our analyses support a predominant role for uORFs in shaping translational regulation, we cannot exclude the possibility that a subset of observed uATG turnover events influences gene expression through other regulatory mechanisms operating within 5′ UTRs, such as RNA structure or interactions with RNA-binding proteins. In addition, the available ribosome profiling data from D. simulans were generated using MNase digestion, which typically lacks strong 3-nt periodicity [12, 63]. While these data provide reliable quantitative measurements of translation, they are less compatible with methods that rely on periodicity to call translated uORFs, potentially leading to conservative estimates of uORF translation in this species. Expanded ribosome profiling datasets from additional species and experimental protocols will help overcome this limitation. Finally, translation initiation at non-canonical start codons and condition- or tissue-specific uORFs is more difficult to predict and quantify, and therefore was not systematically analyzed here. Future studies incorporating additional cell types, developmental stages, and species will be important to fully capture this regulatory diversity.

In summary, our study reveals uORFs as highly dynamic regulatory elements whose evolution reflects a balance between pervasive purifying selection and episodic adaptive change. By integrating phylogenetic, population genetic, and experimental approaches, we show that uORF gain, loss, and compensation collectively shape translational regulation across evolutionary timescales. These findings add a new dimension to our understanding of regulatory evolution and highlight uORFs as an important and flexible layer within gene regulatory networks. Given the interplay of uORFs with other regulatory mechanisms—such as RNA modifications [103], RNA binding proteins [104, 105], miRNAs [106, 107], and mRNA structures [108]—future studies should explore how these elements co-evolve to fine-tune gene expression and drive phenotypic diversity.

Data Availability Statement

The whole-genome shotgun project has been deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under accession number JBGDNA000000000 and under BioProject number PRJNA1143213. The version described in this paper is version JBGDNA010000000. The accession numbers for raw reads are SRR30111494 and SRR30111495. The mRNA-seq and Ribo-seq data regarding the developmental stages, tissues, and cell lines of D. melanogaster were published in previous studies and the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) accession numbers are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The mRNA-seq and Ribo-seq data regarding the developmental stages of D. simulans were deposited in the China National Genomics Data Center Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) under accession numbers CRA003198 and CRA007426. The multiple alignment files, other processed data and results, and the custom scripts used in this study are available via Figshare at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31149217 and https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.31148935.
