Laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy in a 2-year-old child with morbid obesity: A case report with a 2-year follow-up
Authors: Vivek Bindal, Shailesh Gupta, Dhananjay Pandey, Tushar Goel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Morbid obesity in infancy or early childhood is a challenging disease to manage. Here, we present the case report of the successful management of a 2-year-old girl child with morbidly obesity who was bedridden and had sleep apnoea and underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy. Bariatric surgery in this age group comes with a lot of decision-making challenges and technical and ethical considerations, and literature is scant on paediatric bariatric surgery. We describe the case and associated challenges in detail in this report.

INTRODUCTION

There is no consensus on a cut-off point for overweight or obesity in children and adolescents. The Centre for Disease Control and Prevention defined overweight as at or above the 95th percentile of body mass index (BMI) for age and ‘at risk for overweight’ as between the 85th and 95th percentile of BMI for age.[1]

The ecological model, as described by Sahoo K et al.,[1] suggests that factors that contribute to childhood obesity risk encompass unhealthy dietary habits, low physical activity levels and sedentary behaviour. The impact of such risk factors is moderated by factors such as age and gender. Family characteristics parenting style and parents’ lifestyles also play a role.

Genetic milieu is one of the important factors examined as a cause of obesity in children. Genetic susceptibility often needs to be coupled with contributing environmental and behavioural factors to affect weight. Several rare genetic syndromes can cause obesity in children:[2]

Childhood obesity has been linked to numerous medical conditions.[1] These conditions include fatty liver disease, obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), Type 2 diabetes, asthma, hepatic steatosis (fatty liver disease), cardiovascular disease, high cholesterol, cholelithiasis (gallstones), glucose intolerance and insulin resistance, skin conditions, menstrual abnormalities, impaired balance and orthopaedic problems.

In addition to being implicated in numerous medical concerns, childhood obesity affects children’s and adolescents’ social and emotional health.

Weight loss surgery for obese children and adolescents is a controversial topic. In decision-making for bariatric surgery in childhood, multidisciplinary teams should agree on the specific needs and abilities of the given patient and should be considered on a case-by-case basis.

We report the successful management of a 2-year and 3-month-old child with morbidly obesity who was bedridden and had sleep apnoea without any known cause of obesity who underwent laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG). There is limited literature available regarding guidelines, type of bariatric surgery and operative technique for this age group (<3 years). To the extent of our current understanding, there exists solely one published case report of LSG in the age group of <3 years wherein LSG was done in a 2.5-year-old Saudi boy.[3] This makes the index case as the youngest reported case of bariatric surgery till date.

CASE REPORT

A 2-year-old girl child was referred to us from the paediatric endocrinology department for bariatric surgery. She was born in March 2019, to parents who were non-obese, by LSCS at 35 weeks of gestational age. Her birth weight was 2.7 kg. APGAR score at the time of birth was normal her brother, who is 6 years older, maintained a healthy weight and reached all developmental milestones on schedule. She was exclusively breastfeed till the age of 3 months when she started feeling hungry. To compensate the increase appetite, the parents added a top feed from the age of 4 months. Parents observed excessive weight gain starting at 4 months of age. In view of abnormal weight gain at the age of 4 months with a weight of 8 kg, the parents consulted paediatrician who in turn referred them to paediatric endocrinologist. Intensive workup was done to find out the cause of obesity. Thyroid profile (to rule out hypothyroidism), serum cortisol/adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) (increased levels cause insulin resistance and hepatic gluconeogenesis), serum insulin (to rule out insulin resistance), lipid profile and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) brain (to rule out pituitary and hypothalamic mass) were done. All these investigations were normal except that serum leptin levels were raised.

A genetic study for monogenic (LEP, LEPR, POMC, MC4R, MC3R, etc.,) and syndromic obesity panels genes (Prader–Willi, WAGR, BBS syndrome, etc.,) was done, and no abnormality was detected. Sleep study revealed severe OSA. There was a severe delay in developmental milestones.

Her weight increased to 25 kg at the age of 1 year. Weight continued to increase at the same pace and at the time of presentation to us, at the age of 2 years, her weight was 45 kg, height 104 cm and BMI was 41.6 kg/m2. She was bedridden, unable to sit by herself, was eating and drinking excessively and had severe OSA.

The child was evaluated by a multidisciplinary team consisting of paediatric endocrinologists, paediatricians, paediatric intensivists, bariatric surgeons and an anaesthetist. The child was re-evaluated to find out the cause of obesity. Serum insulin levels, serum cortisol/ACTH, MRI brain and genetic analysis were conducted again, yielding normal results once more. Nutritional indicators, such as Vitamin D, Vitamin B12, iron study parameters and calcium levels, were within the normal limits. Sleep study revealed severe OSA (AHI 32) with significant desaturation during sleep. She was put on continuous positive airway pressure (9 cm H2O) after titration to optimise her.

Considering the critical nature of morbid, obesity posing a potential threat to life, and after consulting with the parents and obtaining their consent, the decision to proceed with LSG was made as an essential life-saving intervention.

Administration of general anaesthesia in a child with morbid obesity and severe OSA was challenging as was getting venous access. A video laryngoscope was used for intubation. Central and radial arterial lines were also inserted. Short-acting anaesthesia agents were used for induction and maintenance for anaesthesia to avoid post-operative anaesthesia complications including hypoxia.

Initial pneumoperitoneum was created using Veress needle from Palmer’s point. Supraumbilical 5-mm camera port was inserted, 12-mm port for the stapler was inserted in the right mid-clavicular line. Two 5-mm ports were inserted on the left side along with a Nathanson’s liver retractor [Figure 1]. To start with, an intraoperative upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy was done with paediatric scope. The intraoperative endoscopy assessment demonstrated the feasibility of inserting a 32 French bougie. Gastrolysis was performed 3 cm proximal to the pylorus till the angle of his [Figure 2]. The gastric sleeve was made over 32 French bougie and the stomach was stapled using Echelon™ flex-powered gun using adult 45 mm first green and subsequent blue cartridges [Figure 3]. Staple line was oversewn using barbed absorbable suture [Figure 4]. There was minimal blood loss during the surgery.

The patient was extubated in OT itself, was shifted to the paediatric intensive care unit and was shifted to the ward after 48 h of surgery. Post-operative period was uneventful. She was given clear liquid diet from post-operative day 1 and discharged on post-operative day 3.

The patient was closely followed up in the post-operative period. The child lost around 10% of body weight within the first 1 month of surgery and there was immediate relief in OSA. Snoring completely disappeared. The patient was enrolled in consistent physiotherapy sessions and continued to show ongoing signs of improvement. During the follow-up, the patient’s weight was recorded at 37 kg after 3 months and 30 kg after 1-year post-surgery. Within 6 months of surgery, she was able to stand with support and showed significant progress in general well-being.

Now, at the age of 4 years, 2 years after the surgery, her weight is 32 kg, her height is 111 cm and her BMI is 25.97 kg/m2. She is able to walk now and ride a tricycle. At present, her motor and cognitive milestones are appropriate as per her age. She has joined the school and is able to lead a normal life. On follow evaluation, nutritional indicators, such as Vitamin D, Vitamin B12, iron study parameters and calcium levels, were within normal limits.

DISCUSSION

Decision of doing bariatric surgery in this child age group was the most important and challenging aspect in this case. After exhausting all the options for weight loss, the child was referred to us by paediatric endocrinologist. The child was evaluated again with new team of paediatricians and endocrinologits. No aetiology of morbid obesity was found. In view of life-threatening comorbidities, the possibility of bariatric surgery was discussed with parents as the only measure which could have helped the child. It was the decision with the agreement of paediatricians, endocrinologists, anaesthesists, intensivists, parents and the surgical team.

As per the American Society for Metabolic and Bariatric Surgery (ASMBS), in decision-making for bariatric surgery in childhood, multidisciplinary teams should agree on the specific needs and abilities of the given patient and caregiver and these should be considered on a case-by-case basis with the assistance of the hospital ethics committee where appropriate.[4]

van Geelen et al. wrote besides concerns over efficacy and safety; the influence on health, motivation and the possibility of informed consent-play an important role in the disputes over bariatric surgery for childhood obesity.[5]

As per Hofmann, the risks of bariatric surgery in children are considerable, and its long-term safety and efficacy in children remain largely unknown. Surgery should be reserved for only the most severe cases.[6]

As per ASMBS, LSG has become the most used and most recommended operation in children and adolescents with severe obesity for several reasons, near-equivalent weight loss to the Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) in adolescents, fewer reoperations, better iron absorption and near-equivalent effect on comorbidities as RYGB in adolescents.[4]

There were many technical challenges. Compared to the big size of the abdomen, in toddlers with morbid obesity, coelomic cavity volume is very small. It was a challenge to have ergonomically adequate space between ports. As toddler-size laparoscopic instruments, stapler guns and cartridges are still not manufactured, utilisation of adult-sized Endo GI staplers for stomach stapling presented a limitation. It was ergonomically challenging and difficult to manoeuvre them in a small abdomen. These age group children have a very low total blood volume and reserves, thus allowable blood loss is very low. There was no previous operative bariatric surgery video available for this age group which made the pre-operative planning difficult.

In this case, the child did very well in follow-up. Before surgery, she was bedridden. After the surgery, she underwent intensive physiotherapy, and within 3 months, she achieved the ability to stand. At the time of surgery, her BMI was 41.6 kg/m2. Currently, at present, at the age of 4 years, BMI is 25.97 kg/m2. As per current age, she has achieved all cognitive and motor milestones.

She is being monitored for any nutritional deficiencies (Vitamin D, Vitamin B12, iron study parameters and calcium levels) regularly and is being given supplements appropriate for her age and weight. Bone health is a concern in children after bariatric surgery, as is Vitamin B1 and B12 deficiency. They should be supplemented well with B1, B6, B12, folic acid, iron, calcium and Vitamin D3. As per ASMBS, children and adolescents are more likely to stop taking nutritional supplements. Therefore, annual follow-up with vitamin level monitoring is strongly recommended. Efforts should be done to make sure that the child remembers to take supplements daily.[4]

Her family is very satisfied and happy with her progress. As a caregiver, we were able to achieve the desired result in a short period of time.

CONCLUSION

Bariatric surgery can be done as a life-saving procedure in a toddler with morbid obesity, with severe life-threatening comorbidities. LSG is safe and effective in this age group.

Decision-making and medicolegal/ethical consideration are the very important aspects of paediatric bariatric surgery.
