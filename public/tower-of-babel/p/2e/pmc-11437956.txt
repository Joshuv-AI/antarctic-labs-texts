Genomic analysis of the SARS-CoV-2 variants circulated in the Philippines, 2020–2024
Authors: Arnie Dimaano, Kiana Dominique Carreon, Giselle Sophia Camaya, Irvin Rondolo, Jarel Elgin Tolentino
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGenomic sequencing has been an invaluable tool to determine the evolution of SARS-CoV-2. In the present study, we provided a comprehensive description of the SARS-CoV-2 variants circulated in the Philippines.MethodsThe dataset from the human COVID-19 infections was acquired by downloading the sequences and their associated metadata spanning from March 2020 to April 2024. Then, we executed several filtering criteria to acquire the final dataset for the Philippine samples and performed spatial distribution analysis and phylogenetic tree construction of the reported SARS-CoV-2 sequences.ResultsA total of 16,679,203 SARS-CoV sequences were obtained, of which 17,393 (0.10 %) were sampled in the Philippines. Western Visayas reported the highest SARS-CoV-2 sequences (21.33 %), while the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao reported the least (0.48 %). The phylogenetic tree revealed the evolution of the detected SARS-CoV-2 variants circulating in the Philippines with 19 A as the first reported case (based on the GISAID submission), and 24 A (JN.1) as the currently circulating variant. Omicron variants have dominated the Philippines with 21 L (Omicron, BA.2) having 5102 cases (29.33 %), followed by 22B (BA.5) having 2184 cases (12.57 %). Using Pearson's Chi-square test of independence, we showed that there is a significant association between the age-groups and gender with the detection years.ConclusionAltogether, this analysis showed the updated epidemiological trends of the reported SARS-CoV-2 variants in the Philippines. This increases the importance of conducting surveillance on viral infectious diseases such as COVID-19 to provide the scope and trajectory of viral spread in a country.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2 variants, Philippines, COVID-19, Epidemiology

Introduction

SARS-CoV-2, the virus responsible for the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), was first detected in Wuhan, China, and is identified as the seventh coronavirus causing widespread infection in humans within the Coronaviridae family [1]. According to the data (21 April 2024) from the World Health Organization, this virus has evolved into 38 identified variants (or clades based on Nextstrain) and has caused a total cumulative infection of 775,364,261 cases wherein a total cumulative mortality of 7,045,569 cases were reported [2]. To date, 4,140,383 were the total cumulative reported COVID-19 cases in the Philippines with a death toll of 66,864. The rapid spread and devastating impact of COVID-19 underline the urgent need for continued global cooperation in containing the virus and protecting vulnerable populations to prevent death. This cooperation to combat COVID-19 must be well-coordinated and maintained to prevent the start of another pandemic [3].

Nearly five years since the first COVID-19 case was identified, however, the virus persists and undergoes evolutionary changes resulting in new variants continuing to emerge. As of April 2024, the 23I (BA.2.86) variant and its subvariants (e.g. 24 A-JN.1, 24B-JN.1.11.1) are predominant and continue to spread among the global population [4,5]. The first global case of 23I (BA.2.86) was first identified in Israel and since then has caused a dramatic increase in infections, and outcompeting other variants [e.g. XBB (22F)] [6]. Given that new SARS-CoV-2 variants continue to evolve and emerge, it is essential to strengthen biosurveillance systems in every country.

One crucial aspect of understanding and combating COVID-19 lies in genome sequencing [7], which emphasizes the importance of sequencing facilities and biosurveillance system. Through genomic analysis, researchers around the globe can track the evolution of the virus, monitor the emergence of new variants, and assess the effectiveness of vaccines and treatments [8]. This monitoring system enables the identification of specific mutations that may confer increased transmissibility, virulence, or resistance to current interventions [7,8]. In the Philippines, several variants have been identified through genome sequencing including BA.2.86 and JN.1 [9] but the epidemiological trends of the detected SARS-CoV-2 variants from 2020 up to 2024 were not yet performed. The present study focuses on the comprehensive description of all the available genome sequences uploaded in GISAID to provide the extent and pattern of viral spread across the country [10].

Materials and methods

The complete genome sequences of all SARS-CoV-2 variants and their corresponding metadata were downloaded from the GISAID database with a released date of 29 April 2024. To organize the dataset specifically for the Philippines, we filtered it according to the following criteria: (i) exclusively retained distinct Accession IDs, (ii) confirmed the host was labeled as ‘Human’, (iii) recorded the collection date, (iv) the PANGO lineage column should not be empty, none or unassigned, (v) retained sequences with less than 1 % proportion of ambiguous bases, (vi) the patient age should be numeric and removed those with ‘unknown’ entry, (vii) the gender should be explicitly stated as ‘male’ or ‘female’, and (viii) the location should be "Philippines". We then assigned Nextclade clade information to individual viral sequences using the Nextclade v2.14.0 CLI workflow [11].

The information on the sampling location was obtained from the corresponding metadata of all the downloaded and filtered Philippine SARS-CoV-2 sequences. The locations were then mapped based on the 17 regions in the Philippines. The map and heatmap creation were performed using Microsoft ® Excel version 16.84. The map visualization was performed using R v4.3.3 utilizing ggmap version 4.0.0.9 [12].

To perform phylogenetic tree construction, 20 SARS-CoV-2 sequences were randomly sampled from each clade. The obtained viral genome sequences (EPI SET ID: EPI_SET_240507hx, see Appendix A: Supplementary data) were mapped and aligned to the reference genome sequence of SARS-CoV-2 (Wuhan-Hu-1: GenBank accession number: NC_045512.2) through minimap v2.24 [13], and the acquired sam format file was converted to fasta format using gofasta v1.2.0 [14]. During this conversion, the alignment sites corresponding to 1–265 and 29,674–29,903 positions on the SARS-CoV-2 genome were masked, typically converted to ‘NNN’. Alignment sites with more than 10 % of sequences containing gaps or uncertain nucleotides were executed to trimming using trimAl v1.2 [15]. A maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree of the trimmed genome sequences was inferred by IQTree v2.2.2.6 [16], employing GTR + F + I + G4 nucleotide substitution model as the best-fit model searched by IQTree. The node support value was computed by ultrafast bootstraps with 1000 iterations. The visualization of the final tree was generated in R v4.3.3 using the ggtree v3.8.2 package [17].

To determine if there is any significant association between the age-groups vs year and the gender vs year, Pearson's Chi-square test of independence was performed. In addition to this test, we also calculated the sum, the mean, the percentage, as well as the standard error of the mean. All these tests and computations were performed in R v4.3.3.

Results

A total of 16,679,203 SARS-CoV-2 sequences were obtained from the GISAID database. Following the inclusion criteria (see Materials and methods), 17,393 full-genome SARS-CoV-2 sequences (0.10 %) from the Philippines were attained as the final dataset. As shown in Fig. 1, the spatial distribution of all SARS-CoV-2 sequences was identified in all 17 regions in the Philippines. The majority of the sequences were from Western Visayas which has a total of 3710 cases (21.33 %), followed by the National Capital Region (19.59 %), Davao Region (11.71 %), Calabarzon (10.39 %), Central Luzon (6.42 %), Cagayan Valley (5.88 %), Central Visayas (4.37 %), Cordillera Administrative Region (3.55 %), Zamboanga Peninsula (2.73 %), SOCCSKSARGEN (2.56 %), Ilocos Region (2.52 %), Bicol Region (2.08 %), Northern Mindanao (1.98 %), Mimaropa (1.93 %), Caraga (1.71 %), Eastern Visayas (0.76 %), and Bangsamoro Autonomous Region in Muslim Mindanao (BARMM) (0.48 %).

Pearson's Chi-square test of independence was performed for the metadata to assess whether there is a statistically significant association between the categorical variables such as age groups and years (Table 1), and gender and years (Table 2). We set the null hypothesis (H0) as no association between age groups and years (or gender and years), while we set the alternative hypothesis (Ha) as there is an association between age groups and years (or gender and years). In Table 1, the χ2 statistic is 205.22, with degrees of freedom (df) of 20, and a p-value of 2.2e−16. In Table 2, χ2 statistic is 224.01, with a df of 4, and a p-value of 2.2e−16.

Discussion

Genomic sequencing has been instrumental for verifying the evolutionary trajectory of SARS-CoV-2, and as a result, it can aid in identifying the currently dominating variant in the human population [7,8]. In the present study, we leveraged this approach to analyze a vast dataset obtained from the GISAID database. Following rigorous criteria to specifically obtain the Philippine samples, the dataset was narrowed down, and eventually used for downstream analysis. This curation allowed us to provide a comprehensive description of the spatial and temporal distribution, as well as the evolution of the SARS-CoV-2 across the 17 regions of the Philippines.

Our findings revealed a widespread circulation of SARS-CoV-2 variants throughout the country, with all 17 regions reporting confirmed cases (Fig. 1). Remarkably, Western Visayas emerged as the hotspot, followed by the National Capital Region and then Davao Region. Two possible reasons might have been attributed to the high number of identified SARS-CoV-2 sequences in these regions. First, due to having highly urbanized cities in these regions that can lead to a rapid spread of COVID-19 infection between people [18]. And second, the availability of testing facilities in these regions that led to a more COVID-19 cases detection [19]. This spatial distribution highlights the significance of localized surveillance to effectively track the spread of the virus in a particular region.

Our phylogenetic analysis revealed the evolutionary trajectory of SARS-CoV-2 within the Philippine setting, dating back to the earliest genome sequence on 10 March 2020 (variant 19 A) (Fig. 2). Subsequent variants such as 20 A, 20B, and 20C emerged over time, leading to the introduction of Omicron variants like 21 L (Omicron, BA.2), 22B (BA.5), and 24 A (JN.1- BA.2.86 subvariant) in later years. The reported cases of these variants varied, with 21 L (Omicron, BA.2) dominating with 5102 cases, followed by 22B (BA.5) with 2184 cases, and the currently dominating subvariant 24 A (JN.1) in the country with just 14 cases detected. Omicron subvariants dominated the human population not only in the Philippines but of the world [20]. High rates of transmissibility and immune escape ability have been implicated in its successful dominance over other variants [21]. To date, KP.2, a BA.2.86 subvariant, is the dominant strain worldwide [22] but no genomic cases have yet been reported in the Philippines.

Using the statistical test, we investigated whether the distribution of age groups and gender within the four-year period (2020–2024) differed significantly (Table 1 and Table 2). Both results from these tests provided convincing evidence to reject the H0 hypothesis of no association, in favor of the Ha of association. These show that there is a significant association between the age groups and gender with years, signifying a dynamic shift in demographic distribution over the four-year period. These findings emphasize the significance of demographic factors in inferring temporal trends and underscore the need for further analysis to determine the drivers of these associations needed for targeted interventions in the country.

This study has some limitations. First, there is a potential sampling bias since we based the epidemiological trends of COVID-19 infections on the uploaded genomes in GISAID. The dataset obtained may not represent the entire COVID-19 infections in the Philippines, and it is possible that certain regions are underrepresented. And second, our findings are specific to the Philippine setting and may not be directly applicable to other countries with different population demographics, healthcare infrastructure, and variant epidemiological dynamics.

In conclusion, our comprehensive analysis of SARS-CoV-2 variants in the Philippines provides valuable insights into the spatial and temporal distribution, the epidemiological trends, and the circulation of different variants detected in the Philippines over the four-year period. By leveraging genomic sequencing through rigorous computational methods, we added some new information on the COVID-19 epidemiology and provided value on the importance of doing surveillance.
