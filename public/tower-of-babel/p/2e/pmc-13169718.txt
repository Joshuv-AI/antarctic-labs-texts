Evaluation of surgical visualization headset in dental education for preclinical bone augmentation surgery
Authors: Hao Zeng, Qihang Fan, Ting Xia, Qin Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEffective preclinical training for complex procedures, such as oral bone augmentation, demands high-fidelity visual demonstration. The conventional “display screen combined with a dental microscope” model is limited by inconsistent sightlines, variable viewing angles, and instructor ergonomic challenges. The surgical visualization headset has emerged as a novel technology to provide an immersive, stereoscopic, and synchronized learning view. This study aimed to evaluate the pedagogical efficacy of this headset-based model in preclinical training for oral bone augmentation.MethodsSixty undergraduate students were randomly allocated into two groups (n = 30 per group). Group A (control group) received training using a display screen combined with a dental microscope. Group B (experimental group) was trained using a surgical visualization headset combined with a dental microscope. Both groups performed bone augmentation procedures on porcine mandibles under the guidance of the same instructor. The training outcomes were comprehensively evaluated through objective scoring of surgical skills and subjective questionnaire feedback.ResultsPost-training assessments revealed that the final outcome scores of students in Group B were significantly higher than those in Group A. Furthermore, the scores of fine surgical steps (such as membrane fixation and tension release) in Group B were evidently higher than those in Group A. Students in Group B also reported significantly higher scores for the sharpness, fluency, and integrity of the live practical demonstration visuals. Furthermore, Group B demonstrated superior levels of interest, concentration, and satisfaction compared to Group A. However, students in Group B reported a significantly higher degree of visual fatigue.ConclusionIn comparison to the conventional method of using a display screen with a dental microscope, employing a surgical visualization headset combined with a dental microscope for hands-on training provides a superior pedagogical experience and contributes to more effective learning outcomes.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (Number: ChiCTR2600116864). Introduction

Dentistry is a discipline that demands a high degree of practical skill, particularly in fields such as micro-dentistry, periodontal surgery, and implant surgery, where the success of treatment is contingent upon the precision of fine-motor operations [1–3]. To master these intricate and complex procedures, undergraduate students in dental school need to take a lot of preclinical training. Within this context, complex oral therapeutic operations, such as bone augmentation, are taught with a focus on extensive detail and systematic methodology, especially during hands-on training sessions [4]. The quality of detail demonstrated during these practical sessions is a critical determinant of pedagogical effectiveness [5].

To facilitate the real-time transmission of procedural details, conventional teaching has predominantly relied on broadcasting via projection screens [6]. In this modality, the performance of the broadcasting equipment and display screens—including parameters such as resolution, latency, and color saturation—directly impacts the quality of the visual demonstration. The dental microscope has become a standard broadcasting tool in hands-on training, valued for its stable optics, high-definition camera, and reliable magnification capabilities [7, 8]. As a result, the “display screen combined with a dental microscope” model has substantially enhanced the quality of detail presentation, particularly in terms of clarity and magnification.

However, this broadcasting model still presents inherent challenges in ensuring high congruence between the instructor’s operative view and the students’ learning view [9]. For instance, due to the limited size of display screens, the visual experience can vary significantly among students based on their seating location; those situated closer perceive a larger, more detailed image than those farther away. Furthermore, in a flat classroom layout, the line of sight for students in the rear may be obstructed by those in the front, and architectural features such as support columns can also impede visibility. Another significant issue is that a discrepancy often exists between the view through the microscope eyepiece and the image rendered on the monitor, compelling the instructor to pause frequently to confirm the students’ visual field and make time-consuming adjustments, which can disrupt the fluency of the instruction. Moreover, the fixed optical systems and “man-machine integration” design of these microscopes necessitate that the demonstrator maintains a static posture for extended periods, often leading to musculoskeletal discomfort and making it difficult to sustain long teaching sessions [10].

To address these challenges, the surgical visualization headset has emerged as a novel solution [11, 12]. Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the transformative potential of such immersive digital technologies and novel head-mounted displays in enhancing spatial understanding, precision, and operator ergonomics in both dental education and general microsurgery [13, 14]. This head-mounted display represents a core innovation by employing advanced algorithms and a 3D imaging system to liberate the surgical field of view from the traditional eyepiece, projecting it onto a digital, head-worn terminal. This allows the instructor to obtain a stereoscopic and immersive surgical view without being physically tethered to the microscope [15]. Consequently, this new paradigm of “human-computer collaboration” not only frees the operator’s posture, fostering a more ergonomic and comfortable working state, but also facilitates a highly synchronized visual experience between the instructor’s operative view and the students’ learning view when students wear identical headsets.

This technological innovation offers a powerful catalyst for transforming pedagogical training. The “surgical visualization headset combined with a dental microscope” model allows the instructor to present a high-fidelity replication of the surgical scene to all students, enabling them to perceive every operational detail in real-time and with profound depth [16]. This has the potential to significantly shorten the learning curve and enhance the quality of education. However, there is a paucity of literature reporting on the pedagogical efficacy of this model. This study, therefore, aims to conduct a preliminary evaluation of the “surgical visualization headset combined with a dental microscope” teaching model through assessments of students’ surgical skills and questionnaire-based feedback. We anticipate that the findings will provide valuable insights and contribute to superior solutions for continuing dental education in the digital era.

Randomization and blinding

All recruited students will be randomly assigned to allocated to the Group A (display combined dental microscope, n = 30) and Group B (surgical visualization headset combined dental microscope, n = 30) in a 1:1 ratio. An independent study registrar conducted the randomization using computer-generated random codes with permuted block randomization (block sizes of 4 and 6, generated randomly). Students will be enrolled by a study registrar who assigns treatment numbers according to a pre-generated random allocation and is not involved in other study procedures. All students were informed their allocation and class time by e-mail in the same time.

In this study, blinding of the teacher and students were not possible. However, the final assessment was conducted by two independently calibrated evaluators who remaining blinded to the group allocation. The two independent evaluators were blinded to group allocation. Student identities and group assignments were concealed using coded identifiers during the evaluation process. Inter-rater reliability between the two independent inspectors was assessed using Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC). The ICC value of 0.87 indicated excellent agreement, supporting the consistency and reliability of the skill assessment. Potential discrepancies between evaluators will be resolved through a third senior teacher. Data analysts were performed by an independent evaluator to prevent information bias.

Intervention

The learning content of both groups was how to perform guided bone regeneration on the pig jaw model. Additionally, both groups shared the same group of teachers, who instructed them to complete the bone augmentation hands-on course. There are two teachers in this class. One teacher was responsible for the main lecture, including the explanation and demonstration of both the theoretical and practical parts. The other teacher was responsible for assisting, including distributing the practical equipment and consumables and assisting the main instructor in teaching work. In Group A, the students were leaning the whole surgical procedures and professional details from the live streaming by the display (4k Monitor, Zumax medical co., Ltd, China) combined dental microscope (OMS6000 digital microscope, Zumax medical co., Ltd, China). When the teacher was operating with the dental microscope, the students can learn from the display which can broadcast the view in the microscope.

In Group B, the students were leaning the whole surgical procedures and professional details from the live streaming by the surgical visualization headset (ZuVisionX™ 3D HEADBAND, Zumax medical co., Ltd, China) combined dental microscope (OMS6000 digital microscope, Zumax medical co., Ltd, China). When the teacher was operating with the dental microscope, the teacher and students were all wear the surgical visualization headset (Fig. 2). The students can easily obtain a highly consistent view aligned with the teacher’s perspective through the surgical visualization headset, although minor visual discrepancies may practically exist. The students were not allowed to take off the headset during the teaching phase.

Finally, upon completing the surgery, the students need to fill out the questionnaire about the hands-on course.

Outcome

The primary outcome of this study was the scores of the final outcome, while the secondary outcome contained scores of each surgical steps, evaluation of live streaming and feedback of the course. The final outcome score was the average of the scores given by the same inspector. The scoring criteria were mainly based on the stability and morphology of the bone graft material, with a maximum score of 10 points (the scoring rubric in Table 1). The score of each surgical step was also scored by the inspector after the students completed the corresponding step. These surgical steps contained incision, flap, decortication, membrane fixation, material position, tension release and suture. The scoring criteria were based on whether the students’ execution met the standard operating procedures, with a maximum score of 10 points for each step. Specifically, the evaluation focused on key criteria including the neatness and correct depth of the incision, the integrity and tension-free nature of the flap, adequate bleeding spots during decortication, rigid membrane fixation, stable material positioning without leakage, effective periosteal releasing incisions for tension release, and secure, gap-free wound closure during suturing (the scoring rubric in Table S1). After the students completed the operation assessment, they were asked to fill out a questionnaire on the quality of the live broadcast of the surgery and course feedback. In the survey, students intuitively rated their responses using a visual analogue scale with a maximum score of 10. The quality of the livestream was subjectively assessed by students, including sharpness, fluency, and integrity. Course feedback was also provided based on students’ experience, including feedback on learning interest, concentration, teaching satisfaction, and visual fatigue.

Statistical analysis

All collected data were imported into SPSS version 26.0 for statistical analysis. Quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). Intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent samples t-tests, while intragroup comparisons between pre- and post-training data were performed using paired samples t-tests. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Detailed practical skill assessment

To further analyze the differences in operational skills between the two groups, all students underwent a practical assessment involving a standard microsurgical vascular anastomosis procedure on an animal model (Fig. 4). Specifically, we scored seven key components of the procedure, with a maximum score of 10 for each component. The data revealed that Group B’s scores were significantly higher than Group A’s in two of the seven components: Membrane fixation (P = 0.0167) and Flap advancement (P = 0.0014). The seven assessed components were Incision, Flap, Decortication, Membrane fixation, Material position, Flap advancement, and Suture (Fig. 5; Table 4 and Table S3-S9). These results suggest that the dental microscope and surgical visualization headset training model not only improves comprehensive operational skills but also significantly promotes and reinforces specific procedural abilities.

Subjective evaluation of imaging

Additionally, we collected students’ subjective evaluations of the microscopic imaging in both training models, with a maximum score of 10. As shown in (Fig. 6), students in Group B rated the imaging metrics of the dental microscope and surgical visualization headset system very highly. Their scores for “sharpness,” “fluency,” and “completeness” were all significantly higher than those of Group A for the traditional dental microscope with a projection screen system (P = 0.0239; P < 0.0001; P = 0.0002, respectively) (Table 5 and Table S10-S12). This indicates that the visual experience provided by the dental microscope and surgical visualization headset is perceived by students as comprehensively superior to the traditional system. This high-quality visual feedback may be a key reason for its more effective training outcomes.

Discussion

The findings of this study clearly indicate that in the context of hands-on training in oral microsurgery, a combination teaching model employing a surgical visualization headset with a dental microscope (Group B) is significantly more effective than a model using only a conventional dental microscope with screen projection (Group A). Students in Group B not only achieved superior scores in the final assessment of their surgical skills but also reported a better subjective learning experience, including higher levels of interest, concentration, and satisfaction.

First, the advantage of the combination teaching model in enhancing pedagogical effectiveness may stem from its ability to provide richer, multi-dimensional visual information [17–19]. The surgical visualization headset offers a broad, immersive “global” field of view, which facilitates the students’ understanding of the overall workflow and spatial arrangement of the procedure [11, 12]. Concurrently, the dental microscope ensures a “focused” view of critical details. This synergistic visual input, combining both “macro” and “micro” perspectives, likely helps students construct a more complete cognitive map of the operation, leading to superior skill acquisition [20]. The feedback from students in Group B, who rated the live broadcast visuals as superior in clarity, fluency, and completeness, further corroborates the efficacy of this combined information source.

Second, a superior pedagogical experience is a key factor in stimulating the intrinsic motivation of learners [21]. Building on this, the novel technological combination, coupled with a stronger sense of presence and interactivity, effectively enhanced the students’ interest and concentration, which ultimately translated into higher satisfaction. This suggests that advanced educational technologies are not merely tools but also important media for optimizing the psychological learning environment and increasing student engagement. The new visual experience offered by this head-mounted display could be very helpful for immersive teaching. For instance, in a study on the teaching of surgical instruments, researchers randomly divided 39 students into Virtual Reality (VR) group (n = 19) or a non-VR group (n = 20). The VR group trained using the VR headset, while the non-VR group used physical instruments. Pre- and post-tests assessed instrument recognition, and students rated their confidence using a visual analog scale [22]. They found VR-based training reported high levels of satisfaction with the training environment, which was consistent with our study. Conversely, they found the VR-based training was less effective than traditional methods in overall instrument recognition. In contrast, our study demonstrated that the surgical visualization headset can help students get higher scores in simulated surgery. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that the teaching effectiveness of VR devices was limited by their virtual scenes and interactive design. In our study, however, the surgical visualization headset presented students with real-world scenarios, and compared to conventional teaching methods, this method can display more comprehensive details and help students focus better.

However, this study also revealed a critical issue that warrants in-depth discussion: students in Group B reported significantly higher levels of visual fatigue. Although this finding may seem contradictory to the otherwise positive feedback from Group B, it may accurately reflect the distinct characteristics of the combination model. Students in Group B may have been required to shift their gaze more frequently between displays with different depths of field and fields of view. Alternatively, their heightened concentration and interest may have led them to invest more visual effort during the training, thereby exacerbating fatigue. This serves as a reminder that when promoting new technologies, we must address the potential ergonomic challenges they may introduce [23]. Future efforts could aim to mitigate this issue by optimizing display parameters or incorporating guided rest intervals.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was confined to students in a continuing education program. Second, there is a restricted scope of the clinical assessment, as it primarily focused on short-term immediate technical performance in a simulated environment. Crucially, the current study design did not evaluate long-term knowledge retention, post-training clinical confidence, or actual surgical performance in later clinical settings. Therefore, whether these initial gains translate to sustained competence requires further longitudinal investigation. Future research could be expanded to include learners at different proficiency levels and incorporate assessments of long-term skill retention to more comprehensively validate the value of this teaching model.

Conclusion

This study highlights the pedagogical value of integrating a surgical visualization headset with a dental microscope for preclinical bone augmentation training. Beyond addressing the ergonomic and viewing limitations of traditional screen broadcasting, this dual-view model significantly enriches the subjective learning experience and objectively improves technical skill acquisition. While acknowledging the need for future ergonomic refinements to address visual fatigue, this innovative paradigm holds substantial promise for advancing the standard of micro-dentistry education and warrants broader adoption in digital-era continuing medical education.
