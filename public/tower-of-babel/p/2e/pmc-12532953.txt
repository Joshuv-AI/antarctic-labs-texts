Identifying the quantity profiles of amyloid signature proteins in different types of renal amyloidosis
Authors: Shuang Wang, Danyang Li, Xin Zhang, Fude Zhou, Suxia Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study is to explore the quantity profiles of amyloid signature proteins (serum amyloid P component, SAP; apolipoprotein E, ApoE; apolipoprotein A-IV) in common types of renal amyloidosis by mass spectrometry and immunostaining methods.MethodsTwenty-one patients with renal amyloidosis of different types evaluated at the Renal Pathological Center of Peking University First Hospital from 2000 to 2021 were enrolled. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and laser microdissection combining with mass spectrometry (LMD-MS) were applied to investigate the localization and quantity profiles of signature proteins in renal amyloidosis. The co-localization relationships among signature proteins and amyloid fibrils, as well as the ultrastructural localization of SAP were examined by laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) and immuno-electron microscopy (IEM), respectively.ResultsBy MS-based proteomic analysis, large spectra numbers of ApoE and its higher abundance were noted in four types of amyloidosis when compared with SAP, and ApoA-IV was absent in ALECT2 amyloidosis. LSCM showed ApoE and SAP co-localized with amyloid fibrils in renal AL-κ, AL-λ and ALECT2 amyloidosis. ApoA-IV co-localized with amyloid fibrils in AL-κ and AL-λ amyloidosis, but was not found in ALECT2 amyloidosis. By semi-quantitative analysis based on LSCM and IEM, the quantity levels of signature proteins in AL-κ appeared to be lower than that in AL-λ (P < 0.05) or ALECT2 (P < 0.05), while there was no significant difference between AL-λ and ALECT2 amyloidosis.ConclusionBoth of SAP and ApoE were the ubiquitous signature components of renal amyloidosis (AL, AA, ALECT2), as well as ApoA-IV in AL and AA, but not in ALECT2. ApoE was the key signature protein in renal amyloidosis. The quantity levels of signature proteins investigated through LCSM/IEM demonstrated variability among different types, with AL-κ amyloidosis appeared to have a lower level.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04477-6.Keywords: Amyloidosis, Amyloid signature proteins, Mass spectrometry, Laser scanning confocal microscopy, Immunostaining methods

Introduction

Amyloidosis is a protein conformational disease caused by the deposition of insoluble fibrils which derived from the misfolded precursor proteins [1]. Currently, 42 precursor proteins are identified to cause amyloidosis in human and one third of them affect the kidney [2], all compartments of the kidney, including glomeruli, tubulointerstitium and vessels, can be involved in systemic amyloidosis, with glomerular deposits being predominant among patients with amyloid light chain (AL) and amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis, while leukocyte chemotactic factor 2 (ALECT2), variant transthyretin (ATTRv) and apolipoprotein A-IV (AApoA-IV) amyloidosis are characterized by predominant tubulointerstitial and medullary amyloid deposition [3]. The common types of renal amyloidosis include AL, AA and ALECT2 amyloidosis [4].

Apart from the intrinsic components of the precursor proteins, amyloid deposits are often accompanied by the deposition of extracellular proteins, which mainly include serum amyloid P component (SAP) [5–9], apolipoprotein E (ApoE) [7–11], apolipoprotein A-IV(ApoA-IV) [8, 9], etc. These proteins are commonly referred to as amyloid signature proteins, and some have reportedly been involved in the deposition and progression of amyloid fibrils, as well as been regarded as therapeutic targets in clinical trials [12–14].

SAP, a glycoprotein belonging to the pentraxin family, binds avidly to amyloid fibrils in a calcium dependent manner [15], thereby preventing amyloid fibrils from being degraded by protease in vitro [16] and contributes to amyloid formation and persistence in vivo [17]. Clinical trials targeting SAP have shown that the small-molecule drug, miridesap, which depletes circulation SAP, followed by subsequent administration of anti-SAP antibody, can progressively remove amyloid from kidneys and spleens [12–14]. ApoE, a regulator of lipoprotein metabolism and its gene polymorphism is a major genetic risk determinant of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [18, 19]. ApoA-IV mainly participates in the reverse cholesterol transport pathway [20] and its roles in amyloidogenesis have not been fully investigated yet. Although the above-mentioned proteins have been shown to localize in almost all types of amyloid deposits by using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and MS-based proteomics. However, their quantity levels and co-localization relationships with amyloid fibrils in various amyloid types and affected areas remain unclear.

In this study, we performed IHC and laser microdissection combining with MS (LMD-MS) analysis on renal biopsy specimens to explore the quantity profiles of amyloid signature proteins in the most common types of renal amyloidosis (AL-κ/λ, AA, ALECT2). Laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM), Thioflavin-T (ThT) staining and immuno-electron microscopy (IEM) were employed to semi-quantitively assess the quantity levels of these signature proteins, and reveal their co-localization relationships with amyloid fibrils. The findings of this current study may provide new insights for designing targeted therapy for amyloidosis.

Materials and methods

Specimens and scoring of amyloid deposits

Twenty-one renal biopsy specimens were retrospectively enrolled from the Renal Pathological Center of Peking University First Hospital. The diagnosis of amyloidosis was based on the positive Congo red stain and displayed characteristic birefringence under polarized light, amyloidosis was typed by immunofluorescence or LMD-MS as AL in 12 (8 cases of AL-λ and 4 cases of AL-κ) cases, AA in 5 cases, and ALECT2 in 4 cases, respectively. The extent of amyloid deposition in different renal compartments was scored semi-quantitatively according to our pervious study [21]. The extent of glomerular amyloid deposition (GA), vascular amyloid deposition (VA), and interstitial amyloid deposition (IA) was divided to 5 scores (0–4) according to the percentage of amyloid deposition to the total area of glomeruli, interlobular arterioles, and interstitium, respectively: absent (0), 1–10% (1), 11–25% (2), 26–50% (3), and >50% (4). The control specimens were from renal normal tissues adjacent to the tumor. Patient consent was obtained and this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (2022 − 448) followed the Helsinki guidelines for research of human subjects.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for precursors and signature proteins of amyloidosis

IHC staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues. Briefly, the 4-µm-thick sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, retrieved (3 min at high pressure in pH 6.0 citrate antigen retrieval solution), blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in 0.01 M phosphate buffer saline (PBS), pH 7.4, for 30 min at 37 °C, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S1. Immunoreactions were visualized using DAB substrate (Zsbio, Beijing, China). Negative controls were performed by omitting the primary antibodies. Imaging was performed under bright field microscopy (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Immunofluorescence and Thioflavin-T staining detected by laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM)

FFPE sections, cut at 4 μm, were deparaffinized, retrieved (3 min at high pressure in pH 6.0 citrate antigen retrieval solution), blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in 0.01 M PBS, pH 7.4, for 30 min at 37 °C, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S1. After washing three times in PBS, the slides were incubated with secondary antibodies [Goat anti-Rabbit Alexa Flour 594, Goat anti-Mouse Alexa Flour 594, Goat anti-Rabbit Alexa Flour 647, Goat anti-Mouse Alexa Flour 647, Donkey anti-Goat Alexa Flour 647 (1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, UK)] for 1 h at room temperature in a dark enclosure, then slides were washed in PBS for three times and staining with ThT (Sigma, Saint Louis, USA) solution for 15 min at 37 °C. Negative controls were performed to ensure antibody specificity by omitting primary antibodies.

The quantification and co-localization analysis of proteins were examined by confocal microscopy using a Leica TCS-SP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Image Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, Huston, USA) was used to calculate the ratio of mean fluorescent intensity of proteins to ThT staining. To evaluate the quantity levels of amyloid signature proteins between different amyloid groups, we calculated the ratio of mean fluorescent intensity between signature proteins and ThT.

Proteomic analysis of amyloidotic tissue by laser microdissection combining with mass spectrometry (LMD-MS)

We followed the previously published method [11]. Briefly, 10-µm-thick sections of FFPE tissues were stained with Congo red. Congo red-positive amyloid deposits and glomeruli from renal normal tissues were micro-dissected using the Leica dissector (Leica LMD 6000). Each microdissection contained an area of 50,000–60,000 µm2, at least 2 microdissections were analysed for each case. Each microdissected sample was collected into 0.5 mL microcentrifuge tube caps containing 30 µL Tris-EDTA/0.002% Zwittergent buffer, collected tissues were heated at 98 °C for 90 min. Following 60 min of sonication in a water bath. The microdissected material was digested with trypsin and ready for liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry analysis. Samples were analyzed using an LTQ-Orbitrap Velos Pro mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher) coupled with a nanoLC Easy-nLC II (Thermo Fisher). Each sample was loaded onto a trap column (Easy-column C18-A1, Thermo Fisher) with phase A (0.1% FA in water). Peptide separation was carried out on a C18 column (Easy-column C18-A2, Thermo Fisher) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min with a gradient of phase A (0.1% FA in H2O) and phase B (0.1% FA in acetonitrile). The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ionization with the data dependent acquisition (DDA) mode. The parent MS scan was performed in the Orbitrap from a mass range of 350 to 2,000 m/z with the resolution of 60,000 at 400 m/z. Top 15 method was used in the automatic switching between MS and MS/MS scans. The MS/MS spectra were acquired with LTQ after the collision-induced dissociation (CID) of normalized collision energy of 35. MS raw data files were queried using the Sequest algorithm, the search engines derived tryptic peptides from a composite database containing SwissProt human subspecies and Boston University’s Amyloid Light Chain Database [22]. The results were combined and assigned peptide and protein probability scores in Proteome Discoverer 1.4. Peptide identifications were accepted if they could be established at >99.0% probability as specified by the Peptide Prophet algorithm. For comparing the abundance of different proteins, we followed the published method: protein spectra counts, normalized to the total number of spectra counts per LMD case, were used as a semi-quantitative measure of abundance [23].

Immuno-electron microscopy (IEM) for precursor proteins and serum amyloid P component (SAP)

The 80-nm-thick resin-embedded sections were attached on the surface of a carbon-coated support film grid and blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin in 0.01 M PBS, pH 7.4, for 30 min at 37 °C. Sections were rinsed and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S1. Then, sections were rinsed and incubated for 60 min at room temperature with the secondary antibodies (anti-Rabbit immunoglobulin G; anti-Goat immunoglobulin G), which were conjugated to 12-nm colloidal gold particles (1:40, Jackson ImmunoResearch, PA, USA). the grid was ready for observation using a JEM-1400 electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). Negative controls were prepared by omitting the primary antibody. To evaluate the quantitation of labelling density, the photographs were taken in the four corners of a grid square randomly positioned over the most central area of a glomerulus at a magnification of x5,000, RADIUS software was used to enumerate the number of gold particles in each electron micrograph at a magnification of x30,000, at least ten micrographs were used for calculation in each case. The density of IEM labelling was obtained by dividing the number of gold particles by the area occupied by the amyloid fibrils.

Statistical analysis

The Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Walli’s test were applied to comparisons between two or three different groups. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 statistic software package. P values represented are 2-sided and P < 0.05 was considered to be significant. Continuous variables are presented as Mean ± SD or median (interquartile range, IQR).

Results

Localization of amyloid signature proteins in renal amyloid and nonamyloid tissues by IHC

We firstly investigated the localization of signature proteins in normal human renal tissues by IHC. Strong staining of SAP was observed in glomeruli, predominantly in glomerular basement membranes (GBMs), staining in blood vessels occurred but weakly (Supplementary Figure S1A and B). ApoE was not detected in normal human renal tissues (Supplementary Figure S1C and D). Staining of ApoA-IV was distinct in tubules, especially in proximal tubules. No staining of ApoA-IV was observed in glomeruli or blood vessels (Supplementary Figure S1E-H). Next, we demonstrated their localization in renal amyloid tissues. The results were summarized in Table 1. SAP and ApoE were detected in all amyloid cases, whereas ApoA-IV was identified in AL (κ and λ) and AA but absent in ALECT2. The localization of precursor proteins and signature proteins in renal amyloidosis were revealed among the four different types (AL-κ, AL-λ, AA and ALECT2) by using the appropriate antibody treatment in serial sections (Fig. 1). The staining for ApoE and SAP was strong and matched well to amyloid deposits in the glomeruli (most in the mesangial area). In contrast to the universal presence of SAP and ApoE in all amyloid cases, staining for ApoA-IV was positive in AL (κ and λ) and AA but negative in ALECT2 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Figure S2). Except for the amyloid deposits in glomeruli, we also investigated whether these three signature proteins could be detected in other renal amyloid areas, such as arterioles and tubulointerstitium. Supplementary Figure S3 and S4 revealed the IHC staining for signature proteins in renal arteriolar and tubulointerstitial amyloid areas, respectively. The results were similar to aforementioned findings. SAP and ApoE were found in renal arteriole and tubulointerstitium of all amyloid types, ApoA-IV was contained in these amyloid load areas of AL (κ and λ) and AA, but not in ALECT2.

Mass spectrometry analysis of amyloid signature proteins in renal amyloid and non-amyloid tissues

LMD-MS analysis was performed in all cases (Table 2). Within 3 non-amyloid tissues, low level of SAP was detected in two cases, while ApoE and ApoA-IV were not detected by MS. Among 12 AL cases, the clonotypic lg light chain variable region gene was identified in ten cases, although the variable region was not detected in other two AL-λ cases mainly due to low amyloid burden (Supplementary Table S1), the detection of clonotypic λ light chain constant region could also confirm the amyloid type. As for AA and ALECT2 cases, except for one case (no.20) of ALECT2 failing to detect LECT2 protein due to insufficient amyloid deposits, all other cases successfully identified the spectra numbers of precursor proteins, thereby confirming the previous IHC findings. SAP was ubiquitously present in all types of amyloidosis (median 18.0 (11.0-32.5) for AL-κ; median 37.5 (7.0–48.0) for AL-λ; median 44.0 (27.5–68.0) for AA; median 11.0 (9.5–19.5) for ALECT2). Large spectra numbers of ApoE were identified in AL (median 94.5 (60.5-165.3) for AL-κ; median 79.0 (18.8-129.3) for AL-λ) and AA (median 56.0 (48.0-122.0)), the spectra numbers of ApoE were smaller in ALECT2 amyloidosis (median 19.0 (11.0-38.5)). Unlike SAP and ApoE, which were universally detected in amyloid deposits, MS analysis showed that ApoA-IV was identified in AL (median 5.5 (2.5–30.8) for AL-λ; median 13.5 (3.8–26.3) for AL-κ) and AA (median 9.0 (3.0–32.0)), while was negative in ALECT2. By measuring the abundance of signature proteins semi-quantitatively, we found that the abundance of ApoE in amyloid deposits detected by MS was relatively higher than the other two signature proteins (Fig. 2).

Quantification of amyloid signature proteins in renal amyloid tissues by LSCM and IEM

To reveal the co-localization relationships between amyloid signature proteins and amyloid fibrils, as well as to evaluate the quantity levels of signature proteins, we applied LSCM to show the immunofluorescence of signature proteins alongside ThT staining for labelling amyloid fibrils. The analyses for AA amyloid tissues were excluded due to the poor specimens. To verify the specificity of ThT, we tested ThT staining in renal normal tissues and no staining was observed (Supplementary Figure S5).

In renal amyloid tissues, all types of amyloid fibrils were successfully labelled by ThT (Fig. 3A, column 2), the types of amyloidosis were determined by the immunofluorescence to their precursor proteins (Fig. 3A, column 3), SAP was positive in all amyloid types (Fig. 3A, column 1) and show clear co-localization with amyloid fibrils (Fig. 3A, column 4). We found the quantity level of SAP in AL-λ and ALECT2 was significantly higher compared to AL-κ, however, no significant difference was found between AL-λ and ALECT2 (Fig. 3B). We next investigated whether its quantity levels varied in different amyloid areas within a single amyloid type, but no significant difference was observed in either AL-λ or ALECT2 (Fig. 3C).

ApoE is a major constituent of amyloid and it was identified by immunofluorescence. The merged images showed ApoE co-localized well with amyloid fibrils in renal amyloid deposits (Supplementary Fig. 6A). the quantity level of ApoE showed no significant difference between AL-λ and ALECT2, however, it was decreased in AL-κ (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Furthermore, neither AL-λ nor ALECT2 showed significant variations in ApoE quantity levels across different amyloid areas (Supplementary Fig. 6C).

Apart from SAP and ApoE, we also revealed the co-localization and quantity level of ApoA-IV in different types of amyloid deposits. In accordance with the findings of IHC and MS analysis, ApoA-IV was not detected in ALECT2 by immunofluorescence (Fig. 4A, line 3). In AL (κ and λ) amyloid deposits, it was found to be positive and have clear co-localization with amyloid fibrils (Fig. 4A, line 1–2). As for the quantity level of ApoA-IV, we found that compared to AL-κ, its quantity level was significantly higher in AL-λ (Fig. 4B), while no significant difference was observed between amyloid areas in AL-λ (Fig. 4C).

Electron-dense, 12-nm gold particles corresponding to lambda/kappa light chain-specific (Fig. 5A and C) or LECT2-specific (Fig. 5E) antibodies, as well as SAP-specific antibodies (Fig. 5B, D and F), were clearly visible in areas where the amyloid fibrils were located. Discussion

In this study, we have systematically investigated the expression profiles of amyloid signature proteins and their respective quantity levels in common types of renal amyloidosis. IHC was used to investigate the localization of signature proteins in different amyloid load areas, we found positive staining for SAP and ApoE in all tested cases of renal AL, AA and ALECT2 amyloidosis, further detailed examination showed the similar patterns in all affected compartments of kidneys (glomeruli, arterioles, tubulointerstitium). By MS-based proteomic analysis, we confirmed the presence of SAP and ApoE in the amyloid deposits of all tested cases.

We also found ApoE present with a higher spectra number in all types of amyloid deposits than SAP and ApoA-IV, by semi-quantitatively measuring their abundance, ApoE accounted for the largest abundance among the three signature proteins in amyloid deposits. Previous studies, as well as our unpublished preliminary research utilizing MS also detected the large spectra numbers of ApoE in fibrillary glomerulonephritis, immunotactoid glomerulopathy [11] and light chain deposition disease, while SAP was not present or present in very low spectra numbers. Taken together, these findings suggest ApoE along with the precursor proteins (light chain, SAA, LECT2) is key to organization of the amyloid deposits.

Unlike the ubiquitous presence of SAP and ApoE in amyloidosis, the immunostaining detection of amyloid signature proteins in renal specimens through both IHC and LSCM revealed the presence of ApoA-IV in AL (κ and λ) and AA amyloidosis, while it was absent in ALECT2 amyloidosis, these findings were further validated by MS analysis. Recently, Charalampous et al. have presented the amyloid proteome from 8 different types of kidney amyloidosis and found ApoA-IV was nearly absent in ALECT2 [23], which supported our results. Based on these findings, ApoA-IV could not be considered as a signature protein in ALECT2 amyloidosis. Currently, we cannot clarify why ApoA-IV is absent in ALECT2 amyloidosis because it is almost completely unexplored in this research field. ALECT2 amyloidosis is recently described and the pathogenesis of it remains unknown. We speculate the lack of ApoA-IV in ALECT2 amyloidosis may be related to the structures of amyloid fibrils, it should be noted that the amyloid deposits of ALECT2 is strikingly congophilic, which suggests the amyloid fibrils formed by LECT2 have different characteristics in morphology from that formed by light chain or SAA, so ApoA-IV could bind to the amyloid fibrils in AL and AA, but not to ALECT2.

The fluorescence emitted by ThT is widely recognized to reveal amyloid fibrils, owing to its ability to bind specifically to the β-sheet structures present in amyloid protofilaments, and the fluorescence intensity scales linearly with amyloid fibril mass [24, 25], ThT serves as a reliable amyloid fibril marker. When using LSCM, we could investigate the co-localization relationships among amyloid fibrils, precursor proteins and signature proteins. As expected, these three signature proteins co-localized well with ThT labelling amyloid fibrils in renal amyloidosis. The clear co-localization observed between the signature proteins and different types of amyloid fibrils in our studies suggested these three proteins could bind to amyloid fibrils, thus being involved in fibrillogenesis. Previous studies have demonstrated that SAP and ApoE could affect the formation of Aβ amyloid fibrils in vitro. SAP has been shown to inhibit the aggregation of Aβ into amyloid fibrils [26], whereas ApoE had dual effects depending on its concentration relative to precursor proteins [27]. Specifically, ApoE could promote fibrillogenesis when the precursor protein was present in a large molar excess, while the amyloid formation was inhibited when ApoE was present at high concentrations. Recent study found serum ApoE and ApoA-IV can adhere to amyloid fibrils formed by Aβ and insulin at high concentrations in vitro [28]. Despite these advances, the detailed structural conformations and specific binding sites between signature proteins and amyloid fibrils remain largely unexplored and warrant further investigation.

By employing a semi-quantitative method to compare the quantity levels of signature proteins in renal amyloidosis and calculating the ratio of intensify of signature proteins to ThT by LSCM, we found that the quantity levels of signature proteins vary in different types of amyloidosis, as in AL-λ and ALECT2, the quantity levels appeared to be higher than that in AL-κ. Furthermore, IEM validated the immunogold labelling density of SAP varied in AL and ALECT2, with AL-κ had the least number of gold particles among these three types (AL-κ, AL-λ and ALECT2). These findings suggest that when signature proteins participate in the formation of amyloid fibrils, the ratio between signature proteins and amyloid precursor proteins may vary in different amyloid types. We thought this might be related to the molecular structure of amyloid fibrils. Structure studies based on cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-ET) have demonstrated that systemic amyloid fibrils can be consisted of one or more protofilaments [29–33], which may provide a varying number of binding sites capable of being recognized by signature proteins. We also observed the discrepancy between semi-quantitative MS and LSCM/IEM about the signature proteins in tested cases. Given that MS can reveal the amyloid plaque proteome, it is plausible that other protein components within the deposits may influence the quantity of signature proteins.

Limitations of the study include the relatively small sample size as well as the types of amyloidosis, and in several cases, either the amyloid deposits were not sufficient for MS analysis, or the proteins were not digested into proper size and the peptides did not all ionize similarly. Besides, we did not identify and compare the quantify profiles of signature proteins in other amyloidosis involved organs such as heart, liver or fat tissues.

In conclusion, we have systemically investigated the quantify profiles of SAP, ApoE and ApoA-IV in common renal amyloidosis and in different compartments of kidney affected by amyloid deposits. The relatively higher abundance of ApoE detected by MS, coupled with IHC results, indicating that ApoE may play a role as critical as SAP in progression and maintenance of amyloidosis. Furthermore, we also confirmed ApoA-IV was absent in amyloid deposits of renal ALECT2 amyloidosis. In addition, we revealed the clear co-localization between these three amyloid signature proteins and amyloid fibrils. Based on semi-quantitative analysis using LSCM/IEM, we observed a higher composition of signature proteins in AL-λ and ALECT2 when compared with AL-κ. It is tempting to consider that, except for SAP in clinical trials, ApoE could also serve as a potential therapeutic target in systemic amyloidosis.
