The progestin anchor: salvaging mature oocyte yield after an unplanned six-day gonadotropin pause in oncofertility—a case report
Authors: Przemysław Ciepiela, Arkadiusz Adamczyk, Joanna Aszkiełowicz, Weronika Józefowska, Grzegorz Mrugacz
Published: 2026

Introduction

Oncofertility, the intersection of oncology and reproductive medicine, has become a vital subspecialty focused on preserving reproductive potential in patients undergoing gonadotoxic therapies (1, 2). Controlled ovarian stimulation followed by oocyte or embryo cryopreservation remains the most established, evidence-based method for fertility preservation in women of reproductive age (3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10). Progestin-primed ovarian stimulation protocols, in which oral medroxyprogesterone acetate suppresses premature luteinizing hormone (LH) activity through hypothalamic-pituitary negative feedback, provide scheduling flexibility, allow random-start stimulation, and avoid the need for gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist administration, which may be advantageous in urgent fertility preservation settings (11, 12, 13, 14). Under standard conditions, controlled ovarian stimulation requires uninterrupted daily gonadotropin administration. Significant deviations are generally considered likely to compromise follicular development and oocyte yield. Intentional gonadotropin ‟coasting,” historically used to reduce the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome in excessive responders, is typically brief, closely monitored, and differs in both purpose and clinical context from an unplanned interruption (15, 16, 17, 18). In time-constrained settings, including oncology care and military service, stimulation interruptions may nevertheless be unavoidable (19). Despite the clinical relevance of this scenario, published data describing extended unplanned gonadotropin interruptions during fertility preservation are lacking.

We report the case of a young active-duty soldier undergoing fertility preservation who experienced an unexpected 6-day gonadotropin-free interval during her second progestin-primed ovarian stimulation cycle. Despite reduced total gonadotropin exposure, she achieved a mature oocyte yield comparable with her prior uninterrupted reference cycle. We propose that continuous oral medroxyprogesterone acetate may have acted as a biologically plausible ‟progestin anchor,” while emphasizing that causality cannot be established from a single case.

Case report

A 22-year-old active-duty soldier sought fertility preservation after ovarian surgery for a borderline ovarian tumor. The surgery had been performed approximately 9 months before the first fertility preservation cycle and approximately 12 months before the second cycle. The histopathological specimen included the left ovary and a fragment of the right ovary, and histopathology confirmed a serous borderline ovarian tumor without evident invasive foci. Baseline ovarian reserve assessment before fertility preservation showed an antimüllerian hormone concentration of 1.77 ng/mL. The patient reported regular menstrual cycles and had no other relevant medical history. As a military service member, she faced logistical constraints related to unpredictable deployment schedules and nondeferrable service commitments. The patient was counseled regarding controlled ovarian stimulation and oocyte vitrification, and written informed consent was obtained before both cycles. Written informed consent for publication of this case report and associated data was also obtained from the patient.

First cycle: reference cycle. Controlled ovarian stimulation was initiated with daily subcutaneous follitropin alfa 300 IU combined with oral medroxyprogesterone acetate 10 mg daily as the sole LH suppressor; no GnRH antagonist was administered. Stimulation continued for 10 consecutive days, with a total gonadotropin dose of 3,000 IU. On the trigger day, ultrasound showed 13 follicles ≥14 mm in the right ovary, with follicular diameters ranging from 15 to 20 mm and a leading follicle of 20 mm. Serum estradiol was 2,650 pg/mL, endogenous serum progesterone was 0.8 ng/mL, and endometrial thickness was 10.5 mm. Final oocyte maturation was triggered with subcutaneous triptorelin 0.2 mg. Transvaginal oocyte retrieval was performed 36 hours later. Thirteen oocytes were retrieved; six were classified as metaphase II (MII) on the day of retrieval and vitrified on day 0, whereas the remaining seven oocytes were at the germinal vesicle stage and were not vitrified. No delayed day-1 vitrification after overnight maturation of immature oocytes was performed.

Second cycle: index event. An identical progestin-primed ovarian stimulation protocol was initiated with follitropin alfa 300 IU daily and oral medroxyprogesterone acetate 10 mg daily as the sole LH suppressor. The patient received six doses of follitropin alfa, for a total gonadotropin dose of 1,800 IU. At the ultrasound assessment immediately preceding the unplanned gonadotropin-free interval, serum estradiol was 1,612 pg/mL, and ultrasound showed nine visible follicles in the right ovary, including three follicles measuring <14 mm and six follicles measuring ≥14 mm. Follicular diameters ranged from 13 to 18 mm, with a leading follicle of 18 mm. The patient then received a nondeferrable military summons requiring immediate absence, making continued gonadotropin injections and monitoring impossible. A 6-day unplanned gonadotropin-free interval ensued. Oral medroxyprogesterone acetate was continued daily without interruption throughout this period. Given the time-sensitive fertility preservation context and the absence of features suggesting imminent ovulation at the preceding assessment, the attending physician elected watchful waiting rather than immediate cycle cancellation.

Upon return after the 6-day interval, ultrasound confirmed persistence of an intact follicular cohort without sonographic features of follicular rupture or premature luteinization. On the trigger day after the gonadotropin-free interval, ultrasound demonstrated seven follicles ≥14 mm in the right ovary, with follicular diameters ranging from 17 to 19 mm and a leading follicle of 19 mm. Serum estradiol was 1,580 pg/mL, endogenous serum progesterone was 1.1 ng/mL, and endometrial thickness was 9.7 mm. Final oocyte maturation was triggered the same day with subcutaneous triptorelin 0.2 mg. Oocyte retrieval was performed 36 hours later. Seven oocytes were retrieved; five were classified as MII on the day of retrieval and vitrified on day 0, one was at the germinal vesicle stage, and one was degenerated. No delayed day-1 vitrification after overnight maturation of immature oocytes was performed. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first case report describing the retrieval of a clinically meaningful number of mature oocytes after an unplanned 6-day gonadotropin-free interval during progestin-primed ovarian stimulation for oncofertility. The key observation is that although total oocyte recovery decreased from 13 to 7 oocytes, MII oocyte yield was largely preserved, with 5 MII oocytes retrieved in the interrupted index cycle compared with 6 in the uninterrupted reference cycle. This occurred despite a 40% reduction in total gonadotropin dose and absence of monitoring during the gonadotropin-free interval.

Although this scenario superficially resembles intentional coasting, it differs in several clinically important respects. Intentional coasting is an elective, protocol-driven withholding of gonadotropins, historically used to reduce the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome in excessive responders, and is typically accompanied by close hormonal and ultrasound monitoring. In contrast, the present interruption was unplanned, occurred in a time-constrained oncofertility setting, lacked daily monitoring, and was driven by an external logistical constraint rather than by an excessive ovarian response. In addition, LH suppression in this case was maintained by continuous oral medroxyprogesterone acetate within a progestin-primed ovarian stimulation protocol rather than by a GnRH antagonist. For these reasons, the present case should not be interpreted as an endorsement of planned prolonged coasting but rather as a rescue decision after an unavoidable gonadotropin-free interval.

A plausible protective mechanism in this case is sustained suppression of LH by continuous oral medroxyprogesterone acetate. In progestin-primed ovarian stimulation protocols, medroxyprogesterone acetate suppresses endogenous LH activity through hypothalamic-pituitary negative feedback, thereby reducing the risk of premature LH surge during ovarian stimulation without GnRH antagonist administration. In the index cycle, oral medroxyprogesterone acetate was continued without interruption throughout the 6-day gonadotropin-free interval. This may have provided a sustained suppressive environment during the absence of exogenous follicle-stimulating hormone. The near-stability of serum estradiol across the interruption, from 1,612 pg/mL immediately before the gonadotropin-free interval to 1,580 pg/mL on the trigger day, together with an endogenous progesterone concentration of 1.1 ng/mL and the absence of sonographic rupture or luteinization, is compatible with preserved follicular activity rather than complete follicular collapse. Importantly, this single case cannot establish that a premature LH surge, luteinization, or ovulation would necessarily have occurred in the absence of medroxyprogesterone acetate. Some patients may not experience an endogenous LH surge during a temporary gonadotropin interruption even without progestin exposure. Therefore, the present case should not be interpreted as proof that progestin was required to salvage the cycle. Rather, continuous medroxyprogesterone acetate represents a biologically plausible protective factor that may have reduced the risk of premature luteinization during the gonadotropin-free interval.

The follicular measurements provide a useful window into the dynamics of the interrupted cycle. At the ultrasound assessment immediately preceding the gonadotropin-free interval, nine visible follicles were present, including three follicles measuring <14 mm and six follicles measuring ≥14 mm, with a size range of 13–18 mm. On the trigger day after the interval, seven follicles ≥14 mm remained visible, with a narrower size range of 17–19 mm. This change is compatible with partial attrition or remodeling of the follicular cohort during the interruption while preserving a retrievable, developmentally advanced cohort (20). The subsequent retrieval of seven oocytes, including five MII oocytes, supports the clinical relevance of this residual cohort. However, because serial hormonal and ultrasound monitoring was not performed during the gonadotropin-free interval, the precise follicular dynamics cannot be definitively reconstructed.

The embryological findings are also relevant. In both cycles, only oocytes classified as MII on the day of retrieval were vitrified, and no delayed day-1 vitrification after overnight maturation of immature oocytes was performed. Therefore, the reported mature oocyte yield reflects nuclear maturity on the day of retrieval rather than delayed in vitro maturation. In the reference cycle, 6 of 13 oocytes were MII on day 0, whereas 7 were at the germinal vesicle stage. In the index cycle, 5 of 7 oocytes were MII on day 0, 1 was at the germinal vesicle stage, and 1 was degenerated. The higher maturation rate in the index cycle, 71.4% vs. 46.2%, should not be overinterpreted from a single case, but it is consistent with the possibility that the gonadotropin-free interval preferentially reduced the less mature portion of the cohort while preserving follicles capable of final oocyte maturation after trigger.

The clinical implication is not that prolonged gonadotropin interruption should be planned, but rather that automatic cancellation may not always be necessary when an interruption occurs during a time-constrained fertility preservation cycle. Beyond the military context, this observation may be relevant to other oncofertility scenarios, including unexpected emergency department visits or hospital admissions, acute medical complications requiring temporary interruption of injections, medication supply disruptions, compassionate-care medication limits, or financial delays in obtaining additional gonadotropins. In such settings, a structured reassessment may be reasonable before cancellation. This reassessment should include assessment for premature luteinization, LH measurement when available, serum estradiol and endogenous progesterone, and careful ultrasound evaluation for follicular persistence, follicular rupture, corpus luteum formation, and free fluid, together with explicit counseling that total oocyte yield may be reduced even if mature oocyte recovery remains possible.

This report has several limitations. First, it describes a single case and cannot establish generalizable safety or efficacy. Second, hormone levels and ultrasound findings were not monitored during the 6-day gonadotropin-free interval, so transient endocrine events cannot be fully excluded. Third, although continuous medroxyprogesterone acetate is a biologically plausible protective factor, this case cannot prove that premature LH surge or ovulation would have occurred without progestin exposure. Fourth, baseline antral follicle count and basal follicle-stimulating hormone levels were not available in the source documentation, although antimüllerian hormone was. Fifth, all MII oocytes were vitrified and have not yet been warmed; therefore, postwarming survival, fertilization potential, embryo development, and reproductive outcomes remain unknown. Finally, metaphase II classification on the day of retrieval reflects nuclear maturity but cannot by itself establish full developmental competence.

Conclusion

This case suggests that, in a selected oncofertility patient undergoing progestin-primed ovarian stimulation with continuous oral medroxyprogesterone acetate, an unplanned 6-day gonadotropin-free interval did not preclude retrieval of a clinically meaningful number of mature oocytes. Despite a 40% reduction in total gonadotropin dose and a reduction in total oocyte recovery from 13 to 7 oocytes, mature oocyte yield was largely preserved, with 5 MII oocytes retrieved in the interrupted cycle compared with 6 in the uninterrupted reference cycle. Continuous oral medroxyprogesterone acetate may have acted as a biologically plausible protective factor by maintaining LH suppression during the gonadotropin-free interval, although causality cannot be inferred from a single case. In time-constrained fertility preservation, structured reassessment rather than automatic cancellation may be appropriate when follicular integrity is preserved, and premature luteinization is not evident.
