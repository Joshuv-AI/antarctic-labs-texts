Hodgkin's Lymphoma Presenting With Nephrotic Syndrome and Complicated by Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome in a Child: A Rare Multisystem Interaction
Authors: Balqees Al‐Shohef, Majd Hamed Nasser, Hind Alhiraki, Sameer Baqla, Othman Hamdan, Mona Hussein
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEarly recognition of nephrotic syndrome and neurological complications in pediatric Hodgkin's lymphoma is essential. This case highlights a rare association between Hodgkin's lymphoma, nephrotic syndrome, and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome, emphasizing the importance of multidisciplinary management and prompt blood pressure control to optimize outcomes.Keywords: Hodgkin's lymphoma, nephrotic syndrome, paraneoplastic syndrome, pediatric oncology, PRES

Introduction

The association between Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and Nephrotic syndrome (NS) is uncommon, occurring in less than 1% of cases, with approximately 50 cases reported worldwide [1]. NS may precede, coincide with, or follow the diagnosis of HL, supporting a paraneoplastic mechanism [2].

Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome is a rare but serious neurological condition, most often associated with hypertension or cytotoxic therapies, and may further complicate the clinical course [3].

Here, we report a rare pediatric presentation of HL associated with NS and subsequently complicated by PRES, highlighting the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic considerations associated with this complex clinical interaction.

Case Presentation

Patient Information

A 5‐year‐old boy presented with a 2‐week history of fever and bilateral eyelid and neck swelling. There was no relevant family history or prior medical illness.

Clinical Findings

On examination, the patient appeared edematous with generalized edema, including prominent periorbital swelling. Multiple enlarged cervical, axillary, and supraclavicular lymph nodes were detected. Hepatomegaly (4 cm below the costal margin) and splenomegaly (4–5 cm below the costal margin) were also noted. Initial vital signs were within normal limits.

Diagnostic Assessment

Laboratory Tests

Urinalysis revealed nephrotic‐range proteinuria (370 mg/kg/day).

Serum biochemistry showed severe hypoalbuminemia (1.2 g/dL; normal range: 3.5–5.5 g/dL) and hypercholesterolemia (291 mg/dL; normal range: < 200 mg/dL).

Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was markedly elevated at 131 mm/h (normal range: < 20 mm/h).

Imaging

Chest computed tomography demonstrated right pleural effusion and mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed splenomegaly with heterogeneous echotexture.

Histopathology

Excisional lymph node biopsy demonstrated Reed–Sternberg cells (Figure 1). Immunohistochemical staining was positive for CD30 and CD15, confirming the diagnosis of Hodgkin's lymphoma.

Lymph node biopsy (Reed‐Sternberg cells).

Therapeutic Intervention

The patient was started on ABVD chemotherapy consisting of Adriamycin, Bleomycin, Vinblastine, and Dacarbazine. Supportive management included albumin infusion, diuretics, filgrastim, and prednisolone at a dose of 60 mg/m2/day for nephrotic syndrome management.

Complications and Follow‐Up

Following the first cycle of ABVD chemotherapy, the patient developed generalized tonic–clonic seizures associated with acute hypertension, with blood pressure reaching 140/80 mmHg. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed bilateral parieto‐occipital hyperintensities consistent with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES) (Figure 2).

MRI brain (FLAIR sequence) demonstrating parieto‐occipital hyperintensities consistent with PRES.

The patient was managed successfully with antihypertensive therapy (Lisoten) and levetiracetam. Subsequent follow‐up demonstrated complete clinical resolution of nephrotic syndrome and PRES after completion of 6 cycles of chemotherapy.

Discussion

Literature Review and Epidemiology

The association between Hodgkin lymphoma (HL) and Nephrotic syndrome (NS) was first described by Galloway et al. [4]. Since then, fewer than 50 cases have been reported worldwide, with no previously documented cases from Syria [1].

Regarding the temporal relationship between the two conditions, Audard et al. reported that NS preceded HL diagnosis in 38% of cases, followed it in 43%, and occurred concurrently in 19% [1]. In the present case, HL and NS were diagnosed simultaneously, supporting a paraneoplastic association, while Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome developed 1 month after initiation of chemotherapy.

Most reported cases occur in adolescents, with a median age of 18 years; however, our patient was notably younger (5 years), highlighting the rarity of this presentation in early childhood [2].

PRES complicating HL is exceptionally rare and has been described only in isolated reports. Known contributing factors include chemotherapy agents (e.g., vincristine) and hypertension, both of which were relevant in our patient [5].

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

The pathophysiological link between HL and NS remains incompletely understood; however, several mechanisms have been proposed. Immune dysregulation appears to play a central role, with tumor‐derived cytokines such as IL‐13 and IL‐6 increasing glomerular permeability and inducing podocyte injury, ultimately leading to proteinuria [2]. Reed–Sternberg cells may contribute to podocyte injury through cytokine‐mediated immune dysregulation, particularly involving IL‐13 overexpression, leading to increased glomerular permeability and proteinuria.

Additionally, circulating autoantibodies directed against renal antigens may contribute to the development of NS, as suggested by Peces et al. [6].

The development of PRES in this context is likely multifactorial. Chemotherapy‐induced endothelial injury, particularly related to agents such as bleomycin, in combination with hypertension, may disrupt the blood–brain barrier and lead to vasogenic edema [5, 7]. Furthermore, electrolyte disturbances, including hypomagnesemia, along with renal dysfunction, may exacerbate cerebral edema and increase susceptibility to PRES [3].

Impact of HL Treatment on NS

The resolution of NS following treatment of HL strongly supports its paraneoplastic nature. Recurrence of NS in parallel with HL relapse has been previously reported [8]. In our case, complete remission of NS was achieved after six cycles of ABVD chemotherapy, consistent with previous reports [9]. Clinical Implications

This case underscores several important clinical considerations. First, unexplained proteinuria in pediatric patients with HL should prompt evaluation for paraneoplastic NS [2]. Second, the onset of neurological symptoms such as seizures necessitates urgent neuroimaging to exclude PRES and guide timely management [10].

Optimal management requires a multidisciplinary approach involving pediatric oncologists, nephrologists, and neurologists to address the complex interplay of systemic complications [11, 12]. Strict blood pressure control is essential to prevent progression of PRES, while careful adjustment of chemotherapy regimens, including avoidance of nephrotoxic agents when possible, may help reduce both renal and neurological risks [7].

Limitations and Future Directions

This report has several limitations. As a single‐case study, its findings are not generalizable, and the absence of molecular investigations, such as cytokine profiling, limits confirmation of the proposed mechanisms.

The patient remains under follow‐up at the Pediatric Hematology and Oncology Clinic during the supportive care phase, with plans for radiotherapy initiation. Blood pressure is well controlled without the need for ongoing antihypertensive therapy, and no further neurological manifestations have been observed. Prednisolone has been successfully tapered and discontinued in parallel with the resolution of proteinuria and stabilization of renal function.

Future research should focus on multicenter studies to explore potential genetic or regional predispositions, as well as mechanistic investigations into HL‐associated podocyte injury.

Conclusion

This report describes a rare pediatric presentation involving the coexistence of Hodgkin lymphoma, Nephrotic syndrome, and Posterior Reversible Encephalopathy Syndrome, highlighting a complex interplay between paraneoplastic and treatment‐related complications. The concurrent onset of HL and NS in this patient supports a paraneoplastic mechanism, while the subsequent development of PRES following chemotherapy likely reflects a multifactorial process involving hypertension and endothelial injury.

Effective management of this triad depended on early recognition, prompt blood pressure control, and appropriate oncologic treatment, resulting in resolution of both the malignancy and its associated complications. These findings are consistent with previous reports emphasizing the paraneoplastic link between HL and NS, as well as the role of hypertension and chemotherapy in the pathogenesis of PRES [3, 6, 8].

From a clinical perspective, this case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for paraneoplastic and neurological complications in pediatric HL patients. Early identification of proteinuria or neurological symptoms should prompt timely investigation and intervention. A multidisciplinary approach involving oncologists, nephrologists, and neurologists remains essential to optimize patient outcomes [11, 12], while careful management of blood pressure and treatment‐related risks may reduce the likelihood of complications such as PRES.

Despite its clinical relevance, this report is limited by its single‐case design and the absence of molecular studies to confirm the proposed mechanisms. Future research should focus on multicenter collaborations and mechanistic investigations to better understand the underlying pathways and identify strategies to prevent such rare but significant complications.
