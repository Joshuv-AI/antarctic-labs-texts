Unveiling Mexico's Demographic Transitions
Authors: Roxana Arana‐Ovalle, Lisa Dillon, Alejandro Murua, Francisco Zamudio‐Sánchez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examines Mexico's fertility transition (1930–2015) and how socioeconomic status (SES), geography, and indigeneity shaped reproductive behaviors. Using net fertility—the number of surviving children under five—we assess how prestige bias (adopting high‐status fertility norms) and conformism bias (aligning with local norms) influenced change across distinct population groups. We introduce the time, space, and population model to analyze the combined effects of macrostructural forces, spatial diffusion, and individual decision‐making. Our spatial analysis reveals a concentric diffusion pattern, where fertility changes spread outward from urban, high‐SES municipalities. Findings reveal a consistent negative association between SES and fertility across all periods, though with varying intensity. Higher status populations led the fertility decline, but patterns differed by group and over time. Fertility declined at different rates across four groups: urban non‐Indigenous populations transitioned rapidly, rural non‐Indigenous groups stagnated, rural Indigenous populations experienced delays, and urban Indigenous groups resisted fertility decline. Evidence suggests non‐Indigenous populations regulated fertility through retarding marriage before widespread contraceptive adoption, while Indigenous groups followed more conformist behaviors. This study integrates historical demographic data into a structured framework, improving research on long‐term fertility transitions.

INTRODUCTION

The demographic transition (DT) theory has long served as a foundational framework for understanding shifts in fertility and mortality (Van de Kaa 1996; Leridon and Dutreuilh 2015). However, its assumptions of linear progression—where fertility and mortality are expected to decline in a gradual, sequential manner as socioeconomic development advances—and of universal applicability have been increasingly challenged, particularly in non‐Western contexts where structural inequalities, and cultural dynamics play a significant role (Cosio‐Zavala 2012; Defo 2014). DT theory assumes a shift from natural to controlled fertility, yet historical evidence shows pre‐transitional populations also regulated fertility through spacing and stopping behaviors—strategies shaped by social norms such as extended breastfeeding and delayed marriage, as well as by prevailing economic and social conditions (Anderton and Bean 1985; Cinnirella, Klemp, and Weisdorf 2017; Kolk 2011; Van Bavel 2003).

This study extends DT models by examining Mexico's case through a contextual lens, an approach further developed in the subsequent introduction of the time, space, and population (TSP) model. In line with evolutionary demography, we conceptualize fertility as an adaptive response to environmental constraints rather than a linear progression from biologically determined reproduction to modern contraceptive planning (Colleran 2016; Sear and Burger 2019). Specifically, we draw on insights from evolutionary theory, which suggests that fertility behaviors may diffuse through prestige bias—where individuals imitate socially influential groups—and conformist bias—where behaviors common in a community are adopted (Sear 2015).

Latin America has undergone a delayed but rapid demographic transition, marked by highly heterogeneous fertility patterns across ethnic and socioeconomic groups (Cosio‐Zavala 2014; Pérez Brignoli 2010). In Mexico, distinctive factors—including pronatalist policies preceding 1970 and the establishment of “mestizo” ideology in the early 20th century—played pivotal roles in the rapid growth and the ethnic composition of the population (Sanchez‐Rivera 2023; Stern 2000). These national trends diverged significantly among Indigenous populations, where socioeconomic and cultural factors led to distinct fertility patterns (Heaton, Knowlton, and England 2012; Leyva‐Flores et al. 2013).

Ethnic discrimination and geographic factors—such as language barriers, family structures, and geographical isolation—have shaped fertility norms alongside structural constraints like poverty and limited access to health care (Gallup, Gaviria, and Lora 2003; Núñez 2022; Stern 2000). Traditional gender roles and intergenerational dynamics in Indigenous and rural communities have reinforced high fertility patterns, contributing to unequal access to family planning and education on contraceptive methods (Costa et al., 2022; Mesenburg et al., 2018). These disparities remain particularly acute in rural, Indigenous, and marginalized populations, highlighting the need for spatial fertility analyses that incorporate contextual and socioeconomic variables to better understand regional trends (Cosio‐Zavala 2022; Rentería‐Gaeta, Valencia‐López, and Soto‐Hernández 2020).

Although this study centers on fertility variation, it is essential to situate reproductive behavior within the broader context of mortality decline. Prior to 1930, Mexico's life expectancy remained below 40 years due to high mortality rates driven by civil conflict and socioeconomic inequality (Cosio‐Zavala 2022; McCaa 2003). By the 1970s, however, life expectancy had risen sharply, while fertility rates peaked at over six children per woman, contributing to a fivefold population increase between 1930 and 1990 (Partida‐Bush 2005). In response, the Mexican government launched national family planning programs in 1974, contributing to a rapid fertility decline—reaching an average of 2.1 children per woman by 2020 (INEGI 2022). Meanwhile, high infant mortality and migration patterns contributed to divergent reproductive strategies across regions (Colomé‐Hidalgo, Campos, and de Miguel 2021; Mesenburg et al. 2018). These contrasts highlight that fertility transitions are shaped not only by modernization and policy but also by localized responses to shifting demographic dynamics.

To analyze these dynamics, this study introduces the TSP model, a multilevel framework that integrates historical trends, spatial distribution, and socioeconomic determinants. While classical DT models typically rely on national‐level aggregates and assume a uniform response to modernization, scholarship has shown that demographic transitions vary significantly at subnational levels, as in Ethiopia, where urban centers like Addis Ababa have reached replacement fertility while rural areas remain far above it (Jung 2023; Moultrie, Sayi, and Timæus 2012). The TSP framework builds on these insights by explicitly capturing localized fertility variations and integrating long‐term macro‐structural change, exo‐, and micro‐contextual dynamics. TSP disaggregates fertility outcomes along three dimensions: time, by comparing changes across three periods (1930–1970—2015); space, by identifying spatial clustering and diffusion effects; and population, by distinguishing subgroups defined by ethnicity, rurality, and socioeconomic status (SES). This structure allows us to evaluate how SES, spatial diffusion, and cultural norms influence net fertility within each subpopulation and time. By disaggregating the analysis, the model reduces confounding effects from overlapping contextual factors. A key innovation is the use of newly available 1930 Mexican census microdata, offering unique detail for analyzing historical fertility patterns.

Our findings show that fertility decline is shaped by SES, spatial concentration, and diffusion dynamics. High‐fertility municipalities are not isolated; they form contiguous clusters where fertility decreases gradually with distance from the core. This concentric pattern suggests that reproductive behaviors are influenced not only by shared structural disadvantages—such as poverty, limited access to services, and geographic isolation—but also by the spatial diffusion of norms. The CV analysis identified urban non‐Indigenous populations as the earliest to initiate reproductive change, displaying significantly lower and less variable fertility compared to other groups. Their earlier transition likely reflects both structural factors—such as access to education, formal employment, and reproductive health services—and rising opportunity costs of childrearing. However, structural shifts alone cannot explain how fertility norms spread beyond these advantaged groups. We argue that prestige bias, or the emulation of high‐status behaviors, amplified this diffusion. Urban lifestyles and smaller family sizes may have signaled social status, encouraging adjacent populations to adopt similar patterns. Meanwhile, rural Indigenous populations exhibited greater conformity bias to local norms, with limited behavioral change unless exposed to nearby urban areas. These spatially patterned dynamics point to the need for targeted interventions that support informed reproductive choices, particularly in structurally and geographically marginalized regions.

Beyond Mexico, the TSP model offers potential for application in other contexts, with uneven fertility transitions, such as those observed in Latin America and Africa. In sub‐Saharan Africa, research has highlighted how extended kinship systems and spatial inequality influence reproductive behavior (Ezeh, Mberu, and Emina 2009; Moultrie, Sayi, and Timæus 2012). The TSP framework provides a comparative lens to analyze these complexities. Finally, we hope this study will facilitate broader access to the 1930 Mexican census data and kinship variables, encouraging future studies on Mexican demographic transitions.

BACKGROUND

Demographic Transition Approaches

The DT framework attributes fertility and mortality declines to a modernization process involving industrialization, urbanization, and economic factors (Notestein 1945). However, its assumption of a linear and homogeneous trajectory—where demographic change unfolds in a predictable, sequential relationship with socioeconomic development—has been challenged by growing empirical evidence, particularly in contexts marked by state interventions, structural inequalities, and cultural resistance to contraception (Pritchett and Summers 1994).

While changing family structures, gender roles, and raising women's education often accompany modernization, these transformations do not guarantee fertility decline or follow a uniform path. Caldwell (2007) highlights the role of family structure and gender norms; Bongaarts (2003) underscores the importance of women's education and culturally appropriate contraceptive access. Cleland and Wilson (1987) and Watkins (1987) emphasize the importance of ideas diffusion, while Upadhyay et al. (2014) point to women's empowerment and reproductive autonomy as central to understanding fertility transitions—particularly where modernization is incomplete. These insights help explain fertility transitions in Latin America, where distinct historical, cultural, and institutional factors have driven demographic change beyond what classical models predict.

At the microlevel of analysis—focused on individuals and households—religion, education, and SES are key determinants of fertility decisions (Coale and Watkins 2017; Easterlin and Crimmins 1985). Coale's ready‐willing‐able framework identifies three conditions for fertility decline: motivation to limit births, cultural acceptance, and access to contraceptive methods (Coale, 1973). Easterlin and Crimmins (1985) extend this by showing how both perceived and actual child‐rearing costs—shaped by survival expectations and contraceptive availability—influence fertility preferences. These models reveal how reproductive behavior emerges from the interplay of economic, social, and biological dimensions.

Contextualizing Demographic Transitions: Latin America and Mexico

Traditional approaches to demographic transitions—rooted mainly in Western experiences—highlight the limits of applying a universal theory. This is particularly evident in Latin America, where transitions are characterized by heterogeneity, shaped by colonial legacies, socioeconomic inequality, and cultural diversity (Bongaarts 2009; Leridon 2015; Cosio‐Zavala 2023).

In Mexico, fertility patterns vary significantly by ethnic groups, geography, and SES (Cosio‐Zavala 2012; Gallup, Gaviria, and Lora 2003; Rodriguez 1992). Historical disruptions—such as early 20th‐century civil conflicts and poor health conditions—constrained population growth, reducing the national population by nearly 10 percent (McCaa 2003) and keeping life expectancy below 40 years until 1930 (Pérez Brignoli 2010). A major demographic shift occurred in the 1970s, when life expectancy increased and fertility peaked at over six children per woman, driven by population momentum and pro‐growth policies targeting 2.5 percent annually (Partida‐Bush 2005). In response, the Mexican government implemented a national family planning program in 1974, which contributed to a sustained fertility decline—reaching 2.1 children per woman by 2020 (Baca 2007; INEGI 2022).

This transition, however, has been deeply uneven. Structural inequalities—such as limited access to education, health care, and formal employment—have restricted reproductive autonomy among disadvantaged groups (Bongaarts 2003). Rural Indigenous populations, in particular, continue to face delays in fertility decline due to linguistic, cultural, and institutional barriers that hinder the diffusion of contraceptive use and reproductive health norms (Núñez 2022; Rodriguez 1992). The concept of poverty Malthusianism (Cosio‐Zavala and Leridon, 1996) helps explain how marginalized populations often adopted contraception not as a reflection of greater autonomy, but as a strategy for coping with hardship. These dynamics underscore the need for demographic models that account for institutional exclusion and social stratification in shaping fertility transitions.

Conceptual and Methodological Gaps

Recent research emphasizes that demographic transitions are not solely shaped by economic development but by the interplay of structural, spatial, and cultural forces (Colleran 2016; Dribe and Scalone 2014; Sear and Burger 2019; Szreter 1996). Historical and comparative studies reinforce this view by showing that fertility control—through stopping and spacing births—existed even in pre‐transitional societies, where reproductive behavior responded to child mortality, geographic factors, resource scarcity, and entrenched social norms (Anderton and Bean 1985; Cinnirella, Klemp, and Weisdorf 2017; Kolk 2011; Rindfuss et al. 2004; Van Bavel 2003). These findings align with evolutionary demography, which interprets fertility as a context‐dependent response shaped by both macro‐level constraints and microlevel household dynamics (Kaplan and Lancaster 2003). This perspective reframes fertility decline as a continuum of adaptive behaviors rather than a binary shift.

The diffusion of fertility behavior is a recognized driver of demographic change, but its influence varies depending on language, identity, and resource access (Skinner, Henderson, and Jianhua 2000). Strong diffusion effects, like those found in Prussia (Goldstein and Klüsener 2014), contrast with weaker patterns in Mexico due to cultural barriers among ethnic minorities (Núñez 2022). These differences highlight the need for multilevel analyses that distinguish between structural constraints and the spread of ideas, particularly in Latin America, where longitudinal evidence remains limited.

Midlevel institutional and community factors—what Bronfenbrenner (1977, 1986) termed the “exosystem”—remain underexplored in demographic models, despite their central role in shaping fertility behaviors (Ajayi et al. 2021; Sekine et al. 2021). These include the accessibility of health and education services (Dribe and Scalone 2014), infrastructure (Bongaarts 2003), and the institutional strength governing reproductive programs and social norms (Rindfuss et al. 2004; Szreter 1996). In our framework, exosystem‐level variables include the prevalence of Indigenous languages and the urban—rural divide. These contextual factors mediate the effects of macro‐structural conditions and influence how individuals engage with fertility regulation across geographic and cultural settings.

The Time, Space, and Population Model

To overcome the limitations of conventional models that treat structural, cultural, and spatial factors in isolation, we apply the TSP framework. Rather than proposing a new theory, it serves as a heuristic tool to analyze fertility transitions by integrating multiple demographic perspectives. Here, we use it to identify sources of fertility variation and assess how SES relates to net fertility across those dimensions.

Drawing in Bronfenbrenner's (1977) ecological system theory and historical‐demographic perspective that highlight the role of contextual and diffusion determinants in shaping fertility change as Rindfuss et al. (2004) and Szreter (1996) did in their analysis, the TSP incorporates evolutionary demographic insights that view fertility as an adaptive response to ecological and social conditions as Kaplan and Lancaster (2003) suggest. We use the TSP framework to interpret heterogeneous fertility transitions, focusing on how modernization unfolds unevenly across time, space, and subpopulations.

Rather than claiming to estimate fully independent causal effects for each factor, the TSP emphasizes their joint operationalization, allowing the detection of statistically significant patterns within and across levels. Ethnogeographic subpopulations are treated as key sources of variation, and the model facilitates comparisons among them to assess which groups experienced earlier, delayed, or divergent fertility decline trajectories.

The TSP is built around three core dimensions:

Time (T): Captures long‐term structural shifts—such as economic reforms, public health expansions, and historical disruptions—that shape fertility behavior across three key periods (1930, 1970, 2015). This approach draws from historical demographic research that links fertility change to macro‐structural events and policy shifts (Bertram 1963; Bloom, Canning, and Sevilla 2001; Bongaarts 2015).

METHODOLOGY

Data Sources and Historical Coverage

This study analyzes harmonized microdata from the 1930 Population Census, the 1970 Census, and the 2015 Intercensal Survey—three critical points capturing Mexico's demographic transition from pre‐transitional to ongoing‐transitional stages. Our analytical population includes married or in‐union women aged 15–49.

The 1930 dataset is a 10 percent representative sample of census manuscripts recovered through a research project financed by the National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI) and led by the Department of Statistics of the University of Chapingo (Zamudio et al. 2015). As the first governmental initiative to reconstruct historical census records, it offers the only national data available to study Mexico's early demographic structure. Despite limited resources for digitization, the sample provides sufficient demographic and socioeconomic detail to support historical analysis. However, while enumerators recorded key individual and household characteristics, they did not document kinship ties among household members.

To address this limitation, we employed text‐mining techniques that infer intra‐household relationships from available attributes—surnames, age patterns, marital status, and household roles— to estimate the number of children per woman. Online Appendix B provides full details on these procedures, including data coverage and validation strategies. Although 24 percent of the original manuscripts are missing, the remaining data were deemed sufficient to generate reliable demographic insights. We imputed anomalous or missing age entries and cross‐validated reconstructed family structures against 1930 aggregate data published by INEGI. These measures enhance the dataset's internal consistency and support its validity for historical demographic research.

To capture subsequent phases of the Mexican demographic transition, we incorporate microdata from the 1970 Population Census (1 percent sample) and the 2015 Intercensal Survey (9.5 percent sample), both obtained from IPUMS‐International (Ruggles et al. 2018). Unlike the 1930 dataset, these more recent samples include precoded household relationship variables, facilitating direct estimation of net fertility. Both datasets are statistically robust and contain the required information. The final analytic sample comprises 208,813 married/in‐union women aged 15–49 in 1930, 67,176 in 1970, and 21,806,272 in 2015.

Variables and Operational Definitions

Fertility Measure

Net fertility is calculated for each woman in union aged 15–49 as the number of her own surviving children under age five residing in the household. This indirect measure incorporates infant and child mortality, addressing the lack of total fertility data in the analyzed censuses. While less common in contemporary analysis, it remains a valuable indicator in historical demography. Studies by Dribe and Scalone (2014), Scalone and Dribe (2017), and Hacker et al. (2021) highlight its relevance for tracing fertility transitions, particularly by capturing the influence of child survival on reproductive outcomes. Nonetheless, net fertility may underestimate true fertility in contexts of high child mortality, especially among minority populations (Dribe, Hacker, and Scalone 2014). To address this limitation, we incorporate municipal‐level mortality indicators to contextualize group and period differences in our interpretations.

Originally designed for the 1930 Microdata Recovery Project (Zamudio et al. 2015) at the request of the INEGI, the index was validated using 2010 census data (Zamudio et al. 2018) and adapted for cross‐sectional comparisons in this study.

Indigenous and Rural Classifications

Indigenous status is shaped by a combination of language, territory, worldview, and self‐identification (Corntassel 2003). Although official classification has historically emphasized language, this criterion alone does not fully capture the broader cultural and environmental dimensions of Indigenous identity (INALI 2012). Recent studies highlight the complexity of ethnic boundaries. For instance, Flores, Loría, and Casas (2023) documented both transitory and sustained boundary crossing, while Villarreal (2014) showed that relying solely on language can lead to the underrepresentation of self‐identified individuals.

Given the absence of self‐identification data prior to 2010, we rely on language‐based household indicators, which provide a more accurate cultural setting than focusing solely on the woman in union. This approach also captures the influence of co‐residing relatives—such as grandparents or spouses—on reproductive behavior. We adopted a household‐level Indigenous criterion as follows: A household is classified as Indigenous if at least half of its members aged five or older speak an Indigenous language. At the municipal level, we define a municipality as Indigenous majority when at least 50 percent of its households meet this condition. This method aligns with prior demographic research while acknowledging the dynamic nature of Indigenous identity over time.

Rurality is also defined at the municipal level. Municipalities are classified as rural if more than 50 percent of the population resides in localities with fewer than 2500 inhabitants as INAFED (2024) does for localities in Mexico. These two classifications—Indigenous status and rurality—form the basis of the ethnogeographic subpopulations analyzed in this study.

To ensure temporal consistency despite administrative boundary changes (e.g., municipal mergers or redefinitions), we assign ethnogeographic status based on each municipality's classification within the corresponding census year. This dynamic classification reflects shifts of Indigenous households, adapting to cultural and demographic shifts. It allows for consistent cross‐sectional dynamic analysis across four key subpopulations: rural Indigenous, urban Indigenous, rural non‐Indigenous, and urban non‐Indigenous.

Fertility Variability Across Groups

To assess internal fertility variation within ethnogeographic populations, we applied a bootstrapping subsampling approach, generating 100 random subsamples per municipality within each group. The coefficient of variation (CVg=σg2/μg) was calculated to capture net fertility dispersion among women in group g (defined by ethnogeographic status). Rather than tracking temporal change, this method enables cross‐sectional comparisons of within‐group variability across census years. According to Hruschka and Burger (2016), higher CVs may indicate the coexistence of traditional and emerging reproductive behaviors, highlighting which subpopulations were more likely to drive fertility change. We also compute 95 percent confidence intervals to test whether CVs differences across groups are statistically significant (Wasserman, 2013). This approach helps reveal the diversity of fertility norms within subpopulations and may indicate early diffusion of new reproductive strategies (Kramer 2019).

Spatial Analysis of Net Fertility

To evaluate spatial patterns in fertility and identify localized clustering, we applied a Bayesian hierarchical Poisson model using the Besag‐York‐Mollié (BYM) specification, implemented via the integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA). This model estimates the relative risks (θm) of having children under five years in each municipality (m), based on the number of children under five (Ym) among women aged 15–49 (Equations 5 and 6). This approach provides precise small‐area estimates by integrating spatial dependencies and independent variables, improving accuracy in capturing localized fertility trends (Khana et al. 2018).

Independent variables include the proportion of the population in the lowest SES category (InSoc ≤ 0.37) and the ethnogeographic classification of municipalities. RESULTS

Structural Inequalities and Socioeconomic Evolution

Descriptive data show how urbanization, ethnicity, and SES have shaped fertility patterns over time. In 1930, 78.3 percent of municipalities were rural and 22.5 percent predominantly Indigenous; by 2015, these figures had declined to 41.1 percent and 14.3 percent, respectively (Table 1). During this period, SES (measured by the InSoc index) improved across all groups: urban areas rose from 0.31 to 0.64 (2.1×), rural areas from 0.24 to 0.52 (2.2×), and Indigenous municipalities from 0.21 to 0.49 (2.3×). The InSoc is a scaled variable within each census year; therefore, a consistent increase in group‐specific means supports meaningful temporal comparisons. Notably, Indigenous municipalities remained socioeconomically disadvantaged compared to non‐Indigenous ones—22 percent lower in 1930 (0.21 vs. 0.27) and 19 percent lower in 2015 (0.49 vs. 0.61).1

Fertility Trends and Structural Constraints

Fertility patterns also reflect socioeconomic disparities between rural and urban settings. In all periods analyzed, rural municipalities maintained higher net fertility than urban areas, a pattern shaped by SES inequalities and historical disadvantages. Similar findings were reported by Leyva‐Flores et al. (2013). In Table 1, we show that fertility patterns remained strongly linked to structural inequality. Net fertility was 0.87 in 1930, peaked at 1.12 in 1970 (+29 percent), then declined to 0.50 in 2015 (−55 percent). This trajectory was not uniform. In 1930, non‐Indigenous municipalities had a 13 percent higher fertility rate than Indigenous ones (0.89 vs. 0.79); the gap widened to 17 percent by 1970 (1.13 vs. 0.97). However, by 2015, the trend reversed: Indigenous municipalities exhibited 36 percent higher fertility (0.64) than non‐Indigenous ones (0.47).

These trends reflect changes in access to contraception and infant mortality. In 1930 and 1970, limited contraceptive use (Pullum, Casterline, and Juarez 1985) and high child mortality in rural and Indigenous communities (Arriaga 1967; Malina, Peña Reyes, and Little 2008) contributed to higher fertility. By 2015, fertility had declined due to expanded access to family planning and improved suvival outcomes (Duarte‐Gómez et al. 2015), though these advances were unevenly distributed.

Fertility Differentials by Ethnogeographic Group

To analyze how ethnicity, rurality, and SES intersect in shaping fertility transitions, we compared net‐fertility trends across ethnogeographic groups over time (Table 3). In 1930, Indigenous municipalities had both the lowest SES (InSoc: 0.21–0.23) and net fertility (≈0.77). Urban non‐Indigenous municipalities, despite having the highest SES (0.32), registered higher net fertility (0.81), while rural non‐Indigenous municipalities reached 0.91. By 1970, rural Indigenous municipalities remained disadvantaged (SES: 0.35) with moderate net fertility (0.92). In 2015, urban non‐Indigenous women had both high SES (0.64) had low net fertility (0.45), while rural Indigenous women exhibited the opposite (SES: 0.47; net fertility: 0.67). Notably, urban Indigenous women had higher SES than rural non‐Indigenous women but also slightly higher net fertility (0.54 vs. 0.52). These contrasting patterns suggest that fertility transitions unfold along distinct trajectories shaped by the interaction of structural and cultural norms. Rural Indigenous (Delayed)

This group experienced a delayed path of fertility transition. From 1930 to 1970, net fertility increased modestly by 16 percent, while other groups rose by over 29 percent. The population share declined by over 10 percent after 1930. Between 1970 and 2015, despite a 34 percent SES increase, net fertility declined only 27 percent, compared to 46 percent+ in other groups. While this pattern partially aligns with modernization theory, fertility remained high—as mentioned also by Vázquez (2019), despite family planning programs. The mechanisms associated are persistent poverty, high infant mortality (Duarte‐Gómez et al. 2015), agricultural labor needs (Hackman and Kramer 2021), and cultural resistance to external influences in defense of community structures (Torrado 1981; Barroso 2004).

Additionally, CV results (Figure 4) indicate behavioral convergence in 1930 and 1970, with fertility patterns aligned across neighboring rural Indigenous municipalities. By 2015, spatial analysis (Table 6; Figure 3a and 3c) shows reduced fertility risk, suggesting early clustering of low‐fertility regions. Although these patterns suggest spatial convergence, we interpret them through cultural evolutionary theory as conformity dynamics, whereby individuals adopt locally dominant reproductive behaviors (Kaplan and Lancaster 2003). Spatial proximity facilitates this norm diffusion (Sear et al. 2016). In this context, spatial influence and cultural conformity interact with structural constraints, shaping delayed transitions. These dynamics also contributed to the demographic momentum effect (Bongaarts 1994), amplifying growth even as deep‐rooted inequalities limited the transition potential of disadvantaged populations (Vallin, 2005).

Urban Indigenous (Resistant)

Fertility in this group followed a trajectory similar to rural Indigenous populations but with a shorter delay (Figure 2). It increased rapidly by 1970 (+30 percent), likely due to improved sanitation and high nuptiality (Cosio‐Zavala 2022; Partida‐Bush 2005), yet the subsequent decline was limited. Despite notable SES gains by 2015, fertility levels remained significantly higher than those of urban non‐Indigenous populations. This divergence suggests that improvements in SES and living conditions—although aligned with modernization theory—are insufficient to fully explain demographic patterns.

Evidence from disadvantaged settings shows that fertility tends to decline more slowly where traditional gender norms persist and cultural resistance to change is strong (Delaunay, Adjamagbo, and Lalou 2006; Dorvilier 2010). In 1970, spatial analysis revealed that urban Indigenous municipalities had the highest spatial estimator among all subgroups (Table 6), indicating intense diffusion—likely shaped by pronatalist influences. However, in 1930 and 2015, spatial clustering was absent, pointing to a more closed system of behavioral transmission in low‐fertility contexts.

This limited spatial responsiveness may reflect cultural retention and barriers to external influence that extend beyond structural inequalities. Urban Indigenous populations thus illustrate a resistant path of fertility transition, shaped by cultural boundaries and normative continuity rather than economic transformation alone.

Rural Non‐Indigenous (Stagnant)

The fertility trajectory of this group parallels that of the urban non‐Indigenous population but with a delayed response, shaped by more precarious socioeconomic conditions. Fertility decline was slower, constrained by persistent poverty and limited institutional coverage (Cosio‐Zavala 2022; FAO 2018). While the expansion of family planning programs after 1974 contributed to an overall fertility reduction (Brugeilles 2011; De Márquez 1984), levels remained elevated in municipalities dependent on family‐based agricultural production (Hackman and Kramer 2021).

This trend is consistent with the Malthusianism of poverty hypothesis (Cosio‐Zavala and Leridon, 1996), which posits that fertility declines in response to economic adversity. However, in this case, the mechanism proved to counteract the persistent structural constraints associated with low SES, which continued to limit access to reproductive autonomy and delayed the demographic transition. Even as services expanded in recent decades, they failed to fully compensate for enduring inequalities (Mier y Terán and Rabell 1993). Overpopulation and stagnating development fueled surplus labor migration to urban centers and abroad (Bongaarts 1994; Findley and Orr 1980). Spatial analysis (Table 6) indicates inconsistent diffusion of fertility‐limiting behaviors, despite geographic proximity to more modernized regions. CV results (Figure 4) reveal strong behavioral convergence prior to 1970, followed by increasing variance and emerging gradients in fertility practices—especially among higher SES municipalities. These patterns suggest the presence of prestige bias, whereby fertility change is initiated by the better‐off and does not diffuse uniformly across the population, underscoring the selective nature of behavioral transmission under persistent inequality, as we observe too in the spatial analysis, which shows concentric diffusion of fertility behavior.

Urban Non‐Indigenous (Fast)

As shown in Figure 2, net fertility in this group consistently remained below the national average throughout the study period. These municipalities exhibited the highest SES, lowest mortality rates, and earliest access to contraception, also mentioned by Cosio‐Zavala (2022), which enabled a steady and early fertility decline (Table 3). Fertility limitation was evident as early as 1930, supported by high CV and low infant mortality—suggesting heterogeneity in reproductive strategies and the early emergence of fertility control within some households.

In 1970, fertility levels in this group converged with the national average due to the demographic boom. However, by 2015, elevated CV values again signaled growing diversity in reproductive behavior. Spatially, urban non‐Indigenous municipalities acted as key hubs of demographic diffusion, exhibiting strong clustering effects in both 1930 and 2015 (Table 6; Figure 3a and 3c). These municipalities likely served as sources of behavioral innovation, transmitting fertility‐limiting norms outward through prestige‐biased learning, wherein individuals emulate the behaviors of higher status groups (Sear et al. 2016).

Urban elites—typically wealthier and more educated—had early access to family planning information and services. For them, the opportunity cost of childbearing was heightened by participation in formal employment and urban lifestyles, prompting earlier fertility reduction. Over time, these behaviors spread to other social groups, consistent with cultural transmission models of demographic change (Watkins 1987).

Notably, non‐Indigenous urban (−6.9 percent, −0.9 percent, −10 percent) and rural non‐Indigenous (+4.6 percent, +4.5 percent, +4 percent) subpopulations maintained stable relative positions.

In 1930, urban non‐Indigenous populations exhibited fertility rates below the national average, reaching near parity by 1970, while rural non‐Indigenous populations consistently maintained fertility levels above the national average. In contrast, urban Indigenous (−11.5 percent, −10.7 percent, +8 percent) and rural Indigenous (−9.2 percent, −17.0 percent, +34 percent) populations followed divergent demographic trajectories. Between 1970 and 2015, net fertility increased among rural Indigenous groups despite clear SES improvements, while urban Indigenous fertility remained significantly higher than that of their non‐Indigenous urban counterparts—despite comparable geographic contexts and gains in SES. Figure 2 also reveals two main patterns: (1) steady fertility decline among non‐Indigenous groups, with urban areas leading and rural areas lagging due to slower SES gains; and (2) greater variability among Indigenous groups, with urban and rural trajectories converging after 1970. This convergence—despite SES differences—reflects unequal fertility responsiveness to SES. While SES improvements drove significant declines among urban non‐Indigenous populations, effects were weaker for Indigenous groups, particularly in rural areas where fertility even increased. These trends suggest that SES alone cannot explain fertility change. Cultural norms and territorial context remain key to understanding differentiated transitions, consistent with the TSP framework.

In 1930, rural‐Indigenous populations exhibited the highest fertility elasticity to socioeconomic changes (Table 5). A one‐unit increase in InSoc—about a 15 percent rise—was linked to a 28.1 percent fertility reduction (exp[−2.195 × 0.15] = 0.719).2 This suggests a strong latent demand for fertility constrained by structural barriers. Notably, these populations did not start with higher fertility; both rural and urban Indigenous municipalities had slightly lower levels than non‐Indigenous areas. Thus, the high elasticity likely reflects previously unmet needs rather than greater room for decline. By contrast, urban non‐Indigenous groups showed lower responsiveness, with a 15.6 percent reduction (exp[−1.131 × 0.15] = 0.855), calculated as (0.855–1) × 100=15.6.

In 1970, fertility remained inversely linked to SES across all groups, but with slightly lower elasticity. A 15 percent increase in InSoc was associated with fertility declines of 6.3 percent to 12.3 percent, except for rural Indigenous (42 percent). This period coincided with Mexico's economic boom and the expansion of contraceptive availability, which likely contributed to fertility decline even in lower SES areas (Cosio‐Zavala, 2022). By 2015, SES still influenced fertility but with weaker effects, particularly in urban settings. Fertility dropped by 20.7 percent in rural Indigenous and 17.8 percent in rural non‐Indigenous population groups, while urban areas saw similar reductions (∼15 percent; exp [−1.143] ≈ exp [−1.048] ≈ 0.35). These patterns suggest that SES effects are mediated by historical and contextual factors—such as the timing of transition onset, uneven program exposure, and local norms. While not directly measured here, prior studies underscore their importance in subnational fertility variation (Cosio‐Zavala 2022; Mier y Terán and Rabell 1993; Barroso 2004).

Spatial Patterns and Geographic Clustering

While rurality is explicitly territorial, indigeneity—though defined at the microlevel—tends to be spatially clustered. Table 6 summarizes results across all years. Net fertility is consistently associated with both the proportion of the population in the lowest SES category (InSoc ≤ 0.037) and ethnogeographic status, with credibility intervals excluding zero. Municipalities with a high concentration of low‐SES individuals had elevated net fertility, especially in 1930 (β = 2.102) and 1970 (β = 0.168). In both 1930 (rural = 0.122, urban = 0.064) and 1970 (rural = 0.249, urban = 0.28), non‐Indigenous municipalities had higher net fertility than Indigenous rural municipalities. In 1970, urban Indigenous municipalities also showed higher net fertility (0.401) than their rural counterparts, though the 1930 coefficient was not significant. By 2015, SES and ethnogeographic interactions became significant, indicating diverse relationships between socioeconomic position and subpopulations. Unlike in 1930 and 1970, non‐Indigenous municipalities had lower net fertility than Indigenous rural municipalities (rural = −0.082, urban = −0.97).

Figure 3a–3c illustrates municipal‐level net‐fertility risk through color‐scaled maps (available interactively online at 1930, 1970, 2015 3), highlighting persistent spatial clustering. Although the 1930 data are incomplete, patterns are clear: neighboring municipalities often exhibit similar fertility behavior. In 1970, high‐fertility municipalities formed a mosaic, reflecting uneven socioeconomic progress. By 2015, lighter tones dominate—signaling widespread fertility decline—yet reddish clusters persist in rural Indigenous areas, echoing 1930 patterns. These municipalities remain spatially grouped, underscoring the enduring territorial nature of structural disadvantage.

Upon examination of the maps by year, a clear spatial correlation emerges, indicating that geography plays a growing role in fertility variation. As shown in Table 6, the spatial structure explained (Geo = 51.7 percent) of the variance in 1930, 43.6 percent in 1970, and 91.1 percent in 2015, signaling increased territorial clustering of reproductive behavior. High‐fertility zones persist in disadvantaged regions and suggest that structural inequalities are reinforced by geographic context. While proximity to urban and high‐SES municipalities tends to reduce fertility—suggesting diffusion—these effects are inconsistent: they were weak in 1970, but more evident in 1930 and especially in 2015 (with higher fertility), highlighting a shift in spatial dynamics. In contrast, rural municipalities consistently showed lower net fertility across all years.

Fertility Variation and Cultural Transmission Biases

Figure 4 shows the CV in net fertility across ethnogeographic groups and time. In 1930, the rural non‐Indigenous population had the lowest variability (CV = 100.34), suggesting homogeneous high‐fertility patterns typically of natural fertility regimes. By contrast, urban non‐Indigenous municipalities exhibit the highest variation (CV ≈ 110), pointing to early fertility limitation among some families, likely driven by access to education, economic opportunities, and contraception (Cosio‐Zavala 2012). These patterns are consistent with evidence of fertility control through stopping or spacing, even in pre‐transitional contexts (Cinnirella, Klemp, and Weisdorf 2017; Kolk 2011). Limited access to resources and high infant mortality may explain the lowest fertility variability among Indigenous populations (Malina, Peña Reyes, and Little 2008).

In 1970, variation declined across all groups (CV < 110), indicating temporary behavioral convergence amid demographic pressure and uneven family planning expansion (Cosio‐Zavala 2022; De Márquez 1984). Rural non‐Indigenous municipalities remained the most homogeneous, with high fertility and low internal variance—suggesting slow diffusion of contraceptive practices.

By 2015, all CVs increased (>117), signaling diversification in reproductive strategies. Urban non‐Indigenous groups led this shift (CV < 142.8), reflecting widespread adoption of fertility‐limiting norms and prestige‐biased transmission, also validated with spatial analysis (concentric diffusion of reproductive behavior). Rural non‐Indigenous and Indigenous groups followed with a lag, showing lower CVs, likely due to persistent barriers in access to services and cultural norms. The expanded family planning programs post‐1978 likely contributed to these differentiated transitions.

DISCUSSION

Mexico's demographic transition—often described as “rapid and late” (Cosio‐Zavala 2014)—was shaped by a structural inequality and national policy shifts throughout the latter two‐thirds of the twentieth century. Rather than a uniform process, the transition unfolded unevenly across ethnogeographic groups. Policies such as pronatalism orientation before 1970 and the subsequent expansion of the family planning programs (Brugeilles 2011) had measurable effects, but their impact was far from equal. In rural and Indigenous regions, these policies were delayed or weakened by infrastructure deficits and cultural barriers, contributing to persistent high fertility even as national decline (Mier y Terán 1982; Páez and Zavala‐Cosío 2017).

Integrative Framework

This study examined how fertility transitions varied across Mexico's diverse’ contexts, focusing on whether cultural transmission mechanisms—specifically prestige and conformity biases—contributed to these differentiated trajectories. Drawing on evolutionary theory (Colleran 2016; Sear 2015), we tested whether reproductive behaviors diffused from higher status groups and how structural and spatial inequalities shaped the pace and direction of such diffusion.

Our results support the operation of both mechanisms, though with different intensities and timing across groups. In 1930, rural non‐Indigenous municipalities showed low internal fertility variation (low CV) and strong spatial clustering (Figure 4), suggesting conformity to high‐fertility norms. This likely stemmed from both cultural expectations and shared constraints—such as subsistence economies and limited access to alternatives—that suppressed divergence in reproductive strategies. This is consistent with the Malthusianism of poverty hypothesis, fertility declined reactively, in response to deteriorating economic conditions, rather than proactively through contraceptive use (Cosio‐Zavala and Leridon 1996).

In 2015, urban non‐Indigenous populations began to exhibit higher variation in net fertility (high CV), signaling the emergence of prestige bias. Here, smaller family norms likely spread from wealthier households—who had earlier access to contraception and faced higher opportunity costs of childbearing—to other groups. These shifts reflect not only access to services but also the socially patterned diffusion of fertility‐limiting behaviors. Among Indigenous populations (low CVs), persistent inequalities and cultural autonomy slowed the adoption of state‐promoted family planning (Barroso 2004; Mier y Terán and Rabell 1993), reinforcing group‐specific trajectories.

Thus, while Mexico's fertility transition was partly driven by national policy, its differentiated pace and pattern reflect a complex interplay between structural inequality, economic hardship, and cultural transmission dynamics. These findings underscore the importance of integrating both socioeconomic and cultural determinants in understanding demographic change across heterogeneous populations.

Divergent Fertility Trajectories and Uneven Responses

Our results reveal that fertility declines in Mexico follow at least four distinct ethnogeographic pathways. Urban non‐Indigenous population transitioned earliest, likely driven by early access to education, formal employment, and reproductive health services. By 2015, this population reflects growing internal heterogeneity in reproductive strategies typical of a post‐transitional context. In contrast, rural Indigenous groups not only transitioned more slowly but even experienced a temporary fertility increase between 1970 and 2015—pointing to persistent structural constraints and cultural continuity (Torrado 1981).

Fertility elasticity analysis further supports these differentiated pathways. Rural Indigenous and rural non‐Indigenous groups showed the strongest demographic response to improvements in SES, suggesting that delayed fertility transitions were less about cultural resistance and more about structural exclusion. However, SES improvements alone did not fully equalize outcomes. By 2015, despite measurable SES gains, spatial clustering of high fertility persisted in marginalized Indigenous regions—highlighting the influence of local demographic environments, or what the TSP model conceptualizes as the exosystem.

Our spatial modeling (BYM‐INLA) reinforces this interpretation. High‐fertility zones remained stable over time, particularly in Indigenous and rural areas. As Núñez (2022) argues, explaining spatial fertility patterns requires more than SES indicators—it requires contextual knowledge. Our findings suggest that diffusion processes are shaped by structural continuities such a persistent infrastructural deficit (Gallup, Gaviria, and Lora 2003), historically limited access to reproductive health services (Costa, et al., 2022; Mesenburg et al., 2018), and entrenched patterns of ethnic exclusions (Sanchez‐Rivera 2023; Stern 2011). Together, these dynamics have produced spatially entrenched demographic regimes in which fertility norms resist change, despite national‐level policy interventions.

The analysis of fertility variation (CV) adds further nuance. Before 1970, both rural Indigenous and rural non‐Indigenous groups exhibited, consistent with conformity bias and limited behavioral diversification. In contrast, urban non‐Indigenous populations appear to have initiated early fertility control strategies—including contraceptive use—even before the national transition began, echoing findings from pre‐transitional contexts (Cinnirella, Klemp, and Weisdorf 2017; Kolk 2011). These groups not only led to fertility decline but also served as behavioral reference points during the first and third phases of the transitions. After 1970, their growing internal variation reflects the spread of prestige bias, as fertility‐limiting behaviors diffused from higher status urban households to other groups (Sear et al. 2016). Yet, adoption was not universal: some affluent families maintained high fertility intentionally, viewing large families as a symbol of status and reproductive capacity—an effect noted in other stratified societies (Greenhalgh 1990; Stulp and Barrett 2016).

Meanwhile, Indigenous areas retained lower internal fertility variation, in part due to persistent high infant mortality (Malina, Peña Reyes, and Little 2008), which reinforced compensatory reproductive behaviors and sowed the diffusion of new fertility norms. These communities remained relatively insulated from national demographic shifts, with conformity dynamics and structural disadvantage reinforcing existing patterns.

In sum, Mexico's fertility transition was not only delayed—it was fragmented, uneven, and shaped by more than modernization alone. Fertility change reflected the interaction of national policy, spatial inequalities, economic constraints, and culturally embedded norms. Understanding these differentiated transitions requires attention not just to individual characteristics, but also to the social and spatial systems in which reproductive decisions unfold.

CONCLUSION

Although national fertility levels declined over time, the pace and pattern of this decline varied markedly across ethnogeographic groups, underscoring the need for contextualized interpretations of demographic change.

The TSP model proved useful in disentangling these complex dynamics. By integrating historical depth, spatial variability, and socioeconomic differentiation, it offers a flexible and heuristic framework for analyzing delayed or fragmented demographic transitions—particularly relevant in Latin America and other regions such as Sub‐Saharan Africa. Beyond theoretical contributions, this study also advanced methodological innovation by combining advanced statistical methods with historical census microdata and reconstructing kinship ties from the 1930 Mexican census, enabling new insights into long‐term demographic change and social inequality in Mexico.

Finally, our findings call for policy approaches that are attuned to territorial and cultural heterogeneity. Reducing fertility inequality requires more than universal intervention—it demands locally grounded strategies that acknowledge how reproductive behaviors evolve through interaction with economic constraints, spatial exclusion, and deeply cultural norms. Future research should further explore how kinship structures, migration dynamics, and policy developments continue to shape fertility trajectories in regions where demographic transitions remain incomplete. Addressing persistent inequalities in access to education, health care, and contraception remains essential to fostering more equitable demographic outcomes.
