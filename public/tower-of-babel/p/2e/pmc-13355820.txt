An AI-powered diagnostic system for grading and invasion of non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer via TURBT specimens: A multicenter study
Authors: Xiaoxuan Zhang, Hongyi Wang, Hui Zhou, Dan Xiao, Haoxiang Xiong, Jimin Cheng, Yingying He, Qianjin Feng, Jie Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEarly pathological examination for grading and invasion of non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) via transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) specimens is a labor-intensive, subjective, and experience-dependent task, while the poor quality of TURBT specimens and the high heterogeneity of NMIBC tumors further limit diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. This study proposed a dual-channel multi-instance learning (DCMIL) model to simultaneously integrate the grading and invasion of NMIBC, while efficiently locating minor changes in the morphology and distribution of NMIBC cells in parallel. Developed on a multicenter dataset of 1332 whole slide images (WSIs) from TURBT specimens, DCMIL demonstrated outstanding accuracy and robust performance with areas under the curve of 0.851–0.983. In the reader study, DCMIL-assisted interpretation improved the diagnostic accuracy by an average of 13.31%–18.58% for inexperienced pathologists. Overall, DCMIL holds promise as a reliable initial assessment tool of NMIBC via TURBT specimens to support clinical decision-making.

Introduction

Non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) is the most common urothelial malignancy, accounting for approximately 75% of all bladder cancer (BCa) cases.1,2,3 NMIBC encompasses superficial tumors confined to the urothelium or lamina propria of the bladder without muscularis propria invasion, exhibiting significant heterogeneity and multifocality.4 The molecular pathological characteristics of NMIBC correlate with prognosis. Patients with low-grade or non-invasive have favorable survival outcomes, whereas those with high-grade or superficial-invasive carry a high risk of recurrence or progression to MIBC, typically requiring further treatment and closer long-term follow-up.5,6 Notably, superficial invasion refers to tumors that invade the submucosa or lamina propria without damaging muscularis propria (i.e., pT1 bladder tumors).7,8 Therefore, early and accurate integration of histological grading and superficial invasion is crucial for improving patient outcomes and optimizing clinical supervision in NMIBC.

Currently, transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) serves as the initial treatment strategy and diagnostic gold standard for NMIBC, directly determining subsequent treatment options.9,10 However, TURBT specimens typically exhibit poor quality, fragmentation, sparse, or absence of a complete histological structure of the urothelium, lamina propria, and muscularis propria.11,12,13 The primary challenge for manual interpretation is to simultaneously identify minor changes in the morphology and distribution of NMIBC cells within low-quality TURBT specimens. The complex tissue composition of NMIBC tumors poses further challenges to ensuring accurate diagnosis.14,15,16 Previous studies have demonstrated that the interpretation of TURBT specimens is highly subjective and experience-dependent, with significant intra- and inter-observer variations.17,18 Some rare high-risk cases of NMIBC present with low-grade superficial-invasive, which are misdiagnosed as low-grade non-invasive by inexperienced pathologists, leading to delayed treatment. While certain non-malignant urothelial diseases, such as urothelial proliferation (UP) and papillary urothelial neoplasm of low malignant potential (PUNLMP), are more likely to be misidentified as NMIBC, resulting in unnecessary treatment. Thus, a reliable automated analysis system for TURBT specimens is essential to reduce workload, improve accuracy, and ensure repeatability in the early diagnosis process of NMIBC.

Advancements in computer technology and whole-slide image (WSI) have facilitated the extensive application of artificial intelligence (AI) in the automated pathological diagnosis of various tumors.19,20 Numerous AI-based diagnostic systems have been developed, achieving performance comparable to or superior to pathologists.21,22,23 Notably, AI models also exhibited exceptional performance in WSIs of biopsy specimens, indicating broad application potential in processing low-quality specimens.24,25 Although these efforts have yielded useful insights and tools, they typically focus on the challenges associated with a specific pathological change.26,27,28 Given the inability to capture unique characteristics of heterogeneous tumors (e.g., the coexistence of inertia [low-grade] and aggressiveness [invasion]), existing methods cannot perform a comprehensive pathological assessment and explanation of NMIBC. Furthermore, the clinical utility of AI models in NMIBC pathological diagnosis requires further evaluation. An AI-based diagnostic system utilizing multicenter data, simultaneously analyzing and interpreting histological grading and superficial invasion to achieve early and precise diagnosis of NMIBC via TURBT specimens, holds significant clinical importance.

In this study, we developed and validated a dual-channel multi-instance learning model (DCMIL) to simultaneously classify the histological grading and superficial invasion of NMIBC in hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained WSIs of TURBT specimens. Through parallel analysis of NMIBC cell morphology and distribution followed by dual-channel output arbitration strategies, DCMIL achieved efficient and precise pathological diagnosis of NMIBC across multicenter datasets. Visualization of key areas within the model further located minor changes in the morphology and distribution of NMIBC cells, thereby verifying the diagnostic reliability. Additionally, human-model comparison results demonstrated DCMIL’s capability to aid clinical decision-making. The proposed DCMIL system provides a novel and practical tool for automated early diagnosis of NMIBC via TURBT specimens, with the potential to assist pathologists in clinical practice and improve diagnostic efficiency, accuracy, and consistency.

Results

Overview

Patient characteristics

This study retrospectively collected a total of 1366 patients with pathologically diagnosed NMIBC or non-malignant urothelial diseases through TURBT from two centers, namely Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University (ZJ-SMU) and Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (TAH-SMU). After applying the exclusion criteria, 1194 patients with complete clinicopathological data from the ZJ-SMU cohort were included. These cases were randomly divided into three subsets: an internal training set (n = 812) for model development, an internal test set (n = 347) for the evaluation of overall diagnostic performance, and an internal reader set (n = 35) for the evaluation of assistance performance in diagnostic decision-making. Additionally, 138 patients from the TAH-SMU cohort with the same required data were served as an independent external test set. The patient selection process is shown in Figure S1. Diagnostic performance of dual-channel models

The performance of the dual-channel classifier in DCMIL was evaluated across three datasets (Table S1). In the internal test set, DCMIL achieved an overall area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.950, 0.881, 0.843, 0.929, 0.850, and 0.931 for histological grading (Figures 2B and 2H), as well as 0.920, 0.875, 0.836, 0.918, 0.836, and 0.917 for superficial invasion (Figures 2E and 2K). In the external test set, DCMIL exhibited similar performance with an AUROC, F1-score, sensitivity, and specificity of 0.909, 0.806, 0.750, and 0.883 for histological grading (Figures 2C and 2I), and 0.851, 0.795, 0.733, and 0.869 for superficial invasion (Figures 2F and 2L). The consistent results across multicenter datasets demonstrated the strong generalizability of DCMIL in most cases. Diagnostic performance improvement with dual-channel output arbitration

To reduce the occurrence of some abnormal results and further enhance overall model efficacy, a dual-channel output arbitration strategy was proposed to integrate the predictions from the dual-channel classifier. The experimental results indicated that the dual-channel output arbitration strategy enhanced the overall performance for histological grading classification, achieving an F1-score, sensitivity, and specificity of 0.913, 0.884, and 0.947 in the internal test set, as well as 0.842, 0.794, and 0.902 in the external test set, respectively. Meanwhile, the F1-score, sensitivity, and specificity of superficial invasion classification increased to 0.895, 0.862, and 0.931 in the internal test set, and 0.821, 0.768, and 0.889 in the external test set, respectively (Figures 2A–2F and Table 2). After output arbitration, 42 histological grading prediction errors and 50 superficial invasion prediction errors were successfully corrected; 6 cases remained uncorrected, while 27 cases resulted in incorrect modifications. Overall, the dual-channel output arbitration strategy improved model performance and exceeded baseline results, highlighting the efficacy of dual-channel complementary information in enhancing diagnostic accuracy. Model performance interpretation via dual-channel attention heatmap

To analyze the internal mechanisms affecting DCMIL predictions, the attention heatmaps were generated for the two channels, respectively. Figure 4 illustrates examples of DCMIL predictions for four key subtypes of NMIBC: non-malignant urothelial disease (i.e., UP), low-grade non-invasive, low-grade superficial-invasive, and high-grade non-invasive. The first row presents the heatmap for histological grading classification, and the second row corresponds to superficial invasion classification. Red areas indicated regions strongly correlated with model predictions, whereas blue areas denoted regions with weak correlation. As shown in Figure 4, the regions emphasized by the model indicated the specific morphological features and location distribution of NMIBC tumor cells, which were linked to the key areas identified in manual diagnoses by pathologists. Thus, the information used by DCMIL for diagnostic predictions is reasonable. In addition, the causes of DCMIL prediction errors were analyzed through dual-channel attention heatmaps (Figure S3). In certain WSIs (Figures S3A and S3B), the initial labels annotated by pathologists were found to be incorrect due to overlooked microscopic abnormalities, whereas the DCMIL provided accurate predictions. Figures S3C and S3D demonstrate that DCMIL exhibited limitations in recognizing some atypical lesions. Visual analysis demonstrated that dual-channel attention heatmaps assist pathologists in effectively concentrating on critical target areas, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy and reliability. Comparison of DCMIL with single-channel models

The model based on a dual-channel classifier and the entire DCMIL model were compared with the models that used only a single-channel classifier. We utilized the same extracted features, backbone, and classifier architecture as the baseline, and employed the same training strategy to construct single-channel models for histological grading and superficial invasion prediction, respectively (Table S2). The dual-channel model achieved superior performance compared with single-channel models across three datasets. The AUROC improvements ranged from 3.01%–9.82% for histological grading and from 2.33%–18.72% for superficial invasion. In addition, DCMIL demonstrated outstanding performance in all cases compared to the single-channel and dual-channel models, with a higher F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV (Figures 2A–2F).

Comparison of diagnostic performance between pathologists and DCMIL

To compare the diagnostic performance of DCMIL with that of pathologists in predicting histological grading and superficial invasion of NMIBC, a reader study was conducted using an additional dataset (i.e., internal reader set). Pathologists examined WSIs and then determined histological grading and superficial invasion based on morphological characteristics and spatial distribution of NMIBC cells. Notably, the diagnostic capabilities of pathologists improved with accumulated experience. As shown in Table 3, the overall AUROC for histological grading diagnoses ranged from 0.714 to 0.748 for junior pathologists, 0.791 to 0.850 for intermediate pathologists, and 0.975 to 1.000 for senior pathologists. By contrast, the overall AUROC of superficial invasion diagnosis ranged from 0.628 to 0.703, 0.768 to 0.827, and 0.936 to 0.953 for junior, intermediate, and senior pathologists, respectively. Compared with all pathologists, DCMIL achieved overall AUROCs of 0.969 and 0.946, surpassing the performance of intermediate pathologists and nearing that of senior pathologists. Performance improvement of AI-assisted diagnosis

To evaluate the clinical relevance of DCMIL, a dual-channel, AI-assisted methodology was proposed. When junior and intermediate pathologists were uncertain about the histological grading or superficial invasion of NMIBC during initial assessment, model predictions and corresponding channel heatmaps were provided to assist in the final diagnosis. Figure 6 shows the specific interpretations by junior and intermediate pathologists prior to and following AI assistance. With the aid of the prediction results and heatmaps, the overall AUROCs for the histological grading diagnoses of the four junior pathologists and four intermediate pathologists improved to 0.875, 0.873, 0.839, 0.881, 0.893, 0.916, 0.964, and 0.941. Discussion

In this retrospective, multicenter study, an effective deep learning model, DCMIL, was developed and validated for the efficient pathological diagnosis of NMIBC based on H&E-stained WSIs obtained from TURBT specimens according to the latest WHO classification. DCMIL utilized a dual-channel architecture to simultaneously assess the morphological heterogeneity and location distribution of NMIBC cells and demonstrated outstanding and robust performance in grading and invasion classification across internal and external datasets in most cases. Furthermore, interpretability analysis and diagnostic assistance have demonstrated the reliability and clinical utility of DCMIL. To the best of our knowledge, this work is the first AI study designed for the histological grading and superficial invasion diagnosis of NMIBC via TURBT specimens based on a large-scale, multicenter dataset, providing a novel tool for the standardized and precise pathological diagnosis of NMIBC.

Although AI-based automated histopathological analysis systems have shown potential across various tumor types,30,31 research on the pathological assessment of NMIBC has been limited. Previous research on NMIBC has revealed a strong trend to focus only on predicting grading, staging, or the degree of muscle layer invasion.32,33,34 Conversely, few studies have examined the comprehensive diagnosis of NMIBC, particularly the critical differentiation between non-malignant disease and malignant carcinoma. Jansen I et al.35 developed a fully automated deep learning network for the detection and grading of high-grade and low-grade NMIBC. Yin P-N et al.36 used a range of machine learning approaches to distinguish between non-invasive and superficially invasive NMIBC. Pan J et al.37 proposed a pathology AI diagnostic model (PAIDM) for dividing BCa into high-grade muscle invasion, high-grade non-muscle invasion, and low-grade non-muscle invasion at the patch and WSI levels. Shao W et al.38 designed a computational pipeline specifically for identifying a rare benign urothelial neoplasm (i.e., inverted urothelial papilloma) of BCa. However, the sole assessment of histological grading or superficial invasion could not comprehensively characterize the tumor heterogeneity of NMIBC, and existing studies often relied on a limited or single-center dataset, which constrained their clinical translation and application.

Compared with previous models, the DCMIL model employed a novel dual-channel architecture to establish independent analysis pathways for histological grading and superficial invasion. This framework can individually capture the unique pathological features associated with each diagnostic task, thereby effectively reducing interdependence between grading and invasive assessment. It specifically addressed the diagnostic challenges caused by the tumor heterogeneity of NMIBC. Additionally, a large, multicenter dataset, which covered non-malignant urothelial diseases and all histological subtypes of NMIBC, ensures the robustness of DCMIL, guaranteeing the reliability and clinical utility of NMIBC predictions. The experimental results showed that, compared to the baseline models based on single-channel classifiers, the dual-channel model improved the predictive performance of histological grading and superficial invasion to varying degrees across three datasets. Specifically, the dual-channel model demonstrated superior performance in identifying high-grade with AUROCs of 0.990, 0.969, and 0.923 for the internal training set, internal test set, and external test set, respectively. However, the performance in diagnosing low-grade was somewhat diminished, with AUROCs of 0.969, 0.918 and 0.863, where low-grade NMIBC exhibited pathological morphology similar to PUNLMP/UP and lacked distinct molecular characteristics. Misclassification of low-grade as non-malignant diseases accounted for the highest proportion of all histological grading errors, reaching 28.38% across the three datasets. It was followed by misclassifying low-grade as high-grade (18.24%) and non-malignant diseases as low-grade (17.57%). Meanwhile, the model performed well in identifying superficial invasion, achieving AUROCs of 0.977, 0.924, and 0.883. The performance of diagnosing non-invasive was slightly lower with AUROCs of 0.945, 0.859, and 0.818, due to misdiagnosis as a superficial-invasive (e.g., the nested tumor cells present in inverted growth patterns or the nest-like tumor clusters located precisely in the central portion of the nipple core). More than half of the errors in predicting superficial invasion involved identifying superficial-invasive or non-malignant diseases as non-invasive, accounting for 29.28% and 25.41%, respectively. Furthermore, the dual-channel model demonstrated outstanding performance in detecting non-malignant diseases, which is crucial for the initial assessment. Although DCMIL extracted distinct features for the histological grading and superficial invasion of NMIBC through dual-channel branching, it was unable to avoid abnormal errors: The histological grading classifier predicted a low-grade carcinoma, whereas the superficial invasion classifier predicted a non-malignant disease. Thus, a dual-channel output arbitration strategy was additionally introduced to merge the prediction results, achieving complementary channel information and correcting inconsistent outputs. Utilizing the dual-channel output arbitration strategy, DCMIL improved the accuracy of histological grading predictions by 1.40%, 3.63%, and 4.47% across three datasets, respectively, as well as the accuracy of superficial invasion predictions by 2.40%, 2.29%, and 3.27%, respectively. Among these, 33.33% of error predictions were successfully corrected. Most corrected cases occurred between non-invasive carcinoma and non-malignant diseases (40 cases), as well as between low-grade carcinoma and non-malignant diseases (33 cases). Additionally, 27 new errors emerged after arbitration. These often occur when high/low-grade tumors were predicted as non-malignant with high confidence, leading to incorrect modifications in superficial invasion (9 cases), and when non-malignant tumors were predicted as non-invasive/superficial-invasive with high confidence, leading to incorrect modifications in histological grading (10 cases). Therefore, the dual-channel arbitration can quickly resolve certain logical paradoxes in model inference through simple post-processing operations, particularly the contradiction between malignant and non-malignant diseases. However, this post-processing step did not participate in the model optimization process and lacked a deep understanding of the intrinsic correlation between grading and invasion, making it easier to introduce new misjudgments for high-confidence predictions. In most cases, DCMIL demonstrated satisfactory diagnostic performance in predicting histological grading and superficial invasion of NMIBC.

To further elucidate the decision-making mechanism of the DCMIL, attention heatmaps for the dual channels were generated to illustrate the relative importance of different regions during the prediction process. The highlighted regions could accurately depict the morphological characteristics and spatial distributions of NMIBC cells. The heatmaps of the histological grading classifier revealed distinct cell morphological characteristics: For non-malignant disease (i.e., UP), cells had small papillae without fusion, absence of cellular atypia, and no observable mitotic activity (Figure 4A). For low-grade NMIBC, tumor cells exhibited elongated and fused papillae, slightly disrupted polarity, moderate atypia, and non-basal mitotic figures (Figures 4B and 4C). High-grade NMIBC was characterized by many mitotic figures, disordered cellular structure, poor adhesion, and considerable cellular atypia (Figure 4D). Meanwhile, the heatmaps of the superficial invasion classifier revealed different cell location distributions: For non-malignant disease and non-invasive NMIBC, cell nests exhibited smooth margins, intact basement membranes, well-defined interfaces with surrounding tissues, and an absence of interstitial reactivity (Figures 4A, 4B, and 4D). Superficial-invasive NMIBC had numerous irregular single or small clusters of cell nests in the stroma, as well as uneven margins around the carcinoma cell nests (Figure 4C). It also found that some incorrect predictions were caused by labeling errors, which occurred when pathologists neglected the microscopic anomalous regions. For example, the model identified several mitotic figures in non-basal regions of the WSI labeled as non-malignant disease (i.e., PUNLMP) and correctly classified it as low-grade non-invasive (Figure S3A). Instances of minor peripheral invasion by small clusters of tumor cells in the WSIs were labeled as non-invasive, which the model accurately identified as superficial-invasive (Figure S3B). This not only validated the effectiveness of DCMIL but also demonstrated that the key regions indicated by model predictions aligned highly with the morphological criteria of diagnostic guidelines. Dual-channel heatmaps can be used to guide pathologists in focusing on critical target areas, thereby alleviating the diagnostic workload. Additionally, the causes of model prediction errors were analyzed through dual-channel heatmaps. As shown in Figure S3C, the model misidentified collapsed cystic dilatation areas in adenomatous cystitis as solid aggregates of low-grade non-invasive. The pseudo-clad epithelium and reactive nuclear atypia (e.g., enlarged nuclei, vacuolated chromatin, and prominent nucleoli) in areas of glandular metaplasia shared morphological similarities to solid proliferative areas of low-grade carcinoma, resulting in model ambiguity. Inflammatory lesions exhibited localized mechanical compression accompanied with substantial infiltration of tissue cells (macrophages and fibroblasts) and inflammatory cells (neutrophils and lymphocytes), leading the model to misclassify it as high-grade superficial-invasive (Figure S3D). This phenomenon indicated that the model may still exhibit limitations in generalizability when handling metaplastic lesions or inflammation accompanied by complex artificial illusions. Based on the above case analysis, several strategies for optimizing model performance can be determined.

Furthermore, the DCMIL can serve as a practical tool to bridge the diagnostic gap between junior or intermediate pathologists and experienced senior pathologists. In the internal reader set, an analysis of initial manual diagnoses made by pathologists with varying levels of experience revealed the following findings: (1) In the histological grading diagnosis of NMIBC, the diagnostic accuracy of high-grade consistently outperformed that of low-grade and non-malignant diseases. This was mainly due to high-grade NMIBC exhibiting more typical and easily recognizable malignant features, such as significantly enlarged cells, coarse chromatin, and severe structural disorganization. However, there was considerable variation in diagnostic levels for low-grade and non-malignant diseases. The primary reason was the ambiguous interpretation of the grading criteria. For instance, inexperienced pathologists were likely to misdiagnose reactive changes caused by inflammation (such as transient nuclear enlargement or chromatin darkening) as low-grade or misidentify low-grade as non-malignant diseases by failing to recognize subtle malignant features. (2) In the diagnosis of superficial invasion of NMIBC, non-malignant diseases were easiest to identify due to their regular tissue architecture. However, the distinction between non-invasive and superficial-invasive was highly dependent on an accurate assessment of basal membrane integrity, which is a challenging aspect for junior and intermediate pathologists. This diagnostic challenge primarily resulted from two factors. First, minute invasive lesions were often localized and inconspicuous, easily overlooked when biopsy specimens were limited or when section orientation was suboptimal. Second, artificial artifacts produced during tissue processing, such as tissue shrinkage, tearing, or thermal damage, could significantly interfere with the assessment of basement membrane integrity, thereby increasing diagnostic uncertainty. (3) The assessment of histological grading and superficial invasion often interfered with each other due to the high heterogeneity of NMIBC, making diagnosis highly susceptible to subjective factors. In low-grade superficial-invasive NMIBC, tumor cells exhibited mild cellular atypia, typical nuclear morphology, and orderly arrangement. Despite tumor cells penetrating the basement membrane and infiltrating the stroma, the invasive margins were often subtle and indistinct, leading to frequent misclassification as non-invasive. High-grade non-invasive NMIBC did not penetrate the basement membrane. However, the prominent malignant morphological features, such as intense nuclear hyperchromatism, marked cellular atypia, and severe architectural disorganization, often led pathologists to neglect the assessment of basement membrane integrity, resulting in misdiagnosis as superficial-invasive. Compared with pathologists, the diagnostic accuracy of DCMIL for histological grading and superficial invasion exceeded the average of intermediate pathologists and approached that of senior pathologists (Figure 5). In particular, the high recognition capability for non-malignant diseases, high-grade, and superficial-invasive rendered DCMIL a promising tool to effectively assist junior and intermediate pathologists in enhancing the diagnostic accuracy for NMIBC. For this purpose, the diagnostic predictions and dual-channel attention heatmaps of DCMIL were provided to four junior pathologists and four intermediate pathologists, respectively. The pathologists revised the diagnosis by integrating the prediction results with the lesion-related areas suggested by DCMIL. After AI assistance, the overall AUROCs of junior pathologists in histological grading and superficial invasion improved by 19.09% (from 0.728 to 0.867) and 18.07% (from 0.675 to 0.797), respectively. The overall AUROCs of intermediate pathologists in histological grading and superficial invasion also improved by 13.02% (from 0.822 to 0.929) and 13.60% (from 0.787 to 0.894), respectively. Among all subtypes, junior pathologists showed the most significant average improvements in AUROC of low-grade (from 0.708 to 0.855) and F1-score of superficial-invasive (from 0.663 to 0.814), while intermediate pathologists showed the most significant improvements in AUROC of low-grade (from 0.800 to 0.915) and F1-score of non-invasive (from 0.746 to 0.862). Therefore, DCMIL could effectively enhance the diagnostic confidence and accuracy of junior and intermediate pathologists, particularly by assisting junior pathologists in the initial assessment of malignancy versus non-malignant diseases and intermediate pathologists in the further diagnosis of challenging cases (e.g., non-invasive). In particular, it can provide crucial morphological and localization insights in complex cases, demonstrating significant potential for clinical assistance.

In summary, this study proposed a novel DCMIL model for the comprehensive and precise automatic pathological diagnosis of NMIBC through H&E-stained WSIs of TURBT specimens. The outstanding performance and robustness of DCMIL were validated in recognizing histological grading and superficial invasion of NMIBC across large and multicenter datasets. Notably, the dual-channel-based assistance method can significantly improve the diagnostic levels of junior and intermediate pathologists. Therefore, it has the potential to become an effective and reliable initial assessment aid for NMIBC in clinical applications.

Limitations of the study

Despite these promising results, this study still has several limitations. First, this study is retrospective and therefore requires prospective clinical validation. Second, DCMIL was validated on only one external dataset. Additionally, a clear limitation of this study is that the external dataset did not collect low-grade superficial-invasive NMIBCs, which have an extremely low prevalence in natural populations, leading to a non-negligible data bias. The model’s generalization ability for this rare subtype could not be thoroughly evaluated through the current external dataset. Third, DCMIL can accurately identify non-invasive urothelial carcinoma but cannot further distinguish urothelial carcinoma in situ with a higher risk of progression due to data limitations. Therefore, it is necessary to increase this type of data to make the model more suitable for clinical treatment and supervision. Fourth, the dual-channel output arbitration strategy proposed in this study is essentially a post-processing rule. This post-processing method failed to improve the logical self-consistency of the model during optimization. A dual-channel fusion strategy embedded within the end-to-end model should be adopted in future work to effectively learn the associations between grading and invasion, thereby avoiding logical paradoxes in model inference. We plan to further expand the dataset and optimize the model in the future to enhance the clinical applicability of DCMIL.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement

All patients included in this study underwent TURBT across two centers, and biopsy specimens were obtainable. All patient-related information obtained was ethically approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (2025-KY-014-01), and informed consent was waived for this retrospective study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient cohorts

Two independent cohorts were ultimately enrolled: ZJ-SMU cohort (n = 1194) from Zhujiang Hospital of Southern Medical University (September 2014 to May 2024) and TAH-SMU cohort (n = 138) from Third Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (February 2019 to May 2024). The internal training set comprised 812 patients from ZJ-SMU cohort (mean age, 62.50years ±14.41 [SD]) with 634 male (78.08%), the internal test including 347 patients from ZJ-SMU cohort (mean age, 63.75years ±13.65 [SD]) with 288 male (83.00%), and the external test set containing 138 patients from TAH-SMU cohort (mean age, 61.52years ±15.03 [SD]) with 100 male (72.46%). Although age and gender were well balanced across the datasets to avoid cohort bias, we did not conduct specific subgroup analyses to evaluate the independent influence on model performance. Meanwhile, a reader set from ZJ-SMU cohort (n = 35) was used to explore the AI-assisted capabilities of the model.

For each patient, the most representative slide was selected from the TURBT specimens. All slides were stained with H&E and scanned at ×40 magnification (0.25 μm/pixel) utilizing KF-PRO-120 (KFBIO, China) and IMD-Neo-5X (InteMedic, China) scanners, generating WSIs in SVS format. For each WSI, the histological grading (i.e., non-malignant, low-grade, or high-grade) and superficial invasion (i.e., non-malignant, non-invasive, or superficial-invasive) labels were extracted from the pathological reports and reviewed comprehensively by two senior pathologists (J.Y. and H.Z.) according to the latest WHO classification independently. If there was a disagreement in the diagnosis, the final manually annotated was confirmed through consultation. This multi-center, large-sample design was employed to assess the generalizability of model performance across different patient populations, clinical settings, and institutional workflows.

Method details

Data processing

For each WSI, the regions of interest (i.e., tissue regions) were automatically segmented using the CLAM-based analysis tool.39 First, the WSI was read into memory at × 64 downscale and converted from RGB to the HSV color space. Then, a binary mask for each tissue region was generated by median blurring to smooth the edges, thresholding to the saturation channel and morphological closing to fill small gaps and holes. Finally, the segmentation mask was obtained by further filtering based on an area threshold. After removing the background in each WSI, patches of 256 × 256 pixels were cropped within the segmented tissue regions at a ×40 magnification without overlapping for downstream tasks.

Development of the DCMIL model

The deep learning-based DCMIL model was developed to classify each WSI into multi-class subtypes of histological grade and superficial invasion. DCMIL consisted of the CLAM-based backbone network and a parallel dual-channel classifier architecture, achieving efficient and robust end-to-end optimization using weakly supervised learning (Figure 1). Specifically, based on ResNet50 model40 pre-trained on ImageNet,41 each 256 × 256 input patch is converted into a 1,024-dimensional feature vector output by adaptive mean spatial pooling after the third residual block, with a batch size of 128 on the GPU. This process was identical to that of CLAM, capturing sufficient pathological features while reducing computational costs. Then, two fully connected layers followed by rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation were set up to compress each patch-level representation from 1,024-dimensional vector to 512-dimensional vector. The fully connected layers were shared for extracting features of abnormal regions. Finally, two multi-class attention networks were constructed to focus on features related to histological grading and superficial invasion, respectively. Therefore, the two branches can simultaneously achieve classification of histological grading (i.e., non-malignant vs. low-grade vs. high-grade) and superficial invasion (i.e., non-malignant vs. non-invasive vs. superficial-invasive). The total loss L for a given slide was the sum of both the histological grading classification loss LGrade and the superficial invasion classification loss LInvasion.(Equation 1)L=LGrade+LInvasionwhere LGrade or LInvasion included standard cross-entropy loss for comparing slide-level predictions with ground-truth labels, and binary smooth SVM loss42 for comparing instance-level cluster prediction scores for each sampled patch with the corresponding pseudo-cluster label. The standard cross-entropy loss for multi-class classification is defined as:(Equation 2)LCE=−∑c=1Cyc⁡logpcwhere C is the number of classes, yc is a binary indicator (0 or 1) of whether class c is the correct slide-level classification for the sample, and pc is the predicted probability that the sample belongs to class c. The binary smooth SVM loss is given by:(Equation 3)Lsmooth−SV M(s,y)=τ⁡log∑j∈{0,1}expα⋅1j≠y+sj−syτwhere s is a binary classification task with prediction scores, y is the instance-level pseudo-cluster label, α is a margin parameter that enforces a separation between classes, τ is a temperature parameter that controls the smoothness of the loss, and 1j≠y is an indicator function that is 1 if j ≠ y and 0 otherwise.

The weights and biases of the DCMIL backbone network were initialized using the pre-trained module from CLAM via transfer learning. Other new convolutional layers were initialized with a normal distribution with σ = 0.01 and bias b = 0. Then, DCMIL was trained and fine-tuned end-to-end with the slide-level labels on the training set. The training process employed Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 2 × 10−4 and L2 weight decay of 1 × 10−5 for a maximum of 200 epochs if the early stopping criterion is not met.

Dual-channel output arbitration rules

To avoid abnormal prediction results, a dual-channel output arbitration strategy was proposed to integrate the predictions from both two classifiers. The detailed rules were as follows: (1) If the WSI was labeled as non-malignant disease or malignancy in both the histological grading channel and the superficial invasion channel, DCMIL would output the original dual-channel prediction as a result. (2) If a logical paradox occurred, where the WSI was labeled as non-malignant disease in one channel and malignancy in another, it is necessary to compare the predictive confidence scores of non-malignant disease and malignancy. When the non-malignant disease confidence score was higher, the prediction of another channel was changed to non-malignant disease, and DCMIL would output non-malignant disease as the dual-channel result. Similarly, when the malignancy confidence score was higher, the prediction of another channel was changed from non-malignant disease to the malignancy category with the higher confidence score. Attention heatmap visualization

To interpret the relative importance of different regions in WSIs to the final predictions, all the unnormalized attention scores of the patches were preserved separately before applying the softmax function from both two channels. These attention scores were converted to percentile scores and scaled to between 0 and 1.0, where 1.0 indicated a strongly correlation with the prediction, and 0 indicated a weak correlation. The normalized attention scores were converted into RGB color space and displayed at corresponding spatial coordinates on the WSI through pseudo-color technique. Patches with higher attention scores were represented in red to visually identify and interpret regions contributing significantly to the model’s predictions, while patches with lower attention scores appeared in blue, indicating regions with lesser contribution to the prediction. Experienced pathologists further validated the biological interpretability, including assessing whether highlighted regions corresponded with histopathologically relevant structures (e.g., tumor nests, mitotic figures, or interstitial reactivity).

Pathology reader study

To validate the diagnostic capabilities of pathologists for NMIBC and the auxiliary diagnostic performance of DCMIL, the reader study was incorporated into the pipeline (Figure 1). Twelve pathologists with varying years of experience (four junior pathologists with less 5 years of experience, four intermediate pathologists with approximately 10 years of experience, and four senior pathologists with at least than 15 years of experience) examined the WSIs from the internal reader dataset. Without access to the diagnostic labels and clinical data, the pathologists independently reviewed each WSI and assessed histological grading as well as superficial invasion based on their expertise. Following a wash-out period of at least eight weeks, the pathologists conducted another AI-assisted review and diagnosis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

To evaluate model performance, F1-score, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were used as primary metrics. The 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using bootstrap method based on model predictions and included in brackets for all metrics to ensure the reliability of performance metrics. In addition, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve and confusion matrix were calculated. The statistical analysis was based on Python (version 3.7.7) and open-source scikit-learn toolkit (version 1.3.2).
