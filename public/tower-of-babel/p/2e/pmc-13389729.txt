Mycobacterium haemophilum tenosynovitis of the thumb in an immunocompetent adult: a case report highlighting a diagnostic challenge
Authors: Mariko Abe, Norihiko Terada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: We report a rare case of Mycobacterium haemophilum flexor tenosynovitis in an immunocompetent adult and discuss the diagnostic challenges posed by this organism under standard culture conditions.Patient and Methods: A 72-year-old Japanese man presented with chronic pain and swelling of the left thumb following a rose thorn injury and subsequent exposure to aquarium water. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an effusion in the flexor tendon sheath. The patient underwent synovectomy and debridement. Acid-fast bacilli staining of intraoperative specimens was negative, and liquid mycobacterial culture at 37°C remained negative after 6 weeks.Results: Local symptoms persisted with recurrent tenosynovial effusions. A repeat aspirate showed growth on Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube culture after 5 weeks, and the isolate was identified as M. haemophilum using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-time-of-flight mass spectrometry. Symptoms resolved after drainage and oral antimicrobial therapy, with no recurrence at the 6-month follow-up.Conclusion:M. haemophilum should be included in the differential diagnosis of distal-extremity tenosynovitis, particularly in patients with a history of contact with aquarium water. Negative cultures at 35–37°C does not exclude M. haemophilum infection. Close communication between clinicians and microbiology laboratories is essential to ensure appropriate culture conditions, including low incubation temperatures and iron/hemin supplementation.

Introduction

Mycobacterium haemophilum is a nontuberculous mycobacterium (NTM) that prefers lower temperatures (30–32 °C) and requires iron-containing compounds such as hemin. Therefore, under routine culture conditions, the detection of M. haemophilum may be delayed or the organism may not be isolated1). This organism causes skin, soft-tissue, and osteoarticular infections in immunocompromised patients2). However, reports of such infections in immunocompetent hosts are rare. Here, we report a case of thumb flexor tendon sheath tenosynovitis in an immunocompetent Japanese adult, wherein the initial surgical specimens were culture-negative, but the second aspirate tested positive after prolonged culture. The isolate was identified as M. haemophilum, which was considered the most likely causative organism. This case is also relevant to community and rural practice, where mycobacterial cultures are often outsourced, and specialized incubation conditions may not be routinely available.

Case Presentation

A 72-year-old Japanese man with no history of diabetes, immunosuppressive drug use, or underlying diseases causing immunosuppression punctured his left thumb with a rose thorn approximately 2 months before presentation. He removed the thorn using a needle. He subsequently performed aquarium maintenance for a pet discus fish at home without protecting the puncture site. He experienced pain in his left thumb that gradually worsened over time. The patient visited a local orthopedic clinic and was referred to our hospital for further evaluation and treatment.

On admission, his vital signs were as follows: body temperature, 36.6°C; blood pressure, 120/82 mmHg; and pulse, 88 beats per minute (normal). Physical examination revealed mild erythema extending from the metacarpophalangeal joint to the interphalangeal joint on the left thumb, which was accompanied by mild swelling and warmth. Laboratory findings were as follows: white blood cell count, 7,300/µL; neutrophils, 41.5%; C-reactive protein, 0.10 mg/dL; blood urea nitrogen, 14.3 mg/dL; and creatinine, 1.04 mg/dL. Fourth-generation human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-1/2 antigen and antibody tests yielded negative results. Additional screening for immune-related conditions showed a rheumatoid factor level of ≤3.5 IU/mL and negative results in an antinuclear antibody test (<1:40). Chest radiography performed upon admission revealed no abnormal pulmonary opacities. Short-tau inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging revealed a reticular hyperintense area in the adipose tissue around the thumb flexor tendon sheath and tenosynovial effusion within the tendon sheath from the distal phalanx to the proximal phalanx (Figure 1Figure 1Short-tau inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging of the left thumb showing fluid collection within the flexor tendon sheath (arrows) and reticular hyperintensity in the surrounding volar fatty tissue (arrowheads). These findings were consistent with flexor tenosynovitis and adjacent inflammatory change.). Infectious tenosynovitis was suspected; the patient was hospitalized and underwent synovectomy and debridement. Mycobacterial blood cultures were not performed because the patient showed no clinical findings suggestive of systemic involvement at presentation.

Synovial tissue was excised en bloc, and two specimens were subjected to bacterial and mycobacterial cultures. The wound was thoroughly irrigated with sterile saline, the residual synovium was excised, a drain was placed, and the wound was closed. Postoperatively, oral cephalexin at 1,000 mg/day was initiated for common bacterial infections; however, Gram staining was negative. Both tissue specimens were cultured on sheep blood agar and chocolate agar (AccuRite fractionated sheep blood agar/chocolate EX II; Shimadzu Diagnostics, Tokyo, Japan) at 35°C and 5% CO2 for 48 h, on modified Drigalski agar (Shimadzu Diagnostics) at 35°C for 48 h, and in thioglycollate broth supplemented with vitamin K and hemin (Tube Thioglycollate Vit K Hem; Becton Dickinson) at 35°C for 7 days. All cultures were negative. Cephalexin was discontinued after 12 days of treatment. Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tests for Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTC) and Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) were negative in both specimens. Liquid culture (Mycobacteria Growth Indicator Tube [MGIT]; Becton, Dickinson, and Company, Japan) was performed at 37°C for 6 weeks, and no growth was observed.

During outpatient follow-up, local symptoms persisted, and tenosynovial effusion recurred in the tendon sheath. Needle aspiration yielded a slightly viscous serosanguineous fluid (Figure 2Figure 2Needle aspiration yielded a serosanguineous, slightly viscous fluid.). AFB staining and PCR tests for MTC and MAC yielded negative results. The aspirated fluid specimen was sent to an external laboratory, where mycobacterial liquid culture using the MGIT system was performed at 37°C without hemin/iron supplementation. Incubation at 30–32°C and solid cultures were not performed. Treatment with minocycline (100 mg/day) was initiated, given the history of contact with aquarium water and the suspicion of infection with M. marinum, an NTM that can be missed in cultures incubated at 35–37°C. The local swelling and pain gradually improved with additional aspiration drainage and antimicrobial therapy. The MGIT culture of the specimen was positive at week 5.

Needle aspiration yielded a serosanguineous, slightly viscous fluid.

Using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI)-time-of-flight mass spectrometry (TOF MS), the isolated acid-fast bacillus was identified as M. haemophilum. Spectral analyses were performed using the MALDI Biotyper system (v7.0; Bruker Daltonics, Billerica, MA, USA), which yielded a score of 2.33, indicating a high-confidence match between the protein spectrum of the isolate and the reference entry for M. haemophilum.

Additional molecular analyses, including antimicrobial susceptibility tests, were not performed because of insufficient growth of the isolate. At the time of identification, the patient showed sustained clinical improvement; therefore, the antimicrobial regimen was not intensified. Given the favorable clinical response after surgical and percutaneous drainage and the absence of systemic involvement, we selected close follow-up without intensifying the antimicrobial therapy. Minocycline was maintained and discontinued approximately 3 months after symptom resolution. No recurrence was observed at the 6-month follow-up. Additionally, we later confirmed that the external laboratory workflow consisted of routine MGIT-only incubation at 37°C, consistent with the culture conditions described above.

Discussion

This report describes a case of chronic thumb flexor tendon sheath tenosynovitis in an immunocompetent adult, with M. haemophilum detected by MGIT culture of a second specimen that became positive after 5 weeks, whereas the intraoperative specimens tested negative on MGIT liquid culture incubated at 37°C for 6 weeks. Isolation under routine culture conditions is challenging because M. haemophilum prefers lower temperatures (30–32°C) and may require iron-containing supplements such as hemin1).

Mycobacterium haemophilum causes skin and soft-tissue lesions, arthritis, osteomyelitis, pneumonia, and ocular infections in immunocompromised patients, including hematopoietic stem cell transplant recipients, people living with HIV, and those receiving immunosuppressive therapy2, 3). Mycobacterium haemophilum infection should be suspected in immunocompromised patients with unexplained distal-extremity skin lesions when AFB staining shows positive results but routine cultures show negative findings4). Distal-extremity involvement has been frequently reported, possibly reflecting the organism’s preference for lower temperatures5). Although M. haemophilum is considered an environmental NTM, water reservoirs and water-associated biofilms may also be sources of infection2). This species has also been detected in aquarium environments6). In our case, the puncture site was exposed to aquarium water after the rose thorn injury, which may be etiologically relevant.

Both initial intraoperative tissue specimens and the subsequent aspirated fluid specimen were processed by an external laboratory using MGIT liquid cultures incubated at 37°C; however, only the aspirated fluid demonstrated growth, yielding a positive result after 5 weeks. This discrepancy could be attributed to differences in specimen type and blood contamination; the first specimen had little to no blood admixture, whereas the second aspirate was serosanguineous/light brown, and blood contains iron sources that may have facilitated the growth of M. haemophilum1). Although this species grows preferentially at lower temperatures (30–32°C) and may require iron/hemin supplementation1), it may occasionally be detected under non-optimal liquid culture conditions; therefore, a negative MGIT culture at 35–37°C does not definitively exclude M. haemophilum infection. Mycobacterium marinum and M. ulcerans infections are important differential diagnoses for NTM infections in peripheral tissues and may require low-temperature incubation2).

In cases involving suspected NTM-related distal-extremity tenosynovitis, a close collaboration with the microbiology laboratory can enable the implementation of low-temperature incubation, solid culture, and iron/hemin supplementation to reduce diagnostic delays. However, the optimal treatment for M. haemophilum infections has not yet been standardized. Studies have recommended targeted antimicrobial therapy using regimens guided by susceptibility testing2). Susceptibility tests were not performed in the present case. Since the patient underwent synovectomy and repeated drainage, surgical source control likely contributed to the favorable outcomes, limiting the scope to determine the effectiveness of minocycline monotherapy.

This study has certain limitations. First, susceptibility testing was not performed because of insufficient mycobacterial growth. Second, sequence-based identification was not performed. However, MALDI-TOF MS can identify M. haemophilum with high accuracy (93%), depending on the protein extraction procedure and the coverage of the reference spectral library. High-confidence identification was defined as log (score) ≥1.8 using the MALDI Biotyper Mycobacteria Library v3.0; our score of 2.33 exceeded this threshold7, 8). Third, the effects of antimicrobial therapy alone were difficult to determine because source control through synovectomy, debridement, and repeated drainage reduced the infectious burden.

Conclusion

We report a case of thumb flexor tendon sheath tenosynovitis in an immunocompetent Japanese adult in which a second specimen tested positive on MGIT culture after 5 weeks. The isolate was identified as M. haemophilum using MALDI-TOF MS. Mycobacterium haemophilum infection should be considered in the differential diagnosis of posttraumatic distal-extremity tenosynovitis or soft-tissue infection, particularly in patients with a history of aquarium water exposure. In several community and regional hospitals, mycobacterial cultures are outsourced, and routine protocols often rely on incubation at 35–37°C. Therefore, in cases of chronic distal-extremity tenosynovitis, clinicians should communicate suspected NTM infection to the microbiology laboratory to facilitate the timely implementation of low-temperature incubation, liquid and solid culture, and iron/hemin supplementation.
