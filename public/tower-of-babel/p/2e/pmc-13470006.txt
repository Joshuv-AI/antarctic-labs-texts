Direct extraction of bromine from seawater through an electrolysis-driven styrene enrichment process
Authors: Yini Chen, Jingwen Ke, Wenbin Ye, Yuxuan Zhou, Lei Song, Weizhao Hu, Zhigang Geng, Yuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe seawater contains approximately 99% of Earth’s bromide reserves. Therefore, the extraction of bromine from the seawater offers a promising approach to ensure a sustainable supply of bromine. However, the traditional electrochemical extraction method for direct bromine extraction are inapplicable, owing to the low concentration of bromide in seawater (~60 ppm). In this work, we develop an electrolysis-driven styrene enrichment process for efficient bromide recovery from seawater. As for the electrooxidation of bromide, we screen a series of metal phthalocyanines supported on carbon nanotube as the electrocatalysts. Through accelerating the bromine evolution reaction and suppressing the side reaction, nickel phthalocyanine supported on carbon nanotubes exhibit the highest activity with the Faradic efficiency of 97.8% for bromine production among all catalysts. Coupling this catalyst with the ESE process for direct bromine extraction from the Yellow Sea’s seawater, the extraction efficiency of 90.0% is achieved with the product of pure KBr powder. Moreover, the co-produced styrene oxide can be efficiently separated via vacuum distillation, significantly promoting the economic value of this ESE process.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Chemical engineering

Introduction

Bromine serves as an essential raw material, playing a critical role in various sectors including agriculture, healthcare, and flame retardants1. Driven by the increasing demand and constrained supply, the global market value of bromine exceeds $3.26 billion in 2022, which is projected to reach $4.31 billion by 20302. The conventional methods for bromine extraction include air blowing-out3, steam distillation4, and ion exchange resin adsorption route5,6. For these traditional methods, the resource of bromine mainly relies on the non-renewable and high-concentration brine (over 12000 ppm), such as salt-lake brine and underground brine7. Accordingly, the bromide existing in the high-concentration brine is declining every year at a rate of 5 to 10%8. Notably, seawater contains approximately 99% of the natural bromine reserves, representing another important resource for bromine extraction9. However, the concentration of bromide in seawater is extremely low (around 60 ppm), which is one to two orders of magnitude lower than that in traditional brine resources10. Under these constrained circumstances, traditional bromine extraction processes (such as air blowing-out, steam distillation, and adsorption) encounter significant challenges when directly applied to seawater, including low selectivity, low yield, and high energy consumption11. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop an efficient bromine extraction method from seawater.

Electrochemical extraction method offers a promising pathway for the sustainable extraction of bromine due to its advantages of low energy consumption, simple operation, and environmental friendliness12. Specifically, the bromide is electrochemically oxidized into bromine in anode, which could be blown out via air stripping. The bromine vapour is subsequently purged into equivalent KI solution, enabling the extraction of bromine with the formation of KBr solution (Fig. 1a). When employing electrochemical extraction method for high-concentration bromide solution, most of the produced bromine could be blown out, achieving a yield of 80.5%13. As for the low-concentration bromide solution, the generated bromine was prone to hydrolysis, forming non-volatile species such as hypobromous acid (HBrO). Consequently, the bromine was difficult to be extracted thoroughly via the air stripping method, leading to a relatively low yield of 49.8%14. Based on the aforementioned analysis, the existing electrochemical bromine extraction method exhibits significant limitations when directly applied to seawater. The formed non-volatile bromine species hinder the separation of bromine from seawater via air stripping, thereby limiting the yield. Therefore, developing a electrochemical process for direct bromine extraction from seawater remains challenging.

a The reported electrochemical method for high concentration of Br- in brine. b Our developed electrolysis-driven styrene enrichment process for low concentration of Br- in seawater.

Herein, we develop an electrolysis-driven styrene enrichment (ESE) process for direct bromine extraction from seawater, successfully overcoming the limitations of traditional electrochemical extraction method (Fig. 1b). In this process, hydrophobic styrene is utilized to capture the electrochemically generated hypobromous acid, yielding hydrophobic bromohydrin that drives bromine transfer from seawater to organic phase. Upon reacting with cathodically generated OH-, bromohydrin can be transformed into high value-added styrene oxide, simultaneously releasing the bromide to the catholyte. Through a simple water-organic-water phase transfer process, bromine can be effectively extracted from seawater. As for the electrooxidation of bromide, we screened a series of metal phthalocyanines (MPc, M=Fe, Ni, or Cu) supported on carbon nanotube (MPc/CNT) as the electrocatalysts. The NiPc/CNT exhibited the highest Faradic efficiency (FE) of 97.8% for bromine production at the current density (j) of 100 mA cm−2 among the three types of electrocatalysts. Notably, the FE for bromine exceeded 95% at the wide applied j from 100 to 1000 mA cm−2 over NiPc/CNT. The DFT calculation and in-situ Raman measurement revealed that the bromine evolution reaction (BrER) underwent the Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanism over the MPc/CNT catalysts. Especially, the NiPc/CNT exhibited the lowest Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for the potential-determining step (PDS) of BrER among all MPc/CNT, demonstrating the superiority of NiPc/CNT. During a 72-h continuous electrolysis under real seawater from the Yellow Sea, the applied potential exhibited negligible variation with the selectivity for BrER maintained above 98% over NiPc/CNT. Coupling NiPc/CNT with the ESE process for direct bromine extraction from the Yellow Sea’s seawater, the extraction efficiency of 90.0% was achieved with the product of pure potassium bromide (KBr) powder. Moreover, the co-produced styrene oxide could be efficiently separated by vacuum distillation, thereby enhancing the economic value of this ESE process.

Results

Preparation and characterization of MPc/CNT electrocatalysts

Typically, the MPc/CNT catalysts were prepared by anchoring MPc on CNTs through π–π interactions15,16, achieving molecularly dispersed MPc on CNTs (Fig. 2a). As for NiPc/CNT, the characteristic vibrational peaks at 483, 748, 832, and 1545 cm−1 were observed for both the NiPc and the NiPc/CNT in the Raman spectra, suggesting the successful loading of NiPc onto CNTs (Supplementary Fig. 1)17–19. The loading content of Ni was determined to be 0.32% by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy. Revealed by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), the Ni atoms were observed as bright dots (marked by red cycles), which were atomically dispersed on the CNTs (Fig. 2b). The HAADF-STEM image and corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping further confirmed the uniformly distribution of Ni and N on the CNTs (Fig. 2c). No peaks for the aggregated NiPc molecules were observed in the X-ray diffraction pattern (XRD) of NiPc/CNT, indicating the absence of aggregated NiPc in as-synthesized catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 2). To investigate the electronic and coordination structures of NiPc/CNT, we conducted the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray absorption fine structure spectroscopy (XAFS) measurements. As shown in Fig. 2d, the characteristic peak located at 872.9 eV was ascribed to Ni 2p1/2 for aggregated NiPc. For NiPc/CNT, the binding energy of Ni 2p1/2 shifted to 873.3 eV. The energy shift could be attributed to the π-π interaction between NiPc and CNTs20–22. Figure 2e showed the Ni K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structures (XANES) profiles of aggregated NiPc and NiPc/CNT. The spectra of NiPc/CNT exhibited a positive shift toward higher energy compared to that of aggregated NiPc. This result suggested the electron transfer from the Ni sites to the CNTs, which was consistent with the XPS analysis23–25. The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra of NiPc/CNT and aggregated NiPc exhibited a prominent peak at 1.47 Å, which was attributed to the first coordination shell of Ni−N26,27 (Fig. 2f). To further obtain the coordination parameters of Ni in NiPc/CNT, we conducted a least-squares EXAFS fitting. The fitting results showed that the Ni-N coordination number was 4.22 for NiPc/CNT (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Table 1). This result indicated that the NiPc was successfully anchored on CNTs with the coordination structures perfectly preserved. For comparison, the FePc/CNT and CuPc/CNT were also prepared through the standard synthetic route of NiPc/CNT except for substitution of NiPc with FePc and CuPc, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 4–6, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Catalytic performance of MPc/CNT for BrER

The catalytic activity for BrER over the MPc/CNT was firstly evaluated in 1 M KBr with H-cell. The production of molecular bromine (Br2) was measured by the iodometric titration method. Figure 3a shows the Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves over MPc/CNT. NiPc/CNT exhibited the lowest onset potentials and highest j among all of the MPc/CNT (Fig. 3b). The catalytic performance of MPc/CNT was further evaluated via chronopotentiometry electrolysis. With the increase of applied current, the required anodic potential gradually increased (Supplementary Fig. 7). In comparison, the total j over NiPc/CNT was higher than that over other MPc/CNT at all of the applied potentials. Figure 3c showed the FE for BrER over NiPc/CNT. The highest FE for Br2 (FEBr2) reached 97.8 % over NiPc/CNT at the j of 100 mA cm−2. It was worth noting that the FEBr2 over NiPc/CNT exceeded 95% at all applied currents, which were slightly higher than those over other MPc/CNT (Supplementary Fig. 8). The partial j for Br2 (jBr2) was further calculated. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 9, the NiPc/CNT exhibited the highest jBr2 among all the MPc/CNT at all applied potentials. We further calculated the energy efficiency (EE) of MPc/CNT at different j. NiPc/CNT exhibited an EE of 78% at 100 mA cm−2, which was higher than those of FePc/CNT (68%) and CuPc/CNT (67%). Moreover, the EE of NiPc/CNT remained higher than 45% in the j range from 100 to 1000 mA cm−2. (Supplementary Fig. 10). The durability test for NiPc/CNT was proceeded via chronopotentiometry measurement at the j of 300 mA cm−2. During a 36-h continuous measurement, the applied potential exhibited negligible variation with relatively stable FE, suggesting the durability of the NiPc/CNT electrocatalyst (Supplementary Fig. 11).

To investigate the intrinsic activity of MPc/CNT, we normalized the jBr2 by the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) (Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13). At all applied potentials, the Cdl-normalized jBr2 over NiPc/CNT surpassed that over other MPc/CNT, indicating the higher intrinsic activity over NiPc/CNT (Fig. 3d). The kinetics of BrER over MPc/CNT were evaluated via Tafel slope. As shown in Fig. 3e, the Tafel slope of NiPc/CNT was measured to be 102.3 mV dec−1, which was lower than FePc/CNT (192.6 mV dec−1) and CuPc/CNT (166.8 mV dec−1). The lowest Tafel slope value of NiPc/CNT suggested the fastest reaction kinetics, thereby contributing to the highest catalytic activity for BrER among all of MPc/CNT. Given the high BrER activity over NiPc/CNT, we compared its performance with commercial dimensionally stable anodes (DSA) (Supplementary Fig. 14). The NiPc/CNT demonstrated significantly higher j at all applied potentials, further underscoring its potential for BrER. The competitive activity of NiPc/CNT relative to other reported catalysts in FE and overpotential further highlights NiPc/CNT as a promising electrocatalyst for BrER. (Supplementary Table 4).

To reveal the intrinsic reason for the high catalytic activity over NiPc/CNT toward BrER, we conducted the density functional theory (DFT) calculation over the three types of MPc/CNT. In general, the BrER process follows three possible pathways, including the Tafel, Krishtalik, and Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanisms (Supplementary Table 5)28,29. Owing to the isolation configuration of the M-N₄ sites, the Tafel mechanism involving the combination of two adsorbed bromine atoms (*Br) could be excluded. The Krishtalik mechanism proceeds via a stabilized Br+ intermediate, which is mainly formed over the catalysts with an electron-rich oxide lattice. To further verify the reaction pathway, in-situ Raman measurements were conducted to monitor the BrER process. The characteristic peak at 243 cm−1 was attributed to the *Br, which was the key intermediate for BrER via the Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanism (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). Accordingly, we calculated the BrER process via the Volmer-Heyrovsky mechanism over the MPc/CNT catalysts30,31. Specifically, the Br- undergoes a one-electron oxidation to generate *Br, which then reacts with another Br- to produce Br2 in a second electron transfer step. Figure 3f showed the Gibbs free energy diagrams of BrER over the three types of MPc/CNT (Supplementary Fig. 17). The PDS for BrER was calculated to be 1.45 eV over NiPc/CNT, which was notably lower than those over FePc/CNT (1.68 eV) and CuPc/CNT (1.56 eV). The differential charge density was further performed to show obvious charge redistribution after *Br adsorption on MPc/CNT. In all cases, electron accumulation occurred around the *Br species, indicating electron transfer from the metal site to the Br species over MPc/CNT (Supplementary Fig. 18). To further clarify the different adsorption strengths of *Br on these catalysts, we calculated the d-band centers of the metal sites. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 19, the d-band center values for FePc/CNT, NiPc/CNT, and CuPc/CNT were −0.16, −2.22, and −3.22 eV, respectively. According to d-band theory, a higher d-band center value leads to stronger adsorption of reaction intermediates. Thus, FePc/CNT exhibited the strongest adsorption of *Br, whereas CuPc/CNT possessed the weakest adsorption of *Br among the three catalysts. Gibbs free-energy analysis was also consistent with the adsorption-activity relationship. Excessively strong *Br adsorption on FePc/CNT might hinder the subsequent desorption step, whereas too weak *Br adsorption on CuPc/CNT might make *Br formation thermodynamically unfavorable, resulting in increased energy barriers. In comparison, the NiPc/CNT demonstrated moderate adsorption of *Br, thus contributing to the low ΔG of PDS for BrER. Based on the above analysis, the calculated adsorption behavior of NiPc/CNT was consistent with its experimentally observed BrER performance.

Application of NiPc/CNT in bromine extraction from seawater

With the high activity for BrER over NiPc/CNT, we further demonstrated its practical potential by coupling NiPc/CNT with ESE process for direct bromine extraction from seawater. Specifically, the developed process proceeded with a continuous three-step cycle, including the electrooxidation of Br-, bromine capture, and the Br- release (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 20). At the anode, the Br- in seawater is initially oxidized to Br₂, which can further react with H2O to form HBrO32. Due to the low concentration of Br- in seawater, the disproportionation of Br₂ is favored, resulting in the predominant formation of HBrO33. Subsequently, the anolyte containing HBrO species is transferred to an independent reaction vessel and mixed with styrene, efficiently forming bromohydrin (C8H9BrO). Kinetic analysis showed that the consumption rate of HBrO species was much higher than their electrochemical generation rate, ensuring that HBrO can be consumed completely (Supplementary Fig. 21). Due to the hydrophobicity of bromohydrin, the bromine species could be effectively extracted from the seawater to organic phase. Subsequently, the mixture is transferred to a separation vessel for static phase separation. The upper layer is organic layer comprising styrene enriched with the bromohydrin product, whereas the lower layer is the residual seawater containing negligible Br-. Through continuous cycling, Br- from seawater is progressively enriched in the styrene phase with the presence of bromohydrin (Supplementary Fig. 22). At the cathode, the H2O is split into hydrogen (H2) and OH-. The cycle-enriched bromohydrin product then reacted with the alkaline catholyte, regenerating Br- and producing styrene oxide. Overall, this ESE system achieved high bromine extraction efficiency through the synergistic integration of three efficient cascade reaction steps, thereby enabling direct applications for bromine extraction from seawater.

To avoid the interference of chloride (Cl-) in seawater (0.5 M Cl-, 60 ppm Br-), we explored the optimal reaction conditions for the electrooxidation process. As the standard potential for bromide oxidation (1.08 V vs RHE) is lower than chloride oxidation (1.36 V vs RHE), the applied potential plays a critical role in determining the selectivity towards bromine oxidation34. Chronoamperometry measurements was carried out to explore the optimal applied potential. The Br- concentration during electrolysis was quantitatively monitored by ion chromatography and kinetic-spectrophotometric method (Supplementary Fig. 23)35. As shown in Fig. 4b, a high selectivity of nearly 100% for BrER was achieved when the applied potential was no more than 1.8 V vs RHE (Supplementary Fig. 24). Beyond this potential, the selectivity for BrER progressively decreased, whereas chlorine evolution reaction (ClER) increased. To further confirm the optimal potential, we conducted a controlled-variable experiment. When the potential was set at 1.8 V vs RHE in the electrolyte containing only 0.5 M Cl-, the j remained below 0.5 mA cm−2, with no chlorine detected. Upon adding 60 ppm Br- into the electrolyte, the j jumped instantaneously, which could be attributed to the electrooxidation of Br- (Supplementary Fig. 25). With the increasing of the electrolysis time, the j gradually decreased, corresponding to the consumption of Br- and the generation of Br2. To further assess chloride interference, electrolytes with 0.5 M Cl- and 20-60 ppm Br- were electrolyzed at 1.8 V vs RHE for 30 min. After electrolysis, only trace amounts of Cl- were detected in the obtained KBr solution, with the selectivity of Br- product remaining above 99.5%. (Supplementary Fig. 26, Supplementary Table 6). In-situ Raman measurement was conducted under simulated seawater conditions (0.5 M Cl-, 60 ppm Br-). The peak intensity of *Br at 243 cm−1 exhibited an increasing trend as the potential increased from 1.6 to 1.8 V vs RHE, whereas the peak intensity gradually decreased when the potential exceeded 1.8 V vs RHE (Supplementary Fig. 27). These results demonstrated that selective oxidation of Br- was achievable for seawater over NiPc/CNT at 1.8 V vs RHE.

We further coupled the NiPc/CNT catalyst with the ESE system by directly extracting Br- from the Yellow Sea’s seawater (0.5 M Cl-, 58.6 ppm Br-) (Supplementary Fig. 28, Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). The extraction efficiency for Br- was evaluated in an H-type cell using working electrode with a geometric area of 4 cm2. During 120-min electrolysis at 1.8 V vs RHE, the concentration of Br- in seawater gradually decreased from 58.6 to 5.6 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 29). Remarkably, the bromine extraction efficiency surpassed 90.0% when the electrolysis time exceeded 90 min. We also evaluated the performance of the ESE system for Br- extraction under constantly refreshed seawater conditions. To further evaluate the practical stability and selectivity of the ESE system under real seawater conditions, long-term electrolysis was performed in a membrane electrode assembly (MEA). Since MEA measurements are typically conducted under chronopotentiometric conditions, the j of the ESE system was first evaluated in an H-cell with continuous flow at 1.8 V vs RHE (Supplementary Fig. 30). During the 5-h electrolysis, the j remained above 1 mA cm−2, accompanied by the yield of Br- increased linearly. Therefore, a j of 1 mA cm−2 was selected for the MEA stability test to maintain high selectivity of BrER. Accordingly, a constant current of 16 mA was applied in the MEA using an electrode area of 4 × 4 cm2 (Supplementary Fig. 31). As shown in Fig. 4c, during a 72-h continuous electrolysis, the applied potential showed negligible variation, while the selectivity of BrER remained above 98%. Moreover, the FEBr2 was maintained above 65% and FECl2 remained negligible with the low Br- concentration of 60 ppm during the stability test (Supplementary Figs. 32 and 33). These results demonstrated the durability of NiPc/CNT under real seawater conditions. After the stability test, the Ni 2p XPS spectrum revealed that the structure of NiPc/CNT was perfectly preserved (Supplementary Fig. 34). The Ni concentration remained nearly unchanged in ICP-MS analysis (Supplementary Table 9), indicating negligible Ni leaching from the catalyst. These results confirmed that NiPc/CNT maintained structural and compositional stability during long-term seawater electrolysis.

After 72-h continuous electrolysis, the enriched bromohydrin product then reacted with the alkaline catholyte in equimolar proportions, thus releasing Br- into the catholyte and co-produced styrene oxide. The extracted Br- was further separated out of the electrolyte by a simple recrystallization process, yielding 13.5 mmol of KBr (Fig. 4d). Characterized by XRD analysis, the diffraction peaks of the obtained KBr perfectly matched with the standard diffraction peaks of KBr (JCPDS No. 72-154), indicating the high purity of the obtained KBr (Fig. 4e). Compared with conventional electrochemical bromine production systems, our system delivered a competitive yield with relatively low energy consumption, regardless of the much lower Br- concentration in seawater (Supplementary Table 10). Additionally, the product of styrene oxide was efficiently separated from the organic phase via vacuum fractional distillation at 80°C, which was further confirmed by the ¹H NMR analysis (Fig. 4f). These findings suggest that this ESE process not only extracts bromine from the seawater but also produces high-value-added styrene oxide, representing a cost-efficient and environmentally friendly method for bromine extraction from seawater.

Discussion

In summary, we develop an ESE process employing NiPc/CNT electrocatalysts for direct bromine extraction from seawater with low concentration of Br-, successfully overcoming the limitations of the traditional electrochemical extraction method. Coupling NiPc/CNT with the ESE process for direct bromine extraction from the Yellow Sea’s seawater, we achieved a high bromine extraction efficiency of 90.0%. During a 72-h continuous electrolysis under real seawater from the Yellow Sea, the applied potential exhibited negligible variation, with the selectivity for BrER maintained above 98% over NiPc/CNT. The ESE process not only significantly improves the extraction yield of bromide but also offers a solution pathway for the electrochemical extraction of low-concentration bromide from seawater.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

All chemicals were purchased and used as received without further purification. N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.5%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99.0%), potassium bromide (KBr, 99.0%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36–38 wt%), ethanol (99.7%), and potassium iodide (KI, 99.5%) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). Styrene (99.0%) was purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (China). Multi-wall CNTs were purchased from C-Nano (product number FT 9100). FePc, NiPc, and CuPc were purchased from Bide Pharmatech Co., Ltd. Br- standard solution purchased from Guobiao (Beijing) Testing & Certification Co., Ltd. Nafion solution (5 wt%) and Nafion 115 film (thickness: 127 μm) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Deionized water used in all experiments was purified using a Milli-Q reference water purification system to a resistivity of 18.2 MΩ · cm (at 25°C). The seawater used in this work was sourced from the Yellow Sea, located in Yancheng, Jiangsu Province.

Synthesis of the MPc/CNT

The as-received CNTs were first calcined in air at 500 °C for 5 h and further purified by sonication in a 5 wt% HCl aqueous solution for 30 min. Thereafter, the purified CNTs were collected by filtration and thoroughly washed with deionized water. Subsequently, 20 mg of the purified CNTs were dispersed in 50 mL of DMF via sonication for 30 min. Then, 1mg of the catalyst molecule, including the FePc, NiPc, or CuPc dissolved in DMF, was added to the CNTs suspension. The mixture was sonicated for another 30min to achieve a homogeneous dispersion and stirred at room temperature (23 ± 1°C) for 20 h. The MPc/CNT was finally obtained by centrifugation, washed with DMF, and finally freeze-dried36,37. The metal loadings of FePc/CNT, NiPc/CNT, and CuPc/CNT were determined to be 0.23 wt%, 0.32 wt%, and 0.39 wt%, respectively.

Preparation of working electrodes

For all MPc/CNT catalysts, the as-synthesized materials were first weighed accurately and dispersed in a mixture of ethanol and Nafion solution to form an ink. The mixture was ultrasonicated for 30 min to ensure homogeneous dispersion of the catalyst and uniform blending with the Nafion solution. The resulting ink was then drop-cast onto pre-cleaned carbon paper substrates to achieve a uniform coating. Each deposition was followed by drying at 80°C on a water bath to allow solvent evaporation. Catalyst loading was controlled at 1.0 mg cm−2, which was calculated as the deposited catalyst mass divided by the geometric electrode area. The geometric areas were 2 cm2 for H-cell and 16 cm2 (4 × 4 cm) for MEA tests.

Electrolyte preparation and storage

The KBr electrolyte was freshly prepared by dissolving KBr in deionized water to obtain 1 M KBr solution before electrochemical measurements. Simulated seawater electrolytes were prepared by dissolving chloride salt and KBr in deionized water to obtain 0.5 M Cl- and 20–60 ppm Br-. The seawater was first filtered through a PP cotton filter to remove suspended particles. Afterward, the pH of the seawater was adjusted from 8 to 7 with the hydrochloric acid (0.075 μL per liter of seawater), thereby avoiding the reaction between the generated Br₂ and OH- present in the seawater. The final pH was measured as 6.8 ± 0.2 (n = 3). The prepared electrolytes were stored in sealed containers at 23 ± 1°C.

Membrane preparation

Nafion 115 membranes (thickness: 127 μm) were sequentially treated in 3 wt% H2O2 at 80°C for 1 h, deionized water at 80°C for 1 h, and 0.5 M H2SO4 at 80°C for 1 h, followed by thorough rinsing with deionized water. The effective membrane area was 7.07 cm2 for H-cell measurements and 16 cm2 for MEA measurements.

Electrochemical measurements

For the electrochemical transformation of Br- to Br2, the electrochemical measurements were carried out in a three-electrode H-cell. The anodic and cathodic chambers with volumes of 100 mL were separated by Nafion 115 membrane (thickness: 127 μm). Carbon paper with a geometric area of 4 cm2 and Ag/AgCl electrodes were used as the counter electrodes and reference electrode, respectively. NiPc/CNT-coated carbon paper with a geometric area of 2 cm2 was used as the working electrode. For the long-term electrolysis, the electrochemical measurements were carried out in MEA. The MEA was fabricated using a NiPc/CNT-coated carbon paper as the anode and a carbon paper as the cathode. A Nafion 115 membrane was placed between the two electrodes. Titanium plates served as current collectors on both the anode and cathode sides. The geometric electrode area and the effective membrane area were both 16 cm2 (4 × 4 cm2). The potentials and currents were controlled by a CHI 1140D potentiostat/galvanostat. LSV was recorded at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1. Cyclic voltammetry for double-layer capacitance (CV) measurements was recorded in the non-Faradaic potential region from 0.91 to 1.01 V vs RHE at scan rates from 20 to 80 mV s−1. Chronoamperometry and chronopotentiometry were performed at the applied potentials or currents. The raw current–time, current–potential, and potential–time data were collected directly from the potentiostat software and exported for further analysis without smoothing. Current densities were calculated by normalizing the measured current to the geometric area of the working electrode. All potentials were measured against the Ag/AgCl reference electrode (saturated KCl). The reference electrode was calibrated against a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) in H2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 at 25 ± 1°C. Double-layer capacitance measurements

The Cdl values were used to normalize jBr2. CV measurements were conducted in 1 M KBr using a three-electrode cell equipped with an Ag/AgCl reference electrode and a carbon paper counter electrode. CVs of the MPc/CNT catalysts were recorded at scan rates of 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, and 80 mV s−1. The capacitive current density difference (Δj = ja − jc) at the middle of the potential window was plotted against the scan rates, in which ja and jc were the anodic and cathodic current densities, respectively. The slope of the linear fit was equal to twice the Cdl, from which the ECSA was estimated.

Instrumentations

XRD patterns were recorded by using a Philips X’Pert Pro Super diffractometer with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å). The XPS were recorded on a Thermo NEXSA G2 spectrometer with an excitation source of monochromatized Al Ka (hv = 1486.6 eV). The pass energy for all spectra was set to 40 eV. Computational methods

All DFT calculations were carried out using the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)38. The projector-augmented-wave (PAW) method was employed to describe the ionic cores, while the exchange–correlation interactions were treated within the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation39–41. The plane-wave basis set was expanded with an energy cutoff of 400 eV. Brillouin zone sampling was performed using a 3 × 3 × 1 k-point mesh. During geometry optimization and vibrational frequency analysis, all atomic positions were fully relaxed until the force on each atom was less than 0.02 eV/Å. The computational models of FePc, NiPc, and CuPc supported on a single-layer carbon substrate were constructed in a periodic supercell with dimensions of 19.7 × 19.7 × 30.0 Å (α = 90°, β = 90°, γ = 120°) (details in Supplementary Data 1).
