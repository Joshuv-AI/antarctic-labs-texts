Transcriptional signatures associated with female receptivity and longevity in genetically male-sterile wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)
Authors: Ryan Whitford, Ute Baumann, Xiujuan Yang, Matthew R Tucker, Kewin Ogink, Faezeh Mahdavi Mazdeh, Indeewari Dissanayake, Mark W Davey, Antje Rohde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWidespread deployment of wheat hybrids has been limited by high seed production costs in a commercial environment. This is due to poor outcrossing characteristics of wheat which can, in part, be attributed to poor female organ receptivity to airborne pollen. Here we underpin a detailed characterization of cross-pollination seed set progression in both emasculated wild-type, and a genetically male-sterile wheat line with a comprehensive RNA-Seq and co-expression analysis. We identify three clear phases in the fertilization window: stigma development and onset of receptivity, effective pollination period, and senescence onset and subsequent tissue deterioration. By profiling female-specific marker genes, we determined that physical emasculation of wild-type ‘cv. Chris’ (Ms1) advances female development 2 to 3 days relative to genic male sterility (ms1d). Gene networks linked to gibberellic acid homeostasis were found associated with styloid and stigma hair elongation, and increased expression of peroxidases associated with peak receptivity (3 days after floret gaping). Further, changes in the expression of exocyst complex components coincided with the onset of stigma hair senescence. Together the applied clustering and differential expression strategy identified biologically meaningful expression signatures, particularly in stigma hairs, that correlate with the effective pollination period and the observed cross-pollination seed set. Overall, the datasets represent a valuable resource to help identify and/or engineer improved cross-pollination seed set, and ultimately leading to greater insights into this complex biological process and a more cost-effective hybrid seed production system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41612-1.Keywords: RNASeq, Male sterility, Ms1, Stigma, Receptivity, Effective pollination period, Hybrid breeding, WheatSubject terms: Biotechnology, Genetics, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a major food source that globally provides approximately 20% of our carbohydrates and protein1. However, despite focussed efforts, increases in wheat yields have stagnated over the last decade (< 1% p.a)2. The ability to exploit hybrid vigour (heterosis) has proven exceptionally successful in driving crop yield gains and stability in other crops, with maize production, for example, doubling since the introduction of the first hybrids in the 1930s till 19553, equivalent to an annual ~ 2% yield improvement4.

The commercial deployment of wheat hybrids is limited by low seed production efficiencies which has typically been attributed to poor male pollinators or the inability to effectively synchronise male and female flowering5,6. However, few studies describe the roles of female organs in hybrid seed production efficiencies7,8. More recently it has been shown that wheat hybrid seed production is dependent on the duration of female flowering and ultimately pollen receptivity7,9–11. Furthermore, GWAS studies for both male (i.e., pollen mass and shedding, anther length, extrusion, and visual anther extrusion) and female floral traits indicate that poor receptivity can be attributed to reduced gaping (i.e., the duration and degree of floret openness) as well as to stigma length12,13. The presence of large lodicules, soft lemma/palea, pistil size/shape and viability have also all been implicated in stigma hair exertion and exposure (presentation) and therefore pollen receptivity14–18.

Studies in hybrid rice indicate that stigma presentation outside the lemma and palea is a significant contributor to airborne pollen capture and hybrid seed production yields19,20, with these yields largely correlating with stigma and style length21–29. To date, genes underlying four stigma length QTLs have been identified and encode molecular functions related to gene transcription, hormone biosynthesis and signalling30–33. Similarly, genes underlying grain weight and size QTLs have also been demonstrated to affect stigma exertion and to encode for proteins of diverse molecular functions including G-protein signalling and genes responsible for the hormone - responsive transcriptional regulation of cell elongation and proliferation34–39. Taken together this suggests stigma presentation is a central component of successful cross-pollination.

In wheat, stigma receptivity is strongly influenced by temperature and humidity and typically persists for 7–13 days8,16,17. The onset and duration of receptivity can be quantified by net seed set or by histochemical detection of enzymatic activities40–43. Among these, stigma-specific class III peroxidases (SSPs) are a hallmark of receptivity41,44 and are thought to facilitate auxin-mediated cell wall loosening required for pollen tube penetration45.

The duration of receptivity, also called the effective pollination period (EPP), terminates with senescence and is characterised by loss of stigma hair turgor and integrity, cellular collapse14,46–49, and activation of canonical programmed cell death (PCD) pathways47–52, together with regulated exocytosis in stigma hairs53.

In Arabidopsis, components of the exocytotic machinery play key roles in this process: Exo70A1 is required for pollination54,55. Exo84c modulates senescence-associated autophagy and loss of Exo84c function prolongs the EPP and increases seed set48.

Collectively, these findings from Arabidopsis, rice and maize highlight molecular pathways that regulate stigma receptivity and longevity; however, in wheat—a monocot with a strongly self-pollinating floral architecture—identifying genotypes with improved cross-pollination seed set requires a deeper understanding of the mechanisms involved in female organ presentation, receptivity and lifespan.

Here we present a comparative, developmental, RNA-Seq and co-expression analysis along with a detailed phenotypic characterization of seed set to identify gene networks linked to specific stages of pistil and stigma hair development in both wild-type and genetically male sterile (ms1d) spring wheat cv. ‘Chris’. Key gene networks identified include, (1) GID1a mediated gibberellic acid (GA) signalling, implicated in stigma hair elongation and maturation; (2) Myb-type transcriptional regulation of receptivity-related peroxidase expression; and (3) exocytotic components related to stigma hair senescence. We anticipate that these findings will inform the future identification and/or generation of improved variants (allelic) necessary for breeding female wheat lines capable of cost-effective hybrid seed production.

Results

Cross-pollination seed set in wheat peaks 3 days post gaping before the onset of senescence between 7 and 10 days post gaping

In a first step, we determined female EPP by manual pollination of male sterile (ms1d) spikes with wild-type pollen at different developmental time points. Maximum seed set amounted to 60% of the respective self-pollinated wild-type control Chris (Ms1), higher than the < 50% typically observed in hybrid production using cytoplasmic male sterile females across multiple locations and years (Fig. 1A)56. Investigation of spikes pollinated from Waddington scale stage W9 until 17 days post gaping showed that seed set peaked at 3 days post gaping, coinciding with the peak of pistil development46,57. In agreement with Gupta et al. (2015), this peak seed set is characterized by maximal stigma hair peroxidase and esterase activity, as shown histochemically (Fig. 1B-D). Seed set dropped to 14.5% of the corresponding wild-type controls at 10 days post gaping, coinciding with the visual onset of pistil and stigma hair deterioration/senescence and supported by Evans Blue vitality (Fig. 1E) and Toluidine Blue staining (Fig. 2D) of isolated pistils.

At W9, pistils of ms1d were observed to contain ovules between developmental stage 7 and 8, as per Wilkinson et al., 2019 (Fig. 2A) i.e. just before embryo sac enclosure of the antipodal, central and egg cells. Between 3 and 7 days post gaping, gametophytes had developed a highly vacuolated embryo sac with a slight expansion of the nucellus (ovule stage 10), and at 10 days post gaping, atrophied ovary wall cells were noted (from late ovule stage 10 till cell death)58 These results helped to define the 3 key developmental stages outlined in Fig. 2B-D. Interestingly, ms1d pistils were observed to be delayed in development relative to emasculated Ms1 pistils from 3 days post gaping onwards, as indicated by the presence of more vacuolated nucellar tissue and embryo sacs (SI Fig. 1A-C).

Global transcriptional changes across pistil, styloid and stigma hair development

RNA-Seq of whole seedlings, individual whole anthers, and isolated mature pollen was additionally carried out to provide tissue type controls (SI Table 1). Data quality control (SI Fig. 2), filtering and subsequent analyses were performed according to the flowchart outlined in SI Fig. 3A.

The total number of non-redundant high confidence (HC) gene models that were differentially expressed (FDR < 0.01) in at least one tissue-developmental stage-genotype pair-wise comparison for pistil and stigma samples, was 70,830, whereas for pollen and seedling samples combined it was 56,666 of the 107,891 annotated Chinese Spring HC gene models. These represent 65.6% and 52.5% of the Chinese Spring HC gene models respectively.

Using the top 4000 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from this analysis, a multidimensional scaling (MDS) of global mean gene expressions clearly separated the different tissue types from each other (Fig. 3A), with male tissues (whole anthers and pollen) clustering separately from each other as well as from both whole seedlings and female tissues (whole pistils, styloid/stigma hairs). Whole pistil samples co-clustered with styloids/stigma hairs since the whole pistil samples also contain these tissues.

Importantly, the MDS in Fig. 3B elegantly captures the temporal developmental changes of tissue gene expression levels. When clustering female tissues alone, whole pistil expressions cluster separately from styloid/stigma hair samples whilst displaying a similar developmental trend over time (Fig. 3B). Comparing the global expression of whole pistil and styloid/stigma hair samples in emasculated Ms1 and sterile ms1d, shows that the differences in developmental gene expression between the two genotypes only start to emerge 3 to 10 days post gaping, but is not present in the preceding W8 and W9 time points (Fig. 3B). This clustering suggests that ms1d-derived female tissues are at a slightly delayed developmental stage relative to those derived from emasculated Ms1 florets when sampled post W9, in accordance with the observed delays in embryo development (Fig. 2).

ms1d delays development of female organs relative to emasculated wildtype controls and displays an extended EPP

We further substantiated the observed differences in Ms1 and ms1d female gametophyte development by MDS analysis (Figs. 2 and 3B, respectively) using orthologues of well described developmental markers involved in the development of female reproductive organs in barley and Arabidopsis (SI Table 2 A and references therein).

Wheat orthologues of the barley ovule specific B-Sister MADS-box transcription factor HvMADS31 (TaBS-A2, TaBS-B2 and TaBS-D2)59,60 were found to have pistil - specific transcript accumulation from W8 to 3 days post gaping followed thereafter by a reduction in expression in sterile ms1d samples (SI Table 2 A). In emasculated Ms1-derived samples, however, all three TaBS homeologues already displayed a significant reduction in transcript abundance starting at W9, suggesting that ovule development is delayed in ms1d relative to emasculated Ms1.

Wheat orthologues of barley stigma hair specification and differentiation genes HvSHI/Lks2, TaSHI1-A2, TaSHI1-B2, and TaSHI1-D261 all exhibited transcript accumulation in pistil samples at the point at which stigma hairs are initiating and elongating (W8, W9). This was followed by a significant reduction in transcript abundance in both ms1d and emasculated Ms1 samples (SI Table 2 A).

Differences in the timing of transcript expression between the two genotypes are, however apparent in styloid/stigma hair samples. For example, the reduction in expression of TaSHI1-A2 and TaSHI1-D2 that occurs 3 and 7 days post gaping in ms1d samples, only took place 7 and 10 days post gaping relatively in emasculated Ms1 samples. Similar findings were observed to hold true for the HvSHI-related wheat TaVRS2 and its homeologues (SI Table 2 A).

The period of stigma receptivity ends with the onset of senescence that leads to cellular atrophication and death characterised by upregulation of a set of senescence, exocytosis and PCD-related genes48,51,55,62. Wheat orthologues of a suite of Arabidopsis exocytosis and PCD genes (EXO84b, RNS3, PASPA, and SCPL; SI Table 2 A) are significantly more abundant in both whole pistils and isolated styloid/stigma hairs 7 to 10 days post gaping, with signatures for several of their respective proteins detectable in mature stigmas (a stage whereby anthers are yellow and > 3.4 mm)63. Again, this change in expression was delayed in ms1d tissues relative to emasculated Ms1.

Taken together, the histochemical and tissue-specific gene expression changes, indicate that the developmental differences between ms1d and Ms1 emasculated samples likely stem from a slower female organ development in ms1d relative to emasculated Ms1, even though there were no obvious macromorphological differences.

To further understand the basis for the developmental difference, we compared all DEGs in whole pistils from emasculated Ms1 and ms1d at the earliest timepoint post emasculation (3 days post gaping). A total of 473 DEGs were identified (SI Table 2B), with an over-representation in Ms1 samples of genes implicated in general photosynthesis related processes including electron transport and ATP biosynthesis relative to ms1d pistils (SI Table 2 C). Perhaps surprisingly, there was no significant enrichment of pathways linked to stress or wounding responses in the emasculated Ms1 samples (SI Table 2 C) suggesting that the developmental differences are not linked to stress responses.

However, genes encoding proteins that bind gibberellins (SI Table 2 C), and more specifically, four genes encoding GID1a receptors were enriched and exhibited an accelerated expression profile in emasculated Ms1 relative to ms1d pistils (SI Table 2 A, B). It is known that anthers, and more specifically tapetal cells are major sites of bioactive gibberellin (GA) biosynthesis64–66 and likely serve as the source of GA for the surrounding floral organs67. Therefore, since ms1d (unlike emasculated Ms1) has anthers and functional tapetal cells68, the earlier expression of GID1a receptor genes in emasculated pistils could be a consequence of a reduced availability of bioactive GA. GA perception via GID1 is known to promote degradation of DELLA proteins, thereby releasing growth-related transcriptional responses69,70. Accordingly, delayed GID1a expression in ms1d pistils may be associated with prolonged DELLA-mediated growth restraint, potentially influencing downstream processes such as pistil elongation, stigma hair development, and progression toward peak receptivity. Shifts in GA signalling may also indirectly modulate broader hormonal networks involved in floral organ maturation71,72.

Overall, we conclude that peak receptivity for ms1d pistils occurs 3 days post gaping, coinciding with egg cell maturity, whereas in emasculated Ms1 pistils, peak receptivity occurs approximately 2–3 days earlier. Receptivity (or EPP) ceases with the onset of senescence, which in ms1d pistils starts 7 and 10 days post gaping, and 3–7 days post gaping for emasculated Ms1 pistils. Analysis of DEGs suggests that the faster development in Ms1 pistils is unlikely to be a consequence of stress-responses induced by the physical removal of male tissues, but rather due to the loss of stamen-derived hormonal signal(s). We further speculate that stamen-derived GA may be central to the faster development of Ms1 pistils given its association with stigma development. As such it may therefore represent a potential route to help modify the EPP.

Gene network analysis at peak receptivity reveals modules enriched for peroxidases and gibberellic acid related genes

To identify genes associated with receptivity to cross pollination, and thus ultimately fertilisation and seed set, we focussed our analyses on the DEGs in ms1d samples only.

Across all developmental stages of female tissue, the total number of non-redundant, differentially expressed (FDR < 0.01) high confidence (HC) gene models present in at least one female tissue-developmental stage-genotype pair-wise comparison was 57,728. This represents 53.5% of the 107,891 annotated HC genes present in the Chinese Spring v2 genome (SI Table 3).

We first conducted co-expression network analysis to identify individual co-expressed gene modules and potential transcription factor-target gene relationships as outlined in SI Fig. 3B. Due to the large total number of possible genic interactions in this set of DEGs (53.5% of the genome) we restricted weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to the top 20% of genes with the greatest expression variation (i.e. 28,672) (SI Table 4). Here, 9,096 genes present in 8 out of a total of 25 co-expression modules were significantly correlated (p-value < 0.05) with transcriptional changes in pistil and/or styloid/stigma hair samples between W9 and 3 days post gaping, i.e., the period of peak receptivity (Table 1). Of these 7657 HC gene models that were prioritized in the co-expression analysis, 7,098 HC genes were independently found to be differentially expressed in stigma hairs and/or pistils.

For the 8 co-expression modules correlating with peak receptivity, we carried out a GO enrichment analysis for the top 10% of genes with the most interactions in each module. As summarised in Table 1, this highlighted a broad range of biological processes, from lipid metabolism and lipid transport through to cell wall modification and hormonal responses.

Next, we asked which of these genes have expression dynamics that follow a similar pattern to seed-set during peak receptivity. We correlated the set of 7,098 differentially expressed and co-expressed HC genes to the cross-pollination seed-set values (Fig. 1A). Both normalised expression patterns as well as seed-set measurements were K-means clustered (Fig. 4) leading to the identification of 904 genes whose expression pattern clustered with 7 of the 8 seed set data points. These 904 genes belonged to 7 out of the 8 prioritized WGCNA modules (Table 1) and included 60 transcription factors (SI Table 5), as well as an enrichment (relative to the 7,098 input sequences) for genes and processes associated with stigma hair receptivity and elongation. Of particular interest are 24 genes annotated as peroxidases and 61 annotated as GA-related (SI Table 5). Together, the combined application of clustering and differential expression identified candidates with meaningful expression signatures, particularly in stigma hairs, that correlate with EPP and the observed cross-pollination seed set.

For each of the seven WGCNA modules containing genes clustering with seed set, we carried out GO enrichment analysis to identify biological processes enriched among the individual gene sets. Three of the seven co-expression modules (‘purple’, ‘brown’ and ‘yellow’, SI Table 6) showed a significant enrichment for (1) hydrogen peroxide catabolic process, (2) positive regulation of hydrogen peroxide biosynthetic process and, (3) regulation of hydrogen peroxide biosynthetic process. These three modules contained 13 bona fide extracellular peroxidases as determined by the presence of a polypeptide secretory signal (SignalP prediction), and eight were present in the yellow module alone.

These are encouraging results considering that extracellular stigma hair peroxidase activity is recognised as an indicator of stigma receptivity73(Fig. 1B and C). Therefore, within the yellow module we looked to identify transcription factors for which these eight peroxidases could be putative targets using gene network analysis (GENIE3). Of interest was TraesCS2B03G1541300 which is highly abundant in styloid/stigma hairs (SI Table 5), but we also identified a single Myb-type transcription factor (TraesCS3B03G0305500) as a potential regulator of three of the yellow module extracellular peroxidases (TraesCS2B03G1541300, TraesCS1A03G0790600, and TraesCS1D03G0756500) (SI Table 5; SI Table 7). We therefore speculate that these transcription factors may represent an important regulator of stigma hair peroxidase expression and as such, could help determine the duration of stigma receptivity.

GA signalling and biosynthesis are associated with pistil shape, cell wall loosening and elongation of stigma hairs.

Three co-expression modules (red, yellow, and magenta, SI Table 6) showed significant enrichment for response to GA, GA metabolic process, and regulation of GA biosynthetic processes, respectively.

Since it is known that GA application improves stigma exertion and hybrid seed production yields74–78, we sought to understand dynamic changes in GA biosynthesis, catabolism, and response in ms1d by mapping all expressed GA-metabolic genes across the relevant KEGG pathway (Fig. 5A) throughout pistil and styloid/stigma hair development.

Cheng et al. (2019) functionally annotated 19 gibberellic acid stimulated regulators (GASRs) from wheat transcripts (SI Table 8 A). Analysis of their expression shows that GA-induced gene transcription can be categorised into two general patterns, the first exhibiting peak transcription at W8 and W9 corresponding with the timing of stigma hair initiation and elongation, and the second peak coinciding with peak receptivity and maximal cross-pollination seed set (3 days post gaping) (Fig. 5B). This second, most interesting pattern is typified by well-described GA-responsive transcripts such as TaGASR33, 34 and 36 (previously annotated as TaGASR7, SI Table 8 A)79–81 and for which TaGASR34, here the most abundant of the 3 transcripts, was identified within the 904 seed-set correlated gene set (described as GASR7, SI Table 5).

These TaGASR genes contain GA-responsive cis-elements in their promoters and are key regulators of grain shape79–82. Since pistil shape post-pollination is a likely contributor to final grain shape, we speculate that TaGASR genes could contribute to determining pistil morphology and potentially stigma presentation; e.g., longer thinner pistils could facilitate exertion of styloids and stigma hairs from the opening between the palea and lemma, as has been observed for the GS3/GW8 grain shape genes in rice36.

GA-responsive alpha amylase Amy2 transcription also displays the second developmental expression pattern, with Amy2-2 (TraesCS7D03G089330, SI Table 8 A) also being a member of set of the 904 seed-set correlated genes (SI Table 5). Amy2 genes also have functional GA-responsive cis-elements in their promoters83,84 (Fig. 5B), and AMY2 proteins have also been detected in mature stigmas63.

Expansins are members of a large GA-responsive gene family that facilitate cell expansion85,86. Expression of eight expansins was found to be correlated with cross-pollination seed set (SI Table 5), and in fact we found that. 35 of a total of 80 functionally annotated expansins display the second general GA-responsive transcription pattern (SI Table 8 A; Fig. 5B) with peak expression 3–7 days post gaping, corresponding to the phase of fully extended styloid and stigma hairs.

Taken together, these findings suggest a role for GA signalling in controlling pistil shape and potentially stigmatic hair cell wall loosening and elongation. In this context, the GA signalling gene most highly correlated with our cross-pollination seed set data is TraesCS2D03G0307200, one of the 23 annotated GID1a receptor homologs present in the wheat genome (SI Table 2 A, SI Table 8 A). GID1a expression is particularly abundant in styloids and stigma hairs relative to whole pistils, and the mature protein is also detected in stigmas63.

Co-expression gene network analysis for the onset of senescence in ms1d stigma hairs

The end of the EPP is marked by the onset of senescence (Fig. 1A), a process that has been well-described for reproductive developmental processes and organs87,88.

From the co-expression networks established above (SI Table 4) we focussed on modules showing significant correlation with seed set from 7 days post gaping in stigma hairs. Two co-expression modules (blue, royal blue, SI Table 9) containing 6,020 genes correlated with the decreased seed set, directly proceeding the increased expression of the markers of stigma hair senescence listed in SI Table 2 A. For the top 10% of these genes having the most interactions, GO term analysis exhibited an enrichment for ‘response to oxygen containing compound’ in addition to several senescence-related GO terms (SI Table 10).

When these 6,020 genes from the two co-expression modules were intersected with the 56,391 differentially expressed HC genes, we identified 4,526 genes which can be considered as co-expressed, differentially expressed, and linked to the onset of senescence. Clustering this set of 4,526 genes with stigma hair expression values and the 8 seed-set data points identified 690 genes whose expression pattern clustered with 6 out of the 8 seed set data points (SI Table 11 A). Of particular interest within this set of 690 genes were examples of the evolutionary conserved exocyst complex.

The identified exocyst components are likely interactors with Exo84c, whose loss-of-function mutants in Arabidopsis exhibit delayed stigma hair senescence autophagy and improved cross-pollination seed-set by an extension of the EPP48. Specifically, exocyst components SEC15A (TraesCS4D03G0659200) and EXO70 (TraesCS5B03G0336400, TraesCS7A03G0599900, TraesCS7B03G0428700) all show preferential stigma hair expression and reduced abundance 3–10 days post gaping and correlate with a reduced capacity for seed-set (SI Table 11 A).

Transcription factor-target gene analysis (GENIE3) for these exocyst components identified a total of eight potential regulators for SEC15A alone (SI Table 7), including a single homeogroup of auxin response factors, three MYB transcription factors and one each of a zinc finger and basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor, all of which similarly exhibited stigma hair preferential expression (SI Table 11B). These genes correlate with the onset of senescence, and we suggest that their delayed/diminished expression could be involved in prolonging the EPP.

As with our analysis of genes coinciding with peak receptivity and cross-pollination seed set, the results for genes associated with stigma senescence and reduced capacity for seed set support our clustering and co-expression approach to identify biologically relevant candidate genes, despite the complexity of the biological processes involved.

Discussion

Cost-effective female seed production is a pre-requisite for a commercially viable hybrid seed production system. Floral morphology, particularly of stigma hairs, as well as the duration of receptivity and the timing of the onset of senescence are all important factors contributing to EPP and thus ultimately cross-pollination seed set and yield.

In this study we show that peak receptivity as defined by the highest cross-pollination seed set occurs approximately 3 days post floret gaping in a genic male sterile line (ms1d), coinciding with maximal pistil development/stigma presentation46 as well as with maximal secreted peroxidase expression. We also show that under controlled growth conditions, ms1d delays female development after this 3 day peak by approximately 2–3 days relative to physically emasculated wild type. Whether the observed developmental divergence is also observed when male sterility is induced by chemical hybridizing agent(s) or cytoplasmic male sterility is worthy of future investigation as would assays to assess pollen tube growth dynamics relative to pistil development.

A detailed and precise phenotypic characterisation of the female ms1d seed set at specified days post- floret gaping helped us to define the stages of female gametophyte development as well as the phases of pistil and stigma hair development and to select samples for whole transcriptome profiling. The analysis of the RNA-seq data derived from these female reproductive tissues presented considerable complexity due to the polyploid nature of the wheat genome, the large number of genes involved, high transcript redundancy, and the tissue-specific expression dynamics. To navigate these challenges, a multi-tiered analytical framework was implemented (outlined in SI Fig. 3B). The approach yielded a focused set of genes including their potential transcriptional regulators with possible roles in stigma presentation, receptivity, and senescence, offering new insights into the molecular regulation of female fertility in wheat. Together this represents a valuable genomics resource to further mine genes associated with female reproductive organ development.

A key limitation of this transcriptomic study is the absence of functional validation for candidate genes. While their expression patterns are strongly associated with discrete phases of stigma receptivity and longevity, future work combining qRT-PCR and spatial (in situ) expression validation, mutant analysis, transgenic approaches, or targeted perturbation experiments will be required to confirm their functional roles. More specifically, future research efforts should now focus on the functional characterisation of those candidates associated with female organ development and presentation (e.g. GA hormonal homoeostasis), and the EPP, including markers for peak receptivity peroxidases and stigma hair specific senescence genes. This can ultimately lead to the identification and/or engineering of novel variants that lead to improved female presentation, receptivity and longevity. For example, a role for GA signalling in wheat stigma presentation is likely considering Rht14 has recently been linked to longer styloids89. In this context it would be interesting to see whether prolonging the temporal expression of GID1a receptor could improve stigma presentation by extending the period of styloid and stigma hair elongation, which in turn could result in a greater surface area available to capture airborne pollen. Alternatively, it remains to be seen if changing the relative balance between different bioactive GAs in both styloids and stigma hairs could promote cell elongation and therefore stigma presentation, as has been described in rice90. Additional gene targets from the data sets presented here may also open other avenues to modify EPP, improve hybrid seed production efficiencies and therefore reduce costs, an important requirement for the commercial realization of wheat hybrids. Ultimately however, improved hybrid seed set likely requires optimisation of both male and female specific processes.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The male-sterile mutant ms1d bread wheat (Triticum aestivum) spring cv. Chris91,92 was procured from the Australian Grains Genbank (Horsham, Victoria) as accession number AGG22851 and genotypically validated in this study using ms1d specific SNP markers68. Five wheat seeds were sown in 20-cm pots in growth room facilities (Narrabri Refrigeration & Air Conditioning, Narrabri, Australia) at the University of Adelaide, with temperature and a long day light setting with metal halides at 22 °C/18°C (18 h day/6 h night) till flowering. Soil consisted of coco-peat mixture (mixed by SARDI Greenhouse Services, Plant Research Centre) containing a 1:1 ratio of coir fiber to quarried drainage sand from Waikerie. This soil mix was steam sterilised and supplemented with a basal rate of fertiliser (Osmocote, Scotts Australia, slow release NPK fertiliser plus selected other soil amendments). At each sampling point, four spikelets from the middle of spikes from all the plants were harvested and flash frozen immediately in liquid nitrogen in a 50 ml sterile plastic tube, then stored in a −80 °C freezer prior to further study.

DNA Isolation, KASP-based genotyping

Leaf tissue DNA was extracted from pulverised freeze-dried samples in a 96-well plate format93 using an Alpha 1–2 LDplus Freeze-dryer (Martin Christ Gefriertrocknungsanlagen GmbH, Germany) and a 2010 Geno/Grinder® (SPEX SamplePrep, USA) for tissue pulverisation. Genotyping for ms1d used previously designed KASP™ primers with assays being performed according to the manufacturer’s instruction using KASP™ mix reagent68 using a SNPline and Kraken™ software program (LGC Biosearch Technologies, UK).

Tissue sample collection

Emerged spikes from plants genotypically identified to be homozygous for the ms1d mutation were covered with glassine bags, whereas those deemed to be wild-type segregants (Ms1/Ms1) were hand emasculated then bagged at Waddington stage 8.5 (green anthers) using forceps ensuring as little damage to surrounding floral tissues as reasonably possible. When compared to standard emasculation procedures, the palea and lemma were not trimmed during this procedure, ensuring as much of the floral structure remained intact as possible. On successive days post emasculation, whole pistil samples were isolated and placed in 2 mL snap-lock Eppendorf tubes then immediately flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Pistils were carefully removed by lifting from the carpel base using forceps to minimise tissue damage. Sampling was undertaken at the same time each day (1–2 h) to minimise circadian effects. All samples were stored in a −80 °C freezer prior to RNA extraction.

Fruit set/seed formation assessment from manual cross-pollination

A minimum of eight glassine bag covered (pre-anthesis) spikes from primary and secondary tillers of 4 independent plants each genotypically identified to be homozygous for the ms1d mutation were hand-pollinated at either Waddington stage 9 or successive days post gaping till day 17. Glassine bags were immediately re-applied after pollination using the detached spike method (www.wheat-training.com). Freshly dehisced pollen was derived from genotypically identified Ms1 individuals of staggered planting and flowering time. Pollinated spikes remained covered until seed harvest. Genotypically identified Ms1 control individuals were bagged pre-anthesis through till seed set. Spikelet fertility was assessed based on counting seed from 8 independent spikes. The two basal and two apical spikelets per spike were eliminated from analysis due to their incomplete development. Total seed set and numbers of florets were counted on a per spike basis. The fertility score for each spike or plant was calculated as follows:

Seed set/Spikelet = Total number of seed per spike.

Total number of spikelets per spike.

Histochemical assays and imaging for cell receptivity and vitality

Whole pistils were isolated by placing forceps under the base of the pistil and lifting vertically therefore minimizing tissue damage and associated ROS production. Tissues were either used directly for Evans Blue staining or placed immediately in FAA fixative and vacuum infiltrated for 5–10 min at −0.04 MPa.

Evans Blue stain – whole pistils were placed in 1 mL of 0.25% aqueous Evans Blue for 10 min and gently agitated94. Pistils were then drained and rinsed with 30–40 mL of deionized water or until no further blue was eluted from the tissue.

TMB stain - Samples were removed from the FAA fixative and placed in 50mM Tris-Acetate pH5.0 before adding to a final concentration 1% fresh H2O2 and 0.1 mg mL− 1 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine-HCl (TMB). Stain was allowed to develop for 20–30 min before removal and placed in only 50 mM Tris-Acetate pH 5.0.

Non-specific esterase stain - Samples were removed from FAA fixative, rinsed then placed in 1.5 mL of 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). 30 µL of 60 mg mL− 1 alpha-naphthyl acetate in acetone and 60 µL of 2 mg mL-1 Fast blue RR salt was added to the sample, then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, after which samples were removed, rinsed in the phosphate buffer, then returned to the FAA fixative.

Toluidine blue stain and sectioning - FAA fixed samples were dehydrated in a series of 70, 80, 90, and 100% (v/v) ethanol and embedded in Technovit 7100 resin (Kulzer Technique) as described by the manufacturer. Samples were sectioned to a thickness of 1.5 μm on a Leica Ultramicrotome. Sections were then stained in 0.5% Toluidine Blue (w/v) for 20 min and mounted for visualization by light microscopy.

Whole or sectioned pistils were imaged either using a light dissecting microscope illuminated with white light and fitted with a Dino-Lite ocular digital camera or a Nikon Ni-E optical compound microscope. Images were processed using DinoXcope software version 2.0.4.

RNA isolation, library construction, sequencing, quality control and differential expression analysis

Total RNA (SI Table 1) was extracted from each of non-pollinated ms1d whole wheat pistils sampled at W8, W9, and 3, 7 and 10-days post gaping plus styloid and non-pollinated stigma hair samples isolated at 3, 7 and 10 days post gaping. Non-pollinated pistils from Ms1 (W8, W9) and Ms1 emasculated whole pistils at 3, 7 and 10 days post gaping, Ms1 whole seedlings, and Ms1 mature pollen (3-days post gaping) and anthers (W8, W9, 3 days post gaping) were also sampled to serve as corresponding controls. For all stages except pollen, there were between 4 and 7 biological replicates (SI Table 1). Total RNA was extracted using Spectrum Plant Total RNA isolation kit (Merk KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was removed by digestion with Turbo DNase protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific Australia Pty Ltd, Scoresby, Australia). RNA quality was evaluated using a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer followed by HS Qubit Fluorometric Quantification for both RNA and dsDNA (both from Thermo Fisher Scientific Australia Pty Ltd, Melbourne, Australia). RNA quality was finally assessed using a Perkin Elmer LabChip® GX Touch™ Nucleic Acid Analyzer (PerkinElmer, Sydney, Australia). RNA samples were then immediately stored in a − 80 °C freezer before being sent on dry ice for further processing, library construction (TruSeq Stranded Total RNA), and sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4) at the Australian Genome Research Facility (Melbourne, Australia).

Sequencing of all 106 samples’ libraries yielded a total of 9.51 billion paired-end reads and an average of 27.1 Gbp for each library (SI Table 12). Reads were cleaned by adapter trimming and removal of low-quality regions then quantified for subsequent differential gene expression analysis for all samples used in multidimensional scaling (MDS) analysis using RStudio95 with packages Rsubread96, edgeR and limma97 (SI Fig. 3A, refer to SI Methodologies for further details). DEGs for only female samples were alternatively identified using R package DESeq2 (version 1.38.3, SI Fig. 3B)98 at a FDR (false-discovery rate) < 0.01.

Gene co-expression network analysis

Genes with a total count of greater than 10 across all samples were selected and variance stabilized. The 20% most-varying genes (28,672 genes) were used to infer a co-expression network using WGCNA (version 1.72.1)99. A signed network with soft-thresholding power of 14 was generated.

Gene regulatory network construction

Genes with a total count of greater than 10 across all samples were selected and variance stabilized. The 20% most-varying genes (28,672 genes) were used to infer a gene regulatory network using GENIE3 (version 1.20.0)100. Random forest method was used with default parameters with expression matrix for 28,672 genes and a list of 6,056 wheat transcription factors, where 1,770 of the transcription factors were present in the gene expression matrix. The transcription factor list was compiled by taking the union of wheat transcription factor lists from PlantTFDBv5.0101,102 and https://github.com/Borrill-Lab/WheatFlagLeafSenescence103 after gene ID conversion to IWGSC refseqv2.1 which resulted in a list of 6,056 wheat transcription factors. The regulatory relationships that had a weight measure of > 0.005 in the GENIE3 network were considered further. The output was filtered to only include the transcription factor-target gene relationships for the 60 TFs that are differentially expressed and co-expressed and present in the seed-set data cluster 1.

K-means clustering of the gene expression values with seed-set data

The gene expression values corresponding to the pistil samples from the set of genes that are present in a module that showed significant correlation with pistil and/or stigma hairs samples at peak receptivity, as well as differentially expressed were considered for this analysis (7,098 genes). The gene expression values (in transcripts per million, TPM for pistil samples) and seed-set number per time point (8 replicates per time point) corresponding to W9, 3, 7, and 10 days post gaping were z-score normalized. K-means clustering with K value set to 7 and distance metric set to ‘Eucledian’ was done using functions within the pheatmap R package104. Similarly, the gene expression values (in transcripts per million, TPM for pistil samples) of the set of 4,526 senescence-related genes that were co-expressed and differentially expressed and the seed-set number per time point (8 replicates per time point) corresponding to W9, 3, 7, and 10 days post gaping were z-score normalized followed by K-means clustering with K value set to 7 and distance metric set to ‘Eucledian’ to prioritize candidates related to onset of senescence.

GO enrichment analysis

DEGs were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment as implemented within the GOseq R package. The analysis is based on Wallenius’ noncentral hypergeometric distribution105 to adjust for gene length bias in the DEGs. GO enrichment analysis for individual co-expression and network modules was performed using gProfiler106 and BMKCloud (www.biocloud.net).
