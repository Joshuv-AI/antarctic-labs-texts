KLS-3021: Innovative oncolytic virotherapy for the treatment of advanced primary and metastatic cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Kiwon Park, Jinwon Seo, Sungmin Lee, Hyesoo Kang, Joonsung Kim, Eunjin Lee, Jaeil Shin, Soon-Oh Hong, Sujeong Kim, Sun Jin Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is a highly prevalent malignancy with increasing incidence and limited therapeutic options in advanced or metastatic stages. Patients with high-risk or metastatic cSCC often experience poor outcomes, underscoring the need for novel therapeutic strategies. We investigated the potential of KLS-3021, a recombinant oncolytic vaccinia virus encoding hyaluronidase, interleukin-12, and soluble programmed cell death 1 fused to an Fc domain. KLS-3021 exhibited tumor-selective cytotoxicity and robust replication in cSCC cell lines while sparing normal keratinocytes. In an orthotopic primary cSCC model generated by intradermal injection of A-431 cells, a single intratumoral injection of KLS-3021 induced complete tumor regression. In a metastatic model involving sentinel lymph node lesions, KLS-3021 suppressed tumor progression not only at the primary site but also in metastatic lymph nodes, where viral spread was detected. Histological analyses revealed extensive hyaluronan degradation, viral dissemination, immune cell infiltration, and hallmark features of immunogenic cell death. Similar findings were observed in metastatic lymph nodes, demonstrating viral activity and tumor suppression. Collectively, these results indicate that KLS-3021 exerts potent anti-tumor efficacy in both primary and metastatic cSCC models, supporting its clinical translation as a multifunctional oncolytic virotherapy targeting the physical and immunological barriers of the tumor microenvironment.Keywords: MT: Regular Issue, oncolytic vaccinia virus, cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma, hyaluronidase, interleukin-12, soluble PD1-Fc, immunogenic cell death, virotherapy and immunotherapy

Introduction

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is the second most common subtype of non-melanoma skin cancer, with a rising incidence and considerable disease burden worldwide.1,2 Chronic ultraviolet (UV) radiation, particularly in individuals with Fitzpatrick skin types 1 and 2, is the primary risk factor, making the disease especially prevalent in fair-skinned populations.3,4,5 While early stage cSCC is effectively managed with local treatment modalities such as surgical excision and radiotherapy, advanced or metastatic cases present significant therapeutic challenges, with high recurrence rates and poor clinical outcomes.6 Conventional systemic treatments such as platinum-based chemotherapy with 5-fluorouracil and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-targeting agents, such as cetuximab, have demonstrated limited efficacy, with objective response rates (ORRs) of 15%–30% and substantial toxicity.7

In recent years, immune checkpoint blockade has emerged as a transformative strategy for advanced cSCC.8,9 Cemiplimab, an anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody, achieved an ORR of approximately 47% in the EMPOWER-CSCC-1 trial and became the first US Food and Drug Administration-approved therapy for locally advanced and metastatic cSCC.10 Pembrolizumab, another PD-1 inhibitor, demonstrated an ORR of 34% in the recurrent or metastatic cohort of the KEYNOTE-629 study, leading to its approval for advanced disease.11,12 Patients who respond to PD-1 blockade often experience durable tumor control.10,11 However, approximately ≥50% of patients with advanced cSCC do not show a response, highlighting the limitations of PD-1 blockade. This limited efficacy is largely attributed to the immunosuppressive and structurally complex tumor microenvironment (TME), which restricts immune cell infiltration and diminishes the effectiveness of conventional and immune-based therapies.13 A dense extracellular matrix (ECM), particularly rich in hyaluronan, acts as a physical barrier that limits the penetration of effector T cells and therapeutic agents, whereas resident immunosuppressive cell populations—including regulatory T cells, M2-polarized macrophages, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells—create an immunological barrier that further promotes cancer immune evasion.14,15,16,17,18 These features underscore the need for novel therapeutic approaches that not only remodels the ECM but also enhances anti-tumor immunity.

Oncolytic virotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment modality that integrates direct tumor cell lysis with the induction of systemic immune responses.19,20,21 This effect can be further enhanced by engineering viruses to express immunomodulatory transgenes. We previously developed KLS-3020, a recombinant oncolytic vaccinia virus derived from the IHD-W strain.22 KLS-3021 is a clinically optimized version of this oncolytic virus, in which all reporter genes were removed and therapeutic payloads retained. Specifically, it encodes hyaluronidase (PH-20) to degrade hyaluronan, interleukin 12 (IL-12) to enhance innate and adaptive immune responses, and soluble programmed cell death 1 (sPD1) fused (Fc) (sPD1-Fc) to locally block immune checkpoint signaling.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of KLS-3021 in orthotopic cSCC mouse models of primary tumors and metastatic tumors involving the sentinel lymph node. These models were established by intradermal injection of the human cSCC cell line A-431. Additionally, we sought to investigate the ability of KLS-3021 to overcome physical and immunologic barriers through ECM degradation, immune cell infiltration, and induction of immunogenic cell death (ICD) using histological analyses.

Results

KLS-3021 exhibits selective cytotoxicity and exclusive replication in cSCC cells

KLS-3021 was generated from the previously developed backbone virus KLS-3010, a recombinant vaccinia virus derived from the IHD-W strain, in which the three viral genes C11R, J2R, and K3L were deleted and replaced with reporter genes (LacZ, eGFP/Gpt, and DsRed2). Afterward, these reporter genes were sequentially removed and replaced with therapeutic transgenes by stepwise recombination, resulting in KLS-3021 (Figure 1A). For preclinical studies, a murine-adapted version of KLS-3021 encoding murine IL-12 and murine sPD1-Fc was developed.

(A) Schematic representation of KLS-3021 showing deletions of C11R, K3L, and J2R loci and insertions of therapeutic genes encoding PH-20, IL-12, and sPD1-Fc. (B) Cytotoxicity of KLS-3021 in human cSCC cell lines (A-431 and HSC-1) and normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEKs) was measured via CCK-8 assay at 72 h post-infection. (C) Viral replication in cSCC cell lines and NHEKs was determined via TCID50 assay at 72 h post-infection. Data was represented as mean ± SEM of three independent experiments.

We next evaluated the tumor-selective cytolytic activity and replication capacity of KLS-3021 in cSCC cell lines. In vitro cytotoxicity assays demonstrated the potent and selective cytotoxicity of KLS-3021 in cSCC cell lines, as evidenced by markedly lower ED50 values for A-431 and HSC-1 cells compared with normal human epidermal keratinocytes (NHEKs) (Figure 1B). Consistent with these findings, KLS-3021 exhibited robust replication in cSCC cells, whereas viral production was minimal in NHEKs (Figure 1C). Overall, these data indicate that KLS-3021 preferentially replicates in and lyses cSCC cells while sparing normal keratinocytes, indicating a high degree of tumor selectivity and supporting its tumor-selective oncolytic potential.

Single intratumoral injection of KLS-3021 induces complete tumor regression in orthotopic primary cSCC tumors

To explore the therapeutic efficacy of KLS-3021 in vivo, we utilized an orthotopic primary cSCC model established by intradermal inoculation of A-431 cells in the right flank of BALB/c nude mice (Figure 2A). After reaching a tumor volume of approximately 100 mm3, mice received a single intratumoral injection of the murine-adapted version of KLS-3021 (1 × 107 TCID50/head) or vehicle. Notably, KLS-3021 administration resulted in rapid and sustained tumor regression, with all treated mice achieving complete responses by day 14 (Figures 2B and 2C). Additionally, a comparable anti-tumor activity was observed at a lower dose (1 × 106 TCID50/head), similar to that achieved at the higher dose (data not shown). No treatment-related toxicity was observed during the study period (Figure 2D). Histopathological evaluation of tumors harvested on days 3 and 7 post-treatment (days post-infection [DPI] 3 and 7) revealed widespread cell death and depletion of viable tumor regions in the KLS-3021-treated group, whereas the vehicle-treated group maintained high cellularity and characteristic cSCC histology (Figures 2E and 2F).

These findings demonstrate that a single dose of KLS-3021 is sufficient to induce swift and durable tumor eradication in this orthotopic cSCC model.

KLS-3021 remodels the TME through ECM disruption and increased immune activity

Beyond direct oncolysis, we investigated whether KLS-3021 could alter the TME. Notably, KLS-3021 encodes the human PH-20 hyaluronidase, which degrades hyaluronic acid (HA), a major ECM component and physical barrier within tumors. Degradation of HA is expected to not only enable broader viral spread but also facilitate the infiltration of immune effector cells into the tumor.

Alcian blue staining, widely used to visualize HA, revealed marked HA degradation in the KLS-3021-treated group, indicating effective disruption of the physical ECM barrier (Figures 3A and 3B). Consistent with ECM degradation, immunohistochemical analysis for vaccinia virus demonstrated extensive viral spread throughout the tumors in the KLS-3021-treated group on days 3 and 7 post-treatment, whereas no vaccinia virus was detected in the vehicle-treated group (Figures 3A and 3B).

Histological analysis of the tumor microenvironment following KLS-3021 treatment

(A and B) Alcian blue staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for vaccinia virus in tumors collected on days 3 (A) and 7 (B) post-treatment. (C) IHC for F4/80+ macrophages in tumors on day 3 post-treatment. Scale bars, 300 μm; enlarged views, 100 μm. (D–F) Immunofluorescence (IF) staining for CD86+ (M1 marker), CD206+ (M2 marker), and TNF-α+ cells in tumors on day 3 post-treatment. Scale bars, 500 μm; enlarged views, 100 μm.

We next examined how these changes were associated with immune cell infiltration within the tumor. Following KLS-3021 treatment, increased infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages was observed (Figure 3C). To further characterize the phenotype and activation status of these infiltrating macrophages, immunostaining for polarization and activation markers—including CD86, CD206, and TNF-α—was performed. This analysis revealed a marked increase in CD86+ M1 phenotype (anti-tumor) macrophages and a decrease in CD206+ M2 phenotype macrophages, which are typically associated with tumor growth and metastasis (Figures 3D and 3E). In addition, TNF-α expression, with possible anti-tumor effects, was increased alongside M1 macrophage polarization, indicating a shift of the TME toward an anti-tumor inflammatory state (Figure 3F).

Together, these results indicate that KLS-3021 not only disrupts the ECM and facilitates viral spread but also enhances immune cell infiltration and shifts tumor-associated macrophage toward an activated M1 phenotype characterized by CD86 and TNF-α expression. This shift supports the establishment of an anti-tumor immune microenvironment.

KLS-3021 induces immunogenic apoptosis in orthotopic primary cSCC tumors

To determine whether KLS-3021-induced tumor regression was associated with tumor cell death, we assessed the type of apoptotic activity present in tumors on day 3 post-treatment. TUNEL staining revealed a marked increase in apoptotic cells in the KLS-3021-treated group compared with the vehicle-treated group, indicating effective induction of apoptotic cell death (Figure 4A).

Analysis of apoptotic markers and immunogenic damage-associated molecular pattern-related signals in advanced primary cSCC tumors

(A) Immunofluorescence (IF) staining for TUNEL and CD31 with DAPI in tumor sections collected on day 3 post-treatment. (B and C) IF staining for HMGB1 (B) and calreticulin (CRT; C) with DAPI in tumors on day 3 post-treatment. Scale bars, 100 μm; enlarged views, 20 μm.

Given that oncolytic viruses are frequently associated with immunogenic apoptosis, a subtype of ICD, we next examined hallmark ICD-associated molecular events. This form of cell death, often referred to as immunogenic apoptosis but more properly termed ICD, is distinguished from classical apoptosis by its ability to stimulate antitumor immune responses.23 Functionally, ICD is characterized by the release or exposure of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and calreticulin (CRT). Consistent with these roles, HMGB1 exhibited strong nuclear localization in the vehicle-treated group, whereas pronounced translocation of HMGB1 to the cytoplasm and extracellular space was observed in the KLS-3021-treated group (Figure 4B). In the vehicle-treated group, CRT staining was predominantly perinuclear with minimal membrane localization, whereas KLS-3021 induced prominent membrane localization of CRT in a ring-like or punctate pattern (Figure 4C), consistent with effective surface exposure required for immunogenic signaling.

Notably, the durability of these ICD-associated molecular changes—including TUNEL positivity, HMGB1 release, and CRT membrane exposure—was further confirmed on day 7 post-treatment (Figures S1A–S1C), supporting the sustained induction of ICD by KLS-3021. In addition, we observed that Ki-67 immunofluorescence (IF) on days 3 and 7 post-treatment demonstrated a marked reduction in proliferating tumor cells in the KLS-3021-treated group relative to controls (Figures S1D and S1E), indicating robust suppression of tumor cell proliferation following treatment. Consistently, CD31 immunostaining revealed reduced vascular density in the KLS-3021-treated tumors compared with controls, a change commonly associated with tumor regression.

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that KLS-3021 not only induces extensive apoptotic cell death and inhibits tumor cell proliferation but also triggers ICD-associated molecular events—such as the sustained release of HMGB1 and surface exposure of CRT—that enhance immune recognition of tumor cells and support the formation of anti-tumor immune microenvironment.

KLS-3021 suppresses primary and sentinel lymph node tumor growth in metastatic cSCC

Given the limited treatment options for advanced and metastatic cSCC, we established a mouse model of sentinel lymph node metastasis by inoculating luciferase-expressing A-431 cells intradermally into the dorsum of the foot, thereby generating a primary tumor with subsequent metastasis to the popliteal (sentinel) lymph node (PLN). After confirmation of sentinel lymph node metastasis by bioluminescence imaging (IVIS), mice were administered a single intratumoral injection of either KLS-3021 (1 × 107 TCID50/head) or vehicle into the primary tumor site (Figure 5A).

(B) Longitudinal quantification of total photon flux from the primary tumor and popliteal lymph node (PLN) at each time point post-treatment (n = 5 per group; ∗∗∗p < 0.001 by two-tailed t test). (C) Representative IVIS bioluminescence images showing signals from primary and nodal tumors in vehicle- and KLS-3021-treated mice at indicated time points. (D and E) Primary tumor weights (D) and PLN weights (E) measured on day 3 (n = 5 per group; ∗p < 0.05 by two-tailed t test) and day 28 (n = 10 per group; ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗∗p < 0.001 by two-tailed t test) post-treatment. All data were represented as mean ± SEM.

Tumor burden was longitudinally monitored using serial IVIS imaging. Mice treated with KLS-3021 demonstrated a marked reduction in photon flux at the primary tumor and metastatic sentinel lymph node compared with the vehicle-treated group. Quantitative analysis of total photon flux revealed a statistically significant time-dependent decrease in the KLS-3021-treated group (Figure 5B). These data were obtained from IVIS images of all animals shown in Figure 5C, which consistently demonstrated progressive loss of bioluminescent signals at the primary tumor and sentinel lymph node sites. At the primary site, tumor weights were measured on days 3 and 28 post-treatment and were significantly lower in KLS-3021-treated mice relative to controls (Figure 5D). For sentinel lymph nodes, the entire PLN weight was recorded as an indicator of metastatic tumor burden and significantly reduced in KLS-3021-treated mice compared with controls (Figure 5E).

Together, these results suggest that KLS-3021 achieves a profound and durable anti-tumor effect, resulting in near-complete regression of both primary and tumor-draining sentinel lymph node metastases in this metastatic cSCC model.

KLS-3021 spreads from primary tumors to draining lymph nodes and induces regression of metastatic tumor lesions

To further characterize the therapeutic effects of KLS-3021 in advanced metastatic lesions, we performed histological analyses of PLN, a tumor-draining sentinel lymph node, on days 3 and 28 post-treatment. In the vehicle-treated group, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of PLNs revealed persistent metastatic tumor lesions. In contrast, KLS-3021-treated PLNs showed marked tumor cell loss and regression of metastatic tumor lesions at both time points (Figures 6A and 6B). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that vaccinia virus was widely distributed throughout PLNs in the KLS-3021 group on days 3 and 28, indicating that KLS-3021 injected into the primary tumor can spread to metastatic sites (Figures 6A and 6B).

(A and B) Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) for vaccinia virus in popliteal lymph nodes (PLNs) collected on day 3 (A) and day 28 (B) post-treatment. Scale bars, 200 μm; enlarged views, 50 μm. (C and D) Immunofluorescence (IF) staining for TUNEL/CD31 (C) and Ki-67/CD31 (D) in PLNs on day 3 post-treatment. Scale bars, 100 μm.

We next investigated the mechanisms of tumor cell clearance in PLNs by performing IF analyses. TUNEL staining demonstrated a substantial increase in apoptotic cells in KLS-3021-treated PLNs relative to controls (Figure 6C). In parallel, Ki-67 staining revealed a marked reduction in proliferating tumor cells, and CD31 expression was also decreased, consistent with reduced vascular density typically associated with tumor regression (Figure 6D). Together, these results indicate that KLS-3021 promotes viral dissemination, induces apoptosis, and regress metastatic tumor lesions in sentinel lymph nodes.

Consistent with these findings in PLNs, primary tumors demonstrated similar histological changes on day 3 post-treatment (Figure S2). Histopathological evaluation of the primary tumor site on day 3 post-treatment revealed well-preserved cSCC features in the vehicle-treated group, including keratin pearls and regions of keratinization, whereas KLS-3021-treated tumors exhibited extensive tumor cell death and near-complete loss of viable tumor tissue, consistent with potent oncolytic activity (Figure S2A). Alcian blue staining revealed pronounced degradation of HA, confirming disruption of the ECM barrier (Figure S2B). This was accompanied by extensive vaccinia virus spread throughout the tumor and increased infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages (Figures S2C and S2D). Moreover, apoptotic activity was elevated, as indicated by increased TUNEL positivity, whereas tumor cell proliferation was strongly suppressed, as evidenced by reduced Ki-67 staining (Figures S2E and S2F).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that KLS-3021 can spread from the injected primary tumor and exert potent therapeutic effects in metastatic sentinel lymph nodes, as evidenced by tumor regression, apoptosis, and regression of metastatic tumor lesions. Supplementary analyses in primary tumors further corroborate these findings, showing ECM degradation, viral spread, macrophage infiltration, and consistent patterns of cell death.

Discussion

Although cSCC detected at an early stage is associated with an excellent prognosis, a substantial unmet need remains for effective treatment strategies in patients with locally advanced and metastatic cSCC. Local therapeutic modalities such as surgery, radiation, or cryotherapy often fail to prevent recurrence, and systemic therapies cannot guarantee durable tumor-free survival. Undetectable metastatic lesions at diagnosis significantly contribute to poor prognosis. While immune checkpoint blockade has provided clinical benefit for a subset of patients, most patients fail to achieve durable responses due to various resistance mechanisms within the TME, including limited immune cell infiltration, dense ECM, and the presence of immunosuppressive cell populations. These barriers are particularly pronounced in high-risk and metastatic cSCC; therefore, therapeutic approaches capable of simultaneously addressing both physical barriers and immunosuppressive environments of the TME are urgently needed to overcome the limitations of present treatments.

Our study demonstrates that KLS-3021, a genetically engineered oncolytic vaccinia virus encoding PH-20, IL-12, and sPD1-Fc, exerts therapeutic effects in clinically relevant orthotopic mouse models of cSCC. These models included tumors confined to the primary site and those metastasized to sentinel lymph nodes. A single intratumoral administration of KLS-3021 led to complete regression of primary tumors and significantly induced the regression of metastatic tumor lesions within sentinel lymph nodes. These therapeutic effects were validated by in vivo IVIS imaging, tumor weight measurements, and histopathological analyses. Importantly, the ability to control both local and metastatic disease with a single localized injection highlights the translational potential of KLS-3021 as a next-generation oncolytic immunotherapy.

Mechanistically, KLS-3021 acts through a multifaceted strategy targeting multiple axes of tumor resistance. PH-20-mediated hyaluronan degradation led to substantial ECM remodeling, facilitating intratumoral viral spread and infiltration of immune cells. Alcian blue staining confirmed substantial hyaluronan loss in KLS-3021-treated tumors, broader viral spread, and enhanced accessibility for immune effector cells. Notably, KLS-3021 modulated the composition and phenotype of tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells. IL-12, one of the transgenes encoded by KLS-3021, is a proinflammatory cytokine that promotes macrophage activation and skew polarization toward an M1-like phenotype with anti-tumor functions.24,25 Consistent with this, treatment with KLS-3021 resulted in increased intratumoral infiltration of F4/80+ macrophages, elevated expression of the M1 marker CD86, and reduced expression of the M2 marker CD206, indicating a shift toward an M1-dominant, proinflammatory environment with anti-tumor characteristics. This shift is critical, as M2-like macrophages are significantly related to tumor progression, angiogenesis, and immunosuppression across multiple solid malignancies. Accordingly, reprogramming the TME into a proinflammatory state constitutes a key mechanism for overcoming immunotherapy resistance.

Induction of ICD by oncolytic virus is a well-documented mechanism contributing to their immunostimulatory effects.23 Consistent with this, KLS-3021 induced hallmarks of ICD, a process crucial for activating antigen-presenting cells and generating tumor-specific adaptive immunity.26,27 In orthotopic primary cSCC tumors, we observed robust TUNEL positivity, cytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1, and surface exposure of CRT, which are all established markers of ICD. These features suggest that KLS-3021 not only eliminates tumor cells by direct lysis but also primes the immune system for durable anti-tumor responses. ICD is mechanistically distinct from classical apoptosis, as it stimulates immune recognition through the release or exposure of DAMPs. Key DAMPs include HMGB1, CRT, heat shock proteins, and adenosine triphosphate.27 Therefore, our observation of HMGB1 translocation and CRT exposure directly aligns with the established framework of ICD, in which HMGB1 acts as a DAMP via receptors such as TLR4 and RAGE, and CRT serves as an “eat-me” signal facilitating uptake by dendritic cells and macrophages. These events enhance tumor antigen presentation, promote the initiation of adaptive immune responses, and ultimately contribute to the establishment of long-term immune memory.28 Moreover, this ICD is recognized as a favorable prognostic marker and predictor of response to immune checkpoint blockade, further underscoring the therapeutic potential of KLS-3021 as an immunomodulatory agent.

Our findings align with those of previous studies emphasizing the importance of TME remodeling and innate immune activation in enhancing immunotherapeutic efficacy. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors have transformed the treatment landscape for advanced cSCC, clinical response rates remain suboptimal, and monotherapies often fail to overcome multiple and complex resistance mechanisms. The integrated approach of KLS-3021—combining ECM degradation (PH-20), proinflammatory cytokine signaling (IL-12), and immune checkpoint blockade (sPD1-Fc)—simultaneously targets structural, cellular, and molecular dimensions of immune resistance. This design contrasts with traditional mono- or dual-agent therapies and offers a compelling rationale for its clinical translation.

A major strength of our study is the use of clinically relevant orthotopic models that recapitulate the natural history of cSCC, together with sentinel lymph node-based metastatic models that capture metastatic behavior, both of which better reflect disease features compared to conventional subcutaneous models. These models enabled the overall assessment of local tumor control and lymphatic metastatic spread, providing a multidimensional evaluation of therapeutic outcomes. Such platforms are superior to conventional subcutaneous models in evaluating agents intended for translation into high-risk, locally advanced, or metastatic cSCC.

Our experiments were performed in immunodeficient mice, which precluded the direct assessment of tumor-specific T cell responses and the generation of immunological memory. This design was necessitated by the absence of a murine cSCC model, requiring the use of human cSCC cell lines that can only be evaluated in immunodeficient hosts. Nevertheless, the pronounced innate immune activation and ICD-associated changes observed in our study strongly suggest that KLS-3021 may enhance adaptive antitumor immunity in immunocompetent hosts. This expectation is further supported by our previous findings with KLS-3020, an earlier version of KLS-3021, which demonstrated robust activation of tumor-specific adaptive immune responses in immunocompetent models. Future studies are planned to evaluate KLS-3021 in immunocompetent models to further validate its potential to induce adaptive antitumor immunity.

In conclusion, our findings provide robust preclinical evidence that KLS-3021 mediates potent anti-tumor activity in both primary and metastatic cSCC by orchestrating ECM remodeling, innate immune activation, and induction of ICD. These findings position KLS-3021 as a promising next-generation oncolytic immunotherapy, with the potential to address critical therapeutic gaps in the management of high-risk and metastatic cSCC. Its multifactorial mechanism of action and localized delivery strategy offer unique advantages in efficacy and safety, laying the groundwork for future clinical development.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Human cSCC cell lines A-431 (KCLB, cat. no. 21202, RRID: CVCL_0037) and HSC-1 (JCRB, cat. no. JCRB1015, RRID: CVCL_2807) were purchased from the Korean Cell Line Bank and Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources, respectively. NHEKs were obtained from two providers: the ATCC (cat. no. PCS-200-011, lot. no. 80827232) and Promocell (cat. no. C-12013, lot. no. 4982031). Luciferase-labeled A-431 cells (ATCC, cat. no. CRL-1555-LUC2, RRID: CVCL_A4BS) were purchased from the ATCC.

Cell culture

A-431 cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM; Welgene, with Earle’s salts and L-glutamine) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco). HSC-1 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Welgene) supplemented with 20% FBS (Gibco), 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco), and 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco). NHEKs were cultured in dermal cell basal medium with keratinocyte growth kit (ATCC) or keratinocyte growth medium 3 with supplement mix and 0.06 mM CaCl2 (Promocell), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Luciferase-labeled A-431 cells were cultured in DMEM (ATCC) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Sigma), and 8 μg/mL blasticidin (InvivoGen). All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Plasmid construction

Shuttle plasmids designed to target the C11R, K3L, and J2R loci of the vaccinia virus genome were engineered using a stepwise cloning strategy.29 For the J2R locus, expression cassettes encoding PH-20 and sPD1-Fc were inserted under the control of the hybrid and 7.5 early/late promoters, respectively, between approximately 1 kb of flanking sequences corresponding to the J2R gene. To generate the K3L targeting plasmid, IL-12 cDNA was placed under the I1L-B19R early/late promoter, flanked by the left and right homologous arms of the K3L locus. The C11R-targeting plasmid was constructed to remove the LacZ reporter gene without insertion of additional transgenes. All recombinant constructs were confirmed by restriction enzyme digestion and sequencing prior to virus generation.

Generation of recombinant vaccinia virus

Recombinant vaccinia viruses were generated as previously described.29 Briefly, HeLa cells were infected with the KLS-3010 virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.05 and subsequently transfected with the C11R-targeting shuttle plasmid 15 min after infection. The culture medium was replaced with fresh 5% FBS-supplemented medium 4 h later, and the cells were incubated for 48–82 h at 37°C in 5% CO2. Infected cells were harvested, subjected to three cycles of freeze-thaw, and centrifuged to collect the supernatant containing recombinant virus. Viral clones were isolated via multiple rounds of plaque purification and verified via polymerase chain reaction amplification using primers specific for the target loci. Viral genomic DNA was extracted using either the Maxwell Viral Total Nucleic Acid Purification Kit (Promega) or the NucleoSpin RNA/DNA virus kit (Macherey-Nagel). The KLS-3021 virus was generated sequentially from the KLS-3010 backbone through recombination at the C11R, J2R, and K3L loci using the same strategy described above.

Virus purification and titration

For in vivo experiments, recombinant vaccinia viruses were purified via ultracentrifugation through a 36% sucrose cushion and resuspended in Tris buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.0, or 30 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.7, with 10% sucrose). Viral titers were determined using TCID50 assay in Vero cells, based on cytopathic effects observed 4 days post-infection.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

A-431 and HSC-1 cells were seeded at 5 × 103 cells per well and NHEKs at 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2. The following day, cells were infected with KLS-3021 at MOIs of 0.001–100. After 72 h post-infection, cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (Dojindo Molecular Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, EPOCH), and cell viability was normalized to vehicle-treated wells. The ED50 value (the virus dose at which viability is reduced by 50%) was calculated using the logarithmic interpolation formula. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

In vitro virus productivity assay

A-431 and HSC-1 cells were seeded at 2.8 × 105 cells per well and NHEKs at 1.9 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates and incubated overnight at 37°C in 5% CO2 to reach approximately 80% confluency the following day. Thereafter, cells were infected with KLS-3021 at an MOI of 0.002 and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2. After 2 h, the medium was replaced with fresh culture medium. At 72 h post-infection, both supernatants and cell lysates were collected. For cell lysis, samples underwent three freeze-thaw cycles (using liquid nitrogen and a 37°C water bath). The lysates were centrifuged at 2,500 rpm (Thermo Fisher Scientific, ST16R) for 15 min to remove cellular debris, and the clarified supernatants were stored at 4°C for up to 2 weeks before analysis. Viral titers were determined via TCID50 assay in Vero cells (KFDA, RRID: CVCL_JF53) using the Spearman-Kärber method. Briefly, Vero cells were seeded at 5,000 cells per well in 96-well plates and infected with serial 10-fold dilutions of virus-containing samples. Cytopathic effect was assessed at 4 days post-infection using an optical microscope (Zeiss, Primovert). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

In vivo efficacy: Orthotopic primary tumor model

Animal experiments were approved by the Kolon Life Science Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. KLS IACUC-2024-152, KLS IACUC-2025-55). Six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (JA BIO, Inc.) were used.

For the orthotopic primary tumor model, A-431 cells (1 × 105 cells in 30 μL serum-free medium) were injected intradermally into the right flank using an insulin syringe (BD). Tumor growth was monitored using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: 0.5 × length × width.2 When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into groups and received a single intratumoral injection of either vehicle (formulation buffer, 0 TCID50/head, 50 μL) or KLS-3021 (1 × 107 TCID50/head, 50 μL). Tumor volume and body weight were monitored for 14 days post-treatment. Six mice from each group were euthanized via CO2 asphyxiation under inhalation anesthesia on days 3 and 7 post-treatment. Tumors were harvested, bisected, and processed for histological analysis. One-half of each tumor was fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin for H&E (SSN solutions), Alcian blue (Scytek), and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. The other half was embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura), snap-frozen, and used exclusively for IF staining. All tissue processing and analyses were conducted according to standard protocols.

In vivo efficacy: Orthotopic metastatic tumor model

Animal experiments were performed at NDIC Inc. (Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea), a certified contract research organization, in compliance with the Animal Protection Act (law no. 4379 of May 31, 1991; revision law no. 19486 of June 20, 2023); the experiments were approved by the NDIC Animal Experimental Ethics Committee (approval no. P244058).

For the metastatic cSCC model, luciferase-labeled A-431 cells (1 × 106 cells in 50 μL 1× HBSS buffer) were injected into the left dorsum of the foot using an insulin syringe (BD). Tumor growth and PLN metastasis were monitored via bioluminescence imaging (IVIS Lumina III, PerkinElmer) at weekly intervals. For imaging, mice were administered D-luciferin (PerkinElmer) intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg, and a full-body IVIS scan was performed approximately 10 min after administration. Imaging parameters were standardized across all sessions, and photon flux was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

Mice with confirmed PLN metastasis via bioluminescence signal were randomized into two groups 14 days after tumor cell inoculation and received a single intratumoral injection of either vehicle (formulation buffer, 0 TCID50/head, 20 μL) or KLS-3021 (1 × 107 TCID50/head, 20 μL) into the primary lesion (dorsum of the foot). Tumor progression at both the primary site and metastatic PLN was monitored via bioluminescence imaging at weekly intervals.

At day 3 post-treatment, mice were euthanized using CO2 asphyxiation under inhalation anesthesia, and tissues—including the primary tumor and PLNs—were harvested, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, and processed into paraffin blocks. Thereafter, these paraffin sections were used for H&E, Alcian blue, IHC, and IF staining.

Immunostaining

For IHC analysis, paraffin-embedded tumor sections were incubated with primary antibodies against vaccinia virus (Abcam, cat. no. ab35219, 1:400) and F4/80 (Abcam, cat. no. ab111101, 1:100). Detection was performed using HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Dako, cat. no. K4003) and DAB substrate (Dako). Slides were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Dako) and mounted with glycergel (Dako).

For IF analysis, tissues were embedded in paraffin or optimal cutting temperature compound and sectioned at 7 μm thickness. Single IF staining was performed for CD86 (Invitrogen, cat. no. 14-0862-82, 1:50), CD206 (Abcam, cat. no. ab64693, 1:500), TNF-α (Abcam, cat. no. ab307164, 1:500), HMGB1 (Abcam, cat. no. ab18256, 1:200), and CRT (Abcam, cat. no. ab92516, 1:200) antibodies. Double IF staining was performed using CD31 (BD, cat. no. 553370, 1:200) in combination with Ki-67 (Abcam, cat. no. ab15580, 1:100). The TUNEL assay was performed with the DeadEnd Fluorometric TUNEL System (Promega, cat. no. G3250) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following phosphate-buffered saline washes, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor488-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, cat. no. A11034) for Ki-67 and TUNEL. Additionally, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor594-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, cat. no. A11037) for CD31 and counterstained with DAPI using mounting medium with DAPI (Abcam, ab104139). Images were acquired using a slide scanner (ZEISS, model no. Axio Scan Z1).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v.10 (GraphPad Software, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics v.22 (IBM Corp., USA). Normality was assessed for each group using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t test was used if both groups passed the normality test. If either group failed the normality test, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. When both groups showed normality but variances were unequal (as determined by Levene’s test), the Welch’s t test was used. p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The specific statistical methods used for each dataset are described in the corresponding figure legends.
