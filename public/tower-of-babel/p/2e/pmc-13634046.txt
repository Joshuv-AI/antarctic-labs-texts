Unveiling ocular developmental disorders through short-read whole-genome sequencing
Authors: Bertrand Chesneau, Bertrand Chesneau, Timotéo Cousteix, Abdelhakim Bouazzaoui, Hélène Dollfus, Marjolaine Willems, Audrey Putoux, Philippe Khau Van Kien, Vincent Michaud, Aurore Garde, Isabelle Meunier, David Geneviève, Elise Brischoux-Boucher, Fanny Laffargue, Justine Wourms, Marine Lebrun, Valentin Ruault, Julie Plaisancié, Nicolas Chassaing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital eye malformations represent a clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of disorders. Despite advances in genetic testing, fewer than half of affected patients receive a definitive molecular diagnosis. However, obtaining a molecular diagnosis is crucial for these patients and their families. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) is now standard practice in the genetic investigation of patients, but its contribution has not been extensively evaluated in patients with ocular malformations. In this work, we performed short-read WGS in a cohort of 100 families presenting with eye developmental disorders, including microphthalmia-anophthalmia spectrum (M/A), coloboma, anterior segment dysgenesis, congenital cataracts and/or foveal hypoplasia. Prior to WGS, 47 individuals had undergone targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) of genes panels related to ocular development, 11 had chromosomal microarray analysis (CGH-array) and 20 had a combination of both. None had received a definitive genetic diagnosis. WGS identified a (likely) pathogenic variant in eighteen patients. In addition, candidate variants of uncertain significance were detected in nine patients. Notably, fourteen of these variants would have been missed by conventional genes panels or CGH-array, underscoring the broader diagnostic scope of WGS. Our findings demonstrate that short-read WGS significantly improves diagnostic yield in patients with congenital eye malformations, including those previously undiagnosed despite NGS panel testing. These results support the integration of WGS in the genetic evaluation of ocular developmental disorders.

Introduction

Structural eye disease encompasses a wide spectrum of structural anomalies resulting from abnormal eye development. It account for 10% of visual impairment observed in children, with a prevalence ranging from 3.5 to 4.8 cases per 10,000 living births [1, 2]. It includes ocular globe growth defects (M/A) form a spectrum that ranges from microphthalmia (small eye) of variable severity to anophthalmia (complete absence of the ocular globe) [3]. Ocular colobomas result from defects in optic fissure closure and are frequently associated with microphthalmia [2]. Congenital disorders resulting from abnormal development of the anterior segment of the eye (i.e., cornea, iris, trabecular meshwork, ciliary body and lens), also are frequent malformations of the eye, generally grouped as anterior segment dysgenesis (ASD) [4, 5]. Congenital cataracts are defined as loss of lens transparency diagnosed in the first year of life [6]. Patients may also exhibit abnormalities in the development of the posterior segment of the eye. These can include foveolar hypoplasia, which can be isolated or associated with albinism [7]. Although multiple causes have been identified to date, genetic causes remain predominant in ocular malformations [3, 8]. The ocular malformation can affect one or both eyes. It can be simple but association of multiple ocular defects is common (i.e. microphthalmia and ASD) resulting in a complex ocular malformation. Various extra-ocular features can also be found in 30 to 90% of individuals with M/A [3]. Individuals with other ocular defect (ASD, congenital cataract) may also exhibit non-ocular findings [9].

There is a genetic heterogeneity among these ocular development defects, with 150 genes associated with structural eye disease in the literature (PanelApp v5.0) [10]. Moreover, the involvement of several genes in multiple ocular and extra-ocular phenotypes further complicates phenotype-genotype correlations [3, 11]. In recent years, exome and genome sequencing (WES, WGS) have emerged as interesting tools to screen individuals with congenital eye anomalies [12–14]. This genomic screening has facilitated the description of new genes associated with eye development anomalies [15]. WGS also covers non-coding regions where a growing part of variants are described [16]. Moreover, it allows the detection of balanced structural variants (SVs). Despite these new sequencing approaches, less than half of individuals affected with ocular malformations have a genetic diagnosis after exploration. This number varies greatly between cohorts reported in the literature, depending on the phenotype and genetic analyses performed (Table 1). For M/A, 16-61% of individuals have a genetic diagnosis [17–23]. This is the same regarding ASD with 13-54% of affected individuals with a molecular diagnosis [4, 21, 23, 24, 25], when excluding aniridia which affects the whole ocular globe and is caused by a PAX6 loss of function variant in more than 90% of cases [26]. To date, only four cohorts of individuals with structural eye disease explored using WGS have been reported [21, 22, 27]. Furthermore, the diagnostic rates for individuals with M/A (15.7% and 33%) are similar to that obtained through targeted sequencing of the main genes involved in these anomalies (21%) [36]. This work aims to evaluate the diagnostic yield and define the genetic spectrum of a diverse cohort of 100 unrelated individuals with structural eye diseases using WGS. Specifically, we aim to assess the added value of WGS in detecting complex structural variants and exploring non-coding regions, comparing our findings to established diagnostic rates in the literature to determine its utility as a first- or second-tier diagnostic tool.

Material and methods

Patients

This study was designed in compliance with the Helsinki Declaration and informed consent was obtained from the patient or their legal guardians. All included individuals were explored as part of the French Genomic Medicine Initiative (PFMG2025) [16] with “Eye malformation” as indication for the analysis.

Two-thirds of individuals (67/100) had previously benefited from NGS analysis, either a targeted panel of 24 (n = 8), 119 (n = 36) or 187 (n = 13) genes associated with eye development anomalies (solo analyses, performed in Toulouse University Hospital, the lists of genes are available in Supplementary Data 1), another panel of genes (n = 7) or WES (n = 3, trio analyses) (details in Supplementary Data 2). Furthermore, 31 individuals underwent CGH-array: 20 of these had also received NGS analysis, while 11 had no prior NGS testing. These analyses did not identify any causative variant.

Of note, four individuals (I010, I023, I030 and I062) have been previously reported as part of other cohorts [37–39].

Genetic analysis

WGS was performed by Auragen consortium following the recommendations of PFMG2025 [16]. In addition to the index case, relatives were sequenced in 99% of cases to facilitate the interpretation of genomic findings within the family. Family structures consisted of 79 trios (index and asymptomatic parents), 12 trios with additional symptomatic relatives, 2 quartets, 2 duos with an asymptomatic parent, 1 duo with a symptomatic relative, 1 solo analysis and 3 involving other relative combinations. Whole blood extracted genomic DNA was sequenced according to standard procedures for a PCR-Free genome on a NovaSeq6000 instrument (Illumina – San Diego, California, United States). Sequencing data were aligned to the GRCh38p13 full assembly using bwa 0.7 + . Variants were called by several algorithms including GATK4 + , Bcftools1.10 + , Manta1.6 + , CNVnator0.4 + , and annotated using the variant effect predictor. Variant selection focused on the index case, with relative data used to guide the diagnostic interpretation. ClinVar-labeled pathogenic variants were systematically prioritized. Inclusion criteria required >2 reads, an allelic balance ≥10%, and rarity in population databases ( < 1% in gnomAD and <10% in the internal Auragen asymptomatic cohort). Additionally, variants were restricted to those located within 2 kb of UTR boundaries. Subsequent prioritization was performed via in-house procedures. Briefly, it integrates segregation, variant impact, and phenotypic relevance (based on clinician-provided HPO terms using Phrank score [40]). Notably, Single Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels) predicted to alter the splicing of genes of interest were prioritized using SpliceAI ( ≥ 0.2) and/or CADD (v1.6 phred > 25). Finally, non-prioritized variants underwent manual expert review via SeqOne, incorporating clinical context and geneticist expertise. Further details are available on request.

Given that all reported SNVs and indels exhibited a sequencing depth above 30X and an allele frequency of at least 40%, further validation by an alternative method was not required. Reported inversions (inv(6p25.1p25.3), inv(10q24.3q26.1), inv(11p11.2p13)) were confirmed by Sanger sequencing of breakpoints of genomic DNA extracted from a second blood sample.

Results

We report here a cohort of 100 unrelated individuals affected with eye development anomalies. Around half of them exhibit an eye malformation belonging to the M/A spectrum (n = 54/100) (Table 2, details available in Supplementary Data 2). Half of individuals with M/A (n = 27) had an eye malformation that was accompanied by an ASD phenotype. The remaining individuals had isolated ASD without ocular growth defect (n = 20), coloboma (n = 16), congenital cataract (n = 6) or foveal hypoplasia (n = 4). More than half of them also exhibit systemic features (n = 55/100), notably neurodevelopmental disorders (n = 19; namely: developmental delay (DD), intellectual development disorder (ID), autism spectrum disorder or learning difficulties), cerebral MRI anomalies (n = 7) and congenital genitourinary anomalies (n = 6) (Supplementary Data 2). A female patient (I093) presenting with severe ocular phenotype (bilateral complex microphthalmia with cataract and glaucoma) was found to carry a familial partial deletion of NHS at heterozygous state. While this deletion may contribute to the phenotype, the severity of the ocular manifestations exceeds what is typically observed in female heterozygous carriers of NHS variants, who usually present with cataracts showing incomplete penetrance [46].

In six individuals with ocular malformations in a syndromic context, we identified a (likely) pathogenic variant that partially explain the clinical presentation but are unlikely to account fully for the ocular phenotype. These variants were located in genes associated with neurodevelopmental disorders in which structural eye anomalies have not yet been reported (Table 3, pedigrees available in Supplementary Data 3): DYM [47], MAP4K4 [48], MECP2 [49], MED13L [50], PTEN [51], TLK2 [52]. A dual-etiology mechanism may be at play in several of these cases. Notably, in the individual (I010) carrying the PTEN variant, likely responsible for the DD and macrocephaly, a second variant in ARHGAP35, likely responsible for the ocular phenotype, was also identified. Individual I087 carries a pathogenic homozygous variant in DYM associated with Dyggve-Melchior-Clausen disease, explaining the extra-ocular features. However, this does not explain the familial congenital cataracts (observed in the proband, mother, and sister), as the relatives are only heterozygous for this variant and present with an isolated ocular phenotype.

In eight individuals, we identified variants of uncertain significance (VUSs) that do not meet the ACMG criteria to be classified as likely pathogenic [53, 54]. Six of these VUSs were located in genes previously implicated in eye development anomalies [3, 8] (Table 3, pedigrees available in Supplementary Data 3): CRYGC, GJA3, PITX3, PORCN, SOX2 and TDRD7. One variant was found in ARHGEF12, a gene not previously associated with structural eye defects in the literature, but which is expressed during ocular development and has been linked to an increased risk of glaucoma [55]. In addition, we detected two CNVs that do not encompass any genes currently known to be involved in eye development.

Finally, four cases had single heterozygous recessive alleles identified with no second variant detected (Table 3, pedigrees available in Supplementary Data 3): FAT1 [56], FOXE3 [57], OCA2 [58] and HPS3 [7]. In the case of FOXE3 [57], loss-of-function variants in this gene, such as the one identified here, are more commonly associated with recessive inheritance.

Of note, an incidental finding has been found in 5 individuals with pathogenic variants in genes associated with cancer predisposition (BRCA2: 2, BRCA1: 1, PALB2: 1) or in susceptibility to malignant hyperthermia (RYR1: 1).

Discussion

Diagnostic yield

Here, we report a large cohort of 100 individuals with structural eye disease genetically explored using short-read WGS. This analysis provided 18 diagnoses regarding the ocular phenotype. This result is lower than the diagnostic yield observed in other cohorts of individuals with structural eye anomalies analysed using short-read WGS (24-77%) (Table 1) [21, 22, 27]. This could be explained by the fact that WGS was used as a second-line analysis after targeted NGS and/or CGH-array for three quarters (78%) of the patients in our cohort. However, we observed a similar diagnostic yield (15%) when considering only individuals without prior NGS analysis (n = 33).

Patients with a more severe ocular defect appear more likely to receive a molecular diagnosis, for example when the defect is bilateral (23% vs. 7%) or complex (M/A + ASD: 22% vs. isolated M/A: 13%). These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that the likelihood of identifying a genetic cause increases with the severity of the ocular malformation [3, 23, 36].

In addition, the nature of ocular defect also modifies the mutation detection rate. The group of ocular malformations with the lowest diagnostic yield are isolated ocular colobomas, which confirms the data we previously found in targeted NGS analysis [59]. In their cohort, Jackson et al. [21] reported a higher diagnostic yield in individuals with congenital cataracts (45%) compared to those with M/A (16%) or ASD (13%). Similarly, in a cohort of 52 patients with congenital cataracts studied by Ma et al. [27], 14 remained undiagnosed after the CGH-array and WES/targeted NGS panel approaches were employed. Of these, five were resolved through the additional diagnostic yield of WGS (5/14, 36%).

Moreover, diagnostic yields were similar between patients with isolated ocular involvement (22%) and those with associated systemic features (15%), consistent with previously reported data in the literature [22, 23]. While Neuhann et al. [23] reported higher yields in individuals with anterior segment dysgenesis (ASD) and extra-ocular features, yields for microphthalmia/anophthalmia (M/A) and coloboma remained similar regardless of systemic involvement. Our results showed no significant disparity between isolated and syndromic phenotypes across ocular subtypes, except for M/A + ASD: ASD (18% [2/11] isolated vs. 22% [2/9] syndromic), M/A and coloboma (11% [2/19] vs. 13% [3/24]), and M/A + ASD (33% [4/12] vs. 13% [2/15]). Notably, in six patients with extra-ocular features, the identified variants accounted for the systemic manifestations but failed to explain the ocular phenotype (“Partially conclusive” cases).

Gain in comparison with other existing technics

When considering the gain of WGS compared to targeted NGS panels, at least half of the diagnoses (n = 10/18) would have been missed using our 187 genes NGS panel (list of genes in Supplementary Data 1). A WES approach would likely have identified 16 of these 18 diagnoses (as it could not detect the two balanced inversions). This contrast with other cohorts in which the variants reported are SNVs in the coding regions of genes that are already associated with ocular malformations, which would likely have been identified using a targeted NGS panel. Jackson et al. [21] reported on a cohort of 293 patients with non-retinal developmental eye disorders in the United Kingdom who underwent WGS, achieving an overall diagnosis rate of 24.9%. All of the reported variants were located within or close to coding regions. The authors discuss the fact that their analysis of non-coding variants was limited by the UK Genomics England 100,000 Genomes Project pipeline to splice regions only. They also did not report any SV. Therefore, if we focus on individuals with either M/A, ASD or cataracts, most of the variants found in their cohort would likely have been identified using our targeted NGS panel (only 3/37 variants, 8%, were located in genes absent from our panel).

As previously shown [60], short-read WGS demonstrates an advantage in the identification of balanced and unbalanced SVs in comparison to NGS panel, WES and CGH-array. We were able to detect two inversions and three CNVs. The two inversions are located close to genes known for their implication in eye development (10 kb 3’ from PAX6 and 100 kb 5’ from FOXC1 respectively) and are predicted to disrupt their expression regulation by separating these genes from their cis regulatory elements [26, 37]. In addition to the two inversions, WGS allows easier detection of CNVs such as the intragenic 649 bp deletion of CRYBB1 that delete only 11 bases of exon 6 making it difficult to detect using targeted NGS usually covering only exons and exon-introns boundaries.

Although gene panels are regularly updated, they remain inherently limited and cannot be fully exhaustive. In contrast, WGS (and WES) enables the identification of variants in genes not typically included in eye malformations panels, such as ADAMTSL4 (commonly associated with ectopia lentis) [61] or WFS1 (typically linked to optic atrophy) [44]. WGS also facilitated the detection of variants in newly implicated genes, such as NR6A1 [45]. In addition, it enabled the identification of novel candidate genes, such as ARHGAP35, in which we detected a de novo nonsense variant in an individual with bilateral complex syndromic microphthalmia. Collaboration with other research teams subsequently confirmed the involvement of ARHGAP35 in human eye morphogenesis [40]. Furthermore, unlike targeted NGS panels, WGS offers the advantage of streamlined reanalysis, allowing the detection of variants in genes discovered after the initial sequencing.

Pangenomic sequencing also facilitates the exploration of genes that are not usually included in a targeted NGS eye panel, as they are associated with human diseases in which eye malformation is not a common feature. For instance, we found truncating rare variant in SMARCA4 in two unrelated individuals (previously reported) [38]. This gene has been associated with two distinct phenotypes in the literature: Coffin-Siris syndrome and rhabdoid tumour predisposition [43]. While these conditions are not classically linked to eye development anomalies, cases of M/A have been occasionally reported in individuals with a loss of function variant in SMARCA4 [43].

However, in some patients, the identified (likely) pathogenic variants are unlikely to account for the ocular phenotype, as they were located in genes not currently associated with eye malformations, including DYM [62], MAP4K4 [48], MECP2 [49], MED13L [50], PTEN [51] and TLK2 [52]. While these findings partially addressed the clinical concerns, they do not provide a complete diagnosis based on current knowledge. Notably, a second variant, likely causative of the ocular phenotype, was identified in ARHGAP35 in the individual carrying the PTEN variant associated with Cowden syndrome (I010). Such instances of multiple molecular diagnoses are relatively frequent, occurring in approximately 3.5% of individuals in intellectual disability cohorts [63].

Interpretation difficulties

While the diagnostic utility of WGS for individuals with structural eye diseases is undeniable, this approach also presents new challenges. The most important of these might be its difficulty of interpretation. The millions of variants detected by WGS necessitate robust bioinformatics pipelines for variant calling, annotation and filtering. Prior to the advent of WGS, clinical genetic testing was largely confined to coding regions. It is now well described that variants in non-coding regions (including intergenic regions, enhancers, promoters, introns, 5’ and 3’ untranslated regions (UTRs)) can contribute to human diseases [26, 64, 65]. However, interpreting these variants remains particularly challenging and depends on the development of reliable databases and predictive tools. One such example is MORFEEdb (Mutation on Open Reading FramE annotation), a tool recently developed to enhance the annotation of SNVs within human 5′ UTRs [66]. As a result, some potentially pathogenic variants may have been inadvertently filtered out by our pipeline due to current limitations in knowledge or annotation capabilities. This underscores the importance of regular genome reanalysis to capture newly recognized disease mechanisms. Finally, trio-based analysis (involving the proband and both parents) plays a key role in variant interpretation. This approach was favored in the France Genomic Medicine 2025 initiative [16], from which this cohort originates, and has demonstrated a diagnostic advantage over solo sequencing, with diagnostic yields of 29.1% and 20.7%, respectively.

In line with the challenges of variant interpretation, we identified VUS in nine patients (Table 3). These variants represent potential candidates for explaining the observed ocular phenotypes and may contribute to the discovery of new genes or molecular mechanisms. However, additional analyses are required to confirm their pathogenicity. Five of the identified VUS are SNVs affecting the coding sequence of genes previously associated with genetic eye diseases: CRYGC, GJA3, PORCN, SOX2 and TDRD7 [3, 8]. One case involved a de novo 20 Mb inversion on chromosome 10, with one breakpoint located 10 kb upstream of the 5′ UTR of the PITX3 gene. The patient presented with bilateral microphthalmia and severe DD, a phenotype more severe than typically reported for PITX3-related disorders, which are usually limited to ASD without DD [67]. This discrepancy may be due to ectopic PITX3 expression, disruption of an additional gene by the inversion or the presence of another undetected pathogenic variant. In another case, we identified a de novo intronic variant in ARHGEF12, predicted to affect splicing. Although this gene is not currently associated with a Mendelian disorder, it is expressed during ocular development, is intolerant to loss-of-function variants and has been implicated in glaucoma susceptibility through genome-wide association studies [55]. The final two VUS consist of two CNVs (del 15q13.1q13.2 and dup 17q12q21.2) for which current evidence is insufficient to establish pathogenicity.

In four patients, we identified heterozygous (likely) pathogenic variants in genes typically associated with autosomal recessive inheritance: FAT1 [56], FOXE3 [57], HPS3 [68], OCA2 [58]. Despite extensive investigation, no second pathogenic variant was found in these genes. While the patients’ phenotypes were broadly consistent with those described for these genes, it remains unclear whether the identified heterozygous variant contributes to the phenotype alongside an undetected second variant or whether it represents an incidental finding, with the phenotype instead explained by a variant in another gene.

Incidental findings

In addition, genomic exploration exposes to the discovery of incidental findings. In our cohort, we found an incidental finding in 5% of patients, which is consistent with the literature, highlighting the relative frequency of such findings [69]. Notably, these findings were not actively sought but emerged unintentionally during the search for the primary genetic diagnosis. This raise ethical questions and can be challenging for both patients and clinicians who are dealing with this type of results. The pathogenic variants were located in actionable genes according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (BRCA2: 2, BRCA1: 1, PALB2: 1, RYR1: 1) [70].

Limits of short-read WGS

Following analysis, two-thirds of patients remained without a molecular diagnosis or a variant of interest. Beyond the current limitations in knowledge, short-read WGS also has inherent technical constraints. The commonly used human reference genome (GRCh38) contains unresolved regions, particularly in large repetitive arrays, as well as pericentromeric and subtelomeric regions, which lead to alignment difficulties for short reads. Similarly, the detection of complex SVs remains challenging, especially when breakpoints lie within regions poorly covered or inaccessible to short-read sequencing technologies. Long-read sequencing (LRS), which enables sequencing of reads spanning tens to hundreds of kilobases, offers the potential to overcome these limitations [71]. The adoption of LRS is expected to improve diagnostic yield in the coming years. For example, LR-WGS has recently allowed a better understanding of the molecular mechanism behind M/A spectrum linked to complex rearrangement in 13q region, by identifying a complex SV in this region [72]. However, the added value of LRS in the context of structural eye diseases remains to be fully determined.

A remaining issue with WGS is its cost compared to CGH-array and targeted NGS analysis. Although the financial and technical cost of WGS has fallen sharply in recent years, it remains more expensive that first-line analysis. Moreover, it generates much more data that then needs to be stored, while data storage is now well known to have a huge environmental impact [73].

Conclusion

To conclude, we observed an 18% diagnostic yield using WGS in our cohort of 100 patients with ocular malformations. Although a large number of individuals remain without a molecular diagnosis, considering thediagnostic contribution of WGS, this analysis should be considered and standardized for patients with ocular malformations. The arrival of LRS should further increase the number of molecular diagnoses made, pointing to a promising future in patient care.
