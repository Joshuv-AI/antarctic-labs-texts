Knowledge, Clinical Staging, and Barriers to Early Presentation of Diabetic Retinopathy in a Tertiary Eye Care Centre in Ghana: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Emmanuel Appiagyei, Yaw Akye Essuman, Akwasi Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDiabetic retinopathy is the most common microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus and a leading cause of new‐onset blindness in populations of working age. Late presentation of patients to eye care facilities has been associated with the development of vision‐threatening complications. The study sought to determine the clinical profile, knowledge and factors influencing late presentation of diabetic retinopathy among diabetics at the Eye Centre of the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital in Ghana.MethodsThis descriptive cross‐sectional study involved 78 diabetic patients referred to the retina clinic between February 2022 and April 2022. Participants underwent a comprehensive ocular examination, with the more severely affected eye used to determine the stage of DR based on the ETDRS classification. Data on socioeconomic status, knowledge of DR, and reasons for delayed presentation were collected using a structured, interviewer‐administered questionnaire. Descriptive and inferential statistics were performed using SPSS version 23. Chi‐square tests were used to assess associations, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsOf the 78 participants, 36 (46.2%) were males and 42 (53.8%) females, with a mean age of 49.9 ± 11.4 years. The mean knowledge score on DR was 5.3 ± 3.2 (out of 12). Most participants (65%) were aware that uncontrolled diabetes affects the eyes. Clinical grading showed that 7.7% had mild NPDR, 16.7% moderate NPDR, 10.3% severe NPDR, 19.2% very severe NPDR, 9.0% high‐risk PDR, and 37.2% had advanced PDR. The most cited reason for delayed presentation was lack of knowledge about DR (69.2%).ConclusionThe majority of diabetic patients presented with advanced stages of DR, primarily due to limited awareness about the condition. Strengthening public education and routine screening may help reduce late presentations and prevent vision loss.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a major microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus (DM) and remains a leading cause of visual loss among working‐age populations globally [1, 2]. It is projected that the number of people with diabetes will rise from 366 million in 2011 to 552 million by 2030, with nearly 80% of this population residing in low‐ and middle‐income countries and within the economically active age group [3]. A meta‐analysis estimated the global prevalence of DR at 34.6% [4], with type 1 diabetic patients nearly universally affected within 15–20 years of diagnosis, and more than 60% of type 2 diabetic patients developing some form of DR over the same period [5].

In Africa, the burden of diabetic retinopathy is rising alongside increasing diabetes prevalence. Studies from West Africa, including Ghana and Nigeria, report DR prevalence rates ranging from 15.5% to 17% [6, 7]. In Ghana specifically, DR is a growing public health concern, often diagnosed at advanced stages due to limited access to regular eye screening and poor patient awareness. The disease can progress from non‐proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) to proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), which may result in serious vision‐threatening complications such as vitreous hemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment, and diabetic macular edema [1, 8].

Despite evidence that timely detection and management of DR can prevent or delay vision loss, many patients in Ghana present late for ophthalmic care. Contributing factors include: poor knowledge of the condition, socioeconomic constraints, and inadequate screening services [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. These late presentations limit treatment options and increase the risk of irreversible blindness, further impacting patients' quality of life and diabetes self‐management [15].

There remains a critical gap in understanding the clinical profile of DR patients, their awareness levels, and the specific reasons behind delayed presentation in the Ghanaian population. Such information is vital for developing targeted interventions to promote early detection and reduce visual impairment due to DR.

This study therefore aimed to determine the clinical profile of diabetic retinopathy, assess knowledge about the condition, and identify factors influencing late presentation among diabetic patients at the Eye Centre of Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital in Ghana.

Materials and Methods

This hospital based cross‐sectional study was conducted from February 2022 to June 2022 at the Eye Center, Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH)—a major eye referral center in Ghana. Patients aged 18 years and above, with confirmed cases of diabetes mellitus, were considered eligible for this study whereas diabetic retinopathy patients with other retinal vascular pathologies such as: sickle cell retinopathy, hypertensive retinopathy, retinal vasculitis, were excluded. A purposive sampling method was employed to recruit 78 diabetic retinopathy patients (156 eyes) at the study site. The sample size was determined based on the number of eligible diabetic retinopathy patients who attended the retina clinic during the study period and met the inclusion criteria.

All study participants had their visual acuity measured with the LogMAR Chart. Objective and subjective refraction was performed for all participants and best corrected visual acuity was recorded. A slit lamp biomicroscope (Inami & Co. Japan) was used to examine the anterior segment of the eye and pupillary reaction test. Intraocular pressure was measured using a Goldmann Applanation Tonometer (Haag‐Streit AG, Koeniz, Switzerland). The pupils of all participants were dilated with 1% tropicamide and a detailed posterior segment examination, as much as the media clarity allowed for, was performed by a retina specialist (A.A.A.). A stereoscopic examination of the disc and macula with a slit lamp and 78D Volk lens (Volk Optical, USA); as well as indirect ophthalmoscopy with a 20D lens (Volk Optical, USA) was used for posterior segment examination; clinical staging of DR was done.

Findings were recorded on a data collection sheet developed based on review of relevant literature [9, 10, 11]. The questionnaire underwent content validation by two ophthalmologists and one public health specialist to ensure relevance and clarity. Information on duration of diabetes mellitus diagnosis and the presence of comorbidities such as systemic hypertension was assessed from the history. This study adhered strictly to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Komfo Anokye Teaching Hospital (KATH IRB/AP/028/22). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive analysis was performed using frequencies, percentages, means and standard deviations. Participant's knowledge of DR was assessed by 17 questions with a maximum score of 12 points. A numerical value of 1 for correct response and 0 for incorrect response was given. Participants who scored greater than or equal to the mean (≥ 5.32) of knowledge questions were considered to have good knowledge and those who scored below the mean were considered as having poor knowledge. A two‐sided Chi‐square test was used to determine statistical significance between categorical variables. For analysis involving the continuous knowledge score, non‐parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U) were used to compare scores across groups, as the data were not normally distributed. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Almost all the study participants were enrolled on the National Health Insurance Scheme (83.3%) and served as the only means of accessing healthcare. In the analysis of only the right eye (OD) of participants, 65/78 (83.3%) had DR and 13/78 (16.7%) did not have DR, whereas in the left eye (OS) of study participants, 73/78 (93.6%) had DR and 5/78 (6.4%) did not have DR (Figure 1).

Factors elucidated for late presentation among study participants include: lack of knowledge about diabetic retinopathy (54/78, 69.2%), lack of access to eye care (15/78, 22.1%), cost/insurance (17/78, 21.8%), fear of discovery (25/78, 32.1%), transportation difficulties (3/78, 3.8%) and others (7/78, 9.0%). Among these, lack of knowledge about DR was significantly associated with late presentation (p = 0.002), while other factors such as cost (p = 0.073), fear of discovery (p = 0.089), and access to eye care (p = 0.112) did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

The mean age of our study population; patients with diabetes (50 years) was similar to other hospital‐based studies conducted by in Jordan [16] and Turkey [17], but unlike other developing countries [18, 19], where patients were much younger. This may be due to late detection of diabetes or late presentation for eye examination among our study population.

More females than males were seen during the study period, this was similar to the study in Turkey which reported a preponderance of female participants (51.8%) among their study population [17]. This was contrary to findings seen in studies in Jordan, where male prevalences of 50.4% and 54.9% were recorded [20]. The higher percentage of females found in this study could be attributed to the recent upward trends in the prevalence of diabetes mellitus among women [3].

Majority of the study population were traders (33.3%) followed by professionals (17.9%). Regarding level of education, majority of the study population had attained tertiary education, and most of the study population occupied either a chamber/hall (30.8%) or self‐contained house (48.7%). These sociodemographic characteristics reflect on the fraction of population that have a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus, as reported in prevalence studies (diabetes) in the country [6]. A study in an adult Ghanaian diabetic population found that diabetes is prevalent among adults aged 50 years and above, females, higher level of education, wealth households and professionals [21]. These findings reflect in the sociodemographic characteristics of our study population in this study.

On assessment of the knowledge of participants on diabetic retinopathy, we found that 65.4% of participants were aware that diabetes had ocular effects. Only 38.5% of them had heard about diabetic retinopathy and 32.1% knew DR could result in blindness. Contrary to our findings, another study found that 98.3% of the study participants knew that diabetes could affect eyes with 99.1% believing that it could lead to blindness, while 50.4% of them were aware of diabetic retinopathy [20]. A study carried out in Jordan also revealed that 88.2% of study participants were aware of the effects of diabetes on the eye with 81.9% stating that it could lead to blindness [16]. These findings were also seen in Turkey, where a study also reported higher levels of knowledge among participants with 88.1% of the study participants being aware of the effects of diabetes on the eyes [17]. The disparities in the level of knowledge as reported in this study and other studies could be attributed to a lower educational level among our study population. Also, there is an established national eye health care program and referral guidelines regarding DM and DR management for primary care in Jordan which positively influence knowledge about these conditions. Patients are referred for an eye examination at the same time as their diagnosis of DM. This may explain the high level of awareness of DR found in their study. The lower levels of knowledge regarding the awareness of diabetic retinopathy account for the lower mean scale score of 5.32, found in the study.

The stages of diabetic retinopathy were graded according to the ETDRS classifications. In the current study, 37.2% of the study population showed signs of advanced PDR, while 9.0% had high risk PDR and 19.2% had very severe NPDR. Several findings have been reported in other studies, including in Blantyre, Malawi, where 19.7% of type 2 DM cases were found to have STDR which was defined as pre‐proliferative, proliferative and or the presence of macular edema, while a further 8.5% had PDR. They also found an STDR prevalence of 18.8% and PDR prevalence of 12.5% in type 1 diabetic participants [22]. A study in a Chinese population [23] found an STDR prevalence of 12.6% among 16,305 participants, while another study in Hangzhou, China [24], found that 80% of participants had STDR, including 67% of first‐time attendees at the study site. Even though the methods of classification used in the other studies were different from this study, it was observed that the percentage of the study population at the stages of advanced PDR and high risk PDR was higher (46.2%) than in the studies in Malawi and China [22, 23]. This observation could be attributed to the late presentation of diabetic patients to the eye clinic for examination, resulting in more of them presenting with advanced PDR stages.

The study subjects reported several factors that contributed to the late presentation of diabetic retinopathy cases. A lack of knowledge about diabetic retinopathy was the most reported factor (69.2%) followed by a fear of discovery (32.1%), a lack of access (22.1%), high costs (21.8%) and transportation difficulties (3.8%). Similarly in Owo, Nigeria, a lack of knowledge about DR was cited by 62.8% of participants [25], while Davalgi et al. (2018) also reported a lack of knowledge about DR as the number one barrier to timely eye examination by diabetic patients in their study [26]. Another study highlighted that the cost of service delivery also hindered timely eye examination by diabetic patients [27]. A lack of access to eye examination, as reported by some study participants, may be due to an inadequate number of ophthalmologists in the country and the inequitable distribution of eye care services in the country [27]. Most eye care centers are located in the urban regions of the country, depriving diabetic patients in remote areas from easily accessing eye care with those who decide to make efforts to seek eye care in these urban areas subjected to transportation difficulties.

This study had some limitations. It was a hospital‐based study with a small sample size as compared to other studies and as such findings of the study may not be an exact representation of diabetic patients and diabetic retinopathy in the country. The study, however, provides much‐needed insight into factors influencing late presentation among diabetic retinopathy patients and should thus, serve as a foundation for future research and public health interventions on the subject.

Conclusions

This study revealed that a substantial proportion of diabetic patients had poor knowledge of diabetic retinopathy and often presented with advanced stages of the disease, particularly proliferative diabetic retinopathy. Lack of awareness was identified as the leading factor contributing to delayed presentation. There is a need for the integration of routine ocular examinations into diabetes management protocols and the implementation of targeted educational campaigns, particularly in underserved and rural communities, to improve early detection and reduce vision loss from diabetic retinopathy.
