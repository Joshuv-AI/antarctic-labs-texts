Doxy-PEP interest, awareness, and use among sexual and gender minorities in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru: findings from a web-based survey
Authors: Mayara Secco Torres Silva, Paula Mendes Luz, Kelika A. ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSexual and gender minorities (SGM) face a disproportionate burden of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) in Latin America. Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) is effective in preventing syphilis, chlamydia and gonorrhea among these populations. We explored doxy-PEP awareness, interest and use in Brazil, Mexico and Peru.MethodsCross-sectional online survey among SGM assigned male at birth (18+ years) recruited on dating apps (Scruff and Grindr) and social media (Instagram and Facebook). Adjusted logistic regression models were used to explore factors associated with doxy-PEP awareness overall and by country.FindingsAmong 16,081 participants (Brazil: 10,269; Mexico: 4081; Peru: 1731), 16% reported previous STI diagnosis. Only 37.7% and 7.2% reported doxy-PEP awareness and use, but interest was high (88.3%). Age >30 years (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]:0.88 [95% confidence interval [CI]:0.79–0.98), post-secondary education (aOR: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.08–1.36), 5+ sex partners (aOR: 1.32, 95% CI: 1.20–1.46), stimulant use (aOR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.07–1.35), ever PrEP use or living with HIV (aOR: 2.76, 95% CI: 2.49–3.06), higher HIV knowledge (aOR per one standard deviation [SD] increase: 1.35, 95% CI: 1.28–1.44), and higher internalized homonegativity (aOR per one SD increase: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.83–0.91) were associated with doxy-PEP awareness.InterpretationDespite high interest in using doxy-PEP, its awareness and use remain limited among SGM in Latin America. Targeted efforts are crucial to raise awareness, especially among populations with lower education levels, limited HIV knowledge, and those less engaged with existing prevention services. Strengthening educational campaigns and integrating doxy-PEP into public health sexual health programs will be key to ensuring equitable access and reducing STI incidence.Funding10.13039/100032626Unitaid, 10.13039/501100003593CNPq and 10.13039/501100004586FAPERJ.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We reviewed existing literature on doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) for the prevention of bacterial sexually transmitted infections (STIs), with a specific focus on studies conducted among sexual and gender minorities (SGM) in the context of Latin America. Doxy-PEP prescription was the use of doxycycline 200 mg per oral once up to 72 h after sex relationship. We conducted searches in PubMed, Embase, and LILACS on November 19, 2025, using the following terms: (“doxycycline”, “doxy-PEP”, “post-exposure prophylaxis”, “STI prevention”) AND (“sexual and gender minorities” or “MSM” or “transgender” or “travesti”) AND (“Brazil” or “Mexico” or “Peru” or “South America” or “Latin America”). We also consulted the relevant gray literature and reports from public health organizations to capture regional context regarding STIs burden and prevention efforts.

We found data highlighting the significant burden of bacterial STIs in Latin America and reports detailing challenges in STIs prevention in the region, influenced by societal factors and limitations in accessing comprehensive sexual healthcare services. Anecdotal reports suggested some informal use of doxy-PEP within certain networks in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru. One survey enrolling 447 participants in Peru reported high willingness to use doxy-PEP, despite low awareness and prior use among men who have sex with men and transgender women. One qualitative study enrolling 32 participants from Brazil explored the barriers to access doxy-PEP. No other studies provided a comprehensive assessment of these outcomes or explored the factors influencing access to information and use of doxy-PEP in the region.

Added value of this study

This study provides the first large-scale, multi-country assessment of doxy-PEP interest, awareness, and use among SGM populations in Latin America, specifically in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru. In the largest survey conducted to date on this topic, comprising over 15,000 participants, the study identifies a notable paradox: despite low overall awareness of doxy-PEP (approximately 38%), there is a high level of interest in its use across the region (over 88%), accompanied by marked variability in reported use between countries. Our study quantifies this unmet need for accessible STI prevention tools and highlights that interest is high even among those previously unaware of the strategy. Factors associated with doxy-PEP awareness included identifying as non-binary or other gender diverse identities compared to cisgender man, living in Mexico or Brazil compared to Peru, having higher education and income, living in capital cities, current or previous HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use, living with HIV, recent STI diagnosis, and recent stimulant drug use.

The study also underscores how behavioral markers of elevated STIs vulnerability, such as multiple sex partners and recent STIs diagnoses, are associated with higher awareness and interest, indicating proactive health-seeking behaviors among those most in need. Considering doxy-PEP use in our sample, most participants from Brazil who reported prior use indicated that they had obtained a prescription from a healthcare provider, and many described following recommended dosing schedules and timing. These findings challenge potential concerns about unregulated use and suggest that structured, provider led doxy-PEP implementation is feasible and potentially effective in a Latin American context.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our results combined with existing evidence on doxy-PEP efficacy from other settings, strongly suggest that doxy-PEP could become a valuable tool in addressing the escalating bacterial STI epidemic among SGM in Latin America. The high interest in using the strategy indicates a receptive population ready for innovative prevention options. However, the low awareness points to a critical information vacuum exacerbated by the absence of national clinical guidelines and implementation programs in the region. This highlights an urgent need for public health authorities in the region to develop clear clinical guidance, disseminate accurate information, and invest in implementation research tailored to the diverse regional contexts.

Furthermore, the study reveals that structural and social barriers, such as lower educational attainment, living outside capital cities, and internalized homonegativity are associated with lower awareness. This underscores that equitable scale-up of doxy-PEP must be accompanied by strategies to reach structurally excluded populations. Integrating doxy-PEP into existing HIV prevention infrastructure, such as PrEP programs, presents a significant opportunity to leverage established trust and clinical expertise, potentially improving adherence and promoting rational use. Community-based outreach and education, developed in partnership with civil society organizations, will be essential to ensure equitable access, particularly for vulnerable populations, such as sex workers and SGM. The evidence from Brazil regarding medically supervised and adherent use provides a compelling case for formal implementation within public health systems, paving the way for a regional model of comprehensive STIs prevention that actively addresses health inequities.

Introduction

In countries of the named ‘Global South’, certain populations face heightened vulnerability to sexually transmitted infections (STI), including gay, bisexual, and other cisgender men who have sex with men (MSM), travestis, and transgender women.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 In Brazil and Mexico, syphilis has re-emerged as a significant public health concern, with rising incidence rates in recent years.6,7 Despite the promotion of combination prevention strategies, challenges persist in addressing bacterial STIs. Societal taboos surrounding sexuality, lack of comprehensive sexual education in schools and families, and the weakening of prevention efforts contribute to the ongoing relevance of these infections. Furthermore, conservative movements and anti-gender ideologies hinder open discussions about sexual health, exacerbating the situation.8,9

Emerging STI-related challenges and declining funding for prevention efforts further complicate the landscape. The spread of novel pathogens such as mpox, along with the increasing detection of Mycoplasma genitalium as a cause of symptomatic STI, often carrying antimicrobial resistance genes, has raised significant concern.10,11 Additionally, sexual transmission routes for hepatitis A and C viruses are becoming more prominent, signaling a shift in transmission dynamics.12,13 The emergence of Neisseria gonorrhoeae strains resistant to ceftriaxone and treatment failures with macrolides for M. genitalium underscore the need for alternative prevention strategies.14, 15, 16, 17

In response to these challenges, doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) has emerged as a promising biomedical intervention for preventing bacterial STIs, including chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis. Doxy-PEP involves the administration of doxycycline within 72 h after sexual contact to prevent the acquisition of bacterial STIs. Several studies have demonstrated the efficacy of doxy-PEP in reducing STI incidence among vulnerable populations, including MSM and transgender women. Three pivotal randomized clinical trials (IPERGAY, Doxy-PEP, and ANRS DoxyVAC) have demonstrated the efficacy of doxy-PEP among cisgender MSM and transgender women, particularly those living with HIV or using HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), who reported at least one STI in the preceding six months.18, 19, 20 In contrast, a study in Kenya involving cisgender women did not demonstrate efficacy, attributable to low adherence rates.21 Additionally, an ecological analysis and a clinic-based study conducted in San Francisco reported a significant reduction in populational-level STI incidence following the implementation of doxy-PEP.22,23 Real-world evidence further supports the effectiveness of doxy-PEP in routine clinical practice, particularly for the prevention of syphilis and chlamydia. Its effectiveness against gonorrhea, however, varies according to local patterns of antimicrobial resistance.24, 25, 26

Currently, Latin America lacks clinical trials and official guidelines regarding doxy-PEP. However, anecdotal reports indicate that doxy-PEP has circulated informally within certain social networks, although systematic data on its use among sexual and gender minority (SGM) populations in the region remain scarce. A web-based survey of 447 MSM and transgender women in Peru found high willingness to use doxy-PEP but low levels of awareness and previous use.27 Similarly, a recent qualitative study in Brazil reported that MSM who used non-prescribed doxy-PEP faced challenges in accessing reliable information, relying primarily on informal social networks for guidance.28 To address these gaps, this study seeks to contribute to the still scarce body of regional evidence by generating data on doxy-PEP awareness, interest, and use among SGM across three Latin American countries (Brazil, Mexico and Peru). Additionally, we assessed factors associated with doxy-PEP awareness overall and for each country.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a cross-sectional online survey conducted between August and December 2024 in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru to assess the health, sexual behavior, and knowledge of HIV and prevention among SGM in the context of the ImPrEP Project.29,30 Participants were recruited following approaches used in previous studies.30, 31, 32, 33 We used advertisements on dating apps (Scruff and Grindr) and social media (Instagram and Facebook), which contained a brief study description and a link to the online survey. Participants who clicked on the link were directed to a secure online platform where they provided informed consent before proceeding with the survey. Participants could also share the link with others via messaging apps (e.g. WhatsApp) or e-mail. The survey instrument was programmed on Alchemer (Brazil and Mexico) and SurveyMonkey (Peru), administered in Portuguese (Brazil) or Spanish (Mexico and Peru) and took approximately 20–30 min to complete. The survey included seven sections: sociodemographic, sexual behavior, STI (including doxy-PEP), perceived vulnerability to HIV, HIV (knowledge, testing, prevention and treatment), substance use, and internalized homonegativity. All items included a non-response option. Participants were able to change/review answers but could not skip questions. Only one response per IP address was allowed.

Inclusion criteria for the study were being 18 years or older, identifying as a SGM person, and living in Brazil, Mexico, or Peru. Participants were excluded if they: (1) reported having previously completed the survey (i.e., answered “no” to the question, “Is this the first time you are answering this questionnaire?”); (2) were assigned female sex at birth, given the absence of randomized trial data demonstrating doxy-PEP efficacy in this population and the lack of official recommendations supporting its use; (3) did not self-identify as cisgender man, transgender woman, travesti, or other queer, non-binary, or gender-diverse individuals assigned male sex at birth; (4) cisgender men reporting no sex with men; or (5) did not complete the last question of STI section (doxy-PEP interest).

Outcomes

We assessed three doxy-PEP-related outcomes. Doxy-PEP awareness was assessed by asking whether participants had ever heard about the use of doxycycline for this purpose, reinforcing that we were referring to STI prevention and not HIV PEP (“Have you ever heard about the use of doxycycline for the prevention of syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea after sex (doxy-PEP)?”). Interest was assessed by asking: “Would you be interested in using doxycycline for STI prevention (syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea) after oral, vaginal, or anal sex (doxy-PEP)?”. Doxy-PEP previous use was based on self-reported prior use of doxycycline after any sexual exposure to prevent bacterial STIs: “Have you ever used doxycycline for STI prevention (syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea) after sex (oral, vaginal, or anal)?”. All response options for the three questions were “yes” and “no”.

Measures

The following variables were included in the analysis. Age was described in median and interquartile range [IQR], and categorized as 18–30 and > 30 years, considering high prevalence and incidence of STI during the ImPrEP study among MSM and transgender women aged 18–30 years.4 Other sociodemographic characteristics encompassed gender identity (cisgender man, travesti or transgender woman, and nonbinary, queer or other), race/ethnicity (White, Asian, Black, Indigenous, Pardo or Mestizo or Mixed Race), educational level (primary, secondary, or post-secondary), country and place of residence (state capital city vs. other locations). Individual monthly income (not including other people from the household) was categorized into up to 1 minimum wage or more than 1 minimum wage. By the time of the analysis, one minimum wage corresponded to 270 USD (1518 BRL) in Brazil, 408 USD (7468 MXN) in Mexico, and 277 USD (1025 PEN) in Peru. Sexual behavior was assessed by the number of sex partners in the past six months (≤5 or >5) and by engagement in transactional sex (yes or no). Number of sex partners was collected as “no partner”, “only one”, “2–5”, “6–10”, “more than 10”, “I don't want to answer”, and dichotomized as ≤5 or >5 partners following cut-off used in previous studies to evaluate vulnerability for HIV/STI.30,34,35 Participants were asked whether they had received a diagnosis of any STI (including syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, mpox, hepatitis B, or others) from a healthcare provider in the past six months (yes or no). HIV prevention was assessed by self-reported experience with PrEP, categorized as never or prior use (current or previous). HIV status was self-reported as positive, negative, or unknown. Stimulant drug use was assessed by asking about the use of substances such as cocaine, crack cocaine, or ecstasy/MDMA in the past 6 months (yes or no). Internalized homonegativity was measured using the Reactions to Homosexuality Scale (RHS), a validated instrument that was adapted for use among SGM. The scale includes seven items rated on a 7-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater levels of internalized stigma (scores ranging 0–42).36 HIV knowledge was evaluated using the HIV/AIDS Knowledge Assessment Tool (HIV-KA), based on a predefined set of questions about HIV transmission and prevention, with higher scores meaning higher knowledge (scores ranging 0–12).37 Perceived vulnerability to HIV was assessed among participants who did not report living with HIV, using the 8-item Perceived Risk of HIV Scale (PRHS). Total scores ranged from 10 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater perceived vulnerability to HIV.38 For participants from Brazil who reported having used doxy-PEP, additional characteristics related to its use and prescription were collected.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive analysis to summarize the characteristics of our sample according to country (Brazil, Mexico, and Peru) and any STIs diagnosis in the past six months (yes vs. no). Furthermore, we compared characteristics according to doxy-PEP awareness (yes vs. no), interest (yes vs. no), and use (yes vs. no). For these comparisons, we used chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and Mann–Whitney test for discrete variables. We conducted adjusted logistic regression analyses to identify factors associated with doxy-PEP awareness overall and by country, as awareness represented our primary outcome of interest and a key barrier in the regional context. Additionally, the limited number of doxy-PEP use events precluded stable multivariable modeling for this outcome. Covariates were selected a priori based on established theoretical and empirical relevance in shaping STI vulnerability, health service access, and engagement with biomedical prevention strategies among sexual and gender minorities. Accordingly, we included the following covariates: country, age, gender, educational level, monthly personal income, living in a capital city, number of sexual partners, HIV/PrEP status, any STI, and stimulant use. The continuous variables (internalized homonegativity score, HIV knowledge score, and perceived vulnerability to HIV score) were standardized to z-scores for inclusion in regression models, whereas categorical variables were dichotomized accordingly. Standardization involved subtracting the overall mean score from participant's score and dividing the difference by the standard deviation. We reported adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.0 (www.r-project.com).

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Instituto Nacional de Infectologia Evandro Chagas (INI)-Fiocruz institutional review board (#CAAE 82021918.0.0000.5262) in Brazil, the research ethics committee of the National Institute of Psychiatry Ramón de la Fuente Muñiz (#CEI/C/015/2024) in Mexico, and the Universidad Peruana Cayetano Heredia Ethical Committee for Research with Human Subjects (#214050), in Peru. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the survey. Participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses and received no compensation.

Results

Of 35,466 persons accessed the study link, 11,332 were excluded (Fig. 1). Among 24,134 eligible persons, 16,081 completed the STI section of the survey and were included in this analysis (Brazil: n = 10,269; Mexico: n = 4081; Peru: n = 1731). Overall, the median age was 39 years (interquartile range [IQR] 31–47), with 77.0% aged over 30 years. Most participants identified as cisgender men (96.0%), with 0.7% travesti or transgender women, and 3.3% as non-binary, queer, or other sexual and gender diverse identities (Table 1). Most participants self-identified as Asian, Indigenous, Mixed race (including Pardo or Mestizo), or Black (56.5%), while the majority reported post-secondary education (71.5%), income above one minimum wage (79.7%), and lived in a capital city (79.4%). In the past six months, 39.6% reported having more than 5 sexual partners, 9.0% transactional sex, and 21.0% stimulant use. Overall, 25.0% were living with HIV, 24.0% reported being negative for HIV and ever used PrEP, whereas 51.0% never used PrEP. Internalized homonegativity score was 10 (IQR 4–17), median HIV knowledge assessment score was 11 (IQR 10–12), and median perceived vulnerability to HIV score was 26 (IQR 22–29). Compared to Brazil, participants from Mexico and Peru were younger, less frequently self-identified as White and reported lower income. In addition, participants in Peru had lower education, lived in the capital city, and had higher internalized homonegativity. In adjusted logistic models, participants from Mexico had significantly higher odds of doxy-PEP awareness compared to those from Peru (Table 4). Overall, post-secondary education (aOR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.08–1.36), having >5 sex partners (aOR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.20–1.46), reporting stimulant use (aOR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.07–1.35), previous/current PrEP use or living with HIV (aOR: 2.76; 95% CI: 2.49–3.05), and scoring higher in HIV knowledge assessment (aOR per one standard deviation increase: 1.35; 95% CI: 1.28–1.44) increased the odds of doxy-PEP awareness. Age >30 years (aOR: 0.88; 95% CI: 0.79–0.98) and higher internalized homonegativity (aOR per one standard deviation increase: aOR: 0.87; 95% CI: 0.83–0.91) were inversely associated with doxy-PEP awareness. Interest to use doxy-PEP was high (88.3%), with highest proportions in Mexico (94.9%, n = 3873/4081) and Peru (93.3%, n = 1615/1731) compared to Brazil (84.8%, n = 8711/10,269) (Table 3). Compared to participants not interested in doxy-PEP, those interested were younger (median age: 38 years [IQR: 31–46] vs. 41 years [IQR: 32–52]; p < 0.001), more frequently self-identified as Black, Mixed Race, Indigenous or Asian (57.9% vs. 46.6%; p < 0.001), had post-secondary education (71.9% vs. 68.6%; p < 0.01), and lived in capital cities (80.1% vs. 74.6%; p < 0.001). They also more frequently reported more sex partners (>5: 42.1% vs. 20.8%; p < 0.001), transactional sex (9.3% vs. 6.5%; p < 0.001), stimulant use (22.5% vs. 9.4%; p < 0.001), ever PrEP use (25.5% vs. 12.7%; p < 0.001), or living with HIV (26.0% vs. 17.5%; p < 0.001), lower internalized homonegativity (10 [IQR 4–17] vs. 11 [IQR 5–19]; p < 0.001) and higher perceived vulnerability to HIV (26 [IQR 22–29] vs. 23 [18–27]; p < 0.001). Participants interested in doxy-PEP reported higher awareness (38.4% vs. 31.9%, p < 0.001) and previous use of doxy-PEP (7.6% vs. 4.2%, p < 0.001).

Doxy-PEP previous use was reported by 7.2% of all participants, with higher rates in Mexico (9.0%, n = 366/4081) and Peru (8.4%, n = 145/1731) compared to Brazil (6.3%, n = 644/10,269) (Table 3). Compared to participants with no doxy-PEP prior use, those with prior use were more frequently aged older than 30 years (79.5% vs. 76.8%; p = 0.040), had post-secondary education (78.4% vs. 71.0%; p < 0.001), higher income (more than one minimum wage: 83.5% vs. 79.4%; p < 0.001), lived in capital cities (84.2% vs. 79.1%; p < 0.001), reported more sex partners (>5: 64.0% vs. 37.7%; p < 0.001), transactional sex (13.0% vs. 8.7%; p < 0.001) and stimulant use (36.2% vs. 19.8%; p < 0.001). Doxy-PEP previous use was more common among participants reporting ever PrEP use (45.1% vs. 22.4%; p < 0.001) or living with HIV (34.5% vs. 24.3%; p < 0.001), previous STI diagnosis in the last six months (29.1% vs. 14.4%, p < 0.001), lower internalized homonegativity score (8 [IQR 3–16] vs. 10 [IQR 5–17]; p < 0.001) and higher median score in HIV knowledge assessment (11 [IQR 11–12] vs. 11 [IQR 10–12]; p < 0.001). Participants with prior doxy-PEP use more frequently expressed higher interest in doxy-PEP (93.2% vs. 87.9%; p < 0.001). In Brazil, all participants with prior doxy-PEP use (n = 644) reported obtaining it through a prescription from a healthcare provider. Regarding the number of pills taken per episode, 14.0% reported taking more than three pills, 8.7% took three pills, 61.0% took two pills, and 17.0% took only one pill. Concerning timing, 24.0% reported taking doxy-PEP within 2 h after sex, 34.0% between two and 24 h, and 28.0% between 24 and 72 h.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess awareness, interest and use of doxy-PEP among SGM in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru. To our knowledge, it is the largest survey to date with this objective and the first conducted in Latin America. Despite the high STI burden in the region, awareness of doxy-PEP remains limited. Nevertheless, interest in using doxy-PEP was remarkably high, even among individuals who were not previously aware, suggesting a substantial unmet need for innovative and accessible STI prevention tools in the region. The lack of national clinical guidelines or pilot implementation programs in most Latin American and Caribbean countries likely contributes to low awareness and fragmented access to doxy-PEP across the region. In Brazil, doxy-PEP has received regulatory approval by January 2026, but has not yet been incorporated into the public health system as an implemented policy. To date, no other country in the region has issued original national guidelines or launched government-led pilot programs. Meanwhile, international momentum continues to grow, with several high-income countries publishing guidance on its rational use.39, 40, 41 Our findings suggest that SGM populations in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru are not only interested but also well-positioned to benefit from the rollout of doxy-PEP, if supported by investments in provider training, health system capacity, and robust implementation research.

Our findings align with recent international surveys from high-income countries, which describe a wide range of awareness (24–54%) and generally low to moderate previous use (1.8–19%) of doxy-PEP among MSM and transgender individuals.42, 43, 44, 45, 46 In our multicountry Latin American sample, overall awareness (37%) and previous use (7%) were comparable to reports from Germany (39% and 7%, respectively), Belgium (40% and 10%, respectively), and Italy (28% and 9%, respectively).42,44,47,48 Data from a previous survey in Peru reported lower doxy-PEP awareness (15.7%) and similar prior utilization levels (9%), when compared to participants from Peru enrolled in our study.27 Notably, interest in using doxy-PEP was very high across contexts, reaching 88% in our study. This is similar to the 73–95% reported in the United States, the United Kingdom, Peru and Australia.27,42,46,49,50

We also observed important regional variations. Compared to Brazil, awareness was higher in Mexico, and interest was higher in both Mexico and Peru. Differences in participant characteristics across countries, such as higher education levels, older age and sex partners in Mexico compared to Brazil may partly account for these patterns. However, these differences alone do not allow definitive conclusions about broader cultural, structural, or health-system factors that might also shape awareness and interest. In Brazil, despite substantial interest, few participants knew what doxy-PEP was, possibly reflecting cultural concerns about antibiotic use or limited public discourse on the topic. Encouragingly, most participants in Brazil who had used doxy-PEP did so under medical supervision and generally adhered to recommended dosing schedules. This contrasts with recent data from the Netherlands highlighting concerns about emergent informal and unsupervised doxycycline use for STI prevention.51 Taken together, these findings suggest that, although interest in using doxy-PEP is consistently high, there is room for health services to engage proactively through educational campaigns and structured counseling to support informed, shared decision-making about its risks and benefits. Such strategies could promote rational, evidence-based use of doxy-PEP among those who might benefit most, while also strengthening dialog between at-risk communities and the healthcare system. Accordingly, interest may represent early openness to doxy-PEP rather than true willingness to use it, which aligns with the low actual use observed.

Doxy-PEP awareness was associated with behavioral markers of elevated STI vulnerability, including multiple sexual partners, and previous STI diagnosis (only for Brazil), echoing findings from prior studies in other countries, such as the United States, United Kingdom and Australia.46,49,50 Low awareness but high interest in doxy-PEP may reflect the absence of national guidelines in Latin America, where its use remains off-label, particularly when contrasted with countries in which doxy-PEP had already begun to be formally recommended and more widely prescribed at the time these data were collected. These associations suggest that individuals at higher vulnerability to STI may be more actively engaged in seeking biomedical prevention strategies. However, our results also highlight persistent structural and social barriers, as illustrated by the fact that doxy-PEP awareness was significantly lower among participants reporting higher levels of internalized homonegativity. This aligns with results from a large study conducted in Brazil showing high rates of stigma and discrimination toward SGM during the mpox outbreak.52 Together, these data underscore how intersecting forms of stigma, including internalized stigma, may limit access to and engagement with emerging STI prevention strategies such as doxy-PEP. Ultimately, stigma and discrimination continue to undermine engagement with sexual health services and limit access to essential information.28,53 Addressing these challenges will require inclusive health education and culturally competent care that directly confront stigma at both individual and institutional levels, ensuring that biomedical tools like doxy-PEP effectively reach those who stand to benefit most.

The strong association between doxy-PEP awareness and ever PrEP use underscores the potential of integrated prevention service delivery. Programs combining HIV testing, PrEP, STI screening, and doxy-PEP can provide comprehensive, person-centered care that builds trust and improves retention among marginalized populations. Expanding access to doxy-PEP through existing PrEP delivery platforms, where users already receive biomedical and behavioral support, may enhance adherence and promote rational use, especially when paired with clinical monitoring and guidance. For instance, following the public health recommendation for doxy-PEP prescription in San Francisco, USA, a notable shift was observed in the population-level incidence curves of syphilis and chlamydia, demonstrating the positive impact of this intervention.22

Our findings highlight the potential for doxy-PEP scale-up to exacerbate existing health disparities if not accompanied by targeted interventions. This was evidenced by prior research demonstrating worse outcomes in the PrEP care continuum among people at heightened social vulnerability.29,54,55 In our study, participants with lower educational attainment, those living outside capital cities, and those disconnected from PrEP services had significantly lower levels of doxy-PEP awareness. Addressing these disparities will require health education and implementation strategies that explicitly account for social determinants of health, targeting populations with heightened socioeconomic vulnerability, particularly those disengaged from HIV prevention and care services to foster equitable uptake and sustained access to doxy-PEP. Doxy-PEP implementation in countries from the Global South must also contend with additional structural challenges, including limited capacity for antimicrobial resistance surveillance, the reliance on out-of-pocket payment for doxycycline in many settings, and restricted access to molecular STI diagnostics.

This study has limitations. First, the cross-sectional design captures data at a single time point, preventing assessment of temporal and causal relationships. Second, recruitment through dating apps and social media may have biased the sample toward urban, older, digitally connected SGM, limiting broader generalizability. Third, our sample may be subjected to potential selection bias due to the large number of participants who did not reach the outcome item (interest in doxy-PEP). In previous online studies, a large number of participants did not completed the questionnaire, similar or higher to our study.31,32,56 A previous online survey among Brazilian MSM have shown disparities on study completion, with more participants of younger age, lower education and lower income not completing the study.56 Forth, data were self-reported, which may introduce recall and social desirability biases. Self-reported STI diagnoses may be underestimated due to limited knowledge, irregular testing, or fear of stigma. Retrospective reports of sexual partners, stimulant use, and prior doxy-PEP use are susceptible to recall imprecision or intentional omission. Fifth, our main outcomes (doxy-PEP awareness, interest, and use) were assessed through dichotomous questions, which may not capture the full range of nuances surrounding these constructs. Moreover, caution should be taken when interpreting awareness and interest. Awareness may not capture depth or quality of knowledge and interest does not substitute for validated measures of intention to use and acceptability. Sixth, caution should be taken for comparisons across countries, as our sample is predominately composed by participants from Brazil. According to recent estimates, the total population of the three countries is 379 million, 213 million in Brazil (56%), 132 million in Mexico (35%) and 34 million in Peru (9%). In our study, we included 16,081 participants, 10,269 in Brazil (63%), 4081 in Mexico (35%) and 1731 in Peru (9%). Therefore, although our study is not representative of the whole population, our proportions are aligned with the proportions of the overall population of these countries. Lastly, detailed information on doxy-PEP use is only available for Brazil, precluding generalization for other countries. Reports of obtaining a medical prescription and adhering to recommended dosing may be affected by social desirability bias, given the sensitive and off-label nature of doxy-PEP in Brazil. Participants may have potentially overstate medically supervised or guideline-aligned use. Despite these limitations, our findings provide timely and regionally specific evidence on doxy-PEP interest, awareness, and use among SGM in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates a striking mismatch between high interest and low awareness and prior use of doxy-PEP among sexual and gender minorities in Brazil, Mexico, and Peru. This gap represents a critical but underutilized opportunity to respond to the growing burden of bacterial STIs in the region. Addressing this gap will require more than the inclusion of biomedical tools like doxy-PEP in public health strategies. It demands targeted investments in health system capacity, provider training, and community-centered education. Equally important is the need to confront the social and structural determinants that limit access to care, including stigma, discrimination, and socioeconomic inequality. By integrating biomedical innovation with equity-driven implementation strategies, Latin American countries are uniquely positioned to lead the development of a regional model for comprehensive STI prevention that is inclusive, evidence-based, and responsive to the lived realities of those most affected.
