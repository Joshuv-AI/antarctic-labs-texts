A Comprehensive Water Chemistry Dataset for Iranian Rivers
Authors: Erfan Zarei, Roohollah Noori, Changhyun Jun, Sayed M Bateni, Peiman Kianmehr, Senlin Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRiver water quality data are essential for managing surface water resources and protecting terrestrial-aquatic ecosystems. While the number of compiled global river water quality datasets is growing, there is a notable lack of available data in Asian countries, especially Iran. To address this gap, this study compiled a comprehensive water chemistry dataset for Iranian rivers covering 1964–2020. The dataset includes 14 chemical compounds and 11 water quality indices, totaling 5,968,568 records (i.e., distinct measurement points × variables × time steps) obtained from 1,591 monitoring stations across the country’s rivers. Our avialable chemical compounds include total dissolved solids, pH, electrical conductivity, carbonate, chloride, bicarbonate, sulfate, calcium, potassium, magnesium, sodium, nitrate, ΣCations, and ΣAnions. The calculated water quality indices in our dataset were sodium adsorption ratio, Larson index, sodium percentage, Kelly’s ratio, total hardness, residual sodium carbonates, magnesium hazard, chloro-alkaline index, corrosivity ratio, permeability index, and saturation index. This dataset can support large-scale river water quality assessment studies at the national level and complement the available global river water quality databases.Subject terms: Hydrology, Environmental monitoring

Background & Summary

Human activities and natural processes deteriorate river water quality, engendering human health, livelihoods and food security. In addition, they impact social development and economic growth, as well as the protection of ecosystems1–3. Additionally, the hydrological features of rivers make them susceptible to pollutants, necessitating water quality management to ensure sustainable development4–6. United Nations reports estimate that over three billion individuals worldwide are at risk of illness because the quality of their water sources remains unknown due to insufficient data7. Therefore, insights into river water quality are required to protect public health, conserve ecosystem integrity, and meet SDG #6 — Sustainable Development Goal #6 (Clean Water and Sanitation)8. To meet these objectives, high-quality data are required to monitor the river water quality and make evidence-based policies9. Therefore, consistent, large-scale, hydrological water quality studies are required both regionally and globally10.

Several studies have introduced water quality datasets across different countries and the globe. For instance, Ebeling et al.11 presented the first extensive water quality dataset for 1,386 German catchments. The dataset, called “QUADICA”, includes time series of riverine macronutrient concentrations and diffuse nitrogen forcing data, with monthly median discharge and nutrient concentrations provided for 140 stations with long-term data. do Nascimento et al.12 developed CAMELS-CH-Chem, a database including up to 40 water quality parameters across 115 Swiss catchments covering the period 1981–2020. Sterle et al.13 expanded the widely used CAMELSdataset by integrating comprehensive stream water chemistry data (CAMELS-Chem) for 516 catchments across the continental US, paired with atmospheric deposition, streamflow, meteorology, and catchment attributes from 1980 to 2018. Lin et al.14 developed a comprehensive dataset of water quality for China, including over 330,000 daily, weekly, and monthly measurements collected between 1980 and 2022 from inland and coastal areas. By integrating and standardizing five global datasets, Virro et al.8 developed a river water quality dataset named “GRQA”, which included 42 variables and more than 17 million measurements from 1898 to 2020. While GRQA’s variable coverage is extensive compared to previous global datasets, important limitations remain. One such dataset is GEMStat, the global water quality database and information system maintained by the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP), which provides harmonized, long-term water quality data from monitoring stations worldwide15. Another is GLORICH, an open-access database that compiles river water chemistry records from diverse sources to enable large-scale analyses of global biogeochemical processes and freshwater quality trends16. Although GRQA surpasses these earlier datasets in the number of variables covered, its temporal and spatial coverage in Asia is limited. In particular, GRQA includes only a few parameters—such as dissolved oxygen and total suspended solids—measured at fewer than five monitoring stations in Iran, indicating substantial gaps in water quality data for this region.Rotteveel et al.17 collected, cleaned, standardized, and consolidated an open-access dataset from six (non) governmental programs, which includes information on locations, methods, samples, and a ShapeFile of site locations. This dataset includes water chemistry data for various water bodies—canals, streams, rivers, lakes, ponds, and reservoirs—spanning seven continents, over 33,700 sites, 24 variables, and more than five million water samples (1960–2022). However, the study also identified notable spatial data gaps in Africa and Asia, underscoring the need for increased data gathering and sharing efforts in these regions.

This study’s objective is to address the above-mentioned limitations by compiling an openly available river water chemistry dataset across Iran. This comprehensive, publicly accessible, compiled dataset facilitates national water quality assessment studies at the regional scale (i.e., Iran) and makes notable contributions to global river water quality assessment.

Methods

The dataset was compiled from 30 Excel files, each representing a major Iranian sub-basin. These files contain comprehensive water chemistry parameters essential for assessing river ecosystem health and water usability, including total dissolved solids (TDS), electrical conductivity (EC), potential of hydrogen (pH), carbonate, bicarbonate, chloride, sulfate, nitrate, ΣAnions, calcium, magnesium, sodium, potassium, and ΣCations. The raw data were obtained from the IWRMC Data Archive (https://stu.wrm.ir) and were merged into a single, unified dataset covering the period from 1964 to 2020. In this study, 11 major water quality indices were also calculated and added to the compiled dataset to support assessments of water-use suitability for agricultural, industrial, and drinking purposes. These indices include sodium adsorption ratio (SAR)18, percent sodium (Na%), total hardness (TH), residual sodium carbonate (RSC)17, magnesium hazard (MH)18–20, Kelly’s ratio (KR)21, Larson index (LI)22, corrosivity ratio (CR)23, chloro-alkaline index (CAI-1)24, permeability index (PI)25, and saturation index (SI)26,27.

Study region

Iran, located in a (semi)arid region in southwestern Asia, covers a region of approximately 1.65 × 106 km2 (Fig. 1), with an average annual precipitation of 241 mm (40% less than the global average). The distribution of precipitation across the country is both spatially and temporally unequal: approximately 73% of Iran’s land-surface area is arid and gets only ~27% of the country’s total precipitation28. The annual average temperature in Iran ranges from below 6 °C (northwest) to over 28 °C (southeast)29. Additionally, Iran’s pan evaporation shows notable spatial variation, ranging from about 700 mm/year in the humid northern areas to more than 4000 mm/year in the arid central and southern regions.

Geographic location of Iran, the spatial distribution of its river network, and the locations of hydrometric monitoring stations (scale: 1:14,000,000).

A total of 1,343 rivers covers Iran’s territory (scale, 1:250000). These rivers have a combined length of 66,575 km, with 40,918 km, 23,650 km, and 2,007 km being perennial, seasonal, and dry rivers, respectively30. Of these, 1,015 km are border rivers, whereas the remaining 65,560 km are inland rivers. Notable rivers in Iran include Karoon, Dez, Karkheh, Atrak, Zayanderud, Sefid-roud, and Haraz. Surface water provides approximately 5 km3 of water to around 300,000 km2 of the country’s wetlands, meeting about 44% of the water requirements for urban and agriculture zones. Additionally, it replenishes up to 10 km3 of the country’s groundwater resources31. In recent decades, the management of surface water resources in Iran has been driven more by political considerations than by hydrological factors. For clarity, Iran is divided into 30 hydrological sub-basins—smaller watershed units within larger river basins that help organize water resource management based on natural hydrological boundaries32.

Water pollution in Iran’s rivers is a serious concern. The expansion of agricultural and industrial activities near rivers, without considering environmental impacts, has led to increasing amounts of industrial, urban, and agricultural effluents being discharged into the rivers 33,34. In some areas, the improper disposal of solid waste has exacerbated river pollution, with plastic waste and hazardous materials entering waterways and causing long-term environmental hazards. The pollution of rivers in Iran is widespread and not limited to any specific region. For instance, research has indicated that several of Iran’s major rivers, including the Karun in the west (Iran’s largest river), Zayanderud in center, and the Aras in the northwest, are contaminated with pollutants, such as heavy metals, organic compounds, and microplastics, largely due to urban wastes and industrial discharges35–37. In northern Iran, where almost half of the country’s rice is produced, pesticide-use has contributed to 19% of water pollution, with organic phosphorus pesticides accounting for 70% of pesticide usage38. Additionally, in many Iranian rivers — particularly in the central regions — unregulated aquaculture activities have caused notable ecological damage by degrading water quality and disrupting aquatic ecosystems. Given these findings and the fact that rivers supply over 45% of Iran’s national water demand, maintaining a sustainable water supply and managing resources effectively requires a thorough understanding and assessment of river water quality to protect public health, preserve ecosystems, and support agricultural productivity.

Historical overview of data

The Iranian water chemistry data were collected from various hydrometric monitoring stations across Iranian rivers. All river water quality data used were sampled and analyzed under the supervision of the Iran Water Resources Management Company (IWRMC). The IWRMC has developed a comprehensive framework to enhance knowledge on Iran’s water resources through the establishment and collection of data from hydrometric monitoring stations. The surface water monitoring network dates back to 1964, when the first recorded data were collected from a station on the Mohammad Abad River in north Iran, near the Caspian Sea. That same year, additional stations were established on rivers around Tehran. Since then, the network has been continuously expanded and upgraded, in line with global standards and available resources. Recognizing the need to review and optimize the network after years of operation, IWRMC initiated a comprehensive review in 2001. The goal was to optimize the system based on service requirements and international standards and to evaluate the status of the country’s water resource monitoring network. This review considered factors such as study needs, international best practices, human resources, and available equipment to ensure that the water resources across different hydrological basins and study areas were accurately assessed. Presently, data are available from 1,591 hydrometric stations where water quality data were collected at the station locations. The equipment used for analyzing water samples has also been upgraded.

Spatial distribution of the compiled dataset

The distribution of records across 30 Iranian sub-basins reveals a strategic focus on water-rich regions, with significant variability in sampling density (Fig. 2). Sub-basins such as Lake Urmia and Salt Lake, each with over 500,000 records, are among the most heavily monitored, while northern regions like the Sefid-Roud and Qaresou sub-basins also show high sampling effort. In contrast, arid sub-basins such as the Lut Desert and Siahkooh Desert have fewer than 100,000 records. Sub-basins like Karkheh and Bakhtegan, with approximately 140,000 and 320,000 records respectively, reflect a moderate level of coverage, whereas eastern areas such as South Baluchestan and Patargan remain sparsely sampled, with Patargan registering only 12,903 records. These disparities may introduce spatial biases in hydrological and water quality models, potentially limiting the reliability of predictions in under-monitored regions. Furthermore, uneven monitoring could hinder the development of balanced water management policies, particularly for regions where data scarcity masks emerging environmental pressures. Insufficient data coverage may lead to underestimation of risks such as droughts or pollution hotspots, impairing proactive resource allocation. This unevenness also complicates the calibration and validation of regional models, reducing confidence in their forecasts and management recommendations. Addressing these gaps through more representative sampling would strengthen modeling accuracy and support evidence-based policymaking across diverse hydro-climatic zones.

Data Records

The current version of the Iranian river water chemistry dataset is stored at a Zenodo repository: 10.5281/zenodo.1406565640. The repository consists of a single Excel file that includes 14 chemical compounds and 11 calculated water quality indices, covering records from 1,591 monitoring stations over a period of more than 56 years. This results in a total of 5,968,568 records. The dataset contains 43 columns, beginning with sub-basin names, river names, and geographic coordinates, followed by data collection details, measurement dates, the 14 water chemistry compounds, and the 11 calculated indices. Technical Validation

The IWRMC implements rigorous quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) measures, along with out-of-range protocols, to ensure that collected water chemistry data are reliable and suitable for water management applications. Sampling is first conducted in the field based on the guidelines suggested by Standard Methods for the Examination of Water and Wastewater41. Subsequently, river water chemistry analyses are performed in accredited laboratories licensed by the Iran Department of Environment. Laboratory reports are then submitted to the local authority and relevant water authroty departments for further review. If any measurements are found to be outside acceptable ranges, the corresponding samples are flagged and returned to the laboratory for retesting. After this stage of verification, the validated results are forwarded to the IWRMC, where an additional layer of QA/QC procedures is applied to ensure data reliability. Only datasets that pass these rigorous checks are deposited in the publicly accessible IWRMC Data Archive (https://stu.wrm.ir). This database is widely used for research, and individual researchers can report any problematic entries, further strengthening data integrity. In addition to these measures, we applied two supplementary validation techniques—TDS–EC correlation analysis and ionic charge balance checks—to further enhance the robustness of the dataset. The correlation between TDS and EC can serve as a validation tool in water chemistry analysis. Usage Notes

To effectively utilize our compiled water chemistry dataset for Iranian river sub-basins, overall descriptions are given in Table 1. The deposited Excel file type makes our dataset highly flexible and applicable to many tabular data analysis packages. As a time-series dataset, our dataset has many potential usages for both educational and research purposes. Those interested in our dataset could explore, among other things, water quality trend analysis, agricultural expansion, water quality assessment, and calibration/validation of water quality models. In addition, our dataset complements the available databases, facilitating in-depth analyses of global river water quality. The aim of the collected data is to present the dataset as originally observed. Extreme values have been retained to preserve the integrity and natural variability of the data; however, technical validation procedures were conducted to ensure overall data quality. Users are encouraged to apply domain-specific filtering or transformations as needed for their own analyses.

Limitations

Our compiled dataset focuses on state-monitored water chemistry variables such as pH, EC, TH, TDS, major ions, and key water quality indices relevant to assessing river water suitability for agricultural, drinking, and industrial purposes. However, data on other important pollutant categories—such as heavy metals, pesticides, and microbial contaminants—are largely absent. This gap is not due to measurement errors but reflects the current scope and limitations of national monitoring policies in Iran, which do not routinely include these parameters in public datasets. Such data, when available, are typically limited to localized studies of small water bodies. Therefore, future research could aim to expand this dataset by integrating it with local or regional studies to incorporate a broader range of water quality indicators.
