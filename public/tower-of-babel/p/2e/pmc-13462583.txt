Photocatalytic reductive carboxylation of unactivated hydrazones with CO2
Authors: Guang-Mei Cao, Li-Li Liao, Qi Zhou, Xin-Long Hu, He-Xin Song, Lei Song, Xia Jiang, Jian-Heng Ye, Da-Gang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCarboxylation with CO2 is one of the most sustainable strategies for synthesizing carboxylic acids. Particularly, the reductive carboxylation of carbonyl derivatives with CO2 offers a promising route to valuable α-hydroxy acids, α-amino acids and relatives. However, catalytic methods for this transformation remain underexplored and predominantly limited to carboxylation of activated carbonyl derivatives, which rely on the generation of stabilized organometallic intermediates or carbanions to react with CO2. In this study, we report a reductive carboxylation of unactivated hydrazones with CO2 via visible-light photocatalysis. Diverse unactivated hydrazones, such as dialkyl substituted ketone hydrazones, cyclic ketone hydrazones and alkyl aldehyde hydrazones, are all suitable for such a carboxylation. The process enables the efficient synthesis of diverse valuable α-hydrazino acids in good yields via selective cleavage of C = N π bond, distinguishing it from prior hydrazone carboxylation methods, which typically involve C = N double bond cleavage. Moreover, this protocol exhibits mild reaction conditions, good functional group tolerance, easy scale up and facile derivatization of products into α-amino acids. Mechanistic investigations and density functional theory (DFT) calculations support the radical addition of CO2 radical anion to C = N bonds, which represents a unique umpolung strategy for reductive carboxylation with CO2.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Sustainability, Photocatalysis

Introduction

The high-value transformation of carbon dioxide (CO2), a well-known greenhouse gas that serves as an abundant, renewable and nontoxic C1 source, is a promising research topic towards sustainable chemical synthesis1–3. Carboxylation with CO2 has emerged as an intriguing strategy to generate important carboxylic acids and derivatives4–9. In this context, the carboxylation of readily accessible carbonyl derivatives, such as ketones, aldehydes, imines and hydrazones, with CO2 provides a rapid and user-friendly avenue to generate synthetically and biologically valuable α-hydroxy acids, α-amino acids (α-AAs), α-hydrazino acids and related derivatives (Fig. 1a)10. This strategy holds great advantage over classical methods, such as the Strecker reaction, by substituting toxic cyanide reagents with CO2 as the carboxyl source (Fig. 1b)11,12. Despite these advantages, weak electrophilicity of CO2 presents a substantial challenge and makes it behave differently from nucleophilic cyanide, which readily attacks C = N bonds due to favorable polarity matching. Consequently, significant efforts have been devoted to the umpolung of C = N bonds in imines, generating nucleophilic intermediates that react with CO2 to give α-AAs and relatives. Several strategies utilizing low-valent metal-promoted reaction13–18, electrochemical processes19,20 or photochemical methods21–23 have been reported for the reductive carboxylation of activated imines with CO2, forming α-amino organometallic or α-amino carbanion intermediates. However, such protocols are mainly limited to activated imines, especially those aryl-substituted imines. The bench stable hydrazones, which share structural similarities with imines, have also been employed in carboxylation with CO2. However, these transformations generally result in complete C = N bond cleavage driven by the thermodynamic stability of the released N2, yielding alkenyl or alkyl carboxylic acids24–26. To date, there is no report on reductive carboxylation of hydrazones with CO2 to generate valuable α-hydrazino acids via selective cleavage of C = N π bond. Therefore, it is a strong impetus for the development of a distinct mechanistic pattern to realize reductive carboxylation of C = N bonds in hydrazones with CO2 and to extend this method to a broader range of carbonyl derivatives.

Recently, carboxylation reactions involving CO2 radical anion (CO2•−) intermediate have received extensive attention due to its unique reactivity and selectivity27–29. In general, CO2•− is a highly reactive radical anion species that can act both as a strong reductant and as a carboxyl reagent (Fig. 1c). For instance, visible light-driven or electrochemical thio-, hydro-, carbo- and di-carboxylation of alkenes, alkynes and heteroarenes with CO2•− has been reported via radical addition of CO2•− to carbon-carbon double30–41 or triple bonds42–44. However, the carboxylation of C = X (X = heteroatom) bonds via radical addition of CO2•− to C = X was challenging45,46. We wondered whether radical addition of CO2•− to C = N bonds in hydrazones would be possible to lead to umpolung carboxylation of unactivated hydrazones. We hypothesized that hydrazones could undergo deprotonation in the presence of a base and single electron transfer (SET) oxidation to generate N-centered radical intermediates under visible-light irradiation47. These N-centered radicals might proceed hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) with formate salt, which is generated in situ from CO2 and silane, to form CO2•−. If the nucleophilic CO2•− subsequently underwent radical addition to C = N bonds of hydrazones, a newly generated N-centered radical intermediate48 could participate in a SET reduction and following protonation to give α-hydrazino acids as the final products, which have been demonstrated in potent biologically active compounds, natural products, and building blocks10. Despite its potential, this hypothesis faces several challenges. Initially, the isomerization of hydrazone under basic conditions49 might hinder the photocatalytic generation of N-centered radicals, which could serve as a HAT reagent. Additionally, CO2•−, being a strong reductant50,51, might facilitate undesired reduction of hydrazone, leading to subsequent homo-coupling or hydrogenation of hydrazone radical anion. Furthermore, the relatively unstable α-carboxylated N-centered radicals might undergo β-cleavage52, thus hindering the forward progress of the reaction. Herein, we report our success in realizing the reductive carboxylation of unactivated hydrazones with CO2 (Fig. 1d). This visible-light photoredox-catalytic strategy provides a unique approach for retrosynthetic analysis of carboxylation and offers substantial potential for carboxylation of unactivated carbonyl derivatives.

Results

Reaction optimization

We began our studies by employing N-Boc-protected 2-tridecanone hydrazone 1a as the model substrate (Table 1). After a survey of various reaction conditions, we found that 1a underwent efficient carboxylation to deliver the desired product 2a in 84% yield under 1 atmosphere of CO2 and visible-light irradiation at 60 °C with 3DPFIPN-tBu as photocatalyst, industry-waste PMHS as a reductant and Cs2CO3 as a base (entry 1). Alternative parameters, such as different photocatalysts, bases, silanes and solvents, were also evaluated, resulting in lower yields (entries 2-9). Temperature was identified as a crucial parameter, as lowering the temperature from 60 °C to 50 °C led to a decrease in yield of 2a to 72%. Increasing the concentration of the photocatalytic system had no significant impact on the reaction outcome (entry 12). Rigorous control experiments showed that photocatalyst, PMHS, Cs2CO3, and visible-light irradiation were all essential for this transformation (entries 13–16). Notably, 2a was obtained in 6% yield under N2 atmosphere (entry 17), which indicated that Cs2CO3 might serve as a carboxyl source53. When the reaction was evaluated under thermal conditions, neither the product 2a nor α-azocarboxylate intermediate was observed even increasing the temperature to 120 °C (entry 18, please see Supplementary Information (SI) for details), thus excluding the direct base-mediated C–C formation and highlighting the unique photocatalytic pathway in our protocol.

Substrate scope

With the optimal reaction conditions (Table 1, entry 12) in hand, the scope of the ketone-derived hydrazones was further investigated (Fig. 2). We first evaluated the influence of various electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) on the nitrogen of 2-tridecanone hydrazones. Hydrazone 1b underwent carboxylation smoothly to give 2b in 62%, while hydrazones 1c and 1d gave inferior results with significant amounts of the corresponding ketones being observed. A collection of N-Boc-protected 1-aryl-3-butanone hydrazones bearing both electron-donating groups (EDGs, 1f, 1g) or EWGs (1h) on the aromatic ring were transformed into the corresponding products in good to high yields (77%-89%). N-Boc-protected veratone hydrazone 1j could react well to afford product 2j in 86% yield, which could be deprotected to synthesize the racemic form of Carbidopa54. Meanwhile, some functional groups, such as methoxy (2g, 2j), ester (2h), CF3 (2l), ether (2m) and vinyl (2n), were compatible with the conditions. Notably, acetone-derived hydrazone 1o was tolerated well in this transformation, further demonstrating the potential application of this methodology. In addition to 1°−1° dialkyl substituted N-Boc-protected ketone-derived hydrazones, we next examined more challenging substrates with higher steric hindrance. Acyclic bulkier dialkyl hydrazones were also reactive to deliver the products (2p-2t) in good yields. And the 1-adamantyl methyl ketone-derived hydrazone reacted smoothly to give the target product 2q in 51% yields. The decreased yields of 2q might be due to the excessive steric hindrance impeding the conversion of hydrazones. Moreover, the challenging 2,4-dimethyl-3-pentanone-derived hydrazone 1t also displayed exceptional reactivity. These results highlight the versatility of this catalytic strategy to generate highly bulky α-hydrazino acids, which are difficultly accessible by other methods.

Further investigation into cyclic ketone-derived hydrazones revealed that a variety of substrates, from five- to twelve-membered ring, all reacted smoothly with CO2 to produce the desired α-hydrazino acids (2u-2ac) in good to high yields (67%-84%). Notably, N-Boc-protected piperdinone and tetrahydropyrone hydrazones were also reactive to afford products 2z and 2aa in 73% and 67% yields, respectively. Meanwhile, cyclohexanone-derived hydrazones with α-substituents (1ac) were also suitable for this carboxylation.

Based on the success on reductive carboxylation of unactivated ketone-derived hydrazones, we further explored the scope to including unactivated aldehyde-derived hydrazones (Fig. 3). Several aliphatic aldehyde-derived hydrazones can be suitable to this carboxylation, including the primary, secondary and tertiary alkyl substituted ones (3a-3g). Notably, the relatively hindered pivaldehyde-derived hydrazone 3g delivered the corresponding product in 87% yield. Furthermore, N-Cbz-protected hydrazones derived from propionaldehyde and isobutyraldehyde (3h and 3i) also underwent efficient carboxylation to provide desired α-hydrazino acids. In addition, direct C(sp2)–H bond carboxylation product was observed (please see Supplementary Fig. 2 for details), which might originate from the HAT process of α-carboxylated aminy radical37,48 and be inhibited by the increasing steric hindrance55,56.

Synthetic transformations

To further illustrate the practicality of this method, a gram-scale reaction of hydrazone 1a was conducted under the standard reaction conditions, affording the desired product 2a in 81% yield (Fig. 4a). A key benefit of this transformation lay in the ease of access to the hydrazone starting materials, which could be readily synthesized from the corresponding ketones and tert-butyloxycarbonyl hydrazine. Leveraging this, we successfully developed a streamlined one-pot, two-step process to convert 2-tridecanone into the desired product 2a in 83% overall yield via a condensation and photocatalytic carboxylation sequence (Fig. 4b). Then, we explored several transformations of α-hydrazino acids products to further demonstrate the synthetic utility of this methodology (Fig. 4c). The conversion of product 2 f into α-amino acid derivatives 5 in 75% yield through esterification and reduction, providing a convenient route for the synthesis of unactivated α-amino acid derivatives. The product 2 f was efficiently converted to pyrazole 6 in 81% yield via deprotection and condensation. Importantly, complete deprotection of product 2j gave the (±)-carbidopa as its hydrobromide salt 7 in 77% yield, which could be converted to (±)-carbidopa hydrate54. The product 4e underwent esterification and oxidation to afford important α-ester hydrazone 8 in 69% yield. Additionally, the reductive carboxylation product 2a was successfully utilized to synthesize N‑amino dipeptide 9 in 69% yield via direct amidation of the carboxylic acid group57. These transformations highlight the broad applicability of this strategy in drug discovery and development, offering an efficient pathway to access structurally diverse and biologically relevant compounds.

Mechanistic investigations

To further elucidate the reaction mechanism, we carried out several mechanistic studies, as summarized in Fig. 5. Initially, we examined the role of the N–H bond in the substrate (Fig. 5a). No desired product 2o’ was achieved when a methylated variant of the acetone-derived hydrazone (1o’) was used, indicating that the N–H bond is essential for the smooth transformation within the catalytic system. Then, radical clock experiments were performed to investigate the plausible mechanism of C–C formation in our system (Fig. 5b). Under standard conditions, no ring-opening product 12 was detected when using 1-cyclopropylethan-1-one derived hydrazone 1o as the substrate, suggesting that an α-hydrazino carbon-centered radical is not likely an intermediate in the transformation. In addition, we also tested the reactivity of cyclobutanone-derived hydrazone 13 under the standard conditions, yielding a ring-opening product 15 and the anticipated product 14 (Fig. 5c). This result suggested that the reaction might proceed through an α-carboxycyclobutyl aminyl radical intermediate, which induced ring opening to produce a primary carbon-centered radical58. To further validate the proposed α-carboxylated aminyl radical intermediate, we carefully monitored the formation of formate salt under the optimal reaction conditions to verify the presence of the CO2•− species (Fig. 5 d). In our catalytic system, formate salt was efficiently produced in the presence of CO2, silane, and base, in line with previous reports59,60. Furthermore, the amount of formate salt was significantly increased in the absence of visible light or hydrazone 1a, suggesting that the generated formate salt could act as a CO2•− precursor, participating in subsequent transformation with 1a, facilitated by the photocatalyst under visible-light irradiation. At the same time, we tested a simplified reaction system by replacing PMHS and CO2 with cesium formate (Fig. 5e). This modification led to comparable yields of the reductive carboxylation products (2a, 2j, 2v, 4a) under an N2 atmosphere, further confirming that formate salt can serve as a competent surrogate under the reaction conditions.

Time-resolved luminescence quenching experiments were then conducted with different concentrations of hydrazone 1a, Cs2CO3, HCOOCs, and the mixtures of 1a with Cs2CO3 or HCOOCs. As illustrated in Fig. 5 f, the mixtures of hydrazone 1a with base (Cs2CO3 or HCOOCs) efficiently quenched the luminescence of excited 3DPAFIPN-tBu, with a Stern-Volmer constant of 700.04 or 321.67. In contrast, the Stern-Volmer constants for HCOOCs, Cs₂CO₃, and hydrazone 1a alone were determined to be 194.28, 47.29, and 10.73, respectively. These results suggested that a reductive quenching process might occur between the photocatalyst and the corresponding N-centered anion, generating an N-centered radical that likely acts as a HAT reagent in subsequent steps. Given the strong reduction potential of CO2•− [E1/2 (CO2/CO2•−) = −2.2 V vs SCE]50,51, we further explored the SET reduction process of CO2•− and hydrazone derivatives using cyclic voltammetry (CV) (Fig. 5g). Compared to active aromatic hydrazones, aliphatic hydrazones 1a and 3a displayed significantly more negative reduction potential (<−3.0 V). This potential mismatch between aliphatic hydrazone and CO2•− suggested that the direct SET reduction is unlikely. Furthermore, deuterium-labeling experiments were carried out to assess the possibility of a SET reduction step26,61. No reductive deuterated byproducts were observed (please see SI for details), thus allowing us to exclude the pathway through a SET reduction and subsequent nucleophilic attack on CO2.

Based on our mechanistic studies and previous reports38, a plausible mechanism is proposed as shown in Fig. 6. First, the deprotonated hydrazone N-centered anion A is oxidized by the photoexcited photocatalyst (PC*), leading to a reduced photocatalyst (PC•–) and the corresponding N-centered radical B. Spin density analysis reveals that the spin density on the nitrogen atom attached to the Boc group in B exceeds that on the other atoms (please see Supplementary Fig. 9 for details). This disparity in spin density leads to the occurrence of hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) between this nitrogen atom and formate, which is in situ generated from CO2 and PMHS under basic conditions. Through this HAT process, the hydrazone and CO2•− species were generated. The CO2•− species then undergo a sequential radical addition to hydrazone C = N bond, forming an α-carboxylated aminyl radical C. This intermediate may complete the photocatalytic cycle through a SET reduction62,63, leading to the α-hydrazino acid product. Alternative, this aminyl radical C may undergo HAT with the in situ generated formate salt via a chain-propagation mechanism, affording the desired product64. However, this alternative pathway appears less likely at this stage based on radical initiator and the light-on/off experiments (please see Supplementary Fig. 4 and 5 for details). Moreover, DFT calculations on bond dissociation energies (BDEs) and energy barriers indicated that both HAT and radical addition steps are thermodynamically and dynamically favorable (please see Supplementary Fig. 10 and Source Data for details), further supporting the proposed mechanism.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a general and efficient photocatalytic reductive carboxylation of unactivated aliphatic hydrazones with CO2. A wide array of hydrazones, readily prepared from commercially available aldehydes and ketones, were effectively converted into α-hydrazino acid products in good to excellent yields. Remarkably, this transformation was not significantly affected by steric hinderance, as evidenced by the successful participation of various 3°−1° and 2°−2° dialkyl substituted ketone hydrazones, underscoring the synthetic versatility and potential applications of this approach. Mechanistic studies revealed that the key C–C formation step likely proceeded via radical addition of CO2•−, generated through a HAT process involving an N-centered radical and in situ generated formate salt, to hydrazones C = N bonds. Ongoing research in our laboratory aims to expand the scope of CO2•− radical addition to broader range of unsaturated bonds and to explore late-stage product modifications in drug discovery and development.

Methods

General procedure for the carboxylation of hydrazone

The oven-dried Schlenk tube (10 mL) containing a stirring bar was charged with solid hydrazone (0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) and 3DPAFIPN-tBu (2.0 mg, 0.002 mmol, 1 mol%), then added Cs2CO3 (65.0 mg, 0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) in glovebox. The tube was evacuated and back-filled with CO2 for 3 times. Subsequently, PMHS (30 µL, 0.5 mmol, 2.5 equiv.) and DMSO (1 mL or 2 mL) was added via syringe under CO2 atmosphere. Once added, the Schlenk tube was sealed under atmospheric pressure of CO2 (1 atm). The reaction was stirred in oil bath and irradiated with a 30 W blue LED lamp (1-2 cm away, with cooling fan to keep the reaction temperature at 60 °C) for 12 h. After completion, the reaction was diluted with 2.5 mL EtOAc and carefully quenched by 2 mL HCl (2 N), stirred for 10 min. The reaction mixture was extracted by EtOAc and the combined organic phases were concentrated in vacuo. The residue was purified by silica gel flash column chromatography (petroleum ether/EtOAc/AcOH 10/1/0%-5/1/0.5%-1/3/0.5%) to give the pure desired product.
