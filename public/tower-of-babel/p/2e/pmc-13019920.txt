Reverse triggering in mechanically ventilated ICU patients: Benefit or harm? Insights from bedside electrical impedance tomography in a case series
Authors: Song Zhang, Siyi Yuan, Elias Baedorf Kassis, Songlin Wu, Yi Chi, Huaiwu He, Daniel Talmor, Longxiang Su, Yun Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReverse triggering (RT) has been associated with potentially injurious ventilatory patterns in some settings, yet may also coincide with improved dependent ventilation or recruitment-like redistribution in selected patients. However, the effect of RT on patients with different underlying lung conditions are varied and objective tools or indicators for evaluation the effect of RT are still lacking.Case presentationThere were four cases in this report, which consisted of three males and one female. These patients entered intensive care unit (ICU) for severe sepsis or underwent the hits of major surgeries. Case 1 and 4 represented the heavier lung injury accompanied by RT, even if RT resulted in improved dorsal ventilation or better ventilatory homogeneity, the effect of this transient benefit needs to be considered in the context of systemic conditions and disease progression. Patient-ventilation synchronization in critical situations may alleviate mechanical lung injury. Case 2 and 3 were associated with RT which may represent a physiological, non-injurious pattern of patient-ventilator interaction. One possible explanation is that this pattern may resemble mechanisms such as a diaphragmatic brake or a sigh-like recruitment effect.ConclusionsThese cases suggest that RT may present with different physiological patterns across patients. RT without appreciable pendelluft and without deterioration in global mechanics/EIT indices may be observed with close monitoring, whereas RT accompanied by prominent pendelluft or potentially injurious mechanics may warrant active mitigation.Keywords: Reverse triggering, Electrical impedance tomography, Respiratory mechanics, Risk prediction

Reverse triggering (RT) is caused by patient's spontaneous breathing effort that was “triggered” by an initial passive ventilator-delivered breath, and it occurs in 30-55% of sedated patients [1]. RT has been postulated to induce ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) and physicians generally hold the view that such asynchrony should be eliminated. It also could conversely act like the possible “sigh” or “diaphragmatic brake” and improve aeration, redistribution of air and lung recruitment among acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) patients. This could in theory be beneficial, accelerating the process of extubation and even improve the clinical outcomes. However, objective tools for evaluation the effect of RT are still lacking.

Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) is an effective and non-invasive technique to evaluate ventilation heterogeneity [2]. It can provide information about the distribution of regional ventilation and pendelluft. This case series of fours patients was performed to contrast the effect of RT in patients with different lung conditions and demonstrate the physiologic rationale for harm vs. benefit. Additionally, individualized strategies have also been suggested to further management.

Case report

Case 1

A 63-year-old man was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for severe acute pancreatitis. He was ventilated with a tidal volume (Vt) of 6 ml/kg, respiratory rate (RR) of 20 breaths/min, PEEP of 8 cmH2O, and FiO2 of 70%. His PaO2/FiO2 ratio was 132 mmHg, compliance of the respiratory system (Crs) was 27.2 ml/cmH2O, resistance was 16 cmH2O/(L/s). He was receiving 120mg/h propofol, 2.5ug/kg/h remifentanil, 5mg/h midazolam, with a Richmond agitation-sedation scale (RASS) −4. His ventilator waveform showed RT which were subsequently eliminated by neuromuscular blockade (NMB) and ventilation was monitored by EIT (Dräger Pulmo Vista 500). Results of EIT and respiratory mechanics are shown in Fig. 1. It showed that RT may result in increasing diaphragmatic contraction amplitude and improving ventilation in the gravity-dependent zone. But the transient advantage of RT accompanied by an increased hazard of mechanical injury, which did not represent the long-term benefit. Eliminating this asynchrony to maintain lung protection may be optimal choice.

Case 2

A 73-year-old man was transferred to ICU after the surgery for acute duodenal ulcer with perforation. He was mechanically ventilated with PCV of 10 cmH2O, 16 breaths/min, PEEP of 8 cmH2O, and FiO2 of 40%. Propofol (150mg/h) and fentanyl (45μg/h) were administrated and the RASS was −4. His PaO2/FiO2 ratio was 298 mmHg, Crs was 36.8 ml/cmH2O, resistance was 10.5 cmH2O/(L/s). Results are presented in Fig. 1. There is no need to immediately eliminate RT that has no obvious damage to the ventilation in patients with well-aerated lung tissue. The occurrence of RT in this condition indicates physiological non-damage resonance without mechanical loading. It is necessary to promote the stable recovery of their spontaneous breathing instead of simply increasing the dosage of analgesic and sedative drugs.

Case 3

A 67-year-old man without coexisting lung disease was admitted to ICU for gut-derived sepsis. He received mechanical ventilation with PCV of 10 cmH2O, RR of 15 breaths/min, PEEP of 6 cmH2O, and FiO2 of 35%. Crs was 34.6 ml/cmH2O, resistance was 12 cmH2O/(L/s) and oxygenation index was 312 mmHg. He received 100mg/h propofol, 45ug/h fentanyl and the RASS score was −4. Results are presented in Fig. 1. That is to say, existence of RT might be allowed in patients at lower risk of lung injuries, as it can improve the dorsal ventilation and decrease pendelluft, which promoting the ventilation homogeneity. This may resemble physiological phenomena such as a diaphragmatic brake or a sigh-like recruitment effect, but there were no direct measurements of effort or expiratory diaphragm activity. RT with potential benefits can exist in patients who cannot attempt the first weaning process in the short term but it may be a double-edged sword. The outcomes of possible risk about local hyperventilation should be closely monitored.

Case 4

A 73-year-old female was transferred to ICU after the cardiac surgery. She was mechanically ventilated with PCV of 12 cmH2O, RR of 18 breaths/min, PEEP of 7 cmH2O, and FiO2 of 50%. Propofol (120mg/h) and fentanyl (60μg/h) were administrated and RASS was −4. Her PaO2/FiO2 ratio was 242 mmHg, Crs was 28.8 ml/cmH2O, resistance was 18 cmH2O/(L/s). Results are shown in Fig. 1. It is clear that RT with significant pendelluft, worse ventilation distribution and severe lung jnjury may need to be eliminated in time in sicker patients.

Discussion

While RT has garnered increased attention and interest, the impact on patients regarding harm vs benefit is still inconclusive. Whether RT should be eliminated or tolerated still remains unclear and there is still a lack of methods or indicators for predicting and evaluating its effects [3].

Previous study found that the more frequent the double-triggering occurred, the more serious the pendelluft phenomenon was, which has the potential to increase the risk of VILI [4]. Bedside EIT combined with respiratory mechanics may help characterize RT-associated regional ventilation patterns and provide physiologic context for hypothesis generation regarding potential harm versus potential benefit. To our knowledge, our observations suggest that RT may coexist with a relatively stable ventilation pattern in some patients, representing a physiologic pattern potentially consistent with benefit, characterized by improved ventilation homogeneity with increased dorsal ventilation and no evidence of pendelluft. In patients with poor baseline lung function (e.g. case1), the observed EIT changes were transient and occurred alongside a potentially higher-risk mechanical profile, suggesting that RT in this context may warrant closer monitoring and consideration of mitigation. In addition, pendelluft increased after RT was removed in case 1. This finding suggested that pendelluft alone may not be a reliable indicator for determining whether RT was harmful. Conversely, in patients with relatively preserved lung mechanics (e.g. case4), RT without appreciable pendelluft may be compatible with recovery of spontaneous drive, although this requires prospective validation. However, the choice of timely intervention might not necessary in patients who had better lung ventilation without pendelluft, the existence of RT may represent the recovering of spontaneous breathing (Case 2, 3). NMB is a common treatment to eliminate RT, but this has not been shown to be beneficial in ARDS [5] and may cause prolonged mechanical ventilation. Its application may reference the assessment reults of EIT and respiratory mechanics.

In conclusion, we postulate that RT in patients where RT improves aeration and results in less pendelluft may be tolerated, while in sicker patients where RT is causing significant pendelluft, it may need to be eliminated. It is also worth noting that pendelluft reflects regional timing differences in ventilation, not regional stress or injury directly. At the same time, small reductions in driving pressure and mechanical power after RT elimination might not truly reflect the effect of eliminating RT, as these values were already within lung-protective ranges, and such changes are expected with any reduction in spontaneous effort or with NMB. These results need to be validated by further studies with a larger sample size in the future. And due to lack of measurements about esophageal pressure or diaphragm electrical activity, it is difficult to relate these current EIT findings to breathing effort magnitude, timing, or RT phenotype. More comprehensive detection of esophageal pressure and diaphragm electrical activity considered in the future will be able to confirm the true effect of RT.
