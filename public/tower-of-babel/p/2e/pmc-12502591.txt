Costs, delivery strategies and implementation challenges for malaria vaccines: a rapid review of literature
Authors: AbdulMuminu Isah, Chinelo Obi, Izuchukwu Okeke, MacMillan Gwe, Obinna Onwujekwe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe burden of malaria remains high, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where it accounts for over 200 million cases annually. Vaccination is the latest tool in the armamentarium to prevent malaria. Although a couple of malaria vaccines have been deployed in some countries, gaps in knowledge about their delivery, cost, and effectiveness remain. This rapid review synthesized evidence on issues on malaria vaccines implementation to inform strategies for optimising their accessibility.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and Cochrane Library was conducted to identify studies that examined malaria vaccine delivery mechanisms, costs, and logistical challenges from 2000 to 2024. The articles underwent duplicates removal, title/abstract screening, and full-text review. Eligible studies included those on currently approved and emerging malaria vaccines. Data were extracted systematically and synthesized descriptively.ResultsThirty studies met the inclusion criteria, with 21(%) conducted in Africa. RTS, S/AS01 was the most commonly reported vaccine (n = 23, 76.67%), with reported efficacy rates of 30% − 75%. Cost-effectiveness estimates for the vaccines varied from $20 - $50 per DALY averted, but higher costs were reported in some settings. Delivery strategies largely involved routine immunization (n = 12, 40%), although innovative methods, including drones, showed promise in one study. Key barriers were logistical constraints, community acceptance, and healthcare infrastructure limitations.ConclusionsThe findings highlighted delivery and cost-effectiveness challenges for malaria vaccination. Ways and means of reducing the costs and improving the effectiveness of different sustainable malaria vaccine delivery strategies, especially in remote and underserved communities should be developed. Limitations of this rapid review include restriction to English-language studies, exclusion of grey literature, and variability in study designs, which may have limited comprehensiveness and comparability of findings.

Introduction

Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by Plasmodium parasites and transmitted through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes [1]. It is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, accounting for over 200 million cases and more than 600,000 deaths annually [2]. Many tools have been deployed to prevent and control malaria with varying degrees of progress.

Hence, despite significant progress in reducing the burden of malaria through vector control and chemotherapy and preventive measures malaria remains endemic in many regions [3], with young children and pregnant women being the most vulnerable groups [4].

Malaria vaccination is the latest tool in the armamentarium to prevent malaria. Vaccination has long been recognized as a critical component in the global strategy to eliminate malaria, complementing existing control measures. The development of the RTS, S/AS01 (Mosquirix™) vaccine marked a historic breakthrough as the first malaria vaccine to be recommended for widespread use [5]. It has demonstrated moderate effectiveness in reducing clinical malaria and severe cases in children living in malaria-endemic areas. In 2021, WHO endorsed the broader use of RTS, S/AS01 following the successful pilot introduction in Ghana, Kenya, and Malawi [6].

However, although a couple of malaria vaccines such as the RTS, S/AS01 (Mosquirix™) has been deployed in some countries, various gaps in knowledge surrounding its delivery and cost-effectiveness remain. The introduction of malaria vaccines into national immunization programs presents new challenges related to delivery, logistics, and sustainability, particularly in low-resource settings where healthcare infrastructure is often limited.

Despite the successful development and initial deployment of RTS, S/AS01, the large-scale rollout of malaria vaccines faces numerous barriers that may undermine their potential impact. These challenges are not limited to vaccine effectiveness alone but extend to the mechanisms of delivery, cold chain logistics, and integration into existing health systems [7]. Moreover, the introduction of a malaria vaccine raises questions regarding long-term sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and community acceptance [8]. These are factors that are critical to the success of any public health intervention.

There is paucity of information on the effectiveness of various delivery strategies for malaria vaccines, particularly in regions with seasonal malaria transmission, limited infrastructure, and socio-cultural barriers [9]. There is an urgent need to assess the practical aspects of vaccine deployment in real-world settings. Additionally, new vaccines such as the R21/Matrix-M, which have shown promising results in clinical trials [6], are on the horizon, necessitating a deeper understanding of how vaccine delivery can be optimized across different contexts.

A rapid review is timely as global health authorities consider expanding the use of malaria vaccines, while balancing the need for cost-effective, scalable delivery systems that ensure equitable access [10]. By evaluating the experiences of countries that have implemented malaria vaccine programs, this review aims to inform future vaccination campaigns and identify gaps that must be addressed to optimize vaccine deployment.

This paper provides synthesized evidence on the current state of the delivery mechanisms of malaria vaccines. It provides evidence on the effectiveness of various vaccine delivery strategies employed in different malaria-endemic regions, particularly those with resource constraints. It also identifies barriers to vaccine uptake, including logistical challenges, cold chain management, and community acceptance as well as assesses the cost-effectiveness of malaria vaccine deployment in national immunization programs and its integration into existing health systems. Finally, it explores emerging vaccines and assesses the preparedness of health systems to accommodate new vaccines in the future. The review provides actionable insights on best practices and recommendations for overcoming challenges in malaria vaccine delivery.

Methods

The review explored and synthesized evidence on malaria vaccine delivery strategies, associated costs and implementation challenges in resource-limited regions. The review followed the PRISMA extension for scoping reviews [11].

Rapid reviews are streamlined evidence syntheses that follow systematic review principles but employ methodological shortcuts to generate timely findings while maintaining transparency [12]. These reviews typically take shorter time to complete and often limit databases, languages, or evidence types to accelerate the process. Common methods include using single reviewers for screening and data extraction, omitting quality assessments, and presenting narrative summaries [13]. While there is no standardized methodology, rapid reviews aim to balance rigor and timeliness to inform decision-making [14]. Some evidence suggests that rapid review conclusions are generally congruent with those of full systematic reviews [13].

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies on malaria vaccines and their delivery strategies. This study utilized published peer-reviewed studies to ensure a well-rounded perspective. Databases searched included PubMed, Google Scholar, and Cochrane Library. The search was designed to capture studies related to the development, implementation, and evaluation of malaria vaccines.

Key terms and search strings were used in combination with Boolean operators (AND, OR) to ensure a broad yet targeted search. The terms were “malaria vaccine”, “vaccine delivery”, “RTS, S/AS01*”, “R21/Matrix-M”, “vaccine implementation”, “vaccine logistics”, and “health systems.” Synonyms and MeSH terms were also employed to expand the search, ensuring that relevant articles were captured from peer-reviewed journals.

Selection criteria

The review focused on human populations, specifically women residing in malaria-endemic areas, and encompassed all types of malaria vaccines—including RTS, S/AS01 and emerging candidates like R21/Matrix-M. The study included outcomes, such as vaccine effectiveness, efficacy, delivery strategies, uptake, cost-effectiveness, and logistical challenges. All study designs were considered, including randomized controlled trials, observational studies, qualitative studies, and economic evaluations. Additionally, only English-language articles published between 2000 and August 2024 were included. Recent studies have examined the impact of language restrictions in systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Evidence suggests that limiting reviews to English-language publications has minimal effect on overall conclusions and could be a viable methodological shortcut, especially for rapid reviews [15, 16]. It is, noted, assumed that excluding non-English publications does not significantly alter effect estimates or statistical significance in health science research [16, 17].

The exclusion criteria encompassed studies that concentrated exclusively on non-human populations or unrelated diseases that could act as comorbidities for malaria prevention, as well as correspondence and letters to the editor, and duplicate reports.

Study selection and screening

The selection process was carried out in three stages: duplicate removal, title/abstract screening, and full-text review. Rayyan, a web-based tool, was used at each stage to streamline and manage the screening process. Two authors, OIC and GM, independently conducted the title/abstract and full-text screenings. Conflicts arising from the initial screenings were resolved by a third author, AI. The level of agreement between the two reviewers was assessed using the Kappa score, which demonstrated substantial agreement, confirming that both authors could work effectively as a team. This systematic extraction ensured that all relevant information was captured for subsequent analysis. Figure 1 shows the sequence of the number of articles in this rapid review from the first search until the final review. A rapid review approach was chosen rather than a full systematic review to provide timely evidence that could inform ongoing policy discussions and vaccine implementation planning. Given the urgency of malaria vaccine introduction in sub-Saharan Africa, a rapid review offered a pragmatic balance between methodological rigor and the need for evidence to guide near-term decision-making.

The review process, illustrated in the PRISMA flow chart (Fig. 1), involved a comprehensive search across several databases and registers. A total of 106 records were identified: 21 from PubMed, 80 from Google Scholar, and 5 from registers. After removing 16 duplicate records, 90 records were screened, with 54 excluded based on relevance. The remaining 36 reports were sought for retrieval, and after assessing for eligibility, 30 studies were included in the final review.

In determining relevance, records were excluded if they lacked empirical data on malaria vaccine delivery, costs, or implementation, or if they addressed only basic immunology without application to programmatic settings. Studies focusing solely on non-human models, vaccine candidates not advanced beyond pre-clinical stages, or unrelated immunization programs were also excluded. Furthermore, to ensure rigor, only full-text peer-reviewed articles were retained, excluding conference abstracts, letters, and non-peer-reviewed reports. While this strengthened the reliability of included evidence, it may have limited the breadth of insights captured. In addition, Studies focusing exclusively on basic immunology, non-human models, or vaccines not yet in advanced clinical phases were also excluded.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using a standardized Microsoft Excel sheet. The extracted data included: Study characteristics (author, year, country, region, study design); Study objectives and key outcomes; Vaccine type and dosage regimen; Delivery strategies and logistical considerations; Cost-effectiveness data; and Barriers and challenges identified in vaccine delivery. In this review, “efficacy” refers to the protective effect of malaria vaccines as measured under controlled clinical trial conditions, whereas “effectiveness” denotes the real-world impact of vaccines when delivered through routine health systems. These terms are used consistently throughout the manuscript to reflect the distinction between trial-based outcomes and programmatic performance.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis was performed to summarize the findings from the included studies. Studies were grouped based on the key themes identified, such as delivery mechanisms, barriers to vaccine uptake, and cost-effectiveness. Where appropriate, results were tabulated and descriptive statistics were used to present the frequency of different strategies and challenges.

Results

The findings from the 30 articles included in the review were reported under five themes: characteristics of the studies, geographic and methodological trends, vaccine effectiveness and cost effectiveness, and delivery mechanism and logistical challenges, and adverse effects of the vaccine.

Characteristics of the studies

The review included a total of 30 studies focused on malaria vaccine implementation, with all the person-centred studies conducted in Africa (n = 22, 73.33%). The countries most represented in the study were Ghana (n = 7, 31.82%) [18–24], Burkina Faso (n = 5, 22.73%) [21, 25–28], Kenya (n = 6, 27.27%) [19–21, 24, 29, 30], and Malawi (n = 4, 18.18%) [19, 20, 24, 31]. Qualitative design was adopted in 7(23.33%) [9, 18, 22, 23, 27, 29, 30] and reviews in 7(23.33%) [29, 32–37] studies respectively, while 6 (20.0%) adopted economic modelling [19–21, 26, 31, 38]. The main objectives of these studies were to evaluate the cost/cost-effectiveness [19–21, 26, 28, 31, 38], challenges/barriers/facilitators [18, 22, 24, 35, 36], and delivery mechanisms [9, 29, 37] of malaria vaccines. Almost all the studies, 24 (80.0%), were conducted among children under five years old [9, 18–33, 35, 39–44]. (See Table 1)

Characteristics of the studies included in the review

Geographic and methodological trends

The majority of the studies included in this review were conducted in Africa, particularly in countries with high malaria transmission, such as Ghana, Kenya, Burkina Faso, and Malawi. In terms of methodology, 40% of the studies employed trial or randomized controlled trial designs, while 30% utilized qualitative approaches to assess the broader socio-cultural and logistical barriers to vaccine uptake. This blend of quantitative and qualitative research underscores the complex nature of malaria vaccine delivery, which requires both rigorous efficacies testing and an understanding of the contextual factors influencing vaccine acceptance and distribution. (See Table 2)

Vaccine effectiveness and cost-effectiveness

The RTS, S/AS01 malaria vaccine was the vaccine type evaluated in about 80% of all the studies (n = 23, 76.67%), with other types assessed by fewer studies: RTS, S/AS01E, (n = 6, 20.00%) [9, 18, 23, 25, 26, 40], RTS, S/A02, (n = 1, 3.33%) [39], RH5.1/AS01B (n = 1, 3.33%) [45], mRNA vaccine (n = 1, 3.33%) [34], and R21/Matrix-M (n = 1, 3.33%) [28]. The dosage schedule reported by almost all the studies was 4 doses, with all the 30 studies also indicating that the vaccines were administered intramuscularly. (See Table 2)

For the efficacy/effectiveness of the vaccines, two studies reported a range of 30–40% [18, 29] while one reported just 40% [20], with the highest value reported to be 60–75% by another study [25]. RTS, S/AS01 remains the most extensively studied candidate, with moderate efficacy demonstrated across multiple phase III trials and pilot programs. By contrast, R21/Matrix-M has shown higher efficacy in early-phase studies, particularly in West Africa, and appears more cost-effective in modeled analyses. However, R21 findings remain limited to specific geographies and require further validation before wide-scale policy adoption. The cost of the vaccine ranged from $5 to $7 per dose in 9 [9, 18–20, 25, 29, 33, 38, 39] of the 15 studies that reported costs, with cost-effectiveness estimates varying widely depending on the country and delivery strategy. For example, the cost per DALY averted ranged from $20 to $50 in three studies [18, 25, 29], although a high cost-effectiveness value of $188 ($78 – $22,448) per DALY was reported in one study [38]. The vaccine’s reported effectiveness ranged from 30 to 75%, with higher effectiveness observed in settings with seasonal malaria transmission. However, the review also highlighted that in low-resource settings, the cost of delivering the vaccine—particularly ensuring cold chain logistics—can drive up the overall costs, reducing cost-effectiveness.

Notable differences emerged between RTS, S variants and non-RTS, S vaccines. RTS, S/AS01 and its derivative, RTS, S/AS01E, consistently demonstrated moderate efficacy in clinical trials (30–60%) with proven feasibility for integration into pilot national immunization programs. By contrast, the R21/Matrix-M vaccine has shown higher efficacy in early studies and mathematical models, with projected cost-effectiveness superior to RTS, S in some settings, such as Burkina Faso, where the estimated cost per DALY averted was $154 compared with RTS, S estimates exceeding $180 in similar contexts [22]. Non-RTS, S approaches, including RH5.1/AS01B and emerging mRNA platforms, remain experimental but hold promise for overcoming parasite diversity and antigenic variability. These findings suggest that while RTS, S remains the most advanced in terms of deployment experience, non-RTS, S vaccines may offer improved performance and scalability in the near future.

Delivery mechanisms and logistical challenges

One of the most significant challenges identified across the studies was the logistics of vaccine delivery [47]. The majority of the studies reported routine immunization services as the primary delivery mechanism. However, logistical barriers, such as maintaining cold chain integrity, ensuring timely distribution to remote areas, and addressing vaccine stockouts, were frequently cited as limiting factors [48]. These challenges are not unique to malaria vaccines but are emblematic of broader systemic issues in healthcare delivery across low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).

A small number of studies explored innovative delivery mechanisms, such as the use of drones to transport vaccines to remote areas. These alternative delivery strategies are promising, particularly in regions where geographical barriers and poor infrastructure impede access to healthcare services. Drones, for example, offer the potential to overcome challenges associated with reaching remote populations, though their cost and scalability remain concerns [49]. Another critical finding was the issue of vaccine acceptance. Several studies pointed to socio-cultural barriers, including vaccine hesitancy, misinformation, and distrust in healthcare systems, which can significantly reduce uptake [50]. Community engagement and education are therefore essential components of successful vaccine implementation strategies [51]. (See Table 2)

Adverse effects of the vaccine

All the 12 studies that reported the adverse effects of the vaccines identified pain at the injection site as the most common side effect [9, 18–20, 25, 26, 29, 32–34, 38, 39], with five of the studies [18, 19, 22, 25, 38] also reporting redness and swelling at the injection site in addition to the pain. Discussion

This rapid review synthesized evidence from 30 studies on the implementation of malaria vaccines, particularly RTS, S/AS01, across various countries. The findings provide critical insights into the efficacy, effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and logistical challenges associated with vaccine delivery, which are essential for informing future vaccination strategies and healthcare policies in malaria-endemic regions.

Majority of the studies were undertaken in Africa, reflecting the priority placed on malaria control in sub-Saharan Africa where the disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly among children under five years old. In fact, some studies were a multinational, studying different outcomes in two or more African countries [19, 20, 24–27]. It further highlights the region’s disproportionate burden of malaria and the importance of localized research to address region-specific challenges in vaccine implementation.

The blend of quantitative [40, 41] and qualitative [9, 18, 22, 27, 29] research in this review underscores the complex nature of malaria vaccine delivery, which requires both rigorous efficacies testing and an understanding of the contextual factors influencing vaccine acceptance and distribution. Furthermore, inclusion of qualitative [9, 18, 22, 27, 29] studies is particularly significant, as it emphasizes the need to explore beyond clinical efficacy to address real-world implementation challenges, such as community perceptions, healthcare infrastructure, and cold chain management. These studies provide essential insights into the socio-cultural and logistical barriers that can undermine the success of vaccination programs, particularly in resource-limited settings where healthcare access is often constrained.

The RTS, S/AS01 having a higher efficacy observed in settings with seasonal malaria transmission [18, 25, 26, 28, 29], is consistent with previous findings that malaria transmission intensity and population immunity can influence vaccine performance. For example, in areas with seasonal transmission, the vaccine’s protection may align more closely with peak malaria transmission periods, resulting in higher effectiveness [52]. However, in areas with year-round transmission, the need for continuous immunity may lower the vaccine’s apparent effectiveness over time.

The cost-effectiveness of RTS, S/AS01 suggest that, in many contexts, the vaccine represents a cost-effective intervention [18, 19, 25, 38, 42], particularly when integrated into existing national immunization programs. However, the implication that in low-resource settings, the cost of delivering the vaccine can drive up the overall costs, reducing cost-effectiveness, suggests that while the vaccine is a valuable tool in reducing malaria transmission and morbidity, its cost-effectiveness is heavily dependent on the infrastructure available to support its delivery.

Innovative delivery mechanisms, such as the use of drones to transport vaccines to remote areas was reported in one study [41]. This is a novel and promising strategy that would solve certain challenges particularly in regions where geographical barriers and poor infrastructure impede access to healthcare services. Drones, for example, offer the potential to overcome challenges associated with reaching remote populations, though their cost and scalability remain concerns [49]. Future research should further evaluate the feasibility and sustainability of these novel delivery methods, particularly in resource-constrained environments where traditional delivery systems may not be effective.

Targeted interventions that improve vaccine literacy and foster trust in immunization programs could enhance vaccine campaign effectiveness by addressing the socio-cultural challenges that reviewed studies highlighted as barriers to vaccine acceptance [50, 51].

Despite the progress made in evaluating the RTS, S/AS01 vaccine, this review identified several gaps in the current literature. First, there is limited evidence on the long-term effectivess of malaria vaccines, particularly in different transmission settings. While short-term effectiveness data are promising, more longitudinal studies are needed to assess how long immunity lasts and whether booster doses are required to maintain protection. Additionally, the majority of studies focused on children under five years old, with limited research on other at-risk populations, such as pregnant women, who may also benefit from vaccination.

Furthermore, the reliance on cost-effectiveness as the primary economic measure may overlook other critical aspects of vaccine implementation, such as financial sustainability and equity in access. While RTS, S/AS01 has been shown to be cost-effective in many settings, the question remains whether countries with limited healthcare budgets can afford to implement and sustain large-scale vaccination programs without significant external funding. Future research should explore alternative funding mechanisms and cost-sharing models to ensure that malaria vaccines remain accessible to those who need them most. One area insufficiently explored in the current evidence base is the compatibility of malaria vaccines with the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) schedules. Integration into EPI platforms has the potential to improve delivery efficiency, reduce costs, and ensure higher population coverage, especially in low-resource settings. However, empirical studies directly assessing the feasibility and operational implications of aligning malaria vaccines with EPI remain limited.

Limitations

This review has some limitations. First, the restriction to English-language articles may have led to the omission of relevant studies published in other languages, particularly from malaria-endemic countries where research is often locally disseminated. Second, we excluded grey literature, including programmatic reports and unpublished studies, which may have contained additional implementation insights. Third, the rapid review design, while valuable for providing timely evidence, involved methodological streamlining that may have excluded some marginally relevant studies. Finally, considerable heterogeneity was observed across the included studies in terms of vaccine candidates studied, trial designs, population age groups, and outcome measures reported. This variability complicates direct comparison of results and may partly explain differences in reported efficacy, effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and implementation outcomes. Consequently, findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly when generalizing across settings, and highlight areas where future comprehensive systematic reviews may be warranted.

Conclusion

The findings from this review hold critical implications for policymakers and healthcare practitioners seeking to scale up malaria vaccination in endemic regions. First, the review highlights the need to tailor vaccine delivery strategies to the specific capacities and needs of each region. In countries with established immunization infrastructures, malaria vaccines can be integrated into existing programs; however, in regions with limited resources, alternative delivery methods—such as mobile clinics or drone distribution—may be essential to reach underserved communities.

While the cost-effectiveness of RTS, S/AS01 shows promise, it must be evaluated within each region’s healthcare priorities and financial limitations. Policymakers, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), must ensure that investing in malaria vaccines does not detract from other critical healthcare needs. Beyond technical and clinical considerations, the implementation of malaria vaccines requires deliberate policy and financing strategies. Sustainable funding mechanisms—whether through international donors, co-financing models, or integration into existing health insurance schemes—are essential to ensure continuity once pilot programs conclude. Integration into health systems, particularly through the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), offers a cost-efficient pathway, but requires alignment of schedules, logistics, and cold chain systems.

Moreover, the findings of this review reinforce the emerging consensus that single-antigen vaccines may be insufficient for long-term malaria control. Multi-antigen, multi-stage vaccines, which combine pre-erythrocytic and blood-stage targets, are increasingly advocated as the most promising strategy for durable protection [Tajudeen et al., 2024]. Future vaccine development and deployment should therefore be guided not only by cost-effectiveness and feasibility considerations but also by the strategic pursuit of combination vaccines within integrated malaria control frameworks.

Future research should address gaps in long-term vaccine effectiveness, delivery strategies, and socio-cultural factors that impact vaccine uptake. As new vaccines, like R21/Matrix-M, are introduced, comparative studies will be essential to identify the most effective vaccines for varying populations and settings. Furthermore, research into the integration of malaria vaccines within broader malaria control strategies(including vector control and chemoprevention)will be crucial to achieving long-term reductions in malaria transmission. They should also prioritize examining the integration of malaria vaccines into existing EPI schedules, as this could optimize delivery efficiency and coverage in malaria-endemic countries. Evidence from such studies will be critical for guiding policymakers on the most sustainable and scalable implementation pathways.
