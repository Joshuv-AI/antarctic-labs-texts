Computer modeling to improve measurement of stroke volume with echocardiography
Authors: Sarah Balderston Murthi, Shiming Yang, Peter P Oliveri, Sami Safadi, Sydea Fatima, William Teeter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe stroke volume (SV) can be measured by a human expert (HE) using the left ventricular outflow tract diameter (LVOTd) and its velocity time integral (VTI) with echo. If the SV is known, the cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance can be calculated. We have previously described a machine learning computer model (CM) to estimate the LVOTd. The accuracy of LVOTdCM vs. LVOTdHEM in estimation of the SV relative to a pulmonary artery catheter (PAC) is unknown.MethodOver 20 months, a prospective observational study was done in patients with a PAC placed for clinical indications. Participants underwent an echocardiogram, and at the same time the SV was measured by PAC. The SV with echo was calculated using the LVOTdHEM and LVOTdCM and the VTI. The bias and agreement between the metrics and PAC were determined with Bland-Altman analysis.ResultsComputer modeling improves measurement of stroke volume with echocardiography. Eighty-four patients with a PAC were enrolled. With human expert measurement of the LVOTd and the VTI, the SV could be calculated in 59 (70%), and with the LVOTdCM and the VTI in 78 (92%). Bland Altman analysis comparing PAC and HEM yielded a mean bias of 3.1 with 95% limits of agreement (LOA) -28.8 and 34.9. When comparing PAC and CM, bias was 1.75 and LOA of -29 and 32.5.ConclusionsA machine learning model of the LVOTd allows accurate calculation of the SV using only the VTI, simplifying the assessment while increasing the yield by 22%. Machine learning tools can lower the bar for obtaining quantitative metrics with echo. Adding objective and repeatable measures will improve the ability of echo to guide therapy in the critically ill.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12947-026-00373-7.

Introduction

Cardiac point-of-care ultrasound is a common method of assessing cardiac function in critically ill patients in the emergency department (ED) and intensive care units (ICU). Basic cardiac POCUS exams are simple, qualitative assessments of right and left ventricular (LV) structure and function. Advanced cardiac POCUS, including critical care echocardiography (CCE), involves a more detailed hemodynamic assessment and uses Doppler to quantify parameters such as stroke volume (SV) [1–4]. By measuring the SV, cardiac output (CO), cardiac index (CI), and systemic vascular resistance (SVR) are easily calculated using heart rate (HR) and mean arterial blood pressure (MAP) [1–3].

Echo assessment of SV requires measurement of LV outflow tract diameter (LVOTd) and LVOT velocity time integral (VTI) (Fig. 1). The LVOTd is a static anatomic length that is fixed in adulthood, whereas the VTI is a Doppler signal that varies with physiological changes and must be directly measured to obtain the SV. While the VTI can be obtained in > 90% of critically ill patients, the LVOTd can only be obtained in 70% [5]. Accurate measurement of the LVOTd is acquired from the parasternal long axis (PLA). It is especially difficult in ICU because it requires skill to obtain by angling and titling under the sternum. Expansion of the lungs by mechanical ventilation and various lung pathologies pushes the heart inferiorly and medially, making it hard to obtain adequate windows for measurement at the correct angle, even in expert hands (Fig. 2). Conversely, the VTI is measured at the LV apex from the Apical 5 Chamber (AP-5 C) and can be obtained in more patients [5].

The apical three chamber (AP-3 C) or apical long axis is an alternative way to assess the LVOT, especially with Doppler to obtain the VTI. The AP-3 C is obtained by rotating the transducer counterclockwise from the AP-5 C so that the transducer indicator is bisecting the left clavicle, or between 10 and 11 o’clock on a watch face. If the LVOT takes an acute turn, the AP-3 C can provide better access to the Doppler signal. It is possible to measure the LVOTd from the AP-3 C, however it is difficult to fully open the tract to get an accurate measurement, and it is not the recommended method [6].

We have previously described a machine learning computer model which estimates the left ventricular outflow tract diameter (LVOTdCM) based on patient characteristics including height, weight, age, and sex [7]. In this study we hypothesized that when measuring SV, the estimation derived from LVOTdCM would be as accurate as the human expert measurement (LVOTdHEM), when compared to SV measured by the gold standard pulmonary artery catheter (PAC). The ability to accurately estimate LVOTd without measuring it directly would make SV and CO assessment by CCE easier and more universally applicable in critically ill patients.

Methods

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Maryland, Baltimore (Protocol No. HP-00072560). All procedures were conducted in accordance within human ethical standards. Informed consent to participate was obtained from participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Over 20 months between January 2015 and April 2017, we conducted a prospective observational study in adult patients generated from a convenience sample. Patients greater than 18 years of age at the University of Maryland Medical Center in downtown Baltimore, Maryland receiving either a right heart catheterization in our cardiac catheterization suite (CCS) or patients admitted to one of our intensive care units with a PAC were eligible for inclusion. Enrollment occurred only during daytime weekday hours due to staffing limitations. ICU Patients were screened within 24 h of admission between 8 − 4 pm on Monday-Friday. Enrolled patients underwent a CCE [5], and the cardiac output was measured by the PAC at the same time either in the ICU or in the CCS.

Data collection and calculation

All CCEs were performed by critical care fellows with transthoracic echocardiography training under the direct supervision of a full-time Registered Diagnostic Cardiac Sonographer (RDCS) and reviewed by a RDCS critical care attending. All studies were performed using a portable cardiovascular ultrasound machine (Philips CX 50, Andover, MA) using a phased array probe. LVOTd and LVOT VTI were determined. Baseline demographics were obtained at the time of echocardiography from the electronic medical record including age, height, weight, gender, race, cardiopulmonary medical history, and surgical history. The most contemporaneous measurements of height and weight were used in the calculations.

Stroke volume was calculated using the formula SV = (𝐿𝑉𝑂𝑇𝐷/2) [2] ∗ 𝜋∗𝑉𝑇𝐼. LVOT VTI is measured in the apical five-chamber long-axis view with the beam aligned as parallel as possible to flow through the LVOT. The pulsed-wave sample volume is placed at the same level as the measurement LVOT, and the resulting laminar waveform is traced to produce the VTI. COPAC was measured by serial PAC thermodilution using primarily Arrow® Thermodilution Catheters from Teleflex. LVOTD and VTI were obtained by POC-E. SV derived from LVOTDCM and LVOTHEM are compared to SV derived from the CO by PAC as calculated using the equation COPAC = HR * SVPAC.

Machine learning derived model of LVOTd

As previously described, just over 1000 TTEs were reviewed and a subset of 287 with ideal windows for measuring the LVOTd were selected [7]. Those ideal studies were divided into training and testing datasets in which the model was developed. A further dataset of 116 echo studies was used for model validation. The characteristics found to be relevant to the model included age, sex, height, and weight. There was no overlap in this analysis with the data used to generate LVOTdCM.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses unrelated to SVCM modeling were carried out in GraphPad 10.6.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Pearson coefficient was used to measure correlation and Bland-Altman was used to assess agreement between estimated and measured LVOTd. Bland-Altman results are reported according to published recommendations [8].

Results

Eighty-four patients were enrolled. The SV could be assessed in 59 (70%) with LVOTdHEM because an adequate window of the LVOTd could not be obtained in 19 patients, and the VTI could not be obtained in 6. In 78 (93%) with LVOTdCM the SV could be determined, as only the VTI is needed for assessment.

Of the 59 patients in whom all three measures of SV could be obtained, thirty-one patients (55%) were inpatient in an ICU including 15 (27%) from both the Cardiac Surgical ICU and general Surgical ICU, and 1 (2%) from the Neurotrauma ICU. Twenty-five patients’ data (45%) were obtained as part of their outpatient right heart catheterization. Thirty-three (59%) were male with a mean age 61 ± 9.8 years. Mean height 172.1 ± 11.1 cm and weight 84.3 ± 19.9 kg yielded a mean body surface area of 1.97 ± 0.27m2. Forty-five (80.4%) of patients were in sinus rhythm, with a further 8.9% in atrial fibrillation/flutter and 8.9% in paced rhythms. Seventeen (30.4%) were on mechanical ventilation, of which 53.5% were in the post-operative period. Valvular dysfunction was seen in 57% of patients (Table 1); valvular regurgitation was seen in 21.4 (mitral), 14.3% (aortic), and 40% (tricuspid). Most patients (67%) had intact left ventricular function, with others having mild (1.8%), moderate (8.9%), severe (10.7%) dysfunction, and a further 7.1% with hyperdynamic function.

Discussion

When compared to the gold standard of PAC measurement, our study suggests that measuring SV using a model for LVOTd developed with machine learning techniques is as accurate as a human expert measurement, resulting in an increased yield of SV measurement by 22%.

Accurate measurement of the LVOTd is difficult, especially in mechanically ventilated critically ill patients. We have previously found that about 30% of the time LVOTd could not be measured at all in an ICU population even in expert hands [5]. The LVOTd is most accurately assessed from the PLA, and mechanical ventilation pushes the heart medially and inferiorly making the parasternal windows harder to obtain. Conversely, the LVOT VTI is obtained from the apex of the heart which is at the lateral chest wall. The AP-5 C or AP-3 C can be used to obtain the LVOT VTI. While the LVOTd can be measured at the AP-3 it is not the recommended location [6].

In those in whom it can be assessed, errors leading to over- and under-estimation can occur. Error in measuring the LVOTd can occur when, (a) obtaining the image, for example acquiring it at the wrong angle and/or (b) incorrect measurement on the saved or frozen image by placing the calipers in the wrong location. The LVOTd measurement is especially prone to both types of error and as the value is a squared value in calculation of the SV, the error is compounded. An accurate method of assessing the LVOTd based on patient characteristics would increase the yield of obtaining the SV and not be prone to either measurement error. Indeed, in this analysis, LVOTdCM was slightly better in the estimation of SV than that based on a human expert’s measurement of LVOTd (bias of 1.75 vs. 3.1) as compared to PAC.

The variables determined to be relevant by the model, height, weight, age, and sex, make intuitive sense. The relation of body surface area (BSA) to LVOTd has been long described, however we have shown when used alone, it is inaccurate in estimation of SV with the LVOTd VTI [6, 7]. Like the work reported here, Leye et al. found the BSA modified by the Eq. (5.7 x BSA + 12.1) to correlate well with the aortic valve area and use it to assess severity of aortic stenosis. These authors did not find sex to be predictive, and the effect of age is not reported [9]. Of note, LVOTCM has now been validated in three separate groups of patients not used in its creation, as well as against a human expert in measurement of SV/CO compared to a PAC. This is the most studied tool known to us to allow accurate estimation of LVOTd so far reported in the literature.

Zha et al. have described using deep learning to train an AI model to automatically measure LVOTd on an ideal parasternal long image [10]. While exciting, and likely reducing the error related to incorrect measurement, it still relies on being able to obtain an ideal image. Our method using patient characteristics, once validated, could remove the need to obtain the measurement at all, such that SV, CO, and SVR could be obtained with the VTI alone, which can be obtained 90% of the time in critically ill patients.

All models for estimation of SV require that the VTI be accurately measured. Even if the LVOTd measurement can be replaced by computer modeling, operator training and expertise will remain essential for accurate measurement of the SV with echo. Taken together, this study and the work of others suggest that AI and machine learning techniques could increase the accuracy of quantitative CCE by replacing the need to measure the LVOTd.

Thermodilution via a PAC has traditionally been used for measurement of CO and SV. Echo has been shown to correlate well with PAC with a similar error rate to other catheter-based assessments [1, 3, 7]. At the same time, there is an error associated with any estimator of LVOTd. While PACs have been considered the gold standard for measurement of cardiac output metrics, they are invasive and carry a significant risk [3]. As a result, they are now rarely used, making it impractical to use a PAC as a reference metric in future studies in a general ICU or ED population. Conversely advanced cardiac echo performed by a human expert is a well validated and safe method of assessment of SV and CO which has been in existence for decades and is minimal risk. In this study we found that there was no meaningful difference between the human expert and computer model in estimation of the SV. For practicality and patient safety, it may be more reasonable for advanced echo to be used as a gold standard for assessment of SV and CO moving forward.

This study has several limitations. The study population is from a convenience sample obtained during daylight hours at a single institution. Further work should include more rigorous validation in a consecutive, multi-institutional environment. Also, clinical data was obtained prospectively and analyzed retrospectively introducing the possibility of error. Additionally, there has been a considerable time lag between when this data was collected and its analysis in this manuscript. The delay is due to a variety of factors including research delays related to COVID and difficulty finding additional patients that had a PAC placed for clinical indications. Further, while the images were obtained by fellows in training at our institution, they were supervised by an experienced cardiac sonographer as part of their training, perhaps limiting generalizability.

In summary, a machine learning model estimating the LVOTd allows accurate calculation of the SV using only the VTI. This simplifies the assessment while increasing the yield from 70% to 92% and removes the user error associated with measurement. Adding objective, quantitative, and repeatable measures to CCE could improve its utility in guiding therapy. Computer modeling and machine learning could lower the bar to obtaining reliable quantitative echo measurements in the critically ill.
