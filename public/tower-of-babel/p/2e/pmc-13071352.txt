Context‐Dependent Extinction and Return of Pavlovian Alcohol‐Seeking Behavior in Male and Female Rats
Authors: Taylor A Simmons, Anagha Deshpande, Alicia R Daley, Alexander J Etz, Rueben A Gonzales, Marie H Monfils, Hongjoo J Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEnvironmental cues can become conditioned stimuli when associated with alcohol consumption, facilitating alcohol‐seeking behavior and increasing relapse risk. Extinguishing alcohol‐seeking behavior may reduce cue‐induced responses; however, conditioned responding often occurs when the cue is presented in a different context than the one where the behavior was extinguished. Although prior research suggests that males generally show stronger context‐dependent return of conditioned appetitive and fear responses, it is not known whether this sex difference extends to conditioned responses to alcohol‐associated cues.MethodsAfter a 5‐week alcohol induction period, adult male and female Long–Evans rats underwent Pavlovian conditioning in Context A, where a 15% unsweetened alcohol solution was paired with a light cue. Extinction sessions were conducted in a distinct Context B in the absence of alcohol, followed by a test session in Context B (LTM) and then in Context A.ResultsBoth sexes successfully extinguished alcohol‐seeking behavior in Context B but exhibited robust alcohol‐seeking behavior when reintroduced to the alcohol‐associated context, Context A. No significant sex differences were observed in the magnitude of reemerged conditioned responding.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that changes of context induce alcohol‐seeking behavior to return similarly in male and female rats, indicating that sex‐independent mechanisms may underlie contextual relapse processes.Keywords: alcohol‐seeking behavior, context‐dependent relapse, extinction, Pavlovian conditioning, sex differences

Introduction

Environmental cues associated with alcohol consumption play a pivotal role in the development and persistence of alcohol use disorders (Cofresí, Bartholow, and Piasecki 2019; Kohen et al. 2023). When discrete cues, such as olfactory or visual stimuli, become associated with alcohol access, these cues alone can facilitate alcohol‐seeking behavior (Cofresí et al. 2017, 2018; Cofresí, Bartholow, and Piasecki 2019; Cofresí, Grote, et al. 2019; Cofresí, Monfils, et al. 2019), which can be problematic for individuals struggling to overcome alcohol abuse (Simon et al. 2020; Witteman et al. 2015). Furthermore, the context in which the alcohol‐associated cues are presented greatly impacts how the discrete cues induce conditioned responses such as alcohol‐seeking behavior (LeCocq et al. 2020; Maddux et al. 2014; Valyear and Chaudhri 2020). People with alcohol use disorder who receive treatment in rehabilitation facilities often relapse upon returning to their home environment, where alcohol‐associated contextual cues can promote craving and relapse (Sinha 2011; Tao et al. 2019). This occurs because the environment serves as a contextual conditioned stimulus (CS) that becomes associated with alcohol consumption and leads to alcohol‐craving and seeking even during alcohol abstinence (Chaudhri et al. 2008; Janak and Chaudhri 2010; Valyear et al. 2020, 2023).

One phenomenon that illustrates the impact of context on relapse is the renewal effect (Bouton 2002, 2004). Conditioned alcohol‐seeking responses to specific cues can be weakened or extinguished by repeatedly presenting those cues without alcohol (i.e., extinction). However, this reduction in alcohol‐seeking behavior often renews when the alcohol cues are presented in a context different from the extinction context (Khoo et al. 2020). In one scenario (the ABA model), renewal happens when the cues are reintroduced in the original conditioning environment (Context A), underscoring the role of context in behavior recovery (Bouton and Peck 1989; Wing and Shoaib 2008). In another scenario (the ABC model), renewal can occur in a completely new context (Context C), showing that a return to the original setting is not always required for the behavior to resurface (Bouton et al. 2011; Campese and Delamater 2013). Finally, in the AAB renewal model, both the conditioning and extinction phases occur in the same context (Context A). Renewal is then displayed when the conditioned cues are presented in a different context (Context B). Such paradigms highlight the significance of contextual factors in the persistence and renewal of conditioned behaviors (Bouton et al. 2011; Khoo et al. 2020; Todd 2013). Importantly, definitive demonstrations of renewal require counterbalancing of contextual features and the inclusion of appropriate control groups to dissociate contextual retrieval from spontaneous recovery and other non‐specific effects (Bouton 2002). In the absence of such controls, increases in conditioned responding following extinction might be described as context‐dependent return or context‐induced reinstatement of conditioned responding (Crombag et al. 2008). This terminology reflects the return of responding when testing occurs in a context distinct from extinction, without implying a formal renewal effect.

Sex also appears to influence context‐dependent return of conditioned responding and related renewal phenomena. Males exhibit robust return of appetitive (Anderson and Petrovich 2015, 2017; Moriarty et al. 2025) and fear (Binette et al. 2022; Milad et al. 2006; Schoenberg et al. 2024) responses. However, females show more variable context‐dependent return of conditioned responding: some studies report robust appetitive (Moriarty et al. 2025) and fear (Schoenberg et al. 2024) responses of females similar to the response levels seen in males after context shift, while others report reduced appetitive (Anderson and Petrovich 2015, 2017) and fear (Binette et al. 2022; Milad et al. 2006) responses among females. The mixed evidence on sex differences may stem, in part, from the modulatory role of ovarian hormones on context‐dependent memory retrieval processes (Anderson and Petrovich 2015; Hilz, Smith, et al. 2019; Hilz et al. 2023) and/or extinction learning (Hilz et al. 2022; Milad et al. 2006, 2009). Consequently, examining sex and hormonal effects on both context‐dependent extinction and return of conditioned responding, across different types of unconditioned stimuli, remains an important avenue for investigation.

Sex differences in context‐dependent responding to alcohol cues have been reported (Segal et al. 2022). During the alcohol‐primed reinstatement test, male rats showed greater conditioned response in the alcohol‐associated context compared to the neutral (no alcohol) context while female rats did not show differential response based on contexts. Although their study suggests enhanced effects of context on males' response to alcohol cues, it did not use the renewal paradigm. To our knowledge, no study has yet examined potential sex differences in context‐dependent return of Pavlovian alcohol‐seeking behavior following extinction. Therefore, the current study explores the effects of the context‐dependent return of conditioned responding in a Pavlovian alcohol‐conditioned rat model, examining two different conditioned responses, cue‐directed orienting behavior and goal‐directed sipper approach behavior, and comparing the behavior of adult male and female Long–Evans rats. We hypothesized that context‐dependent return of conditioned responding would differ between sexes, with males exhibiting greater responses. Using an ABBA design, rats were first trained to associate alcohol with a light cue (CS) in one context (Context A). In a different context (Context B), conditioned responding was extinguished by presenting the light cue without alcohol. Extinction memory was tested in Context B, and recovery of conditioned responding was evaluated by re‐exposing rats to the light cue in the original alcohol‐associated context (Context A) in the absence of alcohol.

Methods and Materials

Subjects

Adult male and female Long–Evans rats (Envigo, Indianapolis, IN) were housed in a room on a 12:12 reverse light cycle (lights off at 9 am). Their initial weights ranged from 250 to 275 g for males and 200 to 225 g for females upon arrival. Rats were individually housed with Sani‐Chips bedding, a 5 × 5 × 5 cm wooden block, and a PVC pipe (inner diameter 10 cm). Metal wire cage tops secured the cages. Rats were provided with ad libitum standard chow pellets and tap water via gravity‐fed sippers, inserted at an approximate 45‐degree angle from the cage top. Experimental procedures were conducted 3 (±1) h into the dark cycle each day. Some female rats (n = 9 out of 15) underwent daily vaginal lavage to establish their estrous cycle state for the study's duration. Some males (n = 7 of 9) were handled similar to minimize additional handling effects. All research activities were carried out with full approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Texas at Austin and were in strict accordance with the guidelines established by the National Institutes of Health (NIH).

Apparatus

All behavioral procedures were conducted in standard conditioning chambers (Med Associates, Fairfax, VT). Chambers featured interior dimensions of 30.5 × 24.1 × 29.2 cm and were enclosed in sound‐attenuating cubicles equipped with an exhaust fan and a digital camera. A houselight was positioned atop the center of the right chamber wall and a retractable bottle assembly was affixed to the lower right side of the right chamber wall. A sipper was attached to the bottle and made available through a hole positioned 8.5 cm to the right and 16 cm below the houselight. The chambers contained clear acrylic walls on the front and back sides, aluminum walls on both the left and right sides, and floors made of steel rods.

Experimental Phase 1: Alcohol Induction

After 1 week of acclimation to the vivarium, rats were introduced to unsweetened 15E (15% v/v ethanol in water) in their home cages. Each cage contained a pre‐weighed 50 mL conical tube with 15E and a pre‐weighed 500 mL bottle of tap water, available for 24 h on access days (Monday, Wednesday, and Friday). Drinking solutions were prepared using 95% ethyl alcohol (ACS/USP grade, Pharmco‐AAPER) mixed with tap water every 3 days and served at room temperature. This phase aimed to acclimate rats to the taste and pharmacological effects of alcohol (Cofresí et al. 2017; Sparks et al. 2014). On non‐access days (Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday, and Sunday), two regular 500‐mL bottles of tap water were provided. Rats were weighed prior to bottle insertion on access days to calculate the ingested dose (alcohol in grams per kilogram of body weight, g/kg) for the 24‐h period; weighing was not required on non‐access days.

To minimize attrition and ensure consistent alcohol consumption, rats that did not consume at least 0.5 g/kg during the initial two sessions were offered a 5% alcohol solution in tap water (v/v; denoted as 5E) in subsequent sessions until they achieved a dose of at least 1 g/kg for four consecutive sessions. Subsequently, a 10% alcohol solution in tap water (v/v; 10E) was introduced following the same criteria. If these criteria were met, rats were switched back to the 15E solution. After 5 weeks (i.e., 15 drinking sessions), rats consuming more than 1 g/kg at 10E or 15E for the last three consecutive sessions advanced to conditioning.

Experimental Phase 2: Conditioning (Context A)

Following the alcohol induction phase, rats were conditioned to associate a houselight with a sipper containing either 10E or 15E. Prior to acquisition training, rats underwent drinking training in which they had access to a sipper containing alcohol for 30 min. Rats that did not drink on the first day of this training completed an additional day before transitioning to light habituation. Following the drinking training, a single session of light habituation occurred without the sipper. A total of eight trials of 20‐s light presentation were administered with a variable intertrial interval (ITI). The ITI duration was randomly selected from a list of intervals (120, 200, 210, 280, 310, 320, 280, 200 s).

After the habituation session, rats underwent 14 consecutive days of conditioning. The first trial began after a 5‐min waiting period, indicated by the activation of the cubicle exhaust fan. Each conditioning session consisted of eight trials with the same variable ITI as light habituation. The session ended after the final ITI, marked by the cessation of the exhaust fan. During each trial, the houselight was illuminated for 20 s. After 10 s of light onset, a retractable bottle assembly was activated, inserting a metal sipper into the chamber for 10 s. The sipper, equipped with a ball bearing to prevent spillage, was connected to a bottle filled with either a 10E solution (n = 4, 3 females and 1 male) or a 15E solution (n = 20). The specific solution was determined based on the rat's consumption during the last three homecage alcohol drinking sessions, ensuring that each rat exclusively drank either 10E or 15E throughout the experiment. This variation in alcohol concentrations was maintained to avoid conditioning attrition that might occur if rats previously receiving 10E were switched to 15E. Rats that consumed an average of at least 0.3 g/kg during the last three cue conditioning sessions were retained for subsequent experimental phases.

Context A involved a standard conditioning chamber (see apparatus and Figure 1). Rats were transported from their home cages to conditioning boxes using metal carriers made up of eight individual compartments (each: 19.05 cm (L) × 9.53 cm (H)). To aid in context discrimination, researchers wore standard white lab coats when conducting experiments in Context A (Nemchek et al. 2021).

Experimental Phase 3: Extinction (Context B)

Rats underwent extinction in Context B (Figure 1), a transformed conditioning chamber featuring smooth stainless‐steel flooring over the metal grid, lemon scents in waste trays, and black laminated walls on the front and back of the conditioning chambers. Additionally, rats were transported using a novel carrier composed of stackable plastic shoeboxes with lids (34.93 cm (L) × 20.65 cm (W) × 12.07 cm (H)), each with pre‐made openings on both sides. To introduce further contextual variation, researchers transitioned from white lab coats to blue lab coats during this phase.

In the extinction phase, rats were exposed to the light cue without alcohol to reduce alcohol‐seeking behaviors over 12 consecutive days. Each extinction session consisted of 12 trials. Each session began after a 5‐min waiting period, indicated by the activation of the cubicle exhaust fan, and ended after the final ITI, signaled by the fan's deactivation. During each trial, the houselight was illuminated for 20 s. After 10 s, an empty metal sipper without alcohol was inserted into the chamber for 10 s. The ITI was randomly selected from the same list of intervals used in conditioning.

Experimental Phase 4: Testing in Context B (LTM)

All rats underwent a long‐term memory (LTM) test to assess the persistence of the extinguished cue‐elicited responses in Context B. This evaluation occurred 48 h after the final extinction session. The LTM test consisted of a four‐trial session similar to the extinction session in which a 20‐s light presentation was accompanied by a 10‐s empty sipper insertion.

Experimental Phase 5: Testing in Context A

Rats were reintroduced to Context A to assess the resurgence of cue‐elicited responses in the initial environment where they were originally conditioned to consume alcohol in the presence of the light cue. This assessment occurred 48 h after the LTM testing session. The test session in Context A was identical to the LTM test session with four trials of light‐empty sipper presentations.

Behavioral Observation

Behavioral responses were recorded using digital video footage from the conditioning, extinction, test in Context B (LTM), and test in Context A. Observations were made at 1.25‐s intervals during the illumination of the houselight, resulting in a total of 16 observations per trial. Behaviors documented included (1) sipper site approach, defined as approaching or exploring the sipper insertion hole, and (2) orienting, defined as rearing to the light cue with the rat's forepaws being off the floor but not grooming. In addition, sipper licking was automatically recorded using a contact lickometer. The houselight illuminated for 20 s with the sipper insertion occurring at the 10 s time point and remaining for the remaining 10 s. Therefore, the behavioral data were organized by the first 10 s of light illumination (light only) and the second 10 s of light illumination (light+sipper).

Estrous Cycle Monitoring

Estrous cycle stages of nine female rats were determined by analyzing vaginal cell cytology (Goldman et al. 2007). Daily vaginal lavage was conducted at the beginning of the dark cycle (~9–10 am) by flushing the vaginal cavity with 40 μL of 0.9% saline and collecting epithelial cells to be examined under a light microscope. The proestrus stage, in which the estradiol and progesterone levels are high, typically shows clumped or granular cells. The estrous stage shows nucleated‐cornified cells and keratinized cells. The metestrus and diestrus stages, when the estradiol and progesterone levels are low, are characterized by a mixture of round cells, leukocytes, and needle‐like cells.

Data Analysis

Behavioral and drinking data were analyzed using a repeated‐measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with sex and daily sessions as factors. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. We used linear mixed‐effects model (LMM) to analyze the extinction data of conditioned approach and orienting due to violation of equal variance assumption. To assess sex differences in context‐dependent return of conditioned responding, two‐tailed t‐test was used to analyze behavioral data between the test in Context B (LTM) and the test in Context A for males and females separately. Cohen's d effect sizes with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for all paired comparisons to provide estimates of effect magnitude. We applied a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Estrous cycle stage was assessed for each female subject at relevant time points (conditioning, extinction, and testing). For analysis, cycle stages were coded using the first letter of each phase: P (proestrus), E (estrus), M (metestrus), and D (diestrus). When cytology indicated a transitional state (e.g., P → E), the subject was coded according to the stage from which they were transitioning (P in this example). This approach ensured consistency across subjects and minimized creation of sparse transitional categories that could not be modeled robustly. Entries that could not be reliably assigned to one of the four stages were excluded. All cycle stages were treated as categorical factors in the LMM. Proestrus was used as the reference category, such that positive model estimates indicate higher values relative to proestrus and negative estimates indicate lower values. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3 (R Core Team 2024), with the car package (Fox and Weisberg 2019). Data visualization was executed using the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2024) within R.

Results

Homecage Alcohol Induction

During the 5‐week alcohol induction period, alcohol consumption increased across 15 sessions for both male and female rats. To assess the patterns of ethanol consumption during the homecage drinking phase, two measures were analyzed: (1) absolute ethanol intake (EtOH true), reflecting the total amount (g) of ethanol consumed, and (2) body weight‐normalized ethanol intake (EtOH g/kg), representing the dose of ethanol ingested relative to body weight.

EtOH True

Analysis of absolute ethanol intake revealed a significant main effect of sex, F (1, 22) = 15.34, p < 0.001, indicating that males consumed more ethanol than females during the homecage drinking phase. This difference was large in magnitude, d = −1.59, 95% CI [−2.56, −0.63]. Additionally, a significant main effect of session was observed, F (14, 308) = 13.17, p < 0.001, reflecting changes in drinking behavior across 15 sessions. However, the interaction between sex and session was not significant, F (14, 308) = 1.22, p = 0.26, suggesting that males and females exhibited similar patterns of ethanol consumption over homecage drinking sessions (Figure 2A).

Return of Conditioned Alcohol Seeking in Context A

To assess potential return of sipper contact, approach, and orienting during a testing session in Context A, a priori t‐tests were conducted separately for males and females. First, extinction day 12 was compared to testing in Context B (LTM) to evaluate post‐extinction recall. Subsequently, testing in Context B was compared to testing in Context A to assess the return of alcohol‐seeking behavior. To minimize the influence of within‐session extinction observed during extinction and testing, only the responses from the first two trials were included in the analysis.

Female Rats: Sipper Contact

There was no significant difference in mean sipper contact between extinction session 12 and testing in Context B (LTM), t (14) = −0.61, p = 0.554, d = −0.09 (negligible). The change from testing in Context B to Context A was not statistically significant but showed a trend, t (14) = −1.89, p = 0.080, d = −0.48 (medium). These findings suggest that sipper contact in females showed only a modest, non‐significant increase in the original conditioning context (Figure 7). Visual inspection of individual data points (Figure 7) indicates that variability during testing in Context B (LTM) in females was driven by elevated responding in a single subject, which likely reduced sensitivity for detecting context‐dependent recovery of conditioned responding when using the LTM baseline for sipper contact. Using a standard rule that flags values far outside the range of most animals (1.5 × the interquartile range), one female was identified as an outlier during LTM testing. Effects of Estrous Stages on Alcohol Consumption and Conditioned Responses

We monitored the estrous stages of nine female rats (out of 15) but did not manipulate or time the experimental schedule based on their stages. Overall, we saw no significant effects of estrous cycle on any measured behaviors. Below, we highlight more detailed analysis and results.

Alcohol Intake During Conditioning

To test whether alcohol intake during conditioning differed by estrous stage, we fit a linear mixed‐effects model with alcohol intake (g/kg) as the dependent variable, estrous stage (proestrus, estrus, metestrus, diestrus) as a categorical fixed effect and subject ID as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements across conditioning days. Proestrus was used as the reference category. No significant differences in alcohol intake were observed across estrous stages, suggesting that consumption did not systematically vary during conditioning (Figure 9A).

Sipper Contact During Extinction and Testing

Sipper contact during extinction was examined to determine whether estrous stage influenced behavior across extinction days. A linear mixed‐effects model was fit with sipper contact as the dependent variable, estrous stage and day of extinction as fixed effects, their interaction, and random intercepts for each subject. No significant main effects or interactions involving estrous stage were observed, indicating that extinction did not differ across estrous stages. Together, these results suggest that estrous stage did not exert a reliable influence on alcohol consumption or extinction‐related behaviors in our study.

Discussion

The present study found sex differences in context‐dependent return of conditioned responding in Pavlovian conditioning paradigm using unsweetened ethanol as the reinforcer. Because contexts were not counterbalanced and a no context change control group was not included, the present findings should be interpreted as context‐dependent return rather than a definitive demonstration of renewal of conditioned responding. Using an ABBA design, both male and female rats acquired conditioned alcohol‐seeking behavior with no differences in acquisition rate or in the magnitude of conditioned responses to the alcohol‐paired light cue. Extinction learning was also comparable across sexes. Upon return to the original conditioning context, both sexes exhibited return of alcohol‐seeking behavior, with no significant sex differences in recovery magnitude. This pattern was mostly consistent across multiple behavioral measures, including cue‐induced approach behaviors, orienting responses, and sipper contact behaviors. Although context‐dependent recovery of sipper contact reached statistical significance in males but not females in the full‐sample analysis, this pattern reflects elevated variability during the LTM test rather than a meaningful sex difference, as recovery was evident across conditioned approach measures and was qualitatively similar between sexes. Our use of Pavlovian conditioning procedures allowed for clearer isolation of cue‐outcome learning, independent of operant response requirements (Chaudhri et al. 2008). Thus, our findings suggest that the mechanisms underlying cue‐induced alcohol‐seeking behavior may operate similarly across sexes.

A factor to consider is that some rats were trained with 10% ethanol whereas others received 15% ethanol, which differ in taste cues and expected pharmacokinetic profiles, including the likelihood of faster and higher blood ethanol concentrations. Although alcohol intake was normalized to body weight (g/kg), blood ethanol concentrations were not measured, and thus, g/kg intake alone may not fully reflect pharmacological differences between solutions. Consequently, variability in ethanol concentration should be considered when interpreting individual differences in conditioned responding; although we lacked the power to directly quantify its contribution since only three females and one male were trained with 10E, the overall pattern of context‐dependent recovery was consistent across measures and sexes.

In addition to the sipper licks and sipper‐site approach behavior, we also measured orienting response toward the light cue associated with alcohol access. Orienting behavior has been well characterized in Pavlovian appetitive conditioning where a CS such as light is paired with food (Holland 1977). Conditioned orienting is acquired over the course of light‐food pairings and is extinguished if food is no longer delivered after light presentation (i.e., extinction). Conditioned orienting is considered to reflect enhanced attention to the cue that has now acquired incentive value (Holland and Gallagher 1999). Our current study is the first to measure orienting behavior in Pavlovian alcohol conditioning. Unlike in appetitive conditioning, orienting behavior was not acquired during light‐alcohol conditioning, and, in fact, they decreased over the course of conditioning and remained low during extinction. There was also no sex difference in orienting behavior in our current study unlike what was observed in appetitive conditioning (Hilz et al. 2022). Female rats display greater conditioned orienting than males regardless of their hormonal state (i.e., intact, ovariectomized (OVX), OVX with estradiol replacement). Conditioned orienting behavior is a particular interest to understand factors related to substance use disorders. For example, orienting behavior is a predictor of impaired extinction (Olshavsky et al. 2013; Hilz, Lewis, et al. 2019) and other cognitive traits such as impulsivity, risky decision making, and reduced attention (Olshavsky et al. 2014; Hilz et al. 2022). In our current experimental paradigm, conditioned orienting to the light was not acquired during light‐alcohol conditioning, suggesting that orienting might not be an ideal behavioral measure to reflect enhanced attention to the cue associated with alcohol access.

The existing literature predominantly describes renewal of Pavlovian alcohol seeking in male rats, often employing ABA designs with oral ethanol as the unconditioned stimulus (Maddux et al. 2014; Khoo et al. 2020; Sciascia et al. 2014; Valyear et al. 2018). In contrast, studies examining reinstatement of alcohol seeking typically using operant self‐administration paradigms have revealed sex‐dependent effects. For example, studies found that female rats exhibited enhanced reinstatement of alcohol seeking when both stress (via the pharmacological stressor yohimbine) and alcohol‐related cues were presented together, whereas males showed reinstatement only to cues and a modest enhancement with yohimbine (Bertholomey et al. 2016). Importantly, this reinstatement model involves instrumental responding (lever pressing) for alcohol reward, contrasting with Pavlovian paradigms that isolate cue–outcome learning without an instrumental response requirement. The heightened reinstatement observed in females under complex trigger conditions suggests sex differences in relapse vulnerability, whereas the present Pavlovian findings indicate that context‐dependent recovery engages largely sex‐independent processes. Therefore, while both reinstatement and paradigms model relapse‐like behaviors, their differential sensitivity to sex and stress factors highlights the need to examine how motivational and contextual processes interact across paradigms and hormonal states.

Using an alcohol‐primed reinstatement paradigm, Segal et al. (2022) tested both male and female rats for Pavlovian conditioned response to alcohol‐associated cues with contextual manipulations and reported that males displayed stronger context‐dependent reinstatement of cue approach behavior compared to females. However, this study did not implement a renewal paradigm, limiting insight into classic renewal behavior. In contrast, sex differences in Pavlovian renewal have been more extensively documented in appetitive conditioning models. Anderson and Petrovich (2015) demonstrated that male rats exhibit robust ABA renewal of food‐seeking behavior, whereas females show diminished renewal unless estradiol is administered (in ovariectomized females), indicating hormonal modulation. Hilz, Smith, et al. (2019); Hilz et al. (2023) also showed that estrous cycle phases significantly influenced renewal of appetitive behavior. However, unlike the report from Anderson and Petrovich (2015), female rats in a low hormonal state (i.e., metestrus and diestrus) on the day of renewal testing showed the most renewal effect if they underwent extinction in a high hormonal state (i.e., proestrus). Furthermore, a recent study by Moriarty et al. (2025) reported no sex or hormonal effects on appetitive renewal behavior: males and females (either intact or ovariectomized) showed similar renewal of foodcup approach response. In accordance with Moriarty et al. (2025), our current finding also saw no sex differences in context‐dependent return of Pavlovian alcohol seeking. Sex differences in Pavlovian renewal have also been reported in fear conditioning models. Males show greater renewal of fear response than females (Binette et al. 2022; Milad et al. 2006). However, Schoenberg et al. (2024) saw similar fear renewal between males and females. Taken together, the available evidence suggests that males exhibit robust renewal while females show more variable renewal effects.

Differences in the protocols employed for extinction‐training represent a key yet understudied variable in alcohol‐seeking relapse. Nearly all alcohol studies, including our own, use repeated, multi‐day extinction sessions; no work has tested whether a single, massed extinction session produces comparable return of alcohol seeking across sexes. This gap matters because extinction format influences both extinction‐memory strength and contextual specificity. Repeated extinction likely engages more robust, context‐dependent learning, while massed extinction may produce weaker, less specific memories (Urcelay et al. 2009; Bisby et al. 2015). Clarifying whether females differ in how they respond to different extinction formats would help determine whether observed sex differences reflect retrieval and contextual processes or differences in extinction learning, potentially influenced by estradiol. Additionally, repeated extinction spans multiple hormonal states in females, while a single session captures extinction learning in one hormonal snapshot, offering a clearer window into hormone‐behavior interactions.

Another underexplored area involves the potential for hormonal effects on extinction and renewal. Our results showed that while context‐dependent return of alcohol seeking was robust, it did not systematically vary based on the estrous stages. These findings suggest that context‐dependent return of conditioned response is expressed independently of cycling ovarian hormones, consistent with work demonstrating comparable renewal of intact and ovariectomized females (Moriarty et al. 2025). While our data did not show clear estrous stage effects, it was not designed to control for estrous stages. Thus, extinction training and testing occurred across mixed estrous stages, diluting the potential effects of the ovarian hormones on these behaviors. Conducting extinction and/or testing during specific estrous stages will likely detect ovarian hormonal influence. For example, Hilz, Smith, et al. (2019) showed that rats undergoing amphetamine extinction during proestrus stage only (high hormonal state) showed impaired extinction compared to rats receiving amphetamine extinction only during metestrus/diestrus (low hormonal state). Hilz, Lewis, et al. (2019); Hilz et al. (2023) also showed how estrous stages influence renewal of appetitive behavior in a hormonal state‐dependent manner. When extinction occurred in proestrus (P, high hormonal state) and testing occurred in a different estrous stage of metestrus or diestrus (M/D, low hormonal state), females exhibited elevated renewal of food‐cup responding; renewal was minimal when extinction and test occurred in the same high hormonal state and no renewal was seen if extinction occurred during the low hormonal state regardless of the estrous state during renewal. Therefore, it would be important to explicitly test whether renewal of alcohol‐seeking behavior is influenced by matching or mismatching hormonal states between acquisition, extinction, and testing phases, a question directly tied to internal context‐dependent learning and memory (Bouton 2002; Maren et al. 2013). Mechanistically, ovarian hormones may modulate context retrieval or engagement of prefrontal–hippocampal circuits, thereby influencing return of conditioned responding.

Our study advances understanding of sex similarities and differences in addiction‐related cue learning and relapse by demonstrating no sex differences in Pavlovian alcohol seeking under well‐controlled conditions. Future studies that integrate clinically relevant dependence models, hormonal state matching, and neural circuit analyses will be essential for elucidating mechanisms of alcohol relapse in males and females. Such work has the potential to inform precision medicine strategies and guide the development of tailored interventions for men and women with alcohol use disorder.
