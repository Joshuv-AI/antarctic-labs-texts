A healthcare-associated measles outbreak corroborates long-term waning of vaccine-induced immunity in Taiwan
Authors: Chao-Chih Lai, Hsiao-Chi Wang, Chih-Yen Chang, Pei-Yu Wei, Chu-Chun Cheng, Jia-Wen Gu, Ting-Yu Lin, Dih-Ling Luh, Chen-Yang Hsu, Tony Hsiu-Hsi Chen, Yen-Po Yeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImported measles triggered a healthcare-associated outbreak with 19 confirmed cases. Despite high childhood vaccination rates preventing cases under 20 years, a significant immunity waning was identified. Findings underscore the importance of adult vaccination, pre-travel immunization, and healthcare infection control measures.

Measles persists as a global health challenge despite effective vaccines. In 2023, global first-dose measles coverage was 83%, estimated measles cases rose by 20% to 10.3 million, and 57 countries experienced large or disruptive outbreaks.1 In near-elimination settings, measles control is challenged by slow waning of vaccine-induced immunity and occasional primary vaccine failure, particularly in populations with limited exposure to natural infection.2

Taiwan introduced the measles vaccine in 1968 and established routine vaccination by 1978. After the 1988–1989 outbreak, supplemental campaigns (1992–1994, 2001–2004) targeted cohorts born between 1976 and 1994 to achieve elimination by 2000.3 Since 1998, vaccine coverage has exceeded 95%, reducing indigenous cases to fewer than 10 annually, with incidence rates of 0–1.5 per million from 1999 to 2008. Resurgence occurred during 2018–2019, with 28 and 82 cases, ~70% linked to travel.4 Hospitals were primary transmission sites, prompting Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (TCDC) to mandate Measles, Mumps and Rubella (MMR) vaccination for post-1981-born healthcare workers lacking recent documentation or with negative serology to reduce nosocomial spread. Two-dose MMR coverage in Changhua remained high at 98.82% in the post-pandemic era.

A December 2024 measles outbreak in central Taiwan, linked to travel, involved internal medicine wards. Cases met national criteria; isolation, contact tracing, and quarantine were applied. Vaccination status was verified via records (post-1984) or questionnaires (pre-1984). Vaccine effectiveness was analysed in 20–49 year-olds due to data gaps and natural immunity in older groups. Travel-related exposure was based on history, symptom onset, and contact tracing.

An unvaccinated 48-year-old man returning from Vietnam was admitted to Ward A via the ED on December 2, developed a rash on December 5, and was diagnosed with measles on December 6. He was transferred to isolation. Contact tracing identified 3601 individuals, with 71.7% having complete vaccination records. The index case resulted in 11 secondary infections, which subsequently generated 8 tertiary cases, constituting a mixed transmission outbreak with a total of 20 cases (Figure S1). All 20 confirmed cases were Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) –positive for wild-type B3 genotype (Table S1); one was air travel-related, 19 were healthcare-associated. Most (17/20) were IgG-positive/IgM-negative; the index case was IgM/IgG-positive, and two were IgM/IgG-negative. Three were diagnosed post-rash; two lacked index contact, suggesting airborne transmission in Ward A and the ED. RT-PCR screening of 116 identified five Healthcare Worker (HCW) cases, with two more later (see the section on Public Health Response in the appendix). ED HCWs had direct exposure; inpatient HCWs did not. Throat swabs enabled earlier detection than urine, highlighting sample timing and type in diagnosis. Key details on MMR rollout, cohort classification, and containment measures are in the Supplementary Appendix.

Delayed diagnosis of the index case likely enabled early transmission, underscoring the need for prompt recognition. Missed atypical/mild cases and inter-ward transfers further facilitated airborne spread within the hospital. Inter-ward transmission and patient movement amplified the outbreak. One immunocompromised patient (PCR-positive, including stool) transmitted measles to a long-term care facility, causing six secondary cases. Among 20 confirmed cases (mean age 38.3, 50% male), stool detection in one case suggested possible faecal shedding. Vaccination coverage declined with age, 57.1% in cases and 44.3% in contacts aged 30–39, indicating waning immunity and limited natural exposure. Vaccine effectiveness in 20–49 year-olds was 23.5%. The overall attack rate was 0.50% (95% CI: 0.32–0.79%), with no cases under 20. Rates varied by birth cohort: 0.15% (pre-1978), 1.24% (1978–1998), and 0.19% (post-1998) (Table 1).

Unit-based contact tracing assumed broad exposure. Measures included symptom monitoring, quarantine, admission holds, and visitor limits. Despite 81% HCW presumptive immunity, breakthrough cases occurred. Due to data gaps, 5,368 received MMR prophylaxis. No cases emerged after two incubation periods. Challenges included record gaps, logistics, quarantine compliance, and IgG-positive cases. These may reflect waning immunity or vaccine failure, but also occur in high-coverage settings (Orenstein’s paradox).5 IgG positivity may not indicate true protection due to assay limits, T-cell mediated immunity, or misclassified vaccination.

Similar to U.S. outbreaks (2020–2024) and the 2019 NYC outbreak, this case demonstrates how global mobility challenges elimination efforts. Importations dominated measles cases in regions with interrupted endemic transmission (2001–2011).6,7 Air travel exposure frequently extends to healthcare settings, highlighting the necessity for pre-travel vaccination assessment and border screening.

Although Taiwan maintains measles vaccine coverage above WHO targets, imported cases and hospital transmission persist. The index and second-wave cases were born before 1981, aligning with TCDC's revised criteria for presumptive immunity: prior infection, two vaccine doses with the latest within 15 years, or a positive antibody test within 5 years. Infections occurred in both vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals, most IgG-positive, with several having received multiple MMR doses, suggesting waning immunity and possible secondary vaccine failure.2

A 2021 TCDC study reported an average seropositivity rate of 70%,8 despite high vaccination coverage since 1998. Seropositivity peaked at 97% in pre-vaccination cohorts (born before 1978) and declined to 37% in individuals aged 15–19, indicating waning vaccine-induced immunity (Figure S2). Age-specific seroprevalence reflects natural immunity in older cohorts and vaccine dependence in younger ones. Reduced natural boosting contributes to a V-shaped seropositivity curve, revealing adolescent immunity gaps and highlighting challenges to measles elimination in highly vaccinated populations.

Detection of the measles virus in the stool of an immunocompromised patient with diarrhoea provided novel evidence of faecal shedding. While measles-related diarrhoea is recognized,9 viral detection in stool is newly reported. Faecal-oral transmission remains speculative and undocumented. Faecal viral presence may reflect prolonged shedding in immunocompromised individuals.

This outbreak highlights the challenge of sustaining measles elimination when imported cases infect young adults with waning immunity, despite high vaccination coverage. While waning immunity and occasional primary vaccine failure contribute to vulnerability, maintaining high two-dose vaccine coverage remains a critical public health priority. In high-coverage settings, gaps in two-dose uptake may drive outbreaks more than waning immunity. Presumed immunity is insufficient in healthcare settings. Rapid spread underscores the need for early recognition, strict control, and HCW boosters. Unit-based tracing and universal prophylaxis effectively contained transmission.
