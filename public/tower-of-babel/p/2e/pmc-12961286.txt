Complex Management of an Infectious Aortic Pseudoaneurysm Secondary to Disseminated Tuberculosis in a Patient with Systemic Lupus Erythematosus and End-Stage Renal Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Johana M Pardo-Olivares, Luis H Arzola-Flores, Javier E Anaya-Ayala, Berenice Gonzalez-Eliceche, Ana Gilabert-Garcia, José A Rodriguez-Simón, Felipe J Rodriguez-Hernandez, Carlos A Hinojosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfectious aortic pseudoaneurysm secondary to disseminated tuberculosis is rare and potentially fatal, particularly in immunocompromised patients. We report a 37-year-old female patient with systemic lupus erythematosus and end-stage renal disease on immunosuppressive therapy and hemodialysis who presented with post-dialysis fever. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed a descending thoracic aortic pseudoaneurysm. During hospitalization, she developed persistent fever, abdominal pain, and lower-extremity purpura. Urgent thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) was performed using a 24×100 mm single thoracic stent-graft. Post-TEVAR CTA demonstrated a persistent endoleak, raising concern for a distal type Ib versus type III endoleak; therefore, left thoracotomy was performed, and a type III endoleak was confirmed intraoperatively. Tuberculous aortitis was confirmed by GeneXpert (Cepheid) analysis of the aortic tissue. Renal-adjusted antitubercular therapy was administered with favorable clinical and imaging outcomes. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary management of tuberculous aortitis in high-risk patients.Keywords: Tuberculous aortitis, Aortic pseudoaneurysm, Thoracic endovascular aortic repair, Systemic lupus erythematosus, End-stage renal disease

INTRODUCTION

Infectious aortic pseudoaneurysm caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis is an uncommon form of aortic involvement with high mortality. The medical literature indicates a male predominance and a wide age range (15-79 years) [1,2]. The condition typically presents with persistent fever, weight loss, and nonspecific thoracic or back pain, which often leads to delayed diagnosis and treatment [3]. Aortic involvement may occur through hematogenous dissemination from a primary focus, direct extension from adjacent infected tissues, or seeding onto a previously damaged aortic wall in the setting of chronic inflammation [4,5].

Management generally requires a multidisciplinary approach that combines prolonged antitubercular therapy with thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) or open surgery, depending on the patient’s clinical stability and anatomical location of vascular involvement [3,6]. Long-term outcome data specifically for tuberculous aortic aneurysms remain limited; however, in broader cohorts of infective aortic aneurysms treated with endovascular repair plus prolonged antimicrobial therapy, overall survival has been reported as 91% at 1 month, 80% at 1 year, and 61% at 5 years [7].

This case report describes a female patient with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) on hemodialysis who developed disseminated tuberculosis with aortic involvement. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Instituto Nacional de Ciencias Médicas y Nutrición Salvador Zubirán (Approval No. SCI-5774-25-25-1). Written informed consent was obtained from the patient for publication of clinical data and images.

CASE

A 37-year-old female patient with SLE, diagnosed at 19 years of age and treated with cyclophosphamide, rituximab, and prednisone, progressed to ESRD requiring hemodialysis since 2020. Her comorbidities included central vein stenosis, prior deep vein thrombosis, hypertension, and uremic cardiomyopathy with mildly reduced ejection fraction.

In late June 2024, she developed intermittent fever after hemodialysis sessions. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) performed on June 25, 2024, revealed a penetrating ulcer in the descending thoracic aorta (Fig. 1). Consequently, she was admitted on July 8, 2024, for diagnostic evaluation and consideration of endovascular treatment. Blood cultures were obtained and empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated (a third-generation cephalosporin, vancomycin, and ertapenem). Despite treatment, fever persisted up to 39°C, and she developed abdominal pain and painful purpura on the extremities. Laboratory tests showed normocytic anemia (hemoglobin, 8.6 g/dL), lymphopenia, and elevated procalcitonin (1.13 ng/mL). Peritoneal fluid analysis demonstrated an elevated adenosine deaminase level (51 U/L), supporting tuberculous peritonitis. Anti–dsDNA antibodies were positive, and cutaneous findings were suggestive of vasculitis.

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) findings at initial presentation. (A) Axial CTA showed a penetrating aortic ulcer in the descending thoracic aorta with focal wall disruption (blue arrow). (B) Sagittal CTA confirmed the ulcer in the distal descending thoracic aorta (white arrow). (C) Three-dimensional CTA reconstruction demonstrated a saccular outpouching of the descending thoracic aorta consistent with an infective pseudoaneurysm (yellow arrow).

Because of her history of interstitial lung disease, bronchoscopy with bronchial aspirate was performed on July 29, 2024 (hospital day 21). Follow-up CTA on August 1, 2024, demonstrated rapid progression to a descending thoracic aortic pseudoaneurysm, raising concern for imminent rupture. Given acute hypotension and hemodynamic instability in the setting of suspected infectious aortitis, urgent TEVAR was performed on August 6, 2024. Preoperative CTA showed a maximum pseudoaneurysm sac diameter of 39 mm, with proximal and distal landing-zone aortic diameters of 20-22 mm; therefore, a 24×24×100-mm Terumo Relay®Plus (Terumo Aortic) thoracic stent-graft was selected with planned 10%-20% oversizing at the landing zone. Prophylactic cerebrospinal fluid drainage was not performed because active infection or suspected bacteremia was considered a contraindication. Intraoperatively, a distal type Ib endoleak was noted and treated with adjunctive ballooning, with no residual endoleak on completion angiography (Fig. 2).

Angiographic sequence during thoracic endovascular aortic repair. (A) Initial angiography showed the pseudoaneurysm sac. (B) Deployment of the thoracic stent-graft. (C) Balloon molding after stent-graft deployment. (D) Completion angiography demonstrated adequate exclusion of the pseudoaneurysm with no immediate endoleak.

Post-TEVAR CTA on August 9, 2024, demonstrated persistent contrast opacification within the pseudoaneurysm sac (Fig. 3). Although the imaging findings could not definitively distinguish a distal type Ib endoleak from device-related structural failure, the location and configuration of contrast extravasation suggested a possible type III endoleak. Endovascular relining was considered; however, open repair was selected to enable definitive identification and repair of a suspected device-related defect in the setting of ongoing infection. Therefore, open surgical repair was performed on August 21, 2024.

Post-thoracic endovascular aortic repair computed tomography angiography (CTA) findings at 3-day follow-up. (A) Multiplanar CTA reconstruction suggested a persistent distal type Ib versus possible type III endoleak, with contrast extravasation into the pseudoaneurysm sac (blue arrow). (B) Axial CTA demonstrated contrast extravasation within the pseudoaneurysm sac outside the thoracic stent-graft (blue arrow), raising suspicion for device-related structural failure. A type IIIb endoleak was confirmed intraoperatively.

Through a left thoracoabdominal approach with single-lung ventilation, extensive friable inflammatory tissue (approximately 10 cm) surrounding the descending thoracic aorta was identified (Fig. 4). Proximal and distal control was achieved via partial aortic cross-clamping and the aneurysmal sac was incised. The source of leakage was identified as a stent-graft fabric tear consistent with a type IIIb endoleak adjacent to a severely calcified aortic segment. The defect was repaired primarily with 3-0 polypropylene sutures reinforced with pledgets and further reinforced with a bovine pericardial patch using interrupted sutures. Extensive lavage with rifampicin solution was performed, and a pleural drain was placed.

Pledges (asterisks) are identified. (B) Identification of the pseudoaneurysm capsule (black arrow). Local irrigation with rifampicin (white arrow). Pledges (asterisks) are identified. The bronchial aspirate later tested positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, with the result reported on August 16, 2024. Renal-adjusted antitubercular therapy was initiated immediately. The introduction of rifampicin, a potent inducer of cytochrome P450 enzymes (primarily CYP3A4), resulted in a significant drug interaction with the patient´s immunosuppressive regimen. Following multidisciplinary coordination with rheumatology and infectious disease, rigorous adjustments were made to ensure treatment efficacy. Prednisone was increased to compensate for accelerated hepatic metabolism induced by rifampicin. Other immunosuppressants, including cyclophosphamide and rituximab, were temporarily withheld during the induction phase of the antitubercular treatment. This temporary discontinuation was intended to minimize rifampicin-related drug interactions and the risk of subtherapeutic immunosuppression, while preserving effective antitubercular therapy and maintaining adequate glucocorticoid coverage for SLE control.

The pseudoaneurysm capsule biopsy obtained during open surgery was prioritized for microbiologic analysis to facilitate pathogen identification, leaving no remaining tissue for standard histopathologic processing. Tuberculous aortitis was definitively confirmed by a positive GeneXpert (Cepheid) assay performed directly on aortic tissue. The clinical course was favorable, with hemodynamic stabilization and a progressive decline in inflammatory markers. The patient was discharged on September 10, 2024, after a 64-day hospital stay. At 12-month follow-up, CTA demonstrated no residual endoleak (Fig. 5). The patient remains in the maintenance phase of antituberculosis therapy under multidisciplinary follow-up. DISCUSSION

Tuberculous involvement of the aorta is clinically rare. Fewer than 150 cases of tuberculous aortic pseudoaneurysm have been reported in the international literature. Without timely treatment, reported mortality ranges from 16% to 65% [1,8]. Tuberculous aortic pseudoaneurysm is more frequently encountered in regions with a high tuberculosis burden. In addition, the condition is more common in immunocompromised patients, in whom the likelihood of extrapulmonary involvement increases to 15%-20% [9,10].

This case illustrates a complex clinical course in a patient with disseminated tuberculosis and concomitant SLE and ESRD, managed with immunosuppressants and hemodialysis. SLE has been associated with aortic pathology, including aneurysm formation and dissection, through inflammatory aortitis and accelerated atherosclerosis [11]. In addition, the combination of SLE and ESRD confers substantial immune dysfunction, increasing susceptibility to serious extrapulmonary infections, such as tuberculosis. In our patient, this background likely contributed to the development of tuberculous pseudoaneurysm and rapid progression, emphasizing the importance of early recognition and timely multidisciplinary management in high-risk hosts. Notably, the aortic lesion was initially identified as a penetrating aortic ulcer, and disseminated tuberculosis was confirmed microbiologically at multiple sites, including molecular confirmation from aortic tissue by GeneXpert. Reports describing such extensive microbiologic involvement together with aortic pseudoaneurysm remain limited [7,12].

In this case, persistent sac opacification on CTA performed 72 hours after TEVAR raised concern for an endoleak, with distal type Ib versus type III endoleak considered on imaging. Intraoperative exploration ultimately confirmed a stent-graft fabric tear consistent with a type IIIb endoleak. This case underscores that when early postoperative imaging shows persistent sac opacification, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for device-related structural failure, particularly in the setting of calcified or inflamed fragile aortic tissue.

We hypothesize that the plausible and complementary mechanisms for the early type IIIb endoleak are as follows. First, localized severe calcification at the landing zone may have produced shear forces that predisposed the graft fabric to abrasion or tearing. Second, nonuniform aortic wall compliance in infectious/inflammatory aortitis may have led to localized stress concentration from stent-graft radial force, predisposing the device to early structural failure. In inflammatory or infectious aortitis, structural failure may occur earlier than in typical atherosclerotic disease; therefore, vigilant early postoperative surveillance with CTA (supplemented by digital subtraction angiography when needed) is essential, particularly when TEVAR is used as a temporizing or containment strategy in unstable patients.

Untreated infective aortic aneurysms carry a high risk of rupture, and rupture is often fatal without prompt intervention [13]. Although TEVAR may serve as an effective first-line option for hemodynamically unstable or high-risk patients, endoleaks have been reported in up to 30% of cases after endovascular aortic repair, and persistent infection increases the likelihood of late complications [14,15]. In our patient, a persistent, high-flow type IIIb endoleak required definitive open repair. TEVAR served as an initial containment/bridging measure, followed by unplanned open conversion for definitive management of the type IIIb endoleak despite the patient’s substantial comorbidity burden and high operative risk.

For infection control in a contaminated or potentially contaminated field with a prosthetic graft, radical surgical debridement with in situ reconstruction is generally considered definitive; however, this was not feasible in our patient due to her extreme frailty. Instead, targeted repair of the fabric defect with pledget-reinforced sutures and a bovine pericardial patch was performed to minimize aortic clamping and physiologic stress. Adjunctive measures included rifampin-based local strategies (e.g., irrigation/soaking), which are used in some centers but remain supported by limited observational data rather than high-level evidence [16]. Prior series suggest that open repair may enable more thorough debridement and improved infection control at the expense of higher perioperative risk [17]. In addition, late complications after endovascular treatment of infective aneurysms are not uncommon; Sörelius et al. [17] reported that up to one-third of patients undergoing endovascular treatment for infective aneurysms experienced late complications requiring reintervention or graft explantation, underscoring the need for strict clinical and imaging follow-up [18].

The optimal duration of antitubercular therapy for tuberculous aortic infection remains undefined. However, recent reviews have suggested treatment for at least 9-12 months, tailored to clinical course and microbiologic response [19]. In our patient, an extended, individualized regimen was selected given disseminated disease and chronic immunosuppression, with dosing adjusted for renal function. Ongoing duration will be determined by clinical status, inflammatory markers, and follow-up imaging, and is expected to extend beyond 12 months.

A limitation of this report is the absence of histopathologic images demonstrating classic granulomatous inflammation. Given the limited tissue volume and the diagnostic priority in an immunocompromised patient, the entire specimen was allocated to microbiologic culture and molecular testing (GeneXpert), which provided definitive etiologic confirmation.

In summary, this case highlights the challenges of managing tuberculous infective aortic pseudoaneurysm in a severely immunocompromised patient with ESRD. The combination of endovascular repair and open surgery, together with individualized prolonged antitubercular therapy, achieved clinical stabilization. Multidisciplinary management and close follow-up are essential, and further studies are needed to define optimal strategies for high-risk patients with tuberculous aortic infection.
