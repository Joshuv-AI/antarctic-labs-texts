Predictors of physical and mental health in youth with disabilities: the role of Paralympic sport participation
Authors: Luca Puce, Carlo Biz, Piergiorgio Andrisano, Pietro Ruggieri, Piotr Zmijewski, Nicola Luigi Bragazzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to examine the predictors of physical and mental health in a large sample of young people with disabilities.MethodsA total of 1,134 participants (mean age: 17.46 years; standard deviation: 3.71) completed the SF-12 questionnaire, which provides physical (PCS) and mental (MCS) health scores. Participants were categorized based on their Paralympic participation (individual, team, or non-athlete), disability type, geographical region, and residential context. Multivariate linear regression models were used to identify independent predictors of PCS and MCS scores while controlling for relevant covariates.ResultsPCS scores were higher in participants of individual (46.5) and team (47.6) sports than in non-athletes (39.3; p < 0.001), with no significant differences between the athlete groups (p = 0.324). Similarly, MCS scores were higher in participants in both individual (48.9) and team (48.8) sports than in non-athletes (34.3; p < 0.001), with no significant differences between sport types (p = 0.999). The PCS model (R² = 0.172) identified the following positive predictors: residence in Northern or Southern Europe (p = 0.001 and p = 0.020, respectively); participation in sports activities (p < 0.001); living in rural areas (p = 0.016); and presence of an intellectual disability (p = 0.003). A negative predictive factor was the presence of a neurological disability (p < 0.001). The MCS model (R² = 0.348) identified southern European origin (p = 0.014), participation in sports activities (p < 0.001) and intellectual disability (p = 0.037) as positive predictors, while advanced age (p = 0.025) and acquired disability (p < 0.001) were identified as negative predictors.ConclusionParalympic sport was the strongest predictor of better physical and mental health, followed by geographical origin, age, disability type and residential setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13102-026-01893-5.Keywords: Paralympic sport, SF-12 questionnaire, Sport participation, Intellectual disability, Neurological impairment, Congenital vs acquired disability, Environmental context, Rural–urban differences, Self-determination theory

Introduction

Over the past several decades, the physical and mental health of individuals with disabilities—particularly during adolescence and early adulthood—has emerged as a central concern within public health, rehabilitation sciences, and developmental research [1]. This population is disproportionately exposed to a constellation of interrelated medical, psychological, social, and environmental constraints that may compromise health status, functional capacity, and long-term well-being [2].

In response, a growing body of empirical work has sought to elucidate the determinants underpinning physical and mental health outcomes in young people with disabilities, drawing attention to the influence of demographic and clinical characteristics, socioeconomic positioning, environmental accessibility, and participation in structured and meaningful activities [3, 4]. Within this multidimensional framework, engagement in sport—and, more specifically, adapted competitive sport—has been increasingly conceptualized as a salient and potentially modifiable determinant of health.

In this regard, the Paralympic movement has gained recognition not only as a platform for elite athletic achievement but also as a powerful mechanism for health promotion, empowerment, and social inclusion among individuals with disabilities [5, 6]. Accumulating evidence indicates that participation in Paralympic sport is associated with a wide spectrum of favorable outcomes spanning physical, psychological, and social domains. A systematic review conducted by Mira et al. [7], for instance, documented consistent associations between involvement in Paralympic sport and improvements across multiple health-related and psychosocial indicators in individuals with disabilities. From a physiological standpoint, engagement in adapted sport has been linked to enhanced physical fitness, improved functional performance, and a reduced incidence of secondary health conditions commonly associated with sedentary behavior and physical inactivity [8, 9].

Concurrently, from a psychological perspective, sport participation has been shown to support the development of adaptive psychological resources, including resilience, emotional regulation, and positive affect, while attenuating distress, negative mood states, and vulnerability to psychosocial adversity [10, 11].

Beyond its individual-level benefits, adapted competitive sport also constitutes a critical context for social participation and integration. Structured sport environments provide opportunities for social interaction, collective identity formation, and the establishment of supportive interpersonal networks, thereby mitigating social isolation and fostering inclusive social climates. Within this ecological system, relationships with coaches, teammates, family members, and peers function as pivotal social assets that may buffer stress, enhance motivation, and contribute to more favorable health-related trajectories [11–13].

More recent scholarship focusing specifically on youth para-athletes has further underscored the relevance of these psychosocial processes. A review by Zabala-Domínguez et al. [14] demonstrated that sport participation among young people with disabilities is associated with enhanced autonomy, perceived environmental mastery, personal growth, social connectedness, and self-acceptance—core dimensions of psychological well-being and positive development [15–17].

Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of Paralympic sport to operate as a developmentally salient context capable of supporting health, psychosocial adjustment, and adaptive functioning during a critical life stage. Nevertheless, it is equally important to recognize that the benefits associated with sport participation are neither uniform nor universally experienced. Substantial heterogeneity has been observed as a function of sport modality, competitive level, and disability-related characteristics, including the etiology and timing of disability onset [18, 19]. Individuals with acquired disabilities, for example, may exhibit more favorable indicators of psychological adjustment or perceived mental health, potentially reflecting adaptive coping, meaning-making processes, or post-traumatic growth following life-altering events [16]. However, this pattern is not consistent across all contexts, as unresolved trauma and persistent psychosocial stressors associated with acquired disabilities may continue to exert deleterious effects on health-related outcomes for some individuals [19]. Moreover, high-performance sport settings—such as those characteristic of Paralympic-level competition—may simultaneously introduce a range of sport-specific and disability-related stressors [18–20]. Elevated training volumes, frequent travel, logistical complexity, and sustained performance pressure can impose significant physiological and psychological demands, at times resulting in compromised well-being or less favorable health outcomes compared with those observed in recreational or non-elite sport participants [21–25]. Additional challenges, including sleep disruption and maladaptive nutritional behaviors, have also been documented in elite para-sport contexts [8, 26]. Furthermore, perceived inconsistencies or inaccuracies in functional classification systems may contribute to frustration, diminished satisfaction, and, in some cases, burnout or disengagement from sport participation [27, 28]. Beyond sport-specific factors, broader structural and societal barriers, including stigma, discrimination, and limited access to psychological, social, or material support—may further exacerbate vulnerability and negatively influence health and functioning [29, 30].

Despite increasing scholarly interest in sporting participation as a determinant of health among individuals with disabilities, significant gaps remain within the existing literature. Many investigations are constrained by small or highly specialized samples, focus on a single sport or disability category, or lack appropriate comparison groups. Such methodological limitations restrict the generalizability of findings and impede a nuanced understanding of the complex interplay between individual, environmental, and sport-related determinants. Notably, relatively few studies have systematically compared young people with disabilities engaged in Paralympic sport with non-athlete peers, limiting the ability to isolate the specific contribution of competitive sport participation to physical and mental health outcomes [19].

Considering these limitations, the present study was conceived with two primary objectives. First, it aimed to assess levels of physical and mental health in a large and heterogeneous cohort of young people with disabilities, differentiating between individuals engaged in Paralympic sport and those not participating in competitive sport. Second, it sought to examine the independent contributions of demographic (age, gender, nationality), environmental (urban versus rural residential context), sport-related (type of sport), and clinical (type and origin of disability) factors to variability in physical and mental health-related outcomes. By addressing these aims, the study sought to advance a more comprehensive and generalizable understanding of the determinants of physical and mental health in youth with disabilities. Elucidating the role of Paralympic sport participation within this multidimensional framework may inform the design of evidence-based policies, inclusive health-promotion initiatives, and targeted psychoeducational interventions tailored to the diverse needs, contexts, and lived experiences of young people with disabilities, both within and beyond elite sporting environments.

Materials and methods

The research followed a cross-sectional comparative framework, utilizing self-report questionnaires as the primary data collection tool. The reporting of the main findings adhered to the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE) guidelines [31]. Participants in the case group included young para-athletes who competed in the European Para Youth Games (EPYG), held in Liguria, Italy, between October 9 and 15, 2017. This international multisport event is organized by the International Paralympic Committee (IPC) and targets athletes with disabilities aged between 12 and 23. EPYG is hosted by a different European country every two to three years.

Approximately one month prior to the event, the study was promoted via the official EPYG website and through emails sent to the relevant para-sport federations. These communications—dispatched by the Regional Paralympic Committee of Liguria, which also served as the host organizer for the 2017 edition—contained a link to an online form created using Google Forms. The form included an introductory note, a consent section where participants could indicate their willingness to take part, the main set of questionnaires, and a supplementary help section.

An identical data collection protocol was employed for a control group composed of non-athlete youths with disabilities, matched for age. The survey link was distributed via email to rehabilitation centers across European countries that had delegations attending the EPYG. To maximize participation from both para-athletes and non-sporting individuals with disabilities, the online form remained accessible through July 2019.

The study was approved by the local ethics committee, and its protocol was previously published in a peer-reviewed international journal [32]. It was also prospectively registered with the International Standard Randomized Controlled Trial Number (ISRCTN) registry under the identifier ISRCTN14389453. Full details of the survey are available in [19].

Psychometric tool

The assessment of perceived health status was conducted using the Short Form-12 Health Survey (SF-12), a validated and widely used instrument for measuring health-related quality of life in both general population studies and clinical settings [33]. The SF-12 is a shortened version of the SF-36 and, through 12 items, yields two standardized summary indices: the Physical Component Summary (PCS-12) and the Mental Component Summary (MCS-12), representing perceived physical and mental health, respectively.

The items of the SF-12 sample key aspects of physical and mental health across multiple domains, including physical functioning, role limitations, pain, general health perceptions, vitality, social functioning, and emotional and mental health dimensions. In accordance with standard scoring procedures, these domains are combined to derive the two composite summary scores (PCS-12 and MCS-12), which were used for all analyses in the present study.

Each item is rated on a Likert-type scale, and responses are weighted and aggregated using a standardized scoring algorithm to ensure comparability across studies and populations. The questionnaire can be either self-administered or administered as an interview, allowing its use also in participants with sensory impairments (e.g., visual impairment) or intellectual disabilities when support is required to ensure comprehension (see study protocol [32]). The calculation of PCS-12 and MCS-12 scores was performed using an oblique rotation approach in the factor-analytic scoring procedure, which accounts for the natural correlation between physical and mental health components and provides a realistic representation of perceived health status [34].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the demographic, clinical, and sport-related characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed data and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for skewed distributions. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

To assess differences in PCS and MCS scores across binary variables, including sex, disability origin, residential context, and questionnaire administration mode, independent-samples t-tests were applied after checking normality assumptions using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons across variables with more than two categories, including disability type, geographical area, and sport participation, were performed using one-way analyses of variance. When appropriate, Games–Howell post-hoc tests were used for pairwise comparisons. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables to determine group differences in proportions.

Internal consistency of the SF-12 was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Given the multidimensional structure of the instrument, reliability was evaluated separately for the PCS and the MCS. Items were recoded when necessary to ensure consistent directionality prior to analysis.

To explore predictors of physical and mental health, two separate multivariable linear regression models were constructed, with the Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores of the SF-12 questionnaire as dependent variables. Independent variables included age, sex, nationality (with Western Europe as the reference category), sport participation, residential context (urban versus rural), disability type (with musculoskeletal disability as the reference category), disability origin (congenital versus acquired), and questionnaire administration mode (self-administered versus assisted).

Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), with values below 10 considered indicative of the absence of problematic multicollinearity (Supplementary Table 1). The assumptions of linear regression were also evaluated. Normality of residuals was assessed through visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots and supported by skewness (PCS: −0.03; MCS: −0.21) and kurtosis (PCS: −0.45; MCS: −0.50) indices. Homoscedasticity was examined using residuals-versus-fitted plots, which showed no substantial funnel-shaped patterns, indicating no major violations of homoscedasticity (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Model fit was assessed using R-squared (R²) and adjusted R-squared values. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen’s f², which was calculated from R² using the formula f² = R² / (1 − R²) and interpreted according to conventional thresholds as small (0.02), medium (0.15), and large (0.35). Regression coefficients (β), standard errors (SE), and p-values were reported for each predictor.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted for the multivariable linear regression models using an F-test approach (fixed model, R² deviation from zero). Effect sizes (f²) were derived from the observed coefficients of determination (R²), and the required sample size to achieve 80% statistical power was estimated.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using the open-source Jamovi software (Version 2.6).

Results

A total of 1,226 questionnaires were received for analysis. However, only 1,134 were deemed suitable for inclusion in the study, as the remaining 92 forms contained errors in completion or incomplete/unclear responses. For the entire recruited population, the PCS showed a mean value of 44.37 with a standard deviation of 10.07. The median value was 44.72, with an interquartile range (IQR) between 36.89 and 52.19. Regarding the MCS, in the same population, the mean score was 44.24 with a standard deviation of 12.03. The median was 46.24, with an interquartile range between 34.54 and 54.22. The internal consistency of the SF-12 was evaluated separately for the PCS and the MCS. Cronbach’s alpha indicated modest internal consistency for PCS (α = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.57–0.64) and good internal consistency for MCS (α = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.80–0.83).

A post hoc power analysis was conducted for the multivariable linear regression models using an F-test (fixed model, R² deviation from zero) with α = 0.05. For the PCS model, the observed effect size was moderate (R² = 0.172; f² = 0.208), and the achieved statistical power exceeded 99.9% with the available sample size (n = 1,134). Similarly, for the MCS model, the observed effect size was large (R² = 0.348; f² = 0.534), and the achieved statistical power also exceeded 99.9%. These findings indicate that the study was more than adequately powered to detect the observed associations.

Physical health score

In the descriptive analysis, no significant differences in PCS scores were found according to sex (43.74 ± 10.14 vs. 44.85 ± 10.01; t = -1.84, p = 0.066). Participants with congenital disabilities presented significantly higher scores than those with acquired disabilities (44.86 ± 10.01 vs. 43.27 ± 10.16; t = 2.46, p = 0.015). Participants living in rural areas also reported significantly higher scores than those living in urban environments (44.86 ± 10.19 vs. 43.38 ± 9.78; t = 2.33, p = 0.018). No significant differences were found between participants in the self-administered and assisted questionnaire groups (44.03 ± 10.07 vs. 45.21 ± 10.06; t = -1.79, p = 0.074).

Individuals with intellectual disabilities recorded the highest scores (48.4 ± 8.94), followed by those with musculoskeletal disabilities (45.3 ± 10.41), sensory impairments (44.2 ± 10.66), and neurological conditions (43.2 ± 9.69) (p < 0.001). In the Games-Howell post-hoc test, the comparison between individuals with intellectual and neurological disabilities showed a statistically significant mean difference of 5.3 (p < 0.001), while differences between the intellectual and sensory groups (4.3, p = 0.004), and between the intellectual and musculoskeletal groups (3.2, p = 0.016) were also significant, as well as those between the musculoskeletal and neurological groups (mean difference = 2.1, p = 0.016). Additional pairwise comparisons revealed no statistically significant differences between the musculoskeletal and sensory groups (mean difference = 1.1, p = 0.737), or between the sensory and neurological groups (mean difference = 1.0, p = 0.708).

From a geographical perspective, participants from Northern Europe exhibited the highest scores (45.8 ± 9.93), followed by those from Southern Europe (44.6 ± 10.56), Western Europe (43.3 ± 9.54), and Central-Eastern Europe (39.6 ± 7.91) (p < 0.001). In the Games-Howell post-hoc analysis, individuals from Central-Eastern Europe reported significantly lower scores compared to those from Western (mean difference = -3.7, p = 0.006), Southern (-5.0, p < 0.001), and Northern Europe (-6.2, p < 0.001). Additionally, participants from Northern Europe scored significantly higher than those from Western Europe (mean difference = 2.5, p = 0.009), while the difference between Southern and Western Europe did not reach statistical significance (mean difference = 1.3, p = 0.335). Similarly, no significant difference was found between Southern and Northern Europe (mean difference = 1.2, p = 0.380).

Regarding sports participation, para-athletes engaged in team sports achieved the highest scores (47.6 ± 9.00), followed by those practicing individual sports (46.5 ± 8.27), while non-athletes recorded the lowest scores (39.3 ± 11.35) (p < 0.001). In the Games-Howell post-hoc analysis, participants involved in individual sports demonstrated significantly higher scores compared to non-athletes (mean difference = 7.3, p < 0.001), while the difference between individual and team sport participants was not statistically significant (mean difference = -1.1, p = 0.324). The multivariate analysis identified independent predictors of the PCS, controlling for the simultaneous influence of the included variables (Fig. 2). The model explained 17.2% of the variance in scores (R² = 0.172; f² = 0.208), corresponding to a medium effect size at the model level.

Age showed a negative association with PCS, although this relationship did not reach statistical significance (β = −0.141; p = 0.061). Gender was not a significant predictor (β = 0.279; p = 0.623). Compared to participants from Western Europe, those from Southern (β = 1.693; p = 0.020) and Northern Europe (β = 2.371; p = 0.001) reported higher PCS scores, while no significant difference was observed for participants from Central-Eastern Europe (β = −1.996; p = 0.114).

Sports participation had a strong impact: non-athletes reported significantly lower PCS scores compared to individuals practicing individual sports (β = −7.306; p < 0.001). However, the difference between team and individual sports did not reach statistical significance (β = 1.427; p = 0.089). Participants living in rural areas reported significantly higher PCS scores than those living in urban areas (β = 1.429; p = 0.016).

Regarding disability type, neurological disabilities were associated with lower PCS scores compared to musculoskeletal disabilities (β = −2.324; p < 0.001), while intellectual disabilities were associated with significantly higher scores (β = 3.530; p = 0.003). Sensory disabilities were not associated with significant differences in PCS compared to musculoskeletal disabilities (β = −0.548; p = 0.611). No significant differences emerged for the origin of disability (β = −0.558; p = 0.352).

Mental health score

In the descriptive analysis, no significant differences in MCS scores were found according to sex (43.53 ± 12.25 vs. 44.78 ± 11.85; t = -1.74, p = 0.082). Participants with congenital disabilities presented significantly higher scores than those with acquired disabilities (45.45 ± 11.53 vs. 41.54 ± 12.70; t = 5.12, p < 0.001). No significant differences were found between participants living in urban and rural areas (44.81 ± 12.70 vs. 43.96 ± 11.68; t = 1.12, p = 0.263). No significant differences were found between participants in the self-administered and assisted questionnaire groups (43.85 ± 12.38 vs. 45.21 ± 11.08; t = -1.72, p = 0.086).

Participants engaged in individual sports reported a mean score of 48.9 ± 9.70, while those involved in team sports showed a similar value of 48.8 ± 8.60. In contrast, non-athletes recorded a significantly lower mean score of 34.3 ± 10.87 (p < 0.001). In the Games-Howell post-hoc analysis, non-athletes had significantly lower scores compared to individuals practicing individual sports (mean difference = -14.6, p < 0.001) and those participating in team sports (mean difference = -14.6, p < 0.001). Conversely, no significant difference emerged between participants engaged in individual and team sports (mean difference = 0.03, p = 0.999).

At the geographical level, the highest scores were observed among participants from Southern Europe (44.9 ± 11.8) and Northern Europe (44.7 ± 12.3), whereas the lowest values were found in participants from Central-Eastern Europe (41.4 ± 11.5) (p = 0.079). No significant pairwise comparisons were identified.

Considering disability type, participants with intellectual disabilities showed the highest scores (47.2 ± 10.8), followed by those with neurological disabilities (44.4 ± 11.7), musculoskeletal disabilities (44.0 ± 12.7), and sensory impairments (42.3 ± 12.2) (p = 0.009). In the Games-Howell post-hoc analysis, individuals with intellectual disabilities scored significantly higher than those with sensory disabilities (mean difference = 5.0, p = 0.005). Differences between other pairs did not reach statistical significance: musculoskeletal vs. sensory (mean difference = 1.7, p = 0.495), neurological vs. sensory (2.1, p = 0.234), musculoskeletal vs. intellectual (-3.2, p = 0.061), and neurological vs. intellectual (-2.9, p = 0.081). The multivariate analysis assessed the independent effect of each predictive factor on mental well-being while controlling for interactions between variables (Fig. 4). The model explained 34.8% of the variance in scores (R² = 0.348; f² = 0.534), corresponding to a large effect size at the model level.

Forest plot showing the predictors of Mental Component Summary (MCS) scores among the study participants

Age was confirmed as a negative predictor, with older participants reporting lower MCS scores (β = −0.1788; p = 0.025). Gender was not a significant predictor (β = −0.2317; p = 0.700).

With regard to geographical origin, only participants from Southern Europe reported significantly higher MCS scores compared to those from Western Europe (β = 1.9009; p = 0.014), while no significant differences were observed for participants from Northern (β = 1.0895; p = 0.166) or Central-Eastern Europe (β = 1.1610; p = 0.385).

Non-athletes reported significantly lower MCS scores compared to individuals participating in individual sports (β = −14.5421; p < 0.001), indicating a large effect size. However, no significant differences emerged between participants engaged in team sports and those practicing individual sports (β = 0.1101; p = 0.901).

Environmental context showed a marginal association, with slightly lower MCS scores observed among residents of rural areas compared to urban residents (β = −1.1510; p = 0.066).

Regarding disability type, participants with intellectual disabilities reported significantly higher MCS scores than those with musculoskeletal disabilities (β = 2.6408; p = 0.037), while no significant differences were found for participants with sensory (β = −2.1010; p = 0.066) or neurological disabilities (β = 0.0371; p = 0.957).

The origin of disability emerged as an important predictor, with participants with acquired disabilities reporting significantly lower MCS scores than those with congenital disabilities (β = −2.7919; p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study examined perceived physical and mental health in youth with disabilities, with the aim of identifying key factors associated with variations in perceived physical and mental health outcomes. The results indicate that participation in Paralympic sport represents the strongest and most consistent predictor of both perceived physical health (PCS) and perceived mental health (MCS), with para-athletes reporting higher PCS and MCS scores compared with non-athlete peers. The magnitude of these differences, particularly for mental health, suggests that participation in structured sport may have meaningful implications for the psychological and physical well-being of youth with disabilities.

The present findings extend previous evidence by directly comparing para-athletes and non-athletes using the same measurement framework and by examining multiple demographic, clinical, environmental, and sport-related predictors within a single multivariate model.

Recent systematic reviews have reported similar patterns when examining health-related and psychosocial outcomes in athletes with disabilities using validated, multidimensional assessment tools [7, 14]. In particular, Mira et al. [7] focused on well-being, resilience, and social support among para-athletes, highlighting several studies consistent with the present results. For example, Puce et al. [22] reported that youth athletes engaged in competitive swimming showed significantly higher perceived physical and mental health scores, as assessed by the SF-12, compared with age-matched non-athletes with disabilities.

Similarly, Mudjianto et al. [9] evaluated quality of life among Indonesian para-athletes using the World Health Organization Quality of Life–Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF), reporting high scores across physical, psychological, social, and environmental domains, although the study did not include a non-athlete control group. Mira [35] also reported high levels of life satisfaction and emotional well-being among Portuguese para-athletes who competed at the Tokyo 2020 Paralympic Games, likewise without comparison with non-athletic peers. Kokun et al. [36] found that Ukrainian para-athletes exhibited higher psychological well-being and resilience than both able-bodied students and students with disabilities not engaged in sport. Consistent with these findings, Zelenka et al. [37] reported that wheelchair rugby players with tetraplegia had higher quality-of-life scores than non-sporting individuals with comparable impairments across all WHOQOL-BREF domains, although only the difference in the physical health domain reached statistical significance.

When contextualized against normative data, para-athletes in the present sample reported lower perceived physical health scores than those typically observed in able-bodied populations, while perceived mental health scores were closer to normative values. Normative PCS and MCS values in the general population are approximately 54 and 51, respectively [34], and values for able-bodied athletes typically range between 50 and 54 for both components [38]. In contrast, para-athletes exhibited a more pronounced reduction in PCS scores, whereas MCS values were comparatively preserved. This pattern suggests that disability-related impairments continue to constrain perceived physical health, as sport participation cannot modify the underlying pathological condition or eliminate the functional limitations associated with disability. At the same time, sustained sport participation may partially buffer their impact on perceived mental health.

With respect to sport-related characteristics, participation in team sports was associated with higher perceived physical health scores compared with individual disciplines. However, this association should be interpreted with caution. While group dynamics and social interaction are often proposed as explanatory mechanisms, such processes are conceptually more closely related to perceived mental health than to perceived physical health. Indeed, multivariate analyses indicated that the principal contribution derives from sport participation itself, rather than from the specific modality practiced, consistent with previous observations [39, 40]. From an applied perspective, this suggests that the potential benefit may lie primarily in regular engagement in structured sport per se, rather than in a specific competitive format.

Consistent with this interpretation, no significant differences in perceived mental health were observed between individual and team sports. Perceived mental health outcomes appeared primarily associated with participation in sport as such, supporting theoretical frameworks such as the Self-Determination Theory, which emphasize competence, autonomy, and relatedness within competitive and socially engaging environments [41].

Regarding disability type, participants with neurological impairments reported the lowest perceived physical health scores, reflecting functional limitations associated with chronic pain, muscle weakness, coordination deficits, and hypertonia [42, 43]. In contrast, participants with intellectual disabilities exhibited relatively higher perceived physical health scores. These findings should be interpreted as indicators of perceived health within highly structured competitive contexts, rather than as evidence of superior physical functioning. As previously reported [44], participation of athletes with intellectual disabilities in Paralympic sport is typically limited to specific disciplines and to individuals with defined adaptive profiles, which may influence engagement and health perceptions.

The origin of disability also showed differential associations with perceived mental health outcomes. Participants with acquired disabilities reported lower MCS scores, likely reflecting the psychological burden associated with trauma exposure and identity restructuring following disability onset. By contrast, an initial advantage observed among participants with congenital disabilities in perceived physical health did not persist after adjustment for covariates.

Interpretation of the congenital versus acquired distinction should take into account that intellectual disability—by definition a neurodevelopmental condition—was included exclusively in the congenital group. Consequently, this category encompasses conditions characterized by lifelong cognitive and adaptive features that may influence perceived physical and mental health independently of disability onset, a factor that must be considered when interpreting group-level differences.

These findings partially diverge from previous studies. Campbell [16], for example, reported higher psychological well-being among para-athletes with acquired disabilities, consistent with Wright’s [21] hypothesis that individuals born with a disability may have fewer opportunities to develop autonomy-related experiences during childhood. An alternative interpretative framework is provided by the theory of “growth through adversity” [45, 46], which posits that exposure to stressful life events may foster psychological growth while coexisting with unresolved trauma. Indeed, other studies have reported lower self-efficacy, reduced motivation for sport participation, and poorer psychological health in individuals who recently acquired a disability [47–49], supporting the complexity of adjustment processes [50].

Geographical differences further highlight the contextual nature of perceived physical and mental health. Participants from Northern Europe reported higher perceived physical health scores, likely reflecting broader environmental contexts that facilitate physical activity and functional participation, as suggested by previous studies [51–54]. Conversely, higher perceived mental health scores observed in Southern Europe may reflect stronger social networks and interpersonal support [51].

Finally, age emerged as a predictor of perceived mental health, with older participants reporting lower MCS scores, consistent with previous findings highlighting increasing psychosocial demands and goal-related stress during late adolescence and young adulthood [55, 56]. This may be relevant from a clinical and educational perspective, as older adolescents and young adults with disabilities may require more specific psychological and social support during this transitional phase.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference regarding the association between sport participation and perceived physical and mental health outcomes. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the directionality of these relationships.

Second, the control group was recruited from rehabilitation centers, which may limit the representativeness of the broader population of youth with disabilities. At the same time, differences in motivation, functional capacity, and engagement in structured physical activity represent intrinsic characteristics of Paralympic sport participation, rather than unintended sources of bias.

Third, the data were collected starting in 2017, and this aspect should be considered when interpreting the results. Caution is therefore required when generalizing the findings to current contexts.

Finally, some sport disciplines and disability categories were underrepresented, which may limit generalizability across the full spectrum of Paralympic athletes and disability profiles. In addition, the present findings are specific to athletes engaged in organized, competitive para-sports regulated by the IPC. Therefore, results should not be generalized to individuals with disabilities participating in recreational, school-based, or non-competitive adapted sport contexts, which may be characterized by different motivational, functional, and psychosocial profiles and warrant dedicated investigation.

Conclusion

Participation in Paralympic sport is strongly associated with higher levels of perceived physical and mental health in youth with disabilities. In this large, multinational sample, para-athletes consistently reported higher PCS and MCS scores compared with non-athlete peers. These differences remained significant after adjustment for demographic, clinical, environmental, and sport-related variables, supporting the independent association between competitive sport participation and perceived physical and mental health.

Although sport participation emerged as the strongest predictor, additional contextual and individual factors also contributed to variability in health perceptions. Overall, these findings highlight the potential value of structured and competitive adapted sport contexts, such as Paralympic sport, in supporting perceived physical and mental health among adolescents and young adults with disabilities.
