Beyond the Adversarial Rivalry: A Developmental Rights‐Based Model for Minor‐on‐Minor Crime, Part 2
Authors: Tali Gal, Ruthy Lowenstein Lazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhen children harm children, the conventional victim–perpetrator framework is ill‐equipped to address the developmental, relational, and institutional complexities involved. While juvenile justice scholarship increasingly emphasizes rehabilitation, and victims' rights literature has advanced child‐sensitive protections, minor‐on‐minor offending remains underexplored across legal and social science disciplines. Part 1 of this two‐parts project developed a four‐pillar theoretical framework integrating therapeutic justice, developmental principles, relational procedural justice, and children's rights norms. The present Article considers the implementation of the proposed model through two paradigmatic case studies, concerning victim impact statements and the handling of sensitive disclosures. Through these scenarios, the Article addresses possible critiques against the model and illustrates how it can protect minor victims' rights, foster rehabilitation for juvenile defendants, and mitigate secondary trauma for both parties. Building on the theoretical model and case‐study analysis, we offer some guiding principles for promoting a developmentally informed and rights respecting juvenile justice system.

Introduction

When both victim and defendant are minors, conventional frameworks governing defendants' constitutional protections and victims' rights are inadequate to address the unique vulnerabilities inherent to childhood and adolescence and the distinct needs and characteristics of minors. Therefore, a distinct legal regime should apply in cases of minor‐against‐minor offenses, one that is better suited to the rights and interests of both parties.

In Part 1 of our work, we examined how the unique nature of the juvenile justice system and the distinct needs of minor victims and defendants interact and often amplify each other, creating a cascade of challenges that neither the juvenile justice system nor victims' rights mechanisms were originally designed to address. The analysis revealed four interacting levels of tension and complexity that create substantial obstacles for young people in the criminal justice system, each compounding the difficulties inherent in navigating legal processes.

The first layer stems from the adversarial legal system's inherent limitations in addressing the emotional and other non‐legal needs of all parties—defendants and victims alike, regardless of age, highlighting the system's inability to be “emotionally intelligent” and responsive to participants' psychological well‐being (King et al. 2014; Sherman 2003). When the litigants are minors and the conflict results from a minor‐against‐minor offense, this shortcoming becomes critical, due to the centrality of non‐legal consideration and stakeholders.

The second layer emerges from criminal law's inherent tension between victims' and defendants' rights. Within the conventional public prosecution model, where the State prosecutes defendants in service of the broader public interest, victims' rights advocates have criticized the relegation of victims to “third parties” who merely observe proceedings from the periphery (Sebba 1996). The recognition of victims' rights in recent decades has intensified this tension between competing claims. Even where victims' rights have achieved formal legal recognition, they frequently remain unenforced due to institutional concerns about infringing defendants' constitutional protections (Zappalà 2010). Minor‐against‐minor cases present an impossible dilemma in this dichotomous approach, pitting the rights of one young person against those of the other. At the same time, closer attention to empirically grounded accounts of survivors’ needs suggests that their involvement does not necessarily entail more punitive responses, a dynamic that may be particularly salient where both parties are minors (Doak and O’mahony 2006; Krinsky and Komar 2021).

The third layer of complexity arises from the distinctive legal status accorded to minor defendants accused of delinquent behavior. These young people are typically adjudicated in separate juvenile courts and enjoy specialized rights and protections under both domestic juvenile justice legislation and the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC 1989). Juvenile justice systems worldwide prioritize rehabilitation over punishment, typically imposing lighter sentences even for grave offenses (Scott and Steinberg 2008). This rehabilitative philosophy, coupled with enhanced due process protections for minors, further exacerbates the defendant‐victim tension and frequently subordinates victims' interests, because advocates for both approaches express concerns that victim participation may result in more punitive outcomes (Henning 2009). Despite these concerns, however, empirical victimology research demonstrates that harsh punishment is not necessarily central to victims' needs and preferences, irrespective of age (Booth 2004).

The fourth layer arises from the minor status of the victim. Growing awareness of justice system‐induced trauma highlights the hardships imposed on child victims who are required to testify in court (Field and Katz 2022). Such knowledge has led professionals working with at‐risk or victimized children to adopt an overprotective stance (Finkelhor et al. 2005). While well‐intentioned, this instinct to shield children from further harm often results in their exclusion from legal proceedings, despite research showing that young victims often desire to have their voices heard and participate in decisions affecting them (Gillon 2019). We posit that, when the victim is a minor, a simple all‐protective attitude leading to their exclusion from the criminal justice process is inadequate. Rather, a more nuanced attitude is required, one that relies on empirical knowledge about the typical needs and preferences of victimized children at various ages, combined with concrete familiarity with the specific child's needs, capabilities, and preferences.

Part 1 of this project further introduced a multidimensional model shaped by four interrelated theoretical and practical frameworks: Therapeutic justice, relational procedural justice, child development, and needs‐based rights of children and youth. This framework may prove particularly valuable in cases involving minor‐on‐minor offenses, where traditional legal approaches often fail to adequately address the distinct characteristics of the parties involved and the complex tensions at play. Therapeutic Justice highlights the value of shaping legal processes so that they enhance the emotional well‐being of the parties involved, thereby reducing secondary victimization for minor victims and promoting more constructive coping strategies for minor defendants without jeopardizing due process rights. The Child Development pillar emphasizes the dynamic developmental state of minors and recognizes that punitive interventions or excessive exclusion can precipitate lasting harm and derail normative growth. Furthermore, a challenging yet supportive legal process may provide a developmental opportunity for emotional and moral growth among both minor defendants and victims. Relational Procedural Justice stresses that respectful treatment, transparency, and opportunities to speak and be heard are key components of perceived fairness and a sense of justice by the parties. Incorporating minors' voices, particularly when both sides are underage, can mitigate the alienation that often arises in adversarial proceedings. The fourth pillar of Children's Rights situates these adjustments in a global legal framework reflected in the CRC, confirming that protection from harm, participation in decision‐making, and provision of rehabilitative services are non‐negotiable entitlements of children and youth, whether one has experienced harm or caused it.

Together, this four‐pillar model offers an overarching framework for minor‐against‐minor criminal cases. As we concluded in Part 1, decision‐makers committed to this framework should ask these guiding questions before deciding in specific cases:What are the likely implications of each possible course of action for the psychological well‐being of both the child harmed and the child responsible for the harm?How does each option align with the developmental needs, capacities, and interests of both children?To what extent does each option respect—or limit—the fundamental rights of both parties, including their rights to protection, participation, developmentally appropriate treatment, and consideration of their best interests?How does each option uphold procedural fairness and relational integrity for both parties, including the provision of clear information, opportunities for input, and respectful, trustworthy engagement by decision‐makers?

These questions should be considered both in relation to the decision‐making process and to its outcomes.

To illustrate how this framework engages competing interests, this Article applies the integrated four‐pillar model to two case studies drawn from common forms of juvenile‐on‐juvenile offending. These cases are both representative and analytically salient in that they arise at the trial stage, where legal determinations are formalized and competing claims are most directly confronted, and capture situations in which prevailing perceptions tend to position the rights and needs of one child as standing in direct opposition to those of the other. The first case study examines the submission of victim impact statements by minor victims. The second explores the provision of information about the juvenile defendant or case progression to the minor victim. Following the analysis of these case studies, we discuss possible critiques against the model and its limitations, and conclude with several recommendations for policy and practice.

First Case Study: The Use of Victim Impact Statements

Victim Impact Statements (VIS) play a critical role within the criminal justice system, allowing victims to articulate the emotional, psychological, and financial repercussions of the crimes committed against them (Gal and Lowenstein Lazar 2023; Kragting et al. 2025). This mechanism serves the dual purpose of facilitating a cathartic outlet for victims while informing the judiciary about the broader implications of the crime (Wemmers 2010).

The significance of VIS is particularly salient for minor victims, especially those who are mature enough for active participation. For children and youth, VIS represents a rare opportunity for authentic voice, self‐expression, and agency in proceedings otherwise dominated by adult intermediaries. These statements function as a critical mechanism through which young victims can experience meaningful acknowledgment of their harm, inclusion in the adjudicative process, and consideration of their perspective and narrative (Field and Katz 2022). By facilitating direct participation, VISs enable minors to communicate in an unmediated voice—a significant departure from the typical filtering of their experiences through multiple adult stakeholders (Matthews 2021; Hobbs and Goodman 2018). At the same time, submitting VISs potentially exposes the minor victim to cross‐examination, which is regarded as the most stressful element in the criminal process for them (Matthews 2021). Reopening their story of victimization and repeating its details in court when being cross‐examined about their VIS may exacerbate secondary victimization, and this is likely to be particularly stressful in sexual and other serious crimes (Hoobs & Goodman, 2018).

The use of VIS is perceived as potentially implicating the rights and interests of juvenile defendants. The inclusion of victims' voices in sentencing proceedings raises concerns about possible prejudicial effects on judicial decision‐making. Victim testimonies presented through a VIS may influence sentencing outcomes, potentially contributing to harsher penalties or bias, particularly in cases involving asymmetries such as when the defendant belongs to a minority group and the victim does not (Booth 2004). Such concerns may sit in tension with the rehabilitative orientation of the juvenile justice system and its emphasis on reintegration. Additionally, the use of VIS in juvenile courts may not adequately account for the developmental stage of some young defendants, who may lack the emotional maturity to fully comprehend the harm caused or to express remorse in ways that are perceived as credible when confronted with such statements (Henning 2009).

Both concerns—that of secondary victimization for minor victims and that of overly punitive responses for minor defendants—may be amplified by the age and vulnerability of the children involved, as well as by the severity of the offense. At the same time, when child victims do not testify, whether due to concerns about their vulnerability or the protection of defendants' rights, the courts may lack access to the victim's perspective. In such cases, not only may young victims' participatory interests be limited, but young defendants may also lose an opportunity to understand the impact of their actions and to engage in processes of accountability and potential rehabilitation.

Hypothesized correlations between VIS and enhanced punitive outcomes for defendants broadly (Bandes 1996; Tobolowsky 1999), and particularly for juvenile offenders (Henning 2009), while theoretically conceivable, remain empirically unsubstantiated (Erez and Laster 1999; Kunst et al. 2021). Furthermore, apprehensions that VIS implementation may compromise juvenile defendants' constitutional right to equal protection under the law fundamentally misunderstand the inherently differentiated nature of juvenile adjudication. The juvenile justice paradigm deliberately employs individualized sentencing frameworks and diverse dispositional options precisely to optimize alignment with each minor defendant's specific rehabilitative requirements (Breen and Mills 2015; Monahan et al. 2015). Moreover, heterogeneity in victimization experiences and their articulation already constitutes a recognized variable within sentencing calculus across all jurisdictional forums (Nadler and Rose 2003).

From young victims' perspective, although being cross‐examined about their submitted VIS might add stress and elevate secondary victimization, research shows that defense attorneys seldom use the opportunity to conduct cross‐examination at the sentencing stage, particularly when the witness is a minor (Wallace 1996). Moreover, when appropriately prepared and supported, and when given a choice regarding whether and how to submit a VIS, young victims may derive therapeutic benefit from recounting their experiences and receiving acknowledgment from the court (Piamenta and Gal 2025).

We argue that the presentation of VIS in the dispositional stage in cases involving minor victims requires a fundamentally different approach from that applied in adult courts. Transposing VIS practices from the adult system without meaningful adaptation to the juvenile context risks undermining both the rehabilitative orientation of the juvenile justice system and the particular vulnerabilities of young victims. These adaptations must account for the developmental stage of both parties, the nature of the offense, and the availability of familial and social support, among other considerations.

The four‐pillars model offers a reconceptualization of this stage. Therapeutic justice (TJ) principles support reconfiguring dispositional hearings, including the use of VIS, in ways that promote the well‐being of both minors and draw on evidence‐based practices tailored for their needs. At a minimum, TJ cautions against procedures likely to induce unnecessary emotional stress and harm. In this context, a TJ‐informed approach would seek modes of VIS delivery that are least harmful and potentially beneficial for child victims, while also encouraging responses from the minor defendant that are constructive for both parties. Beyond the specific format adopted in any given case, TJ‐informed practices may include active listening, empathetic communication, thorough preparation of both parties, and structured opportunities for dialogue. Importantly, these practices should be applied to both minors, so that the use of VIS contributes not only to the well‐being of the child harmed but also to the development and accountability of the child responsible for the harm.

These elements are central to enhancing the parties' perceptions of fairness and procedural justice. Interpersonal dynamics within the legal process carry particular significance for adolescents; when they are treated with respect by authority figures who acknowledge their voices and needs, they are more likely to view the process as legitimate. Consistent with procedural justice theory, decisions regarding the form and implementation of VIS should ensure that both parties are treated with respect (dignity), provided with clear and developmentally appropriate information (understanding), given meaningful opportunities to express their views (voice), and engaged through the exercise of neutral and trustworthy discretion (Tyler 1990).

From a child development perspective, the disposition hearing should be adjusted according to the developmental stage and maturity of the minors involved. Some scholarship suggests that presenting a VIS in court before a juvenile defendant has received counseling or other rehabilitative services may be less conducive to fostering empathy and remorse than post‐disposition, treatment‐linked interventions (Henning 2009). With regards to victims, VISs delivered in the highly charged and formal environment of the courtroom are also unlikely to foster the type of emotional healing and restoration and a sense of justice that victims seek. Thus, this stage should be carefully designed and attuned to the specific needs and capabilities of the parties involved. At the same time, it should be regarded as a developmental opportunity in which both minors are challenged to step out of their “comfort zones”, and eventually learn from the experience and demonstrate moral and emotional growth (Buss 2021).

A relational children's rights lens contributed an additional layer, one which highlights the importance of enabling both minors to make choices regarding the format of the VIS delivery, to the extent possible. Adopting a relational children's rights lens, a child‐inclusive presentation of a VIS requires the involvement of supportive others, preferably drawn from the child's natural environment, or, in their absence, suitably trained and authorized personnel, to provide necessary scaffolding.

Integrating the TJ perspective, the procedural justice principles, and the developmental and relational children's rights lens, we envisage a range of procedural options. The specific format will be decided after obtaining the preferences of both parties, considering their age and developmental stages, weighing the potential risks and benefits for their well‐being, and ensuring that their rights are being addressed. Some minors will want to be directly heard and included in the disposition hearing, while others will prefer their parents or other supporters acting as buffers and speaking on their behalf. Some will want to speak in front of the defendant, seeing it as an opportunity for a face‐to‐face dialogue, while others will prefer being removed from the defendant using CCTV. Others still might wish to speak in the privacy of the judge's chambers, and this can be conducted with the presence of the defense attorney or, again, using technology to protect the defendant's due process rights. From the victims' perspective, having these various options from which to choose their level and form of participation in the disposition is inherently therapeutic (Gal 2011), and elevates the perceived sense of fairness and justice. Alternatives to in‐court delivery of VIS may be beneficial not only from the victims' perspective, but also for the benefit and rehabilitation of the young defendants, provided that their rights and interests are preserved. Their parents and other support persons should be invited and included. Written or video‐recorded statements can be submitted to the court and subsequently incorporated into the defendant's rehabilitative interventions that can mediate and contextualize them. Such interventions can help translate the content and significance of the VIS into developmentally appropriate terms, thereby promoting accountability, empathy, and rehabilitation (Henning 2009). In addition, therapeutic professionals working with the defendant may provide the court with a report on how the VIS was conveyed and processed, which may also be shared, where appropriate, with the victim. This may enable the victim to receive acknowledgment of the impact of their statement without necessitating direct exposure, while supporting the defendant's development of understanding and accountability.

While these ideas may seem desirable, their limitations should be acknowledged. Some of these solutions run the risk of victims' intermediaries silencing and sidelining the child victim, as emerges in other processes in which professionals take over the process to shield children from stressful interactions. From the defendants' perspective, even when a VIS is presented by someone else on behalf of the victim, such submissions pose a risk of inflammatory or biased sentencing.

Restorative platforms such as sentencing circles (Goldbach 2015), court‐operated victim‐offender mediation (Bibas and Bierschbach 2004), restorative juries (Bibas 2012), and post‐adjudication conferences (Kimbrell et al. 2023) may, in some cases, provide suitable forums, as they create structured spaces for dialogue about harm and needs, including the expression of remorse by the wrongdoer (Hansen and Umbreit 2018). However, the suitability of these practices depends on conditions including the willingness of both parties to participate, the safety of the victim, the readiness of both parties to engage constructively, the availability of support networks for either of them, and broader justice considerations, particularly in more serious offenses. Where appropriate, restorative settings have been associated with reduced anger and lower expectations of punitive responses (Strang 2002). Conducting portions of the process in a closed or confidential setting may further protect the privacy and emotional well‐being of both parties (Buckingham 2013). Although a departure from the traditional disposition hearing, this structure is still situated within the criminal process and aligns with its goals.

Second Case Study: Revealing Private Information

In cases involving minor defendants and victims, the inherent tension between protecting the privacy rights of defendants and upholding the rights of victims to access pertinent case information is magnified. The fundamental conflict arises from two competing legal and ethical imperatives. On one hand, the juvenile justice system has long prioritized the confidentiality of proceedings involving minor offenders. This approach is rooted in the belief that protecting a juvenile delinquent's privacy is crucial for their rehabilitation and successful reintegration into society. The underlying rationale is that stigmatization and negative public exposure could irreparably harm a young offender's prospects, thereby undermining the rehabilitative goals of the juvenile justice system (Buckingham 2013).

Conversely, crime victims' rights movements have emphasized the importance of victims' participation in and understanding of the justice process. This principle extends to child victims, who may require access to case‐related information to facilitate their recovery and ensure meaningful participation in the legal proceedings. The right to information is not merely procedural but is often essential for the victim's healing process and sense of justice (Beijer and Liefaard 2011; Erez 2004). Another layer of exclusion stems from concerns based on the child victims' young age. While some argue that providing detailed and updated information directly to child victims respects their autonomy and helps them feel involved and acknowledged, others contend that such disclosures could be overwhelming and potentially harmful, advocating instead for conveying information primarily through parents or guardians. For the sake of the present case study, we focus on the tension between the defendant's right to privacy and the victim's right to information, without differentiating between the delivery of information directly to the minor victims and its delivery to the victim's representative (parent, guardian ad litem, or lawyer).

While the confidential nature of proceedings is designed to protect juvenile defendants' privacy, it often results in the exclusion of victims from courtroom proceedings, which is even more prevalent for minor victims, who already experience a diminished sense of control and procedural agency. Consequently, minor victims face multiple barriers to participation: they may not receive timely information about scheduled hearings, including crucial proceedings like arraignments that carry significant symbolic weight, and plea bargains might be negotiated without their input. A particularly contentious aspect of this dilemma involves the sharing of information about the defendant's mental state or background. While such information can be crucial for the victim's psychological processing of the offense and potentially aid in reducing feelings of anger or vengeance, this approach directly conflicts with the defendant's right to privacy, especially concerning sensitive mental health information.

In navigating this complex landscape, courts have begun to advocate for careful, individualized assessment of information sharing, differentiating between essential information necessary for the victim's understanding of the case, and others, such as psychological evaluations of the young defendant, which could be detrimental if disclosed (State v. C.P 2022). However, there are no clear guidelines as to the desired balance in juvenile cases when the victim is a minor as well. Even if this individualized approach is adopted in minor‐against‐minor cases, courts will need to grapple with ensuring consistency in their decisions despite the individualized nature of each case analysis. This challenge is compounded by the need to establish clear criteria for determining which documents are truly “essential” for a young victim's understanding of the process and exercise of rights. Moreover, the courts must develop effective strategies for redacting or summarizing sensitive information in a way that protects juvenile offenders' privacy while still providing clear and useful information to victims—and simultaneously considering the specific age and developmental state of both parties.

The four‐pillars model has the potential to address the dilemmas and tensions that emerge regarding the disclosure of personal information about the defendant or the criminal process.

When applying a children's rights framework in considering sensitive information sharing, it is important to distinguish between the various rights of the juvenile defendant and those of the minor victim. A central right of young offenders is rehabilitation and reintegration into society, as stipulated in numerous international documents, such as United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Administration of Juvenile Justice (The Beijing Rules, 1985); the United Nations Guidelines for the Prevention of Juvenile Delinquency (The Riyadh Guidelines, 1990); and the CRC (1989). As long as the sensitive information is not shared with the public, the defendant's right to rehabilitation and reintegration remains intact. Also central is a right against a biased disposition, which may occur following an inflammatory appearance by the victim. Following the principles discussed in the previous case study may minimize such risk.

From the minor victim's perspective, receiving case‐related information enhances the victim's sense of control and ability to meaningfully participate in the process (Beijer and Liefaard 2011). To be sure: the victim does not have a right for a punitive outcome for the defendant; and the defendant does not have a right for complete privilege of process‐related information. If the court can ensure that the information remains at the hands of the victim and the victim's family only, and that it is not abused in ways that jeopardize the defendant's right to an unbiased process, then, from a children's rights perspective, it is possible to share such sensitive information with the victim.

From a therapeutic justice perspective, studies conducted about the therapeutic and nontherapeutic effects of the criminal process on young victims indicate that lacking knowledge about the process and the decisions made in it constitutes a significant source of stress. Conversely, receiving age‐appropriate information about the process, the status of the defendant, and the decisions made can alleviate their stress (Piamenta and Gal 2025; Cashmore 2008). For defendants, a stigmatizing criminal process can have detrimental effects resulting in disintegrative shame (Braithwaite 1989). On the other hand, if the message delivered in the court is one that denounces the crime but recognizes the wrongdoer as a respected human being, then reintegrative shaming may emerge, leading to positive results (Shi 2024). A TJ approach can promote the young victim's sense of control while minimizing stigmatizing shame and maximizing reintegrative shame on the young defendant. This can be achieved if the victim is given the sensitive information but instructed to use it only to understand the process or its outcomes and refrain from using it to stigmatize the young defendant. In fact, a better understanding of the life circumstances of the juvenile offender might reduce the victim's sense of vengeance and provide an alternative outlook on the offender's behavior, which might even lead them toward forgiveness, compassion, or understanding (Strang 2002). A TJ approach, then, highlights the importance of having someone mediate the sensitive information to the young victim so that they can comprehend it in ways beneficial for both themselves and the young perpetrator.

For the involved minors to experience procedural justice, they should both be fully informed, treated with respect, have their opinions heard, and consider the court to act in a trustworthy and unbiased manner (Wemmers 2010). In deciding on the disclosure of sensitive information, judges should therefore be as informative and transparent as possible; they should explain the rationale behind their decision to either share the information with the young victim or keep it confidential; whichever their decision is, they should pay particular attention in acknowledging the interests and rights of the party that “lost” in the dispute (Wexler 2008); and reach a decision only after listening to the arguments of both parties. Understanding why information about the process is important for the young victim might help the young defendant accept the decision to disclose it, not only because it makes sense, but also because by giving such an explanation the judge acknowledges that this is a challenging moment for the defendant. Conversely, by explaining to the young victim why a certain piece of sensitive information may not be disclosed to them, the court provides an important new perspective for the victim and at the same time expresses recognition of the importance that the court assigns to the victim's wellbeing.

In striking an appropriate balance between the defendant's right to privacy and the victim's right to information judges need to weigh various components in child development: What is the developmental stage and capacity of the child victim to understand the content of the requested information? What might be the effect of the information on them and how might they use it to promote their sense of control and process fairness? And what is their ability to comprehend the defendant's interests and maintain confidentiality? In addressing these questions, the court should also consider the long‐term potential effects of the disclosed information. For example, even if learning about the defendant's mental health background is likely to be ineffective for the minor victim during the trial, it might be helpful for them as they grow to better apprehend their victimization and reflect on it later in their lives.

Similarly, judges should consider the immediate and long‐term implications of information‐sharing and the victim's involvement in the legal process on the young defendant. If disclosing the information to the victim is likely to be stigmatizing, this can have detrimental effects on their development and might impact their chances of desisting from crime in the future (Abrah 2019; Bernburg et al. 2006). If, on the other hand, there is a way to enforce a ban on disclosing the information to the public, then perhaps sharing the information with the young victim who, for developmental and therapeutic reasons, has a strong interest in receiving it, will not harm the young delinquent's development.

Addressing Concerns and Critiques

Any proposal to reimagine the treatment of minor‐against‐minor cases must confront critiques and concerns. Since the suggested framework demonstrated through the two paradigmatic examples departs from conventional adversarial models, it raises legitimate questions which we aim to address.

A primary concern centers on the potential risk to fundamental constitutional protections afforded to young defendants. The model's emphasis on extra legal aspects as reflected in TJ and child development approach, namely, the developmental, psychological and emotional needs of the parties involved, and particularly those of young victims, may inadvertently compromise defendants' constitutional rights.

This critique overlooks the framework's internal safeguards. While TJ and child development prisms encompass extra‐legal dimensions and broaden cases' adjudication beyond the traditional legal framework, the procedural justice and children's rights pillars operate within established legal doctrine and constitutional jurisprudence. These legally grounded elements provide countervailing protections that mitigate potential constitutional dilution, creating a structurally balanced framework that preserves defendant rights while accommodating developmental considerations. Moreover, the model is not an external alternative to the traditional legal process but rather is situated within the criminal process and bound by its rules and structure, including the right to appeal. It does not seek to transform the juvenile justice system but to improve it by accounting for additional elements while maintaining basic constitutional safeguards for defendants.

An additional objection challenges the need for a specialized model to minor‐on‐minor cases, arguing that numerous marginalized populations experience similar systemic inadequacies within the criminal justice framework. According to this critique, individuals with disabilities, survivors of sexual violence, and other vulnerable populations similarly encounter institutional alienation and procedural insensitivity to their distinct psychological and emotional needs. This critique questions whether the proposed framework addresses a genuinely unique phenomenon or merely replicates broader systemic failures that affect multiple populations, thereby challenging the theoretical justification for group‐specific procedural innovations.

This critique, while identifying legitimate concerns about systemic exclusion, fails to recognize the distinct characteristics that distinguish minor‐on‐minor cases from other contexts of vulnerability within the criminal justice system. The proposed framework addresses a distinct phenomenon: cases where developmental immaturity and unique needs and vulnerabilities exist simultaneously on both sides of the adversarial divide. Unlike other marginalized populations, who typically occupy either victim or defendant roles, these cases present bilateral minority status.

More broadly, however, the four‐pillar model offers an opportunity to rethink the experience of other vulnerable populations in the justice system. The integration of therapeutic justice, developmental considerations, procedural fairness, and rights‐based approaches may provide a transferable framework that, with appropriate modifications, could enhance criminal justice responses to other marginalized groups.

A related concern is that courts may lack the expertise to balance children's emotional and developmental needs while safeguarding defendants' rights. By broadening judicial attention beyond strictly legal considerations, the model presupposes familiarity with child psychology and therapeutic practice that may exceed courts' core institutional competencies and established decisional frameworks. This concern can be mitigated through targeted training for judges and other key actors in the justice system. The inclusion of non‐legal professionals as integral members of child‐centered proceedings adds another layer of protection against uninformed or misguided decision‐making. Pre‐trial and post‐trial practices led by non‐legal professionals such as pedagogues, psychologists, and criminologists and guided by the same integrated framework can further strengthen the coherence and robustness of the treatment that the young people receive throughout the criminal justice process. Ultimately, the proposed framework depends on an interdisciplinary, problem‐solving orientation within the juvenile justice system.

Finally, the proposed framework may be criticized for insufficiently differentiating between cases that vary significantly in terms of offense severity, the ages and developmental stages of the children involved, and their surrounding environments. A cyberbullying case between adolescents differs markedly from the rape of a young child by an older adolescent, just as an attempted murder in a gang context differs from the theft of a debit card. In response, the framework does not seek to prescribe uniform solutions. Rather, it offers guiding principles, operationalized through key questions to inform the exercise of legal discretion in minor‐on‐minor cases. The integration of the four approaches is intended to provide both structure and flexibility. The developmental perspective requires sensitivity to each child's stage of development and experience of trauma; the relational children's rights lens directs attention to the availability of supportive environments and the need to supplement them where absent; therapeutic jurisprudence highlights potential power imbalances, coercion, and emotional harm; and procedural justice emphasizes fairness, transparency, and the legitimacy of decision‐making. Together, these elements are designed to support context‐sensitive responses without sacrificing normative consistency.

The following practical recommendations represent the lessons learned through the analysis of the two case studies and the precautions required based on the abovementioned concerns and critiques.

Recommendations for Policy and Practice

Rather than delineating a detailed and strict set of guidelines, we propose some initial guiding principles that jurisdictions can adopt and adapt according to their specific legal and social contexts. The implementation of the proposed quadruple theoretical model will necessarily vary across different jurisdictions. Nevertheless, these recommendations chart a path toward justice systems that are both more child‐inclusive and more just, extending equal concern to minor defendants and minor victims alike.

Extended Use of Restorative Justice Processes

One of the most powerful instruments for harmonizing the diverse goals, values, and interests in minor‐on‐minor cases is restorative justice (RJ) (Marshall 1999; Hansen and Umbreit 2018; O’Brien 2023). RJ processes position crime as harm done to individuals and relationships rather than merely a violation of law. It assigns decision‐making authority to those most affected by the crime and seeks outcomes that repair the harm rather than inflict further pain (Braithwaite and Strang 2000; Bazemore and Schiff 2001; Gonzalez 2020). Endorsed by the CRC's emphasis on recovery and reintegration, RJ fosters accountability while ensuring that all children, regardless of their role in a particular incident, receive the support necessary to thrive (Wolthuis and Chapman 2022; Gal 2022). Research demonstrates that properly implemented RJ programs reduce recidivism rates among juvenile offenders while simultaneously increasing victim satisfaction compared to traditional court processes (Bergseth and Bouffard 2012; Shem‐Tov et al. 2024). While it is essential to ensure that key preconditions are met before RJ processes are undertaken, including the offender's acceptance of responsibility, the voluntary consent of both parties, and the availability of appropriate support networks, RJ has been applied across a range of offense types, including beyond minor or first‐time offending. Systematic reviews of the evidence suggest that such processes may have positive effects for victims across a spectrum of offenses, including the most serious ones, with reported benefits such as reductions in post‐traumatic symptoms, anger, desire for revenge, and fear (Nascimento et al. 2023). At the same time, RJ processes must be carefully adapted to the developmental needs of children of different ages, as research indicates that their benefits do not necessarily translate to young participants when implemented without such modifications (Gal and Moyal 2011). A juvenile justice system that uses child‐tailored RJ processes as a default option in cases involving minor‐on‐minor offending is aligned with the rehabilitative approach and relational framework of children's rights, ensuring that justice outcomes for both victims and offenders fulfill their rights as children and their broader developmental needs (Buckingham 2013; April et al. 2023).

Specialized Minor‐on‐Minor Case Hearings

Juvenile courts should establish dedicated dockets specifically for cases involving minor victims and defendants. These specialized proceedings should incorporate multiple evidence‐based modifications that go beyond the settings in standard juvenile court hearings. The physical environment should feature adaptations with flexible seating arrangements that reduce intimidation and facilitate comfort for young participants. Courts should integrate technology solutions like CCTV, recorded testimony, or virtual participation options to minimize stress and confrontation between the parties. Hearings should allow for expanded professional participation, reflecting the developmental and rehabilitative needs of the minor victims, and should operate under modified timelines that acknowledge children's different perceptions of time. Hybrid restorative processes, such as court‐connected mediation or sentencing circles, can give victims a voice and facilitate constructive engagement between the parties, all under judicial oversight to ensure fairness.

Collaborative Teamwork

The accumulated experience of problem‐solving courts demonstrates the benefits of having the various players, including prosecution, defense, probation service, and victim advocacy, working together. This collaborative approach should be formalized through regular cross‐disciplinary meetings; shared outcome goals focused on rehabilitation, harm repair, and developmental progress; and established protocols for managing conflicts when they arise. This does not mean stakeholders should surrender their respective roles when disagreements emerge. Defense attorneys must protect their clients' rights and prosecutors the public interest, but ongoing collaboration facilitates a level of trust that allows for more future‐oriented problem solving (Jordens and Richardson 2014; Berman and Gulick 2003).

Continuity Across Extra‐Legal Services

While the present framework focuses on the trial stage, its underlying principles should also inform the work of professionals who engage with the child victim and the child responsible for the harm before, during, and after the proceedings. Practitioners such as social workers, psychologists, psychiatrists, pedagogues, and criminologists should be familiar with the four‐pillar model and trained to apply its core considerations within their respective domains. Although these professionals typically work with only one of the parties and are therefore less frequently required to navigate direct conflict of interests, awareness of the broader framework is essential to ensure coherence and continuity across interventions. Aligning pre‐ and post‐trial services with the principles guiding the court process can help reinforce developmental, therapeutic, relational, and rights‐based objectives, thereby supporting more consistent and effective outcomes for both children.

Comprehensive Training

Judges, lawyers, social workers, and victim advocates who are involved in such cases require specialized skills in child development, trauma‐informed interviewing, children's rights, and restorative justice. All professionals involved should also receive cultural competency training to address the specific needs of children from diverse backgrounds. Training should be ongoing rather than one‐time, with regular updates reflecting emerging research in adolescent development, trauma science, youth addictions, mental health, and effective interventions. Such a comprehensive training scheme is key to aligning practice with the model's four pillars, maintaining due process, and reducing the risk of secondary victimization.

Revisiting Confidentiality Barriers

Strict confidentiality rules often limit victim participation and info‐sharing in juvenile proceedings. Judicial officers might employ individualized disclosure reviews, balancing the offender's privacy rights with the victim's interest in understanding and engaging with the process. Such disclosure reviews may include age‐appropriate explanations for both the victim and defendant about the dilemmas at hand and competing interests. They can also include a commitment by the victim and the victim's representative to keep the information confidential. Adapted notification systems specifically tailored for children of various ages and their parents or guardians can keep victims informed about significant case developments without compromising the defendant's privacy rights.

Structured Guidelines for Victim Impact Statements

Rather than using VIS in juvenile settings as a straightforward import from adult court, guidelines should ensure that child victims can choose how and when to deliver a VIS (written, video, artistic expression, or in‐person) and who would act as their support system (victim advocate, trusted adult, or specially trained intermediary). Guidelines should also ensure a reintegrative focus, emphasizing harm articulation and recovery needs rather than stigmatizing or punitive language. Whether or not utilizing restorative approaches for such hearings, VIS guidelines should address temporal considerations, recognizing that child victims may need more time to process and articulate impacts and that young defendants may need the mediation of therapeutic professionals to comprehend VIS content. They should include feedback mechanisms to help victims understand how their input influenced outcomes, and offer protective measures ensuring that VIS delivery does not create opportunities for intimidation or re‐traumatization. When appropriate, such guidelines can offer reciprocal opportunities for young defendants to express remorse and accountability in developmentally appropriate ways.

Monitoring and Follow‐Up

Effective case resolution requires robust monitoring and support mechanisms that extend beyond initial disposition. Whether the resolution involves a restoration agreement with the victims or a more conventional probation plan, the court (or a specialized oversight body) should conduct parallel tracking of victims' recovery indicators and the ongoing support needs of the perpetrator. Regular judicial or professional review hearings with strengths‐based feedback can reinforce positive behaviors and identify emerging challenges before they escalate (Cavanagh 2022). When compliance issues arise, the system should employ graduated responses that prioritize additional support over punitive sanctions, recognizing that setbacks are often part of the developmental process for young delinquents. Interagency coordination is essential to ensure that educational, psychological, and social services are aligned and working toward common goals for both victims and defendants as they rehabilitate and reintegrate following the closure of the criminal process.

Conclusion

The foregoing analysis of the two case studies reveals the profound misalignment between traditional adversarial criminal processes and the unique dynamics of minor‐on‐minor cases. Part 1 of this Review showed how criminal procedure, historically structured around adult‐centric paradigms and binary adversarial assumptions, proves fundamentally inadequate in cases where both victim and defendant are minors, and how the rehabilitative ethos of the juvenile justice system and victims' rights reforms fail to provide full remedy. Because of their age, both minors are entitled to heightened protections and rights, specialized support, and meaningful opportunities for rehabilitation and healing, yet these needs often collide within an adversarial framework that was not designed for such a scenario.

The adversarial criminal justice system's persistent tensions—the competition between victims' and defendants' rights, alongside the conflict between rigid legal proceduralism and stakeholders' emotional needs—have catalyzed a fundamental reconceptualization of justice processes. Rather than framing justice as a zero‐sum contest between competing rights claims, contemporary scholars have reimagined it as an integrated system encompassing multiple goals, values, and problem‐solving mechanisms (Dancing‐Rosenberg and Gal 2019). However, this theoretical evolution has largely overlooked the specific complexities in the juvenile justice system emerging in cases where both parties are minor. The four‐pillar model developed in Part 1 of the Review and applied in Part 2, addresses this gap by proposing guiding principles for navigating specific dilemmas in minor‐on‐minor cases, grounded in both normative scholarship and behavioral science. The integration of therapeutic justice, child development principles, relational procedural fairness, and children's rights offers a transformative reconceptualization of the juvenile justice legal process in cases where both the defendant and victim are minors. The four‐pillar model can minimize the binary framing that pits one minor's well‐being against another's. A juvenile system reimagined through this lens, which recognizes the overlapping vulnerabilities and developmental needs of all minors, can simultaneously safeguard victims' rights while preserving defendants' future potential and addressing their rehabilitation and reintegration. This balanced approach ultimately creates an equitable, holistic framework for addressing minor‐on‐minor offenses, which considers the distinctive nature and complexity of these cases.
