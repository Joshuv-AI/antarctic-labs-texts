Investors demand carbon damage risk compensation in China
Authors: Ziqi Lu, Yu Zhang, Zengkai Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAs climate awareness grows, investors are integrating climate performance into their investment decision-making processes to mitigate potential risks. This paper examines the societal costs associated with the carbon footprints of publicly listed companies in China and explores whether investors demand compensation for their exposure to risks associated with investments in assets with greater carbon damages. We find a continuous rise in the carbon footprints of Chinese listed companies, with a particularly noticeable increase following the COVID-19 pandemic. The carbon damages of Chinese listed companies are sourced mainly from their value chain emissions, with significant sectoral and regional heterogeneity. Finally, we observe a carbon damage risk premium throughout entire value chain when viewed from an absolute risk perspective. Even for less perceptible relative risks, we find that listed companies with greater scaled carbon damages originating from direct and upstream value chain carbon footprints are associated with higher expected returns.Subject areas: Environmental science, Environmental policy, Social sciences, Economics

Introduction

To effectively address climate change, it is widely acknowledged that companies should be held financially accountable for their carbon emissions, as these represent a negative externality that imposes costs on society.1,2,3 Companies—particularly listed companies—face increasing pressure to internalize the costs to society associated with their emissions, which is also referred to as company carbon damages.4 The stakeholders of listed companies with greater carbon damages are facing a greater exposure to climate risk and may advocate for compensation for their exposure to carbon emission risk.5,6 Effective climate risk management is a vital aspect of China’s National Climate Change Adaptation Strategy 20357 and is dependent on the recognition of carbon damage and the associated risk premium.

The present study is grounded in prior studies on the carbon risk premium. Previous literature usually uses companies’ carbon emissions as a proxy for climate risk and has identified the presence of a carbon risk premium in both developed8,9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 and emerging economies.5,6,14,17,18,19 However, climate risk arises from the internalization of the external costs of company carbon emissions and cannot be fully captured by carbon emissions. To provide a more effective assessment of investor-perceived climate risk, this study adopts a novel measure grounded in the internalization of company carbon damages (Tables S1 and S2). Given that company carbon footprints can be further disaggregated into the direct emissions of enterprises (scope 1 carbon footprint), emissions from the generation of purchased electricity and energy (scope 2 carbon footprint), and emissions embodied in the upstream and downstream value chains of enterprises (scope 3 carbon footprint),20 we examine whether and how the capital market differentiates its pricing of these distinct components of carbon risk exposure (Figure S1).

Mandatory carbon disclosure is crucial for revealing companies’ carbon damages to stakeholders4; nevertheless, the scope of mandatory disclosure is restricted to the direct emissions of key industries in China.21 Although a large share of carbon damages of a business are originated from its value chain emissions,4 only a few percentages of public companies in China measure and disclose their carbon footprints, especially the scope 3 carbon footprints.22,23 The Trucost database estimate scope 3 carbon footprints of companies on the basis of the traditional demand-driven input-output model, but it does not fully capture the downstream scope 3 carbon footprints.6 To address this issue, we adopt a new database from the study by Zhang et al.,24 which provides a time-series database of both upstream and downstream value chain carbon footprints of Chinese listed companies. In this study, we further extend the database to the year 2022. On the basis of this database, we can analyze the distribution of carbon damages throughout value chain.

This study contributes to the literature from three perspectives. First, this study contributes to the debate on the relationship between companies’ environmental and financial performance by incorporating empirical evidence from emerging economies. According to the risk premium theory,12 investors may demand additional returns to compensate for the uncertainty associated with holding assets with higher climate risks. Yet, existing empirical studies provide conflicting evidence, reporting either a positive6 or negative11 climate risk premium. This study adds to this debate by providing new evidence from the Chinese capital market, an emerging economy that has actively pursued climate change mitigation. Compared with developed countries, China’s climate policy development follows a “pilot-before-policy” trajectory, which may encourage more anticipatory and policy-sensitive investor behavior.17,25,26

Second, this study stands out in the literature by leveraging a full sample of Chinese listed companies, allowing for a more robust assessment of the climate risk premium. Previous studies on climate risk premium have mainly focused on stock markets in developed countries. Even the limited number of empirical studies targeting the Chinese stock market is often based on insufficient samples, which may introduce sample selection bias in the findings. For instance, there are only 1,660 Chinese listed companies in the study by Bolton and Kacperczyk.5 To address this sample coverage gap, we construct a company-level carbon footprint dataset covering full sample of Chinese listed companies over the period 2015–2022, enabling a more robust analysis of climate risk premium in the Chinese capital market.

Third, this study contributes to the literature on climate risk premium by incorporating a value chain perspective, which enables a more nuanced understanding of carbon-related risks. Prior studies failed to differentiate climate risk stemming from upstream and downstream scope 3 carbon footprints.4 In addition, previous studies on carbon risk premium mainly focused on the magnitude of carbon footprints,5,6 ignoring the coverage of climate policies. To fill this research gap, this study offers a more detailed evaluation on the carbon damages of listed companies from a value chain perspective, enabling a compensate estimation of company-level climate risk under the carbon trading framework in China. Furthermore, we assess the carbon risk premium from two perspectives: absolute carbon damage risks, measured by company carbon damages under regulation, and relative carbon damage risks, measured by corporate carbon damages under regulation scaled by revenue.

Results

Carbon footprints of Chinese listed companies

Figure 1A shows that the volume of carbon emissions related to Chinese listed companies increased from 5,461.7 million tons in 2015 to 7,837.1 million tons in 2022. The magnitude of direct emissions (scope 1) of Chinese listed companies exceeds that of scope 2 emissions, whereas the magnitude of scope 3 emissions of these listed companies is greater than that of both of the former. This highlights the critical significance of value chain decarbonization initiatives by listed companies for effective climate action in China. Before the outbreak of COVID-19 in 2019, the volume of the carbon footprints of Chinese listed companies increased continuously. Figure 1B shows that the major contributing factor to this increase over the periods 2015–2017 and 2017–2019 was the growth in the gross revenue of Chinese listed companies (scale effect); meanwhile, the carbon footprint intensity of Chinese listed companies decreased sharply (intensity effect), offsetting their overall carbon footprints. For example, the scale effect caused the carbon footprints of Chinese listed companies to increase by 29.0% over the period 2017–2019, whereas the contribution from the intensity effect was −19.3%. Although the growth rate of the carbon footprints of Chinese listed companies decreased in 2020 due to the outbreak of COVID-19, the subsequent economic rebound in 2021 led to a sharp increase in the carbon footprint. The increase in the carbon footprint from 2019 to 2022 was also driven mainly by the scale effect.

Figure 1C shows the volume of the carbon footprints of listed companies categorized by sector in 2022, with the industry sector, the electricity sector, and the mining sector being the top three in terms of the carbon footprint volume. As many as 3,445 industry companies are listed on the stock market (Tables S3 and S4), and the volume of carbon emissions related to these companies reaches 4,138.6 million tons, including 1,007.5 million tons of scope 1 emissions, 533.7 million tons of scope 2 emissions, and 2,597.4 million tons of scope 3 emissions. Although fewer electricity companies are listed on the stock market, their direct emissions reach as much as 1,564.8 million tons, much greater than their scope 2 (404.0 million tons) and scope 3 (925.2 million tons) emissions. The carbon footprints of listed mining companies, which supply raw materials to support the production of downstream industries, are made up mainly of their scope 3 carbon footprint, reaching as much as 1,949.8 million tons. Services and transportation companies also provide fundamental services to support various economic activities; thus, the scope 3 carbon footprint also accounts for a greater share of these two sectors. This conclusion also applies to listed construction companies, which are heavily dependent on upstream material suppliers. The number of agricultural listed companies is only 49; thus, the volume of their carbon footprints is much smaller than that of the other six sectors. Figure 1D shows the volume of the carbon footprints of listed companies categorized by seven regions in 2022. As most listed companies are located in North China, South China, or East China (Tables S5 and S6), the volume of the carbon footprints of listed companies in these three regions is much greater than that in other regions. The volume of emissions related to listed companies in North China reaches as high as 5,099.9 million tons.

Carbon damages of Chinese listed companies

Figure 2A(I) shows the distribution of carbon damages of Chinese listed companies throughout value chain. The carbon damages embodied in the first-tier suppliers of listed companies account for an average of 21.58% of the gross carbon damages of listed companies. As the number of supply chain tiers increases, emissions related to listed companies decrease, and the extent of carbon-related damages caused by these companies also tends to decline. This highlights the importance of cooperation between listed companies and their first-tier suppliers to reduce their carbon damages. Figure 2A(II) shows that in 2022, the average social cost associated with the carbon footprints of Chinese listed companies, scaled by their revenue, was 0.22%. For every dollar of revenue generated by Chinese listed companies, 0.04 cents should be allocated to cover the negative externalities caused by their direct carbon emissions, 0.02 cents for scope 2 emissions, and 0.15 cents for scope 3 emissions. This finding indicates that the carbon pricing risks faced by listed companies are sourced mainly from their indirect emissions, especially the emissions embodied in their upstream supply chains.

There is significant sectoral heterogeneity in carbon damages. The listed electricity companies correspond to the largest amount of carbon damages scaled by revenue, reaching as much as 0.88% (Figure 2D(II)). The carbon damages associated with electricity companies predominantly arise from their scope 1 carbon footprints, which means that their impact is more straightforward, as the carbon damages originate primarily from their combustion of fossil fuels. This suggests that emission reduction efforts within the electricity sector could have an immediate effect on lowering overall carbon damages. Compared with those of electricity companies, the average scaled carbon damage of listed mining companies is slightly lower at 0.51% (Figure 2F(II)). On average, first-tier downstream value chain partners of listed mining companies contribute 29.40% of gross carbon damages, which is considerably higher than the share (16.08%) that is attributed to the direct emissions of these companies (Figure 2F(I)). This highlights the importance of collaboration between listed mining companies and their downstream users in mitigating climate change. The carbon damages caused by listed companies in the agriculture and transportation sectors also stem primarily from their direct emissions (Figures 2B(I) and 2H(I)). In contrast, listed companies in the industry, construction, and services sectors contribute to negative societal externalities mainly through their indirect emissions (Figures 2C(I), 2E(I), and 2G(I)). Listed construction companies should prioritize climate collaboration with upstream material suppliers to reduce carbon damages, whereas industrial and service companies should focus on partnerships with their electricity suppliers to better control their carbon impact.

Carbon damage risk premium in the Chinese stock market

Table 1 shows the impacts of absolute carbon damage risks on the stock returns of Chinese listed companies (Tables S7 and S8). The results indicate a significant positive relationship between the stock returns of listed companies and the absolute carbon damage risks associated with scope 1, scope 2, upstream scope 3, and downstream scope 3 emissions, all of which are significant at the 1% level. A one-standard-deviation increase in the absolute carbon damage risks of scope 1 increases quarterly stock returns by 0.23% (equals to 32.03% of the average quarterly return), whereas for scope 2, the increase is 0.46% (equals to 62.30% of average). For upstream and downstream scope 3, each standard deviation increase in the absolute carbon damage risks raises quarterly returns by 0.50% and 0.28%, respectively, equating to 68.78% and 37.91% of average. The results confirm the existence of a carbon risk premium in the Chinese stock market. The Chinese stock market has accounted for the level of company carbon damage risk exposure as part of investment risk, and investors have expanded their focus from the direct carbon risk exposure of listed companies to include the carbon risk exposure arising from their purchased energy and value chain involvement. Finally, the control variables in the regressions behave as expected.

Although the relative carbon damage risks are less perceptible for investors than the absolute carbon damage risks, Table 2 also supports the existence of a carbon risk premium from the relative risk perspective. The relative carbon damage risks across various scopes of companies can positively predict stock returns, except for downstream scope 3. A one-standard-deviation increase in the relative carbon damage risks of scope 1, scope 2, and upstream scope 3 leads to quarterly stock return increases of 0.09%, 0.13%, and 0.28%, respectively, equating to 12.21%, 18.39%, and 38.29% of the average quarterly return. These regression results are significant at the 10%, 5%, and 1% levels, respectively. A comparison of the regression coefficients reveals that absolute carbon damage risks have a greater impact on stock returns than do relative carbon damage risks, suggesting that investors have not yet fully linked carbon damage risks to the size of company output. This pattern likely reflects the structure of China’s climate policy, which emphasizes total emissions control at the production level.21 Regulatory instruments—such as emissions trading schemes27 and proposed carbon taxes28—primarily target high-emission upstream sectors and are based on absolute emission volumes,29 while downstream emissions receive comparatively limited scrutiny and remain outside most compliance frameworks.21 This asymmetry weakens regulatory signaling around downstream carbon risks. As a result, relative risks related to downstream scope 3 emissions are less likely to be incorporated into investor decision-making, which may explain their lack of significance in our empirical results.

To ensure the robustness and credibility of our findings, we perform a series of validation analyses. First, to evaluate the reliability of the carbon emission estimates employed in this study, we conduct uncertainty analyses by (1) replacing the input-output table with alternative versions and (2) benchmarking our estimates against company-level emission disclosures from the Carbon Disclosure Project (CDP). Both approaches confirm the validity of our emission estimates. Second, to verify the robustness of our empirical results, we undertake four complementary tests: (1) we re-estimate the models using a two-quarter lag of carbon damage risks as the explanatory variable, thereby addressing potential concerns related to data temporal alignment; (2) we implement a univariate portfolio sorting approach to assess whether long-short portfolios formed on carbon damage risks yield abnormal returns unexplained by conventional risk factors; (3) we augment our baseline regression models with additional control variables that account for macroeconomic conditions, company-level heterogeneity, and external exposures such as exchange rate fluctuations; and (4) we employ Fama-MacBeth regressions to assess the consistency of our results within a cross-sectional asset pricing framework. Across all robustness tests, the effects of carbon damage risks remain directionally consistent and largely statistically significant, reinforcing the credibility of our baseline findings (Tables S12–S18; Figures S2 and S3).

Discussion

This study systematically evaluates the carbon footprints of publicly listed companies in China, estimates the corresponding carbon damages, and examines the associated carbon risk premium. We find an increasing trend in the magnitude of carbon emissions related to Chinese listed companies over the period 2015–2022. For most sectors, the social cost associated with the value chain emissions of listed companies surpasses that of their direct emissions. Finally, investors demand compensation for their exposure to carbon damage risks.

The huge magnitude of carbon footprints of listed companies found in the present study reveals the leading role of listed companies in coordinated climate actions through the entire value chain.30 Listed companies typically play a dominant role in optimizing processes and allocating benefits within their respective value chains, enabling them to exert significant influence over carbon mitigation through value chain.31 It is suggested that Chinese climate policies, which traditionally focus mainly on promoting company-level emission mitigation, should further incentivize collaborative emission reduction efforts through value chain. Some listed companies have already begun driving the decarbonization of their value chain. For instance, China Mobile has released the Opinions on Green Supply Chain.32 We suggest that policymakers should adopt more targeted measures to encourage more publicly listed companies to take the lead in value chain decomposition, such as adopting standards like the Science Based Targets initiative, which mandates that companies include scope 3 emissions in their near-term science-based targets if these emissions represent 40% or more of their total emissions.33

The substantial company carbon damages arising from scope 3 emissions underscore the significance of extending carbon disclosure beyond direct emissions. In Europe, the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive mandates that large companies disclose the environmental impacts through their entire value chain, helping European investors access more holistic information.34 In contrast, in China, the current sustainability reporting standard for listed companies requires only scope 1 and scope 2 emission disclosure, with scope 3 emission disclosure remaining voluntary.21 The lack of scope 3 carbon footprint data may result in information asymmetry in Chinese capital markets.35 Thus, to reduce this information gap, China is advised to accelerate scope 3 carbon disclosure and adopt quantifiable assessment methods to enhance transparency and align with global practices.36 For example, assigning at least 15% weight to scope 3 emissions—following the S&P Global framework, where they comprise 16.75% of the environmental score—would promote greater alignment with international practices.37

The existence of the carbon damage risk premium demonstrates that investors in Chinese stock market have incorporated climate-related risk into their investment decisions. Given the increasing pressure from stricter domestic climate regulations, such as the national carbon trading system,38 and international measures, such as the EU’s border adjustments,39 the social and financial costs of carbon emissions are expected to be further internalized in the future. For companies with substantial carbon footprints, this premium can erode market value, especially as investors and stakeholders become more attentive to climate transition risk.12 Companies are encouraged to enhance carbon management by adopting measures such as purchasing renewable energy, deploying green technologies, and involving suppliers in emission reduction, thereby appealing to investors focused on sustainable and low-carbon portfolios. In addition, Chinese government should strengthen the development of climate risk hedging mechanisms to ensure financial stability under increasing environmental uncertainties.40

Limitations of the study

Future research could further investigate this topic in several important directions. First, this study uses carbon damages to reflect companies’ climate transition risk but does not account for physical climate risks such as extreme weather, which may have greater long-term socioeconomic impacts.41 As investors may perceive these two types of risks differently,42 future research should extend to the pricing of physical climate risk premium.43 Second, this study focuses on companies’ climate-related risk exposure but does not consider their mitigation or adaptation strategies. Since investment decisions may also be shaped by mitigation measures such as renewable energy use, supplier engagement, and technological innovation, future research could further assess how these actions interact with risk exposure to influence the carbon risk premium.44,45 Finally, this study does not account for cross-country differences in climate regulations,46,47 disclosure standards,48 and investor preferences.49 Future research could conduct comparative analyses between advanced and emerging economies to explore how varying regulatory environments influence investors’ behavior and companies’ carbon strategies.

Method details

Carbon footprint evaluation method

A comprehensive carbon footprint database of Chinese listed companies is essential for the present study. We first collect the carbon footprints of public companies from the CDP database; however, only a small number of listed companies in China calculate and disclose their value chain carbon footprints.23 We have to estimate those companies that have not disclosed their carbon footprints. According to the Greenhouse gas (GHG) protocol,51 both bottom-up methods, such as the physical energy content method,52 and top-down methods, typically using the environmental input-output (EIO) model,52 can be applied to quantify companies’ carbon footprints. Due to the challenges in collecting detailed value chain data required for a bottom-up approach, the present paper adopt the top-down method and uses the industry-average data to estimate the value chain carbon footprints.

First, we collect the revenue of listed companies at both regional and sectoral levels from their annual reports. Then, we match the revenue items to the corresponding sectors in the input-output table based on their economic classification. Finally, we estimate the carbon footprints of companies based on the carbon footprint intensity at sectoral level calculated based on the EIO model. A more detailed explanation of the carbon footprint evaluation method for public companies can be found in the study by Zhang et al.24

Company carbon damage evaluation method

According to the study by Greenstone et al.,4 company carbon damage is equal to the multiplication between the volume of the carbon footprint and the Social Cost of Carbon (SCC)—the economic damages that would result from emitting one additional ton of carbon dioxide are measured in dollars.53 In the present study, the SCC, which is deflated to yuan based on the central parity rate of the RMB published by the State Administration of Foreign Exchange of China, is obtained from Wang et al.’s study (2022).54 Wang et al.54 developed a provincial-level SCC estimation framework integrating climate and economic factors.54 We adopt their SSP2-RCP6.0 scenario estimates, which aligns with historical trends and future projections55 (Table S21). Company carbon damage risk evaluation method

According to Pigouvian theory, the negative externalities associated with a company’s carbon footprints can be internalized through policy and market-based mechanisms, such as carbon trading systems.56 Building on this theoretical framework, we conceptualize carbon damage risk as the company-level financial exposure to climate regulation. In specific, we define absolute carbon damage risk as the monetary value of company-level carbon emissions that fall within the scope of carbon trading frameworks in China, reflecting direct exposure to climate policy enforcement. Carbon damage risk premium estimation method

To explore the relationship between a company’s carbon damage risks and stock returns, we establish Equation 4, in which all explanatory variables are lagged by one-quarter to address potential endogeneity concerns and ensure more robust results.

The dependent variable, ROSn,t, represents the return on stock for company n in quarter t. The primary explanatory variable is the company’s carbon damage risks Riskn,t−1. The coefficient α1 captures the influence of carbon damage risk on a company’s financial performance. Additionally, we include a set of control variables Controln,t−1, which are also lagged by one-quarter, to account for other relevant factors: firm size (LOGSIZE), book-to-market ratio (BM), return on equity (ROE), investment rate of return (IRR), leverage (LEVERAGE), beta (BETA), fixed asset ratio (FAR), the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), idiosyncratic volatility (IR), and momentum risk (MOM). These variables help isolate the effects of carbon damage risks from those of other performance drivers. We also introduce quarter fixed effects (θt) to capture time-specific influences and company-specific fixed effects (δi) to control for heterogeneity at the company, industry, or regional level. The error term εi,t is used to capture unexplained variability in the model.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Robustness checks for carbon emission estimates

To assess the accuracy of the carbon emission estimates used in this study, we implement two validation steps. First, we conduct an uncertainty analysis by replacing the original input-output table with an alternative version developed by Zhao et al., which incorporates different assumptions about inter-industry linkages and sectoral emission profiles.57 Second, we evaluate the external validity of our estimates by comparing them with firm-level emissions disclosed by the Carbon Disclosure Project.22

Robustness checks for temporal mismatch

Due to data limitations, annual carbon damage risk measures—constructed from input-output tables released by the National Bureau of Statistics—were used to explain quarterly stock returns. This creates a potential temporal mismatch, as quarterly-level social cost of carbon estimates are unavailable. To assess whether this mismatch biases our inference on the carbon risk premium, we follow Bolton and Kacperczyk6 and re-estimate our baseline regressions using carbon damage risk measures lagged by two-quarters as explanatory variables. Both absolute and relative risk measures are tested across Scope 1, Scope 2, upstream Scope 3, and downstream Scope 3 emissions.

Robustness analysis based on univariate grouping

We employ a univariate portfolio sorting approach to examine whether long–short portfolios based on carbon damage risks can generate excess returns unexplained by standard risk factors. In each quarter, companies are sorted into quintiles according to their total absolute carbon damage risks or relative carbon damage risks. For each of the five portfolios, we compute both equal-weighted and value-weighted excess returns in the subsequent quarter. We also construct a Brown-minus-Green (BMG) portfolio by taking a long position in the highest-risk quintile (“brown”) and a short position in the lowest-risk quintile (“green”), and calculate its excess return in the same way.

To risk-adjust the excess returns of the quintile portfolios and the BMG portfolio, we incorporate factors from the Fama–French three-factor, five-factor, and Carhart four-factor models. Specifically, portfolio excess returns are regressed on the relevant factor returns via OLS, and the intercept alpha obtained from the regression is the risk-adjusted return. Robustness analysis with additional control variables

We further assess robustness by augmenting the baseline regressions with additional controls: (1) GDP growth rate (ΔGDP, %) to capture macroeconomic fluctuations; (2) firm age (AGE, measured in quarters) to account for differences in development stage; and (3) quarterly exchange rate volatility (ER, %) to capture risks from exchange rate movements and export exposure.

Robustness analysis with Fama-MacBeth model

We re-estimate the relationship between carbon damage risks and stock returns using the Fama–MacBeth two-step procedure. In the first step, we conduct quarterly cross-sectional regressions of stock returns on company-level measures of absolute and relative carbon damage risks, disaggregated by emission scope. In the second step, the estimated slope coefficients from each cross-section are averaged over time to derive the associated risk premium.
