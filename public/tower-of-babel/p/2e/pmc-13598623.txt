Impact of obstructive sleep apnoea on nocturnal blood pressure: mechanisms and treatment responses – a narrative review
Authors: Bastian Gruber, Lara Benning, Zoe Bousraou, Dario Somaini, Esther Irene Schwarz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nocturnal blood pressure (BP) is a major determinant of cardiovascular risk. In obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), abnormal nocturnal BP patterns, particularly nondipping and nocturnal hypertension, are common and contribute to excess cardiovascular risk. However, this association remains insufficiently integrated into routine clinical practice, as BP assessment still relies largely on office or home measurements and ambulatory BP monitoring is underused. This narrative review summarises current evidence on the physiological regulation of nocturnal BP, the mechanisms by which OSA disrupts nocturnal BP control, the epidemiology and prognostic relevance of abnormal nocturnal BP patterns in OSA, and the effects of available therapies. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) has the strongest evidence base and lowers nocturnal BP modestly on average, with greater effects in patients with uncontrolled or resistant hypertension and with better treatment adherence. By contrast, evidence for non-CPAP therapies, including mandibular advancement devices, supplemental oxygen, hypoglossal nerve stimulation and upper-airway surgery, remains limited and less consistent. In summary, nocturnal BP abnormalities are common, clinically important and frequently overlooked in OSA. Greater use of ambulatory BP monitoring and more precise identification of patients most likely to benefit from targeted treatment may improve cardiovascular risk stratification and management in OSA

Introduction

Nocturnal blood pressure (BP) is an independent predictor of cardiovascular risk, outperforming daytime measurements in prognostic value. Abnormal nocturnal BP patterns such as nondipping and nocturnal hypertension are associated with increased risk of stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure and all-cause mortality [1]. These disturbances are prevalent in obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) and may represent a key mechanistic link through which OSA contributes to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, as well as a potential target for therapeutic intervention with OSA therapy.

In patients with OSA, nondipping and nocturnal hypertension are common, with prevalences far exceeding those observed in the general population [2, 3]. Recurrent upper airway obstruction with intermittent hypoxaemia, arousals, intrathoracic pressure swings and respiratory effort changes result in sympathetic activation and promote nocturnal BP surges at apnoea- or hypopnoea-termination and impair the normal nocturnal decline in BP [4–6]. Increasing OSA severity has been associated with higher BP and a greater likelihood of impaired nocturnal dipping [7, 8].

Despite recognition of nocturnal hypertension as a cardiovascular risk marker and OSA as a cardiometabolic disorder affecting nocturnal BP, this association remains insufficiently integrated into routine clinical practice. In OSA patients, hypertension management still relies predominantly on office or home BP measurements, while ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM) is underused [9, 10]. In parallel, OSA remains under-recognised in hypertension care, despite guideline recommendations to evaluate patients with resistant or poorly controlled hypertension for OSA [11, 12].

Thus, the relationship between OSA and nocturnal BP warrants clinical attention. This review addresses the clinically relevant questions of how OSA alters the physiological regulation of nocturnal BP, what is known about the epidemiology and prognostic impact of abnormal nocturnal BP control in OSA, and how therapeutic strategies can modify nocturnal BP and potentially cardiovascular risk in OSA.

Design and methods of literature search

This narrative review was conducted through a comprehensive literature search of peer-reviewed studies indexed in PubMed/Medline from database inception to 23 May 2026. We included observational, interventional and mechanistic research, without formal systematic review methodology, focusing on the relationship between nocturnal BP and OSA as well as the effects of CPAP and non-CPAP therapies. Because the scope of this review spans physiology, pathophysiology, epidemiology, prognosis and treatment, separate PubMed/Medline searches were conducted. For therapeutic and prognostic sections, searches combined terms related to OSA, nocturnal BP, ABPM, cardiovascular events, CPAP, mandibular advancement devices (MADs) and antihypertensive therapy. Priority was given to meta-analyses, randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and large prospective observational studies, while smaller observational or mechanistic studies were included when directly relevant or when higher-level evidence was unavailable. For physiology and pathophysiology, targeted searches were used to identify landmark mechanistic studies and key reviews. Reference lists of key articles were reviewed to identify additional publications. Article selection was guided by study design and clinical relevance.

Physiological regulation of nocturnal BP

During sleep, particularly during nonrapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, vagal tone increases while sympathetic nervous system activity decreases [13–16]. Consistent with this nocturnal autonomic shift, urinary catecholamine excretion reaches its nadir during the night [16]. Rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is associated with greater autonomic instability and transient sympathetic activation, which partly explains the greater BP variability observed during REM sleep [13, 17]. Components of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) and vascular clock gene expression show circadian variation, and the arterial baroreflex resets to a lower operating point during sleep [2, 18]. These coordinated circadian changes result in lower nocturnal heart rate and BP, as well as a morning rise in BP [19].

Patterns of nocturnal BP abnormalities

Nocturnal BP abnormalities in OSA are best characterised by ABPM and can be described using two complementary approaches, namely the relative nocturnal BP fall and the absolute night-time BP level. Dipping status is defined as the percentage decline in mean night-time BP relative to mean daytime BP with a fall of 10–20% considered normal [19, 20]. Abnormal dipping patterns such as nondipping and reverse dipping are associated with increased cardiovascular risk [1, 20–24].

Absolute night-time BP offers a second classification. The 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines define nocturnal hypertension as a night-time BP of ≥120/70 mmHg [12]. However, nocturnal BP thresholds differ across guidelines. The 2025 American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology guidelines refrain from recommending a single cut-off value. Instead, they relate ABPM values to office BP categories, where a nocturnal BP 120/70 mmHg corresponds to an office BP of 140/90 mmHg [25]. This difference reflects how guideline committees weigh the available evidence, which is derived mainly from observational cohorts from Europe, Australia and Asia.

Nocturnal hypertension and nondipping remain undetected when BP evaluation relies solely on office measurements. These abnormalities are common in the general population, but particularly frequent in untreated OSA. Reported prevalences vary substantially across studies. This variability likely reflects differences in OSA severity, cardiometabolic comorbidity, background hypertension, antihypertensive drug treatment and whether cohorts included untreated patients or referred sleep clinic populations [3, 26, 27]. Pathophysiology of nocturnal hypertension in OSA

OSA disrupts nocturnal BP control through respiratory event-related repetitive acute increases in BP and longer-term neurohumoral and vascular adaptation (figure 1). During obstructive events, inspiratory effort against a narrowed or occluded upper airway generates increasingly negative intrathoracic pressure. The magnitude of these pressure swings varies substantially and is greatest during complete obstruction with high respiratory drive [28]. Additionally, hypoxaemia and hypercapnia activate peripheral chemoreceptors and trigger sympathetic activation [4, 29, 30]. Recurrent cortical arousals contribute to this sympatho-excitation, resulting in abrupt surges in heart rate and BP at apnoea termination [6, 14, 29, 31]. The arterial baroreflex buffers BP fluctuations. In OSA, however, baroreflex sensitivity is impaired, reducing the capacity to dampen these pressure surges [32]. As a result, the normal sleep-related fall in sympathetic tone is attenuated and BP becomes increasingly unstable across the night. Over time, these acute disturbances result in more sustained cardiovascular dysregulation. Recurrent intermittent hypoxia promotes persistent sympathetic activation and stimulates the RAAS, contributing to vasoconstriction, sodium retention and myocardial and vascular remodelling [14, 30, 33, 34]. These mechanisms extend the haemodynamic impact of individual apnoeas beyond the event itself.

OSA has acute and sustained haemodynamic effects. Negative intrathoracic pressure swings increase venous return and right ventricular preload, while hypoxia-induced pulmonary vasoconstriction raises right ventricular afterload. The resulting right ventricular distension impairs left ventricular filling through leftward shift of the ventricular septum. Negative intrathoracic pressure increases left ventricular transmural pressure and thereby left ventricular afterload, even before systemic arterial pressure rises. In addition, chemoreflex-mediated vasoconstriction adds further afterload, so that each obstructive event imposes a combined mechanical and neurogenic stress on the myocardium [6, 29, 31].

Intermittent hypoxaemia and subsequent re-oxygenation also promote endothelial dysfunction [35–39] and a shift toward vasoconstriction, which blunts the normal nocturnal decline in BP. Beyond these functional abnormalities, persistent oxidative stress and low-grade vascular inflammation contribute to structural vascular remodelling. Immune activation, extracellular matrix remodelling, perivascular fibrosis and increasing arterial stiffness reduce vascular compliance and further impair baroreflex buffering [40–43]. These chronic vascular changes help explain why nocturnal BP abnormalities in OSA often persist beyond isolated respiratory events and may evolve into a stable nondipping or nocturnal hypertensive pattern.

Impact of OSA severity and comorbidities on nocturnal BP

Multiple large cohort studies have demonstrated a dose-dependent relationship between OSA severity and BP. A cross-sectional analysis from the Sleep Heart Health Study demonstrated a positive relationship between the apnoea–hypopnoea index (AHI) and systolic BP, which persisted after adjustment for body mass index and other confounders [7]. Data from the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort further support this relationship by showing that increasing REM-AHI was independently associated with higher systolic BP and higher prevalence of hypertension in the general population [44]. In a subsequent analysis, higher REM-AHI also predicted an increased likelihood of a nondipping nocturnal BP pattern [45]. Earlier reports from the same cohort had already shown a significant association between overall AHI and nocturnal hypertension, even without stratification by sleep stage [46]. Comorbidities are important modifiers of nocturnal hypertension in OSA cohorts. Obesity, type 2 diabetes and dyslipidaemia may contribute to nocturnal hypertension or impaired dipping independently of OSA. At the same time, these conditions frequently coexist with OSA and the relationship is likely bidirectional. OSA-related hypoxaemia and sympathetic activation may contribute to insulin resistance, adipose-tissue inflammation and altered lipid metabolism, while obesity and metabolic dysfunction may increase OSA severity and amplify its vascular consequences. This interplay makes it difficult to determine the relative contribution of obstructive events versus underlying cardiometabolic disease in individual patients [47–54]. Methodological limitations further complicate interpretation. Many observational studies linking obesity, type 2 diabetes and dyslipidaemia to abnormal nocturnal BP regulation did not systematically assess sleep-disordered breathing. As a result, reported associations may partly reflect unrecognised OSA rather than direct metabolic or vascular effects alone.

Additional complexity arises from overlap with other sleep disorders. Insomnia and restless legs syndrome may coexist with OSA and have also been associated with nondipping and nocturnal hypertension [55, 56]. By contributing to microarousals, sleep fragmentation and autonomic activation, these disorders may confound the observed association between OSA and nocturnal BP abnormalities if not systematically assessed [57].

Prognostic impact of nondipping and nocturnal hypertension

The detrimental effects of nocturnal hypertension are well established in hypertensive and cardiovascular populations [58]. In addition, an elevated night-to-day BP ratio and a nondipping pattern have been associated with increased risks of cardiovascular events and death [19]. A meta-analysis of prospective studies including >23 000 hypertensive patients and >9000 individuals from the general population showed that nocturnal systolic BP is a stronger predictor of cardiovascular events and mortality than daytime BP, with a 14% increase in total mortality risk per 10 mmHg increase in night-time systolic BP (hazard ratio (HR) 1.14, 95% CI 1.08–1.20) [22]. However, dedicated prospective studies in OSA cohorts directly comparing the prognostic value of nocturnal versus daytime BP are lacking.

Subsequent large-scale cohort analyses have reinforced this observation. In a pooled analysis of 11 135 individuals from the International Database on Ambulatory BP in Relation to Cardiovascular Outcomes (IDACO), night-time systolic BP was independently associated with total mortality (HR 1.23 (95% CI 1.17–1.28) per 20 mmHg) and composite cardiovascular events (HR 1.36, 95% CI 1.30–1.43). These associations persisted even after adjustment for daytime and 24-h BP, indicating that nocturnal BP conveys prognostic information beyond daytime BP [23]. Recent data from the Spanish Ambulatory BP Registry including over 59 000 primary care patients further substantiated these observations. In this real-world cohort, ambulatory BP was markedly superior to clinic BP for mortality prediction and night-time systolic BP emerged as the most informative ambulatory measure. Importantly, night-time BP remained strongly associated with both all-cause and cardiovascular mortality even after adjustment for daytime BP, whereas daytime BP lost prognostic significance once night-time BP was accounted for [24]. These data indicate that nocturnal BP provides prognostic information that is not explained by daytime or overall BP increases.

None of these studies systematically assessed sleep-disordered breathing and therefore they could not determine whether OSA contributed to nocturnal hypertension or modified its prognostic impact.

Whether nocturnal BP abnormalities carry the same prognostic significance in OSA remains uncertain, because this question has rarely been addressed directly in dedicated OSA cohorts. In the 2000s, several landmark prospective cohort studies established OSA as a condition associated with increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Across sleep clinic and community-based populations, untreated moderate-to-severe OSA was linked to higher risks of stroke, incident coronary heart disease, heart failure and all-cause mortality [59–63].

More recently, attention has shifted from AHI toward physiological metrics that better capture the severity of nocturnal hypoxaemia. The concept of “hypoxic burden” as quantitative marker of sleep apnoea-specific intermittent hypoxaemia demonstrated that it predicts cardiovascular disease-related mortality better than AHI [64]. Subsequent studies have confirmed the prognostic relevance of hypoxic burden for incident cardiovascular events and mortality in both community-based and clinical OSA populations [65, 66]. Despite substantial evidence linking both OSA and nocturnal BP abnormalities to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, the two fields have largely evolved in parallel rather than in combination. Most longitudinal OSA cohorts did not include systematic ABPM and therefore could not determine the extent to which nocturnal hypertension or impaired dipping contributed to excess cardiovascular risk. The large ambulatory BP cohorts that established the prognostic importance of night-time BP did not systematically assess sleep-disordered breathing. As a result, the available evidence is convergent but largely indirect, and prospective studies integrating polysomnography, ABPM and long-term cardiovascular outcomes are lacking.

Treatment response of nocturnal BP to CPAP

CPAP has a mechanistically plausible effect on nocturnal BP because it directly prevents obstructive respiratory events, intermittent hypoxaemia, arousals, intrathoracic pressure swings and sleep-related sympathetic surges. However, its average antihypertensive effect in randomised trials is modest. In the largest recent study-level meta-analysis, including 75 RCTs and 10 025 participants, CPAP reduced 24-h systolic BP by −2.6 mmHg and nocturnal systolic BP by −3.5 mmHg, with smaller effects on office and diurnal systolic BP. However, trials differed substantially in baseline BP status, OSA severity, symptoms, antihypertensive therapy, CPAP use and BP measurement method; moreover, nocturnal ABPM data and information on antihypertensive medication changes were incompletely reported [67].

The most consistent determinant of BP response is not OSA severity as measured by AHI, but the baseline BP phenotype. In a worldwide individual patient-data meta-analysis, CPAP reduced BP mainly in patients with uncontrolled BP at baseline, whereas patients with controlled hypertension or true normotension showed little or no significant BP reduction [68]. This finding is clinically important because it suggests that hypertension in OSA is often multifactorial. CPAP can remove an OSA-related pressor stimulus, but it cannot fully reverse BP elevation driven by obesity, arterial stiffness, renal mechanisms, long-standing hypertension or established cardiovascular disease.

Resistant hypertension is the best-established high-response clinical phenotype. But even here CPAP response is not uniform. In the HIPARCO trial, patients with OSA and resistant hypertension had a greater BP reduction with CPAP than with usual care and a higher rate of normal dipping pattern. However, HIPARCO was an open-label trial lasting only 12 weeks without assessment of long-term cardiovascular outcomes, limiting inference about long-term cardiovascular benefit [69]. The heterogeneity of BP response is further illustrated by a subsequent HIPARCO sub-analysis. Among 98 patients treated with CPAP, 38 had an increase in 24-h BP. Older age, ischaemic heart disease, longer duration of resistant hypertension and lower CPAP use were independently associated with this paradoxical BP increase [70]. In patients with advanced cardiovascular disease and long-standing hypertension, the BP phenotype might be less dependent on OSA and less responsive to CPAP.

CPAP adherence is central when interpreting these results because CPAP is only active while worn [71]. Partial-night use may leave later sleep periods untreated, including REM-rich sleep when obstructive events and BP surges may be more pronounced. Studies with mean CPAP use >5 h·night–1 showed larger reductions in nocturnal BP compared to studies with a lower mean adherence (−4.2 mmHg versus −1.6 mmHg) [67]. HIPARCO showed a similar dose–response relationship between hours of CPAP use and BP reduction [69].

OSA severity and nocturnal hypoxaemia remain biologically plausible predictors of BP response, but the evidence is inconsistent. The meta-analysis by Benning et al. [67] suggested larger BP reductions in severe OSA and in patients with higher baseline BP. In contrast, the individual-patient-data meta-analysis did not confirm minimum nocturnal hypoxaemia as a consistent predictor. This discrepancy may reflect limitations of conventional polysomnography metrics. AHI does not capture event duration, desaturation depth, REM predominance, arousal intensity and autonomic activation, while minimum oxygen saturation is an unreliable measure of intermittent hypoxaemia [67, 68]. Hypoxic burden integrates the depth, duration and frequency of respiratory event-related desaturations and has shown stronger prognostic value than AHI for cardiovascular outcomes in several cohorts, but it has rarely been evaluated as a predictor of BP response under CPAP. Endotypic and physiological predictors may eventually improve patient selection, but the evidence remains exploratory. In a secondary HeartBEAT analysis, higher loop gain predicted greater reduction in 24-h mean BP with CPAP, independent of AHI, demographics and adherence, whereas baseline hypoxic burden, symptom subtype, Epworth Sleepiness Scale and AHI did not consistently predict BP response; in the oxygen treatment arm, no consistent biomarker-response relationship was observed [72]. In a subsequent HeartBEAT analysis, event-related heart-rate response emerged as another potential marker of treatment responsiveness, where a high heart-rate response to respiratory events predicted a greater reduction in 24-h systolic BP after treatment with CPAP or nocturnal supplemental oxygen, compared with a mid-range heart-rate response. In the same study, a greater treatment-related reduction in hypoxic burden was also independently associated with larger systolic BP reductions [73]. Thus, the relevant signal may not be baseline hypoxic burden per se, but whether treatment effectively reduces the hypoxaemic and autonomic consequences of obstructive events. However, these secondary analyses were relatively small and included patients already receiving extensive cardiovascular and antihypertensive therapy. Further research is needed before endotypic traits or autonomic markers can be used to guide treatment selection.

One unresolved key question is not whether CPAP lowers BP on average, but which patients have a reversible OSA-related BP component that is large enough to be clinically meaningful.

The distinction between average BP reduction and clinically meaningful long-term benefit is also important when interpreting the major cardiovascular secondary-prevention trials. SAVE, RICCADSA and ISAACC evaluated routine CPAP therapy for secondary cardiovascular prevention. SAVE enrolled patients with established coronary or cerebrovascular disease and moderate-to-severe OSA, but most were minimally sleepy, patients with severe sleepiness or very severe hypoxaemia were excluded, and mean CPAP use was only 3.3 h·night–1, with only 42% achieving ≥4 h·night–1 [74]. RICCADSA similarly enrolled nonsleepy patients with revascularised coronary artery disease and was neutral in the intention-to-treat analysis, although an adjusted on-treatment analysis suggested benefit among patients using CPAP for >4 h·night–1, a finding that remains susceptible to adherence-related self-selection bias [75]. ISAACC enrolled nonsleepy patients after acute coronary syndrome, had mean CPAP adherence of 2.78 h·night–1 and found no cardiovascular benefit even in adherence-based analyses [76]. These trials therefore indicate that modest BP reductions with CPAP do not necessarily translate into fewer cardiovascular events in broad secondary-prevention populations. However, they did not specifically study patients with nocturnal hypertension, impaired dipping, resistant hypertension or sustained nightly CPAP use. Whether improvement in nocturnal BP identifies a subgroup more likely to derive long-term cardiovascular benefit from CPAP remains open.

Potential mechanisms of BP reduction with CPAP

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain how CPAP lowers BP, with the strongest evidence supporting effects on autonomic activation and vascular function, and less consistent evidence for pathways such as RAAS activity, baroreflex control and chemosensitivity.

A meta-analysis of RCTs and prospective observational studies showed that CPAP reduces 24-h urinary catecholamine levels, indicating attenuation of sympathetic activation under treatment [77]. This is supported by several studies showing that muscle sympathetic nerve activity (MSNA), which is elevated in OSA, is reduced by OSA therapy [78]. Complementing these physiologic data, Henderson et al. [79] demonstrated that 12 months of CPAP was associated not only with lower resting MSNA, but also with restoration of MSNA-related functional and structural abnormalities in key brainstem regions involved in sympathetic regulation. Overall, these findings support a decrease in sympathetic overactivity as a plausible mediator of BP reduction under CPAP.

Endothelial function appears to improve with CPAP therapy in OSA, a finding shown consistently in two meta-analyses using flow-mediated dilation, a marker of endothelium-dependent vascular function [80, 81]. Mechanistic human studies further suggest that this improvement may be mediated, at least in part, by enhanced nitric oxide-dependent endothelial function [82, 83]. Endothelin-1 levels also decrease under CPAP, indicating that reduced endothelial vasoconstrictor activity may contribute to this response [84].

Effects of CPAP on arterial stiffness are more difficult to interpret. Arterial stiffness in CPAP studies has been assessed mainly by pulse wave velocity (PWV) and augmentation index (AIx). PWV reflects the speed at which the pressure wave travels through the large arteries and is generally regarded as the more direct marker of large-artery stiffness, whereas AIx is additionally influenced by wave reflection, peripheral vascular resistance and endothelial function. Meta-analytic data suggest that CPAP is associated with a modest reduction in PWV, while AIx does not appear to improve consistently [85, 86]. However, both metrics are influenced by the BP level at the time of measurement, particularly PWV, which increases with greater arterial distending pressure. Meta-analysis could not determine to what extent the observed reduction in PWV reflected a direct vascular effect of CPAP beyond the concomitant BP-lowering effect of treatment.

Evidence that CPAP meaningfully modulates RAAS activity remains limited and relates mainly to aldosterone rather than the broader RAAS. A 2016 meta-analysis suggested only a small reduction in aldosterone with CPAP, while the pooled analysis of the two randomised trials showed no significant effect [87]. A similar impression emerges from the resistant-hypertension meta-analysis by Labarca et al. [88], which primarily addressed BP outcomes but also examined aldosterone excretion and suggested, at most, a modest RAAS signal alongside the clearer BP effect of CPAP. Interpretation is further limited by incomplete reporting of concomitant antihypertensive medication, particularly agents that directly affect aldosterone.

Baroreflex improvement is likewise plausible but not firmly established. Small physiologic studies suggest that CPAP can improve baroreflex sensitivity, reduce heart rate and reset the operating point of BP control to a lower level [89–91]. However, the broader evidence remains mixed and a more recent meta-analysis did not show clear overall improvement in baroreflex sensitivity, although that analysis was not restricted to CPAP and included other OSA therapies such as oral appliances and surgery [92]. The same cautious interpretation applies to chemosensitivity. In hypertensive OSA, Casitas et al. [93] showed that CPAP reduced peripheral chemosensitivity and that this measure was independently related to night-time BP. Other physiologic studies similarly suggest attenuation of abnormal hypoxia-driven reflex activation under treatment [94]. Yet, these data remain limited to small studies, and larger cohort-level confirmation is lacking.

By contrast, reversal of established structural vascular remodelling is unlikely to account for the short-term BP reductions seen in most CPAP trials. Animal data suggest that at least some hypoxia-induced vascular injury may be reversible. In a mouse model of chronic intermittent hypoxia, 6 weeks of exposure induced aortic intima-media thickening, elastin disorganisation, collagen accumulation and left-ventricular perivascular fibrosis, whereas six subsequent weeks of return to normoxia largely normalised these abnormalities [43]. However, the translational relevance of this model is limited because it reproduces intermittent hypoxia but not the full pathophysiological complexity of human OSA. In human studies, structural vascular remodelling has been assessed mainly by carotid intima-media thickness (cIMT) and the available evidence is largely neutral. A meta-analysis including seven studies found no significant overall reduction in cIMT with CPAP, while subgroup analyses suggested only modest benefit in patients with very severe OSA (AHI >50 events·h–1) or longer treatment duration (>6 months) [95]. These findings suggest that any structural vascular effect of CPAP is likely to be small, delayed, confined to selected patients and depend on the stage of vascular damage.

Effect of non-CPAP therapies on nocturnal BP

Non-CPAP therapies for OSA are heterogeneous and differ in mechanism, evidence base and patient selection. MADs, hypoglossal nerve stimulation (HNS) and upper-airway surgery primarily target upper-airway mechanics, whereas weight-loss interventions address obesity-related pathophysiology and oxygen therapy targets nocturnal hypoxaemia.

MAD therapy has the strongest evidence base among non-PAP therapies. MADs are particularly relevant in patients with mild-to-moderate OSA, favourable craniofacial anatomy and poor CPAP tolerance. In the 2018 systematic review by de Vries et al. [96], MAD therapy reduced daytime systolic and diastolic BP in pooled RCT data, and BP effects appeared broadly comparable to CPAP; however, meta-analysis of 24-h ABPM and night-time BP did not show significant reductions. The subsequent 2024 meta-analysis by Belanche Monterde et al. [97] including only four studies in the pooled BP analysis found no significant overall reduction in systolic or diastolic BP versus control, although meta-regression suggested greater systolic BP reduction at night and with longer treatment duration. Thus, MAD therapy may provide small BP benefits in selected patients.

Weight-loss interventions are unique because they target a major causal driver of both OSA and hypertension. In SURMOUNT-OSA, tirzepatid substantially reduced AHI, body weight, hypoxic burden and systolic BP in adults with moderate-to-severe OSA and obesity [98]. However, BP was not assessed by 24-h ABPM and nocturnal BP was not a dedicated end-point. Broader data on obesity treatment indicate that pharmacological and surgical weight-loss interventions can improve BP and cardiovascular risk factors, but their specific effects on nocturnal BP patterns in OSA remain insufficiently studied [99]. New pharmacological approaches targeting upper-airway neuromuscular function, such as the investigational atomoxetine–aroxybutynin combination AD109, may improve AHI and oxygenation, but effects on nocturnal BP have not yet been established. Because atomoxetine is a norepinephrine-reuptake inhibitor, BP and heart-rate safety require specific attention in future studies [100].

Nocturnal supplemental oxygen has been explored mainly in CPAP-intolerant patients. Oxygen reduces intermittent hypoxaemia without directly correcting upper-airway obstruction, arousals or negative intrathoracic pressure swings. In the SOX CPAP-withdrawal trial, supplemental oxygen attenuated the rise in morning BP after CPAP withdrawal, with lower systolic and diastolic BP compared with sham air [101]. However, more recent meta-analytic evidence showed nonsignificant systolic or diastolic BP changes. Importantly, most included studies only measured office or morning BP rather than ABPM [102].

Evidence for HNS is limited but methodologically stronger than much of the surgical literature because sham-controlled data are available. In the CARDIOSA-12 crossover trial, active HNS did not significantly reduce 24-h systolic BP compared with sham stimulation [103]. Interpretation is complicated because sham stimulation was not physiologically inert and already reduced AHI compared with pretreatment baseline. Current evidence therefore supports HNS as an OSA treatment for selected CPAP-intolerant patients, but not as an established BP-lowering intervention.

The surgical literature is the most heterogeneous. A systematic review of cardiovascular outcomes after OSA surgery found mostly small observational studies with variable procedures, follow-up and outcome definitions; tracheostomy showed the most consistent cardiovascular signal, whereas pharyngeal procedures showed inconsistent effects [104]. Small ABPM studies suggest that successful pharyngeal surgery may reduce nocturnal BP in selected patients; for example, lateral pharyngoplasty reduced nocturnal systolic BP by 7.4 mmHg and nocturnal diastolic BP by 4.2 mmHg at 6 months in a small prospective series [105]. However, these data are limited by small sample size, lack of controls, variable surgical response and potential confounding by changes in OSA severity, weight and medication use [105, 106]. Surgery should therefore be viewed as an anatomical OSA treatment for selected patients rather than as a primary BP-lowering strategy.

CPAP remains the treatment modality with the strongest evidence for lowering nocturnal BP in OSA. MAD use has the most developed BP evidence among non-PAP therapies, but its nocturnal BP effect appears clinically insignificant. Weight-loss pharmacotherapy and bariatric operations may be more relevant for patients in whom obesity is a dominant driver of both OSA and cardiometabolic risk, although nocturnal ABPM data are lacking. While other therapeutic options such as oxygen, HNS and upper-airway surgery are appropriate for selected physiological or anatomical phenotypes, but current evidence does not establish these therapies as BP-lowering interventions.

Role of antihypertensive drugs

Pharmacological antihypertensive therapy is the main treatment approach for hypertension while the treatment of OSA as a secondary cause should be considered in treatment-resistant hypertension according to guidelines. Antihypertensive medication is usually prescribed for morning intake.

One proposed strategy to improve nocturnal BP control is chronotherapy; that is, shifting antihypertensive medication from morning to bedtime. The rationale for this comes from the prognostic importance of nocturnal hypertension and the hypothesis, that evening dosing might lower night-time BP substantially more compared to morning dosing. This concept gained considerable attention after the MAPEC and HYGIA trials, both conducted by the same Spanish research group, which reported striking reductions in cardiovascular events with bedtime dosing; HYGIA also suggested a reduction in all-cause mortality [107, 108]. However, these findings were met with substantial scepticism, with several authors raising concerns about the methodology and the plausibility of the reported effect sizes [109, 110]. Consistent with these concerns, more recent RCTs, including TIME and BedMed, failed to demonstrate a cardiovascular benefit of bedtime dosing [111, 112]. In line with this, a 2023 meta-analysis found that any apparent cardiovascular benefit of bedtime dosing was largely driven by the results of the Spanish research group. After exclusion of these studies, no cardiovascular benefit remained, despite a reduction in mean night-time ambulatory BP of −3.5 mmHg (95% CI −1.32– −5.60) [113].

Of note, these data were derived from hypertensive populations in which sleep-disordered breathing was not systematically assessed. In OSA populations, data on chronotherapy remain limited. Available studies lack long-term follow-up and have primarily assessed BP rather than cardiovascular events as the primary end-point. Overall, current data do not support routine bedtime dosing as a strategy for patients with OSA [114, 115].

Several RCTs have compared different antihypertensive regimens, with or without concomitant CPAP, but these trials were generally small [116, 117]. Although mechanistic and clinical data suggest potential benefits of beta-blockers, renin–angiotensin system inhibitors and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, current evidence is insufficient to recommend any one class for all patients with OSA [118–120].

In clinical practice, antihypertensive therapy in OSA should therefore be individualised and in line with hypertension guidelines. Beyond BP-lowering efficacy, the choice of agent should also consider adverse effects, comorbidities and patient preferences. Even the most effective regimen fails if not taken consistently. The same approach applies to medication timing; in the absence of convincing outcome data, the best dosing schedule is the one the patient is most likely to maintain reliably.

Summary and conclusions

In patients with OSA, abnormal nocturnal BP patterns are common and include both nondipping and overt nocturnal hypertension. These abnormalities are often missed when BP assessments rely only on office measurements. However, use of ABPM is currently limited by device availability, cost and local referral pathways. ABPM should therefore be considered selectively, especially in patients with resistant or poorly controlled hypertension, suspected masked or nocturnal hypertension, or when nocturnal BP assessment is likely to influence management. Conversely, nocturnal hypertension, impaired dipping or resistant hypertension should prompt evaluation for underlying OSA.

CPAP has the most robust evidence for improving nocturnal BP in OSA but is likely most effective when combined with antihypertensive therapy. Adherence is critical. Future research should move beyond average treatment effects and more precisely define the clinical phenotypes and underlying OSA forms and endotypes most likely to benefit from specific OSA therapies in terms of nocturnal BP control. Prospective studies integrating pheno-/endotyping, ABPM and cardiovascular outcomes will be essential to clarify the role of nocturnal BP as both a mechanistic link for cardiovascular outcomes and a therapeutic target in OSA. Ultimately, better recognition of nocturnal BP abnormalities in OSA may help identify patients at increased cardiovascular risk and support a more individualised approach to care.
