Protocol for inducing cystitis via direct instillation of acrolein into the bladder of mice
Authors: Sara A Stuedemann, LaTasha K Crawford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMouse models of bladder inflammation, termed cystitis, can be used to study urinary dysfunction and visceral pain in both sexes. Here, we present a protocol for a mouse model of cystitis that is induced by instilling acrolein directly into the urinary bladder using a transurethral catheter. We provide instructions for preparing the acrolein solution and catheterizing female and male mice. We then detail procedures for confirming the voiding dysfunction phenotype using the void spot assay.Subject areas: Health sciences, Model organisms, Behavior

Before you begin

Rodent models of cystitis are used to better understand the mechanisms underlying urologic pain and urinary dysfunction, yielding critical insight for understanding urologic diseases in patients, such as urologic chronic pelvic pain syndromes (UCPPS), an overarching diagnosis that includes both interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome and chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome. UCPPS affects about 6.5% of both women and men1,2 and is characterized by chronic pain in the lower pelvic region and genitalia, often accompanied by urinary dysfunction such as increased urinary frequency, urgency, and nocturia.3,4 Modeling UCPPS is challenging. There is no consensus in the field as to how UCPPS starts, though there is a strong association with a history of cystitis.2 Rodent models of cystitis can help to fill the fundamental knowledge gaps in the acute-to-chronic pain transition that is critical to advance the field. This protocol describes specific steps for inducing cystitis in female and male mice via direct, intravesicular instillation of a chemical irritant, acrolein, into the lumen of the bladder using a transurethral catheter. The use of this irritant help reduces the confounding factors introduced by infectious agents, many of which can directly influence sensory nerve endings, independently of tissue inflammation.5 We confirm voiding dysfunction with the void spot assay (VSA), which is a widely used and established test for urinary function.6 This protocol can be adjusted to accommodate the use of a different acrolein dosage, alternative chemical irritants, or infectious pathogens. This protocol uses a single acrolein exposure, typically used to evaluate the acute to subacute effects of cystitis. Multiple exposures over time can be used to generate recurrent cystitis models. Further testing is required to establish appropriate dosage and timeline for recurrent or chronic models. If applicable, perform baseline behavioral and function tests prior to inducing cystitis. The required materials and equipment appear in the key resources table below.

Innovation

This protocol advances a published protocol that induced cystitis via intravesicular instillation of acrolein in female mice.7 Cystitis experiments that involve catheterization often use only female mice because it is difficult to catheterize males. This is unfortunate because it is now known that urologic pain like UCPPS is prevalent in male patients.2 A common, closely related rodent model of cystitis utilizes intraperitoneal (IP) injection of cyclophosphamide (CYP), wherein CYP is metabolized to acrolein and then excreted in the urine. The acrolein metabolite then accumulates in the bladder where it induces disease prior to urination. There are limitations to CYP model, such as variability in the rate of CYP metabolism,8 variable and often unknown duration of exposure of the bladder to acrolein, and unknown and uncontrollable admixture with urine.7 There could be potential off-target effects of systemic CYP, as it is also a well-known chemotherapeutic drug and immunosuppressant.9 For these reasons, the acrolein induced cystitis model is advantageous because the bladder is targeted with exposure to a known amount of acrolein. The phenotypes of our intravesicular acrolein model are more consistent and robust, lasting at least 48 h, compared to CYP where voiding dysfunction starts to resolve within 4 h.10 Catheterization of male mice has not been utilized in an acrolein induced cystitis model. Here we present a protocol that helps fill this gap.

Sterilize PE-10 tubing for catheters

Timing: 30–45 min

This step enables customization of catheter length using polyethylene (PE-10) tubing and a blunt needle. If using a commercial catheter, skip this step.

1.Spray down scissor and work area with 70% EtOH (or approved disinfectant).

Spray down scissor and work area with 70% EtOH (or approved disinfectant).

2.Measure and cut PE-10 tubing into 2.5 cm segments and place in empty, sterile dish.

Measure and cut PE-10 tubing into 2.5 cm segments and place in empty, sterile dish.

3.Sterilize the segments in by submerging in 70% EtOH for at least 30 min prior to use. Alternatively, exposure to UV for at least 20 min will sterilize them.

Sterilize the segments in by submerging in 70% EtOH for at least 30 min prior to use. Alternatively, exposure to UV for at least 20 min will sterilize them.

Note: For male mice, it may be advantageous to make the catheter because the PE-10 tubing is more flexible than the commercially available catheters (BD Insyte). This allows for easier navigation of the bend and narrowing of the urethra as it travels beneath the pubic bone and encounters the bulbourethral gland.11 However, published protocols have used the commercially available catheters for both sexes.12

Gather the necessary items and prepare the procedural space

Timing: 10–15 min

These steps prepare the procedural space and the mouse for catheterization. Gathering the necessary items to be within reach allows the investigator to focus and complete the catheterization in an efficient and safe manner.

1.Wipe down procedural area (includes heating pad, induction chamber) and any tools with 70% Ethanol (or approved disinfectant) and let dry.

Timing: 10 min

These steps ensure an appropriate level of anesthesia to minimize possible discomfort and pain responses for the mouse. Mice are also immobilized and do not tend to urinate while under anesthesia, easing catheter placement and ensuring adequate exposure time to the treatment solutions.

CRITICAL: Place a finger and thumb on the lower abdomen, a little below the level of the knees, feel for the bladder (firm, size of a pea), and gently squeeze to empty the bladder. Note: An alternative anesthetic protocol utilizes an IP injection of ketamine/xylazine (90 mg/kg ketamine, 10 mg/kg xylazine, 0.01 mL/g dosage) in lieu of isoflurane. Mice typically remain anesthetized for 45–75 min.

Insert the catheter in female or male mice

Timing: 5–15 min per mouse

These steps detail careful insertion of the transurethral catheter to minimize possible traumatic damage of the urethral tissue. This helps to ensure that control mice exhibit behaviors similar to naïve mice, rather than phenotypes linked to urethral damage from the catheter itself.

Note: This procedure can be practiced on spare mice or recently euthanized mice to help the investigator master the technique.

11.Invert the tubes containing the solution several times to mix. Attach the catheter to the syringe.

Invert the tubes containing the solution several times to mix. Attach the catheter to the syringe.

12.Pull up the solution to fill the catheter hub and then continue to pull up to the desired volume (0.1 mL).

Pull up the solution to fill the catheter hub and then continue to pull up to the desired volume (0.1 mL).

Note: This will remove any air prior to instilling the solution. Slightly push the plunger down to confirm solution is coming out of the catheter tip.

13.Apply a thin coat of surgical lubricant along the length of the catheter.

Apply a thin coat of surgical lubricant along the length of the catheter.

Expected outcomes

The expected outcome of this protocol is the development of cystitis, characterized by disruption of the urothelium that lines the bladder lumen, inflammation of the bladder wall, pathologic urination (voiding) patterns, and bladder pain.15,16 Evaluation of H&E staining of control (Figure 3A. i and ii) and cystitis (Figure 3B. i and ii) bladders confirm bladder inflammation and presence of edema in the suburothelium causing increased wall thickness (black brackets) at 48 h. Additionally, due to increased wall thickness, cystitis bladders weighed more (Figure 3C.). The void spot assay (VSA) is an assay of voiding dysfunction.6,13 It is used in this protocol to confirm the presence of a cystitis phenotype in experimental mice. In contrast, control mice do not develop voiding dysfunction and their post instillation VSA measures remain comparable to their pre-instillation baseline. Statistical analyses and graphs are generated in GraphPad Prism 9. A significant increase in the number of microvoids, void spots that are 0.1 cm2 or less, indicates voiding dysfunction, a confirmed feature of cystitis.16 Figures 4A and 4B are representative images of VSA papers from a control and cystitis mouse at 24 h post instillation. There is a significant increase in total urine count in cystitis mice (F (1,14)=10.12, Time x Treatment, p=0.0013; Treatment, p=0.0067; Time, p<0.0001; 2-way ANOVA) when compared to control mice at 24 h (p<0.0001, Sidak’s multiple comparison test) and compared to their own baseline (p<0.0001, Sidak’s multiple comparison test) (Figure 4C). In contrast, control mice do not develop voiding dysfunction and their post instillation VSA measures remain comparable to their pre-instillation baseline. When evaluating treatment effects on the population of urine spots organized by size, there is a significant increase in pathologic microvoids (0–0.1 cm2) after cystitis (F (7,112)=49.57, Bin area x Treatment, p<0.00001; Bin area, p<0.0001; Treatment, p<0.00001, 2-way ANOVA; 0–0.1 cm2 bin cystitis vs. Control, p <0.0001, Sidak’s multiple comparisons test; Figure 4D). For this cystitis model, voiding dysfunction typically peaks at 24–48 h and has resolved by day 7. Other cystitis phenotypes may persist longer.

Limitations

This protocol induces cystitis via direct instillation of acrolein into the bladder, providing an opportunity to study cystitis pathology and cystitis-induced pain mechanisms. There are a few limitations to this protocol. While we try to mitigate possible mouse-to-mouse variability when inducing cystitis, we cannot fully account for biological variability in the response to acrolein. Strain, age and sex can affect voiding behavior17 so it is important to know the baseline for your mice and to be consistent between experimental groups. Additionally, very small or young mice are harder to catheterize.

Potential solution

•Ensure the bladder is empty prior to instilling the solution. If the bladder is full, any added solution will reflux out.
