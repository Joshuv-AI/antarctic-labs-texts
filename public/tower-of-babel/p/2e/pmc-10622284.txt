Is a Preoperative MRI Scan Necessary for Acute Achilles Tendon Rupture?
Authors: Xiang Geng, Xiong‐gang Yang, Zhao‐lin Teng, Xing‐xi Hu, Chen Wang, Chao Zhang, Li Chen, Jia‐zhang Huang, Xu Wang, Xin Ma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDifferent treatment methods have been developed for acute Achilles tendon rupture (ATR), including conservative treatment, minimally invasive or transdermal surgery, and open surgery, and there is no consensus about which method is superior. It is important to clarify the presence of Achilles tendon (AT) degeneration, the rupture site, and the rupture shape before surgery to determine whether minimally invasive or open surgery should be selected, thereby reducing the re‐rupture rate following acute ATR. The aim of this study was to investigate the diagnostic value of MRI in identifying the presence of AT degeneration, the rupture site, and the rupture shape for acute closed ATR.MethodsFrom January 2016 to December 2019, patients with acute closed ATR who had undergone repair surgery were retrospectively enrolled. All patients received MRI examination, and the distance between the insertion site and broken end and the rupture shape (types I, II, and III) were independently determined by two observers. Then, the stump of the AT was exposed during the operation. The rupture site and rupture shape were recorded and compared and analyzed with the MRI results. Consistency analyses (using Cohen's kappa coefficient or intraclass correlation coefficient‐ICC) and calculation of diagnostic performance indexes were, respectively, conducted to evaluate the diagnostic value of the MRI.ResultsThis study included 47 consecutive patients with acute ATR, with an average age of 38.4 years. Among them, 40 were male, and seven were female. The intraoperative exploration demonstrated a total of 34 (72.3%), 10 (21.3%), and three (6.4%) patients with type I, II, and III ruptures, respectively. The average distance between the insertion site and the proximal broken end measured intraoperatively was 4.07 ± 1.57 cm. High or excellent consistencies were found for ATR classifications (kappa: 0.739–0.770, p < 0.001) and rupture sites (ICC: 0.962–0.979, p < 0.001) between two observers and between observers 1 and 2 and intraoperative findings. Tendinopathy was identified in 22 patients by MRI and confirmed during surgery.ConclusionsMRI scanning of acute closed ATR can help determine whether there is degeneration of the AT, as well as the location and shape of the rupture, which can guide the selection of the optimal operation method for orthopedic surgeons. Therefore, it is necessary to take preoperative MRI scans for patients with acute Achilles tendon ruptures.Keywords: Acute Achilles Tendon Rupture, Classification, MRI, Tendinopathy

Introduction

Although the Achilles tendon is the strongest tendon in the human body, acute Achilles tendon rupture (ATR) accounts for 20% of tendon ruptures, with an overall incidence of approximately 9–37 per 100,000. 1 , 2 , 3 Among these ATR patients, 75–80% are male, and most are aged between 37 and 42 years. 1 , 2 , 3 In 2012, a systematic review by Soroceanu et al. 4 found two peak ages of onset of acute ATR: 25–40 years and around 60 years. High‐energy sports injuries are the main cause of the first peak of acute ATR, while acute ATR in patients over 60 years of age is mostly due to low‐energy injuries, which cause spontaneous ATR in the case of chronic Achilles tendon degeneration. For patients over 60 years, therefore, the re‐rupture rate is very high even with surgical treatment.

Over the past two decades, different treatment methods have been developed for acute ATR, including conservative treatment, minimally invasive or transdermal surgery, and open surgery. There is no consensus about which method is superior. Open surgery is often associated with infection, wound healing problems, gastrocnemental nerve injury, and scar formation. The literature reports the complication rate of open surgery as around 34%, which is much higher than for conservative treatment. 2 , 5 , 6 In 1977, Ma et al. 7 first compared the outcomes of percutaneous minimally invasive surgery with open surgery, demonstrating that complications can be significantly reduced by minimally invasive surgery. Then, several small incision technologies, such as the Dresden technology, 8 the Kakiuchi technology, 9 and the Achillon 10 , 11 technology, were introduced, which could further reduce wound complications but had no effect on postoperative re‐rupture rates, gastrocnemental nerve injury, and American Orthopaedic Foot and Ankle Society rating for return to exercise.

The re‐rupture rate is significant for conservative treatment and minimally invasive and open surgery. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 In 2019, a systematic review by Wu et al. 16 summarized the characteristics of 2060 patients from 29 studies, reporting an overall re‐rupture rate of 5%. In a systematic review, Reda et al. 17 found that surgical treatment can reduce the re‐rupture rate relative to conservative treatment, but the distance between the broken ends and the stitching technique affects the postoperative re‐rupture rate. Additionally, in 2019, a survey on the treatment strategy for acute ATR among Dutch physicians was reported by Olivier et al., 18 showing that the gap size between the broken ends will affect the therapeutic effect, so it is necessary to confirm the exact site of rupture and the distance between the broken ends before surgery. Re‐rupture after an operation for acute closed ATR may be related to insufficient contact and tension of the intraoperative suture. In the presence of tendinopathy or degeneration of the Achilles tendon itself, failure due to insufficient suture retention is more likely. 10 , 11 , 19 Therefore, it is speculated that the minimally invasive technique, due to the inability to directly observe the broken end, cannot determine whether the Achilles tendon has degenerated, clear the hematoma, or maintain the consistency of the Achilles tendon fibers during the suture process, which negatively affects the healing of the Achilles tendon after minimally invasive surgery, affects the strength of the reconstructed tendon, and causes further breaking. Identifying the presence of Achilles tendon degeneration, the rupture site, and the rupture shape before surgery is recommended to determine whether minimally invasive or open surgery should be selected, thereby reducing the re‐rupture rate.

MRI is a commonly used and effective method for diagnosing Achilles tendon diseases and can also determine the location of an acute Achilles tendon rupture, the shape of the Achilles tendon, and distance between the broken ends. 20 , 21 , 22 Karjalainen et al. 23 demonstrated that the function of patients after acute ATR was moderately correlated with MRI imaging indicators. Tam et al. 24 found that the pathological state of the Achilles tendon on MRI scans was correlated with post‐treatment function. To sum up, we speculate that MRI scanning can not only determine the location and shape of acute ATR and whether there is degeneration of the Achilles tendon but can also predict whether it is suitable for minimally invasive techniques through MRI scanning images, with the goal of reducing the postoperative re‐rupture rate.

Hence, the current study was carried out with the following aims: (i) to investigate the diagnostic accuracy of preoperative MRI for the presence of AT degeneration, tge rupture location (i.e., distance from the insertion to the rupture of tendon), and the shape of the rupture (types I–III according to rupture shape) for acute closed ATR, with the intraoperative vision as the “gold standard”; and (ii) to evaluate the value of preoperative MRI in guiding the choice of operation method.

Materials and Methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and upon attaining ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Huashan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval number: [2022] Provisional Examination No. 069). All subjects provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

From January 2016 to December 2019, patients diagnosed with acute ATR who had undergone repair surgery in our institute were retrospectively recruited for our study. Patients would be eligible for inclusion according to the following criteria: (i) patients definitely diagnosed as having ATR based on imaging (B‐scan ultrasound and MRI) and clinical examinations; (ii) patients with injury history of the Achilles tendon within the acute stage (within 2 weeks) 25 ; and (iii) patients who are 18 years of older.

The following patients were excluded: (i) patients who had undergone revision repair for re‐rupture of the Achilles tendon or other reasons; (ii) patients with previous surgical history of the Achilles tendon; and (iii) patients who had not agreed to take the MRI scan.

MRI Scanning

All eligible participants received an MRI scan of the ankle joint. An MRI was performed using 3.0‐T systems with sagittal and transaxial protondensity‐weighted turbo spin echo (3.0T GE Signa VH/I 3.0‐T, GE Healthcare, GE Asian Hub, Shanghai, China; slice thickness, 3 mm; slice gap, 0 mm; pixel matrix, 514 × 410; scanning sequence: SE T1WI, T2WI, FSET1WI, FSE T2WI). SE T1WI and T2WI sequences were selected for this research. The conventional axial, sagittal, and coronal scans were obtained. All patients were instructed to take a relaxed ankle posture during the examination. The rupture location (i.e., the distance between the tendon insertion and the rupture of the tendon), rupture shapes, and signal changes were all assessed and recorded independently by two individual authors. Based on the MRI presentation, a novel classification was introduced to describe the rupture shape, including type I (transverse type), type II (sleeve type), and type III (avulsion type). The representative cases for the three types are available in Figures 1, 2, 3. In the transverse type, the Achilles tendon is predominately torn in the shape of the cauda equina, and the two broken ends are completely separated, with a hematoma between two ends, showing a low signal on the T1 image and a high signal on the T2 image (Figure 1). Tortuosity of the Achilles tendon and decreased tension are visible in the sleeve type, without an obvious stump gap or hematoma (Figure 2). In avulsion type, the Achilles tendon was almost or even completely severed from its insertion point, with or without an avulsion fracture at the insertion point (Figure 3). A hematoma would be formed between the broken end and the retrocalcaneal tubercle. According to the MRI assessment criterion for degeneration of the tendon in ATR proposed by Bäcker et al., 26 the signal change was assessed, and the presence of degenerative tendinopathy was recorded. In Figure 4, a case of ATR with tendinopathy at both broken ends, especially at the distal end, was presented. Intraoperatively, the AT can be observed with color change, stiffness, and partial calcification.

Operation Procedure

All ATR cases were treated by operative repair in a prone position, with their feet hanging freely at the end of the table. According to the MRI scan results, the distance between the broken end and the insertion point was determined, and an arc incision with a length of approximately 3 cm was made slightly medial to the midline at the level of the rupture. The deep fascia was incised, and the detailed rupture shape and the distance between the proximal broken end and the insertion site were recorded. Then, the surgeon sutured the tendon using the Bunnell or Kcrachow technique according to the surgeon's personal preference while attempting to achieve adequate tension when the foot was manually dorsiflexed. For avulsion fracture (type III), we usually use an anchor to help fix the ruptured end to the retrocalcaneal tubercle. Thereafter, the deep fascia and the subcutaneous tissue were closed with a No. 2‐0 Vicryl suture, and the skin was closed with a No. 4‐0 Prolene suture.

During the operation, the exact rupture shape, the distance between rupture ends, and the presence of tendinopathy were identified following exposure of the tendon. Visual changes in tendinopathy include thickening of tendon diameter, uneven surface, yellowing of color, hardening of texture, decreased elasticity, and degenerative tearing.

Statistical Analysis

All recorded categorical variables were described as sample sizes with percentages, and continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation. The consistency of rupture shapes and rupture location (distance from the insertion site to the broken end) between two observers was tested utilizing Cohen's kappa coefficient and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), respectively. Then, the consistencies of rupture shapes and rupture location between observer 1/observer 2 and intraoperative descriptions were also analyzed using Cohen's kappa coefficient and ICC, respectively.

To depict the performance of the MRI on diagnosing the rupture shape before the operation, the indexes including true positive (TP), true negative (TN), false positive (FP), false negative (FN), sensitivity, specificity, positive prediction value (PPV), negative prediction value (NPV), accuracy (ACC), positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), false positive rate (FPR), false negative rate (FNR), and Youden's index were calculated. We also generated the Sankey plot to depict the rupture shapes diagnosed by observer 1, observer 2, and intraoperative findings. Additionally, the Bland–Altman plot was generated to qualitatively display the consistency of distance between the insertion site and the broken end.

All the above statistical analyses and plotting procedures were conducted using R language version 4.0.5 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p‐value of less than 0.05 was defined as statistical significance. Kappa and ICC coefficients represent the degree of consistency, which ranges from 0 to 1. Value ranges (0.0, 0.4), (0.4, 0.6), (0.6, 0.8), and (0.8, 1.0) indicate poor, general, high and excellent consistencies, respectively.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

A flowchart of patient screening is shown in Figure 5. Of the initially yielded 228 patients, 56 were excluded from enrolling in the study for the following reasons: (i) they had undergone revision repair surgery for re‐rupture (n = 10); (ii) they had been diagnosed as having an open rupture (n = 9); (iii) they had previous surgical history on AT (n = 2); (iv) they were aged under 18 (n = 1); and (v) they had injury history beyond the acute stage (>2 weeks, n = 38). Afterwards, a total of 125 patients were excluded in the stage of MRI scanning, with the following reasons: (i) the patient had not agreed to wait for MRI examination (n = 112); and (ii) the patient had inadequate MRI scanning (n = 13). Forty‐seven consecutive cases of acute ATR clinically diagnosed and treated with repair surgery were finally eligible for inclusion in the study, with an average age of 38.40 ± 8.64 years. The baseline characteristics of the included patients are available in Table 1. The sex distribution amounted to 40 men (85.1%) and seven women(14.9%). The mean body mass index was 24.70 ± 2.80 kg m−2. There were 25 (53.2%) cases on the left side and 22 (46.8%) cases on the right side. The presence of tendinopathy was identified in 22 patients before the operation according to the crieteria of Bäcker et al., 26 and all tendinopathy were visually confirmed during the operation. The average times from injury to MRI examination and surgery were 1.89 ± 2.65 and 3.81 ± 3.44 days, respectively.

Consistency Analyses for Achilles Tendon Rupture Classifications and Rupture Site

According to MRI appearance and rupture shape, observer 1 screened out 31 (66.0%), 13 (27.7%), and 3 (6.4%) participants with types I, II, and III ruptures, respectively, but observer 2 screened out 31 (6.0%), 12 (25.5%), and 4 (8.5%) patients with types I, II, and III ruptures. The results of the consistency analyses for rupture classifications are shown in Table 2. The intraoperative examination demonstrated a total of 34 (72.3%), 10 (21.3%), and 3 (6.4%) patients with types I, II, and III ruptures. High consistencies were found between two observers (kappa = 0.739, p < 0.001***), between observer 1 and intraoperative findings (kappa = 0.769, p < 0.001***), and between observer 2 and intraoperative findings (kappa = 0.770, p < 0.001***). In Figure 6A–C, the Sankey diagrams for matching the relationship of ATR classifications between two observers, between observer 1 and intraoperative findings, and between observer 2 and intraoperative findings are presented. Table 3 shows the diagnosing performance indexes for MRI on the ATR classifications. Generally, excellent diagnosis performance was demonstrated in both observers, with sensitivity ranging between 0.882 and 1.000, specificity ranging between 0.892 and 1.000, accuracy between 0.894 and 1.000, and Youden's index ranging between 0.792 and 1.000.

Discussion

Main Findings

In this study, all acute ATR cases were evaluated using preoperative MRI for rupture shape, rupture site, and signal change, and the results were compared with intraoperative findings. The main results demonstrated that MRI could help determine the presence of Achilles tendon degeneration, the rupture site, and the rupture shape before surgery, with relatively high accuracy.

Role of MRI Scanning for Acute Closed Achilles Tendon Rupture

Over the past 20 years, different treatment methods have been used for acute closed ATR, including conservative treatment, minimally invasive or percutaneous surgery, and open surgery. The advantages and disadvantages of these methods remain controversial. Numerous studies suggest that minimally invasive surgery can reduce postoperative complications. However, there is no difference in the rate of re‐rupture after surgery between minimally invasive and open surgery. Understanding how to reduce complications, especially postoperative re‐rupture, is critical. 5 , 6 The re‐rupture of acute closing ATR may be related to insufficient contact and tension of intraoperative sutures. In 2019, Oliver et al. 18 performed a survey on the treatment selection for acute ATR among Dutch physicians, demonstrating that the gap size between the rupture ends affects the therapeutic effect. Therefore, Oliver et al. suggests it is necessary to determine the distance between the broken ends before surgery. In Amlang et al., 27 a novel sonographic classification system according to the location of rupture revealed that the contact of the tendon ends in 20° plantar flexion, and the structures between the tendon ends were proposed to have a total of five types and seven subtypes. Accordingly, the authors recommended graded and differentiated treatments for these types of acute ATR. Cao et al. 28 investigated the relationship between the ATR site (the distance from the calcaneal insertion to the proximal broken end) and the postoperative outcome (Achilles tendon rupture score) through ultrasound, demonstrating that a proximal rupture had a negative influence on the postoperative outcome. To sum up, the rupture site and shape must be comprehensively considered before surgery to obtain a satisfactory postoperative outcome.

MRI is a common technique for diagnosing Achilles diseases and can help determine the location, shape, and distance between broken ends for acute closing ATR. 20 , 21 , 22 , 29 Park et al. 20 investigated the site of acute ATR using MRI, reporting a mean distance of 6.4 cm between the insertion site and the proximal stump end. Similarly, Pierre‐Jerome et al. 29 used MRI to review the anatomy and biomechanics of AT and the imaging characteristics of overuse tendinopathies. We have conducted research to prove that MRI can provide preoperative planning value for minimally invasive surgery techniques. According to Bäcker's assessment criteria, 22 patients were found to have tendinopathy by MRI before the operation, all of which were confirmed during surgery. Moreover, MRI was proven to effectively determine the location of broken ends and ATR classifications, with high or excellent consistency between observers and accuracy between observer and intraoperative findings. Given the high accuracy and reliability of preoperative MRI in determining the rupture location, the ATR classification, and the occurrence of Achilles tendinopathy, it holds great potential in guiding preoperative surgical decision‐making, particularly for selecting minimally invasive procedures. Optimal surgical methods based on MRI findings can effectively mitigate the risk of long‐term postoperative complications, especially postoperative rupture. However, further confirmation of the role of preoperative MRI in this regard requires large‐scale clinical observational studies in the future.

Strengths and Limitations

A rigorous diagnostic test was completed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of MRI scans for the rupture site and rupture type, and the diagnosis performance was assessed using the ICC coefficient and other indexes. However, this study has several limitations that should be pointed out. First, there was a limited sample size for the randomly selected patients with MRI scans, which may lead to insufficient statistical power. Second, as many previous studies have found that B‐scan ultrasound has high sensitivity and accuracy, and can assess the degree of Achilles tendon degeneration and changes in viscoelasticity, it is necessary to compare the results of MRI in the current study with other imaging examinations such as B‐scan ultrasound Moreover, comparative study of MRI and B‐scan ultrasound is helpful to improve the diagnosis and treatment strategy for acute Achilles tendon ruptures. Finally, the slice thickness of the MRI and the position of ankle joint may cause some bias on the measured distance between rupture ends. However, the slice thickness (3 mm) of the MRI equipment is within an acceptable range comparing to the average distance from insertion to broken end (4.07 ± 1.57 cm). To overcome the influence of ankle position, all patients were scanned with the foot in a relatively relaxed position.

Conclusions

Using MRI scanning combined with intraoperative exploration, it can be concluded that MRI scanning of acute closed ATR can help determine whether there is degeneration of the AT and the location and shape of the rupture. For cuff‐like ruptures, ruptures with degeneration of the Achilles tendon, and avulsion ruptures at the insertion point, the surgical method should be carefully selected.
