Associations of proteomic and epigenetic aging clocks with Alzheimer's disease phenotypes: An exploratory analysis
Authors: Cindy David Sarmento, Gabin Drouard, Toni T Saari, Aino Aaltonen, Aino Heikkinen, Mikaela Hukkanen, Teemu Palviainen, Sanna‐Kaisa Herukka, Tarja Kokkola, Sari Kärkkäinen, FinnGen, Aarno Palotie, Valtteri Julkunen, Heiko Runz, Jaakko Kaprio, Miina Ollikainen, Eero Vuoksimaa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONProteomic aging clocks detect disease‐related systemic and organ‐specific changes and are easily accessible by minimally invasive blood draws. However, their potential in Alzheimer's disease (AD) assessment remains unestablished.METHODSWe investigated associations of proteomic and epigenetic clocks with AD‐related blood‐based biomarkers and cognitive tests. Omics were generated from blood samples of 153 cognitively unimpaired individuals (average age 62 years); blood biomarkers and cognition were measured approximately nine years after.RESULTSProteomic clocks explained up to 23% of variance in cognitive and biomarker measures not explained by epigenetics. Accelerated systemic and brain‐specific proteomic aging were linked to poorer cognition and higher levels of plasma neurofilament light chain. Exploratory interaction analyses suggested weaker proteomic aging‐cognition associations in individuals with higher genetic liability for diabetes.DISCUSSIONOur study illustrates the potential of plasma proteomic clocks in detecting AD‐related phenotypes. However, co‐morbidities possibly constitute confounding factors, compromising the performance of proteomic aging models.Keywords: aging, Alzheimer's disease, cognition, epigenetics, plasma biomarkers, proteomics

BACKGROUND

Due to increasingly older populations, it has been estimated that over 150 million people will be affected by dementia globally in 2050, the equivalent of a 166% increase in just 30 years. 1 Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia. As the current practice often results in late diagnosis and a long lifetime spent with severe disability, AD represents a great burden to individuals, their families, and public health. 2 Early diagnosis ensures opportunities for future financial and legal planning, and for early medical interventions to delay cognitive decline. 2 , 3

AD is a highly complex and multifactorial neurodegenerative disease greatly dependent on genetics and potentially modifiable risk factors, with age recognized as the strongest risk factor. 4 Aging‐related molecular changes are typically interconnected, leading to physiological repercussions across several organs, and consequently, to the co‐occurrence of several diseases. 5 In recent years, advances in different omics have enabled the development of so‐called aging clocks. These models infer biological age by summarizing aging‐related molecular features and have successfully detected accelerated molecular aging associated with dementia. 6 Some DNA methylation (DNAm) ‐based epigenetic clocks, such as DNAm PhenoAge and GrimAge, have shown significant associations between accelerated aging and poorer cognition as well as several AD hallmarks. 7 , 8 However, findings in AD literature remain inconsistent, with multiple studies failing to find significant associations with AD phenotypes. 9

Epigenetic clocks currently represent the most widely used approach to assess biological aging, but newly developed proteomic clocks hold great promise in AD research. 10 , 11 The characteristic accumulation of amyloid‐β (Aβ) and phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) aggregates in the brains of AD patients results from disruptions in protein homeostasis networks. This should be reflected in the blood proteomes and, thus, detectable by proteomic clocks. 12 , 13 More recently, studies have introduced organ‐specific proteomic clocks which evaluate the heterogeneity of organ aging and its impact on morbidity. 14 , 15 , 16 Overall, these have provided further evidence that molecular aging processes are interconnected, as most fast‐aging organs are associated with multiple diseases. 14 , 15 , 16 Regarding dementia and AD, in addition to accelerated proteomic brain aging, 14 , 16 fast‐aging immune 15 and vascular 16 systems have also been associated with a higher incidence risk.

The 2024 Alzheimer's Association Workgroup's revised guidelines emphasized a more comprehensive approach to AD risk stratification to tackle the complexity added by common co‐pathologies. 3 Combining existing AD biomarkers, lifestyle and health data with molecular biomarkers of aging, including those captured by aging clocks, could provide better insights into an individual's pathophysiology of AD before occurrence of cognitive symptoms, which is essential to early diagnosis. However, to our knowledge, the utility of the recently developed proteomic aging clocks in predicting early cognitive impairment and state‐of‐the‐art AD‐related plasma biomarkers has not yet been evaluated in cognitively unimpaired individuals.

In this study, we leveraged an extensively phenotyped population‐based sample of 153 individuals without a clinical diagnosis of AD to explore the extent to which proteomic and epigenetic aging clocks may explain variation in AD‐related phenotypes. Under an exploratory, mostly assumption‐free study design, we investigated associations of aging clocks with blood‐based biomarkers (BBBs) and cognitive screening tools. Additionally, we investigated common dementia risk factors, namely hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and diabetes, as potential modifiers. We hypothesized that proteomic aging clocks, particularly those specific to organs involved in AD, may better explain variability in BBBs and cognitive performance than epigenetic clocks.

METHODS

Study design and participants

The population‐based Older Finnish Twin Cohort was established in 1974 with the recruitment of all Finnish same‐sex twins born before 1958 residing in Finland. 17 The baseline questionnaire had an approximate 90% participation rate, and follow‐up data collections included questionnaires on health‐related topics and in‐person visits (Figure 1). Based on a 2011 questionnaire, twin pairs born between 1945 and 1957 discordant for blood pressure were identified and invited to participate in the Essential Hypertension Epigenetics (EH‐Epi) study. 18 From 2012 to 2015, participants visited the clinic, where blood samples were collected. From these, multi‐omics data were generated, including the epigenetic and proteomic data used in this study. Additionally, genetic analyses were performed to compute polygenic risk scores (PRS) of dementia‐related traits. Weight and height were also measured at the clinic, that is, at the time of blood collection. In 2023, these individuals were invited to participate in the TWINGEN study. 19

Flow chart of sample selection. Created in BioRender (https://BioRender.com/14pw0ri).

In TWINGEN, exclusion criteria were a diagnosis of AD or any other disorder affecting cognition, based on health registry data and confirmation via telephone interview. 19 Individuals were included in the study independently of their co‐twin's eligibility. The protocol included health questionnaires, telephone‐ and computer‐based cognitive testing, blood pressure measurements, and blood draws. These blood samples were used to quantify AD‐related plasma biomarkers.

Outcomes: Cognition and AD‐related biomarkers

AD‐related outcomes are summarized in Figure 2 and Table S1 (Supplementary Material 2, SM2). Total scores from two dementia screening instruments were used as cognitive measures. These included a re‐modified version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS‐m3) 20 and the computer‐administered tool cCOG. Both have been validated for early detection of cognitive impairment and AD against the gold‐standard, in‐person administered AD screening battery. 20 Additionally, reaction time (RT) was measured in the cCOG battery. This subtest was not included in the cCOG total score (Global Cognitive Score [GCS]) but as a separate non‐AD‐specific outcome sensitive to general aging.

Among the BBBs considered in this study, plasma p‐tau217 corresponds to an early‐changing Core 1 biomarker, as defined by the Alzheimer's Association workgroup in 2024. 3 It is specific to AD neuropathological changes, reflecting both Aβ and tau pathologies. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) and glial fibrillary acidic proteins (GFAP), indicative of neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation, respectively, are non‐specific to AD, but suggested as possible prognostic markers. 3 BBBs were quantified in TWINGEN blood samples using single‐molecule assay (Simoa) HD‐X Analyzer (Quanterix, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA). 19

Explanatory variables: Epigenomic and proteomic aging clocks

DNA methylation (DNAm; Illumina Infinium Human Methylation 450K Beadchip) and proteomic data (Olink Explore 3072) were obtained from EH‐Epi blood samples, collected eight to 10 years before the TWINGEN study, as described elsewhere. 21 , 22 , 23 DNAm and proteomic data were processed for quality control (Extended Methods in Supplementary Material 1, SM1) and used to compute epigenetic and proteomic age estimates, respectively.

The following epigenetic clock estimates were computed as described in detail by Kankaanpää et al. 22 : Horvath, 24 Hannum, 25 PhenoAge, 8 GrimAge, 26 GrimAge2, 27 and DunedinPACE. 28 For the first four clocks, versions retrained on CpG principal components were used to improve reliability, as suggested by Higgins‐Chen et al. 29 To account for variation in blood cell composition, epigenetic age estimates were defined as the residuals resulting from the regression of DNAm age onto blood cell count estimates for sorted CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, B cells, natural killer cells, monocytes, and neutrophils (Extended Methods, SM1).

We also employed recently developed second‐generation systemic and organ‐specific proteomic aging clocks, trained on mortality or morbidity instead of chronological age. 14 These estimates correlate with chronological age and have been found to outperform other clocks in predicting risk of organ‐specific diseases. 10 , 11 , 14 System‐wide proteomic clocks included Proteomic Aging Clock (PAC), 10 Healthspan Proteomic Score (HPS), 11 and the conventional second‐generation systemic clock, 14 which were estimated according to the developers’ instructions. In contrast with other clocks, which predict chronological age or mortality risk, HPS informs about the likelihood of remaining healthy, that is, not developing any disease included in the healthspan definition (e.g., cancer, heart failure, and dementia) nor dying within 10 years from baseline. 11 Here, we used the estimate 1 − HPS to depict mortality hazard, in conformity with other clocks.

We selected organ‐specific clocks relevant to organs either directly (brain) or potentially involved in AD pathology, considering previously reported associations with different dementia types (arterial system, heart, 16 immune system, and intestine 15 ) or relation to major AD risk factors (diabetes – pancreas). The kidney‐specific clock was included due to evidence that accelerated kidney aging has effects across multiple organs and is associated with diverse age‐related diseases. 15 Additionally, over‐representation analysis of gene sets (Extended Methods and Figures S1–S9 in SM1; Tables S2–S11) motivated the inclusion of the adipose‐ and liver‐specific clocks due to significant enrichment in gene ontology terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways relative to glucose transport and metabolism (Table S2), acute inflammatory responses (Table S9), and type II diabetes (T2D; Table S11).

Possible modifiers: Dementia risk factors

We retrieved information on years of education, self‐reported diabetes status (yes/no), measured glucose and cholesterol levels (elevated/normal) from available TWINGEN questionnaires. We also gathered continuous blood pressure measures taken in the context of the TWINGEN study and self‐reported values when measures were not available. Additionally, we calculated PRSs for AD, educational attainment (EA), T2D, fasting glucose and insulin, coronary artery disease, low‐density lipoprotein, and systolic and diastolic blood pressures (see Extended Methods, SM1; Table S12).

Generalized Estimating Equation models were used for each step to account for correlations within the data due to twins’ familial relatedness. In all models, covariates included sex assigned at birth obtained from a population registry, chronological age at first blood sampling, and time in years between first blood sampling and cognitive testing (DiffAge). Two separate sensitivity analyses were performed with years of education or binary apolipoprotein E (APOE) ‐ε4 carrier status as additional covariates in univariate models to examine the potential confounding effects of education level and the APOE‐ε4 risk allele, respectively. All biological age estimates and outcomes were normalized using rank‐based inverse‐normal transformation to unify the scales and correct distributional skewness characteristic of plasma biomarkers (Figures S10,S11). Additionally, continuous interactors were scaled to mean of zero and standard deviation of one. All outcomes and clock estimates were missing in less than 4% of idividuals, with the exception of RT (20% missingness, Table 1). To account for this, only complete cases were considered in each model. We reported Z‐values, 95% confidence intervals, and p‐values (α = 0.05) adjusted for multiple testing with the Benjamini‐Hochberg method in step (1), and nominal p‐values in steps (2) and (3). Additionally, β coefficients representing change in outcome in units of standard deviations (SDs) per SD change in aging clock were also reported.

Interaction analyses were performed to test the potential effect of genetic predisposition to AD and EA, as well as diabetes, hypertension, and hypercholesterolemia—common dementia risk factors—on associations between AD‐related outcomes and aging clocks. Due to statistical limitations, nominal, rather than adjusted, p‐values were used. Therefore, interaction analyses were regarded as explorative, and results should be cautiously interpreted. Possible interactors considered in the analyses were described in section 2.4. and summarized in Table S1. To correct for population structure, PRSs were regressed onto the first ten genetic principal components and scaled in subsequent analyses. Continuous PRSs were used in interaction models but, for result visualisation purposes and ease of interpretation of significant interactions, individuals were stratified into two PRS groups: below‐ and above‐average. Biological age estimates were plotted against outcome estimates, fitting separate regression lines for each stratum.

Additionally, multivariable analyses were conducted to evaluate how much more information proteomic clocks collectively provide over epigenetic clocks in explaining variance in each outcome (Extended Methods, SM1). The conventional systemic second‐generation proteomic clock was removed from this analysis due to high collinearity with other clocks (Table S13, Figure S12). Reduced models (epigenetic clocks only) and full models (epigenetic plus proteomic clocks) were adjusted for the same covariates (sex, age, and DiffAge). The proportion of variance explained by the full models that is incrementally explained beyond the reduced models was reported.

RESULTS

We investigated a total of 153 individuals, with an average age at blood sampling for omics data generation of approximately 62 years. Cognition and BBBs were measured on average nine years later (Table 1).

Associations of cognitive tests and BBBs with aging clocks were reported in Figure 3 (additional results in Figure S13, Table S14). Given the status of AD as an age‐related disease, we expected accelerated aging detected by any of the clocks (higher estimates) to associate with lower cognitive performance in cCOG and TICS‐m3 (negative coefficients), higher RT, and increased levels of BBBs (positive coefficients). Approximately 90% of significant associations detected involved proteomic clocks. Only one epigenetic clock, DunedinPACE, associated with an AD‐related phenotype, specifically, decelerated pace of aging associated with higher levels of GFAP (β = ‐0.29, adjusted p < 0.0001). Accelerated proteomic brain aging was associated with lower cognitive performance in both TICS‐m3 (β = ‐0.27, adjusted p = 0.0276) and cCOG (β = ‐0.26, adjusted p = 0.0296), and higher plasma NfL levels (β = 0.32, adjusted p = 0.0021). NfL showed the highest number of significant associations with biological aging, in particular, positive associations with multiple organ‐specific and system‐wide proteomic clocks. Only the adipose‐specific clock was significantly associated with p‐tau217 (β = 0.17, adjusted p = 0.0406) and none with RT.

In univariate models additionally adjusted for education level, associations with GCS and TICS‐m3 were attenuated and did not pass multiple testing correction, while still nominally significant (Figure S14, Table S17). Similarly, adjustment for APOE‐ε4 carrier status attenuated the p‐tau217–adipose clock association (adjusted p = 0.06; Figure S15, Table S18). Of note, although associations of education with cognitive performance were nominally significant (Figure 4.2), we found no evidence that education or genetic risk of AD significantly modulate associations between aging clocks and cognition/BBBs in interaction analyses (Tables S15–S16).

Multivariable analysis revealed that including both proteomic and epigenetic clocks accounted for additional 8 to 23% of the variance remaining after epigenetic clocks and covariates alone (Table 2). Sensitivity analysis showed only minor decreases in model fit when dropping each proteomic clock (< 8%; Table S19), failing to identify one with a particularly greater contribution to all outcomes.

DISCUSSION

Our study leveraged an extensively phenotyped population‐based sample of individuals without a clinical diagnosis of AD to unravel potential associations between molecular aging and state‐of‐the‐art markers of cognition and AD, as well as possible modifiers of these associations.

The results suggested that accelerated systemic and brain‐specific proteomic aging are implicated in lower cognitive performance, which was expected given prior reports of associations with cognitive decline 31 and increased risk of all‐cause dementia and AD. 10 , 11 , 14 , 32 It is important to note, however, that cognition‐clock associations were attenuated when accounting for the variance explained by years of education, indicating these may have been partially driven by differences in education level.

Additionally, accelerated multi‐organ aging was associated with higher plasma levels of NfL, a biomarker of neurodegeneration. However, only one significant association with p‐tau217, the only AD‐specific outcome tested, was observed suggesting clocks may be capturing general aging‐related phenotypes more efficiently than AD‐specific changes. Notably, the brain‐specific proteomic clock had moderate‐to‐high correlations with system‐wide proteomic clocks (Pearson r = 0.69–0.76; Figure S12). Adiponectin, leptin and FABP4, proteins included in the adipose clock, have been implicated in regulation of cerebral insulin resistance/sensitivity, neuroinflammation, and cardiometabolic diseases, having potential neuroprotective effects depending on the individual's metabolic profile. 33 , 34 The association between adipose aging and higher plasma levels of p‐tau217 possibly reflects the shared inflammatory components linking diabetes to early AD‐related neuropathological processes. Upon further adjustment for APOE‐ε4 carrier status, this association did not withstand multiple testing correction and should, consequently, be regarded as exploratory.

In addition to the brain‐specific and systemic proteomic clocks, NfL was significantly associated with the artery‐ and heart‐specific clocks. Considering nominally significant p‐values (Figure S13, Table S14), we retrieved additional associations with the adipose, immune, liver, and pancreas clocks. Previous studies have also reported associations of artery, immune, and liver proteomic aging with cognitive decline, 31 dementia risk, 15 and/or AD diagnosis and biomarkers. 35 The variety in these associations may have multiple explanations. With growing research on the brain–gut and brain–liver axes, 36 emerging evidence indicates that metabolic dysfunction, peripheral inflammation, 37 and infections 38 , 39 may contribute to higher risk of dementia. As previously suggested, 35 this indicates that AD and other neurodegenerative diseases may reflect more systemic health‐related changes instead of purely brain disease. NfL, as a non‐AD‐specific biomarker of neurodegeneration, 3 may reflect those multi‐system changes affecting neurons. Alternatively, certain proteins used in modeling of these organ‐specific clocks are involved in processes or cellular components related to the nervous system or AD risk factors, possibly driving AD pathogenesis and the associations with NfL. For example, over‐representation analysis highlighted the aggrecan protein (ACAN) from the artery clock and its role in extracellular matrix structure (Table S3). Aggrecan is recognized as the major component of perineuronal nets, which, in turn, are suggested to have protective functions against tau pathology: aggrecan binds to exogenous tau aggregates, trapping them in neurons’ extracellular space and preventing their internalization (see Zhu et al. 40 for a review). Thus, changes in ACAN gene expression alter the composition of perineuronal nets, disrupting their integrity and protective role. 40 These changes may be captured by the artery clock, pointing to its potential in detecting AD‐related pathologies, including neurodegeneration and elevated levels of the respective biomarker, NfL. To note, PAC, 1 – HPS, the brain‐specific and conventional second‐generation proteomic clocks included NfL and/or GFAP in their computation, which could partially explain the identified associations with these BBBs. Although differences in platforms (Simoa versus Olink), quantification methods (absolute versus relative), proteins’ weights in clock computations, and timepoints, suggest that the effect of this information overlap on associations is likely modest, a prudent interpretation of these findings is, nonetheless, recommended.

Over‐representation analysis also suggested possible confounding effects of AD risk factors on the associations. Proteins encoded by BMP10, MYL4, TNNI3, and NPPB, included in the heart‐specific model, are involved in pathways regulating blood circulation and blood pressure (Table S5). Additionally, proteins from the adipose clock, significantly associated with changes in p‐tau217, indicated enrichment in KEGG's T2D pathway (Table S11). More specifically, adiponectin and leptin are involved in several gene ontology terms related to glucose metabolism and transport (Table S2). Hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes are all considered as potentially modifiable AD risk factors and common co‐morbidities. 4 By detecting changes in the mentioned proteins, proteomic clocks may differentially detect changes in AD‐related outcomes in individuals at risk for or with cardiovascular diseases or diabetes. This prompted us to perform exploratory interaction analyses to clarify the influence of these risk factors on detected cognition‐clock associations. Our findings indicated weaker negative associations between cognition and braging in individuals with above‐average genetic liability for T2D, independently of disease status. This was consistent with a previous study that reported stronger associations between BBBs of AD, particularly p‐tau217, and level of brain Aβ in those without diabetes or cardiovascular disorders. 41 Previous research also showed varied effects of risk factors, such as hypertension, obesity, and education on associations between proteomic aging and cognition. 31 Altogether, these proof‐of‐concept findings indicate that AD‐risk factors, in particular diabetes, may significantly compromise the performance of proteomic clocks in explaining variability in cognition. However, due to the exploratory nature of our study and inherent statistical limitations, further studies are needed to validate these findings.

Collectively, proteomic clocks explained up to 23% of the variance in cognition and AD‐related biomarkers that was not captured by epigenetic clocks and covariates. This supports the hypothesis that plasma proteomes may detect additional aspects of AD pathophysiology that epigenetics do not. Yet it is worth noting the imbalance in the number of epigenetic and proteomic clocks studied here, six and 12, respectively, as well as the differences in data types used to develop them and the magnitude of correlations between different proteomic clocks.

Our analyses included both first‐generation clocks, trained on chronological age (Horvath and Hannum), and second‐generation clocks, trained on either mortality or morbidity. Previous reports have shown that both epigenetic and proteomic mortality‐based models perform better than first‐generation models in predicting risk of age‐related diseases. 8 , 14 , 26 Additionally, DunedinPACE, developed based on longitudinal epigenetic data, constitutes a fundamentally distinct clock that could be classified as third generation. It estimates not exactly biological age, but the pace at which aging and system decline occur. 28 Notably, not only was DunedinPACE the sole epigenetic‐based clock with a significant association with any outcome, but it was also the sole clock, overall, significantly associated with the non–AD‐specific plasma biomarker of inflammation, GFAP. In the end, the performance of clocks in this context might depend not only on the omic layer from which they are derived but also on their methodological foundation.

Moreover, given moderate‐to‐high correlations between some organ‐specific and systemic proteomic clocks in our sample (Figure S12), it is not surprising that collective associations between these clocks and several outcomes were observed. Although substantial correlations among proteomic clocks may inflate the number of reported associations, this overlap also suggests that the variance explained in the outcomes is likely shared across different proteomic clocks.

We acknowledge the limitations imposed on the generalizability of our results by the lack of racial and ethnic diversity in our dataset, which consisted exclusively of Finnish individuals with a homogeneous white European background. However, regarding educational background, our study sample provided more diversity than most, comprising individuals with poorer educational attainment as well as highly educated individuals.

Additionally, we acknowledge that our study remains exploratory due to the small sample size, which limits the statistical power of the analyses, and the limited set of covariates, which ultimately indicates that reported associations may still be prone to confounding effects. Despite the limited statistical power of our study, the variety of data types, encompassing genetics, epigenetics, and proteomics, as well as cognitive testing, health registry, and questionnaire data, may be seen as a strength to enable a more thorough investigation of AD risk. On the downside, since, to our knowledge, similar datasets were not available at the time of this study, validation of the findings was not possible.

We also acknowledge the possibility of selection biases caused by the inclusion criteria applied in EH‐Epi and follow‐up TWINGEN sampling. However, these biases are likely minimal, as blood pressure and other demographic characteristics were highly comparable between the EH‐Epi sample and the remaining individuals from the broader twin cohort it originated from, as reported in. 42

To conclude, in our context of a small population‐based sample of non–AD‐diagnosed individuals, the findings suggested that proteomic mortality‐based aging clocks explain substantially more variability in AD‐related phenotypes than epigenetic clocks alone. Our study also underlined the importance of integrating multiple data modalities to address disease complexity. Although exploratory, this comprehensive approach showed that proteomic aging clocks may differentially capture variability in AD‐related outcomes among individuals with above‐average genetic risk for diabetes. Further research efforts into investigating the mediating effect of other co‐morbidities and risk factors, particularly those involving immunity and inflammation, could clarify the applicability of proteomics in risk assessment or clinical diagnosis. Longitudinal studies may also be beneficial to understand how medical interventions targeting modifiable risk factors can modulate biological aging and cognitive functioning.
