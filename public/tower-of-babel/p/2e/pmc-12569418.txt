Impact of Bedtime Digital Media Use on Sleep Across Age Groups: Insights From a Nationwide Survey in South Korea
Authors: Jae Wook Cho, Jin A Kim, Hea Ree Park, Keun Tae Kim, Jee Hyun Kim, Seo-Young Lee, Yong Won Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeGiven the widespread use of digital media, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between bedtime digital media use and sleep disturbances, with a focus on age-related variations.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide cross-sectional study of 4,000 participants aged 20–69 years using an online survey between January 2022 and February 2022. The questionnaire assessed the frequency and duration of bedtime digital media use and resulting sleep procrastination, Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), and awareness of digital media’s impact on sleep.ResultsDaily bedtime digital media use and sleep procrastination were reported by 64.1% and 23.5% of the participants, respectively. Bedtime digital media usage was correlated with insomnia symptoms, with the highest risk in older males (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 4.627; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.868 to 11.463 for ISI≥15 in daily users). Video content (aOR, 1.208; 95% CI, 1.006 to 1.450) and social networking service use (aOR, 1.215; 95% CI, 1.001 to 1.474) were linked to ISI≥10, though these associations became insignificant after adjusting for the frequency and duration of use. Older age was associated with lower awareness of the potential sleep-disrupting effects of bedtime digital media use (aOR, 0.977; 95% CI, 0.971 to 0.982 per year).ConclusionsBedtime digital media use and resulting sleep procrastination are highly prevalent. Older adults are particularly susceptible to insomnia linked to bedtime digital media use and have a lower awareness of its negative effects. These results indicate a need for public health initiatives to increase awareness and mitigate the negative impacts of digital media on sleep.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep is an integral component of human health. The quality and quantity of sleep have direct implications for overall well-being and play a pivotal role in maintaining good health.12 However, recent lifestyles characterized by constant connectivity and digital interaction disrupt healthy sleep patterns, exacerbating sleep disorders such as insomnia.3 This trend is further compounded by occupational and societal pressures, with demands for productivity and entertainment often extending into traditional rest periods, potentially leading to chronic sleep deprivation across diverse populations.

Technological advancements have paralleled an increase in sleep disorders, particularly among adolescents and young adults with prevalent usage of smartphones and the internet.456 A study targeting the population aged 18 to 30 years in the United Kingdom revealed that 39% of young adults were smartphone addicts. Notably, 68.7% of these smartphone-addicted individuals reported experiencing poor sleep, underscoring the strong correlation between smartphone addiction and sleep deprivation.7 Similarly, Japanese high school students who spend more than five hours daily on mobile phones are significantly more likely to experience reduced sleep duration and have a 3.89-fold greater likelihood of developing insomnia, especially because of online chatting and internet browsing.8

While existing studies predominantly focus on younger demographics, recent data indicate increasing smartphone and internet use among older adults.910 Particularly after the COVID-19 pandemic, there has been a notable increase in the engagement of older adults with smartphones and social networking services (SNSs), suggesting that older individuals are increasingly integrating digital platforms and technologies into their daily lives.11 This trend highlights a gap in research concerning this group’s sleep health. A recent consensus statement of the National Sleep Foundation did not reach an agreement for adults because few studies have separately examined the effects of screen media on sleep health in this age group.12

The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between digital media usage and sleep disturbances among various age groups in South Korea, a country with high smartphone usage across all age groups.13 The research questions are as follows: 1) What is the prevalence of bedtime digital media usage and resulting sleep procrastination? 2) How does bedtime digital media usage impact insomnia? 3) How does this impact vary across different age groups? 4) What is the level of awareness regarding the effects of bedtime digital media use on sleep? Understanding these aspects will help in developing targeted interventions to improve sleep health in the context of increasing digital media consumption in an aging population.

METHODS

Participants and survey procedure

We conducted a nationwide population-based cross-sectional study examining sleep disturbances and public awareness. A web-based survey employing a structured questionnaire was administered from January 2022 to February 2022. The participants were recruited from the online survey panel of Embrain Public Company. A stratified multistage random sampling method was used to select a representative sample of 4,000 individuals aged 20–69 years. The sampling criteria included sex, age (20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, or 60–69 years), and residence (capital and five regions). The Dongsan Medical Center’s Institutional Review Board approved this study (IRB No. 2021-12-063), and all participants provided written informed consent. For this analysis, we utilized data relevant to our specific research objectives.

Measures

The questionnaire included questions on smartphone use, social/internet media use, and the types of media that participants often use. Queries addressed the duration and frequency of digital media or TV use before bedtime. We categorized social media into chatting apps, SNSs, and video content. The chatting apps included KakaoTalk, Telegram, Line, WhatsApp, Snapchat, and others. SNSs included Facebook, Instagram, Twitter, or others. The video content included YouTube, Netflix, Wavve, and other over-the-top (OTT) platforms. We asked how often (during the past week) and how long (on average during the past month) they used digital media at bedtime. We also asked about the frequency of delaying bedtime and wake-up time due to the use of digital media at bedtime (Supplementary Material in the online-only Data Supplement).

Sleep outcomes were assessed via the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI). We asked the participants to assess the extent to which they believed that the use of digital media and TV before bedtime interferes with their sleep (not at all, slightly, a lot, or seriously). For analytic purposes, responses categorized into the “a lot” and “seriously” categories were considered indicative of awareness of the potential impact of bedtime media use on sleep disturbance.

The ISI includes seven items that evaluate difficulty falling asleep, difficulty staying asleep, problems waking up too early, satisfaction with current sleep patterns, interference with daily functions, noticeability of impairment attributed to sleep problems, and distress caused by sleep problems. We modified the answers for the first three questions about the severity of the symptoms to fit the following categories: 0: less than once per week; 1: once per week; 2: 2–4 times per week; 3: 5–6 times per week; and 4: daily. An ISI score of 10 or 15 was considered the cutoff value for moderate or severe insomnia, respectively.14

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the data using the chi-square test for categorical data, with a p value of <0.05 denoting statistical significance. Multiple comparison correction was applied in subgroup analyses and adjusted-p values were calculated using the False Discovery Rate method. Logistic regression analysis was employed to compute the relative risk of social media use associated with insomnia, adjusting for confounding variables, including age, sex, job status, coffee intake, and alcohol consumption. The results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All the statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS statistics version 23.0 for Windows (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The 4,000 participants consisted of 2,035 (50.9%) males and 1,965 (49.1%) females, with 717 (17.9%), 717 (17.9%), 874 (21.8%), 924 (23.1%), and 768 (19.2%) of the participants aged 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, and 60–69 years, respectively. Among them, 2,852 (71.3%: 80.3% of males and 62.0% of females) had a job, and 2,581 (90.5%) were day workers.

Overall, 3,983 (99.6%) participants reported using smartphones, and 3,395 (84.9%) participants indicated using social/internet media. Younger participants were more likely to use social/internet media, but usage was still high among older participants, with 79.0% of those aged 60–69 reporting usage. Among digital media users, 70.3% accessed video content, 69.2% engaged with chat applications, 48.5% used SNSs, and 69.4% utilized internet websites. Chatting apps, video content, and SNSs were more frequently used by younger participants, whereas internet website usage was similar across all age groups (Table 1).

Bedtime media use and sleep time procrastination

Overall, 3,515 (87.9%) participants used smartphones or tablets at bedtime once or more per week. Daily users accounted for 2,565 (64.1%) participants, and 1,321 (33.0%) participants used these devices for more than 60 minutes. Due to digital media use, 2,982 (74.6%) participants experienced bedtime procrastination, and 2,100 (52.5%) participants experienced a delayed wake-up time once or more per week.

Females demonstrated a higher frequency of bedtime digital media use than males, with 70.6% of females using it daily compared to 57.9% of males. Additionally, females tended to use digital media for longer durations, with 35.5% using it for more than 60 minutes compared with 30.6% of males. This behavior correlates with higher rates of daily bedtime procrastination due to digital media use, reported by 27.4% of females and 19.7% of males. Similarly, daily delayed wake-up times were more common among females (17.5%) than among males (13.6%). Similarly, a higher percentage of females watched TV for more than 60 minutes at bedtime (40.8%) compared with males (31.6%).

Younger people used digital media at bedtime more frequently (daily: 84.8% in those aged 20–29) and for longer durations (>60 minutes: 48.4%), whereas older people watched TV for longer periods at bedtime (>60 minutes: 53.5% in those aged 60–69). However, 46.2% of the participants aged 60–69 years still used digital media daily at bedtime, and 21.7% used it for more than 60 minutes. Younger people more frequently delayed bedtime (daily procrastination in 39.2% of participants aged 20–29) and wake-up time (daily delays in 29.7% of those aged 20–29). In contrast, among the participants aged 60–69 years, 13.7% procrastinated bedtime daily, and 9.0% experienced daily wake-up time delays due to digital media (Tables 2, 3, and Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Bedtime media use and insomnia

Insomnia symptoms, as indicated by an ISI of 10 or greater (moderate insomnia), were present in 1,710 participants (42.8%), and an ISI of 15 or greater (severe insomnia) was observed in 516 participants (12.9%).

The participants who used digital media at bedtime at least once per week exhibited a greater likelihood of having an ISI≥10, with daily users facing the greatest risk (aOR=1.920 [95% CI: 1.541 to 2.392], adjusted for age and sex). Moreover, daily users had an increased probability of having an ISI≥15 (aOR=1.872 [95% CI: 1.320 to 2.656]). Digital media usage for just 10–30 minutes was associated with an ISI≥10 (aOR=1.328 [95% CI: 1.025 to 1.721]), with the risk increasing for longer usage durations. Using digital media for more than 60 minutes significantly elevated the risk of an ISI≥15 (aOR=2.028 [95% CI: 1.379 to 2.980]) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Specific to the type of content, video content (aOR=1.208 [95% CI: 1.006 to 1.450]) and SNSs (aOR=1.215 [95% CI: 1.001 to 1.474]) were associated with an ISI≥10. However, these associations became statistically insignificant after further adjustments for the frequency and duration of bedtime digital media usage (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Because the interaction between age and sex was significant in the association between media use and insomnia (p=0.024, for ISI≥15), we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age and sex. In these subgroups, the frequency or duration of bedtime digital media use was correlated with insomnia symptoms, except in females aged 60–69 years. The results of the logistic regression analysis for each subgroup, adjusted by age, job status, coffee intake, alcohol consumption and frequency or duration of media usage, are as follows (Figs. 2, 3, Supplementary Tables 4 and 5 in the onlineonly Data Supplement): for 20–39-year-old males, bedtime digital media use ≥1 time/week was linked with an ISI score of ≥10, with the highest risk associated with digital media use 4–6 times/week (aOR=3.624 [95% CI: 1.350 to 9.729]). In 40–59-year-old males, 4–6 times/week use was associated with a higher probability of an ISI≥10 (aOR=1.929 [95% CI: 1.086 to 3.426]). Digital media usage for 30 minutes or longer was associated with an ISI≥10, with the risk increasing for longer usage durations (aOR for >60 minutes=2.529 [95% CI: 1.447 to 4.420]). For 60–69-year-old males, daily bedtime digital media use was associated with a high probability of an ISI≥10 (aOR=2.623 [95% CI: 1.515 to 4.542]).

Among females aged 60–69 years, those who used digital media 1–3 times/week had the highest probability of having an ISI score of ≥10 (aOR=2.855 [95% CI: 1.390 to 5. 865]). For the other female age groups, the association between bedtime digital media usage and an ISI score ≥10 was not significant.

An ISI≥15 was associated with a greater frequency of bedtime media use (aOR=4.627 [95% CI: 1.868 to 11.463 for daily usage]) among males aged 60–69 years. In the other age and sex groups, the associations between frequency and duration of bedtime digital media use were not significant.

For TV watching, bedtime use for longer than 60 minutes was associated with both an ISI≥10 (aOR=1.305 [95% CI: 1.099 to 1.549]) and an ISI≥15 (aOR=1.337 [95% CI: 1.038 to 1.722]) (Supplementary Table 6 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Awareness about bedtime media use and sleep disturbances

Females more frequently thought that bedtime digital media use or TV watching substantially disturbed sleep compared to males (28.7% vs. 22.8%). Younger participants exhibited greater awareness of these effects, with 35.9% of those aged 20–29 recognizing the impact compared with only 14.6% of those aged 60–69.

Factors associated with higher levels of awareness regarding the potential sleep-disturbing effects of bedtime media use included the following: female sex (aOR=1.320 [95% CI: 1.140 to 1.529]), younger age (aOR=0.977 [95% CI: 0.971 to 0.982] per year increase), residing in the capital area (aOR=1.332 [95% CI: 1.120 to 1.585] in reference to noncapital rural areas), digital media use for 30 minutes or longer before bedtime (30–60 minutes: aOR=1.686 [95% CI: 1.209 to 2.351] and ≥60 minutes: aOR=2.615 [95% CI: 1.880 to 3.637], and TV watching for less than 10 minutes before sleep (aOR=1.555 [95% CI: 1.283 to 1.885]). DISCUSSION

This nationwide study in South Korea revealed a high prevalence of bedtime digital media use, which significantly contributed to sleep schedule disruptions across all age groups. An increased frequency and duration of digital media use were associated with higher prevalence of insomnia symptoms, with video content and SNSs posing relatively greater risks. Notably, the dose–response relationship between digital media use and moderate or severe insomnia was particularly significant in older adults and males. This finding was coupled with a lower level of awareness regarding the potential impact of digital media on sleep health in these groups, underscoring the need for targeted interventions.

The relationship between digital media use and insomnia is likely influenced by multiple factors. One crucial factor is the effect of blue light emitted by electronic devices on melatonin production. A well-controlled experimental study demonstrated that, compared with reading printed books, evening use of light-emitting eReaders significantly suppressed melatonin secretion, delayed circadian timing, and reduced next-morning alertness.15 In addition to light exposure, cognitively stimulating content and social interaction may contribute to the negative impact of bedtime digital media. This study found that the associations between video content and SNS use and insomnia symptoms diminished after adjusting for the frequency and duration of their use. This suggests that the effects on sleep are attributed not only to the inherent properties of these media types but also to their comparatively longer usage times.

Our study revealed an intriguing sex disparity. Bedtime digital media use and subsequent sleep procrastination were more frequent among females, yet the impact on insomnia was more pronounced in males. Several factors may contribute to this sex difference. First, while our survey did not specifically address the type of content consumed, it is possible that males and females engage with different forms of content, leading to varied impacts on sleep.16 Second, females already have a higher prevalence of insomnia regardless of digital media use, which may be attributed to various biological, psychological, and social factors.17 This higher baseline prevalence could mask the additional impact of digital media use on females’ sleep quality. Third, men might be more susceptible to the sleep-disrupting effects of nighttime digital media use. This could be due to differences in circadian rhythms, stress responses, or other physiological factors that interact with digital media exposure.181920 These sex-specific findings require further studies employing more tailored assessments of media content and usage patterns, as well as objective measures of sleep.

The negative impact of digital media on sleep was particularly pronounced in older adults, who are already vulnerable to sleep disturbances due to age-related changes in sleep architecture.21 Additionally, lifestyle factors unique to older adults, such as reduced exposure to natural light during the day and less physical activity, might amplify the impact of even short durations of digital media use on sleep quality. Our findings are in line with the finding that bedtime media use and shorter sleep duration were associated only in those >66 years old in a previously reported Flemish study.22

Interestingly, while longer digital media use was associated with greater awareness of its negative effects on sleep, the opposite trend was observed for watching TV in this study. This discrepancy may indicate a broader recognition of TV’s impact on sleep, leading those affected to reduce their viewing times. In contrast, heavy digital media users may only begin to acknowledge the potential negative effects on their sleep through personal experience, which has not yet led to reduced usage.

In 2020, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) released a guideline titled “Healthy sleep habits,” which recommended discontinuing the use of electronic devices at least 30 minutes before bedtime to promote better sleep hygiene.23 Similarly, the United Kingdom’s National Health Service (NHS) provided a sleep hygiene guideline titled “How to get to sleep,” which advised individuals to avoid using TVs, smartphones, and computers for at least an hour before bedtime.24

Our study’s results underscore the importance of enhancing public awareness of the negative impact of bedtime digital media use and the need for the widespread dissemination and adoption of such guidelines across various age groups. Given the heightened vulnerability of older adults and males to sleep disturbances associated with digital media use, targeted education and intervention programs for these populations are particularly crucial.

The current study has several limitations. First, the crosssectional design of this study does not preclude reverse causality. We could not determine whether digital media use caused sleep problems or whether individuals with sleep issues tended to use digital media more. Second, we assessed digital media exposure by self-reports, which may not accurately capture the true extent of usage. Third, this mass survey could not measure the intensity of the screen light or the stimulating level of the content. Fourth, since this study was based on an online survey, the participants were likely already prone to using digital media, potentially inflating the prevalence of digital media use. As this study was conducted during the pandemic-a period of reduced outdoor activities, the observed patterns may not fully reflect typical conditions.2526 Nevertheless, this unique context clearly highlights the association between digital media use and sleep health. Future research should further investigate individuals’ awareness and use of blue light reduction settings on digital devices. It has been suggested that exposure to blue light from devices such as smartphones negatively impacts sleep quality, making this an essential factor to explore in depth.27 Additionally, subsequent studies should incorporate detailed assessments of media content to better understand how different types of content influence sleep disturbances. Future interventional studies that reduce media consumption or alter the media type and content would confirm the negative and differential impact of digital media on sleep.

Despite these limitations, this study is among the few to investigate the impact of digital media on sleep in various age groups, including older adults. Given the global trend toward an aging population and the pervasive nature of digital technology, we believe our study provides essential insights into digital media use and sleep health.

In conclusions, this study highlights the widespread influence of digital media across different age groups, with older adults and males exhibiting greater susceptibility. The low awareness among these populations calls for public health initiatives aimed at improving awareness of the negative impact of digital media on sleep. The development of guidelines to mitigate these effects is crucial for improving sleep quality across these populations.
