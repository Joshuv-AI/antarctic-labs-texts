Prevalence of hearing loss and other ear disorders among patients with hepatitis B infection: a nationwide retrospective Polish cohort study
Authors: Piotr Rzymski, Agnieszka Genowska, Dorota Zarębska-Michaluk, Jerzy Jaroszewicz, Krzysztof Kanecki, Katarzyna Lewtak, Paweł Goryński, Aneta Nitsch-Osuch, Robert Flisiak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Reports from Asian populations have suggested that chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection may predispose patients to hearing impairment, particularly sudden sensorineural hearing loss, yet evidence from Europe has been lacking. To address this gap, we conducted a nationwide, retrospective analysis of the prevalence of ear and hearing disorders among patients hospitalized with HBV infection in Poland between 2012 and 2023, using the national hospital discharge database. Hospital records with ICD-10 codes for acute or chronic HBV infection were examined, and cases with concomitant ear or hearing disorders were identified. In total, 80,181 HBV-related hospitalizations were recorded, of which 36.6% represented first-time admissions, while the remainder were repeat hospitalizations. Diagnoses of hearing loss were rare, occurring in only 0.05% of first-time and 0.04% of all hospitalizations, and all affected patients were adults. Hearing loss diagnoses, including those encompassing both conductive and sensorineural forms, were exceptionally uncommon. Notably, conditions most frequently discussed in the context of HBV, such as sensorineural hearing loss, were rare and showed no tendency to recur across repeated admissions. Importantly, the prevalence of hearing disorders in this HBV-infected cohort was substantially lower than that reported for the general population, suggesting no increased prevalence of auditory disorders among patients with HBV infection in this setting. These findings indicate that, in contrast to observations from parts of Asia, chronic HBV infection is not associated with an increased prevalence of hearing impairment in this European population. Discrepancies with Asian studies likely reflect multiple regional factors, including differences in HBV genotype distribution, disease characteristics, healthcare practices, and host or environmental influences, rather than genotype alone.

Introduction

Despite the introduction of effective vaccination programs and antiviral therapies, hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a major global health problem, affecting over 250 million people worldwide. Chronic HBV infection primarily targets the liver, leading to progressive hepatic inflammation, fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma1.

Beyond hepatic injury, HBV is a systemic infection that can trigger extrahepatic manifestations in 10–20% of patients2, including polyarteritis nodosa3, glomerulonephritis4, and cryoglobulinemia5. However, whether HBV can cause ototoxicity and auditory dysfunction is not fully known, though certain mechanistic pathways behind it have been recently put forward6. First, polyarteritis nodosa, found in 7% of patients with chronic HBV infection7, may involve the auditory arteries and microvasculature, leading to ischemic or inflammatory injury of the cochlea and subsequent sensorineural hearing loss8. Second, chronic HBV infection is associated with increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α9, which may alter cochlear homeostasis and contribute to hearing impairment through immune-mediated or metabolic mechanisms10. Third, HBV has been suggested to infect endothelial and neuronal cells directly11–13, as viral particles may gain access to extrahepatic tissues via the hematogenous route or through extracellular vesicles carrying intact virions14,15. This could result in local inflammation and structural damage within the inner ear. The biological mechanisms proposed for HBV-related auditory involvement, such as immune-mediated injury, vascular compromise, or direct viral effects, primarily affect the inner ear and auditory nerve, and would therefore be expected to result in sensorineural rather than conductive hearing loss. Conductive hearing loss typically arises from pathology of the external or middle ear, including infection, obstruction, or ossicular dysfunction, which are not known consequences of HBV infection16.

In line with this, recent epidemiological studies, including large retrospective cohort analyses conducted in Asian populations, have suggested an association between chronic HBV infection and an increased risk of sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) and elevated hearing thresholds, particularly at higher frequencies17–21. This association was also found when controlling for confounders and increased with the patient’s age and years of follow-up17,18. However, findings remain inconsistent since the study conducted in Korea found a contrary relationship, i.e., HBsAg-positive patients having a lower prevalence of hearing impairment compared to HBsAg-negative individuals22. At the same time, large-scale data from other world regions, including Central and Eastern Europe, are completely lacking.

This study assessed the prevalence of ear and hearing disorders among HBV-infected patients in Poland (2012–2023), with particular emphasis on hearing loss, to better characterize the burden of auditory conditions in this population.

Materials and methods

This was a retrospective, population-based study on all hospitalizations due to HBV infection recorded in Poland between 2012 and 2023, based on data from the national hospital discharge database of the Nationwide General Hospital Morbidity Study (NGHMS), conducted by the National Institute of Public Health – National Research Institute (NIPH-NRI). In Poland, all hospitals, except psychiatric facilities, are legally required to send discharge data to the institute. The NGHMS is a national administrative database containing anonymized records of all hospitalizations in Poland (excluding psychiatric facilities).

The data are not publicly available due to national data protection regulations, but can be accessed upon reasonable request to the NIPH– NRI. The data are anonymous and include information on hospitalizations, with ICD-10 codes assigned by the reporting hospitals for each patient, as well as dates of admission and discharge, sex, date of birth, and place of residence. All hospitalization records from 2012 to 2023 with a discharge diagnosis of HBV infection (ICD-10 code B16, B16.x, B17.0, B18.0, B18.1), recorded as either primary or secondary, were included. In this study, a subgroup of patients with hearing diseases (codes H60-H95) was identified. All codes were used as recorded, without additional reclassification.

Hearing loss was identified using ICD-10 codes H90, which includes both conductive and sensorineural hearing loss, and H91, which covers other types of hearing loss not classified under H90. Because the database does not allow reliable differentiation between these subtypes, all cases were analyzed and reported collectively as a single outcome. To identify the number of repeated hospitalizations, the date of birth, sex, and coded place of residence were compared.

Results and discussion

During the 2012–2023 period, 80,181 patients in Poland were hospitalized due to HBV infection, of whom 29,335 (36.6%) were first-time hospitalizations. In the study group of HBV patients with selected ear disorders (n = 26 persons with 28 diagnoses), the mean ± SD of the study patients was 47.6 ± 17.3 years, with 38.5% represented by women, and 61.5% represented by men.

In this group, 18 individuals (69.2%) were urban residents and 8 (30.8%) were rural residents. Among the urban residents, 12 (66.7%) were men aged 20–57 years, and 6 (33.3%) were women aged 29–80 years. Among the rural residents, 4 (50%) were men aged 42–83 years, and 4 (50%) were women aged 21–82 years. Considering the county deprivation index values, 12 individuals (46.1%) came from counties with the lowest risk of deprivation (i.e., the 25% of counties in Poland with the lowest deprivation index nationwide), including 4 women and 8 men. In contrast, 4 individuals (15.4%), all of whom were men, came from counties with the highest risk of deprivation (i.e., the 25% of counties with the highest deprivation index).

The general prevalence of identified ear disorders (ICD-10 codes H60-H95) in the studied cohort was very low – 0.095% in first-time hospitalizations and 0.06% in all-time hospitalizations (Table 1). The prevalence of hearing loss (ICD-10 codes H90–H91) among first-time and all-time hospitalized patients was 0.05% (16/29,335) and 0.04% (33/80,181), respectively. Hearing loss diagnoses (ICD-10 code H90), encompassing both conductive and sensorineural types, were very rare, present in only 0.014% (4/29,335) and 0.012% (10/80,181) of first-time and all-time hospitalized patients, respectively. Notably, prior literature on HBV has primarily implicated sensorineural hearing loss, particularly SSNHL6,17–21. As shown in Table 1, during the study period, only a few diseases exhibited a slight increase in their number among hospitalizations compared to first-time hospitalizations (ICD-10 codes: H81, H90, H91, H93). This indicates that hearing-related disorders were rare and showed no clear pattern of recurrence in subsequent hospitalization in the population of HBV-infected patients in Poland.

These figures are lower than the prevalence of self-reported hearing problems in the general population. According to the EuroTrak Poland 2019 survey, 17.4% of adult Poles reported experiencing hearing loss23. The annual SSNHL incidence in developed countries, including those in Europe, falls within the 5–20 or 5–30 per 100,000 range24,25, which again is several times higher than the prevalence observed among the patients included in our study. Taken together, these results show that, at least among Polish patients hospitalized with HBV infection, the recorded prevalence of hearing impairment is very low.

This finding contrasts with previous reports from East Asia and the Middle East, suggesting a positive association between HBV infection and hearing loss. For example, two large retrospective matched-cohort studies conducted in Taiwan demonstrated that HBV-positive individuals had a higher risk of SSNHL, with adjusted hazard ratios ranging from 1.3 (95% CI: 1.2–1.5) to 5.1 (4.5–5.8), and the risk increased with longer follow-up17,18. Similarly, case–control studies from Iran and China reported elevated hearing thresholds, particularly at higher frequencies, among HBV-positive individuals19,20. Interestingly, a study from Korea, where genotype C is dominant (almost exclusively C2)26, reported a contrary pattern: HBsAg-positive individuals exhibited a lower prevalence of hearing impairment compared to HBsAg-negative individuals22. However, genotype C, along with genotype B, is also predominant in Taiwan and China27, where an increased risk of hearing impairment has been observed17,18,20. Therefore, the presence of genotype C, which has been linked to higher viral loads, more aggressive liver disease, and less favorable clinical outcomes28, alone is unlikely to account for the association between HBV infection and auditory dysfunction. In Poland, genotype A predominates29, and our data also show a very low prevalence of hearing impairment among HBV-infected patients. In contrast, Iran is characterized by an overwhelming predominance of genotype D30, which exhibits distinct molecular and clinical features31.

Taken together, these observations suggest that differences in HBV genotype, while potentially contributing to disease severity, cannot fully explain the divergent findings across populations. Other factors, including infection routes, disease activity, population characteristics, healthcare practices, and study methodology, likely play important roles. Overall, these findings underscore that the relationship between HBV infection and hearing impairment is context-dependent, and further genotype-stratified and mechanistic studies are warranted to clarify the underlying pathways.

The main strength of this study lies in its nationwide scope, allowing a decade-long, comprehensive assessment of ear-related morbidities among HBV-infected patients in Poland. Standardized ICD-10 coding enabled identification of a wide range of auditory and vestibular disorders, minimizing selection bias and improving generalizability to the broader hospitalized HBV population. However, limitations include the retrospective nature of the data, which prevents causal inference and depends on diagnostic accuracy. It was designed as a descriptive analysis of prevalence based on administrative data, and therefore, no adjustments for potential confounders were performed. The absence of clinical variables, such as HBV viral load, genotype, serological status, antiviral therapy, and audiometric data, limits insight into disease severity and hearing outcomes. Among sociodemographic data, we only had data on the age and gender distribution of hospitalized patients. Although ICD-10 codes H90 and H91 were analyzed separately as defined in the classification system, they do not allow precise differentiation between hearing loss subtypes. In particular, H90 includes both conductive and sensorineural forms, while H91 comprises heterogeneous and unspecified diagnoses. Therefore, the findings are interpreted conservatively as reflecting overall hearing loss rather than specific subtypes. Referral bias may also exist, as only more severe cases are hospitalized, and hearing assessment is not routine in infectious-disease wards, possibly underestimating prevalence. Nonetheless, clinically significant hearing loss should have been captured through hospital coding, supporting the validity of the findings. To obtain a reliable answer to the question about the relationship between HBV and hearing disorders, prospective studies would be necessary.

Conclusions

In this nationwide Polish cohort (2012–2023), an increased prevalence of ear or hearing disorders in HBV-infected patients was not documented. The extremely low prevalence of auditory diagnoses indicates that HBV may contribute minimally, if at all, to hearing loss in European settings. Contrasts with Asian data likely reflect a combination of regional factors, including, but not limited to, differences in HBV genotype distribution, disease biology, healthcare practices, and host or environmental characteristics, rather than viral genotype alone.
