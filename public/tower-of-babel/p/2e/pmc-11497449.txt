Enhanced adhesion and wear resistance of DLC films on AZ31 alloy achieved with high bias voltage
Authors: Haitao Li, Chong Liu, Pengfei Sun, Jingping Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Diamond-like carbon (DLC) films exhibiting high adhesion and exceptional wear resistance were successfully applied onto the AZ31 surface without a adhesive layer. The structural features of the film were examined utilizing Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and Raman spectroscopy. Mechanical properties were evaluated through Nano-indentation testing, while adhesion was evaluated using a scratch tester. Residual stress was determined by Stoney's equation. The results indicate that utilizing a high substrate bias ranging from −500 to −800 V, results in the presence of multiple craters and dislocations, which contribute to the enhancement of high residual compressive stress and hardness in the DLC film. The heightened compressive stress effectively embeds carbon atoms into the surface of the soft AZ31, creating an interfacial layer that significantly improves adhesion and wear resistance of the film. In contrast, at moderate bias levels with a range from −300 to −500 V, an increase in substrate temperature leads to the expansion and discontinuation of adjacent layers that decreases the mechanical properties, adhesion, and wear resistance of the film. When at low bias levels between 0 and -300 V, the hardness, crack resistance, residual stress, and wear resistance all increase with increasing of bias. Additionally, in this work, the mechanisms of adhesion and wear for the biased film are analyzed.

Introduction

The DLC film with hybrid bonds sp2 and sp3 has recently gained popularity due to its exceptional hardness [1], self-lubrication properties [2], ultra-low friction coefficient [1,3], chemical inertness [4,5], and superior resistance to wear and corrosion [6]. This material is recognized for its ability to provide protection against corrosion and wear, making it a promising candidate for enhancing the surface properties of magnesium-based alloys [1,[6], [7], [8]]. Nowadays, numerous varieties of DLC films have been applied to Mg-based alloys in previous research. Yamauchi et al. [8] utilized the RF capacitively coupled plasma CVD technique to deposit a DLC film onto a Mg-Li alloy. However, the improved properties are constrained by inadequate adhesion, resulting in limited durability for long-term applications. Cui et al. [9] fabricated a MAO/DLC film on AZ31B through the integration of microarc-oxidation and unbalanced magnetron sputtering techniques. Subsequently, the MAO/DLC film underwent modification using a conventional dip-coating approach with a polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) solution. While this method improves the adhesion of DLC films to AZ31B, it is characterized by complexity and the generation of waste liquid, posing challenges in terms of handling and potential environmental contamination. Masami et al. [10] conducted an investigation into the impact of coating conditions on the corrosion resistance of DLC-coated AZ91 through plasma-based ion implantation (PBII). Additionally, the study examined both the adhesion and corrosion resistance of various coatings, including a Ti interlayer beneath the DLC, Si-DLC and Ti-DLC coatings produced by multi-target magnetron sputtering. While the Ti layer enhances adhesion between the DLC coating and the AZ91 substrate, it also compromises the corrosion protection properties of the material. Choi et al. [11] conducted a study on the friction properties of the DLC/interlayer/AZ31 sample, which exhibited favorable friction and wear characteristics in a NaCl solution. However, significant corrosion pits were observed on the surface during friction, which were attributed to the inadequate adhesion and high internal stress of the DLC coating on AZ31. In contrast, Zhu et al. [12] synthesized a bilayer (DLC:H)/SiNx film on N+ ion-implanted AZ31 using reactive magnetron sputtering. The coated AZ31 demonstrated improved corrosion resistance after 120 h of immersion in a 3.5 wt% NaCl solution. Nevertheless, they did not delve into the investigate of adhesion and wear resistance.

The long-term protection of DLC film on Mg alloys is a significant challenge attributed to poor adhesion and high residual stress, stemming from substantial physical disparities between a hard film and a soft alloy [13]. Numerous researchers have implemented bottom or buffer layers prior to DLC film deposition to mitigate residual stress and improve adhesion, including materials such as Cr [14], Al [13,15], Ti [16], W [13,17], Cu [18,19] and Si [4,6,7]. However, the improvement of interfacial bond strength is limited by the presence of columnar crystals and defects such as cracks, pinholes, and pores, ultimately diminishing the efficacy of the coating, as reported in Ref. [10]. Additionally, the determination of the optimal type and thickness of the underlayer, which greatly influences the film properties and residual stresses, poses a challenge. Consequently, it is advisable to forego the use of an underlying layer.

As evidenced by previous studies [20,21], the structure and properties of a film is significantly influenced by the deposition energy. When utilizing conventional coating methods on the substrate, the film growth is subjected to minimal implantation and bombardment, leading to a slightly rougher and less compact film structure. In contrast, the cage-like hollow cathode discharge (CLHCD) demonstrates exceptional efficacy in depositing thick films on substrate of diverse geometries and dimensions without the need for sample manipulation [4,22,23]. In the CLHCD process, a meshed metal cage is employed to encase the components. Plasma generation occurs both within and outside the cage; however, the plasma within the cage is significantly more intense due to the hollow cathode effect. This phenomenon ensures that all components are thoroughly immersed in plasma. To enhance the ion kinetic energy imparted to the samples, an additional bias power supply is implemented between the samples and the meshed cage. This configuration facilitates independent control of the ion energy delivered to the growing film. When a bias voltage is applied, the hydrocarbon precursor undergoes ionization, resulting in the formation of CH+, CH5+, and CH3+ ions. These ionized hydrocarbon species are then individually accelerated and directed towards the substrate during deposition, with their energy levels controlled by the bias voltage [24].

Currently, the majority of researchers concentrate on the advancement of fabrication technologies (e.g., power supply) and the enhancement of wear resistance for DLC films on silicon wafers, often through the doping of additional elements. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is a paucity of reports addressing the enhancement of adhesive strength, residual stress, and wear resistance of DLC films on magnesium alloys. In this study, we employ bias voltage to fabricate a three-layer structure for the DLC film, which increases its density and improves its adhesion to AZ31 alloy. This process can be likened to a snowflake that has settled and firmly adhered to the ground after being compressed. Moreover, we investigated the structure, residual stress, adhesion, mechanical properties, and wear resistance of DLC films under varying negative bias voltages, specifically low bias (0–300 V), medium bias (300–500 V), and high bias (500–800 V). The findings of this research have significant implications for improving the surface characteristics of Mg-based alloys and advancing the adhesion and wear resistance of DLC films.

Materials and methods

Film preparation

The AZ31 alloy was subjected to thorough pre-processing procedures, including grinding to a specific 2000 grit on metallurgical sandpaper and ultrasonic cleaning in acetone and absolute ethanol for 15 min each. Following drying in a nitrogen environment, the substrate was introduced into a vacuum chamber for a process involving 20 min of Ar+ cleaning and etching to achieve a pristine micro-convex surface. Subsequently, a series of DLC films were deposited onto the AZ31 surface using a integration of cage-like hollow cathode discharges with DC bias in an atmosphere composed of Ar and C2H2. The modulation of the energies of the generated ions is influenced by the bias values. Initially, a thin DLC layer was deposited onto AZ31 with a bias of −200 V (referred to as the A layer). Subsequently, a DLC layer was fabricated without bias in order to alleviate residual stress (referred to as the B layer). Following this, an outermost layer (referred to as the C layer) was produced at varying biases to modulate adhesion, mechanical properties, and wear resistance of the film. Finally, the bias voltage was gradually decreased from −800 V to 0 V at a rate of 20 V/min to facilitate slow cooling. In Fig. 1a, a metal cage measuring 300 × 200 × 200 mm3 is utilized as a hollow cathode to produce a superimposed negative glow region and amplify plasma density, leading to heightened dissociation of C2H2 molecules and subsequent generation of a higher quantity of ethinyl radicals [22,23]. The plasma is created through the utilization of a pulsed DC power supply with a peak voltage of −1300 V applied between the metal cage and the outer wall of the vacuum chamber, while a DC bias voltage is concurrently applied between the AZ31 substrate and the metal cage. Structure and properties

The morphology and thickness of the films were assessed utilizing a field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM), while the microstructure was examined via transmission electron microscope (TEM). Raman spectroscopy, employing a 532 nm Ar+ laser spanning from 820 to 1900 cm−1, was employed to investigate microstructural and chemical bonding states. Mechanical properties were assessed using a nano-indenter equipped with a Berkovich diamond tip and continuous stiffness option. Adhesion was evaluated through the use of a WS-2005 scratch tester, applying a load within the range of 0–30 N and a loading rate of 30 N/min, with a scratch velocity and length set at 3 mm/min and 3 mm, respectively. Analysis of the acoustic emission signal (AE) and examination of the optical morphology (OM) of the scratches were utilized to determine results. Residual stress was calculated utilizing Stoney's equation (Eq. (1)) [25,26] with the FST1000 film stress tester, using a Si (100) wafer as the substrate for testing residual stress, with the values of Es and vs set at 160 GPa and 0.22, respectively [27]. To ensure the validity of Stoney's formula, the AZ31 substrate was prepared with dimensions of 50 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm, yielding an aspect ratio of 10:1. Furthermore, to minimize testing inaccuracies and reduce errors arising from measurement contingencies, the sample was measured and calculated three times. The average value of these measurements was then used to determine the final residual stress of the film.(1)σ=Es(1−vs)ts26tf(1R1−1R2)where Es, Young's modulus. νs, Poisson's ratio. Wear tests were performed using a ball-on-disc tribometer, with a 6.0 mm diameter Si3N4 grinding sphere operating at a velocity of 300r/min, a normal load of 200 gf and duration of 1800 s. Prior to testing, both the film and Si3N4 sphere were cleaned with ethanol to ensure the absence of any residual contaminants on the sample surface. The average wear volume was determined by measuring each worn track three times. Following testing, the films were examined using SEM, while the wear tracks were observed using a three-position optical profiler. Results and discussion

Morphology observation

Fig. 2 illustrates the morphology of the film, revealing that in the absence of bias (Fig. 2a), the film surface displays non-uniform particles in terms of size and shape. Upon the application of bias, a noticeable reduction in both size and quantity of particles on the surface of the −200 V film is observed (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, the −300 V film (Fig. 2c) exhibits diminutive white particles scattered randomly on the film surface, resulting in a smoother surface compared to the 0 V and −200 V films. For the −600 V film (Fig. 2d), the surface is smooth and the white particles are almost invisible. In addition, strip-shaped shallow pits can be observed on the surface. It is concluded that the surface roughness and the size of the white particles significantly decrease with increasing bias. Primarily, an elevated bias voltage induces a higher plasma density, consequently causing more significant charging of larger particles by electrons and their subsequent repulsion from the substrate due to their negative charge [28]. Moreover, increased ion bombardment has been shown to aid in the removal of specific large particles and the fragmentation of large carbon clusters into smaller ones, ultimately leading to the formation of a dense microstructure and a polished surface [23]. Furthermore, the introduction of bias further boosts the ion energy directed towards the film surface, consequently minimizing the occurrence of surface voids and micro-convex features, resulting in a more homogeneous and condensed film [29,30]. This elevation of ion energy also serves to alleviate the interface shadowing effect, thereby leading to the development of a denser film [31]. Subsequently, upon achieving a critical level of atomic diffusion, a relatively dense planar layer is formed [32]. The sputtered particles undergo epitaxial growth along the substrate of the preceding layer as a result of surface free energy, resulting in the formation of an amorphous structure. Nevertheless, elevating the bias to −800 V leads to a rise in surface defects, specifically larger strip-like defects induced by excessive bombardment and deposited energy, as depicted in Fig. 2e. Furthermore, the energetic particles present on the surface of AZ31 demonstrate a proclivity to migrate towards areas of lower temperature under the impact of heightened bias. This migration serves as an additional factor contributing to the development of shallow stripped pits, with the dimensions and depth of these pits notably increasing when the negative bias exceeds 600 V.

Fig. 2f to h depict the cross-sectional architecture of the film. Fig. 2f demonstrates that the film without bias displays a rugged cross-sectional morphology characterized by numerous elevated hills and discontinuous large grains on both the surface and within the cross-section, measuring approximately 3.62 μm in thickness. In contrast, Fig. 2g reveals that the −400 V film exhibits a uniform thickness and a coarse cross-sectional morphology with smaller particles, maintaining an average thickness of approximately 3.12 μm. It is evident that the particle size and thickness of the −400 V film are reduced compared to the unbiased film. However, its cross-section is still rough with many particles. Additionally, the absence of a visible interface suggests enhanced bonding between the −400 V film and substrate. Examination of the cross-section at −800 V in Fig. 2h shows a very smooth and dense structure with a thickness of approximately 2.84 μm. The heightened energy bombardment results in a dense film structure and strong bond between the film and substrate, decreasing in film thickness. Furthermore, the impact of energetic particles (atoms, ions) bombarding the film surface leads to the formation of pits and etching, ultimately causing a decrease in film thickness [23,28,33,34].

The B layer, which was prepared without bias, likely acquired these pits due to the implantation of carbon atoms from the C layer during bombardment at −600 V. Subsequent to raising the bias to −800 V, the HRTEM image in Fig. 3e reveals a substantial presence of dislocations, particularly in the vicinity of the interface between layers B and C. Films deposited at −400 V exhibit a propensity for crystallization. However, as biases exceeding −600 V, numerous defects such as pits, caves, and dislocation emerge, which may influence the mechanical properties, and wear resistance of the film. These HRTEM images are consistent with observations made through SEM.

It is evident that the growth of the unbiased film primarily involves the adsorption of atomic hydrogen and highly hydrogenated carbonaceous substances, such as precursor gases, CH3 and C2Hx free radicals [37]. This results in a weak Raman signal and a highly hydrogenated film. Furthermore, it can be inferred that the film is not entirely amorphous at 0 V bias, despite the presence of a faint halo in the FFT image. Because if the material is totally amorphous, no edge of hexagonal cyclic ring sp2 clusters are formed, and there is no peak (narrow band) observable at nominally ∼1350 cm−1 [7]. Additionally, the introduction of bias leads to fluctuations in the ID/IG ratio, initially decreasing from 1.23 to 1.09 as the bias is raised from 0 V to −300 V, subsequently increasing to 1.11 at −400 V, and then decreasing again to 1.01 as the bias is further increased to −600 V.

In the range of 0 V to −300 V, the reduction in the ID/IG ratio is attributed to the disruption of C-H bonds and surface hydrogen overflow during deposition at high bombardment energy, as well as the inability to form π-bonds in DLC structures [37,38]. Moreover, the introduction of bias voltage causes high-energy ions to penetrate the surface layer via lattice gap, resulting in a localized rise in film density and deformation under compressive stress. This process facilitates the formation of more sp3 bands [39]. Furthermore, a decrease in the ID/IG ratio indicates the presence of smaller clusters and fewer chain-like structures [22,40], aligning with the finding of SEM and TEM analyses in Fig. 2, Fig. 3. Nevertheless, when subjected to a bias of −400 V, the impact of high-energy particle bombardment can have a significant contribute to substrate heating, leading to the transformation of sp3 to sp2 bonds [34,37], a decrease in sp3 bond concentration. Conversely, at a bias of −600 V, the disruption of C-H bonds and surface hydrogen overflow can lead to an increase in sp3 concentration. Furthermore, the elevated bombardment energy associated with a −600 V bias results in an increase in defects, as shown in Fig. 2, Fig. 3d, thereby substantially augmenting the residual compressive stress and ultimately promoting the formation of more sp3 bonds.

Additionally, Fig. 4 and Table 2 illustrate that the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the G peak increases and the position of the G peak shifts towards lower wavenumbers as the bias voltage increases from 0 to −600 V. The observed rise in FWHM suggests an elevation in internal stress and hardness of the film. Moreover, the observed shift of the G peak towards lower wavenumbers suggests an increase in stress, with a proportional relationship to the degree of wavenumber displacement. It is apparent that the film subjected to −600 V should displays the highest residual stress and hardness within the bias voltage range of 0 to −600 V. Furthermore, it is noted that the FWHM values for the films at −300 V and −400 V are very close. Additionally, the decline in hydrogen content with increasing bias from 0 to −600 V, as illustrated in Table 2, indicates that the C-H bond is disrupted under bias bombardment, causing the recombination of hydrogen atoms into hydrogen molecules and their subsequent escape from the film.

Residual stress

Fig. 5 depicts the correlation between residual stress and bias voltage in DLC films. The data shows a clear trend of increasing compressive stress, from 385 to 1245 MPa, as the bias voltage is raised from 0 to −300 V. Then, the residual stress experiences a modest decline to 1228 MPa upon the bias voltage reaching −400 V. This observation indirectly suggests that the sp3 content attains its peak at approximately −300 V. The observed increase in residual stress from 0 to −300 V can be attributed to changes in the sp3 bond fraction and hydrogen content [23,34], as determined through Raman analysis. Specifically, as the bias increases, the sp3 fraction rises while the hydrogen concentration decreases, leading to an increase in residual stress. Additionally, the creation of high-density structures through bias bombardment results in notable compression stresses. Moreover, the presence of numerous carbon cluster boundaries within the fine structure serves to further intensify these compression stress.Fig. 5Residual stress values vary depending on bias voltages.Fig. 5

As the bias is increased from −300 to −500 V, the residual compressive stress decreases slightly from 1245 MPa to 1070 MPa. It is important to acknowledge in this situation that the transformation of sp3 phases to sp2 phases results in the release of residual stress. Conversely, the increase in substrate temperature leads to a rise in thermal stress, and the expansion of the AZ31 substrate due to temperature exacerbates the separation between adjacent layers and amplifies interface discontinuity, causing localized stress concentration at film interfaces and an increases in residual stress. Thus, the residual stress level is a consequence of the synergistic effects of these two factors.

Applying biases ranging from −500 to −800 V resulted in a significant increase in residual compressive stress from 1070 to 5335 MPa. This phenomenon is supported by the presence of numerous bombardment pits in layers C and B, as shown in Fig. 3d and e. The B layer, which was prepared without bias, likely acquired these pits through the implantation of carbon atoms from the C layer during bombardment at −600 V. In this instance, the presence of pits and the introduction of carbon atoms contributed to the development of substantial residual stress. This observation aligns with the results presented in report [26], which suggest that the compact microstructure created by ion bombardment, characterized by high defect densities such as pits, contributes to the heightened compression stress. Subsequent to raising the bias to −800 V, the HRTEM image in Fig. 3e reveals a substantial presence of dislocations, increasing residual stress. It is important to note that the measurement of residual stress was conducted under conditions of slow cooling, which may not accurately reflect the state of highly biased bombardment. Consequently, the residual stress calculated after slow cooling in the chamber is only approximately 0.5 GPa.

Mechanical properties

Fig. 6 (a) illustrates the variations in hardness (H) and elastic modulus (E) of the films. The values of H and E were observed to exhibit an initial increase, followed by a decrease, and subsequently, a significant increase with the increment of bias within the range 0 to −800 V. In the absence of bias, the values of H and E were recorded at 12.35 GPa and 125.63 GPa, respectively. Upon increasing the negative bias to 300 V, the values of H and E were measured at 25.81 GPa and 227.55 GPa, respectively. The rise in values of H and E can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the bombardment of Ar+ ions compacts the film and breaks C-H bonds, resulting in a decrease in hydrogen fraction and an increase in film hardness and elasticity modulus [23]. Secondly, an increase in bias leads to carbon atoms being deposited in a higher energy state, facilitating the formation of more sp3 hybrid bonds as confirmed by Raman spectroscopy. This heightened concentration of sp3 bonds contributed to increased hardness [23]. Lastly, higher compressive stress can also contribute to the enhancement of film hardness through the inhibition of plastic deformation [28,41,42]. Consequently, the high compression stress of the −300 V film (−1245 MPa) effectively increased its hardness.Fig. 6Mechanical indexes of the films deposited at various biases, obtaining from nano-indentation test (a) H and E values; (b) ratios of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2.Fig. 6

Mechanical indexes of the films deposited at various biases, obtaining from nano-indentation test (a) H and E values; (b) ratios of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2.

However, as a bias beyond −300 V, a rise in substrate temperature leads to the conversion of sp3 to sp2 bands and subsequently reducing the film residual stress and hardness. This is evidenced by the decrease in H and E values to 22.22 GPa and 205.96 GPa, respectively, for the film bias at −500 V. Following bias reaches to −600 V, higher bias voltages results in the generation of a greater number of high-energy particles (atoms, ions, and atom clusters), intensifying the implantation effect on the subsurface of the film and promoting the formation of a more sp3 bond structure [34].

Furthermore, the rapid escalation of residual compressive stress, as presented in Fig. 5, as well as the pinning effect of dislocations and pits, causing a notable increase in the values of H and E, culminating in measurements of 31.55 GPa and 279.48 GPa at −600 V, and 39.05 GPa and 345.72 GPa at −800 V, respectively.

The endurance capability index (H/E∗) plays a crucial role in determining a film's ability to withstand elastic deformation and cracking, while the resistance factor to plastic deformation (H3/E∗2) is utilized to predict wear resistance and tribological behavior of DLC films [28,[43], [44], [45]]. A higher ratio of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2 indicates a lower wear rate. It should be noted that E∗=E (1-ν2), where E represents Young's modulus and ν is Poisson's ratio (in this case, ν=0.3). Fig. 6 (b) exhibits that as the films are biased from 0 to −800 V, the values of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2 change concurrently. Initially, there is an increase in the range of 0 to −300 V, followed by a decrease from −300 to −500 V, and then another increase from −500 to −800 V. In general, a lower H/E∗ value indicates elastic–plastic behavior and implies that the film has a weak resistance to cracking, whereas a higher value indicates a higher resistance to plastic deformation [4,28,34,43]. Furthermore, a higher H/E∗ ratio suggests an increased presence of C-C sp3 bonding or an augmented diamond-like structure [4]. Fig. 6b indicates that the −300 V film possesses a high sp3 content, and it exhibits a notable resistance to cracking and plastic deformation. However, spanning from 0 to −800 V, the −800 V film shows the highest ratio of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2, indicating superior wear resistance, strong resistance to plastic deformation, and low risk of brittle fracture [39,43].

Table 3 shows the mechanical properties parameters of DLC films, with each value presenting the average of five indentations and error bars derived from their standard deviation [23]. When the bias is below −500 V, the film biased at −300 V exhibits the highest ratios of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2, measuring 0.125 and 0.403 GPa, respectively. This finding suggests that DLC films biased at −300 V possess superior mechanical properties and resistance to cracking and wear. Furthermore, when subjected to a constant external load and test configuration, a DLC film with the highest H3/E∗2 value is least prone to plastic deformation and consequently exhibits enhanced wear resistance. Adhesion strength

Fig. 7 displays the AE curves for the DLC film, with corresponding optical scratch traces depicted in Fig. 8. By combining AE signals with microscopic images of scratches, the adhesion of the DLC film can be determined. In the adhesion assessment, Lc1 represents the critical load at which the substrate is initially exposed, while Lc2 signifies the complete detachment of the film from the substrate [43,[46], [47], [48]]. In this context, Lc2 is denoted as the ultimate adhesion of the film. The findings revealed that the unbiased film exhibited adhesion of 16.8 N (Lc2), with initial exfoliation occurring at a mere 6.5 N (Lc1) due to its low crack resistance index of 0.108 and low hardness of 12.35 GPa. As bias increased, adhesion also increased, with the −300 V film showing a high adhesion of 20.3 N and improved crack resistance of 11.5 N. However, in the presence of a −500 V bias, the thermal expansion of the substrate exacerbates the separation between adjacent layers, resulting in increased interface discontinuity. This phenomenon ultimately diminishes the hardness, mechanical properties, and the film's adhesion. Thus, compared to the film bias at −300 V, the adhesion of the −500 V film reduced to 12.3 N, and the Lc1 also decline to 7.6 N. In the case of the −800 V film, the AE curve exhibits a notable consistent and stable trajectory with minimal fluctuations. It is noteworthy that no complete exposure was observed within the range of 0–30 N, instead, a slight peeling was only observed at 23.8 N (Lc1). This observation suggests that the adhesion of the −800 V film is substantial, with the critical load (Lc2) surpassing 30 N. Clearly, the film biased at 800 V has sufficient adhesion to protect substrate to subject long-term wear. In the following, we analyze the adhesion mechanism of films subjected at various bias levels. The increased adhesion observed in films exposed to low bias levels (0 to −300 V) is primarily ascribed to a notable elevation in mechanical properties, which allow the film to withstand shear and normal loads exerted by the diamond tip. The adhesion of the film is reduced within a medium range of −300 to −500 V as a result of expansion caused by phase transition and elevated substrate temperature, leading to discontinuity between layers. Additionally, the decrease in film hardness and mechanical properties renders it unable to resist shear and normal loads applied by the diamond tip. Conversely, the robust adhesion of the high bias film (−500 to −800 V) can be elucidated by the compressive stress-induced squeezing and atoms implantation effects, as illustrated in Fig. 9.Fig. 9Schematic illustration of the adhesion mechanism for highly biased films.Fig. 9

Schematic illustration of the adhesion mechanism for highly biased films.

At the outset, incident ions are introduced to the surface of the film through prolonged application of high bias, leading to a progressive elevation of internal stress. With continued ion bombardment on the film surface, the residual stress within the film surpasses a critical threshold, causing it to propagate towards the thicker regions within the film until it reaches the interface with the substrate. Ultimately, the film undergoes heightened compaction as a result of sustained high-energy bombardment, resulting in an escalation of internal stress and subsequent extrusion and compression at the interface between the DLC layer and the substrate. This phenomenon leads to the immobilization of atoms and ions deposited on the AZ31 surface due to the accumulation of stress and interfacial compression effects.

Concurrently, the rise in surface temperature of the AZ31 serves to facilitate diffusion by supplying ample thermodynamic energy, enabling carbon atoms to penetrate the soft matrix and form a thin injection layer on the surface. The substantial disparity in size between carbon and metallic atoms results in the compression of small carbon atoms within the DLC film into the interstitial space between larger metallic atoms under high levels of residual compressive stress. This deduction is supported by the observation of bomb craters and high density dislocations in Fig. 3d and e, indicating the existence of considerable compressive stresses within the DLC films. The robust adhesion exhibited by the −800 V film can be ascribed to the interfacial bonding between carbon and metallic atoms, as well as the mitigation of residual stress facilitated by the B interlayer. Additionally, the gradual decrease in bias voltage post-deposition also contributes to enhance the adhesion of the film, which allows slow cooling to alleviate residual stress.

Tribological properties

Fig. 10 (a) illustrates the coefficient of friction (COF) of the samples during dry sliding, showing a transient period of fluctuation followed by stabilization. The COF of AZ31 exhibits high and fluctuating values, with an average of approximately 0.6, whereas DLC films demonstrate significantly lower and more uniform values in comparison to AZ31. In the absence of any applied bias, the COF stabilizes at 0.25 after an initial run-in phase under normal load. Following 480 s of sliding, stripped fragments are introduced into the frictional track where they undergo grinding by the Si3N4 sphere, serving as a lubricant and resulting in a slight reduction in the COF. As the friction process progresses, the surface of the unbiased film undergoes damage, resulting in the generation of a substantial amount of wear debris that enters the worn track. The accumulation of this debris within the track impedes the movement of the Si3N4 ball, leading to fluctuations in the COF after 1370 s. The film at −300 V exhibits notable enhancement in hardness and mechanical properties, resulting in a more streamlined frictional process and a reduced COF of 0.12 when compared to the unbiased case. In contrast, the film at −400 V initially experiences a sharp rise in COF to 0.15, which then stabilizes at 0.15. For the −800 V film, it demonstrates the lowest COF of approximately 0.04, attributed to its exceptional hardness exceeding 39 GPa, as well as its highest H/E and H3/E∗2 ratio, enabling it to withstand the Si3N4 ball.

As depicted in Fig. 10b, the wear rate of the unbiased film is recorded as 11.72 × 10−8 mm3/Nm, which is the highest among the tested samples. In comparison, the wear rate of the −300 V film is significantly lower at approximately 3.13 × 10−8 mm3/Nm. The wear rate of the −400 V film is measured at 5.67 × 10−8 mm3/Nm, surpassing that of the −300 V film. The −800 V film demonstrates the lowest wear rate among the four groups, registering at 0.89 × 10−8 mm3/Nm. This notable reduction in wear rate exhibited by the −800 V film compared to its Si3N4 counterpart results in a substantial enhancement in the wear resistance of the coated AZ31. Additionally, Raman spectroscopy was conducted on the surface of the Si3N4 sphere in contact with the −600 V film, as illustrated in Fig. 12d. The Gaussian fitting results indicate that the ID/IG ratio is approximately 1.06, which is notably higher than the corresponding ratio of 1.01 observed on the surface of the −600 V film, as reported in Table 2. This finding suggests the formation of a graphitized transfer film, indicating that the actual sliding interface has been transformed into a graphitized film and a graphitized transfer film.

The following discourse will focus on the wear mechanisms of DLC films under varying bias voltages. In instances of unbiased film, the friction process initiates with the exfoliation of large particles from the film surface, resulting in a temporary fluctuation in friction. Subsequently, a small mount of particles undergoes crushing into small fragments, serving as a lubricant to facilitate a smoother friction process and reduce the COF. As an escalating number of large particles enter the wear track, causing a buildup of particles at the interface and increasing frictional resistance during sliding, these particles begin to abrade the wear track, resulting in the formation of some furrows and cracks due to the insufficient hardness of the film. Simultaneously, the rise in frictional resistance results in the production of frictional heat, leading to an increased absorption of oxygen from the surrounding air into the frictional orbit. As the duration of the friction process increases, the quantity of furrows and cracks also increases, leading to the breakdown of the film due to its low crack resistance index. Subsequently, a large portion of the film detaches from the substrate under the action of shear forces, ultimately resulting in coating failure. Based on the COF curve and the observed characteristics of the worn surface, it can be deduced that the wear mechanism of the unbiased film is a blend of abrasive, adhesive, and oxidative wear.

When subjected to a bias voltage of −300 V, films initially experience detachment of minute particles on their surfaces under the normal load and tangential force of the counterpart. These particles then enter the wear track, causing abrasion and the generation of minor furrows. Despite this, the film maintains its structural integrity and does not exhibit significant splintering or cracking, a phenomenon attributed to its high hardness and the ratio of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2. Moreover, Fig. 12a illustrates that the ratio of sp2/sp3 on the surface of the Si3N4 ball is 1.78, a significantly higher value compared to the ratio within original film (1.09) as exhibited in Table 2. This indicates the transfer of graphitized tribofilm to the counter surface in the course of sliding. Consequently, the actual sliding interface transitions from the interaction between DLC and Si3N4 ball to that of graphitized tribofilm and graphitized transfer film [52]. This alteration initiates abrasive wear of the −300 V film, followed by the formation of a transfer film predominantly composed of sp2 phases, ultimately decreasing both the COF and wear loss rate. During the friction process, the graphitized layer is consistently transferred onto the Si3N4 ball surface, leading to the formation of a dense transfer film [52]. This continuous and compact transfer membrane effectively inhibit direct contact between frictional pairs, resulting in a notable increase in wear resistance.

Regarding the 400 V film, the film is susceptible to cracking under normal loads and shear forces exerted by the counter body, owing to its diminished cracking resistance and adhesion. As the wear process persists, the cracks will expand, leading to the detachment of small debris into the wear track. Consequently, the surface of the wear track is scratched by the debris, resulting in the formation of plough marks. This phenomenon exemplifies a classic illustration of the abrasive wear mechanism. The findings of this work demonstrate that at a bias voltage of 400 V, an increase in matrix temperature leads to the conversion of a portion of sp3 bonds to sp2 bonds. This suggests that a higher concentration of sp2 bonds within the film may be transferred to the counter body during the friction process, leading to the formation of a transfer film at the friction interface that effectively reduces friction. The XPS analysis conducted on the surface of the Si3N4 ball substantiated this deduction. The analysis revealed a sp2/sp3 ratio of 2.11 on the surface of the counter body, as illustrated in Fig. 12b. This ratio is twice that of the deposited film, suggesting a significant transfer of sp2 phase to the Si3N4 surface, resulting in the formation of a transfer film during the frictional process. The presence of a transfer film separates the counter body from direct contact with the DLC film. Therefore, despite potential reductions in hardness, adhesion, and mechanical properties of the −400 V film, the wear track observed in Fig. 11c suggests that the film remains effective in safeguarding the substrate against wear failure.

In contrast, the −800 V film, its exceptional hardness and ratio of H/E∗ and H3/E∗2 contribute to a low propensity for cracking and the ability to withstand wear. Consequently, the −800 V film shows a minimal wear scar width and maintains its initial structural characteristics of diamond-like carbon, indicating an increase in disorder and the formation of additional sp3 bonding. The increased hardness and elastic modulus contribute to enhancing the wear resistance of the film [1,42,53]. Additionally, the robust adhesion of the −800 V film prevents the detachment of coating from the substrate during friction, resulting in superficial wear marks and the absence of substantial grooves, thereby further improving its wear resistance. The predominant wear mechanism of the −800 V film is still characterized by abrasion.

Conclusion

The enhanced adhesion, mechanical properties and wear resistance of a DLC film on a soft substrate are achieved with bias voltage. Moreover, the formation of the transfer layer is another factor in its exceptional wear resistance. Adhesive strength, mechanical properties, and wear resistance demonstrate an initial increase at low bias levels, followed by a decrease at moderate levels, and a substantial increase at high bias levels. Additionally, the three-layered structure and a gradual cooling process following deposition, serves to reduce residual stress, as well as improve the adhesion and wear resistance of the film. Regrettably, the study of the effects of higher voltages (greater than −800 V) on film adhesion, mechanical properties, and wear resistance is not conducted in this research due to time limitations. This aspect will be addressed in future research endeavors.
