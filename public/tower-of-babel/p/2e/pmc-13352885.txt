Concurrent lumbar degenerative disease is independently associated with chronic post-surgical pain after total knee arthroplasty for knee osteoarthritis: a retrospective study
Authors: Xichen Zhang, Xinfei Zhu, Dunliang Rao, Lei Sha, Liang Hao, Shouhai Jiang, Ziyang Zhang, Chuankai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChronic post-surgical pain (CPSP) after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains a significant clinical challenge. This study aimed to investigate the association between concurrent lumbar degenerative disease (LDD) and CPSP after TKA for knee osteoarthritis (KOA).MethodsA total of 348 KOA patients undergoing first unilateral TKA (June 2023-June 2025) were enrolled and stratified into LDD (n = 167) and non-LDD (n = 181) groups. CPSP incidence, pain severity, and postoperative knee function were compared. Logistic regression models were constructed to identify influencing factors for CPSP after TKA.ResultsAt 6 months postoperatively, both groups showed significant improvements in knee function, mobility, and pain (all P < 0.05). Compared with the non-LDD group, the LDD group exhibited lower knee society scores, reduced knee range of motion, a shorter 6-minute walk distance, a longer timed up and go test time, higher visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for pain at rest and during activity, a higher rate of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use, and a higher incidence of CPSP (all P < 0.05). Depression/anxiety history, preoperative VAS scores, and LDD were independently associated with CPSP after TKA in patients with KOA (all P < 0.05). Among patients with KOA with concurrent LDD, depression/anxiety history, preoperative VAS scores, and preoperative radicular symptoms were independently associated with CPSP after TKA (all P < 0.05).ConclusionLDD is independently associated with CPSP after TKA. In patients with KOA with concurrent LDD, preoperative radicular symptoms, depression/anxiety history, and intense preoperative pain further increase CPSP risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-026-03839-7.Keywords: Lumbar degenerative disease, Total knee arthroplasty, Chronic post-surgical pain, Influencing factors, Retrospective study

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA), characterized by joint pain that worsens with activity and subsides with rest, imposes a significant global disability burden. Its incidence is projected to surge by 74.9% by 2050 [1, 2]. Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) serves as the gold standard procedure for end-stage KOA, effectively alleviating pain, correcting joint deformity, restoring function, and significantly improving quality of life [3]. However, chronic post-surgical pain (CPSP) remains a common and challenging complication that severely limits optimal surgical outcomes, with reported incidence rates ranging from 10% to 34% [4, 5]. Affected patients endure persistent pain that impedes recovery, elevates the risk of complications, and prolongs both hospital stays and functional rehabilitation [6]. Therefore, systematically identifying influencing factors for CPSP and implementing targeted interventions has become a critical clinical priority in joint surgery.

Previous investigations into risk factors for CPSP following TKA have primarily focused on local knee-related elements. These include preoperative pain intensity, severity of joint deformity, surgical technical details, prosthesis type, and compliance with postoperative rehabilitation [7, 8]. Nevertheless, some patients develop persistent pain postoperatively despite optimal local knee conditions and standardized surgical technique. This suggests that, beyond local factors, systemic influences and comorbid conditions may contribute to the development and persistence of CPSP [9]. In recent years, the roles of systemic factors such as central sensitization and psychological state have gained considerable attention [10, 11]. Concurrent spinal pathology, particularly lumbar degenerative disease (LDD), represents a highly clinically relevant issue due to its complex anatomical and neurological connections with lower limb pain [12].

Sensory innervation of the knee joint relies primarily on the lumbar plexus (L2-L4 nerve roots) [13]. Stimulation or compression of these nerve roots due to LDD can provoke knee region pain through referred or radicular pain mechanisms, creating symptomatic overlap with pain originating from KOA [14]. This overlap not only complicates the preoperative differentiation of pain sources, potentially leading to clinical misjudgment, but may also persistently influence pain perception after TKA, thereby increasing the risk of chronic pain [15]. However, in current clinical practice, a systematic evaluation of the lumbar spine, particularly for asymptomatic or atypically presenting degenerative conditions, is not included in the standard preoperative workup for TKA. This omission may underestimate the impact of spinal pathology on patients’ pain symptoms and postoperative recovery. Although KOA and LDD frequently coexist in clinical practice, a scenario sometimes termed “knee-spine syndrome” [16], high-quality evidence is still lacking regarding whether concurrent LDD constitutes an independent risk factor for CPSP after TKA. This knowledge gap prevents the identification of specific risk factors for CPSP in patients with KOA with concurrent LDD and hinders the development of precise clinical assessment and intervention strategies.

To address this research gap, the present study intends to employ a retrospective cohort design enrolling patients with KOA undergoing unilateral TKA. It aims to investigate the association between preoperative LDD and CPSP and to identify the factors contributing to CPSP in the subgroup of patients with LDD. The findings may refine the perioperative assessment framework for TKA and provide a theoretical basis for optimizing preoperative patient counseling and formulating individualized analgesia and rehabilitation strategies.

Materials and methods

Subjects

A single-center retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the electronic medical record system (EMRS). A total of 421 patients undergoing first unilateral TKA for KOA in Xuzhou Renci Hospital between June 2023 and June 2025 were consecutively selected. After excluding 15 patients who did not meet inclusion criteria, 27 who met the exclusion criteria, and 31 with incomplete clinical data, 348 patients with KOA were ultimately enrolled.

All patients underwent TKA surgery at our hospital and received standardized postoperative rehabilitation guidance from the same rehabilitation team in accordance with departmental protocols. These patients were divided into an LDD group (n = 167) and a non-LDD group (n = 181) based on the presence of concurrent LDD prior to surgery. All patients with LDD had been diagnosed with lumbar degenerative changes via lumbar MRI or CT prior to TKA based on relevant symptoms, with the diagnosis made at least 6 months before TKA. Cases of postoperative onset or diagnosis of LDD (i.e., patients with no history of LDD or radiographic evidence prior to TKA who developed LDD during the 6-month postoperative follow-up period) were excluded from this study. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuzhou Renci Hospital. Informed consent was waived, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. (Ethical review approval: XZRCLL-LW-202508001)

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) diagnosis of KOA according to the relevant criteria outlined in the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons Appropriate Use Criteria: Surgical Management of KOA [17], confirmed by clinical manifestations, physical examination, and imaging studies such as X-ray; (2) diagnosis of concurrent LDD based on the presence of chronic low back pain with or without lower limb radicular symptoms such as radiating pain and numbness, and confirmed by degenerative changes (lumbar disc herniation, spinal stenosis, facet joint degeneration, or degenerative spondylolisthesis) on lumbar magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography; (3) first-time unilateral TKA for primary KOA; and (4) availability of complete preoperative assessment data and postoperative follow-up records.

Exclusion criteria: (1) patients scheduled for staged bilateral TKA; (2) history of prior ipsilateral knee or hip surgery; (3) documented neurological disorders that could significantly affect pain perception and assessment, such as diabetic peripheral neuropathy, complex regional pain syndrome, Parkinson’s disease, or cognitive impairment; or (4) severe postoperative complications.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from the hospital’s EMRS by two trained researchers independently, followed by cross-verification. The collected data encompassed the following categories:

Demographics and general characteristics: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia), depression/anxiety history, smoking, and alcohol use.

Knee-specific factors: affected side, disease duration, severity of valgus deformity (classified according to the Keblish classification: anatomical tibiofemoral valgus angle of 5°-7° as normal, 7° to < 15° as mild, 15°-30° as moderate, and > 30° as severe), as well as knee range of motion (ROM), knee society score (KSS), timed up and go (TUG) test time, and 6-minute walk distance (6MWD) measured at 1 week preoperatively and 6 months postoperatively.

Pain assessment: Visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for pain and analgesic use such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) at 1 week preoperatively and 6 months postoperatively.

Perioperative variables: Surgical time, anesthetic use, total blood loss, tourniquet time, and postoperative hospital stay.

LDD characteristics: involved segments, type of LDD (e.g., lumbar disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and degenerative spondylolisthesis), and preoperative radicular symptoms (lower limb radiating pain, numbness, or paresthesia).

KSS scoring system

The KSS comprised two components, each scored out of 100 points, with higher scores indicating better outcomes. The clinical score assessed the knee joint itself, including pain (50 points), ROM (25 points), and stability (25 points), with deductions applied for malalignment, flexion contracture, or extensor lag. The functional score evaluated the patient’s ability to perform daily activities, including walking (50 points) and stair climbing (50 points), with deductions for the use of walking aids. This system allowed for separate evaluation of joint status and functional ability, providing a comprehensive assessment of TKA outcomes.

Assessment of functional mobility recovery

Functional mobility was assessed using the TUG test and the 6MWD test. (1) TUG test: This test evaluated basic mobility and fall risk. Participants stood up from a chair with armrests, walked 3 m in a straight line at their usual pace, turned around, walked back, and sat down. The time taken was recorded. Results were interpreted based on time: for older adults, a time of less than 10 s was generally considered normal; 10–20 s indicated basic independent mobility but a certain risk of falling; and more than 20 s suggested significantly reduced mobility and a high risk of falling.

(2) 6MWD test: This test assessed participants’ exercise tolerance and overall functional status by measuring the maximum distance they could walk on a flat, hard-surfaced corridor within six minutes. The measured distance was compared to predicted values based on age, sex, height, and weight. Typically, healthy adults can walk 400–700 m. Results were influenced by multiple factors and should be interpreted in the clinical context.

Pain assessment

VAS scores for pain at rest and during activity, both preoperatively and 6 months postoperatively, were summarized. CPSP was defined as a VAS score ≥ 4 at rest or during activity 6 months postoperatively [18]. Specifically, a 10-cm line was used, anchored with “no pain” (0 points) at one end and “worst pain imaginable” (10 points) at the other. The patients marked a point on the line corresponding to their perceived pain: 0 (no pain); 1–3 (mild pain that does not interfere with sleep); 4–6 (moderate pain that interferes with sleep but is tolerable); 7–10 (severe pain that is intolerable and significantly impairs sleep and daily activities).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphing were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Normally distributed continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test, while comparisons between pre- and post-operative measurements within the same group were performed using the paired samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as median (Q1, Q3). Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, while comparisons between pre- and post-operative measurements within the same group were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Count data were expressed as n (%). Intergroup comparisons were performed using the chi-square test, and intragroup comparisons were performed using the paired chi-square test. Univariate logistic regression was employed to analyze the factors associated with CPSP following TKA, and variables with P < 0.05 were included in the multivariate logistic regression model. Multicollinearity was assessed. Variance inflation factor (VIF) < 5 and tolerance > 0.1 indicated no severe multicollinearity among the variables included in the model. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics

No significant differences were observed between the LDD group and the non-LDD group regarding sex, age, BMI, comorbidities, smoking/alcohol history, affected side, disease duration, severity of valgus deformity, or perioperative variables (all P > 0.05). Among the 167 patients with concomitant LDD, the most common distribution of LDD segments was two segments (72 cases, 43.11%), followed by a single segment (65 cases, 38.92%) and multiple segments (≥ 3) (30 cases, 17.96%). The most commonly affected segment was L4/5 (145 cases, 86.83%), followed by L3/4 (102 cases, 61.08%) and L5/S1 (88 cases, 52.69%). Disc herniation was the predominant type of LDD, occurring in 82 cases (49.10%). Spinal stenosis was observed in 53 cases (31.74%), degenerative spondylolisthesis in 20 cases (11.98%), and other or mixed types in 12 cases (7.19%). Preoperatively, 112 patients (67.07%) presented with radicular symptoms (Table 1).

Factors influencing CPSP after TKA in patients with KOA

The incidence of CPSP after TKA at 6 months postoperatively was 24.14% (84/348). Univariate logistic regression was performed with CPSP occurrence (0 = no, 1 = yes) as the dependent variable and patient demographics, preoperative knee function, and pain scores as independent variables. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Multicollinearity diagnostics found that the VIF for all included variables ranged from 1.017 to 1.051, and the tolerance ranged from 0.952 to 0.984, indicating no significant multicollinearity (Supplementary Table 1). Depression/anxiety history (OR: 4.378; 95% CI: 2.006–9.555), preoperative VAS score at rest (OR: 1.508; 95% CI: 1.211–1.879), preoperative VAS score during activity (OR: 1.497; 95% CI: 1.183–1.893), and LDD (OR: 2.215; 95% CI: 1.282–3.826) were independently associated with CPSP after TKA in patients with KOA (all P < 0.05, Table 4). Moreover, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded P = 0.084, indicating good model fit without significant overfitting.

Analysis of factors influencing TKA-CPSP in the overall KOA cohort

Factors influencing CPSP after TKA in patients with KOA with concurrent LDD

The incidence of CPSP after TKA in patients with KOA with concurrent LDD was 31.14% (52/167). Univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted within this subgroup with CPSP occurrence (0 = no, 1 = yes) as the dependent variable and patient demographics, preoperative knee function, and pain score as independent variables. Variables with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were then entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. Multicollinearity analysis revealed that the VIF for all included variables ranged from 1.012 to 1.023, and the tolerance ranged from 0.978 to 0.988, indicating no significant multicollinearity (Supplementary Table 2). Among patients with KOA with concurrent LDD, depression/anxiety history (OR: 7.333; 95% CI: 2.264–23.757), preoperative VAS score at rest (OR: 1.654; 95% CI: 1.203–2.275), preoperative VAS score during activity (OR: 1.454; 95% CI: 1.051–2.011), and preoperative radicular symptoms (OR: 4.386; 95% CI: 1.765–10.899) were independently associated with CPSP after TKA (all P < 0.05, Table 5). Furthermore, the Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded P = 0.625, indicating no significant discrepancy between the model’s predictions and the observed data as well as adequate model fit, without overfitting.

Analysis of independent risk factors for TKA-CPSP in KOA patients with LDD

Discussion

This retrospective analysis of 348 patients undergoing unilateral TKA provides systematic evidence that preoperative LDD is independently associated with CPSP after TKA. Compared with patients without LDD, those with LDD exhibited significantly worse functional recovery, a higher incidence of CPSP, and more severe pain at 6 months postoperatively. Within the LDD cohort, preoperative radicular symptoms, depression/anxiety history, and preoperative pain intensity were independently associated with CPSP after TKA. These findings not only highlight the critical role of LDD in CPSP after TKA but also provide valuable evidence for the clinical identification of high-risk patients and the implementation of stratified intervention strategies.

Our results demonstrated that although both groups showed significant improvements in knee function, mobility, and pain by six months postoperatively, patients with concurrent LDD had inferior recovery. Specifically, this group exhibited significantly lower KSS clinical and functional scores, reduced knee ROM, a longer TUG test time, and a shorter 6MWD compared with the non-LDD group. A population-based cohort study has reported that the coexistence of low back pain and lumbar kyphosis in women with KOA is associated with lower KSS symptom scores [19]. Similarly, a retrospective case series has found that knee arc of motion does not significantly improve after TKA in patients with pre-existing severe lumbar spinal stenosis (LSS) [20]. Another study has noted that in patients with lumbar spine sagittal mismatch deformity, postoperative range of motion and flexion contracture are limited following TKA [21]. The LDD group had significantly higher VAS scores at rest and during activity, a higher rate of NSAID use, and a greater incidence of CPSP. This is consistent with a previous study showing that KOA patients with comorbid LSS experience less pain improvement following a conservative intervention program compared to those without stenotic symptoms [22]. Furthermore, Pivec et al. have suggested that spinal stenosis was associated with worse clinical outcomes after TKA [12]. Our results corroborate clinical observations of suboptimal recovery in patients with knee-spine syndrome [15] and indicate that LDD is not merely a comorbidity but rather a key negative prognostic factor for TKA outcomes.

The observed association between LDD and CPSP may be explained by several plausible but as yet unconfirmed mechanisms. Mechanistically, LDD may exacerbate postoperative pain and impede recovery through several pathways. First, lumbar nerve root compression from LDD can cause lower limb radicular pain, which may superimpose on residual periarticular pain after TKA, creating a pain amplification effect and increasing the risk of chronification [16]. Second, chronic nerve root irritation may heighten the excitability of dorsal horn neurons, inducing central sensitization. This lowers the pain perception threshold, making patients prone to persistent pain dysregulation even after local knee inflammation subsides [4, 23]. Effective analgesia, such as peripheral nerve blocks targeting key knee innervations like the saphenous nerve, has been shown to reduce opioid use, improve function, and decrease CPSP incidence after TKA [24], underscoring the importance of comprehensive pain control. It should be noted that, although these signaling pathways are biologically coherent, the retrospective nature of our study precludes definitive causal inferences. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypotheses to guide future mechanistic investigations. Third, preoperative diagnostic uncertainty regarding the primary pain source may result in surgery failing to address the patient’s fundamental pain generator. Postoperative persistent neurogenic pain can reduce compliance with rehabilitation, further impairing functional recovery. The high prevalence of preoperative radicular symptoms (67.07%) in our LDD cohort likely compounded these negative effects.

Concurrent LDD was independently associated with CPSP after TKA in patients with KOA, along with depression/anxiety history and preoperative VAS scores at rest and during activity. Previous studies have indicated that coexisting lumbar kyphosis is an adverse clinical indicator associated with functional impairments in patients with KOA [25], and that mental health conditions and pre-existing pain are significant predictors for CPSP in patients undergoing TKA [9]. Notably, our subgroup analysis within the LDD cohort revealed that, besides psychological history and preoperative pain, the presence of preoperative radicular symptoms was independently associated with CPSP. This refines risk stratification, moving from identifying a high-risk population to precisely stratifying risk within that group. It aligns with prior observations that patients with mild to moderate lumbar degenerative symptoms without severe radiating pain are more likely to experience symptom relief after total knee replacement [26]. For KOA patients with LDD, preoperative assessment for radicular symptoms can optimize risk prediction and provide targets for individualized intervention.

Furthermore, this study confirmed that depression/anxiety history was associated with CPSP in both the overall cohort and the LDD subgroup, exhibiting a synergistic effect with LDD, which aligns with the biopsychosocial model of pain [27]. Depression and anxiety may increase the risk of pain chronification by lowering pain thresholds, exacerbating central sensitization, and reducing rehabilitation adherence [28]. Patients with concurrent LDD, burdened by dual somatic pain, may be particularly susceptible to developing or worsening psychological distress, potentially creating a vicious cycle of somatic pain-psychological distress-pain chronification. This underscores the need for perioperative pain management in TKA to address both somatic and psychological factors. Psychological interventions may be a crucial adjunct, especially for high-risk patients with LDD.

The findings of this study have direct clinical implications. Based on the findings of this study, the following clinical translation pathways are proposed for practical reference. First, all patients undergoing TKA should routinely be screened for LDD through a detailed medical history and physical examination. For patients with LDD and preoperative radicular symptoms, a preoperative spinal surgery consultation should be strongly considered to identify the primary source of pain and optimize treatment, which may require addressing the lumbar spine pathology prior to TKA. Furthermore, this raises the possibility that, for certain high-risk patients, lumbar interventions prior to TKA, such as targeted epidural steroid injections or decompression surgery for severe radicular symptoms, may reduce the incidence of CPSP. This hypothesis requires validation in future large-scale prospective trials. Second, they support optimizing individualized intervention strategies. For high-risk patients with LDD, particularly those with radicular symptoms or a psychological history, a preoperative multidisciplinary consultation involving orthopedics, pain management, and psychology could facilitate tailored plans. These might include short-term analgesic prehabilitation, symptomatic treatment for nerve root compression, psychological support, enhanced multimodal analgesia postoperatively, extended follow-up, and timely rehabilitation adjustments. Third, they highlight the importance of improving patient education. Preoperatively informing patients with LDD about their specific risks for postoperative pain and the expected trajectory of functional recovery may enhance rehabilitation adherence and mitigate psychological distress arising from unmet expectations.

Our study still has several inherent limitations. First, its single‑center, retrospective design may limit generalizability, and while major confounders were adjusted for, the influence of unmeasured factors such as pain beliefs, social support, and rehabilitation details could not be fully excluded. Furthermore, since a retrospective design cannot establish causality, further validation is needed through multicenter, prospective studies. Second, although the postoperative rehabilitation protocol is standardized at the department level, due to the retrospective nature of the study, it was not possible to obtain specific data on each patient’s adherence to the rehabilitation regimen (such as daily exercise frequency, duration, and whether regular follow-ups were conducted after discharge), which may introduce residual performance bias. Furthermore, defining CPSP solely based on a VAS score ≥ 4 at 6 months is overly simplistic. It does not fully reflect the complexity of CPSP. It does not account for neuropathic pain, the impact of pain on daily life, or patient satisfaction, all of which are key dimensions of CPSP. A more validated and multidimensional definition (e.g., incorporating criteria from the International Classification of Diseases, or specific neuropathic pain scales and pain interference scales) would strengthen the study’s primary findings. Future prospective studies should incorporate such tools to better characterize the phenotype of CPSP in this population. Third, the absence of long‑term follow‑up beyond one year precludes conclusions about the long‑term impact of LDD on CPSP, and no predictive model was developed or validated. Fourth, including only primary KOA patients restricts the applicability of findings to other TKA populations. Fifth, LDD itself encompasses a variety of pathological conditions (such as disc herniation, spinal stenosis, and degenerative spondylolisthesis). Different LDD subtypes may influence postoperative pain following TKA through distinct pathological pathways. For example, persistent nerve compression caused by spinal stenosis is more likely to induce central sensitization and neuropathic pain, leading to limited postoperative improvement. Whereas the dynamic compression associated with degenerative spondylolisthesis, combined with weight-bearing activities during the TKA rehabilitation period, may further increase the risk of pain. Due to the limited sample sizes in each subgroup of this study (e.g., only 53 cases of spinal stenosis and 20 cases of degenerative spondylolisthesis), statistical power was insufficient to detect the impact of specific subtypes on TKA outcomes or potential associations with CPSP. Future studies should focus on this aspect and conduct stratified analyses. Finally, this study did not further analyze the graded association between the severity of preoperative radicular symptoms and the risk of CPSP. In clinical practice, the severity of radicular symptoms varies significantly, ranging from mild, occasional numbness to persistent, severe radiating pain, and their impact on patients’ functional status and central sensitization may differ. Future studies should focus on quantifying radicular symptoms using standardized tools (e.g., the VAS or Numerical Rating Scale to measure the intensity of radiating pain, and the Oswestry Disability Index or radicular symptom-specific scales to assess functional impact). Using such data, further hierarchical correlation analyses (e.g., Spearman’s correlation) or the inclusion of symptom severity as an ordinal or continuous variable in regression models could be conducted to determine whether a dose-response relationship exists between symptom severity and the incidence of CPSP, thereby enabling the development of more refined risk prediction models. Correspondingly, future research should: (1) conduct prospective multicenter studies with extended follow‑up to validate the long‑term association of LDD and radicular symptoms with CPSP across diverse patient groups; (2) develop and validate a CPSP nomogram incorporating LDD‑related indicators for improved risk stratification; (3) employ mechanism‑oriented tools such as quantitative sensory testing to elucidate the role of central sensitization in pain chronification; and (4) design randomized trials to evaluate targeted interventions such as preoperative nerve decompression or multidisciplinary rehabilitation for preventing CPSP in this high‑risk subgroup.

In conclusion, this study establishes concurrent LDD as an independent influencing factor for CPSP after TKA in patients with KOA. Patients with KOA with concurrent LDD experience a higher incidence of postoperative pain and inferior functional recovery. These findings emphasize the necessity of incorporating lumbar assessment into the perioperative evaluation for TKA and advocate for multidimensional, individualized risk management and intervention strategies to optimize patient outcomes.
