A high-gain, low-SAR UWB all-textile two-port MIMO antenna based on an AMC structure for wireless body area networks
Authors: Saïd Douhi, Zakaria Zahriladha, Adil Eddiai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Flexible antennas have emerged as essential components for next-generation wireless body-centric networks (WBSNs), healthcare monitoring platforms, and 5G-enabled IoT architectures. Nevertheless, their close placement on the human body substantially increases electromagnetic energy absorption, resulting in elevated specific absorption rate (SAR) levels and degraded radiation performance. This paper introduces an all-textile, dual-port, ultra-wideband (UWB) MIMO antenna integrated with an artificial magnetic conductor (AMC) to concurrently enhance radiation gain and mitigate SAR for wearable applications. The proposed antenna is realized on a felt textile substrate and employs conductive textile elements to ensure mechanical flexibility and seamless garment integration. Experimental characterization demonstrates that the AMC-backed configuration achieves an impedance bandwidth ranging from 3.7 to 14 GHz, a peak gain of 14.26 dBi at 4.45 GHz, and up to 98% SAR reduction under both 1-g and 10-g mass-averaging criteria. Furthermore, deformation assessments confirm that the antenna maintains robust electromagnetic performance when bent, exhibiting behavior consistent with its planar counterpart. The fabrication process is straightforward, and the measured data exhibit excellent concordance with full-wave simulations in free-space and human-body environments. These results validate the effectiveness and reliability of the proposed textile antenna for high-performance wearable and body-centric communication systems.

Introduction

Wireless communication technologies have become a cornerstone of modern society, driven by the rapid growth of wearable devices for applications such as connected health, consumer electronics, and the Internet of Things (IoT). These systems rely on reliable, high-performance wireless connections to optimize functionality, enable real-time data exchange, and enhance the user experience. Wireless communication technologies have become a cornerstone of modern society, driven by the rapid growth of wearable devices for applications such as connected health, Internet of Things (IoT), and consumer electronics. These networks depend on stable, high-performance wireless connections to optimize functionality, enable real-time data exchange, and enhance the user experience. Traditionally, antennas are fabricated using rigid dielectric substrates and metallic conductors. However, these conventional designs present several limitations: rigid substrates restrict mechanical flexibility, making them unsuitable for integration into wearable or conformal devices, while metal-based conductors are prone to oxidation and corrosion, which can degrade performance over time. Moreover, rigid antennas often have higher weight, limited adaptability to irregular surfaces, and reduced tolerance to mechanical stress or bending. In this context, wearable and flexible antennas play a critical role, ensuring efficient and stable radio frequency (RF) signal transmission between devices and external networks. Beyond their fundamental function as radiating elements, antennas can be designed to incorporate sensing capabilities, providing multifunctional features for smart devices. Considering the increasing demands for miniaturization, mechanical flexibility, and electromagnetic performance, the development of innovative antennas suitable for smart textiles and advanced substrates has become a key challenge for next-generation communication systems1–6.

Numerous studies have explored wearable antennas, focusing on design configurations, advanced materials, fabrication processes, and specialized applications. Nevertheless, developing antennas for wearable devices entails specific obstacles that go beyond flexibility, compactness, and limited bandwidth. The close interaction of the antenna with biological tissues is crucial due to near-field effects caused by contact with human tissue. The elevated conductivity, high permittivity, and inherently lossy characteristics of biological tissues complicate electromagnetic optimization. Furthermore, energy absorption in tissues raises safety concerns, quantified by the SAR, which must comply with regulatory limits to ensure user safety7–9. A widely adopted strategy to mitigate SAR is to integrate an artificial magnetic conductor (AMC) behind the antenna. AMCs act as reflectors to minimize backward radiation and improve antenna performance near the body. Unlike a perfect electric conductor (PEC), which produces out-of-phase reflection requiring at least a quarter-wavelength spacing, AMCs can significantly reduce this distance, allowing more compact wearable designs. Additionally, AMCs enhance forward radiation and antenna gain, unlike lossy absorbing materials such as ferrite sheets, which dissipate energy. Consequently, AMCs have been extensively studied to improve radiation directivity, gain, efficiency, and SAR reduction10,11. In comparison with traditional compact planar antennas, AMC-based multilayer flexible antennas with large surface areas provide distinct benefits, including stacked resonances, reduced inter-element coupling, and enhanced beam steering—key features for advanced 5G, radar, and wearable communication systems. Nonetheless, most studies concentrate on narrow frequency bands, particularly around the 2.4 GHz and 5.8 GHz ISM frequency ranges, which restricts the overall bandwidth performance7. Ultra-wideband (UWB, 3.1–10.6 GHz) antennas provide operation over an extensive frequency range, rendering them well-suited for wireless connectivity and sensing scenarios, including WBANs12,13. Compared to narrowband antennas, UWB supports enhanced throughput, energy-efficient operation, precise measurements, and strong security. However, research on integrating AMCs for SAR reduction in UWB wearable systems remains limited, highlighting the need for innovative designs that combine wide bandwidth, optimized electromagnetic performance, and user safety. WBANs connect body-mounted or implanted biomedical devices to enable real-time physiological data transmission, playing a central role in connected healthcare, home rehabilitation, and health monitoring. Meeting the growing requirements for high throughput, minimal latency, and dependable operation in these applications requires UWB technology, which offers robustness against multipath interference and low propagation delay. Furthermore, combining UWB with MIMO configurations employing multiple transmitting and receiving antennas can achieve data rates exceeding 1 Gbps, enhance throughput, and mitigate multipath fading, making UWB-MIMO antennas highly suitable for wearable and IoT devices14–17. In compact MIMO antenna systems, the close placement of multiple radiating elements at the front-end often leads to strong mutual coupling, which becomes a major design challenge. Therefore, achieving high isolation while maintaining a reduced overall size is essential18,19. To address this issue, several isolation enhancement techniques have been widely investigated, including defected or modified ground structures14,20 neutralization lines21, decoupling networks22, and metamaterial-based solutions23. In addition, Ensuring flexibility in wearable antennas requires the use of soft substrate materials that facilitate bending and deformation24. Various materials and fabrication techniques have been explored, including inkjet-printed antennas25, polyimide (PI)26, polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)27, silicone28, metal sheets29, Composite materials30–32, and textile-based substrates33. Textiles, among humanity’s earliest materials, have played a central role in social and technological progress over the centuries. From traditional fibers including cotton and wool to contemporary synthetic materials such as nylon and polyester, their development has been marked by significant innovations that have transformed production methods. The industrial revolution represented a major turning point by introducing mechanization, which not only increased production capacity but also expanded textile applications beyond conventional clothing to advanced uses in defense, healthcare, and construction34. Textile materials, classified as natural or synthetic fibers, offer flexibility, lightweight properties, low dielectric permittivity, and straightforward integration into clothing. Among these materials, felt, a synthetic fabric made from natural fibers, stands out as an excellent substrate owing to its low surface-wave loss, broad impedance bandwidth, mechanical flexibility, and user comfort, making it highly suitable for high-performance wearable antennas35–38.

This paper presents an all-textile dual-port UWB MIMO antenna integrated with an artificial magnetic conductor (AMC) to achieve simultaneous SAR reduction and gain enhancement for wearable body-centric applications. A compact notched polygonal patch ensures wideband performance, while an L-shaped decoupling element enables the transition from a single-element radiator to a dual-port MIMO configuration with improved isolation and stable radiation characteristics. Without the AMC, the antenna covers an ultra-wide impedance bandwidth from 3.1 to 12.75 GHz. The design is fully textile-based, using felt as a low-loss flexible substrate and conductive fabric as the radiator, enabling lightweight and durable integration into garments. An AMC layer is placed beneath the antenna to suppress backward radiation and enhance user safety. The prototype, fabricated using laser-cutting techniques, shows strong agreement between measurements and simulations. With AMC backing, the antenna achieves a 3.7–14 GHz bandwidth (≈ 116%) and a peak gain of 14.26 dBi at 4.45 GHz—representing a 230% improvement over the standalone antenna. The AMC further reduces peak SAR by up to 98% for both 1-g and 10-g standards, meeting international exposure limits. These results confirm the suitability of the proposed AMC-integrated textile MIMO antenna for next-generation wearable wireless systems. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section “Antenna design” details material characterization, single-element UWB antenna design, the MIMO configuration, AMC integration, diversity performance, and fabrication processes. Section “Antenna results” presents the antenna’s performance, including reflection coefficient, radiation patterns, gain, and efficiency. Section “Analysis of antenna and application” addresses wearable performance, including human-body effects, SAR evaluation, bending tests, and comparisons with previous works. Finally, section “Conclusion” concludes the study and summarizes the key findings.

Antenna design

UWB single antenna design

To demonstrate the functionality of the AMC, a flexible UWB antenna was realized using a textile-based substrate, as shown in Fig. 1(a). UWB antennas can transmit data across an extremely broad frequency spectrum (3.1–10.6 GHz), which makes them well suited for wireless communication and sensing systems, such as WBANs. The suggested antenna is implemented on a rectangular dielectric substrate with an asymmetric configuration between the upper and lower layers. On the top surface, the radiating element adopts a modified polygonal geometry with overall dimensions denoted as wp and lp. To further improve impedance characteristics and optimize the frequency response, two symmetrical notches with lengths l1 and widths w1 are embedded along the lateral edges of the patch. The structure is excited through a microstrip feed line of length lf, which is physically coupled to the radiating patch to enable efficient energy transfer. The antenna is simulated using conductive fabric material, where both the radiating patch and the ground plane present a thickness of 0.09 mm. The substrate consists of a felt-based layer characterized by a thickness of 1 mm. On the bottom surface, an inclined partial ground plane with dimensions a and b is implemented. The truncated ground structure broadens the impedance bandwidth and stabilizes the radiation performance across the operating band. Table 1 summarizes the optimized geometrical parameters of the single antenna configuration, with all dimensions expressed in millimeters (mm). As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the proposed antenna maintains ∣S11∣ less than − 10 dB from 1.50 to 12.81 GHz, corresponding to an impedance bandwidth of 11.31 GHz.

UWB MIMO antenna design and AMC integration

In order to improve the overall performance, the single-element antenna described in the previous section has been extended to a two-port MIMO configuration, as illustrated in Fig. 2. In this design, two identical radiating elements are placed side by side on the top surface of the substrate, separated by an edge-to-edge spacing denoted as K1​. Each element is excited through an independent microstrip feed line, ensuring dual-port operation. A partial ground plane is maintained on the lower surface. In the initial configuration (Fig. 2(a)), the ground plane is continuous with two inclined rectangular sections corresponding to each element. However, due to the proximity of the radiators, strong mutual coupling occurs, which degrades the isolation and overall MIMO performance. To mitigate this effect, an L-shaped decoupling structure is introduced between the two elements, as shown in the Fig. 2(b). This isolation block, formed through the introduction of L-shaped stubs within the ground plane, effectively suppresses surface current coupling and improves the isolation between ports without significantly altering the impedance bandwidth or radiation patterns. Consequently, the integration of the notched polygonal radiating elements with an inclined partial ground plane and an additional L-shaped isolation structure leads to a space-efficient two-port MIMO antenna exhibiting enhanced isolation, and wideband performance. Table 2 presents the corresponding dimensions of the designed MIMO antenna. All dimensions are given in millimeters (mm).

As shown in Fig. 3(a), the reflection coefficient |S11| without the L-shaped isolation block achieves good impedance matching over the frequency range 1.38–12.75 GHz, whereas with the L-shaped isolation block, the effective bandwidth slightly shifts to 3.1–12.75 GHz, exhibiting a resonance peak at 3.36 GHz and a narrow region between 3.53 and 3.98 GHz where |S11| slightly exceeds − 10 dB. Figure 3(b) illustrates the isolation characteristics between the two ports. Without the isolation block, S12​ remains relatively high across several frequencies, limiting isolation. With the L-shaped isolation block, S12 is significantly reduced, achieving values well below − 20 dB over most of the operating band, thereby enhancing the isolation between antenna elements. Owing to the antenna’s symmetrical structure, it is noted that S12 = S21 and S11 ​= S22​. Furthermore, to gain deeper insight into the functioning of the two-port MIMO antenna system, the distribution of surface currents at 3.5 GHz, 5 GHz, and 10 GHz was simulated and depicted in Fig. 4. It can be clearly seen that the surface currents are mostly concentrated on and in the vicinity of Antenna 1, with minimal coupling toward Antenna 2, when the port corresponding to Antenna 1 is excited and the remaining ports are terminated with 50 Ω matched impedances. This observation confirms the low mutual coupling and the high level of electromagnetic isolation between adjacent radiating elements (Table 3).

AMC structure integration

After optimizing the antenna, an AMC structure was implemented to improve performance and provide safe operation for body-worn applications. Its main purpose is to block backward radiation toward the user, thereby reducing the SAR while maintaining antenna efficiency. Placing the AMC between the antenna and the body not only limits electromagnetic wave penetration but also enhances antenna performance, particularly by increasing forward gain and reinforcing radiation in the desired direction. As illustrated in Fig. 5(a), the proposed AMC is composed of 117 square unit cells arranged in a 13 × 9 lattice. The total dimensions are Wd = Ld = 100 mm, with each unit cell having sides of ax = ay = 10 mm and an inter-element spacing of Wh= 0.6 mm. The shape and configuration of the electrodes strongly influence microwave resonances, which ultimately determine the AMC response. The AMC structure is realized on a felt substrate with a thickness of t3 = 2 mm, relative permittivity εr = 1.2, and loss tangent tanδ = 0.02. The conductive parts, including the AMC patches and ground plane, are fabricated using highly conductive textile materials. The AMC adopts a four-layer sandwich configuration (conductive fabric–felt–spacer–felt–conductive fabric), as shown in Fig. 5(b). A 5 mm thick foam layer (t2) is positioned between the AMC and the antenna, acting as a spacer to preserve the appropriate separation and prevent electrical shorting. Figure 5(c) illustrates the reflection behavior of the AMC, where a strong interaction occurs between the wave reflected by the AMC and the directly radiated wave from the antenna in the near-field region. For optimal operation, the AMC must produce in-phase reflection across the UWB band to minimize destructive interference. Achieving this requires precise selection and fabrication of structural parameters to align the AMC resonance frequency with the antenna’s operational range. Finally, the simulation setup of a single AMC unit cell, along with its boundary conditions, is presented in Fig. 5(d), enabling accurate characterization of the AMC response and reliable integration with the UWB MIMO antenna. The simulated electromagnetic characteristics of the designed AMC are illustrated in Fig. 6(a–c). In Fig. 6(a), the reflection phase reaches zero near 9.8 GHz, indicating that the AMC functions as an in-phase reflector at this frequency. This alignment promotes constructive interaction between the wave reflected by the AMC and the direct radiation from the antenna, enhancing forward gain while reducing backward radiation toward the body. The effective permittivity (Fig. 6(b)) shows a pronounced resonance around 9–10 GHz, where the real part becomes negative, signifying an artificial electric response. Similarly, the effective permeability (Fig. 6(c)) exhibits resonant behavior with a negative real component in the same frequency range, corresponding to the artificial magnetic response. The simultaneous presence of negative permittivity and permeability confirms the metamaterial characteristics of the AMC. Peaks in the imaginary components indicate moderate dielectric and magnetic losses, which are acceptable for UWB operation.

Fabrication procedure

To experimentally verify the proposed design, the antenna was realized using a textile-based method involving high-precision laser cutting. The conductive layer, made from a flexible fabric approximately 0.09 mm thick with a surface resistivity lower than 0.009 Ω/sq, was shaped using a Boye Laser BY1102 cutter, as illustrated in Fig. 7(a). This process enables precise patterning of flexible conductive layers, which can be directly adhered to the felt substrate thanks to their adhesive backing, allowing smooth integration into textile structures. The complete assembly process is shown in Fig. 7(b). Initially, the monopole was bonded onto the top substrate layer, while the AMC and the partial ground plane were positioned on the front and rear sides of the bottom substrate layer, respectively. The two felt layers were subsequently stitched together using thread to ensure mechanical stability. Ultimately, a SubMiniature Version A (SMA) connector was attached to the monopole feed line to enable port excitation. This fabrication method provides significant advantages, including enhanced mechanical flexibility, seamless integration into clothing, and suitability for wearable antenna implementations.

Antenna results

S11 parameter

For experimental validation, the S-parameters of the antenna were measured using a Rohde & Schwarz ZNLE14 vector network analyzer (VNA) over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 14 GHz. The fabricated prototype of the fully textile dual-port antenna, including the AMC structure, is illustrated in Fig. 8, where both the front and back views of the antenna are presented. The measurement setup used for characterizing the S-parameters is shown in Fig. 9. The comparison between simulated and measured S-parameters of the proposed AMC-based antenna is presented in Fig. 10. As shown in Fig. 10(a), the simulated reflection coefficient (red dashed curve) indicates an operating band ranging from 3.93 to 12.73 GHz, demonstrating that the integration of the AMC structure does not significantly affect the impedance matching performance. The measured results (blue dash-dot curve) exhibit a slightly wider bandwidth extending from 3.7 to 14 GHz, showing good agreement with the simulated results. The minor discrepancies between simulation and measurement can be attributed to fabrication tolerances and measurement uncertainties. The transmission coefficient |S₁₂|, shown in Fig. 10(b), indicates that the antenna with the AMC layer maintains a high isolation level below − 20 dB across most of the operating band, with a slight degradation approaching − 20 dB observed in the frequency range between 4 and 6 GHz.

Far-field radiation performance of the antenna

The experimental setup used to evaluate the antenna’s far-field radiation characteristics is illustrated in Fig. 11. Figure 12 compares the antenna gain and radiation efficiency for configurations with and without the AMC. Figure 12(a) presents the gain characteristics. The standalone antenna exhibits a gain exceeding 1.7 dBi across the entire operating band, with a maximum value of 4.33 dBi at 7.13 GHz. When integrated with the AMC, the antenna gain is significantly enhanced, reaching a peak value of 14.26 dBi at 4.45 GHz and remaining above 5 dBi over the whole operating band. This improvement is mainly attributed to the reflective and directive properties of the AMC, which effectively suppress backward radiation. Figure 12(b) shows that the radiation efficiency remains above 88% for the standalone antenna and exceeds 74% when the AMC is incorporated, indicating that the gain enhancement is achieved with minimal additional losses. A comparison between the simulated and measured results demonstrates strong agreement, validating the proposed design. Minor discrepancies observed at certain frequencies can be attributed to fabrication tolerances and measurement uncertainties. Overall, the results confirm that the AMC-integrated MIMO antenna provides a substantial gain enhancement and improved directivity without degrading UWB performance, impedance matching, or radiation efficiency. The close correspondence between simulation and experimental results further confirms the robustness of the proposed antenna design. Figure 13 presents the simulated and measured radiation patterns of the AMC-backed antenna at 3.5, 7.5, and 10.5 GHz in both E- and H-planes. A good agreement is observed between simulations and measurements. At 3.5 GHz, the patterns are relatively smooth and quasi-omnidirectional, with dominant co-polarization and suppressed cross-polarization. At 7.5 GHz, the patterns become more directive, especially in the H-plane, with slight pattern distortion. At 10.5 GHz, multiple lobes and increased ripples appear in both planes due to the larger electrical size. In all cases, co-polarization remains dominant, while discrepancies are mainly due to fabrication and measurement uncertainties.

Analysis of antenna and application

Effects of the human body

For a practical evaluation, the AMC-integrated antenna was numerically analyzed using the GUSTAV anatomical human model from the CST Voxel family, representing a 38-year-old male. The antenna was positioned at multiple on-body locations, including the chest, arm, stomach, and thigh, maintaining a 1 mm separation from the body surface. To reduce computational complexity, only a localized portion of each anatomical region was simulated, as indicated by the green dashed outlines in Fig. 14, instead of modeling the entire human body45. Figure 15(a) illustrates the simulated reflection coefficient |S11| of the antenna–AMC configuration when positioned on different body locations. The results indicate that the antenna preserves stable multiband operation for all placements, with |S11| remaining below − 10 dB over most of the operating frequency band. Slight shifts in resonance frequencies and variations in the reflection minima are observed, which can be attributed to differences in local tissue composition, curvature, and near-field coupling between the antenna and the body. Placements on the chest and arm exhibit more consistent impedance matching due to smoother anatomical profiles and relatively homogeneous tissue layers, whereas the stomach and thigh placements introduce stronger electromagnetic loading, leading to deeper resonant features at specific frequencies. Nevertheless, the AMC structure effectively compensates for these body-loading effects, ensuring stable impedance behavior when the antenna is placed on the human body. The simulated transmission coefficient |S21| for the same on-body scenarios is presented in Figure. 15(b). Across all investigated body locations, |S21| remains at sufficiently low levels over the operating frequency range, indicating weak mutual coupling and effective isolation. Minor fluctuations in |S21| arise from changes in near-field interactions and tissue properties; however, the overall isolation performance remains well preserved. This confirms that the AMC structure efficiently suppresses surface-wave propagation and backward radiation toward the human body, thereby maintaining stable inter-element coupling under realistic wearable conditions.

Figure 15(c) shows the simulated realized gain of the antenna for the different body placements. The antenna demonstrates stable radiation characteristics over the entire frequency band, with moderate variations depending on operating frequency and placement location. These variations remain within comparable limits, indicating that the influence of the surrounding biological tissues on the radiation behavior is effectively minimized. The corresponding radiation efficiency is depicted in Figure. 15(d). The efficiency remains relatively high across most of the band, with a noticeable but acceptable reduction when the antenna is positioned on the arm, mainly due to increased electromagnetic absorption and curvature effects. In contrast, placements on the chest, stomach, and thigh maintain more consistent efficiency levels, highlighting the effectiveness of the AMC structure in isolating the radiator from lossy tissues. Overall, these results demonstrate that the proposed antenna integrated with the AMC exhibits robust electromagnetic performance under realistic on-body conditions.

Figure 16 presents the simulated and measured S-parameter responses of the proposed MIMO antenna under free-space and on-body conditions. The inset in Fig. 16(a) shows the measurement setup, where the antenna integrated with the AMC layer is placed on the subject’s abdominal region to emulate realistic wearable conditions. As shown in Fig. 16(a), the reflection coefficient |S11| is evaluated for three cases: free-space simulation, simulation in the presence of the human body, and on-body measurement. When the antenna is placed on the body, slight resonance shifts can be observed due to the dielectric properties of human tissues. Nevertheless, |S11| remains below − 10 dB across the operating band, indicating stable impedance matching. The measured results are in good agreement with the simulated responses. The transmission coefficient |S12|, illustrated in Fig. 16(b), indicates that the isolation between antenna elements remains better than − 20 dB over most of the operating band. This demonstrates that the proposed antenna maintains low mutual coupling even under on-body conditions.

SAR Evaluation

The SAR quantifies how much electromagnetic energy the human body absorbs during exposure. Since wearable antennas operate in proximity to the human body, assessing SAR is essential46. The human chest and abdominal areas generally offer relatively flat surfaces, allowing the AMC structure to be positioned parallel to these regions. This setup is shown in Fig. 17(a), where the UWB antenna (UWB ANT) is separated from the high-permittivity, lossy human tissues by a reflective AMC layer. The tissue model includes three layers representing an average adult: skin (2 mm), fat (8 mm), and muscle (14 mm). The electromagnetic characteristics of these tissues are summarized in Table 4. The SAR values are calculated based on the following equation.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{array}{*{20}c} {SAR = \frac{{\sigma \left| E \right|^{2} }}{\rho }} \\ \end{array}$$\end{document}

where E represents the electric field, σ and ρ are the conductivity and mass density of the human tissues, respectively.

The calculated SAR distributions were initially presented for an antenna without AMC shielding (ANT only), as depicted in Fig. 17(b), with corresponding outcomes presented in Fig. 17(c) for typical frequencies of 3.5, 7.5, and 10 GHz. It is observed that significant energy absorption occurs in the tissues located directly beneath the antenna, with peak SAR levels reaching 2.46, 2.32, and 3.29 W kg–1 for 1 g of human skin tissue, and 1.07, 1.67, and 1.6 W kg–1 for 10 g of human skin tissue when fed with 0.5 W of input power. In contrast, the SAR distributions for the antenna with AMC (ANT + AMC), in the released case (Figs. 17(d, e)), clearly exhibit improved SAR characteristics, with reduced and more dispersed absorption, without a localized high-SAR region. The corresponding maximum SAR levels are markedly decreased, reaching only 0.13, 0.0568, and 0.333 W kg–1 for 1 g, and 0.444, 0.0319, and 0.175 W kg–1 for 10 g of human skin tissue under the same input conditions due to the incorporation of the AMC structure. The reduction ratio, defined as R = SARANT only/SARANT+AMC​, shows that the integration of the AMC decreases the SAR levels by factors of 18.9, 40.8, and 9.9 for 1 g, and 2.4, 52.4, and 9.1 for 10 g, respectively, depending on the operating frequency. These results, summarized in Fig. 17(e), clearly demonstrate that the AMC not only significantly reduces electromagnetic absorption in biological tissues but also ensures better compliance with international safety requirements for wearable devices. Bending assessment

The flexibility of wearable antennas is essential for reliable operation on curved body surfaces. Figure 18(a) and (b) present the measured |S11| response of the proposed antenna under horizontal and vertical bending conditions with three configurations: flat, R = 250 mm, and R = 160 mm. Under horizontal bending, the antenna preserves its multiband behavior with only slight shifts in the resonance frequencies compared to the flat configuration. Although a small degradation in the resonance depth is observed for the tighter curvature (R = 160 mm), the antenna maintains ∣S11∣ ≤ −10dB over the operating bands, indicating good robustness against deformation along this direction. In contrast, vertical bending causes more noticeable variations in the reflection coefficient, including moderate resonance shifts and a reduction in the depth of some resonances, particularly for R = 160 mm. These effects are mainly due to the influence of bending on the main current paths and the feed region. Nevertheless, the antenna still preserves acceptable impedance matching across most of the operating band. Overall, the results demonstrate that the proposed antenna maintains stable performance under mechanical deformation, confirming its suitability for wearable and conformal wireless applications.

Antenna application scenario

Although the chest badge remains upright while worn, it may undergo bending when the doctor picks it up, removes it, or opens it to replace the identification card. This highlights the need for a flexible and mechanically robust antenna solution. The textile-based antenna we have developed is fully flexible and, as demonstrated above, can withstand bending without significant performance degradation, making it well suited for this application. In addition, the antenna features a dual-port configuration, providing the benefits of MIMO operation—such as improved link reliability, higher data throughput, and enhanced robustness against body-induced detuning and movement-related effects. Furthermore, the antenna incorporates an AMC layer, which is essential for wearable applications. The AMC mitigates rearward radiation toward the wearer, thereby decreasing the SAR and enhancing operational safety. It also contributes to improved antenna gain and radiation efficiency when positioned near the body, ensuring consistent performance in medical environments. The proposed antenna can be embedded into the chest badge by mounting it on the inner back surface of the card holder. This configuration preserves the badge’s external appearance, minimizes mechanical friction, reduces the risk of physical damage, and ensures long-term comfort for the user. For a realistic evaluation of the antenna’s suitability for a chest-mounted medical badge, SAR analysis was performed using the GUSTAV numerical human model from the CST Voxel Family. The antenna, integrated with the AMC, was positioned on the chest with a 1 mm separation distance, representing the practical setup of a wearable identification badge.

Figure 19 presents the simulated peak SAR distributions averaged over 1 g and 10 g of tissue at 3.5 GHz, 7.5 GHz, and 10 GHz. Across all frequencies and averaging masses, the SAR values remain concentrated immediately beneath the antenna, with rapid attenuation inside the tissue, which is consistent with the presence of the AMC surface. The AMC effectively suppresses backward radiation toward the body, thereby limiting power absorption and reducing electromagnetic exposure to the chest tissues. At 3.5 GHz, the SAR levels are very low, with peak values of approximately 0.0446 W/kg (1 g) and 0.0253 W/kg (10 g). At 7.5 GHz, slightly higher but still controlled values appear—around 0.14 W/kg (1 g) and 0.0474 W/kg (10 g). At 10 GHz, a more localized hot spot can be observed, with peak SAR values of about 0.247 W/kg (1 g) and 0.147 W/kg (10 g), yet the energy remains confined to superficial tissue layers. These results demonstrate that the AMC maintains strong shielding performance even at higher frequencies by significantly reducing backward radiation. Importantly, all SAR values measured at 0.5 W input power remain well below international exposure limits defined by IEEE and ICNIRP (1.6 W/kg for 1 g and 2 W/kg for 10 g). This confirms that the proposed dual-port textile antenna backed by an AMC can be safely used on a chest badge for long-term operation without posing any health risks to the user. These results validate the practical applicability of the antenna in the selected scenario: a flexible, body-conformal medical badge worn daily by healthcare professionals. The combination of mechanical flexibility, dual-port MIMO operation, and AMC-based SAR reduction makes the antenna highly suitable for continuous wearable use in hospital environments, ensuring both performance and user safety.

Conclusion

In this work, an all-textile dual-port UWB MIMO antenna integrated with an AMC layer has been designed, fabricated, and experimentally validated for wearable applications. The AMC-backed configuration achieves a wide impedance bandwidth of 3.7–14 GHz (≈ 116%), a peak gain of 14.26 dBi at 4.45 GHz, and a significant SAR reduction of up to 98% for both 1 g and 10 g averaging criteria. These results indicate that the proposed antenna is a promising candidate for safe and efficient operation in body-centric environments. Deformation analyses show that the antenna maintains relatively stable electromagnetic performance under bending conditions, with characteristics comparable to those obtained in the planar configuration. In addition, good agreement between simulated and measured results is observed in both free-space and on-body scenarios, supporting the feasibility of the proposed design. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The electromagnetic properties of textile substrates are sensitive to environmental conditions such as humidity, temperature, and mechanical stress, which may affect antenna performance. In addition, textile materials typically exhibit higher dielectric losses compared to conventional rigid substrates, potentially reducing radiation efficiency. Fabrication tolerances, including cutting accuracy and possible misalignment between the antenna and the AMC layer, may also contribute to discrepancies between simulated and measured results. Furthermore, although bending analysis has been performed, the antenna behavior under more complex deformations and realistic on-body conditions may vary. The proximity of the human body can introduce additional detuning and absorption losses. Finally, the long-term durability of the textile antenna under repeated use, including washing and wear, has not been investigated in this study and requires further exploration. Overall, the proposed AMC-integrated textile MIMO antenna provides a flexible and broadband solution for wearable wireless systems, with potential applications in 5G-enabled devices, body area networks, and health monitoring systems.
