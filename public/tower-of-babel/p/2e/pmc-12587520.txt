Metabolic syndrome and health-related quality of life: unraveling the role of gender (A SUMS employees cohort-based study)
Authors: Fatemeh Rezaei chegini, Mozhgan Seif, Mohebat Vali, Seyed Jalil Masoumi, Haleh Ghaem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMetabolic syndrome (MetS), beyond its well-established link to the rising tide of chronic conditions, casts a significant shadow on individual’s daily well-being, diminishing their Health-related quality of life (HRQOL). This study examines the association between MetS and HRQOL with a focus on gender differences among a cohort of healthcare workers.ResultsIn this study, the prevalence of MetS was 9.3%, with a higher prevalence in men (10.3%) than in women (8.5%). Women with MetS reported significantly lower overall HRQOL scores compared to women without MetS (β = -1.89, p< 0.001), while no such association was found in men (β = 0.05, p = 0.922). Additionally, a significant gender-dependent interaction was observed, with a stronger association between MetS and lower physical HRQOL scores in women than in men (β = -3.17 vs. β = -2.17). Findings highlight a gender-specific pattern in the association between MetS and reduced HRQOL, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07532-5.Keywords: Metabolic syndrome, Health-related quality of life, Gender differences, Healthcare workers

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a combination of health disorders that increase the risk of developing chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease and impair health-related quality of life (HRQOL) [1, 2], whose prevalence is rapidly increasing in different populations in developed and developing countries [3]. According to the definition of the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), the prevalence of MetS in adults in the world and Iran is 23 and 34.6%, respectively [4, 5]. MetS is characterized by the clustering of several interconnected physiological abnormalities, notably central adiposity, dyslipidemia, elevated blood glucose levels, and high blood pressure (BP) [6–10]. The increasing trend of non-communicable diseases, including MetS, in recent decades has made HRQOL a necessary outcome and the final goal for health promotion programs [3]. Beyond objective outcomes, MetS are also associated with HRQOL, and understanding this relationship is essential for medical and public health interventions [11]. HRQOL is a significant health outcome that considers the entirety of an individual’s health experience, encompassing physical, mental, and social well-being [12]. It has emerged as a vital component of modern healthcare, facilitating a shift from disease-centered to patient-centered approaches that prioritize the comprehensive well-being and experiences of patients [13, 14]. Various studies have been conducted to investigate the association between MetS, its components, and HRQOL. Some studies have reported the absence of a statistically significant association between MetS and HRQOL [15, 16], and others have reported the existence of an inverse association between MetS and HRQOL [17–19]. Studies that have reported the existence of an association, some only in a gender subgroup [18, 19] and some have seen an association only in the dimensions of physical and mental HRQOL [19, 20]. Despite these existing contradictions, this study was conducted with the aim of investigating the association between MetS and its components with HRQOL and its dimensions in a group of employees, focusing on gender differences.

Methods

Study design

This study was based on the baseline data of the Shiraz University of Medical Sciences’ Employee’s Cohort (SUMSEC), a substantial component within the expansive Persian Cohort (Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran) [21]. The SUMSEC study commenced in August 2018, inviting university employees to a purpose-equipped cohort study clinic. The present study was conducted on the baseline data of 7073 participants who were in the age group of 20 to 70 years. The inclusion criteria of this study were age, Iranian nationality, and employee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences. The exclusion criterion was unwillingness to participate in the study.The data used in this study were in 4 sections: demographic data, clinical measurements, behavioral and lifestyle measures, and HRQOL questionnaire. Before inclusion, all participants provided informed consent in writing, following a comprehensive explanation of the study’s aims and examination procedures [22].

Variables and data collection

Demographic and socioeconomic variables

These variables included gender, age, marital status, education level, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and employment-related factors, including job position and shift work. Socioeconomic status was categorized into 5 levels (very low, low, moderate, high, and very high) and shift work was classified as regular, rotational, and irregular.

Behavioral and lifestyle measures

Lifestyle and behavioral measures included physical activity, smoking and alcohol consumption, and sleep quality. physical activity with the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), and sleep quality with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), respectively, all of which have been validated and shown to be reliable in Iran [23–25].

Clinical measurements and definition of MetS

Clinical data included underlying diseases, waist circumference, BP, serum triglycerides and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and fasting plasma glucose. Components of the MetS, including waist circumference, BP, serum triglycerides and HDL cholesterol, and fasting plasma glucose, were measured according to the IDF definition. MetS status was also defined according to the IDF definition as central obesity (waist circumference >94 cm (men) or >80 cm (women)) plus any two of the following four factors: fasting plasma glucose >100 mg/dL or a documented history of type 2 diabetes; HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women, or drug treatment for low HDL-C; serum triglyceride level >150 mg/dL or specific treatment for this lipid abnormality; systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg and diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg or a documented history of hypertension with current pharmacological treatment [26, 27]. The underlying diseases variable included individuals with any self-reported chronic disease such as cardiovascular disease, cancer, and hepatitis, excluding diseases previously defined under the IDF criteria.

Assessment of HRQOL

HRQOL was assessed using the validated Persian adaptation of the World Health Organization Quality of Life – Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF) questionnaire, an instrument whose psychometric properties have been established and confirmed within the Iranian populations [28]. This 26-item tool assesses four domains: physical health, mental health, social relationships, and environment. Domain scores are calculated by averaging the items of each domain and then converted to a 0–100 scale, where 0 indicates the poorest and 100 the best perceived health status. The overall HRQOL score is derived by summing the scores of all questionnaire items [29]. MetS and its components were treated as main exposures. The dependent variables were the overall HRQOL score and its domains (physical dimension, psychological dimension, social relations, and social environment), and other variables (e.g., ethnicity, age, sleep quality, marital status, and education) were treated as confounders.

Statistical analysis

Firstly, normality of quantitative variables was checked using through Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and related graphs. All quantitative variables were normal. Then, descriptive statistics for continuous variables, which included age, waist circumference, BP, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, fasting plasma glucose, and HRQOL scores, were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and for categorical variables, including gender, marital status, education level, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, job position, shift work, smoking status, alcohol consumption, presence of MetS, and physical activity level, frequencies and percentages were reported. Independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to compare means between two groups and across multiple groups, respectively. Differences in the distribution of categorical variables were examined using the chi-square test. To investigate the associations between the components of MetS and HRQOL scores, multiple linear regression models were utilized, adjusting for potential confounders (e.g., ethnicity, age, sleep quality, marital status, education, and physical activity). Then, we checked the interaction between the variables of age, gender, and quality of sleep with MetS and its components. From a statistical perspective, if the interaction is statistically significant, it is best to report the results separately [30]. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS version 22 and Stata version 11. It is worth noting that the missing values ​​were less than 10%, and the strategy for dealing with missing values ​​was that missing values ​​were estimated using R software version 4.2.0 and the Random Forest SRC package and the Imput function.

The mean waist circumference of all participants was 93.73 ± 10.55 cm, with a significant difference between the waist circumference of men and women (p < 0.001). In women, certain MetS components were significantly associated with lower HRQOL. Reduced HDL levels (β = -0.98; 95% CI: -1.60 to -0.36; p = 0.002) and central obesity (β = -1.18; 95% CI: -1.95 to -0.40; p = 0.003) showed a negative association. Other components of MetS, such as high glucose, triglyceride, and BP, were not significantly associated with HRQOL in women, after adjusting for potential confounding variables. In men, no statistically significant associations were found between MetS and its components and HRQOL (β = 0.05; 95% CI: -1.09 to 1.21; p = 0.922).

The association between metabolic syndrome and its components with the dimensions of Health-related quality of life

Figure 1A–D illustrates the associations between MetS components and QoL across the four HRQOL domains (physical, psychological, social relationships, and environment), adjusted for potential confounders. Regression models, including β coefficients, 95% CIs, and p-values by gender, are detailed in Supplementary Tables S1–S4. As noted in the methods, the interaction between MetS components and gender was significant; therefore, analyses were stratified by gender, with men shown as blue circles and women as red circles, and 95% CIs represented by horizontal lines. In the physical domain (Fig. 1a), the presence of MetS was significantly associated with a reduction in HRQOL for both genders. In women, MetS was strongly associated with lower HRQOL (β = -3.17, 95% CI: -4.53 to -1.82, p < 0.001). Men also experienced a significant decline, although the effect was slightly smaller (β = -2.17, 95% CI: -3.52 to -0.83, p = 0.002). In examining the individual components of MetS, several key factors were identified as significantly associated with HRQOL. Low HDL cholesterol was strongly associated with a lower HRQOL in both women (β = -1.82, 95% CI: -2.60 to -1.05, p < 0.001) and men (β = -1.11, 95% CI: -1.93 to -0.28, p = 0.008). Additionally, high glucose levels were significantly associated with lower HRQOL in women (β = -1.27, 95% CI: -2.39 to -0.15, p = 0.025), while no such association was found in men (p = 0.549). Similarly, high triglycerides reduced HRQOL in women (β = -0.96, 95% CI: -1.88 to -0.04, p = 0.040), but not in men (p = 0.969). Central obesity also had a more negative association with HRQOL in women (β = -1.30, 95% CI: -2.71 to -0.11, p = 0.071), but was not significantly associated in men (β = -0.31, 95% CI: -1.34 to -0.71, p = 0.549). High BP was not a significant predictor of HRQOL in women (p = 0.071) or men (p = 0.775).

In the psychological domain (Fig. 1b), no statistically significant associations were found between MetS or its components and HRQOL in men. However, in women, only a significant association was observed in low HDL cholesterol (β = -0.83, 95% CI: -1.64 to -0.02, p = 0.042). Moreover, in the social relationship domain (Fig. 1c), no significant associations were found between MetS or its components and HRQOL in either women or men, although reductions in HDL (p = 0.083) in women were close to significance. Additionally, in the social environment domain (Fig. 1d), none of the MetS components showed significant associations with HRQOL in women.

Discussion

In this population-based study conducted to investigate the association of MetS and its components with HRQOL, we found the following: Firstly, the prevalence of MetS in this group of employees was 9.3% and was higher in men than in women. Secondly, the association between MetS (and its components) and HRQOL followed a gender-specific pattern, and MetS and its components were more strongly associated with the physical and mental health dimensions of HRQOL. Lastly, among the components of MetS, abdominal obesity and low HDL cholesterol showed the highest inverse association with the physical health dimension of HRQOL.

Compared with studies on occupational groups, the prevalence of MetS in this study is lower than that of nurses in Brazil (38.1%) [31], hospital workers in Taiwan (10.3%) [32], and Mexico (38%) [33], and is similar to a study in Mexico on fixed shift workers (9.3%) [34]. The differences between our findings and previous results may be explained by changes in sample size and differences in genetic background, lifestyle and work, and socioeconomic status in the study population. In addition, our results show that the burden of MetS in men is slightly higher than in women, which is in line with the findings of the Taiwan study on hospital workers [32], which indicates a possible gender-specific vulnerability.

The present study demonstrated that, after controlling for confounding variables, the association between MetS and its components with HRQOL follows a gender-specific pattern, being more pronounced among women and particularly evident in the physical and psychological dimensions. This finding is consistent with previous research that has reported an inverse association between MetS and HRQOL exclusively in women [18, 35, 36]. For example, a review study reported an inverse association between MetS and HRQOL, and Other studies conducted in Bangladesh and Taiwan showed that MetS negatively affects HRQOL, especially in the physical health domain, with these effects being more prominent among women [18, 37, 38]. On the other hand, some studies have reported this association only in men [39, 40]. This gender-specific association between MetS and HRQOL may be attributed to biological factors such as hormonal differences and body fat distribution, as well as social and psychological factors [41–43]. Similar to our findings, some studies have reported this association in both physical and psychological domains [20], in contrast to other studies that identified the association only in the mental [39] or solely in the physical [3] aspects of HRQOL. These contradictions may be due to differences in the study population or cultural factors.

In addition, our study showed that decreased HDL was more strongly associated with decreased physical measures of HRQOL in women. This could be due to the critical role of HDL in protecting the cardiovascular system, which is more pronounced in women due to hormonal changes or the greater sensitivity of lipid metabolism [15, 44]. In men, this association was less pronounced and may be due to different behavioral and lifestyle factors in men [45]. In addition to decreased HDL cholesterol, central obesity was another important factor in decreased HRQOL, especially in women. This association could be due to physical consequences such as decreased ability to perform daily activities and joint problems, as well as psychological consequences such as decreased self-esteem and increased anxiety [46, 47].

Overall, our findings indicate that the association between MetS and HRQOL is gender-dependent and most evident in the physical and psychological domains. No significant associations were found with the social relationships or social environment domains, possibly because social functioning is less influenced by metabolic factors than physical or emotional health. Previous studies also report that MetS is linked to reduced HRQOL, particularly in physical and mental domains, while the impact on social relationships remains less clear [39, 48]. To better understand the mechanisms behind these associations, cohort or longitudinal studies are needed, as well as studies that control for dietary habits and psychological stressors.

Limitation

Although this study provides valuable insights into gender differences in the association between MetS and HRQOL, the subjective nature of HRQOL questionnaires increases the potential for response bias. Given that HRQOL is influenced by cultural and social factors, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Furthermore, the healthy worker effect may have led to an underestimation of the true association, as healthier individuals are more likely to participate in such studies.

Conclusion

Our findings underscore the pivotal role of gender in shaping the association between MetS and HRQOL, with women showing more pronounced impairments. These differences may reflect a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural influences. Our findings underscore the need for gender-sensitive interventions aimed at reducing the negative impact of MetS and improving HRQOL.
