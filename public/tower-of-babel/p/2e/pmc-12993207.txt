Key considerations for measuring α-galactosidase A activity after long-term migalastat therapy―Avoiding in vitro inhibition effects
Authors: Miyo Munakata, Takeo Iwamoto, Wu Chen, Keiko Akiyama, Yoshikatsu Eto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Assessment of α-galactosidase A (α-GAL) activity is a reliable method for evaluating the pharmacological efficacy of migalastat (Galafold) in Fabry disease patients undergoing chaperone therapy. Although some of mutant α-GAL retain normal catalytic function, their aberrant conformations lead to intracellular degradation and impaired lysosomal trafficking. Approximately 30% of Japanese Fabry patients are reported to be responsive to migalastat. In such cases, measurable enzyme activity following migalastat administration suggests successful stabilization and lysosomal delivery of the mutant enzymes. To monitor long-term therapeutic effects, dried blood spots (DBSs) are periodically prepared from patient samples as a convenient method for α-GAL activity measurement. However, residual migalastat in blood escaped from excretion can competitively inhibit the substrate 4-Metylumbellifel-α-D-galactoside (4-MUG) during DBS-based assays, resulting in underestimated enzyme activity. To address this, we investigated the impact of migalastat interference and demonstrated that removing migalastat using Amicon®Ultra filter from DBS samples enables accurate assessment of α-GAL activity for monitoring therapeutic response in Fabry disease.

Introduction

Fabry disease (FD) is a multisystemic lysosomal storage disorder caused by pathogenic mutations in the GLA gene located on the X chromosome. These mutations lead to reduced or absent activity of α-galactosidase A (α-GAL), resulting in progressive intracellular accumulation of globotriaosylceramide (GL-3) in multiple organs, including the nervous system, kidneys, and heart. Clinically, FD presents with a wide range of symptoms, such as acroparesthesia, gastrointestinal disturbances, angiokeratomas, hypohidrosis, left ventricular hypertrophy, corneal opacity, renal failure, and unexplained stroke [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].

Enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with agalsidase alfa (Replagal®) or agalsidase beta (Fabrazyme®) has been the standard treatment for FD. However, in patients carrying specific GLA missense mutations, the catalytic activity of α-GAL is preserved, whereas the mutant enzymes are misfolded, fail to undergo proper processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, and are degraded before reaching the lysosome. For such patients, the pharmacological chaperone migalastat (Galafold®) provides an alternative therapeutic option. Migalastat binds to mutant α-GAL in the endoplasmic reticulum, stabilizes its conformation, and promotes its trafficking to the lysosome. In the acidic lysosomal environment, migalastat dissociates, allowing the enzyme to exert its GL-3–degrading activity. In 2018, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved migalastat for the treatment of FD patients with amenable GLA mutations [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7],[17], [18], [19], [20]].

During chaperone therapy, in addition to regular monitoring of blood biomarkers such as Lyso-Gb3, α-GAL activity is essential for evaluating therapeutic response. We collected blood samples from six FD patients receiving long-term migalastat therapy (123 mg every other day) and prepared dried blood spots (DBSs) to measure α-GAL activity as a potential indicator of chaperone efficacy. However, α-GAL activity on DBSs showed substantial variability among patients and across sampling days, making it difficult to accurately interpret the pharmacological effects of migalastat.

This issue is closely related to findings by Lenders et al., who demonstrated that migalastat escaped from excretion is present in blood samples collected within 24 h after dosing and can competitively inhibit α-GAL activity in DBS assays using the artificial substrate 4-MUG [[4], [8]]. Because DBSs contain all blood components including low-molecular-weight compounds, the residual migalastat is co-extracted in reaction mixtures and directly interferes with enzymatic measurements. Lenders reported a strong correlation between the time from migalastat intake to blood sampling and the degree of α-GAL inhibition (r2 = 0.8140; p < 0.0001), with minimal inhibition observed after 24 h [[4], [8]].

To investigate whether migalastat interference contributed to the variability observed in our measurements, we filtrated the eluate of DBSs using Amicon® Ultra filters to remove low-molecular-weight migalastat, while enzyme molecules are retained on the filters. Using eluates of the filters, we anlyzed α-GAL activity and the levels of migalastat, and compared the results before and after Amicon filtration.

Material and methods

Sample collection

DBSs (Four half-inch circles/spots; Toyo Roshi Kaisha, Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) were prepared from blood samples collected at Tokyo Clinic, with informed consent and approval from the Ethics Committee of the Brain Research Institute (approval number 117). Patients received orally migalastat hydrochloride (150 mg capsules containing 123 mg of migalastat) at 48-h intervals at home. Blood samples were obtained prior to treatment initiation and subsequently on days 14 and 28, as well as at monthly follow-up visits at clinic. All DBS specimens were immediately stored at −80 °C until analysis. Fresh plasma and serum were also separated at each visit, stored at −80 °C, and used for the quantification of Lyso-Gb3.

Because the interval between migalastat taking and blood collection was strongly associated with the measurable suppression of α-galactosidase A (α-GAL) activity, patients were asked to report the exact time of drug intake at each visit. Removal of migalastat from DBS samples

Two 3 mm diameter circles were punched out from DBSs and each punch was incubated with 500 μL of deionized water. The sample was shaken at 17,000 rpm for 1 min followed by 900 rpm for 45 min at room temperature to extract soluble enzyme components. Half of the eluate (250 μL) was transferred to a tube as the pre-filtration sample. The remaining 250 μL was subjected to migalastat removal using an Amicon® Ultra centrifugal filter unit (Merck & Co., Inc., Rahway, U.S.A.). The sample was centrifuged at 14,000 ×g for 15 min at room temperature using a fixed-angle rotor, and the filtrate was discarded. The retained protein fraction was reconstituted with 250 μL of deionized water, placed in an inverted filter cup, and centrifuged again under the same conditions to recover the concentrated enzyme solution.

α-GAL enzyme activity assay

For the first 96-well plate, 50 μL of DBS extract prior to migalastat removal was combined with 50 μL of substrate mixture, consisting of 1 μL of 140 mM 4-methylumbelliferyl-α-D-galactopyranoside (4-MUG, Sigma-Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), 15 μL of 500 mM N-acetyl-D-galactosamine (GalNAc; Sigma-Aldrich), and 34 μL of 50 mM citric acid/100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 4.5). The reaction was incubated at 37 °C for 20 h. To terminate the reaction, 200 μL of 150 mM EDTA (pH 11.5) was added. Fluorescence intensity (Ex 355 nm / Em 460 nm) was measured using a microplate reader (CORONA MTP-810; Corona Electric Co. Ltd., Ibaraki, Japan), and α-GAL activity was calculated in units of pmol·h−1·punch−1. For the second 96-well plate, 50 μL of DBS extract after migalastat removal was similarly combined with 50 μL of substrate mixture and subjected to the same incubation and measurement protocol. The concentration of migalastat per DBS punch was determined before and after removal. The results of 3 punches obtained were averaged and used as the reported data.

Enzyme activity values before and after migalastat removal were plotted to assess the impact of residual drug on assay accuracy.

LC–MS/MS analysis for migalastat

Instrument and detection

System: Acquity H-Class UPLC coupled to a Xevo TQ-S Micro tandem quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters, Wilford, MA, USA). Ionization and mode: Electrospray ionization in positive-ion mode with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) for the migalastat transition m/z 164.1 → 128.2.

Chromatographic conditions

Column: Accucore 150 Amide HILIC, 100 × 2.1 mm I.D., 2.6 μm (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Injection volume: 5 μL of each prepared sample. Mobile phases: A: 10 mM ammonium formate in Milli-Q water; B: 10% (v/v) of 10 mM ammonium formate in methanol. Gradient (%A/%B): 0–0.1 min, 20/80 → 5.0 min, 50/50 → 6.0 min, 90/10 → 6.0–7.0 min, 20/80. Flow rate: 0.20 mL/min. Temperatures: Column 25 °C; autosampler 10 °C.

Sample preparation and injection

A 250 μL aliquot of either pre-removal samples (DBS eluates) or post-removal samples (DBS eluates processed with Amicon Ultra to remove migalastat) was dried in a speed-vac concentrator. The dried residue was reconstituted in 20 μL methanol, and 5 μL of the reconstituted solution was injected for UPLC–MS/MS analysis.

Calibration and relative quantification

The fragment ion m/z 128.2 was quantified by peak area. The calibration curve was prepared by spiking varying amounts of migalastat into eluates from healthy-control DBSs without using an internal standard. The calibration range was 0–2000 pg, with a correlation coefficient R2 = 0.999. Quantification was performed using this calibration curve without an internal standard. The limits of detection and quantification were 5 and 10 pg, respectively.

Stability

Short-term stability of spiked DBS samples was found to be stable after storage at 4 °C and room temperature for at least 7 days.

Quantification of Lyso-Gb3

Quantification of Lyso-Gb3 was performed according to a previously reported method [21,22]. Fresh plasma was separated from whole blood cell. 60 μL fresh plasma was then placed into a 1.5 mL tube containing 540 μL2-propanol(HPLC-grade, Kanto, Japan)and 20 μL internal standard. After mixing for 2 min with a microtube mixer (Tommy, Japan), the extraction was centrifuged for 20 min at 12,000 ×g. The supernatant was evaporated under a stream of air and reconstituted in 20 μL of methanol. The final solution was transferred to a microvial for autosampler injection and analyzed using the LCMS-8040 system (Shimadzu Corp., Japan).

Calibration standards were prepared using Lyso-Gb3 (Matreya LLC, USA) dissolved in methanol, containing an equal amount of N-glycated Lyso-Gb3 (internal standard, Matreya LLC, USA). The calibration range for Lyso-Gb3 was 0.0–0.8 ng, with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.996. Calibration was performed every 50 samples.

A 5 μL aliquot of each sample was injected into a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system coupled to a tandem mass spectrometer (LCMS-8040, Shimadzu Corp., Japan). Chromatographic separation was achieved using a C8 column (Inertsil C8–3, 3 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm; GL Sciences Inc., Japan) connected to an inline filter unit (ACQUITY, Waters Corp., USA). The mobile phases consisted of solvent A (10 mM ammonium formate, Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Japan) and solvent B (10% 10 mM ammonium formate in methanol). Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transitions were set at m/z 786.5 > 264.3 for Lyso-Gb3 and m/z 843.5 > 264.3 for N-glycated Lyso-Gb3. Quantification was performed by comparing the peak area of the target analyte to that of the internal standard.

Results and discussion

Pharmacological effects of migalastat (123 mg administered orally every other day) in long-term therapy on FD patients were evaluated by measuring α-GAL activity in DBSs collected from six patients over approximately five years. In parallel, serum and plasma Lyso-Gb3 concentrations were quantified to assess biochemical response. Across all patients, Lyso-Gb3 levels remained stable or demonstrated gradual declines following treatment initiation, with pronounced reductions observed in several individuals (Fig. 1). Recent studies by Lenders et al. have demonstrated that DBSs prepared within 24 h of migalastat administration may contain residual circulating drug not excreted, which competitively inhibits α-GAL by occupying the catalytic site and interfering with the synthetic substrate 4-MUG. In the present study, DBSs were prepared from samples collected from 2 h to 44 h post-dose (Table 1), making residual migalastat a plausible contributor to the observed variability. LC-MS/MS analysis confirmed substantial residual migalastat in DBS eluates (Fig. 2) associated with reduced α-GAL activity. For instance, Patient 1's day 812 sample showing reduced enzyme activity (Fig. 1A, Table 2) contained 13,817 pg/punch of migalastat, which decreased to 404 pg/punch following filtration (Fig. 2A, Table 2).

To mitigate this interference, DBS extracts were processed using Amicon® Ultra 10 K filters. Removal of migalastat (Fig. 2, Table 2) consistently increased α-GAL activity across all patients and markedly improved measurement stability (Fig. 1, Table 2), confirming that competitive inhibition by residual drug was the primary cause of apparent activity suppression. In a minority of samples, α-GAL activity decreased after migalastat removal, potentially reflecting possible loss of eluates during the filtration procedure.

These findings underscore the importance of considering the interval between migalastat administration and blood collection when interpreting α-GAL activity. They further demonstrate that removal of residual migalastat is essential for accurate DBS-based enzyme activity assessment and for avoiding worry about the timing of blood drawing. As pharmacological chaperone therapy continues to evolve, precise evaluation of enzyme activity is critical on clinical outcomes., particularly given mutation-specific differences in responsiveness and the influence of absorption and metabolic pathways to drugs.

Conclusion

This study confirms that Lyso-Gb3 is a dependable biomarker for assessing biochemical response to migalastat therapy. We also demonstrate that residual migalastat in DBS samples substantially suppresses apparent α-GAL activity through competitive inhibition, representing a critical source of measurement error. Incorporation of a filtration-based drug-removal step effectively eliminates this interference, markedly improving assay accuracy and reproducibility. This refinement enables reliable assessment of α-GAL activity independent of blood-sampling timing and establishes a practical, standardized approach for monitoring the pharmacological effects of migalastat in clinical practice.
