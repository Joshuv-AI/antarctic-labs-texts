Salt/alkali‑tolerant Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 improves pepper growth and soil quality in acidic and alkaline soils
Authors: YiHuang Chen, XiRui Wang, YuXin Si, Feng Zhang, KaiLe Ding, JiaXin Zhang, JiangMing Wang, Ling Zhou, XiaoXia Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the potential of the salt-alkali-tolerant actinobacterium Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540, isolated from Lop Nur saline-alkali soil in Xinjiang, to alleviate pH-related iron deficiency and promote pepper growth. We hypothesized that this strain could mobilize and compete for soil iron via siderophore production, thereby benefiting plants under variable-pH conditions. To test this hypothesis, we compared its effects with conventional iron fertilizers (EDDHA-Fe₆, ferrous sulfate) and compound microbial fertilizer on pepper growth, soil physicochemical properties, and rhizosphere microbial communities in acidic (pH 5) and alkaline (pH 7.69) soils. TRM 45540 exhibited good stress tolerance, growing well at 3%–9% NaCl and pH 9–12. In both soil types, it significantly promoted pepper growth: fresh weight was increased by over 44% in acidic soil relative to the control, while root and stem lengths were elevated by 26%–91% in alkaline soil compared with conventional amendment groups. The strain increased the content of indigenous soluble iron in soil, raising soluble iron to 42 mg/kg− 1 in acidic soil without exogenous iron addition, neutralized acidic soil toward neutral pH, increased total nitrogen to 6.5 g/kg− 1, and enhanced phosphorus and potassium availability. Redundancy analysis identified pH and total iron as the dominant factors shaping microbial communities in acidic and alkaline soils, respectively. KEGG pathway enrichment revealed significant changes in organic pollutant degradation, nutrient metabolism, and stress response pathways following TRM 45540 inoculation. The strain enriched functional microorganisms related to siderophore secretion and nitrogen fixation, thereby improving soil microbial diversity and richness, while compound microbial fertilizer was associated with relatively lower microbial community activity. These findings demonstrate that S. luteus TRM 45540, with cross-pH adaptability and functional stability, enhances pepper growth and soil quality via synergistic effects of stress tolerance, nutrient activation (especially iron mobilization), and microbial community regulation. This strain provides a promising microbial inoculant for sustainable pepper production in variable-pH soils, especially saline-alkali soils.

Introduction

Pepper, a crop of global significance due to its nutritional richness and promising market potential [1, 2], holds a pivotal role in agricultural production. Xinjiang, benefiting from its unique natural conditions characterized by ample sunlight and significant temperature fluctuations between day and night, has emerged as a key region for high-quality pepper cultivation in China. Nevertheless, the region’s soils are commonly afflicted by severe salinization, poor nutrient availability, weak water-holding capacity, and easy compaction. These constraints restrict pepper root elongation and impair fruit development, thereby reducing yield and quality, which has become a major bottleneck restricting the sustainable development of the local pepper industry [3]. Soil microorganisms are essential components of farmland ecosystems and play a crucial role in maintaining ecosystem structural integrity and functional stability. The composition and diversity of soil microbial communities are widely recognized as key indicators for evaluating soil quality and health [4]. Soil microorganisms are highly sensitive to environmental changes; small variations in soil physicochemical properties or field management practices can reshape microbial community structure and function, and further affect the sustainability of agricultural ecosystems.

Soil pH is a dominant driver shaping microbial community composition, often reducing microbial diversity and disrupting soil nutrient cycling processes [5]. Most microbial taxa have a narrow pH adaptation range, with metabolic activity and viability declining sharply under alkaline conditions (pH > 8). In contrast, some stress-tolerant microorganisms can survive and maintain normal physiological functions in strongly alkaline environments [6, 7]. Iron deficiency is a key limiting factor restricting crop growth in both acidic and alkaline soils. Extreme pH conditions greatly reduce iron bioavailability, which constrains pepper development even when other nutrient elements are adequate.

Beyond general plant growth-promoting traits such as nitrogen fixation, phosphorus solubilization and phytohormone secretion, some stress-tolerant microorganisms can secrete siderophores to chelate, mobilize and transport insoluble soil iron, thereby regulating plant iron nutrition across variable pH environments. Unlike non-specific plant growth promotion, this iron-mobilizing mechanism directly targets iron bioavailability under extreme pH conditions, which may play an important role for crops grown in calcareous or saline-alkali soils [8]. Excessive application of inorganic soil amendments such as gypsum can inhibit the activity of indigenous soil microorganisms and aggravate soil microecological imbalance.

Various strategies have been applied to alleviate constraints associated with acidified and alkalized soils [8–13]. Physical measures including deep ploughing, soil replacement and subsurface drainage can improve saline-alkali land, but they are high-cost, labor-intensive and difficult for large-scale promotion. Chemical amendments such as lime and gypsum can adjust soil pH in the short term but exert only unidirectional regulatory effects: lime mainly ameliorates acidic soils, while gypsum is widely used for saline-alkali soil improvement [9]. Long-term application of these chemical materials may induce soil particle cementation and compaction [10, 11]. Conventional compound microbial fertilizers are environmentally friendly, but their functional strains are mostly isolated from non-stressed habitats. They lack tolerance to extreme acid-base and salinity conditions, resulting in poor rhizosphere colonization and unstable growth-promoting performance in acidified and alkalized soils [8, 12, 13].

Therefore, screening microbial inoculants with the capacity to produce siderophores, mobilize soil iron and maintain stable plant iron nutrition under different pH conditions is critical for sustainable pepper production. To fill this research gap, exploring and utilizing functional microorganisms with good stress tolerance from extreme habitats has become a promising approach [14–16]. For instance, actinomycetes isolated from extreme saline-alkali environments not only tolerate harsh soil conditions but also regulate soil pH and promote crop growth [17].

Many previous studies have adopted commercial iron fertilizers (EDDHA-Fe₆, ferrous sulfate) as positive controls to evaluate the iron-mobilizing and growth-promoting performance of siderophore-producing microbial strains, providing a mature reference for selecting comparative treatments in this study.Specifically, recent work has demonstrated that bacterial siderophore-based biochelates can achieve comparable or superior iron supply efficiency to commercial chelates like HBED/Fe³⁺ and EDDHA-Fe under alkaline conditions, highlighting the potential of microbial iron mobilization as an alternative to conventional chemical fertilizers. Notably, Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 isolated from Lop Nur saline-alkali soil exhibits good iron-chelating and iron-mobilizing capacity, making it an ideal strain to compare with commercial iron fertilizers for clarifying its underlying growth-promoting mechanism.

Against this background, this study selected Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 from the extreme saline-alkali soil of Lop Nur, Xinjiang, as the experimental strain, and pepper cultivar Xianjiao 8819 as the test crop. Five treatments were set up: TRM 45540 fermentation broth, EDDHA-Fe₆, ferrous sulfate, compound microbial fertilizer, and ISP4 medium. EDDHA-Fe₆ and ferrous sulfate were selected as reference treatments to evaluate the iron-mobilizing efficiency and growth-promoting superiority of strain TRM 45540. To evaluate the strain’s cross-pH adaptability, its performance in acidic (pH 5) and alkaline (pH 7.69) soils was comprehensively investigated via salt-alkali tolerance assays, pot experiments, metagenomic sequencing and soil physicochemical determination. We explored the effects of the strain on pepper growth performance, soil physicochemical properties and rhizosphere microbial community structure, aiming to reveal its functional stability and regulatory mechanisms in contrasting acid-base soil environments. This study clarifies the advantages of TRM 45540 over chemical amendments and conventional microbial fertilizers, and provides valuable microbial resources and technical support for improving soil fertility and realizing sustainable pepper production in variable-pH soils, especially saline-alkali soils.

Materials and methods

Materials and methods

The strain Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 was isolated from saline-alkali soil in Lop Nur, Xinjiang, China. The strain is preserved in the Key Laboratory of Protection and Utilization of Biological Resources in Tarim Basin, with a preservation number of TRM 45540. Iron fertilizers tested included EDDHA-Fe₆ (iron content ≥ 6%, fully water-soluble, provided by Sichuan Sanrenxing Fertilizer Co., Ltd., China), ferrous sulfate (Fe content ≥ 18%, agricultural grade), and compound microbial fertilizer (containing ≥ 0.2 × 10⁸ CFU/g functional microorganisms, ≥ 3% organic matter, and iron and other trace elements. The pepper cultivar used was Xianjiao 8819, a widely planted local cultivar with moderate tolerance to salt stress. The ISP4 medium was composed of 10 g soluble starch, 1.0 g anhydrous K₂HPO₄, 1.5 g NaCl, 1.0 g (NH₄)₂SO₄, 2.0 g CaCO₃, 1.0 mL trace salt solution (containing 0.1 g/L FeSO₄·7 H₂O, 0.1 g/L MnCl₂·4 H₂O, 0.1 g/L ZnSO₄·7 H₂O), and 18 g agar, made up to 1000 mL with distilled water, adjusted to pH 7.2 before autoclaving at 121 °C for 20 min.The acidic and alkaline experimental soils were collected from typical farmland in Xinjiang, China. The acidic soil belonged to sandy loam texture, while the alkaline soil was classified as light loam texture, which are the dominant soil types in local pepper cultivation areas. This study mainly focused on the response of pepper growth and soil microbial characteristics to acid-base stress gradient. Routine soil pH was determined as the core limiting factor, and the baseline electrical conductivity and soil organic matter content were not further determined in the current experiment.

Investigation on salt and alkali tolerance of strain TRM45540

TRM45540’s salt and alkali tolerance were investigated by inoculating it onto ISP4 medium with sodium chloride concentrations ranging from 3% to 9% and pH levels from 9 to 12. The plates were then placed in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C for 7 d before observations were recorded.

Growth-promoting effects of strain TRM45540 and different fertilizers on pepper in acidic/alkaline soil

Pepper seeds were planted in seedling pots containing nutrient soil and placed in a constant temperature and humidity chamber (25 °C, 70% relative humidity, 16 h ligh/8 h dark) for 7 d. Seedlings of uniform size (3 true leaves stage) were selected and transplanted into plastic pots (diameter 12 cm, height 10 cm) filled with 300 g of pre-prepared acidic or alkaline soil. The pot experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design (CRD) to eliminate marginal environmental interference and ensure homogeneity of experimental layout. Three pepper seedlings were transplanted into each pot, with 9 replicate pots per treatment. Following transplantation, specific fertilizers were applied weekly via irrigation as follows: Group A received EDDHA-Fe₆ diluted 500-fold (100 mL pot− 1), Group B received ferrous sulfate diluted 1000-fold (100 mL pot− 1), Group C received compound microbial fertilizer at 0.5% (v/v) concentration (100 mL pot− 1), Group D received TRM 45540 fermentation broth (100 mL pot− 1), and Group E received ISP4 liquid medium (100 mL pot− 1). All treatments were applied at the same volume (100 mL pot− 1), and additional deionized water was added to all pots to maintain consistent soil moisture content (70% of field capacity) throughout the experiment. After a 45-d cultivation period, pepper growth parameters including root length, stem length, leaf length, and above-ground fresh weight were measured. For each pot, all three plants were measured individually, and the average value of the three plants was taken as the representative data for that replicate pot. Each pot was considered one biological replicate (n = 9 per treatment), and the mean value per pot was used for statistical analysis.

Changes in physicochemical properties of acidic/alkaline soil induced by strain TRM45540 and different fertilizers

After harvesting, rhizosphere soil samples were collected from each pot at a depth of 5 cm and 1 cm adjacent to the root system, with 100 g of soil per sample. Three biological replicates were established for each treatment group, and all samples were submitted to Paisinuo Company for sequencing analysis. Soluble iron content was determined through DTPA extraction-atomic absorption spectrophotometry; total iron was quantified using atomic absorption spectrophotometry following microwave digestion (nitric acid-perchloric acid or nitric acid-hydrofluoric acid-perchloric acid system). Total nitrogen was assessed via the Kjeldahl method after digestion with concentrated sulfuric acid and mixed catalyst. Total phosphorus was measured using molybdenum-antimony colorimetry subsequent to microwave digestion. Total potassium determination involved alkali fusion with anhydrous sodium carbonate for soil samples and acid digestion followed by flame photometry for plant sample. Soil pH was determined potentiometrically using the STISM method, with a soil-water suspension prepared at a ratio of 1:2.5 (m/v).

Changes in soil microbial communities of pepper induced by strain TRM45540 and different iron fertilizers in acidic/alkaline soil

Rhizosphere soil samples were collected at a depth of 5 cm and within 1 cm adjacent to the root system post-harvest, with 5 g of soil per sample. Rhizosphere soil was defined as the soil tightly adhering to the root surface after gentle shaking and collected using a sterile brush. Each treatment group had three biological replicates, and all samples underwent metagenomic sequencing analysis at Paisennuo Company. Metagenomic sequencing was conducted using the Illumina NovaSeq platform with the Whole Genome Shotgun (WGS) approach, constructing paired-end (PE) libraries with an average insert size of 350 bp and targeting a sequencing depth of 6 G clean reads per sample. Total metagenomic DNA was extracted using the MoBio PowerSoil® Pro DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, USA), and DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a Qubit fluorometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. An insert library for paired-end (PE) sequencing was constructed, and raw data were stored in FASTQ format. Quality control was performed using fastp software (v1.0.1) [18, 19] with default parameters to remove low-quality reads and adapter sequences. Host contamination was eliminated using KneadData software (v0.12.2) with the hg37dec_v0.1 reference database and Bowtie2 alignment parameters. Species classification and functional annotation utilized MetaPhlAn4 (v4.1.1) [20, 21] and HUMAnN3 (v3.8) [22], respectively. HUMAnN3 invoked the ChocoPhlAn (v201901_v31) gene family database and utility_mapping (v201901b) pathway mapping library to profile microbial metabolic pathways. Species annotation validation was performed with Kraken2 software (v2.1.5, PlusPFP database, released on 20250402) [23–26], coupled with Bracken for abundance correction [27]. Multidimensional data visualization analysis, including redundancy analysis (RDA) and functional pathway enrichment analysis, was conducted using R software (v4.4.3) with the “vegan” and “ggplot2” packages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed for all quantitative data among different treatments. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05, and Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons after ANOVA.Data analysis followed the EasyMetagenome tutorial [28, 29].

Comprehensive analysis of growth-promoting effects of TRM45540 on pepper in acidic and alkaline soils

Xianjiao 8819 was selected as the experimental material to investigate the growth performance of pepper inoculated with strain TRM45540 and different iron fertilizers under acidic and alkaline soil conditions after three months of cultivation.

Under acidic soil condition (pH 5), Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth showed observable influences on pepper root growth. The average root length in the TRM45540 group was 20.56 ± 2.60 cm, which was lower than those in the EDDHA-Fe₆ group (24.17 ± 2.78 cm) and ferrous sulfate group (24.44 ± 3.28 cm), but higher than in the compound microbial fertilizer group (22.39 ± 2.29 cm) and the ISP4 medium group (23.11 ± 2.62 cm) (Fig. 2A). The average stem length of the TRM45540 group was 37.0 ± 8.5 cm, which was similar to the compound microbial fertilizer group (37.0 ± 3.0 cm) and relatively higher than the EDDHA-Fe₆ group (33.0 ± 4.1 cm) and ferrous sulfate group (33.0 ± 3.0 cm), and showed no obvious difference compared with the ISP4 medium group (38.0 ± 3.5 cm) (Fig. 2B).

For leaf length, the average value of the TRM45540 group was 11.5 ± 1.0 cm, which was 15.0%, 9.5%, 15.0% and 15.0% higher than the EDDHA-Fe₆ group (10.0 ± 1.5 cm), ferrous sulfate group (10.5 ± 1.5 cm), compound microbial fertilizer group (10.0 ± 1.5 cm), and ISP4 medium group (10.0 ± 1.5 cm), respectively (Fig. 2C). In terms of plant fresh weight, the TRM45540 group exhibited better growth performance than other treatments in acidic soil. The fresh weight of the TRM45540 group was 26.1 ± 8.9 g, which was 1.82 times that of the EDDHA-Fe₆ group (14.3 ± 7.3 g), 1.44 times that of the compound microbial fertilizer group (18.1 ± 7.8 g), and also higher than the ferrous sulfate group (22.2 ± 12.5 g) and ISP4 medium group (18.9 ± 4.3 g) (Fig. 2D).

Overall, strain TRM 45540 positively affected pepper leaf length and fresh weight, and also regulated root and stem growth to a certain extent, showing application potential for pepper cultivation in acidic soil.

Under alkaline soil conditions (pH 7.69), the growth performance of peppers treated with Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth differed from other groups.

The average root length in the TRM45540 fermentation broth group was 9.0 ± 0.5 cm, which was 42.9%, 57.9%, and 26.8% higher than the EDDHA-Fe₆ group (6.3 ± 0.9 cm), ferrous sulfate group (5.7 ± 0.4 cm), and compound microbial fertilizer group (7.1 ± 0.8 cm), respectively (Fig. 2E). The root length of the ISP4 medium group (3.1 ± 0.6 cm) was only 34.4% of that in the TRM45540 group.

The TRM45540 fermentation broth group exhibited a mean stem length of 16.87 ± 2.10 cm, which was 50.3%–91.7% higher than the EDDHA-Fe₆ group (12.35 ± 1.02 cm), ferrous sulfate group (8.80 ± 1.55 cm), and compound microbial fertilizer group (11.23 ± 0.41 cm) (Fig. 2F).

In terms of leaf length, obvious differences were observed among treatments (Fig. 2G).

As for plant fresh weight, the TRM45540 group recorded 5.60 ± 0.87 g. This value was 1.65 times that of the EDDHA-Fe₆ group (3.39 ± 0.24 g) and 2.32 times that of the compound microbial fertilizer group (2.41 ± 0.07 g), and was also higher than the ferrous sulfate group (1.50 ± 0.42 g) and ISP4 medium group (1.38 ± 0.17 g) (Fig. 2H).

Overall, strain TRM 45540 affected root growth, stem elongation and biomass accumulation of pepper under alkaline soil conditions, and its effects were different from those of EDDHA-Fe₆, ferrous sulfate and compound microbial fertilizer. The results provide basic data for the application of this strain in pepper cultivation in saline-alkaline soils.

Ferrous sulfate produced the highest soluble iron content in both soil types, which is mainly determined by its inherent chemical characteristics. Soil pH acts as a key regulator of iron availability: acidic conditions facilitate iron dissolution and activation, while alkaline environments tend to induce iron precipitation and reduce its bioavailability. Combined with the subsequent metagenomic results, the variation in soluble iron can alter soil microbial community structure and metabolic activity. The functional microbes enriched in the TRM 45540 treatment could further participate in soil iron transformation, thereby linking iron availability to microbial activity.

In Fig. 3C, the total iron content varied among treatments in acidic soil. The Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth and ISP4 liquid medium control groups exhibited higher total iron contents, with no significant difference between them. Conversely, the compound microbial fertilizer and ferrous sulfate treatment groups showed lower total iron contents, also with no significant difference between them. Notably, the EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group had a total iron content of approximately 24 g/kg− 1. In alkaline soil, Fig. 3D illustrates that the total iron contents of the ISP4 liquid medium control group, compound microbial fertilizer treatment group, and EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group were comparable. The total iron content of the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth treatment group was approximately 23 g/kg− 1, lower than that of the ISP4 liquid medium control group, compound microbial fertilizer treatment group, and EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group, but higher than that of the ferrous sulfate treatment group.

In Fig. 3E and F, pH variations among distinct groups under diverse soil conditions are depicted. In acidic soil (pH 5), the treatment group utilizing Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth effectively stabilized pH at a neutral level. Conversely, in alkaline soil (pH 7.69), none of the interventions resulted in further soil salinization or alkalization.

In acidic soil, the highest total nitrogen content (approximately 6.5 g/kg− 1) was observed in the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth treatment group, as indicated in Fig. 3G. The ISP4 liquid medium control group, compound microbial fertilizer treatment group, and EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group exhibited similar total nitrogen contents, 4 to 4.5 g/kg− 1, with no significant differences between groups. In alkaline soil, the ferrous sulfate treatment group exhibited the highest total nitrogen content at approximately 3.2 g/kg− 1, while the total nitrogen contents of the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth treatment group and EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group ranged from 2.3 to 2.4 g/kg− 1, showing no significant difference between them (Fig. 3H).

In Fig. 3I, variations in total phosphorus levels across distinct groups in acidic soils are depicted. The ferrous sulfate treatment group exhibited the highest total phosphorus content at around 0.6 g/kg− 1,followed by the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth treatment group at approximately 0.55 g/kg− 1. The ISP4 liquid medium control group, compound microbial fertilizer treatment group, and EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group demonstrated similar total phosphorus values, each at about 0.4 g/kg− 1. In alkaline soil, the total phosphorus contents of the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth treatment group, ISP4 liquid medium control group, compound microbial fertilizer treatment group, and EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group were comparable at around 0.65 g/kg− 1, showing no significant differences among the groups (Fig. 3J).

In Fig. 3K, the total potassium content varied among different groups in acidic soil. The treatment groups with Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth and EDDHA-Fe₆ exhibited the highest total potassium contents, around 14 g/kg− 1. Following closely were the ISP4 liquid medium control group and ferrous sulfate treatment group, with total potassium contents ranging from 13 to 13.5 g/kg− 1. The compound microbial fertilizer treatment group showed a slightly lower total potassium content of approximately 13 g/kg− 1. In alkaline soil, Fig. 3L reveals that both the ISP4 liquid medium control group and the compound microbial fertilizer treatment group exhibited the highest total potassium contents, approximately 19 g/kg− 1. Following closely were the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth treatment group and the EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group, with total potassium contents ranging from 17 to 18 g/kg− 1, showing no significant difference between them. The ferrous sulfate treatment group displayed the lowest total potassium content at about 15 g/kg− 1.

Analysis of the regulatory effects of strain TRM45540 on the diversity and richness of soil microbial communities in different soils

Analysis of various soils revealed that the bacterial broth treatment group of Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth significantly enhanced the diversity and richness of soil microbial communities. The treatment group maintained species distribution uniformity and enhanced overall community diversity based on diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, invsimpson). The species richness of the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 fermentation broth treatment group did not differ significantly from the ferrous sulfate and compound microbial fertilizer treatment groups. However, it was notably higher than the EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group, suggesting the bacterial broth’s efficacy in enriching soil microbial species. The fermentation broth treatment group of the Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540 has the potential to enhance the optimization of soil microbial community structure, thereby enhancing microbial functional diversity for soil nutrient cycling and crop growth (Fig. 4).

Conclusion and discussion

Conclusion

This study explored the agricultural application potential of functional strain Streptomyces luteus TRM 45540, which was isolated from extreme saline-alkali soil in Lop Nur, Xinjiang. This strain is presumed to produce siderophores to mobilize soil insoluble iron, which may help regulate plant iron nutrition under soils with different pH values. We conducted salt-alkali tolerance tests, pot cultivation trials, soil physicochemical detection and metagenomic sequencing to clarify the effects of TRM 45540 on pepper growth, soil fertility and rhizosphere microbial community under acid soil (pH 5) and alkaline soil (pH 7.69). Three common soil improvement products were set as reference treatments for mechanism comparison, including two commercial iron fertilizers (EDDHA-Fe₆ and ferrous sulfate) and a commercial compound microbial fertilizer. Tolerance tests showed that TRM 45540 possessed strong stress resistance, and could grow stably under 3%–9% NaCl and pH 9–12. Pot experiments revealed that TRM 45540 greatly improved pepper biomass accumulation in both acidic and alkaline soils, though divergent effects on root elongation were observed in acidic soil: pepper fresh weight increased by more than 44% compared with the control group under acidic conditions; in alkaline soil, the root and stem lengths of peppers treated with TRM 45540 increased by 26%–91% relative to conventional fertilizer treatments. In terms of soil nutrients, TRM 45540 significantly activated soil insoluble iron without exogenous iron input, and the content of soluble iron reached 42 mg kg⁻¹ in acidic soil. In addition, the strain neutralized acidic soil pH, raised soil total nitrogen to 6.5 g kg⁻¹, and improved the availability of soil phosphorus and potassium. Redundancy analysis indicated that the key environmental factors shaping rhizosphere microbial communities differed between two soil types: pH dominated community variation in acidic soil, while total iron (TFe) acted as the primary driver in alkaline soil. KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated that inoculation of TRM 45540 significantly changed microbial pathways related to organic pollutant degradation, nutrient metabolism and stress resistance in both soils, indicating that the strain could regulate the metabolic function of rhizosphere microorganisms. TRM 45540 treatment enriched beneficial functional genera such as Streptomyces, Bradyrhizobium and Sphingomonas, and improved the diversity, richness and evenness of soil microbial communities. By contrast, compound microbial fertilizer reduced the activity of indigenous soil microorganisms. In this single-strain and single-crop pot experiment, TRM 45540 showed integrated advantages of salt-alkali resistance, iron activation, nutrient release and rhizosphere microecology regulation, which provides a new idea to alleviate the defects of conventional chemical iron fertilizers and common microbial inoculants. However, this study lacked comparison with physical soil improvement measures, and the effectiveness of the strain remains to be verified under field conditions. Although TRM 45540 exhibited promising growth-promoting and soil-improving effects on pepper in acidic and alkaline pot soils, multi-location field experiments involving various crops and soil types are required to verify these preliminary results and evaluate its wide application value. This research provides basic theoretical reference for developing extreme-environment actinomycete inoculants to improve soil quality under variable pH conditions, especially saline-alkali farmland, and offers a potential microbial resource for sustainable pepper planting.

Discussion

Actinomycetes, a crucial group in soil ecosystems, serve as significant reservoirs of bioactive compounds and contribute substantially to soil fertility and crop health regulation [30–33]. The excessive application of chemical fertilizers has exacerbated the issue of soil pH imbalance in agricultural practices [34]. Traditional actinomycete resources are constrained by their environmental adaptability, leading to limited efficacy in alkaline soils. Thus, actinomycetes sourced from extreme habitats have become crucial for overcoming this technical challenge, leveraging their distinct metabolic pathways and stress tolerance traits [35].

The strain S. luteus TRM 45540, which was chosen for investigation in this research, was obtained from the saline-alkali soil of Lop Nur, Xinjiang. This region is a typical extreme environment characterized by a summer temperature exceeding 47℃, a winter a minimum temperature of -20℃, low soil moisture, high ultraviolet radiation, and a soil pH ranging from 8.5 to 9.5 [36]. The strain has evolved good salt-alkali tolerance due to specific habitat conditions, establishing a physiological basis for its extensive use in both acidic and alkaline soils [37].

TRM 45540 can adapt to both acidic and alkaline soils, which is different from most common actinomycetes by maintaining stable activity in acidic (pH 5) and alkaline (pH 7.69) soils. Its growth-promoting effects surpass those of traditional enhancement techniques, a feature attributed to its distinctive metabolic adaptation mechanisms.KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these metabolic adaptations are reflected in significant differences in soil microbial metabolic pathways induced by TRM 45540: in acidic soil, Functional prediction analysis indicated that, in acidic soil, the TRM 45540 treatment significantly enriched microbial functional pathways involved in organic pollutant degradation and amino acid metabolism. In alkaline soil, pathways related to xenobiotic metabolism, carbon fixation, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis were enhanced. These functional differences across soil types may contribute to the strain’s consistent performance under both acidic and alkaline conditions. The TRM 45540 treatment increased soluble iron content in both soil types. In acidic soil, the soluble iron concentration reached 42 mg Kg⁻¹, while in alkaline soil, the soluble iron level was similar to that observed in the EDDHA-Fe₆ treatment group. Furthermore, the iron mobilization efficiency of TRM 45540 exceeded that of most commercial fertilizers included in the experiment.Previous studies have indicated that conventional compound microbial fertilizers may negatively impact soil microbial diversity, potentially due to the presence of bacteriostatic components or high concentrations of inorganic nutrients. Consistent with these reports, our study found that the Shannon index in the conventional compound microbial fertilizer treatment was significantly lower than in the ISP4 medium control. Conversely, the TRM 45540 treatment significantly enhanced microbial diversity and richness, as reflected in higher Shannon, Simpson, OTU richness, and Chao1 indices, and specifically enriched key functional taxa such as Streptomyces, Bradyrhizobium, and Sphingomonas. These changes in community composition are likely to improve soil nutrient cycling efficiency and increase the resilience of the microbial community to environmental perturbations. In line with this hypothesis, the microbial community in the TRM 45540 treatment exhibited greater structural stability under pH fluctuation compared to other groups. Although these findings demonstrate short-term beneficial effects on microbial community stability, conclusions regarding long-term soil productivity cannot be drawn from this pot experiment. Future multi-season field trials are needed to validate these preliminary observations and assess their agronomic relevance under realistic agricultural conditions.Redundancy analysis (RDA) was used to assess the influence of environmental factors (pH, TN, TP, TK, and TFe) on soil microbial community structure across the pH gradient. In the global analysis including both acidic and alkaline soils, the set of measured environmental variables did not significantly explain variation in microbial community composition (p > 0.05). Further examination suggests that this non-significant global effect masks good context-dependent relationships: pH was the primary driver of community structure in acidic soils, whereas total iron (TFe) was the dominant factor in alkaline soils. The divergent and opposing effects of these factors across the pH gradient likely cancel each other out in the combined analysis, resulting in the absence of a significant overall explanatory effect.

The salt-alkali tolerance of TRM 45540 is closely associated with its adaptive evolution in the extreme habitat of Lop Nur. Previous research has demonstrated that microorganisms in highly saline-alkali environments enhance stress tolerance primarily through synthesizing osmotic regulatory substances such as proline and betaine to maintain cellular osmotic pressure balance [38]; and expressing salt-alkali tolerance functional genes (e.g., sodium-hydrogen exchange genes, siderophore synthesis genes) to improve environmental adaptability [39]. Under the controlled conditions of this pot experiment, strain TRM 45540 exhibited good salt tolerance. This finding indicates that the strain warrants further investigation for potential use in slightly acidic, alkaline, and moderately to severely saline–alkaline soils. While its stress tolerance is a desirable trait, confirming its efficacy for saline–alkali land improvement in China will require multi-site field trials with different host crops, as the current study provides only preliminary evidence from a single-strain, single-crop system.

Herein, recent advances regarding siderophore-producing actinomycetes and saline-alkali soil bioremediation provide good theoretical support for the present findings. Li et al. (2026) systematically summarized the mechanisms and research progress of algae–bacterial synergistic remediation of saline-alkali soils, and pointed out that high pH in saline-alkali environments severely reduces the bioavailability of iron, phosphorus and other essential soil nutrients [40]. Tolerant functional microorganisms can secrete siderophores and organic acids to activate insoluble soil nutrients and regulate rhizosphere microecological balance. This is highly consistent with our result that strain TRM 45540 efficiently mobilized soluble iron and improved the availability of soil nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium in both acidic and alkaline soils. Meanwhile, they emphasized that indigenous habitat-adapted strains possess stronger colonization ability and community stability than exotic strains. Similarly, TRM 45540 isolated from the extreme saline-alkali habitat of Lop Nur exhibited excellent cross-acid-base adaptability, further confirming the unique application potential of extremophile actinomycetes in saline-alkali soil improvement.

In addition, Dely et al. (2025) reported the genomic and metabolomic characteristics of the rhizosphere strain Streptomyces sp. ADLamb9. Their study verified that streptomycetes can synthesize multiple siderophore components such as desferrioxamine and mirubactin, and adapt to nutrient deficiency and salt-alkali stress by regulating secondary metabolic pathways [41]. Functional enrichment further revealed that actinomycetes significantly enriched microbial pathways related to organic pollutant degradation and amino acid metabolism, which was consistent with the KEGG pathway enrichment results of TRM 45540 in this study. Moreover, their findings indicated that actinomycetes could reshape soil microbial community structure and enrich key functional genera including Streptomyces and Sphingomonas. This coincides with our observation that TRM 45540 markedly increased soil microbial diversity and selectively enriched siderophore-secreting and nitrogen-fixing functional taxa. Collectively, the above literature further supports that TRM 45540 improves soil quality and promotes pepper growth via an integrated mechanism of iron mobilization, metabolic adaptation, and microbial community regulation under acid and alkaline stress conditions.

S. luteus TRM 45540 overcomes the limitations of traditional improvement methods through its synergistic mechanism of “environmental adaptability-nutrient cycling-microbial interaction”. Its stress tolerance and cross-acid-base regulatory capabilities derived from extreme habitats not only provide a novel solution for improving the fertility of soils with different pH levels but also enrich the theoretical framework for utilizing extreme environmental microorganisms in agriculture. This strain has good application potential and can be used as a candidate microbial resource for agricultural production.
