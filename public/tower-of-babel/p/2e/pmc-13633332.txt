Intraoperative spectral imaging versus ICG fluorescence for gastric conduit viability in esophagectomy

Esophageal cancer is the eleventh most common and seventh most lethal malignancy worldwide [1]. Despite advances in multimodal therapy, surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment. Following esophagectomy, the stomach is most commonly used to restore continuity of the upper gastrointestinal tract. Anastomotic leakage (AL) remains the most feared complication, occurring in 8-208-20% of cases and associated with substantial morbidity and mortality [2]. Adequate vascularization and oxygenation of the gastric conduit are critical determinants of anastomotic healing. However, intraoperative selection of the anastomotic site is still largely based on subjective assessment of tissue color, bleeding, and vessel pulsation. A quantitative, non-invasive method for intraoperative assessment of tissue ischemia could substantially improve surgical decision-making.

Indocyanine green fluorescence imaging (ICG-FI) is widely used for intraoperative perfusion assessment, yet important limitations remain. The technique requires intravenous dye administration, a wash-out time, and primarily reflects blood flow rather than actual tissue oxygenation. Alternative approaches, including laser speckle contrast imaging, gastric tonometry, Doppler-based techniques, and spatial frequency domain imaging, have been investigated [3–6], but limited integration into minimally invasive workflows and insufficient reproducibility have hindered their use in everyday practice.

Spectral imaging (SI) has recently emerged as a promising modality for assessing gastric conduit perfusion and oxygenation [7–9]. Comparative studies indicate that SI provides complementary information on tissue status beyond ICG-FI [9, 10]. However, conventional spectral imaging systems rely on scanning mechanisms that are bulky and require several seconds per acquisition, limiting their applicability in real-time laparoscopic surgery. Snapshot spectral cameras overcome the speed limitations of scanning-based systems by capturing all spectral bands simultaneously in a single exposure, enabling video-rate tissue oxygenation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2) monitoring without disrupting the surgical workflow.

Recent advances in deep learning have further improved estimation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 from spectral data, replacing traditional linear unmixing approaches with robust neural network-based methods [11, 12]. Training on physics-based simulations of light–tissue interaction enables accurate oxygenation mapping without the need for invasive reference measurements. To address the domain gap between simulated and clinical data, we developed a domain-invariant deep learning framework based on previous work [12].

In this study, we integrated a snapshot spectral camera into a laparoscopic system to enable real-time intraoperative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 monitoring during esophagectomy in 33 patients. Previous work has either employed slow scanning-based systems [7, 8, 13], estimated an ischemia index rather than quantitative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 directly [11], or demonstrated real-time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 imaging only in a single-animal preclinical model [14]. To our knowledge, the present study is therefore the first to apply a video-rate spectral imaging system for real-time intraoperative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 estimation in a clinical human cohort undergoing minimally invasive esophagectomy. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 represents the ratio of oxygenated hemoglobin to total functional hemoglobin, within the microvascular bed of the sampled tissue. It is conceptually analogous to pulse oximetry, but reflects microvascular oxygenation rather than systemic arterial oxygenation.

Methods

Study population, design and ethics

This single-institution, single-arm prospective observational study included 38 patients scheduled for elective laparoscopic esophagectomy for cancer between February 2024 and November 2025 at Ghent University Hospital. The sample size of 38 patients was determined by logistical feasibility for this initial observational study, rather than formal power calculations, as the primary aim was to evaluate the technical feasibility. Five of the 38 enrolled patients were excluded due to technical acquisition issues (e.g., incorrect light cable connection, lens condensation or corrupted data files), resulting in a final cohort of 33 patients with complete data. This same subset of 33 patients was used consistently across all comparative analyses, ensuring a matched dataset between SI and ICG-FI. Patient demographics, clinical status and tumor characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In this cohort, five patients had postoperative AL (15%). The study was approved by the ethical committee of Ghent University Hospital (B670201836427), and written informed consent was obtained from all patients. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03587532). Inclusion criteria were: (a) age between 18 and 75 years, with ability and willingness to provide informed consent, (b) planned minimally invasive Ivor Lewis esophagectomy, and (c) intrathoracic circular stapled esophago-gastric anastomosis.

Exclusion criteria were: (a) pregnancy or lactation, (b) participation in other investigational studies, (c) use of anti-VEGF agents (e.g., Avastin) within 30 days prior to surgery, (d) intraoperative findings preventing study procedures, (e) non-standard anastomotic technique, and (f) excessive bleeding (>500>500 mL) prior to anastomosis.

Operative workflow and ROI selection

Standard minimally invasive Ivor Lewis esophagectomy was performed by experienced upper-GI surgeons, with intrathoracic circular stapled anastomosis. During the thoracic phase, the gastric tube was mobilized into the chest. Prior to constructing the anastomosis and performing any imaging, three regions of interest (ROIs) were demarcated with sutures (Fig. 2): the surgeon-chosen anastomotic site (ROI-A), a well-perfused proximal region 2–3 cm from the pylorus (ROI-P), and an ischemic control region (ROI-I) excised from the proximal stomach. As this was an observational study, an a priori decision was established that SI and ICG-FI findings would not influence the surgeon’s choice of anastomotic site, which was based on standard clinical criteria. Intra-opertive decision-making and anastomosis site selection thus remained entirely dependent on the surgeon’s clinical judgment. Spectral imaging

Imaging hardware and data acquisition

Spectral recordings of the gastric graft were obtained using the XIMEA SNAPSHOT VIS camera (Imec, Belgium), capturing 16 spectral bands in the 460–600 nm range (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$460 \pm 9, 469 \pm 10, 475 \pm 16, 484 \pm 10, 496 \pm 16, 504 \pm 19, 514 \pm 15, 526 \pm 19, 532 \pm 17, 546 \pm 15, 550 \pm 15, 564 \pm 16, 570 \pm 13, 581 \pm 18, 589 \pm 17, 596 \pm 16$$\end{document}460±9,469±10,475±16,484±10,496±16,504±19,514±15,526±19,532±17,546±15,550±15,564±16,570±13,581±18,589±17,596±16 nm (peak ± full width at half maximum). This spectral resolution and range has shown to be sufficient for reliable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 estimation [11, 15–17]. The sensor resolution is 2048××1088 pixels (5.5 µm pixel pitch). The camera was mounted to a standard laparoscope via a C-mount zoom adapter (18–35 mm, RVA Synergies, UK) and coupled to a Xenon light source (CLV-S190, Olympus, Japan). The field of view was adjusted to include all three ROIs.

Preprocessing and reflectance estimation

Radiometric calibration was applied [18]. Raw images underwent dark subtraction, followed by demosaicking using an algorithm developed by Muszyński and Luong [19]. Pixel spectra were corrected using the manufacturer-supplied matrix and normalized by the min-max spectrum of the light source. Further area-under-the-curve normalization compensated for variations in imaging distance and angle, preserving spectral shape critical for oxygenation estimation.

To create images familiar to the surgeon, synthetic RGB (sRGB) images were reconstructed by mapping bands at 600, 540, and 450 nm to R, G, and B channels, respectively, followed by histogram equalization and gamma correction to enhance visual clarity.

ICG fluorescence imaging

Acquisition and preprocessing

A 0.5 mg/kg bolus of ICG (ICG-Pulsion, Pulsion Medical Systems, Germany) was administered via central venous catheter. Fluorescence imaging (Visera Elite II, Olympus, Japan) was recorded at 15 fps from immediately before injection for three minutes. ROIs were manually annotated based on sutures and tracked using PerfusionWorks software (PerfusionTech, Denmark), obtaining the time-intensity curves. Baseline subtraction used the first five seconds to correct for background fluorescence. The ischemic ROIs were excluded from analysis, as no dye perfused these resected areas.

Perfusion metrics

The obtained time–intensity curves were low-pass filtered (4th-order Butterworth) and normalized by maximum intensity to reduce noise and mitigate inter-subject variability. This preserves relative perfusion dynamics and facilitates comparison between ROIs and across patients. Absolute metrics were computed, as summarized in Table 2, grouped into: ingress (wash-in), egress (wash-out), and global perfusion indices. Metric selection was guided by comprehensive reviews [20–22] and informed by prior studies on esophagectomy [23–25] and bowel or colonic perfusion [9, 10, 26, 27] with particular focus on anastomotic healing. Definitions of perfusion metrics

Mapping perfusion and oxygenation along the graft

Points were manually annotated along the graft centerline on the first ICG-FI frame (avoiding specular reflections) and tracked along the video recording using CoTracker [28]. For each 5× 55×5 pixel ROI around these points, mean green-channel intensity generated time–intensity curves, and were processed to obtain perfusion metrics as in Sect. "ICG fluorescence imaging".

Spectral images were registered to the first ICG-FI frame using their sRGB reconstructions. Registration was performed in two stages: (1) a perspective transformation based on LoFTR features [29], followed by (2) a non-rigid B-spline transformation using SuperGlue-based correspondences [30]. Results

The primary endpoint of this study was to investigate the ability of spectral imaging–derived \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 to discriminate between perfused and ischemic tissue. Secondary endpoints included comparison with quantitative ICG-FI perfusion metrics and local capillary lactate levels.

In each patient, three ROIs were defined intraoperatively, before any imaging was performed: a well-perfused control region located 2–3 cm proximal to the pylorus (ROI-P), the selected anastomosis site (ROI-A), and an ischemic region consisting of excised gastric tissue (ROI-I). LCL levels were measured for each ROI as a physiological reference for tissue hypoxia. Spectral imaging results were obtained for all three ROIs, whereas ICG-FI metrics were available only for ROI-P and ROI-A, as excised tissue does not receive fluorescent dye.

Due to the limited sample size of this study, it lacks statistical power to evaluate the predictive value of SI and ICG-FI for AL. As an exploratory analysis, the predicted \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 values and ICG-FI perfusion metrics at the anastomosis site are compared between the patient cohort with and without AL. In addition, an anecdotal case study is presented comparing the two modalities in a patient who developed leakage versus one who did not, illustrating their potential clinical utility for guiding anastomosis placement.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the feasibility of SI-based intraoperative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 to assess gastric conduit ischemia during esophagectomy, providing a direct quantitative comparison with ICG-FI. The primary challenge in preparing the gastric conduit and preventing AL is the identification of the demarcation between perfused and ischemic tissue by the operating surgeon. Our findings indicate that both SI and ICG-FI successfully identify tissue ischemia, and capture the ischemic gradient toward the distal tip of the graft, with SI providing a real-time assessment of actual tissue hypoxia. The compact snapshot spectral camera allowed for seamless OR integration by attachment to a standard laparoscope. It provided continuous, video-rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 mapping without the need for large scanning equipment, contrast injections, or added operative time.

The median \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 in well-perfused gastric tissue was approximately 69.16%69.16% and significantly different from ischemic tissue, aligning with other spectral imaging studies, reporting values around 70%70% [7, 8, 36, 37]. However, reported values vary in the context of conduit formation. Safi et al. observed an average \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 decrease of 16%16% after creation of the gastric conduit, reflecting the physiological impact of vascular division, which should be considered when comparing absolute \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 values across studies and clinical settings [36]. Moreover, we found no significant difference in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 or LCL between the selected anastomosis site and the well-perfused control region, suggesting the surgeons generally selected suitable anastomosis sites in this cohort.

Based on receiver operating characteristic analysis, an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 threshold of 52.90%52.90% was identified to discriminate perfused from ischemic gastric tissue. This range is consistent with previously proposed thresholds of approximately 60%60% in colorectal surgery [27] and around 50%50% in plastic and vascular surgery [8]. While promising, such thresholds remain system- and cohort-dependent and are derived from the most perfused and ischemic regions. In clinical practice, the relevant threshold for anastomosis selection would likely be higher. Moreover, the leave-one-patient out calculation showed a relatively high standard deviation of 15.54%15.54% for this threshold, indicating that the optimal threshold may vary significantly between patients. This underscores the need for larger studies to establish robust, generalizable thresholds for clinical decision-making.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 demonstrated a statistically significant correlation with LCL, supporting its physiological validity as a marker of tissue hypoxia. In contrast, ICG-derived perfusion metrics showed only moderate or non-significant correlations. This likely reflects the fact that ICG-FI metrics are calculated solely from clinically well-perfused tissue, as no dye enters the ischemic control tissue. Detecting significant associations between fluorescence metrics and LCL likely requires sampling more physiological states. However, previous preclinical studies also reported stronger correlations between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 and LCL compared to ICG-FI parameters, although most were conducted in small animal models [6, 10, 31].

Among quantitative ICG-FI parameters, ingress-related metrics (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {T_{\frac{1}{2}}}$$\end{document}T12, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {AUC_{50\%}}$$\end{document}AUC50%, rise time, and inflow slope) demonstrated the strongest discriminatory performance, whereas intensity-based parameters such as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {I_{max}}$$\end{document}Imax appeared less robust, consistent with prior reports [20, 22]. Egress-related metrics did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited three-minute acquisition window. Longer recordings are recommended in future studies to enable reliable assessment of wash-out dynamics, which may provide additional information regarding venous congestion. Because this study did not include a sufficiently large cohort with clinically confirmed leakage, no definitive ICG-FI thresholds were established. Nevertheless, previously reported reference values provide useful context. Suggested cut-offs include TTP > 30 s, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {T_{\frac{1}{2}}}$$\end{document}T12 > 10 s, and abnormal inflow slope values, although these vary widely depending on imaging platform and analysis methodology [21, 24, 26, 27]. In esophagectomy specifically, delayed fluorescence intensity beyond 60 s has been associated with impaired perfusion [35]. These observations underscore the ongoing need for standardization of quantitative fluorescence metrics.

In the exploratory comparison between patients with and without AL, quantitative ICG-FI ingress metrics at the anastomosis site were significantly different between groups, whereas \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 alone did not reach significance. This likely reflects the multifactorial nature of AL. While reduced perfusion and tissue hypoxia frequently co-occur, as illustrated by leakage patients 1 and 3, other contributing factors including technical, mechanical, and patient-related variables may lead to AL, as seen in leakage patients 2, 4, and 5. Together, these observations suggest that perfusion and oxygenation metrics provide complementary rather than redundant information. However, given the small leakage cohort (n=5n=5), these findings are exploratory and should not be interpreted as evidence of superiority of either modality for outcome prediction.

Both imaging modalities demonstrated a clear decline in oxygenation or perfusion toward the distal conduit. In an anecdotal case study of a patient who developed AL, ICG-FI demonstrated high fluorescence intensity at the chosen anastomosis site, whereas SI revealed that this region was already hypoxic. This finding suggests that although \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 mapping and ICG-FI are complementary, they visualize different physiological phenomena. Similar observations have been reported by Hennig et al. and Studier-Fischer et al., who described a more peripheral ischemic boundary with ICG-FI compared with SI in esophageal and small bowel surgery [9, 13]. This discrepancy likely stems from the different light penetration depths of the two modalities. ICG-FI captures fluorescence signals up to 6 mm deep, reflecting deeper vascular inflow and potential dye pooling in the tissue bed. In contrast, SI evaluates the superficial serosal and subserosal microcirculation at a depth of up to 2 mm. Additionally, diffusion of ICG dye into partially ischemic regions may further alter perfusion boundaries, as discussed by Diana et al. [38]. As such, both modalities could offer complementary insights, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 potentially providing a more direct assessment of the tissue’s metabolic viability, while ICG-FI reflects macro-perfusion dynamics.

An important consideration when interpreting these metrics is the differentiation between arterial inflow-related hypoxia and venous congestion. SI measures \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2, which can drop in both scenarios, either due to a lack of freshly oxygenated arterial blood or the pooling of deoxygenated blood in congested veins, as demonstrated by Fischer et al. [39]. ICG-FI, particularly its wash-out dynamics, is theoretically better suited to specifically evaluate venous congestion. Our limited three-minute acquisition window restricted the evaluation of egress metrics. Future studies with extended monitoring could clarify the modalities’ combined ability to differentiate these states.

Due to the limited sample size of this study, it lacked statistical power to formally evaluate the predictive value of SI and ICG-FI for AL. Crucially, the lack of outcome-based validation limits the definitive clinical applicability of these findings. The evidence supporting the potential of SI is primarily derived from an exploratory analysis and an anecdotal case presentation. Therefore, conclusions regarding the superiority of one modality over the other cannot be drawn. Rather, they should be viewed as providing complementary insights. Future large-scale, multicenter trials are essential to correlate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2 and ICG-FI metrics with clinical outcomes, such as the Clavien–Dindo classification, and to establish statistically robust predictive models.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the clinical feasibility of utilizing intraoperative spectral imaging (SI) and indocyanine green fluorescence imaging (ICG-FI) to assess gastric conduit viability during minimally invasive esophagectomy. While both modalities successfully map the progressive ischemic gradient toward the distal tip, they yield distinct physiological insights. SI provides continuous, contrast-free mapping of superficial tissue oxygenation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm {StO_{2}}$$\end{document}StO2) showing a strong correlation with local capillary lactate, suggesting it may be sensitive to early metabolic hypoxia. In contrast, ICG-FI captures deeper vascular macro-perfusion dynamics, with ingress-related metrics serving as robust indicators of blood inflow. Crucially, our findings indicate that both modalities are complementary

Future large-scale trials with outcome-based validation are essential to translate the obtained metrics into clinically validated thresholds, ultimately aiming to optimize anastomosis site selection and reduce postoperative anastomotic leakage.
