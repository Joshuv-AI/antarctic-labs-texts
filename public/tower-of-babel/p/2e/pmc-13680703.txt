Clinical evaluation of plasma D-dimer concentrations as a marker for thromboembolism in cattle with inflammation
Authors: Saki OHKUBO, Masaki MAEZAWA, Ken-ichi WATANABE, Yoshiyasu KOBAYASHI, Hisashi INOKUMA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasma D-dimer concentrations were measured in 15 cases with thromboembolism and 19 cases without thromboembolism among bovine patients with inflammatory diseases, and the clinical usefulness of plasma D-dimer as a thromboembolic marker was evaluated. No significant difference in plasma D-dimer concentrations was observed between the two groups. A receiver operating characteristic curve was created, and with a plasma D-dimer cutoff value of 0.38 mg/L, the sensitivity and the specificity were 86.7% and 31.2%, respectively. Furthermore, there was a large variability in plasma D-dimer concentrations in cases with thromboembolism. These results indicate that the measurement of plasma D-dimer alone in the diagnosis of thromboembolism in cattle does not have sufficient specificity. In a multivariate logistic regression analysis regarding thromboembolism detection, platelet count was significant in the likelihood ratio test, suggesting that considering platelet count in addition to plasma D-dimer may allow for the detection of thromboembolism.Keywords: cattle, D-dimer, inflammatory disease, thromboembolism

Thromboembolism occurs when a thrombus form or fibrinolysis is impaired in arteries and veins of patients with conditions such as vascular endothelial damage, abnormal blood flow, and hypercoagulation [12]. A number of bovine thromboembolism cases have been reported in association with inflammation and sepsis [2, 14]. Caudal vena caval thrombosis is a well-known example of thromboembolism in cattle [5, 12]. Pulmonary embolism and vegetative endocarditis are also included in the broader definition of thromboembolism [12]. However, diagnosing thromboembolism in cattle is difficult, and currently there is no detection method other than observing large intravascular thrombi by ultrasound [12]. Meanwhile, in human and companion animal medicine, coagulation and fibrinolysis tests are routinely performed when thrombosis is suspected. D-dimer, a substance produced during fibrinolysis, is used as a biomarker for diagnosing thromboembolism [1, 3, 18]. As D-dimers promote the activation of neutrophils and monocytes and induce the release of IL-6, thereby influencing inflammatory responses [13], Plasma D-dimer concentrations have also been experimentally measured in cattle with several infectious diseases [6, 11, 15,16,17]. However, the relationships between D-dimer concentration, thromboembolism, and inflammation have not been fully evaluated in bovine medicine. In this study, we evaluated the clinical utility of plasma D-dimer concentration in cattle presenting inflammatory diseases with and without thromboembolism.

A total of 34 patient cattle with inflammatory diseases brought to Obihiro University of Agriculture and Veterinary Medicine (OUAVM) for confirmed diagnosis between February 2017 and November 2018 were included in this study. All cattle underwent clinical diagnosis through clinical examination and hematological and blood biochemical tests before necropsy. Based on necropsy findings and histopathological examination, the 34 cattle were classified into the following two groups: 15 cases of inflammatory diseases with thromboembolism (thromboembolism group) and 19 cases of inflammatory diseases without thromboembolism (non-thromboembolism group). The 15 cases in the thromboembolism group included six cases of macroscopic pulmonary thromboembolism, seven cases of vegetative endocarditis, one case of caudal vena caval thrombosis, and one case of renal infarction, all of which showed large, clearly visible thrombi macroscopically. The 19 cases in the non-thromboembolism group all had severe inflammatory lesions in one or more organs, including pneumonia, meningoencephalitis, enteritis, mastitis, arthritis, cystitis, nephritis, and dermatitis, but no thrombus formation was observed by macroscopic or microscopic examination. Separately from these inflammatory disease-affected cows, coagulation tests including plasma D-dimer were measured on 12 cows, including four cases of congenital skeletal malformations and eight cases of poor growth without any lesion, in order to calculate the reference value of plasma D-dimer measured in this study.

Blood samples were collected from the jugular vein using a 20-mL syringe and an 18-gauge needle on days 1–3 after the patient’s arrival at the university hospital, prior to necropsy. A portion of the blood was dispensed into EDTA tubes, and platelet counts were measured as soon as possible using a multiparameter automated hematology analyzer (poch-100i v Diff, Sysmex, Kobe, Japan). The remaining blood was dispensed into tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C, preferably within 30 min, to separate plasma. Plasma was then frozen at −30°C until D-dimer and coagulation testing. Plasma D-dimer concentrations were measured using a bovine D-dimer ELISA kit (Bovine D-Dimer ELISA, Kamiya Biomedical Co., Seattle, WA, USA). Prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinolytic concentration of each patient were measured at the Daiichi Kishimoto Clinical Laboratory Center (Obihiro). Physical examination, complete blood counts, blood chemical analysis, other specific examinations, and necropsy were performed under approval Nos. 29-38 (2017) and 18-32 (2018) from the Animal Experiment Committee of OUAVM.

All data values ​​are presented as mean, standard deviation, and median. Continuous variables between the thromboembolic and non-thromboembolic groups were compared using the Wilcoxon test, a nonparametric comparison. Furthermore, for thromboembolic detection using plasma D-dimer measurements, a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was constructed to examine sensitivity and specificity. In addition, multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed for thromboembolic detection. All statistical analyses were performed using JMP student edition Ver. 9.1 (STS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A significance level of 0.05 was used for the analysis results.

Significant differences were observed only between the two groups in age platelet count and A/G. The age in the thromboembolic group was significantly higher than that of the non-thromboembolic group, and the platelet count was significantly lower. However, no significant difference was observed in plasma D-dimer concentration between the two groups. Regardless of the presence or absence of thromboembolism, the range of plasma D-dimer concentration in cattle with inflammatory diseases was wide, ranging from 0.05 mg/L to the upper limit of measurement of 10.0 mg/L (Fig. 1).

In this study, a provisional reference value for D-dimer was first calculated at 0.79 mg/L, which is the mean + 2 × standard deviation of 12 non-inflammatory cattle. Wittek reported a median D-dimer concentration of 0.30 mg/L in adult cattle before abdominal surgery [19], and Rasoulzadech reported a D-dimer concentration of 0.266 ± 0.006 (mean ± standard deviation) in control cattle [11]. Human D-dimer reference values vary depending on the measurement system but are less than 0.5 mg/L with ELISA [9], which is approximately the same as the reference value for cattle. In this study, plasma D-dimer concentrations exceeded the normal value of 0.79 in 11 out of 15 cows in the thromboembolic group, and the detection sensitivity was 73.3%. On the other hand, plasma D-dimer concentrations also exceeded the normal value in 12 out of 19 cows (63.1%) in the non-thromboembolic group. Furthermore, plasma D-dimer concentrations in cattle with thromboembolism show a very wide range, indicating that thromboembolism cannot be ruled out even at low D-dimer concentrations. ROC curves were constructed using this data, and the best plasma D-dimer cutoff value was found to be 0.38 mg/L, at which point the sensitivity was 86.7%, specificity 31.2%, positive predictive value 80.0%, negative predictive value 31.6%, and area under the curve (AUC) 0.4662 (Fig. 2). These results suggest that the measurement of plasma D-dimer alone in the diagnosis of bovine thromboembolism lacks sufficient specificity, posing problems in differentiating it from inflammatory diseases without thromboembolism. Therefore, caution is necessary when clinically applying plasma D-dimer concentration.

D-dimer levels are elevated in DIC and inflammatory diseases due to repeated coagulation and fibrinolysis, regardless of the presence or absence of thrombus, and hence, the specificity is not high in the medical field [7, 12]. Furthermore, D-dimer is not a highly sensitive marker for thromboembolism in humans, with reported sensitivity of 53% for deep vein thrombosis and 50% for pulmonary thromboembolism [9, 18]. In addition, it is known that plasma D-dimer concentrations in human are also affected by age and liver function [10]. In this study, although there was no significant difference in AST, and GGT activities between the two groups, the age of patients in the thromboembolic group was significantly older. It should be noted that the effect of age was not considered in this study.

Plasma D-dimer concentrations were not elevated in a small number of cases despite the presence of clear thromboembolism, such as cases of vegetative endocarditis. Although the reason for this is unclear, suppression of the fibrinolytic system may have led to a decrease in D-dimer concentration. In human sepsis, the fibrinolytic inhibitor PAI-1 is overproduced, suppressing the fibrinolytic system [9]. Thus, in cattle with verrucous endocarditis and severe suppurative diseases, the fibrinolytic system might have been impaired due to sepsis. In addition, D-dimer concentrations reportedly decrease with time elapsed since thrombus formation [4]. In this study, all cases of vegetative endocarditis, suppurative pneumonia, and caudal vena caval thrombosis were caused by chronic bacterial infections with long progression periods. Thus, variation in the time elapsed since thrombus formation might have contributed to the large fluctuations in plasma D-dimer concentration. Although the measurement of plasma D-dimer concentrations was performed at only one point in this study, observing the time course of plasma D-dimer concentration in a single case may provide interesting insights.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed on various laboratory values ​​as factors in the detection of thromboembolism. The likelihood ratio test for the effect showed that D-dimer concentration was not a significant factor (P=0.3561), while only platelet count was significant (P=0.0212). The cutoff values ​​for both factors combined were D-dimer concentration >0.10 mg/L and platelet counts <50.7 × 103/µL. At this point, the sensitivity was 73.3%, specificity was 89.5%, and AUC was 0.8351 (Fig. 3). This suggests that the combination of D-dimer concentration and platelet counts may improve the accuracy of thromboembolism detection. In all 15 cases of thromboembolism in this study, large thrombi were observed macroscopically, and the decrease in platelet counts was thought to reflect the consumption of platelets. In the medical field, it has been reported that combining multiple coagulation/fibrinolytic markers and inflammatory markers in addition to plasma D-dimer concentration improves the accuracy of thromboembolism diagnosis. For example, the combined use of D-dimer and the Systemic Inflammatory Response Index has been shown to be effective in diagnosing deep vein thrombosis [8]. This study is preliminary and involves a limited number of cases. In the future, in order to establish plasma D-dimer concentration as a marker of thromboembolism in cattle, it is expected that combinations with other coagulation/fibrinolytic markers and inflammatory markers will need to be attempted.
