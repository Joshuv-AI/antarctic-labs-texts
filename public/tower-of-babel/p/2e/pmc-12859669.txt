A trojan horse selenium-containing polymer for protective co-delivery of Fe(II) and cisplatin to boost chemo-ferroptosis synergy via GSH-triggered ROS storm
Authors: Zheng Luo, Haohua Hu, Zeyu Zheng, Ying Chen, Yuting Lin, Choon Peng Teng, Xian Jun Loh, Changliang Ren, Yun-Long Wu, Bin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, is critically influenced by ferrous ions (Fe2+) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). However, the inherent instability of Fe2+ and insufficient levels of endogenous H2O2 within the tumor microenvironment necessitate the combination of current iron-based nanoparticles with other modalities to achieve effective cancer therapy. Herein, we designed a glutathione (GSH)-responsive selenium-containing polymeric nanoreactor co-loaded with Fe2+ and cisplatin. Selenium-containing polymers can effectively enhance the stability of both Fe2+ and cisplatin, and undergo cleavage in tumor cells with high GSH levels to achieve specific cisplatin and Fe2+ release, resulting in a substantial depletion of GSH and downregulation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX-4). More interestingly, cisplatin not only serve as a chemotherapeutic drug, but also promote intracellular H2O2 generation to fuel the Fe2+-mediated Fenton reaction, thereby cascading chemotherapy and ferroptosis to effectively kill cancer cells. This GSH-activated selenium-polymer armored Fe2+/cisplatin cascade nanoreactor effectively disrupts the anti-ferroptosis system in cancer cells and continuously drives ferroptotic cell death. By overcoming the limitations of current ferroptosis-based cancer therapies, this approach holds great promise for the development of safe, efficient, and precisely controllable ferroptosis-based cancer therapies.

Introduction

Ferroptosis has emerged as a promising strategy for cancer therapy in recent years, characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation-induced cell death [1,2]. It primarily relies on the Fenton reaction, where Fe2+ reacts with H2O2 to generate hydroxyl radicals that cause oxidative damage and cell death [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. However, clinical applications are hindered by Fe2+ instability and insufficient H2O2 in tumors, leading to inadequate ROS production and often necessitating combination therapies. To overcome these challenges, various iron-based nanomaterials, such as iron-organic frameworks, iron oxides, and iron-doped particles, have been developed to efficiently deliver iron and potentiate ferroptosis [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. However, their therapeutic efficacy is frequently compromised by poor Fe2+ retention and uncontrolled release. Additionally, the high concentrations of glutathione (GSH) in cancer cells act as an effective antioxidant, inhibiting ferroptosis by neutralizing reactive oxygen species and providing an essential reducing substrate for glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX-4), a key ferroptosis-regulating enzyme [[13], [14], [15]]. Consequently, these materials often require combination with alternative antitumor therapies to achieve therapeutic effects [16,17]. Therefore, it is crucial to develop novel strategies that simultaneously enhance Fe2+ stability, elevate intracellular H2O2, and deplete glutathione (GSH) to safely and effectively potentiate ferroptosis for cancer therapy.

Selenium is an essential nutrient for biological functions. Excessive or insufficient intake of selenium can cause serious diseases in humans and other animals. Selenium, due to its relatively low electronegativity and large atomic radius, gives selenium-containing compounds high reactivity and sensitivity [18,19]. Studies have shown that selenium-containing compounds can not only effectively bind iron ions, but also promote the valence state transition of trivalent iron ions to divalent iron ions [[20], [21], [22]]. In recent years, selenium-containing polymers have been widely studied as redox-responsive nanocarriers for the release of active anticancer drugs or self-healing materials [23,24]. Therefore, selenium-containing polymers may be promising carriers for the specific delivery of exogenous iron ions, which can consume the antioxidant substance GSH and increase the stability of Fe2+, thereby responsively delivering iron ions to cancer cells, safely and effectively enhancing the ferroptosis of cancer cells. Insufficient endogenous H2O2 is another limiting factor that hinders the efficacy of ferroptosis therapy [25,26]. Cisplatin, as a traditional chemotherapy drug, can not only exert anticancer effects by damaging DNA, but also specifically activate nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase (NOX) in cancer cells, effectively increase the concentration of intracellular H2O2 through a series of enzymatic reactions [[27], [28], [29]], thereby effectively promoting the Fenton reaction and enhancing the therapeutic effect of ferroptosis. However, cisplatin is also susceptible to interference from intracellular GSH, resulting in reduced efficacy, and the co-delivery of cisplatin and iron ions in existing nanosystems often suffers from insufficient drug stability, premature leakage, and systemic toxicity [[30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Therefore, it is still necessary to develop a safe, effective, simple, and controllable drug delivery system to coordinate cisplatin chemotherapy and ferroptosis.

Herein, we report the development of a GSH-activated selenium-containing polymer based ferroptosis nanoamplifier for the synergistic delivery of Fe2+ ions and cisplatin (see Scheme 1). Selenium-containing polymer consists of a GSH-responsive selenium-containing segment, a hydrophilic segment of polyethylene glycol (PEG), and a hydrophobic segment of polypropylene glycol (PPG), which enable the stable encapsulation of both Fe2+ ions and cisplatin by coordination and hydrophobic interactions. The polymer can specifically lysate in tumor cells with high GSH levels, effectively consuming high concentrations of GSH in cells, while releasing Fe2+ and cisplatin. The released cisplatin induces apoptosis and boosts H2O2 generation via NOX activation, while Fe2+ ions catalyze H2O2 in cells to produce highly toxic ·OH. Collectively, these cascades result in elevated oxidative stress, suppressed GPX-4 activity, and enhanced lipid peroxidation, culminating in robust ferroptosis of cancer cells. To our knowledge, this is the first report of a GSH-responsive selenium-containing polymer platform that both inhibits the cellular antioxidant system and continuously supplies the necessary components for sustained ferroptosis, which provides a novel and controllable strategy for potentiating ferroptosis-based cancer therapy.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of PSF nanocomposite with GSH depletion for self-enhanced cascade ferroptosis and chemotherapy.Scheme 1

Result and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of PSF

As shown in Fig. S1 in the Supporting Information, poly (DH-Se/PEG/PPG polyurethane) was synthesized according to previous established protocols, which can simultaneously encapsulate cisplatin (Pt) and ferrous ions (Fe2+) in the micelles through the hydrophobic interaction force and coordination interaction, thereby forming a nanocomplex (PSF) (Fig. 1A). The morphology and elemental composition of PSF was characterized using transmission electron microscopy equipped with energy dispersive spectroscopy (TEM-EDS). As shown in Fig. 1B, PSF exhibits a spheroid morphology and elemental mapping confirms the homogeneous distribution of Pt, Fe, and Se within the PSF nanocomplex. The relatively weak signal of selenium is likely attributed to its low content and the limited amount of polymer present on individual nanoparticles. High-resolution scans of the Se 3d and Fe 2p core levels confirm the successful incorporation of both elements. The Se 3d spectrum reveals three distinct peaks (Fig. S2): Se-Metal (54.5–55.5 eV), Se-C (55.5–56.5 eV), and Se=O (58–59 eV), indicating the presence of a selenide/selenoxide redox pair in the selenium-containing polymer and and the coordination of Se with the metallic elements Pt and Fe. The selenium center (selenide/selenoxide) readily undergoes thiol-selenium chemical reactions and redox reactions with glutathione (GSH), consuming glutathione by forming selenium-sulfur adducts/selenoxide species, resulting in glutathione-responsive release/activation [[35], [36], [37], [38]]. Furthermore, the abundant O and N elements in the polymer have been reported in numerous publications to coordinate cisplatin and iron ions [[39], [40], [41]]. The above results indicate that cisplatin and Fe2+ may be loaded into micelles mainly through coordination with Se, O and N in selenium-containing polymers. The Fe 2p spectrum (Fig. 1C) shows characteristic peaks at 709.8 eV (Fe2+, 2p3/2), 715.0 Ev (Fe3+, 2p3/2), 724.6 (Fe2+, 2p1/2), and 728.7 eV (Fe3+, 2p1/2), indicative of a mixture of Fe2+ and Fe3+ species. Following PSF nanoparticle formulation, the supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm, and residual concentrations of Pt and Fe were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). The encapsulation efficiencies were determined to be 89.64 % for Fe and 56.68 % for Pt, respectively (Table S1). The oxidative state of Fe ion within PSF was determined to be Fe2+, as verified by characteristic colorimetric reactions using KSCN (Fig. S3) and K4[Fe(CN)6] (Fig. S4). The poly (DH-Se/PEG/PPG polyurethane) copolymer and PSF nanocomposites were further measured by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. S5, the characteristic absorption bands at 3450, 2870, 1710 and 1100 cm−1 confirmed the presence of -NH, -CH2, -C=O and -C-O- groups, substantiating the successful synthesis of poly (DH-Se/PEG/PPG polyurethane) copolymers. The high Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio in PSF has important functional significance because Fe2+ is an effective catalyst for the Fenton reaction, which produces hydroxyl radicals that induce lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. We further compared the antioxidant capacity of selenium-containing polymers for Fe2+. As shown in Fig. 1C and D, we can see that in the selenium-containing polymer group, the content of ferrous ions is significantly higher than that in the F127 group, and the amount of Fe3+ generated is significantly less. The Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio was determined by deconvolution and fitting of the Fe 2p3/2 XPS peak. As shown in Fig. 1D, the XPS peak area corresponding to Fe2+ in the selenium-containing polymer (PSF) was significantly larger than that in the F127 control group, indicating effective protection of ferrous ions by the polymer matrix. Moreover, the Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio in PSF (2.20 ± 0.83) was markedly higher than that in F127 (0.55 ± 0.38) (Fig. S6 and Table S2). Student's t-test confirmed that this difference is statistically significant (p < 0.05). GSH-responsive dissociation of PSF and release of drug

The selenium-containing polymer demonstrated GSH-responsive behavior, as illustrated in Fig. 1E. We validated the glutathione (GSH)-depleting capacity of PSF in vitro using 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) assay. As shown in Fig. S8, PSF treatment induced a significantly faster reduction in free GSH levels compared to spontaneous oxidation controls, demonstrating its potent GSH-scavenging activity. In addition, we used a GSH concentration of 10 mM to mimic the intracellular redox environment of tumor cells. Aggressive tumors, including breast cancer such as 4T1, typically exhibit elevated GSH levels (up to 10 mM) as a mechanism to counteract oxidative stress and confer chemotherapy resistance. Thus, in our in vitro studies, the 10 mM GSH condition accurately mimics the reducing cytoplasmic environment encountered by the nanocomposite after uptake by tumor cells. Firstly, PSF nanocomposites exhibit excellent structural stability under physiologically relevant conditions. Dynamic light scattering (DLS) is used to monitor hydrodynamic size over time, and PSF maintains consistent hydrodynamic diameter at 37 °C regardless of PBS or fetal bovine serum (FBS), indicating strong stability in physiological media (Fig. S9). These findings provide a solid foundation for evaluating the GSH-responsive behavior of the PSF nanoplatform. To further investigate this property, the release profiles of cisplatin and Fe2+ from PSF nanocomposites in GSH-rich environments were evaluated in vitro. The corresponding morphological and dimensional changes of PSF nanocomposites in GSH-rich environments can be clearly observed from TEM (Fig. 1E) and DLS analysis (Fig. 1F). The particle size of PSF nanocomposites shown in Fig. 1F is slightly larger than that shown in Fig. 1E, likely due to polymer swelling in aqueous solution. The release kinetics of Fe2+ from PSF in a GSH environment revealed a significantly higher release compared with a normal saline control. As illustrated in Fig. 1G, Fe2+ release reached 82.0 % within 12 h under GSH conditions, while only 20 % was released in saline, confirming the GSH-responsiveness of the nanoparticles. Concurrently, Pt release kinetics were analyzed by ICP-OES, revealing accelerated Pt release in the presence of GSH, with a significant increase observed between 6 and 8 h. These results further validate the redox-responsive behavior of the PSF nanoplatform (Fig. S10). The catalytic potential of the released Fe2+ ions for hydroxyl radical generation via the Fenton reaction was evaluated using methylene blue (MB) as an indicator. In the presence of H2O2, the absorbance of PSF and SF to MB decreases significantly at 550–700 nm (Fig. 1H), and the color of the solution fades (Fig. 1I). In contrast, negligible changes were observed in control groups containing MB with H2O2, PSF or SF alone. This indicates that H2O2 reacts with Fe2+ in PSF or SF undergo Fenton-type reactions to generate hydroxyl radicals, which degrade MB. Therefore, there findings verify that the selenium-containing polymer-based nanoparticles enables GSH-triggered release of drugs and Fe2+, indicative of their potential for redox-responsive therapeutic applications.

This uptake was further quantified by flow cytometry, which showed that the fluorescence intensity in the PSF-FITC-treated group was 1.85 times higher than that in the FITC control group, as shown in Fig. 2B. Based on the above results, the in vitro cytotoxicity of PSF nanocomplexes against tumor cells was investigated by using the standard 3-(4,5)-dimethylthiahiazo (-z-y1)-3,5-di- phenytetrazoliumromide (MTT) assay. 4T1 cells were incubated with varying concentrations of SF, Pt, PS or PSF for 24 h. As shown in Fig. 2C, the PSF-treated group showed the most pronounced reduction in cell viability, which verified the synergistic effect between chemotherapy and ferroptosis induced by Pt and Fe2+ release. In addition, PSF had positive effects on other tumor cell lines (Fig. S11), with more than 50 % cytotoxicity at 8 μg/mL. PSF induced ferroptosis more potently than erastin, a classical ferroptosis-inducing agent (Fig. S12), highlighting its potential as a therapeutic ferroptosis inducer. Notably, compared with the PSF group, the cytotoxic effect of PSF was substantially reduced when Ferrostain-1 (Fer-1), an ferroptosis inhibitor (Fig. 2D), was co-administrated, confirming that ferroptosis plays a key role in the PSF-mediated cell death mechanism. In addition, double staining with annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propyl iodide (PI) was performed to study the apoptosis and necrosis of 4T1 cells incubated with PSF. As depicted in Fig. 2E and F, a higher apoptosis rate (42 %) was detected in the PSF-treated group compared with the PBS, SF and Pt treatment groups. These results underscore that the PSF nanocomposites enhance therapeutic efficacy through a dual mechanism of action, simultaneously triggering apoptosis and ferroptosis in tumor cells.

Intracellular ROS production

Building on previous findings [28,29,42], we hypothesized that cisplatin released by PSF in tumor cells might activate NOXs, which subsequently convert O2 to O2·-. This process promotes endogenous H2O2 production (Fig. 2G). The released Fe2+ from PSF can then catalyze H2O2 to produce toxic ·OH. To verify this mechanism, the NOXs pathway and intracellular H2O2 levels in 4T1 cells were evaluated by Western blotting and quantitative assays, respectively. Western blot analysis revealed increased expression of NOX1 (Fig. S13), a key component of the NOX complex, indicating activation of the pathway. Furthermore, measurement of intracellular H2O2 concentration using the fluorescent probe Amplex Red showed a significant increase in oxidative signal after treatment, further confirming enhanced H2O2 production (Fig. S14). Intracellular Fe2+ levels are critical for catalyzing the Fenton reaction with H2O2. To evaluate ability of PSF to facilitate cellular iron uptake, we employed FerrOrange, a fluorescent probe selective for Fe2+. 4T1 cells were treated with PSF, and analyzed by fluorescence microscopy. As shown in Fig. S15, PSF treatment significantly increased intracellular Fe2+ levels compared to untreated controls. This enhanced iron accumulation likely contributes to the promotion of Fenton chemistry within the cells. The results of CLSM observation and the corresponding fluorescence intensity analysis (Fig. 2H) showed that 4T1 cells incubated with PSF had stronger ROS fluorescence intensity than those treated with Pt or SF alone, indicating that PSF combined chemotherapy and chemodynamic therapy (CDT), leading to a self-amplification Fenton reaction cascade. Given that DCFH-DA can be oxidized by multiple reactive species, including ·OH, ONOO−, and H2O2, it is not specific for Fenton-derived hydroxyl radicals. To directly probe ·OH generation, we performed additional experiments using HPF, a highly selective fluorescent probe for hydroxyl radicals. As shown in Fig. S16, PSF treatment induced a marked increase in HPF fluorescence compared to both control and Pt-only groups. These results confirm that PSF robustly promotes ·OH generation in an iron-dependent manner. These results were further supported by flow cytometry analysis, which demonstrated a consistent increase in ROS levels in the PSF group (Fig. 2I).

In vitro mechanism verification of enhanced ferroptosis effect of PSF

As shown in Fig. 3A, elevated levels of GSH in tumor cells are known to impede ferroptosis by supporting the activity of GPX-4, an enzyme that detoxifies lipid peroxide (LPO). To explore whether PSF nanocomplexes can overcome this limitation, we evaluated the intracellular GSH levels in 4T1 cells incubated with PSF. As shown in Fig. 3B, both PSF and SF nanocomposites can effectively reduce the intracellular GSH level, with PSF showing a more pronounced effect. Consumption of GSH interferes with the reaction involved in GPX-4, leading to decreased activity and expression inhibition of GPX-4, causing accumulation of LPO, and enhancing ferroptosis. Western blot analysis (Fig. 3C) and confocal imaging (Fig. 3D) confirmed that GPX-4 expression was reduced in 4T1 cells treated with Pt or SF alone, but was more significantly enhanced in PSF-treated group. These results are s in good agreement with the observed reduction in GSH levels. Notably, when cells were co-treated with PSF and Fer-1, GPX-4 expression was markedly restored, confirming that the downregulation of GPX-4 by PSF is ferroptosis-related. Then, Liperfluo-LPO probe was used to detect the intracellular LPO levels after different treatments. As shown in Fig. 3E, compared with PBS control group, LPO levels in SF-treated group was increased, and LPO level in PSF group was further increased after the introduction of Pt, while LPO level was attenuated after the addition of inhibitor Fer-1. In addition, we also studied the ferroptosis status of cells in different treatment groups by monitoring the mitochondrial membrane potential changes of tumor cells. The results showed that the degree of depolarization of cells in PSF treated group was the highest (Fig. S17). GPX-4, a key inhibitor of ferroptosis, reduces lipid peroxides through GSH, a primary mechanism by which cancer cells acquire ferroptosis resistance. This novel selenium-containing polymer nanoreactor (PSF), co-loaded with Fe2+ and cisplatin, selectively decomposes within the glutathione (GSH)-rich tumor microenvironment, ensuring the specific release of its cargo. This design simultaneously disrupts multiple anti-ferroptosis defense mechanisms: released cisplatin consumes GSH (Fig. 3B) and generates intracellular H2O2 (Fig. S14), which Fe2+ ions utilize to drive a potent Fenton reaction, generating lethal lipid peroxides. Furthermore, this system downregulates the expression of GPX-4 (Fig. 3C and D), a key ferroptosis inhibitor. By self-supplied reactants and disrupting cancer cell defenses, this system combines chemotherapy with enhanced ferroptosis, synergistically enhancing anti-tumor effects. This multi-pronged attack, which integrates ferroptosis and chemotherapy, makes it significantly more difficult for cancer cells to develop resistance compared to single-mechanism therapies. In vivo biological distribution and antitumor behavior of PSF

Next, the in vivo antitumor effect of the PSF nanocomplex was validated in an orthotopic 4T1 cancer mouse model (Fig. 4A). To evaluate the blood circulation capability of PSF, PSF nanocomplexes were administered intravenously to BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 tumors. Blood samples were collected at predetermined time points post-injection (2, 12, and 24 h), followed by plasma separation via centrifugation. The Pt and Fe concentration was quantified using ICP-OES. As shown in Fig. S18, Pt and Fe levels in the tumor site gradually increased during 12 h, demonstrating that PSF can effectively deliver the drug to the tumor site via the EPR effect. To evaluate in vivo GSH depletion by PSF, we measured tumor GSH levels using the DTNB assay. As shown, PSF reduced tumor GSH by ∼67 % within 24 h (Fig. S19). To study the biodistribution, accumulation and excretion of nanodrugs, FITC-labeled FITC-Se-Fe nanocomplexes were intravenously administrated via caudal vein. Mice were sacrificed 24 h later, and tumors and major organs were collected for fluorescence imaging. As shown in Fig. 4B and S20, compared with Rod B control group, mice injected with Rod B-Se-Fe showed significant fluorescence signals at the tumor sites 6 h after injection, possibly due to the enhanced permeability and EPR effect of nano drugs. In the meanwhile, strong fluorescence signals were observed in the kidney of both groups, indicating renal clearance of the nanocomplexes, which may reduce systemic toxicity. Therapeutic efficacy was further evaluated by monitoring tumor progression across treatment groups (Fig. 4C–E). Compared to the phosphate buffered saline (PBS) control group, all treatment groups exhibited varying degrees of tumor suppression. At the end point of treatment, tumor volumes in Pt, SF, Se and blank control groups were 1.8, 2.6, 4.6 and 5.8 folds larger than those in the PSF group, respectively. The results show that Pt-mediated chemotherapy alone was more effective than SF-induced CDT, while PSF achieved superior tumor inhibition by combining both modalities. The enhanced efficacy of PSF can be attributed to its ability to deplete GSH, amplify ferroptosis, and synergize CDT with chemotherapy. To further examine the efficacy of treatment in different groups, one mouse from each group was sacrificed at the end of treatment and tumor tissues were collected for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. It can be observed from Fig. 4F that the PSF treatment group showed obvious cell contraction and nuclear condensation, and the tissues of the PSF treatment group were more porous than the PBS control group. Biocompatibility evaluation

To assess the biocompatibility of PSF nanocomplexes, a hemolysis assay was performed. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, the hemolysis rates of erythrocytes treated with PSF, Se and Pt at varying concentrations were all less than 5 %, indicating the excellent hemocompatibility of the PSF nanocomplexes. In order to evaluate the biosafety of PSF, the body weight changes in treated mice were regularly recorded throughout the treatment. As shown in Fig. 5C, the body weight of mice in the control group and the material group increased slightly, which may be attributed to tumor growth outpacing potential weight loss, thereby contributing to overall weight gain. A mild decrease in body weight was observed in the SF group, while the body weights in the PSF and Pt groups remained relatively stable. suggesting minimal systemic toxicity at the administered dose. At the end of treatment, major organs of sacrificed mice were collected for H&E staining. No noticeable pathological abnormalities were observed in stained sections of all organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) in each treatment group (Fig. S21). Moreover, blood supernatants from mice were used for routine and biochemical assays, and the results (Fig. 5D) showed no significant alternations in liver function markers or signs of inflammation at 14 days after PSF injection compared to the control group. Conclusion

In summary, we have engineered a GSH-activated, selenium-containing polymer-based nanocomplex (PSF) that disrupts tumor cell defenses by depleting intracellular GSH and downregulating GPX-4, while simultaneously amplifying ferroptosis through elevated ferrous ion and H2O2 production. The PSF system responds selectively to high GSH levels in cancer cells, triggering the controlled release of cisplatin and Fe2+. Cisplatin further activates NADPH oxidases to boost H2O2 generation, which, together with Fe2+, drives a robust Fenton reaction and intracellular ROS surge. GSH depletion potentiates cisplatin's toxicity and inhibits GPX-4, leading to enhanced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. In vivo, PSF achieved significant tumor growth inhibition while demonstrating excellent biosafety and biocompatibility. By integrating precise GSH responsiveness, drug protection, and cascade amplification of oxidative stress, this ferroptosis nanoamplifier overcomes key limitations of current therapies and offers a promising platform for safe, effective, and targeted cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Anhydrous dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), dimethylformamide (DMF), 2,7-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), hydrogen peroxide (30 %), thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide, and annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection kit were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), PBS, trypsin-EDTA and penicillin/streptomycin (5000 U/mL) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alkaline phosphatase (AKP) assay kits were obtained from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (China). LiperFluo was purchased from Dojindo laboratories (Kumamoto, Japan). Monochlorobimane (MCB) which used as a glutathione (GSH) specific fluorescent probe, Alexa Fluor 488 AffiniPure donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (34206ES60), MTT assay reagent, H&E staining kit were purchased from YEASEN. Anti-GPX-4 was purchased from Proteintech.

Preparation of selenium-containing polymer nanoparticles PSF

Cisplatin (10 mg/mL) and the selenium-containing polymer (100 mg/mL) were dissolved in DMF and mixed for 10 min. The resulting solution was slowly added to deionized water under sonication for 10 min to induce nanoparticle self-assembly. Then, ferrous sulfate solution (FeSO4 = 5 mg/mL) was added, and the mixture was allowed to react at room temperature for 12 h to obtain PSF.

Preparation of selenium-containing polymer nanoparticles PS

The preparation of PS followed the same procedure as for PSF, except that ferrous sulfate solution was replaced with deionized water.

Preparation of selenium-containing polymer nanoparticles SF

The preparation of SF followed the same procedure as for PSF, except that cisplatin solution was replaced with DMF.

Preparation of fluorescence-labeled selenium-containing polymer nanoparticles

The preparation of PSF-FITC/Rod B-Se-Fe followed the same as procedure for PSF, except that cisplatin was replaced with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) or Rhodamine B (Rod B).

Characterization of materials

PSF images were obtained through a Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) while the chemical composition of the material (in different points of the sample) was analyzed via energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS).

Hydrodynamic particle size was measured using Malvern particle size meter. FT-IR spectra were recorded using an Infrared spectrophotometer.

PSF responsive release of cisplatin was determined by HPLC: PSF was incubated at 37 °C in normal saline containing H2O2, sampled at specific time points, and supplemented with normal saline (preheated at 37 °C). The integrated area of each sample was determined by HPLC, and then the concentration was calculated via standard calibration curves.

Generation of extracellular Fe2+: PSF was incubated without or with GSH, centrifuged at different time points (12000 rpm, 10 min), sampled at specific time points, and supplemented with normal saline. The collected supernatants were mixed with phenanthroline solution (100 mM), reacted for 15 min, and absorbance at 510 nm was measured.

Detection of •OH

The ·OH-induced MB degradation was monitored by the absorbance change at 665 nm. Cisplatin, PS, SF and PSF (FeSO4 = 0.25 mg/mL) were incubated with MB (20 μM) and H2O2 (30 mM) in a phosphate buffer solution (pH = 4–5) at 37 °C for 20 min. After returning to room temperature, the absorbance at 665 nm was recorded by a multifunctional microplate reader to monitor the •OH-induced degradation of MB. (1) MB + PSF + H2O2, (2) MB + PS + H2O2, (3) MB + SF + H2O2, (4) MB + cisplatin + H2O2, (5) MB + PSF, (6) MB + PS, (7) MB + SF, (8) MB + cisplatin, (9) MB + H2O2, (10) MB.

Cellular uptake assays

Confocal and flow cytometry were used to observe and analyze the uptake capacity of 4T1 to selenium-containing polymer nanoparticles. For CLSM observation, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 48-well plate with 8 mm cell slides at a density of 1 × 105 per well for 24 h. After the cell is attached to the wall, the culture media were replaced with fresh media containing FITC-labeled polymer nanoparticles (PSF-FITC) or free FITC as control, the cells further incubated for 12 h, respectively. Then, the cell slides were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution for twice, fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed three times with ice-cold PBS, removed and sealed with anti-fluorescence quenching tablet containing DAPI. Finally, the cells were imaged by a Zeiss LSM5 confocal microscope to monitor the green fluorescence from FITC-labeled polymer nanoparticles.

For flow cytometry analysis, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 per well for 24 h. Then the cells were treated with PSF-FITC or free FITC for another 12 h. After washing twice with ice-cold PBS, the cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged, resuspended with 500 uL PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cytotoxicity analysis

MTT assay was performed to evaluate the cytotoxicity of different polymer nanoparticles. Mouse breast cancer 4T1 cells were inoculated in a 96-well plate according to the density of 10,000 cells/well. After cultured in a cell incubator for 24 h, cells were treated with varying concentration of samples. After continuous culture for 24 h, MTT assay reagent was added into the 96-well plate for further incubation for 4 h. Finally, the medium was removed, DMSO was added to dissolve the product, and the multifunctional enzyme plate analyzer was used to detect and characterize the product at 490 nm.

Annexin-V FITC/PI assay was also used to analyze apoptosis. 4T1 cells were plated at a density of 1 × 105 in 6-well cell culture plates and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. Then the medium was replaced with fresh media containing PBS, cisplatin, SF, and PSF at equivalent cisplatin (Pt) dose of 2 μg/mL. The cells were incubated for 24 h before washed with PBS and stained by annexin-V FITC and propidium iodide (PI) according to the kit protocol. The fluorescence intensity of cells was viewed by flow cytometry.

Evaluation of intracellular ROS production

The total ROS produced by different polymer nanoparticles inside 4T1 cells was measured by ROS-sensitive DCFH-DA probe through both CLSM observation and flow cytometry assay. For CLSM observation, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 48-well plate with 8 mm cell slides at a density of 1 × 105 per well for 24 h. Then the cells were treated with PBS, cisplatin, SF, or PSF at equivalent Pt dose of 2 μg/mL for another 8 h. The treated cells were washed with PBS twice and incubated with 10 μM of DCFH-DA for 30 min. After removal of the free probes with PBS, the cell slides were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed three times with ice-cold PBS, and then the cell slides were removed and sealed with anti-fluorescence quenching tablet containing DAPI. Finally, the fluorescence intensity of cells was monitored by a confocal microscope.

For flow cytometry analysis, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 per well for 24 h. Then the cells were treated with PBS, cisplatin, SF, or PSF at equivalent Pt dose of 2 μg/mL for another 8 h. After washing twice with ice-cold PBS, DCFH-DA probe was added and incubated for 30 min. Then, excess probe was washed with ice-cold PBS, and the cells were digested with trypsin, centrifuged, resuspended with 500 μL PBS and analyzed by a flow cytometry.

Evaluation of GSH consumption in tumor cells

Intracellular GSH depletion was observed and recorded by confocal microscopy. The experimental procedure was similar to that used for evaluation of intracellular ROS production, except that DCFH-DA was replaced with MCB and cell slides were sealed with an anti-fluorescence quench without DAPI.

Evaluation of intracellular GPX-4 expression

The intracellular expression of GPX-4 levels was analyzed by immunofluorescent microscopy and western blot assay. For immunofluorescent microscopy, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 48-well plate with 8 mm cell slides at a density of 1 × 105 per well for 24 h. Then the cells were treated with PBS, Se, cisplatin, SF, PSF or PSF with Fer-1 (Cells were treated with Fer-1 1 h in advance) at equivalent Pt dose of 2 μg/mL for another 8 h. After washing twice with ice-cold PBS, the cell slides were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Then, the cells were permeated with 0.2 % Triton X-100 for 30min and blocked with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 30min. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C in anti-GPX-4 antibody (1:500). After washing with PBS, the cells were incubated with secondary antibody conjugated to goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 488) for 2 h at 4 °C. Finally, the cell slides were removed and sealed with anti-fluorescence quenching tablet containing DAPI. Fluorescence images were observed with a Zeiss LSM5 confocal microscope.

For western blot assay, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 per well for 24 h. The cells were treated with PBS, Se, cisplatin, SF, PSF or PSF with Fer-1 (Cells were treated with Fer-1 1 h in advance) at equivalent Pt dose of 2 μg/mL for another 24 h. After lysing cells, the obtained proteins were quantified using BCA Protein Assay Kit and mixed with 5 × loading buffer to heat in 95 °C for 10 min to produce the protein samples. Proteins were loaded to 10 % sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis for electrophoresis and electronically transferred onto the polyvinylidene fluoride mem-branes, followed by submerging in 5 % skim milk to prevent the nonspecific protein signals. Then, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against GPX-4 (1:1000), and internal control GAPDH (1:1000) overnight at 4 °C, followed by the incubation of goat anti‐mouse or anti‐rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) H&L (HRP) secondary anti-body (1:5,000) for 1 h. Finally, BeyoECL Star was used to measure the protein signals combined with antibodies and the level analyses were carried out by ImageJ software.

Evaluation of intracellular LPO production

Intracellular LPO production was observed and recorded by confocal microscopy. The experimental procedure was similar to that used for evaluation of intracellular ROS production, except that DCFH-DA was replaced with LiperFluo.

Intracellular mitochondrial membrane potential (Δφ m) assay (JC-1)

Mitochondrial membrane potential (Δφ m) was detected by observing fluorescence shifts using the mitochondrial permeability fluorescent dye JC-1. 4T1 cells were cultured in 48-well plates as described above. Then the cells were treated with PBS, Se, cisplatin, SF, PSF or PSF with Fer-1 (Cells were treated with Fer-1 1 h in advance) at equivalent Pt dose of 2 μg/mL for another 8 h. After washing twice with PBS, the cells were incubated with JC-1 (4 μg/mL) at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for 30 min. After washing twice with ice-cold PBS, the cell slides were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Potential changes were observed by confocal microscopy in a similar manner as above, after blocked with anti-fluorescence quencher and observed. The 488 nm filter was used to excite JC-1. Mitochondrial activity was assessed with fluorescence in green (JC-1 monomer) and red (JC-1 aggregates) using 535 and 595 nm emission filters, respectively.

Anti-tumor effect in vivo

BABL/c mice were obtained from Xiamen University Laboratory Animal Center and all animal experiments were approved by the Animal Management and Ethics Committee of Xiamen University (XMULAC20230118). 4T1 tumor-bearing BABL/c mice were randomly divided into five groups when the tumor volume exceeded 100 mm3, with 5 mice in each group and were intravenously injected with: 1) PBS; 2) Se; 3) cisplatin, 4) SF and 5) PSF (equivalent Pt dose of 2.00 mg kg−1). During 14 days of the corresponding treatments, the body weight and tumor size of the mice were recorded every two days. The tumor size was calculated according to the following formula: Volume = tumor width squared ∗ length/2. At the end of the treatment, tumors and major organs were taken out from the sacrificed mice for histological analysis.

In vivo biodistribution

To investigate the biodistribution of the prepared nanoparticle in tumor-bearing mice, each 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse were intravenously injected with Rob B-Se-Fe and Rob B, respectively. At 12 h post-injection, the mice were sacrificed, and the major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) and tumors were extracted. The distribution of polymer nanoparticles in tumors and major organs was observed by Caliper IVIS Lumina II.

In vivo biosafety evaluation

Mice body weight was recorded during the in vivo experiment, and the biochemical biomarker including ALP, ALT, AST, AKP, and ALB were examined after mice sacrificed. Simply, the mice serum was collected at the end of in vivo experiment, and biochemical factor kit was applied to examine the level of indicated biomarker according to the standard sample curve.

Also, the main organs including heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney were collected for histological analysis. The isolated tumors or major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) are routinely processed before frozen sectioning. The Leica microtome is used to slice tissues with a thickness of 6 μm, and then hematoxylin and eosin are used for tissue staining analysis.

The whole blood of mouse was collected into 2 mL EP tube that was rinsed with 1 % heparin sodium, and then centrifuged at 3500 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min to collect red blood cells for hemolysis test. Next, red blood cells were suspended twice with normal saline and centrifuged under the same conditions. After that, 1 mL red blood cells were diluted to 10 mL with normal saline. 200 μL diluted red blood cell solution was added to the normal saline prepared with different concentrations of PSF, SF, Pt, and distilled water was used as the positive control while normal saline was used as the negative control. After incubation at 37 °C for 3 h, centrifugation was performed at 4 °C at 5000 rpm for 5 min. Finally, photos were taken and the supernatant was absorbed to determine the absorption value at 580 nm. Hemolysis (%) = [(ABS - ABS0)/(ABS100 - ABS0)] × 100 %. ABS0 represents the absorption value of the negative control (normal saline), ABS100 represents the absorption value of the positive control (distilled water), and ABS represents the absorption value of different sample groups. Each experiment was repeated three times.

Cisplatin release experiment in vitro

PSF was incubated without or with GSH, centrifuged at different time points (12000 rpm, 10 min), sampled at specific time points, and supplemented with PBS. The collected supernatant was digested with aqua regia, diluted 10-fold and detected by ICP-OES (SPECTROBLUE FMX36).

Statistical analysis

The data here were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.1 software. Differences between the two groups were analyzed by two-tailed unpaired student's t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were performed using one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA. Differences between the two groups were determined to be significant at ∗P < 0.05 and highly significant at ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001 and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001.
