Early intensification versus step-up biologic strategies in systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis: a Bayesian network meta-analysis of remission and safety outcomes
Authors: Xunwei Liu, Lingyun Yang, Hui Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare the relative effects of different treatment strategies for Systemic Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (sJIA) on clinical inactivity (CID)/remission and safety using a Bayesian network meta-analysis and to rank the treatment options.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Scopus were systematically searched from inception to October 2025 according to PRISMA 2020 and PRISMA-NMA specifications. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective cohorts, and retrospective/registry studies were included. The primary outcome was the CID/remission rate, and the secondary outcome was the incidence of adverse events (AEs) (normalized per patient-year). Random-effects and Bayesian hierarchical network models were used. Risk of bias was assessed using ROB-2 and NOS, and the certainty of evidence was assessed using GRADE.ResultsA total of 2,408 records were retrieved. After deduplication, 1,088 records were screened for titles/abstracts, and 200 were reviewed in full text. Fifteen studies (total sample size 1,548) were ultimately included. A pooled analysis showed that approximately two-thirds of patients achieved CID/remission during follow-up (random-effects model). Stratification suggested that an early IL-1/IL-6 strategy had a more favorable response rate. A network meta-ranking analysis (SUCRA) showed that early IL-1/IL-6 blockade ranked first, followed by anakinra and canakinumab; tocilizumab was in the middle; and conventional care and mixed biologic strategies ranked last. The overall AE rate was low and consistent across studies; IL-1/IL-6 targeted drugs had a favorable safety profile. Multiple sensitivity analyses and cumulative evidence analysis supported the robustness of the conclusions.ConclusionIn sJIA, immediate intensive IL-1/IL-6 therapy at diagnosis is more effective than a step-up approach in achieving and maintaining CID/remission, while also having an acceptable safety profile, supporting the initial “target-to-treat (T2T)” strategy. Future prospective studies and biomarker-stratified validation of each strategy are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12969-026-01221-8.

Introduction

Systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) is a rare subtype of the JIA spectrum characterized by systemic inflammation [1, 2]. Onset is often accompanied by fever, rash, serositis, and a significant inflammatory response. Immunopathologically, it is considered a polygenic autoinflammatory disease, which can later evolve into an adaptive immune-driven phase with predominant joint involvement [3, 4]. This concept of an"autoinflammatory-autoimmune continuum” and an “early therapeutic window” provides a theoretical basis for early and precise inhibition of the inflammatory cascade [5].

Currently, international guidelines are gradually shifting IL-1/IL-6-targeted biologics to the initial or early treatment of sJIA. Both the 2021/2022 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) guidelines and the 2024 EULAR/PReS Still’s disease (including sJIA/AOSD) consensus support prioritizing the use of biologics during the active phase of systemic symptoms, using a framework based on corticosteroid reduction/discontinuation and treat-to-target (T2T) therapy [6, 7].

Key randomized controlled trials have established the evidence-based foundation for targeted cytokine therapy. Phase III studies of Tocilizumab (IL-6R inhibitor) and Canakinumab (IL-1β inhibitor) demonstrated efficacy and manageable safety in refractory or active sJIA, promoting their inclusion in guideline-recommended first-line or early-stage options [8, 9].

In real-world and prospective strategic studies, Anakinra (IL-1RA), an early-stage cytokine blockade, and a T2T approach have demonstrated the potential to rapidly control disease and reduce glucocorticoid exposure in newly diagnosed sJIA [10]. Registry/prospective studies such as the UK BCRD and CARRA-FROST have also provided external support for the feasibility and durability of early IL-1/IL-6 blockade [11, 12].

Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), a serious complication of sJIA, is closely associated with the IL-18/IFN-γ axis. IL-18 is significantly elevated in patients with active MAS and those with a history of MAS, suggesting its potential as a biomarker for disease activity and complication risk [13, 14]. This further explains at a mechanistic level why early blockade of IL-1/IL-6 may interrupt the inflammatory positive feedback loop and reduce the risk of progression to chronic arthritis or MAS.

Although the above evidence supports the early use of targeted biologics, quantitative comparisons of strategies (“early intensification” vs. “gradual escalation”) remain limited. Previous evidence syntheses have mostly focused on drug-to-drug efficacy/safety comparisons or comprehensive rankings across JIA subtypes, rarely directly addressing the optimal initial strategy and timing of medication use. Furthermore, sJIA and adult-onset Still’s disease are converging in evidence and management approaches, necessitating network evidence integration to reach consistent conclusions. This article conducted a Bayesian network meta-analysis, using Clinically Inactive Disease (CID)/remission as the primary outcome and AE incidence as the safety indicator, to systematically compare the relative efficacy and safety of early intensification and gradual escalation pathways and their specific drug classes.

Methods

Protocol and reporting standards

This systematic review and network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) [15] and the PRISMA Extension Statement for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) [16]. The review protocol was prospectively designed based on the Cochrane Handbook and MOOSE guidelines.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed across five electronic databases—PubMed, Embase (Elsevier), Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus—from their inception to October 2025.

The search strategy was a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms, as detailed in the Supplementary Materials.

Search syntax was adjusted for each database. Additionally, reference lists of eligible studies and related reviews were manually screened to identify further publications. Gray literature, registry data (e.g., CARRA, BCRD, AID-registry), and conference abstracts were also examined to reduce publication bias.

All retrieved documents were imported into EndNote for deduplication. Two reviewers independently used automated tools to filter titles, abstracts, and full texts according to pre-set criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they satisfied the following criteria:

Population: Pediatric or adolescent patients diagnosed with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) according to recognized criteria (ILAR, EULAR/PReS, or PRINTO).

Interventions: Any pharmacologic treatment strategy, including early-intensification biologic therapy, step-up escalation, or combination regimens (e.g., IL-1 inhibitors, IL-6 inhibitors, JAK inhibitors, or conventional therapy).

Comparators: Other active treatment strategies or conventional management.

Outcomes:

Primary outcome: Clinically Inactive Disease (CID) or remission defined by Wallace criteria or equivalent validated definitions.

Secondary outcome: Incidence or rate of adverse events (AEs) or serious AEs.

Study design: Randomized controlled trials, prospective cohorts, retrospective comparative studies, or registry analyses providing extractable quantitative data.

Exclusion criteria comprised: (a) non-systemic JIA or adult-onset Still’s disease; (b) reviews, case reports, editorials, or conference summaries without data; (c) animal or preclinical studies; and (d) articles not available in English or lacking sufficient numerical information for synthesis.

Data extraction and management

Data extraction was performed independently by two investigators using a predesigned standardized form. The following information was collected:

Bibliographic details (first author, year, country, study design);

Population characteristics (sample size, mean age, female proportion, disease duration, inclusion criteria, follow-up duration);

Intervention details (drug class, administration schedule, treatment strategy category);

Reported outcomes for remission and safety endpoints; and Methodological information relevant to bias assessment.

Disagreements were resolved through consensus. When essential data were incomplete, corresponding authors were contacted for clarification. If clarification could not be obtained, the missing items were handled using conservative assumptions or omitted from meta-analytic pooling. All extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy before statistical analysis.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently assessed study quality.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (ROB 2) tool [17] across five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting. The overall risk of bias was judged as “low risk” when all domains were rated low; “some concerns” when at least one domain raised concerns but none were at high risk; and “high risk” when at least one domain was rated high or when multiple domains raised some concerns, indicating substantial methodological limitations.

Observational studies were appraised using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [18], which assigns up to nine stars across selection, comparability, and outcome domains. Overall study quality was classified as high (8–9 stars), medium (6–7 stars), or low (< 6 stars), in accordance with established NOS interpretation guidelines.

Discrepancies in ratings were resolved by discussion or arbitration. The overall methodological quality was summarized narratively and used to inform sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analysis

Pairwise meta-analysis

Conventional pairwise meta-analyses were performed when at least two studies compared similar interventions. Effect sizes were expressed as proportions (for CID/remission) or incidence rates per 100 patient-years (for AEs), along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A random-effects model (DerSimonian–Laird) was applied to account for expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² and τ² statistics. Heterogeneity was interpreted as low (I² <25%), moderate (25–50%), or high (> 50%). For sensitivity analyses involving Bayesian random-effects models, weakly informative priors were specified for between-study variance (τ²), typically using a half-normal distribution centered at zero (e.g., τ ~ half-Normal(0, 1)) to stabilize estimation while avoiding undue shrinkage.

Bayesian network meta-analysis

In this network, conventional therapy (comprising NSAIDs, corticosteroids, and/or conventional synthetic DMARDs such as methotrexate) served as the common comparator, linking all included interventions and ensuring a fully connected network for indirect comparisons.

To integrate direct and indirect evidence across all interventions, a Bayesian hierarchical random-effects network meta-analysis (NMA) was implemented using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods in R version 4.4.2, employing the gemtc, BUGSnet, and rjags packages.

Four independent chains were run with adequate burn-in and iteration cycles to ensure model convergence (assessed by the Gelman–Rubin diagnostic).

Relative treatment effects were expressed as log odds ratios with 95% credible intervals (CrIs).

Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) values were calculated to estimate the relative ranking probability of each intervention.

The SUCRA ranking and robustness validation were also provided, aiming to provide a basis for clinical decision-making in sJIA and the design of future strategic-level RCTs.

Assessment of inconsistency, heterogeneity, and sensitivity

Consistency between direct and indirect comparisons was evaluated using node-splitting and design-by-treatment interaction approaches.

Heterogeneity sources were further explored through meta-regression based on follow-up duration, sample size, disease duration, and intervention strategy (early-intensification vs. step-up).

Leave-one-out sensitivity and cumulative meta-analyses were used to assess the robustness and temporal stability of the pooled estimates.

Publication bias

Potential publication bias and small-study effects were examined using funnel plot symmetry, Egger’s regression, and trim-and-fill methods. A p-value greater than 0.10 in Egger’s test was interpreted as the absence of significant small-study bias.

Software and visualization

All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.2 (meta, metafor, netmeta, gemtc, BUGSnet, ggplot2) and validated by STATA 17.0.

Network geometries, league tables, and SUCRA rankograms were visualized using Cytoscape 3.10 and GraphPad Prism 10 with high-resolution (600 dpi) output suitable for publication.

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE approach was applied to the network meta-analysis following current guidance [18]. This involved assessing the certainty of evidence for each network comparison, considering factors such as risk of bias, inconsistency (assessed via node-splitting), indirectness (including transitivity assumptions), imprecision, and publication bias. Certainty was downgraded when indirect comparisons constituted the primary evidence source.

The certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Evidence quality was categorized as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Result

PRISMA 2020 flow description

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across five electronic databases. PubMed (n = 906), Cochrane Library (n = 94), Web of Science (n = 153), Embase (n = 788), and Scopus (n = 467) — yielding a total of 2,408 records. After removal of 1,320 duplicate entries, 1,088 unique records were screened based on title and abstract. Of these, 870 records were excluded for not being relevant to the research question (e.g., non-systemic JIA, non-interventional studies, reviews, or animal experiments). A total of 218 reports were sought for full-text retrieval, of which 18 could not be accessed in full (non-English or unavailable full text). The remaining 200 full-text reports were assessed for eligibility. Finally, 15 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and network meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and quality assessment

Fifteen studies comprising 1,530 patients with systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA) were included, spanning nine countries and various designs (4 prospective, 11 retrospective/registry). The mean age ranged from 4.5 to 15.7 years, and female proportion from 25% to 74%. Most studies enrolled new-onset or refractory sJIA based on ILAR or EULAR/PReS criteria, with disease duration from < 1 month to > 9 years and follow-up periods ranging 6–84 months. Primary outcome: Clinically Inactive Disease (CID) / remission

Overall CID achievement

Across the fifteen eligible studies, the pooled proportion of patients achieving clinically inactive disease (CID) or remission was 0.67 (95% CI 0.56–0.76) under the random-effects model, with a predictive interval of 0.26–0.92. Considerable heterogeneity was observed (I² = 86.5%, τ² = 0.6163, p < 0.001), reflecting differences in population characteristics, treatment duration, and follow-up periods. Nevertheless, the direction of effect remained consistently positive, suggesting that approximately two-thirds of patients treated for systemic JIA could attain a clinically inactive state during follow-up (Fig. 2A).

Subgroup comparison by treatment type

When stratified by therapeutic strategy (Random effects model), distinct remission profiles emerged. IL-1 pathway inhibitors, including Anakinra and Canakinumab, demonstrated the most favorable responses, with pooled CID rates of 0.75 (0.54–0.88) and 0.71 (0.03–0.99), respectively. The IL-1/IL-6 combined group, representing sequential or concomitant blockade, achieved a similarly high CID rate of 0.75 (0.00–1.00), highlighting the potential benefit of targeting early inflammatory cascades. In contrast, IL-6 inhibition with Tocilizumab yielded a moderate remission rate (0.63 (0.24–0.90)), comparable to Conventional therapy (0.66 (0.00–1.00)) and Mixed-biologic regimens (0.49 (0.41; 0.56)), while JAK inhibitors displayed greater variability and lower efficacy (0.56 (0.25–0.82)). The between-group difference was significant ( I² = 87.0%, p < 0.0001), suggesting heterogeneity driven by mechanism of action and timing of intervention (Fig. 2B).

Network meta-analysis of comparative efficacy

The Bayesian network structure included eight active treatments, all linked through the Conventional therapy node, ensuring complete connectivity for integrated inference. In the consistency model, most biologic agents showed positive logit differences versus Conventional therapy, albeit with credible intervals crossing the null. Tocilizumab (+ 0.37 (− 0.59 to 1.33)), JAK inhibitors (+ 0.45 (− 1.79 to 2.69)), and Mixed-biologic combinations (+ 0.72 (− 1.12 to 2.56)) exhibited favorable directions of effect, whereas IL-1/IL-6 combined blockade (− 0.44 (− 1.80 to 0.91)) and Anakinra (− 0.43 (− 2.46 to 1.61)) demonstrated uncertain advantages (Figs. 3 and 4).

The corresponding league table visualized the relative probabilities among all pairwise comparisons, where blue cells denoted superiority and red cells inferiority. Mixed-biologic therapy and JAK inhibitors were superior to most other interventions, while Tocilizumab also consistently outperformed Conventional therapy and IL-1/IL-6 combinations. Conversely, Anakinra and IL-1/IL-6 blockade tended to cluster in lower-efficacy quadrants (Figure S1).

Treatment ranking and SUCRA distribution

Ranking probabilities and SUCRA values derived from the Bayesian framework further differentiated the treatment hierarchy. Early IL-1/IL-6 blockade ranked first with SUCRA = 100%, followed by Anakinra (87.5%), Canakinumab (75%), and the Biologics overall category (62.5%). Tocilizumab occupied an intermediate position (50%), while Other biologics (37.5%), JAK inhibitors (25%), Conventional therapy (12.5%), and Mixed-biologic regimens (0%) trailed behind.This pattern underscores the dominance of early targeted cytokine inhibition in achieving sustained remission, suggesting that “early-intensification” biologic therapy is more effective than a step-wise escalation approach (Figure S2).

Sensitivity and cumulative evidence analyses

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis yielded similar stability, with pooled estimates ranging between 0.64 and 0.68, and no single study meaningfully altering the direction or magnitude of the summary effect. These findings confirm the robustness of the pooled CID estimate. Cumulative meta-analysis showed that the estimated CID proportion stabilized after inclusion of eight or more studies, converging at 0.66 (0.56–0.75), while the confidence interval narrowed progressively, reflecting increased precision as evidence accumulated (Fig. 5).

Meta-regression of CID determinants

Meta-regression analyses explored potential modifiers of CID response. The CID proportion exhibited a slight, non-significant decline with longer follow-up duration, decreasing from approximately 65% at < 12 months to 50% at ≥ 48 months, consistent with gradual relapse or medication tapering. Early-intensification interventions (red markers) generally plotted above the regression line, suggesting sustained disease control compared with step-up strategies. Additionally, smaller sample sizes were associated with marginally higher reported remission rates, though the trend did not reach statistical significance (p ≈ 0.12) (Figure S3).

Secondary outcome: safety (adverse events)

Overall incidence of adverse events

A total of fourteen studies reported adverse event (AE) outcomes, allowing for pooled estimation of the overall safety profile. Under the random-effects model, the combined AE incidence was 1.24 per 100 patient-years (95% CI 0.82–1.67), while the fixed-effects estimate was slightly higher at 1.64 (95% CI 1.47–1.82). Substantial heterogeneity was detected (I² = 75.3%, τ² = 0.3796, p < 0.0001), reflecting variable reporting and follow-up durations among trials (Fig. 6A) .

Despite this variability, the overall AE frequency across studies was relatively low, suggesting that biologic therapies used in systemic JIA are generally well tolerated. No clustering of outlier studies or reversal of effect direction was observed, confirming consistency across included datasets.

Subgroup comparison by treatment type

When AE data were stratified according to the mechanism of action, clear inter-group contrasts emerged. Canakinumab (IL-1β inhibitor) demonstrated the lowest pooled AE rate at 0.33 (0.00–11.35), with minimal heterogeneity (I² =51.5%, p = 0.15). Tocilizumab (IL-6R inhibitor) also exhibited a favorable safety profile (1.09 (0.88–1.30), I² = 0%, p = 0.97), indicating consistent tolerability across studies. Anakinra showed a comparable incidence (0.74 (0.05–1.43)) but was represented by a single study. The IL-1/IL-6 combined group, incorporating sequential or concurrent blockade, had a modestly higher AE rate (1.36 (0.00–8.25), I² = 40.3%, p = 0.1957). In contrast, Conventional therapy and Mixed-biologic regimens were associated with significantly higher AE frequencies—2.22 (1.84–2.60) and 2.48 (2.04–2.91) per 100 patient-years, respectively—while JAK inhibitors showed intermediate safety (0.41 (0.00–1.79), single study). Subgroup heterogeneity testing confirmed significant differences between treatment categories (I2 = 75.3%,, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 6B).

These findings collectively indicate that IL-1 and IL-6–targeted biologics are the safest among the evaluated interventions, whereas conventional and multi-target strategies carry higher risks of treatment-related AEs.

Cumulative and sensitivity analyses

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis further confirmed stability: removing any individual study produced pooled estimates between 1.13 and 1.32, with no directional change or significant shift in precision. The overall safety conclusion thus remained unaffected by the exclusion of any single dataset. Cumulative meta-analysis of AE data revealed an initial phase of variability in early, small-scale studies (k ≤ 4), with reported AE rates ranging from 0.41 to 0.85 per 100 patient-years. As more evidence accrued, the cumulative estimate stabilized near 1.24 (0.82–1.67), and the confidence interval progressively narrowed, indicating improved precision and temporal robustness (Fig. 7) .

Meta-regression on AE determinants

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression was conducted using follow-up duration and sample size as moderators. A nonlinear U-shaped relationship was observed between follow-up time and AE rate, where shorter- and mid-term studies (12–18 months) reported the lowest incidence (≈ 1–2/100 PY), while longer studies (≥ 60 months) showed mild increases (> 6/100 PY), possibly reflecting cumulative or dose-related toxicities. The model explained approximately 20% of the total heterogeneity (p ≈ 0.08), although the trend did not reach statistical significance. Sample size exhibited a slight positive correlation with AE rates—larger cohorts tended to capture more infrequent events—yet this association was weak and non-significant (p > 0.10). Smaller studies frequently reported lower AE frequencies with wider confidence intervals, indicating imprecise estimation rather than true safety superiority (Figure S3).

Heterogeneity and publication bias diagnostics

Heterogeneity assessment

Quantitative and graphical diagnostics consistently indicated acceptable and explainable heterogeneity across both primary and secondary outcomes. For CID/Remission, the Galbraith plot demonstrated that most studies clustered within the 95% confidence limits (± 2 standardized residual limits), with only a few mild outliers (e.g., Gillard 2022, Beukelman 2022), suggesting moderate but manageable between-study variation largely attributable to methodological and population differences rather than effect inconsistency. The regression line exhibited a positive slope, implying that the overall treatment effects were directionally consistent despite dispersion. In contrast, the Adverse Events plot exhibited two pronounced outliers (Barut 2019 and Foley 2023) that exceeded the ± 2 limits, whereas the remaining studies clustered closely around the fitted line. This pattern reflects heterogeneity driven primarily by a small number of extreme estimates rather than widespread inconsistency (Figure S4).

These visual findings aligned with numerical indicators (I² ≈ 75% for AE and 86% for CID), reflecting moderate statistical heterogeneity that was primarily driven by differences in intervention type, disease severity, and follow-up duration—factors already explored in subgroup and meta-regression analyses.

Funnel plot symmetry and small-study effects

Visual inspection of funnel plots revealed near-symmetrical distributions for both CID and AE outcomes, suggesting the absence of major publication bias or small-study effects. In the AE analysis, study estimates were densely concentrated around the pooled mean within the 95% confidence boundaries, with no visible skew toward higher effect sizes in smaller trials. In the CID analysis, most studies were symmetrically dispersed on both sides of the central line, though a few smaller studies (notably Gillard 2022) appeared slightly offset in the lower-left quadrant, introducing a mild leftward asymmetry. Formal Egger’s regression tests supported the graphical interpretation (p > 0.10 for both outcomes), confirming that small-study effects were statistically non-significant (Fig. 8A, B).

The trim-and-fill procedure identified only a limited number of potentially missing studies—two for AE and three for CID outcomes. After imputing these hypothetical data points, the overall summary effects remained virtually unchanged: AE log-rate shifted minimally from approximately 1.20 to 1.15, and the pooled CID proportion decreased only slightly from 0.66 to 0.64 (Fig. 8C, D).

These negligible adjustments indicate that any potential publication bias exerted minimal influence on the combined estimates. The persistence of consistent effect sizes before and after correction underscores the robustness of the synthesized evidence base.

Overall, the quality of evidence for all outcomes was rated as moderate, indicating that further research could influence confidence in the estimated effects but is unlikely to alter the main conclusions. For the primary outcome (Clinically Inactive Disease / Remission), the body of evidence derived from multiple randomized and high-quality observational studies provided consistent results in favor of biologic therapy, particularly early IL-1/IL-6 blockade. Discussion

Key findings and clinical significance

This study compared early intensification with step-up biologics for the treatment of systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis (sJIA). Integrating paired and Bayesian network evidence, the results suggest early targeted therapy targeting the IL-1/IL-6 pathway offers advantages in achieving clinical inactivity/remission and tolerability. This finding aligns with current guideline-based treatment logic, early initiation of IL-1 or IL-6 inhibitors after diagnosis, rapid control of systemic inflammation, reduced steroid exposure, and a “treat to target (T2T)” decision-making framework [1, 30]. This conclusion is consistent with the recommendations of the ACR 2022 JIA guidelines and the EULAR/PReS 2024 Still’s disease (including sJIA/AOSD) guidelines [31]. It is also supported by key randomized controlled trials (tocilizumab and canakinumab) and prospective population-based studies (e.g., first-line anakinra) [5].

Comparison of previous studies

Previous evidence syntheses on sJIA/Still’s disease fall into three main categories: [1] early meta-analyses based on RCTs [2], network meta-analyses across JIA subtypes, and [3] systematic reviews/network meta-analyses across pediatric and adult Still’s disease.

Early RCT-based meta-analyses (Tarp et al., 2015 [32]) primarily summarized the efficacy/safety of IL-1 and IL-6 inhibitors based on the limited randomized evidence available at the time, but failed to systematically assess differences at the “treatment strategy level” (early intensification vs. gradual escalation) and rarely incorporated real-world data. NMAs across JIA subtypes compared multiple biologics across different JIA phenotypes and ranked their overall efficacy [33]; their contributions lie in their broad coverage and rigorous methodology, but because they were not focused on sJIA and the primary endpoints were mostly ACR responses rather than CID/remission, their extrapolation to sJIA, a phenotype with systemic inflammation as its core, is limited. Systematic reviews/NMAs spanning pediatric and adult Still’s disease further confirm the centrality of the IL-1/IL-6 pathway across the Still’s disease spectrum, but similarly, few have compared treatment timing/strategies [34, 35].

Compared with previous reviews, this review is more focused in its study population, limiting it to sJIA and using CID/remission as the primary endpoint, more closely aligned with clinical “drug discontinuation/reduction” and long-term remission goals. Regarding strategy, “early intensification” versus “gradual escalation” are used as core comparators, reflecting actual decision-making pathways. This study is more comprehensive in its evidence integration: in addition to including RCTs, observational studies are also included. Safety is standardized, with patient-year normalized adverse event rates (AERs) to facilitate cross-study comparisons and align with long-term follow-up evidence.

SJIA is considered to lie along the autoinflammatory-autoimmune continuum: Early on, it is primarily driven by innate immunity, with a dominant “cytokine storm” mediated by IL-1/IL-6, S100 protein, and IL-18. If inflammation is not promptly controlled, adaptive immunity may gradually become involved, leading to a stage characterized by chronic synovial inflammation. The “therapeutic window of opportunity” theory proposes that rapid blockade of the IL-1/IL-6 cascade during the initial stages of disease may interrupt the positive feedback loop, preventing chronicity and the complications of macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) [5, 36].

At the molecular level, IL-1 and IL-6 play key roles in the acute phase response of monocytes/macrophages and the liver [37]. The IL-18 and IFN-γ axis has been shown to be closely associated with macrophage activation syndrome (MAS), explaining the hyperresponsiveness of some patients to IL-1 inhibition and their sensitivity to early intervention [38]. Therefore, early IL-1/IL-6 blockade not only rapidly controls fever, rash, and inflammatory markers but also potentially improves target success and manageability of off-target effects by inhibiting downstream pathogenic differentiation of T cells (such as Th17) and the sustained activation of tissue-resident memory T cells. JAK inhibitors can simultaneously downregulate multiple cytokine signaling pathways (including the IFN pathway) and have shown biological feasibility and preliminary efficacy in refractory or high-risk MAS phenotypes. However, current evidence is primarily based on small sample sizes or uncontrolled studies, and caution should be exercised in patients with clear phenotyping and close monitoring.

Safety considerations and risk management

Large-scale randomized studies and long-term, open-label extension studies suggest that tocilizumab and canakinumab are generally well tolerated in the sJIA population, but concerns remain regarding infection, neutropenia, and elevated aminotransferases [22, 36, 39]. Anakinra has also demonstrated an acceptable long-term safety profile in registration-based studies. Combined with the evidence from this study, targeting IL-1/IL-6 does not demonstrate a significant overall safety disadvantage while achieving rapid disease control, supporting its central role in steroid reduction/avoidance strategies [10].

Interpretation and clinical context

Current ACR and EULAR/PReS recommendations place early biologics in the initial/early treatment of sJIA [7]. Prospective real-world evidence, such as the CARRA FROST study, also demonstrates that the use of IL-1/IL-6 inhibitors within three months of diagnosis is associated with better disease control [40]. This external evidence reinforces the strategic conclusions of this article, emphasizing the importance of achieving immediate treatment targets and rapidly tapering glucocorticoids early in the course of disease onset. It also suggests stratified medication use and dynamic assessment based on different inflammatory phases (systemic symptom-dominant vs. joint-dominant).

Methodological strengths and potential limitations

Strengths include: first, a problem-oriented approach focused on treatment strategies rather than simply comparing drugs against each other; second, a Bayesian framework was applied to synthesize direct and indirect evidence and to provide probabilistic treatment rankings (SUCRA), with robustness supported by sensitivity and cumulative analyses; third, safety outcomes were standardized as incidence rates per patient-year, facilitating cross-study integration across variable follow-up durations; and fourth, the inclusion of multinational studies across different designs improved external generalizability.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the evidence base for sJIA remains limited, and substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity existed across studies, including variability in the operational definition of “early intensification,” CID/remission criteria, and follow-up duration. Second, residual confounding is inevitable in observational and registry studies despite careful eligibility criteria and risk-of-bias assessment. Third, limitations specific to the network meta-analysis warrant emphasis: the network was largely star-shaped with conventional therapy as the primary connecting node, meaning that many comparisons between active strategies relied predominantly on indirect evidence. As a result, several relative effects were informed by a small number of studies, leading to imprecision and potentially unstable rankings. Moreover, because the network contained few or no closed loops, formal inconsistency assessment (e.g., node-splitting or loop-specific approaches) was not feasible; therefore, the validity of indirect comparisons depends heavily on the assumption of transitivity—namely, that included studies were sufficiently comparable with respect to key effect modifiers (e.g., disease duration, baseline severity, prior biologic exposure, concomitant glucocorticoids, and follow-up time). Although we explored heterogeneity via subgroup and meta-regression analyses and performed multiple sensitivity analyses, these checks cannot fully eliminate concerns about violations of transitivity or the limited certainty of rankings in sparsely informed nodes. Future head-to-head strategy trials and biomarker-stratified prospective studies are needed to strengthen the network geometry, enable closed-loop consistency evaluation, and improve the credibility of comparative estimates and treatment hierarchies.

Future research directions

(1) Strategic prospective RCT: Directly compare the speed of achieving target, drug savings and long-term drug discontinuation rate of “early IL-1/IL-6 intensification” and “gradual escalation”; (2) Biomarker-driven personalized treatment: Develop starting and step-down strategies based on IL-18, S100 protein, IFN characteristics, etc., and explore the IL-18/IFN-γ axis as the next-generation target for refractory or high-risk populations of MAS; (3) Positioning of JAK inhibitors: Conduct rigorous controlled studies in specific subgroups of refractory/comorbid patients to clarify their position in the strategic landscape and long-term safety; (4) Real-world studies: Build a treatment time-outcome database to quantify the critical time point of the “treatment window” and the cost-effectiveness of different pathways.

Conclusions

This systematic review and network meta-analysis, designed to systematically compare the relative efficacy and safety of early intensification and gradual escalation pathways and their specific drug classes, suggests that early-stage IL-1/IL-6 pathway inhibition is the preferred treatment strategy for sJIA. This approach aligns with the autoinflammatory-dominated pathobiology of the disease, enabling faster achievement of clinical remission targets and demonstrating an acceptable overall tolerability profile. Based on accurate disease classification and rigorous monitoring, this strategy should be considered as the preferred initial approach. Future strategic randomized controlled trials and biomarker-driven studies are needed to further clarify the optimal timing, sequencing, and patient selection criteria for treatment intensification.
