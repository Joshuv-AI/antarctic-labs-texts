Group competition mechanism in surgical robot training: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Huiqin Zhou, Liyuan Huang, Yuanzhuo Du, Jonathan Lio, Bin Luo, Xingyuan Xiao, Kun Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited training equipment and instructors at training centers cannot fulfill the simultaneous training required of numerous trainees. Optimizing the training organization can improve the training efficiency and trainee performance to enhance the overall training effectiveness. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness and feasibility of group competition mechanism under peer evaluation during robot training.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 60 robotic-naive medical students, divided into two groups: independent training and team-based learning with a competitive element. Both groups performed Pick-and-Place (PP), Match-Board-2 (MB), and Thread-the-Rings-1 (TR) tasks on a dV-Trainer robot simulator. Training scores were automatically recorded to construct learning curves and calculate scoring efficiencies, which were compared between the two groups.ResultsCompared with the conventional training group, the group utilizing the group competition training format demonstrated significantly higher average operational scores, scoring efficiency, and learning curves (P < 0.05). In addition, the group competition mode demonstrates a higher performance in cultivating the trainees’ satisfactory operational habits (P < 0.001).ConclusionsBased on task-reporting strategies guided by peer evaluation, the group competition mechanism is a novel and effective model for improving robot simulator training. This method holds promise as an effective tool for the surgical education of practicing surgeons and clinical medical students.Keywords: Robot training, Robot simulator, Group competition, Peer evaluation

Background

With the widespread application of surgical robotic technology [1], the characteristics of eye–hand–foot coordination in surgical robotic operations require clinicians to undergo systematic training [2]. Systematic training using surgical robots is gradually becoming available to both undergraduates and surgical residents [3].

Training centers play a crucial role in this process. The scale of a training center, the number of instructors, and number of trainees enrolled simultaneously directly affect the efficacy of training [4–6].

A shortage of qualified instructors and a large number of trainees are typical challenges faced by training centers. In previous research, numerous training centers have explored these challenges; however, such studies often required the purchase of additional equipment as a prerequisite [7].

In this study, a novel, innovative group competition mechanism is introduced, aiming to investigate the possibility of improving the training efficiency of a group of students using existing resources. This method is expected to significantly enhance the operational efficiency of training centers.

Methods

The simulator

The dV-Trainer simulator has been confirmed as an effective tool for robotic surgery training by multiple research institutions [8]– [9]. It features various scoring metrics and tasks to assess the training skills and task completion of an operator, rendering it an effective tool for beginner-level training. Two identical dV-Trainer simulators were used in this study.

Exercises

This study used the Pick-and-Place (PP; for warming up), Match-Board-2 (MB; for testing), and Thread-the-Rings-1 (TR; for testing) exercises. PP is an exercise of simple difficulty used to help trainees become familiar with the basic operations of the dV-Trainer. MB and TR are moderately difficult to perform and have appropriate learning curves. The wide-ranging transfer of MB and the needle driving operation of TR are both frequently used skills in clinical practice. Hence, these two tasks were selected for the experiment. MB is sensitive to the range of instrument movement, whereas TR is sensitive to excessive forces.

Organization

In this study, a total of 60 undergraduate students with an interest in surgery, consisting of 30 fourth- and 30 fifth-year students, were recruited as volunteers from the Second Clinical College of Zhongnan Hospital. None of the participants had received prior robotic simulation training before joining the study. These volunteers were randomly assigned to Groups A and B to undergo robotic instructional training. A senior trainer from the Department of Urology at Zhongnan Hospital participated in the training. At 40 years of age, this trainer has 12 years of experience in robotic surgery and 10 years of experience in robotic training, with over 2000 h of training on the robotic simulator. All participants signed informed consent forms to indicate their understanding of the basic content of the experiment; however, they were not informed of the specific objective of this study.

Group A underwent self-training. The 30 trainees in Group A were divided into three subgroups of 10 trainees, and each subgroup attended three training sessions. In the operation process, all trainees watched a standardized operation video to learn the key points of the surgical robot operation. This was followed by free discussion and training. Subsequently, each trainee performed ten MB and TR practices. The automatic scoring system of the simulator provided a score for each operation.

Group B underwent team-based learning in a competition mode. The 30 trainees in Group B were randomly divided into three subgroups of 10 trainees, and each subgroup attended three training sessions. Each subgroup was further randomly divided into two “red versus blue” teams of five members each. Both teams used robot simulators to engage in head-to-head competitions. The teams could only partially understand the opponent’s training progress with the displayed specific operational processes, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data collection

The operational scores of all students participating in the training were provided by the automatic scoring system of the simulator, including the overall score and seven sub-standards. The results were scientific and comprehensive. SPSS 21.0 was used to analyze the data and evaluate the training efficacy.

The purpose of the experiment was not disclosed to the trainer, who was solely responsible for the training and teaching tasks.

Results

Participant characteristics and baseline performance

None of the volunteers had previously undergone robot simulation training. Group A comprised 16 males and 14 females, with an age distribution of (21.53 ± 1.74) years. Group B comprised 18 males and 12 females, with an age distribution of (21.77 ± 1.55) years. There was no statistically significant difference in age between the two groups (P > 0.05). After warming up with three trials of PP, the initial baseline level was determined in the first MB trial. The score for Group A was (534.33 ± 61.45), and that for Group B was (574.08 ± 62.90), with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05).

MB learning curve

Group B scored significantly higher than Group A from the baseline test to the end of the study (P < 0.05). The logarithmic simulation of the scoring curve for each group follows: yA = 115.77ln(x) + 534.51 (R2 = 0.909); yB = 174.41ln(x) + 604.74 (R2 = 0.891). These results indicate that Group B had a higher scoring efficiency than Group A, as presented in Fig. 2.

MB learning curves; P < 0.05 via one-way ANOVA. TR learning curves; P < 0.001 via one-way ANOVA

TR learning curve

At the end of the study, the scores of Group B were significantly higher than those of Group A (P < 0.001). The scoring curve for each group was simulated using a logarithmic function as follows: yA = 64.775ln(x) + 272.2 (R2 = 0.962); yB = 75.272ln(x) + 364.48 (R2 = 0.945). However, the scoring efficiency of Group B was only slightly higher than that of Group A, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

A systematic clustering analysis was performed on the seven substandard outputs by the simulator. In this analysis, the performance metrics were divided into a group of work habit parameters reflected by the distance of arm movement, a group of work safety parameters reflected by the duration of instrument force, the time the instrument was out of view, and a group of work efficiency parameters that were collectively reflected by four other items.

Workspace range proficiency

The workspace range value of the dV-Trainer simulator reflects the proficiency of the operator in using the clutch pedal. Previous studies have shown that proficiency in using a clutch pedal largely reflects the overall operating level of the operator [10]. This parameter may be related to the future formal learning of surgical robots by a trainee. To cultivate satisfactory operational habits in trainees, the trainer highlighted the importance of this parameter in the experiment, although it accounted for a relatively small proportion of the scoring by the simulator.

The workspace range values for Groups A and B were 9.06 ± 2.06 and 8.74 ± 1.96 cm, respectively, with no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05).

A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was performed to compare the arm movement distances between the two groups. There was a statistically significant difference between the groups (P < 0.05), with Group B performing significantly better than Group A, as presented in Fig. 3.

MB master workspace range value; P < 0.05 via one-way ANOVA. TR master workspace range value; P < 0.05 via one-way ANOVA

Safety parameters

The safety parameters group includes the duration of instrument force and time the instrument is out of view. The Pearson’s test exhibited a significant weak correlation (Pearson’s coefficient = 0.287, P < 0.05). During the operation of surgical robots, both the excessive force on the robotic arm and its movement out of view are statistical parameters measured in time that can lead to accidents during surgery. Therefore, the total duration was defined as the “total duration of unsafe equipment” and used to conduct further analysis.

The total durations of unsafe equipment for Groups A and B were 3.53 ± 5.01 and 3.71 ± 7.78 s, respectively, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (P > 0.05).

A one-way ANOVA test was performed to compare the total duration of the use of unsafe equipment between the two groups, and there was a statistically significant difference between the groups (P < 0.05). Group B performed significantly better than Group A (Figs. 4 and 5).

Proficiency parameters

The proficiency parameters group includes the practice completion time, number of instrument collisions, distance of instrument movement, and number of support target detachments, which demonstrated significant strong correlations (Pearson’s coefficient = 0.826, P < 0.01). The practice completion time comprehensively reflects the accuracy, stability in gripping and moving, and smoothness of action transitions of the trainee. Hence, it is a key indicator of training proficiency. Therefore, the completion time was used as the main variable to assess the practice proficiency of each trainee.

The practice completion times for Groups A and B were 284.95 ± 70.74 and 254.38 ± 53.27 s, respectively, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (P > 0.05). At the end of the study, the time required for Group B to complete the task was significantly less than that of Group A (P < 0.05), as illustrated in Fig. 6.

Discussion

An analysis of the results of the two different training methods revealed that under the grouping and competitive mechanisms, the operational performance and habits of the participants in Group B were superior to those of the participants in Group A. The statistically significant differences between the two groups indicated that this organizational form offered unique advantages and effectiveness.

A video playback system was used for both teaching methods, which reduced trainer involvement. The training methods for the participants in both groups were different forms of self-directed learning. In the training mode for Group A, participants could only learn through standardized videos. Hence, they independently explored operational techniques and corrected erroneous movements. Mutual promotion among peers during the learning process was weak, particularly because of the lack of motivation to correct the operations of others. Grouped competitive training helped students participate in the competition to form a learning competitive relationship between groups, thereby stimulating learning motivation [11]. Each change in participants during the operation reconstructed the knowledge of robot operation. Hence, self-reflection and peer guidance could be realized, thereby highlighting the superiority of the competitive mechanism.

Research has demonstrated that grouped competitive training has been used in clinical training [11] to effectively improve the surgical skills of residents [12]. Moreover, multiple studies have revealed that operational skills acquired in a simulated environment can be transferred to the surgical environment [13–15]. Thus, the novel adversarial organizational form of this experiment can be used clinically by surgeons to potentially reduce adverse events during surgical operations and indirectly reduce the cost of robot simulation training.

In this study, several design methods were used to increase the competition intensity and enhance participation. The rules for winning were announced after grouping, and the two groups were trained together. Therefore, in addition to peer learning, it was necessary to understand the operational capabilities of the other group to determine a competitive strategy. Therefore, during training, students were required to not only continuously summarize their experiences but also share operational experiences with each other and learn from their opponents.

A five-versus-five competitive mechanism was used in combination with timely adjustments to the rules for participation to fill the competition results with suspense. Victory depended on the overall strength and strategy of the team, and the understanding of their members and competitors was tested. Among the three pairs of player-versus-player matches, two pairs had a score of 3-to-2 and one pair had a score of 3-to-1. Under the rule of being able to participate at any time, each student had a high level of participation in a winning game.

The immediate scoring system and head-to-head player-versus-player match significantly increased the external pressure, thereby rendering the postoperative debriefing strategy discussion important. A study by Pradarelli et al. [12] demonstrated that the collaborative analysis, coaching gap, constructive feedback, and peer learning support were significant for surgical training. Peer view also has referential value for major decisions in the medical industry [12, 16]. Based on the final scores of Groups A and B, frequent intragroup task reports can improve the scoring efficiency and validate the reported improvement in team performance during team-based task-reporting strategies [17].

It was observed that the participants in Group B adopted different strategies for different levels of difficulty in competitive exercises to achieve more satisfactory results. The MB practice was relatively simple, and most participants quickly achieved the passing score set by the simulator during training, with the training strategy focusing on simultaneous improvement. The TR training was more difficult, with only three participants reaching the passing score by the end of the training session. Their training strategies were adjusted according to the effect of substandard outputs on the total score such that the plateau period of the substandard outputs for the participants in Group B appeared at different times. This training strategy reflected the initiative of the participants during the training and helped them establish reasonable operational habits in the early stages of the surgical robot training, rather than simply pursuing improvements to speed.

In summary, this study introduced an intragroup competitive mechanism in robot simulation training to improve training performance and build a competitive relationship to achieve mutual progress between individuals and teams. During the team task feedback process, participants completed their role transformations and guided each other, which aligned with the peer review mechanism. Furthermore, the organizational form of this study was similar to that of a flipped classroom, where students engage with lecture content outside of class and use classroom time for discussion and application, which is conducive to cultivating critical thinking and aligns with the philosophy of higher education [18]– [19]. Therefore, in large-scale training conditions or training institutions with lower training levels where many novice students undertake robotic training, the grouping and competitive mechanism are recommended to address these two major challenges.

These findings suggest that integrating a group competition mechanism into robotic surgical simulation, guided by peer evaluation, may significantly enhance training outcomes. This approach promotes adaptive learning, improves operational efficiency and safety, and does not rely on additional equipment or intensive instructor supervision—making it particularly suitable for training settings with limited resources. Future research should evaluate the long-term retention of these improvements and explore implementation among clinical residents in real-world surgical environments.

Limitations of the method

First, the age structure and attributes of the sample were relatively limited, as only fourth- and fifth-year undergraduate students were selected from a clinical college. The desire to win at this age yielded effective training, even with an unattractive reward mechanism. However, to extend this study, clinical residents, clinical experts, and professors would be recruited for clinical surgical training to avoid the limitations of fixed training programs provided by robot simulators.

Second, the timespan of the experiments was not sufficient. The differences in the final learning outcomes between the two groups of participants should be studied further. The difference in scoring efficiency between the two groups on the TR scoring curve was not statistically significant. The curves for both groups revealed that after the MB training, the initial level of TR in Group B was significantly better than that in Group A, and it had already surpassed the plateau score of Group A after the first training. Therefore, the participants in Group B might not have reached the second plateau period by the end of the training owing to the higher difficulty of the TR operations. The difference between the two plateaus limited the scoring efficiency improvement for Group B participants. These results can guide the development of courses for various training programs.

Finally, targeted simulation training subjects were meticulously selected. However, it is evident that while they are comprehensive, training programs on simulators cannot cover all the skill requirements required for robotic surgery. Consequently, they cannot completely replace actual machine practices. Currently, comparative data is limited on real-world surgical robots, which will be a key direction for future research.

Conclusions

The randomized controlled trial demonstrated that grouping and competitive mechanisms guided by peer feedback through task-reporting strategies were innovative and effective methods for improving robot simulator training. Careful grouping and competition may become useful tools in robot training centers to alleviate the problems of insufficient training equipment, a lack of trainers, and robot teaching for large-scale cohorts of trainees.
