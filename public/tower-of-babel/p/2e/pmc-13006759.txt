A new laparoscopic technique for pediatric epigastric hernia repair: a feasibility case series
Authors: Eunyoung Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis report is a novel laparoscopic technique for pediatric epigastric hernia repair using 3-mm instruments and intracorporeal barbed sutures. A series of three children aged 3 to 6 years presented with a visible or palpable midline bulge, with or without intermittent pain. All had solitary epigastric defects confirmed by ultrasound. Laparoscopic repair was performed via three left lateral abdominal 3-mm ports, avoiding visible central incisions. Preperitoneal fat was reduced or excised, and the fascial defect was closed with a running barbed suture without knots. All patients were discharged the same day without complications. Follow-up up to 2 years revealed no recurrences and excellent cosmetic outcomes, with nearly invisible scars and high parental satisfaction. This technique offers a safe, effective, and cosmetically superior alternative to traditional open repair, especially in small children with central abdominal wall defects.Keywords: Ventral hernia, Laparoscopy, Pediatrics, Absorbable sutures, Minimally invasive surgical procedures

INTRODUCTION

Epigastric hernias are midline abdominal wall defects occurring between the xiphoid process and the umbilicus, representing approximately 4% to 6% of pediatric hernia repairs [1,2]. They typically present in preschool-aged children as a small palpable lump, sometimes associated with pain or tenderness [3]. Unlike umbilical hernias, these defects do not close spontaneously and may enlarge over time, prompting consideration for surgical repair [2]. While serious complications are rare, the decision between early surgical repair and observation remains debated, particularly in asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients. Elective repair may be favored to prevent enlargement, avoid potential incarceration of preperitoneal fat, and achieve optimal cosmetic outcomes [2,4].

Recent reports have described laparoscopic approaches aiming to improve cosmesis and allow inspection for multiple defects [1,5,6]. The present study introduces a novel laparoscopic technique using three 3-mm ports and intracorporeal barbed sutures, designed to optimize cosmetic results while maintaining surgical efficacy. This study aimed to describe this technique in detail and assess its feasibility, safety, and early outcomes in a series of pediatric patients.

CASE REPORT

Study design and patients

This study was conducted a retrospective case series of pediatric patients with epigastric hernias who underwent laparoscopic repair using a new technique. The cases were drawn from a single tertiary pediatric surgery center between 2022 and 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≤6 years; (2) a clinically diagnosed epigastric hernia (defect in the linea alba between the xiphoid and umbilicus) confirmed by ultrasound; and (3) elective laparoscopic hernia repair performed by the single surgeon using a uniform technique. Three patients met these criteria during the study period, two boys and one girl, ages ranging from 3 to 6 years. This case series was conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. The Institutional Review Board waived ethical approval due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study.

Preoperative assessment

All patients were evaluated in the outpatient clinic with a detailed history and physical examination. The primary symptom in each case was a visible or palpable midline bulge in the upper abdomen. Two patients reported intermittent pain or discomfort at the hernia site, especially during crying or activity, while one patient was asymptomatic aside from the parents noticing the lump. The duration of symptoms or lump visibility ranged from 1 to 5 years. Physical exams in all cases revealed a small, firm mass presumed to be preperitoneal fat in the upper midline. The fascial defect was difficult to definitively measure on palpation due to its small size, so an ultrasonographic examination was obtained for each patient. Ultrasound confirmed the presence of an epigastric hernia in all cases, showing a defect in the linea alba with herniation of fat (Fig. 1). Ultrasound measurements of defect size ranged from 2 to 5 mm, consistent with the intraoperative findings. No concomitant umbilical hernias were present. Patients had no significant comorbidities and were otherwise healthy. They were scheduled for elective hernia repair, given the risks of enlargement or incarceration of the fat and the parents’ desire for definitive treatment.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. A high-resolution 3-mm laparoscope was used for visualization. To minimize visible scarring on the anterior abdominal wall, peritoneal access and insufflation were achieved using a Veress needle at the base of the umbilicus. After achieving pneumoperitoneum, a total of three working ports were inserted along the left anterior axillary line. Two 3-mm trocars were placed in the left upper and lower quadrants, and one 3-mm port was inserted at the level of the umbilicus (Fig. 2). A 3-mm 30° laparoscope was inserted through the middle port, and a 3-mm needle driver or grasper was used through the upper and lower ports. The laparoscopic examination confirmed the location of the fascial defect in each patient, visible as a small opening in the linea alba with preperitoneal fat protruding. No additional defects were found elsewhere along the linea alba. The hernia sac (preperitoneal fat) was gently grasped with a forceps and pulled inward (Fig. 3A). In two cases, the fat was easily reduced back into the peritoneal cavity. In the third case, the fat was fibrous and adherent to the defect; it was cauterized and divided flush with the abdominal wall, allowing it to retract. This exposed the circular fascial defect (Fig. 3B). The peritoneum underlying the defect was not formally opened; instead, the fascial gap was closed directly.

For intracorporeal closure, a 3-0 V-Loc barbed suture (polyglactin, absorbable monofilament with barbs; Medtronic) was introduced into the abdomen through the skin. The barbed suture greatly facilitated laparoscopic suturing by maintaining tension without the need for knot tying. A running suture was performed beginning at one edge of the defect to approximate the fascial edges in a transverse orientation. Typically, 2 to 3 bites on each side of the defect were sufficient to close the small opening securely. The barbed suture was then cut flush, and due to its self-anchoring barbs, no knots were required to maintain closure (Fig. 3C). The 3-mm port sites were removed and not sutured, but only skin adhesive strips were applied. The umbilical Veress puncture also required no suture. The patients tolerated oral intake and ambulated within a few hours. All three patients were discharged home on the day of surgery. Follow-up visits were scheduled at 2 weeks and 3 months postoperatively, with telephone or clinic follow-up extending up to 1 to 2 years for long-term surveillance of recurrence.

Data collection

Data were collected from medical records on patient age, sex, hernia characteristics including symptom duration, number of hernia defects, defect size on ultrasound, and operative defect including operative time, any intraoperative events, and postoperative outcomes including complications, length of hospital stay, recurrence, cosmetic results. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the findings, given the small sample size.

Case series

Three patients (two males and one female) were included in this case series, with ages ranging from 3 to 6 years (mean, 4.3 years). All had a single midline epigastric hernia defect located between the umbilicus and xiphoid. Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics of each patient, including symptom duration and hernia size. Symptom duration prior to surgery varied from 1 to 5 years. The fascial defect size measured on ultrasound was 2 to 5 mm in diameter. In all cases, only preperitoneal fat (and no bowel) was noted within the hernia sac on imaging and at surgery. All three hernia repairs were completed successfully with the described laparoscopic technique. There were no intraoperative complications. Reduction of the hernia contents and closure of the fascial defect were achieved in all cases. The operative time from skin incision to closure was 25, 25, and 35 minutes for patients 1, 2, and 3, respectively. No intraoperative difficulties with the barbed suture were encountered; the V-Loc held the tissue securely, and no knot tying was needed, streamlining the closure. There was no need for conversion to an open incision in any case. All patients had an unremarkable recovery. They were discharged home on the same day of surgery after a short observation period (approximately 4–6 hours). No postoperative complications were observed. In particular, there were no instances of wound infection, hematoma, or seroma at the port sites or hernia site. The patients’ pain was minimal without the need for any analgesics. The small lateral port incisions healed well with excellent cosmetic results. At the 2-week follow-up, all wounds were well healed and the parents expressed high satisfaction with the appearance (the 3-mm scars were barely perceptible and the umbilicus was untouched, giving a “scarless” look in the midline). Patients were followed for recurrence with clinical examinations at approximately 3 months, and then via annual follow-up calls or visits. At a median follow-up of 12 months, none of the children had any recurrence of the epigastric hernia. The abdominal wall contour remained normal and flat with no new bulge. There were also no late-onset complications reported.

DISCUSSION

This case series demonstrates the feasibility of a new minimally invasive technique for repairing epigastric hernias in young children. All three patients underwent successful laparoscopic herniorrhaphy with excellent outcomes—no complications, no recurrences, and superb cosmetic results—suggesting that this approach is a safe and effective alternative to the conventional open repair for small midline fascial defects. Although the sample size is limited, the results align with the favorable outcomes reported in earlier studies of laparoscopic pediatric ventral hernia repairs [1,6]. One of the main advantages of the described technique is improved cosmesis. Laparoscopic principles established in pediatric inguinal hernia repair may be effectively adapted to midline abdominal wall defects such as epigastric hernia [7]. Even though open epigastric hernia repairs in children require only a small incision, the resulting scar on the upper abdominal midline can be noticeable and cosmetically suboptimal [5]. In contrast, the laparoscopic approach avoids any incision in the visible central abdomen. Only three 3-mm lateral abdominal ports were utilized, which leave tiny punctate scars that are hard to discern. The umbilicus itself was not incised (apart from a needle puncture), preserving its natural appearance. All parents in the series were pleased with the postoperative cosmetic outcome, with the operative sites described as “nearly invisible.” This mirrors the findings of other minimally invasive approaches: for example, Moreira-Pinto et al. reported that their percutaneous laparoscopic repair left no visible scar in any patient [5], and Babsail et al. described the result of their single incision pediatric endosurgery repair as “virtually scarless” [1]. Cosmetic benefit is an important consideration in pediatric surgery, and the laparoscopic technique clearly excels in this regard by minimizing external scarring.

Another benefit of the laparoscopic approach is the ability to thoroughly inspect the abdominal wall for additional defects. In children, it is not uncommon to find multiple small epigastric hernia defects (“Swiss cheese” defects) that might be missed on external exam. Laparoscopy allows the surgeon to visualize the entire linea alba from underneath. In the series, no additional defects were found beyond the primary hernias, but this diagnostic advantage can be valuable, as noted in other studies [8]. Additionally, if an epigastric hernia is found in conjunction with another condition requiring laparoscopy, both issues can be addressed in one session. A recent retrospective review by König et al. [3] observed that surgeons tended to choose laparoscopy for epigastric hernia repair more often when another concurrent procedure was being performed, highlighting the convenience of a combined minimally invasive approach.

The use of a barbed absorbable suture (V-Loc) for intracorporeal defect closure is another technical point worth highlighting. Barbed sutures have gained popularity in laparoscopic surgery because they eliminate the need for knot tying while providing continuous tension distribution. This is particularly useful in a confined space when closing a small fascial hole. In this series, the V-Loc 3-0 suture allowed rapid and secure closure of the defect with a running stitch, and no suture-related complications were encountered. Other authors have also employed barbed sutures in laparoscopic ventral hernia repairs with success [8,9]. The choice of technique should consider the availability of resources, institutional expertise, and surgeon familiarity with advanced laparoscopic suturing.

However, a potential rare complication is tissue entanglement caused by long barbed suture tails. To minimize this risk, the suture tail should be trimmed flush with the tissue and the barbed segment buried [10].

The operative times in the series were comparable to or slightly shorter than those reported for other laparoscopic techniques and even open surgery [1,5,6]. The operative times include the learning curve of adopting a new technique; with increased experience and possibly using all 3-mm instrumentation, operative efficiency could further improve. Importantly, all cases were done as day surgeries. Laparoscopic repair did not necessitate any overnight hospitalization, which is similar to open repair for this minor procedure. Prior studies have indicated that minimally invasive hernia repairs can yield shorter hospital stays and quicker recoveries due to reduced pain and smaller wounds [1,4–6]. In the children, postoperative pain was minimal, and normal activity was rapidly resumed, underscoring the child-friendly nature of this approach.

There are some limitations to consider. First, the sample size is very small (n = 3), reflecting the rarity of epigastric hernias presenting for surgery in a short time frame. This limits the generalizability of the outcomes. The results, while positive, should be interpreted cautiously until a larger series or comparative study is available. Second, the follow-up, though up to 2 years in one case, is still relatively short for assessing long-term recurrence. However, most pediatric hernia recurrences, if they occur, tend to manifest early due to technical failure. The absence of any early recurrence in the series is reassuring. Third, the laparoscopic cases were not directly compared to a cohort of open repairs. Thus, while it can be qualitatively stated that the cosmesis is superior and outcomes are excellent, a prospective comparison would be ideal to quantify differences in pain scores, recovery time, and long-term outcomes between open and laparoscopic methods. Despite these limitations, the findings contribute to the growing evidence that laparoscopic epigastric hernia repair in children is feasible and safe.

In conclusion, the findings indicate that this new laparoscopic repair of pediatric epigastric hernias is safe, effective, and cosmetically superior, with potential applicability in centers equipped with pediatric laparoscopic instruments. Further larger-scale studies are warranted.
