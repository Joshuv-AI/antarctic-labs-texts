Comparative analysis of early post-transplant infections after DCD with hypothermic oxygenated liver perfusion and DBD liver transplantation: A retrospective cohort study
Authors: Stephanie Klinzing, Olivia J. Kotoun, Linard D. Hoessly, Andreas E. Kremer, Nicolas J. Mueller, Philipp Dutkowski, Rea Andermatt, Peter W. Schreiber, Daniel A. Hofmaenner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liver transplantation outcomes have improved. However, immunosuppression also raises infection susceptibility. Potential differences in infections between donation after circulatory death (DCD) and donation after brain death (DBD) recipients remain unclear. This study analyzed postoperative infections and compared their incidence between DCD and DBD recipients. Patients undergoing liver transplantation from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022, were included. DCD and marginal DBD liver grafts underwent hypothermic oxygenated machine perfusion (HOPE). Bacterial, viral, and fungal infections were identified using standardized definitions. Infections were categorized into periods: 0–2 weeks (period 1), 3–4 weeks (period 2), 1–6 months (period 3), and beyond 6 months (period 4). The primary outcome was the occurrence of infection within the first post-transplant year, including pathogen spectrum, timing, and infection site. The secondary outcome was the assessment of infection risk factors investigated with Poisson regression. A total of 300 liver transplants were included. In all, 42.8% of DBD recipients and 44.3% of DCD recipients suffered from at least 1 infection. In total, 298 relevant infections occurred, with bacteria accounting for 73.2% in DBD and 86.4% in DCD recipients. Bacterial infections predominated in all periods. In periods 1 and 2, bloodstream infections, abdominal and surgical site infections were most frequent, while liver-related infections, especially in DCD, and bloodstream infections dominated in periods 3 and 4. Viral and fungal infections were less frequent. One-year survival was 87.1% for DBD and 87.7% for DCD recipients. Poisson regression identified DCD recipients (IRR 1.99, 95% CI 1.37–2.92, p<0.001) and younger age (IRR 0.98, 95% CI 0.97–0.99, p<0.001) as more likely to develop infections. DCD liver recipients experienced more infections in the first post-transplant year. While mortality was not different, the increased infection rates in DCD recipients highlight the need for infection prevention and surveillance.

INTRODUCTION

Patient survival after Liver transplant (LT) has steadily improved during the last decades, partly due to improvements in immunosuppression.1 However, higher levels of immunosuppression are associated with an increased susceptibility to infections, which influence mortality and morbidity and remain a major challenge in the post-transplant period. Given the limited availability of donor organs, efforts to increase the donor pool have intensified, with a growing emphasis on promoting donation after circulatory death (DCD) and improving its safety profile with a focus on patient and graft selection, ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), surgical site infections (SSIs), and preoperative organ machine perfusion.2–4 Compared with donation after brain death (DBD), DCD is associated with an increased risk of ischemic injury and other post-transplant complications, primarily due to the warm ischemia time during which organs are deprived of oxygen supply.5–7 In contrast to earlier studies, Chen et al.8 have not reported significant differences between DCD and DBD recipients in long-term outcomes. Recently, favorable long-term survival in a large cohort after transplantation of hypothermic oxygenated machine perfusion (HOPE) treated livers has been reported.9

Early postoperative infections are among the most critical complications after transplantation, affecting over 75% of liver recipients within the first year and significantly influencing mortality and morbidity.10,11 SSIs have been independently associated with long-term outcomes.12

The number of infections within the first 6 months post-transplant has been identified as a key determinant of long-term survival,13 which underlines the importance of further optimizing the postoperative management of transplant patients and understanding potential differences between DCD and DBD recipients regarding infections. Bacterial, viral, and fungal infections as post-transplant complications have been investigated in some studies.14,15 Bertuzzo et al.14 report infections in over 30% of liver transplant recipients within the first month after transplantation. However, uncertainties persist regarding differences in infection rates and the pathogen spectrum between DBD and DCD recipients.14,15 Moreover, the impact of warm ischemic conditions and procedural variations in DCD liver transplantation on postoperative infections remains an open question.10

Thus, the aim of this study was to analyze postoperative bacterial, viral, and fungal infections in liver transplant recipients, and to compare the incidence rate and timeline of infections between DCD and DBD liver organ recipients within the first year after LT.

METHODS

Study design

This was a retrospective observational cohort study including adult patients (≥18 y) undergoing DCD or DBD liver transplantation from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022, at the University Hospital of Zurich. The hospital is a tertiary academic teaching hospital with a long-standing experience in transplant medicine. Patients undergoing combined liver-kidney transplantation were excluded from this study. Patients undergoing re-transplantation during the study period were included, and each post-transplantation period was analyzed separately. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Canton of Zurich (BASEC-Nr. 2024-01565). All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. All patients gave written informed consent for data analysis.

Operative management

DBD livers were treated according to our standard retrieval protocol. DCD livers were retrieved by the super rapid retrieval technique, followed by cold storage with Institute-George-Lopez-1 solution, as previously described. DCD and marginal DBD liver grafts underwent routine HOPE treatment during recipient hepatectomy according to our local practice as previously described.16 Marginal DBD grafts were defined as described previously.17

Organ implantation was performed according to the routine approach using classic cava-replacement technique without veno-venous bypass. Reperfusion was initiated through the portal vein with subsequent arterial reperfusion.

Immunosuppression and prophylaxis

Immunosuppression was performed in accordance with the in-house protocol and consisted of methylprednisolone switched to prednisolone after 6 postoperative days, which was tapered over 90 days (exception: autoimmune hepatitis with prednisolone 15 mg lifelong). Tacrolimus was started between postoperative days 1 and 5. DBD recipients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <40 mL/min, as well as DCD recipients, received an induction with basiliximab 20 mg i.v., which was repeated on postoperative day 4.

All patients received perioperative antibacterial prophylaxis for 24 hours, usually with cefuroxime unless otherwise indicated. Patient undergoing re-transplantation, under steroid therapy, with biliary or vascular complications and/or in the ICU >5 days on dialysis or mechanically ventilated, received antifungal treatment with fluconazole 100 mg/day for 2 weeks post-transplant. All patients received amphotericin B suspension buccal and were started on sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim prophylaxis on day 7 after transplantation. CMV Prophylaxis with valganciclovir was started on day 7 after transplantation in high-risk patients (D+/R−) for 3 months post-transplant, while the pre-emptive approach with weekly measurement of CMV load by quantitative CMV PCR assay was used in intermediate (D+/R+), (D−R+), and low-risk (D−/R−) patients.

Postoperatively, all patients were admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) until stabilization allowed discharge to the intermediate care unit (IMC) or normal ward.

Data collection

Data not related to infections were collected from the in-hospital medical record systems (KISIM, Cistec AG, Switzerland and PDMS Metavision, iMD, Germany) and included demographic information, pre-transplant medical conditions, peri-transplant characteristics, laboratory values, ICU treatment, and patient outcomes in terms of graft loss and survival within the first post-transplant year. Data related to bacterial, viral, and fungal infections were obtained from the Swiss Transplant Cohort Study (STCS) database (http://www.stcs.ch), a national database for data collection of organ transplant recipients.

Definition of infection and time periods

Clinically relevant infections were identified by transplant-infectious disease physicians using electronic hospital records in intervals ranging from twice weekly to every third month, according to standardized definitions. These definitions have been developed by the STCS Infectious Diseases Working Group and have been described in detail elsewhere.11 Shortly, proven bacterial infections require isolation of a bacterial pathogen, clinical signs/symptoms, and a specific antibiotic treatment. Probable bacterial infections require clinical signs/symptoms and a specific antibiotic treatment, but no pathogen isolation.

Proven viral infections are defined by detection of viral replication [herpes simplex virus (HSV) and varicella zoster virus (VZV)] in skin vesicle; coronavirus, influenza, human metapneumovirus, parainfluenza, rhinovirus, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in respiratory secretion; norovirus and rotavirus in stool, or detection of virus in biopsy tissue [eg, cytomegalovirus (CMV), BK polyomavirus (BKPyV)], together with clinical signs/symptoms. Probable viral infections require viral replication and symptoms/signs of organ dysfunction. Viral syndromes require viral replication [eg, CMV, Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), human herpes viruses (HHV)-6, HHV-8], and non-organ-specific clinical signs/symptoms. Proven fungal infections require histopathology of a tissue biopsy showing invading fungal hyphae or yeasts. Probable fungal infections require host, clinical, and microbiological criteria.

For this study, post-transplant infections, irrespective of anatomical location, were subject to analysis if they were considered clinically relevant. We considered proven and probable bacterial infections, proven and probable viral infections, and viral syndromes, as well as proven and probable fungal infections, as clinically relevant.

According to Fishman and Rubin,18,19 since 1998 3 time periods of infections after solid organ transplantation are distinguished: (1) nosocomial infections until 1 month after transplantation; (2) opportunistic infections from 1 to 6 months; and (3) community-acquired or persistent infections after 6 months. We chose to modify this model for a more detailed description of the infections occurring early during the first month after transplantation. Accordingly, infections during the first months are classified as period 1 (infections within the first 2 weeks after transplantation) and period 2 (infections within weeks 3 and 4 after transplantation), while period 3 (infections from month 1–6) and period 4 (infections after 6 mo) were not modified.

All infections were counted as discrete episodes and analyzed for all 4 periods. Counting methods included overall infection count, infection site, number of infections per transplanted patient, pathogen spectrum, and first infection (considering the first infection only).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of clinically relevant bacterial, viral, and fungal infections in DBD and DCD liver organ recipients within the first post-transplant year, including pathogen spectrum, timing, and infection site. The secondary outcome was the assessment of risk factors for infections investigated by means of a Poisson regression model.

Statistical analysis

Baseline criteria and infection data were reported descriptively. Infection variables included incidence rate, time between LT and infection, infection site, and pathogens. We investigated risk factors for infections with Poisson regression. Variables in the multivariable regression were chosen based on a combination of biological and clinical plausibility, and included basic epidemiological variables, recipient age and sex, as well as donor characteristics (DBD vs. DCD), location before transplantation, MELD, comorbidities, units of intraoperative red blood cells, basiliximab induction, and HOPE treatment.

Transplant outcomes, encompassing death and graft loss, were extracted from the STCS dataset and presented for a 1-year follow-up. Cumulative incidences for infection with death and graft loss as competing risks were calculated. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. R version 4.4.3 was used for statistical analysis and visualization.

During the first post-transplant year, 298 clinically relevant infections were diagnosed, subsequently termed infections. Of those, 187 infections were registered in DBD recipients (n=194) and 111 infections in DCD recipients (n=106). Bacteria accounted for 73.2% of infections in DBD and 86.4% in DCD recipients. Virus was registered in 17.6% of DBD and 9.9% of DCD recipients. Fungi were responsible for 9.1% of infections in DBD and 3.6% in DCD recipients.

In all, 42.8% of DBD recipients and 44.3% of DCD recipients suffered at least 1 infection within the first year after LT (Table 3). Enterococcus species (Enterococcus faecalis, Enterococcus faecium) and Enterobacteriaceae (Klebsiella species, Escherichia coli, Enterobacter species) were the most common bacteria cultivated. Enterococcus spp. was cultivated in 29.1% of infections in DBD and 31.6% of infections in DCD recipients, while Enterobacteriaceae were cultivated in 26.8% of DBD and 27.8% of DCD infections. Concerning viral infections, herpes viruses (HSV, VZV, HHV-6, EBV) were detected in 7.3% of DBD infections and 5.1% of DCD infections. Candida species was cultivated in 7.8% of infections in DBD and 2.5% of infections in DCD recipients (Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/LVT/B107).

Restricting the analysis to the first infection only, a total count of 150 first infections was registered. In all, 43.8% of these first infections occurred in period 1, 14.6% during period 2, 33.8% during months 2–5, and 8% during months 6–12. While the first infection in DBD recipients occurred most frequently in periods 1 and 2 after a median of 15 days (IQR 4.5–45), in DCD, the first infections occurred after a median of 37 days (IQR 5.5–91.5). Overall, bacteria were the most common cause of the first infection in all periods. While some fungus and virus infections occurred as first infection in DBD in periods 1 and 2, all first infections in DCD were bacterial except for 1 in this time period (Supplemental Tables S3, http://links.lww.com/LVT/B109 and S4, http://links.lww.com/LVT/B110).

DISCUSSION

Infections remain a frequent and life-threatening complication after transplant surgery, significantly affecting patient morbidity and mortality, thus mandating high suspicion and initiation of appropriate prophylactic or therapeutic antimicrobial approaches.20 In the changing landscapes in liver transplantation with increased usage of marginal donor organs, including DCD, to counteract the worldwide organ shortage21 new potent immunosuppressive strategies to avert rejection, machine perfusion to optimize viability of marginal organs, understanding the potential impacts of graft origin on subsequent infection patterns and timeline might thus be valuable for optimizing post-transplant management.

We report a high incidence of infection in DBD (42.8%) and DCD recipients (44.3%), with a higher risk for infections in DCD recipients within the first year after transplantation. Most infections were of bacterial origin. Enterococcus species and Enterobacteriaceae were responsible for the majority of infections in both DBD and DCD recipients throughout the first year after transplantation, together accounting for around 60% of infections. Infection rates within the first 2–4 postoperative weeks were high, especially accentuated in DBD, but remained a frequent problem during the follow-up period, with an increased rate in DCD after month 2. While early bacterial infections frequently were nosocomial, associated with the surgical procedure and early postoperative ICU care, there was a shift to liver-related infections like cholangitis, frequently associated with bacteremia, within the next few months after transplantation.

Similar infection rates and accentuation during the first post-transplant month were reported,13,22 though unlike in our cohort, impacting patient survival. Lower Infection rates of 20% after LT have been reported by Ayvazoglu Soy et al,10 including living donation in 72% of cases as a potential explanation, in addition to other methodological reasons.

A main concern and focus in DCD is the risk of ischemic cholangiopathy ascribed to warm ischemia time,23 while differences in infection patterns in DCD and DBD recipients are not extensively addressed in the literature. The higher risk of infections in DCD in our cohort and the increasing incidence of graft-related infections some months after transplantation are very likely attributable to the concern of ischemic graft complications, thus underlining the importance of further measurements to improve graft viability and, thus, hopefully reduce infections.

Enterococcus and Enterobacteriaceae accounted for most bacterial infections in both DBD and DCD recipients. This aligns well with previous research, except that the described predominance of gram-positive bacteria in the early postoperative phase is assigned to selective digestive decontamination.13,22,24

Basiliximab induction is routinely integrated in our DCD immunosuppression protocol and selected DBD recipients with impaired renal function, thus potentially associated with a higher incidence of infections due to a higher degree of immunosuppression. In accordance with Hashim et al.25 we did not notice an association of basiliximab and risk of infection in our cohort.

While DBD recipients appeared sicker at baseline as reflected in higher MELD scores, DCD recipients had more severe pre-transplant conditions as estimated by the Charlson Comorbidity Index, with a higher risk for infection in the DCD group. This finding reflects the complex interplays between donor organ quality, host immune status, as well as surgical and local institutional practices, emphasizing that recipient pre-transplant clinical status alone appears not to be a reliable predictor of early post-transplant infections. Nevertheless, the higher incidence of pre-transplant antibiotics and early postoperative infections in DBD recipients may indicate that these patients were sicker at baseline and more frequently required ICU treatment before transplantation. Recently, multiple further factors affecting infection risk have been described including female gender, age, operation duration and intraoperative blood loss.24 In contrast, in our cohort, we did not notice an independent association of gender and the amount of intraoperative red blood cell transfusions with the postoperative infection rate.

In daily clinical practice, patients in the early phase after liver transplantation presenting with signs of SIRS are challenging. Is the cause infection, potentially evolving into full-blown sepsis, thus demanding urgent diagnostic and prompt, calculated antimicrobial therapy and potential source control? Or is SIRS ascribed to non-infectious causes, including surgical trauma, ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), and intestinal injury with leakage of endotoxins, where optimized supportive therapy is mandatory and broad antimicrobial therapy is not indicated or may even be potentially harmful? As results of microbiological sampling are available with a significant time delay, immediately available parameters to guide clinical decision-making are warranted.26 Particularly, PCT has been shown to be only a moderately accurate diagnostic marker for postoperative infections in LT.27 Furthermore, clinicians should be aware of the fact that profoundly elevated PCT may occur in the early post-transplant phase in early allograft dysfunction,28 potentially mimicking true infection. To date, close monitoring and correlation of microbiological findings with the course of laboratory inflammation parameters is common practice.

Knowledge concerning the most prevalent pathogens- presumable bacteria— and spectrum—presumable Enterococcus species and Enterobacteriaceae in our cohort—is helpful to tailor empiric antimicrobial therapy. The high incidence of bacterial infections in the very early phase after transplantation justifies calculated antibiotic coverage in case of severe SIRS and upon clinical suspicion of infection, regardless of graft origin. In our cohort, fungal infections with mainly Candida species in the early phase after transplantation were noticed in DBD recipients only. Recipient selection might contribute to this finding, as in our institution, at the beginning of the DCD program and partly still nowadays, the sickest candidates in terms of, for example, MELD and acute liver failure, are typically allocated DBD organs. The confinement of CMV prophylaxis to high-risk liver transplantation recipients is linked to the finding of herpes viruses as the most frequently detected virus in our study population. Both findings may be helpful to risk-stratify diagnostic and choice of empirical antimicrobial treatment, as the risk and pattern of infections may differ by donor type, while pathogens and spectrum remain largely consistent.

The results of our study have relevant clinical implications. As DCD liver transplantation becomes more prevalent, transplant and ICU physicians should be aware of the potential increased infection risk and could consider targeted prophylactic infection surveillance (eg, longitudinal sampling). In addition, DCD grafts commonly are subject to warm ischemia times and thus are more susceptible to ischemia-reperfusion injury, which might impair immune defense mechanisms or liver tissue integrity. While hypothermic oxygenated machine perfusion (HOPE) was regularly used in DCD organs to minimize ischemic damage, its potential to counteract these effects remains currently uncertain and warrants further investigation. As abdominal normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) is reported to optimize graft viability, this measure may be linked to decrease in infection risk and further studies in this area are warranted to clarify whether NRP contributes to a decrease in infection rates in extended donor organs.29 However, while NRP improves graft viability, demonstrating a causal effect on infectious outcomes might be difficult, as such studies likely are methodologically challenging owing to potential confounding factors (eg, by donor type, recipient risk profile, perioperative management, and heterogeneity in perfusion protocols).

Our study has several strengths. It included a large and well-characterized cohort with a strict definition of infection. Moreover, it reflects real-world clinical practice in a high-volume transplant center. The findings might therefore serve to improve patient monitoring and inform immunosuppressive decision-making in the future.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective study design inherently carries some risk of unmeasured confounders. Furthermore, our single-center setting might limit generalizability. In addition, even though standardized and careful coding of infections was performed, miscoding in some infections may have occurred, though leading to over- or underestimation in incidence. As body mass index (BMI) in our study cohort is classified as overweight—obese, there may be an association with an increased risk of infection.30

In conclusion, we found that DCD liver recipients were at higher risk for infections in the first post-transplant year compared with DBD recipients, while a higher rate of infections occurred rather early after LT in DBD—and after several months in DCD, presumably associated with biliary ischemic lesions.
