Wheat yield prediction using integrated optical and radar remote sensing with machine learning across key phenological stages
Authors: Mir Naser Navidi, Erfan Fazli, Rasoul Kharazmi, Javad Seyedmohammadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise and timely prediction of wheat yield is pivotal for ensuring global food security and optimizing agricultural management practices, particularly through advanced remote sensing and machine learning techniques. In this study, wheat yield was accurately estimated by leveraging remote sensing-derived soil and vegetation indices. Yield data from 189 study points were collected, and Sentinel-2 (10-meter resolution) imagery from the Tillering and Anthesis growth stages was used. The models incorporated 25 variables, including 16 optical indices (e.g., NDVI, SAVI, MSAVI2) and three topographic factors. The key novelty of this research is the rigorous comparison of the predictive value and synergistic contribution of Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data when integrated with Sentinel-2 optical indices within machine learning frameworks. Three machine learning approaches (Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest (RF)) were employed and evaluated using 70% training and 30% testing subsets. Results revealed that the RF model, leveraging data from the Anthesis phenological stage, exhibited superior performance in wheat yield estimation, achieving an R² of 0.92 and an RMSE of 0.14 ton ha-1 for the training set, and an R² of 0.90 and an RMSE of 0.29 ton ha-1 for the testing set. To enhance model accuracy, Sentinel-1 radar data were integrated into the RF framework. This addition reduced the training set RMSE to 0.13 ton ha-1 but increased the testing set RMSE to 0.33 ton ha-1, with R² values remaining stable at 0.92 and 0.90 for the training and testing sets, respectively. Variable importance analysis indicated that optical soil and vegetation indices were the dominant predictors. Although the inclusion of Sentinel-1 SAR data offered additional insights, it did not outperform the predictive capacity of optical indices. These findings validate the efficacy of the combined Sentinel-2 remote sensing approach for generating reliable wheat yield forecasts approximately 50 days prior to harvest.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is a cornerstone of global food security, serving as a vital staple crop for billions of people around the world. Agriculture consumes over 70% of global freshwater resources1, and to meet projected food demands by 2050, industrialized nations require a 60% increase in production, while developing countries need a 100% rise2. In recent decades, climate change and human activities have intensified water scarcity, with prolonged droughts and rising agricultural demands threatening food production3. Wheat, a high-yielding cereal, provides a significant portion of caloric and protein intake in developing nations, including Iran4. Wheat holds paramount importance in Iran, not only as a strategic staple crop crucial for national food security but also as a cornerstone of the agricultural economy, deeply intertwined with the livelihoods of millions across diverse climatic regions5. Accurate and timely crop productivity estimations are essential for a stable grain market, famine prevention, and maintaining food security. Early yield forecasting also aids in optimizing field management practices such as fertilization, irrigation scheduling, pest control, and harvest planning, thereby improving resource efficiency and minimizing regional impact6–8. Iran contributes less than 2% to global wheat production, equivalent to approximately 14 to 16 million tons annually9. Qazvin Plain, a key wheat-producing region in Iran, accounts for a significant share of national production despite its semi-arid climate and variable rainfall (210–550 mm annually). Given its substantial impact on agricultural advancement and regional food security, a prompt and precise assessment of wheat production in this Plain is crucial5.

For decades, researchers have predominantly employed empirical statistical models and process-oriented crop growth models to enhance agricultural productivity estimations10,11. Traditional statistical models predict crop yield using regression equations that link weather parameters, such as temperature, precipitation, and solar radiation, to historical yield records across varying times and locations12–15. While these models highlight climate influences on yield, their limited spatial generalization restricts broad regional applications16. In contrast, process-based crop models, such as Decision Support System for Agrotechnology Transfer (DSSAT)7,17, Agricultural Production Systems Simulator (APSIM)7,18, and Modeling Crop Water and Land Allocation (MCWLA)19, simulate yield reliably but require extensive inputs, including climate, soil, and management data4. These requirements make such models time-consuming, costly, and challenging to scale in developing and arid regions like Qazvin, where detailed data are often unavailable. Arid regions require predictive models that address the limitations of linear assumptions and restricted spatial generalization to effectively capture the nonlinear dynamics of remote sensing and climate-crop yield interactions3. Process-based models, which demand extensive soil, hydrological, and management data, are often impractical in arid and developing regions due to data scarcity, hindering their widespread application8,20.

Remote sensing provides critical spatial data on vegetation health and environmental factors, with advancements enabling finer temporal and spatial resolutions21. Integration of vegetation growth indices significantly enhances the accuracy of crop yield predictions6. Advances in remote sensing technologies have enabled the derivation of these indices at higher temporal and spatial resolutions, facilitating more precise and scalable agricultural assessments. The techniques referred to include multispectral and hyperspectral satellite imagery (e.g., from Landsat, Sentinel-2), UAV/drone-based sensors for high-spatial-resolution data, and derivation of vegetation indices such as NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), EVI (Enhanced Vegetation Index), and chlorophyll content estimation. These advances enable temporal resolutions from daily to sub-daily (via constellations like PlanetScope or Sentinel) and spatial resolutions down to centimeters with drones, supporting scalable assessments like crop health monitoring, yield prediction, and stress detection22–24.

Machine Learning (ML) offers a viable alternative, leveraging multi-source data, such as remote sensing and climate variables, to develop nonlinear models with minimal prior assumptions. Its computational efficiency, scalability, and ability to handle complex spatio-temporal patterns make ML ideal for yield prediction in arid regions3. ML excels in analyzing multidimensional remote sensing data, improving prediction accuracy and efficiency in agricultural studies, particularly for crop classification and yield assessment. Recent studies have integrated multi-source remote sensing data with ML for wheat yield prediction25–32, but applications in arid regions remain limited3. This gap highlights a critical area for further investigation4.

To date, most research on vegetation health assessment and crop yield prediction in arid regions has primarily relied on single remote sensing indices, such as NDVI33–36, which, in these environments, often fail to capture critical factors like vegetation structure, climate extremes, and soil moisture, thereby constraining the integration of multifaceted data for enhanced predictive accuracy. While numerous studies have effectively employed remote sensing and meteorological data for winter wheat yield prediction in humid regions25,37,38, their application in arid regions encounters significant challenges due to high atmospheric interference, frequent dust storms, and sparse vegetation cover, which impede accurate retrieval of vegetation indices39. The pronounced geographical variability in arid regions further complicates the identification of optimal phenological stages for yield prediction, necessitating an optimized framework that integrates multiple remote sensing indices and machine learning techniques to address these region-specific constraints and enhance forecasting accuracy.

Recent advances in radar remote sensing, such as Sentinel-1 SAR data, offer complementary insights by penetrating cloud cover and providing structural information on vegetation, which is particularly valuable in arid regions prone to atmospheric disturbances and sparse plant cover40–42. By integrating radar backscattering coefficients with optical indices, models can better account for soil moisture variations and crop biomass under extreme conditions, mitigating the limitations of optical sensors alone43. This multi-sensor approach not only improves robustness against weather variability but also enhances overall predictive accuracy in data-scarce regions.

Predicting crop yield across extensive spatial scales requires processing vast datasets and complex data integration, often entailing significant costs for data acquisition and analysis44. Google Earth Engine (GEE), a cloud-based geospatial computing platform, provides an efficient and scalable solution for analyzing petabyte-scale environmental datasets, enabling robust visualization and processing of geospatial information45. This study aims to develop a sophisticated predictive model for wheat yield in arid regions of Qazvin plain by integrating multi-source remote sensing data with advanced machine learning techniques, addressing region-specific challenges such as sparse vegetation and climatic variability. Materials and methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in the irrigated agricultural lands of Qazvin Province, a key agricultural region in northern Iran, covering approximately 15,623 km² (1% of Iran’s total area). Located between 48°44′E to 50°51′E longitude and 35°24′N to 36°48′N latitude, Qazvin Province is bordered by Gilan and Mazandaran to the north, Markazi and Hamedan to the south, Zanjan to the west, and Alborz to the east (Fig. 1a and b). The province features diverse topography, with the Alborz mountain range in the north and the Avaj highlands in the south framing the central Qazvin Province. Elevations range from 300 m above sea level in the Tarom Sofla region (near Sefidrud Dam Lake) to 4,200 m at Shah Alborz peak (Fig. 1d). The Qazvin Province, with a slope extending from northwest to southeast (1130–4000 m), supports extensive agriculture due to significant groundwater reserves and surface water from surrounding mountains. Qazvin Province experiences an arid and semi-arid climate with annual rainfall of 210–550 mm and an average temperature of 14 °C, though central areas exhibit higher temperatures and lower humidity46. The province encompasses 453,000 ha of agricultural and orchard lands, with wheat and barley as the primary crops, cultivated across 153,000 ha, of which 75% is rain-fed5. This region contributes significantly to Iran’s agricultural output, producing approximately 10% of the nation’s cereal crops despite its limited area5. The arid conditions, variable rainfall, and sparse vegetation cover pose challenges for remote sensing applications, necessitating advanced multi-source data integration to capture crop dynamics effectively6. Specifically, this study focuses on the Qazvin Plain (Fig. 1b), characterized by its arid climate, which amplifies the challenges of wheat yield prediction due to regional conditions (Fig. 1c). These characteristics make Qazvin Plain an ideal case study for developing robust wheat yield prediction models tailored to arid regions.

Wheat yield ground truth data

Wheat yield data, expressed in ton per hectare (ton ha− 1), were acquired from the Soil Identification and Land Evaluation Research Department, Soil and Water Research Institute (http://www.swri.ir/en-US/DouranPortal/1/page/Home), for spring wheat cultivated in Qazvin Plain during the 2020 growing season. A comprehensive dataset was initially collected from irrigated agricultural fields across the Qazvin Plain, representing diverse soil and topographic conditions. To ensure data quality, rigorous statistical analyses, including normality tests and descriptive statistics, were conducted to assess the dataset’s consistency with regional yield distributions. Subsequently, 189 representative samples were selected using an outlier detection method, where two extreme yield values (21.0 ton ha− 1 and 0.5 ton ha− 1, which were likely due to measurement error) exceeding ± 3 standard deviations from the mean were excluded to enhance data integrity. These 189 validated yield observations were used for model training and evaluation (see Fig. 3). This statistically refined dataset provides a robust foundation for calibrating and validating predictive models tailored to arid regions. The collection and refinement of wheat yield ground truth data are depicted as the initial stage in the methodological flowchart (Fig. 2).

Optical sensor data

To support wheat yield prediction in Qazvin Plain, satellite imagery was acquired via the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform for two critical phenological stages of spring wheat: Tillering (15th April 2020) and Anthesis (15th May 2020). This choice is based on consensus in agricultural literature, identifying these as the most critical phenological periods for final yield determination. Tillering is essential for establishing stand density, and Anthesis corresponds to the period of peak greenness and maximum photosynthetic capacity in wheat. While acknowledging that higher temporal resolution provides a more complete picture, utilizing features from these two key stages ensures we capture the most predictive information while maintaining modeling efficiency3,47. Optical data after applying cloud and cloud‑shadow masking, checking atmospheric correction (Sen2Cor) were sourced from Sentinel-2 Level-2 A surface reflectance products at a 10-meter spatial resolution48, selected for their high-resolution capability to capture fine-scale vegetation dynamics in wheat fields49. Six spectral bands (Table 1) were used to derive 16 soil and vegetation indices, as described in “Soil and vegetation indices” (see Fig. 2). It is important to note that the Aerosol Optical Thickness (AOT) and Water Vapor Pressure (WVP) bands were included in the initial feature set strictly for exploratory purposes. While these are primarily atmospheric correction parameters, they were tested to ensure no potential indirect correlation with regional micro-climate or yield variability was overlooked. Our subsequent Feature Importance analysis (detailed in “Optical sensor data”) ultimately confirmed their negligible predictive contribution compared to the biophysical features. Topographic attributes, including elevation, slope, and aspect, were derived from the ASTER DEM at a 30-meter resolution, accessed through GEE, to account for terrain variability influencing crop growth50.

Soil and vegetation indices

To enhance wheat yield prediction, 16 soil and vegetation indices were derived to quantify crop health and productivity across the Qazvin Plain (Table 2). These indices were selected based on their established correlation with wheat yield in arid regions27,53. The indices were calculated using four Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance bands (B02, B03, B04, and B08) at a 10-meter spatial resolution, processed within the RStudio environment using the ‘raster’ and ‘RStoolbox’ packages54. Data were extracted for the 189 sampling points corresponding to the Tillering (15 April 2020) and Anthesis (15 May 2020) phenological stages, ensuring alignment with ground truth yield observations. The statistical distributions of the derived indices for both phenological stages are presented in Fig. 6. These indices capture critical biophysical properties, such as canopy density and soil moisture interactions, which are essential for accurate yield modeling in arid conditions characterized by sparse vegetation and variable climate. The inclusion of Soil-Adjusted Vegetation Indices (SAVI, MSAVI) was strategically vital for the accuracy of models. These indices do not directly measure soil fertility. However, their predictive value is rooted in two critical roles: First, during the Tillering stage, when canopy closure is low, these indices effectively mitigate soil background noise (brightness and moisture effects), leading to more robust early-season biomass estimations. Second, in later stages (Anthesis), they maintain their importance by effectively discriminating between areas of full canopy closure and areas with low-density or sparse canopy, which directly relates to final yield variability. Feature Importance analysis (Sect. 3.3) validates this dual contribution, confirming that these indices significantly improve predictive performance over raw vegetation indices55,56. The derived dataset forms a robust basis for integrating with radar and topographic data to develop predictive models (see Sect. 2.6).

Adding radar sensor data to enhance model performance

To strengthen the predictive model for wheat yield in the Qazvin Plain, Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) data were systematically integrated with the existing set of 25 auxiliary variables (see “Optical sensor data” and “Soil and vegetation indices”). This integration was conducted in a secondary modeling phase to enhance the model’s capability to capture soil moisture and vegetation structure, using the same 189 sampling points and preprocessing pipelines as the baseline analysis. Sentinel-1, a C-band SAR system operating at a frequency of 5.405 GHz (approximately 5.7 cm wavelength), actively transmits microwave signals and measures backscattered energy, enabling imaging under all weather conditions and during day or night, unlike passive optical sensors61. This capability is particularly valuable in the Qazvin Plain, where sparse vegetation and atmospheric interference can limit optical data reliability40. Sentinel-1 Level-1 Ground Range Detected (GRD) data, acquired on 2 May 2020 (corresponding to the Anthesis stage of spring wheat), were retrieved from the GEE platform at a 10-meter spatial resolution. The data, comprising VV (vertical-vertical) and VH (vertical-horizontal) polarizations, underwent standard preprocessing, including radiometric calibration and terrain correction, to derive backscatter coefficients reported in decibels (dB)62. These coefficients, influenced by surface roughness, dielectric properties (related to soil and vegetation moisture), and canopy geometry, were used to compute radar indices (listed in Table 3) for the 189 sampling points. By incorporating these radar-derived indices with optical and topographic variables, the model was enhanced to better represent the biophysical and hydrological conditions critical for wheat yield prediction4.

Yield estimation

To predict wheat yield in Qazvin Plain, three models were employed: Multiple Linear Regression (MLR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest (RF). These models were selected for their robustness in handling high-dimensional datasets, resilience to noise, and suitability for complex regression tasks in agricultural applications55,65,66. MLR was chosen as a baseline for linear modeling, while RF and SVM were prioritized for their ability to capture nonlinear relationships and adapt to the diverse environmental and phenological conditions of arid regions67. The dataset was partitioned into 70% training and 30% testing subsets to ensure robust model development and evaluation. A total of 25 features were used, including six Sentinel-2 Level-2 A 10-meter spectral bands (B02, B03, B04, B08, AOT, and WVP), 16 soil and vegetation indices (see Table 2), and three topographic attributes (elevation, slope, aspect), as detailed in “Optical sensor data” and “Soil and vegetation indices”. Data preprocessing, feature selection, model training, and evaluation were conducted in R (version 4.4.2; R Core Team, 2024; https://www.r-project.org/) using RStudio (version 2025.05.0 + 496; Posit Software, PBC, Boston, MA, USA; https://posit.co/products/open-source/rstudio/) using packages such as ‘raster’, ‘rastervis’, ‘gdalraster’, ‘terra’, ‘RStoolbox’, ‘caret’, ‘randomForest’, ‘kernlab’. Feature selection was performed using Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) to identify the most predictive variables, optimizing model accuracy68. This systematic workflow enabled the identification of the optimal model and feature set for accurate wheat yield prediction in arid regions.

Methods for estimating wheat yield

To predict wheat yield in the Qazvin Plain, three models, MLR, SVM and RF, were applied using auxiliary data across the Tillering (15 April 2020) and Anthesis (15 May 2020) phenological stages. These features served as independent variables, with observed wheat yield (ton ha− 1) from 189 sampling points as the dependent variable (see “ Wheat yield ground truth data”).

MLR models the relationship between wheat yield and the 25 input features by minimizing the sum of squared residuals using Ordinary Least Squares69. MLR was selected for its interpretability and computational efficiency, making it a suitable baseline for yield prediction. However, its assumption of linearity and sensitivity to multicollinearity can limit its performance with complex, non-linear environmental data8,70.

SVR, a variant of SVM, was employed to capture non-linear relationships by mapping input features into a higher-dimensional space using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel55,67. SVR minimizes prediction errors within an epsilon-insensitive tube, defined by support vectors, offering robustness in high-dimensional datasets. Its performance relies on optimal hyperparameter tuning47, achieved through grid search in this study. Despite its effectiveness in arid agricultural contexts, SVR can be computationally intensive for large datasets8.

RF, an ensemble learning method, constructs multiple regression trees from bootstrap samples of the training data, with random feature subsets selected at each node to minimize mean squared error7,71. RF was optimized with 500 trees (ntree) and one-third of the features per node (mtry), balancing predictive accuracy and model diversity47. Its ability to handle non-linear relationships and robustness to noise makes it particularly suitable for wheat yield prediction in arid regions.

Optimal wheat training time for yield estimation

Accurate wheat yield estimation in arid regions requires identifying optimal training period that capture critical phenological stages influencing yield formation. In this study, two time windows corresponding to the Tillering (15 April 2020) and Anthesis (15 May 2020) stages were evaluated for the Qazvin Plain, based on their significant influence on vegetative growth and grain development, respectively. The Tillering stage drives biomass accumulation, a key determinant of yield potential, while Anthesis governs grain number and size, serving as a primary predictor of final yield3,8. These stages were selected to align with the local crop calendar, reflecting region-specific phenological and climatic conditions in Qazvin’s arid region. Model performance (R² and RMSE) was assessed for each stage using the 25 features derived from Sentinel-2 Level-2 A and topographic data (see “Optical sensor data” and “Soil and vegetation indices”), enabling the identification of the optimal training period for robust yield prediction in arid conditions. This approach accounts for challenges such as sparse vegetation cover and variable climate, enhancing forecast accuracy72.

Spatial patterns of wheat yield prediction

The spatial map of predicted wheat yields, generated using the RF model with integrated Sentinel-2 and Sentinel-1 data at the Anthesis stage (Fig. 12), showed yields ranging from 3.4 to 8.3 ton ha⁻¹ across the Qazvin Plain. This process involved three main steps: (1) Feature Stack Generation: All 30 predictive features (spectral bands, indices, and topographic factors) used in the trained RF model were co-registered and stacked into a single raster cube. (2) Resampling and Computational Optimization: Since the study area was large and the native 10 m resolution resulted in an excessive computational burden (millions of pixels), the entire feature stack was resampled to a 30 m pixel resolution using the Nearest Neighbor technique. This step significantly reduced the computational overhead while maintaining the integrity of the categorical and numerical data. (3) Pixel-wise Application: The final RF model was then applied pixel-by-pixel to this 30 m resolution Feature Stack to output the spatially explicit predicted yield raster map. High-yield zones (> 7 ton ha⁻¹) accounted for approximately 30% of the study area, while low-yield zones (< 4 ton ha⁻¹) comprised about 15%, reflecting significant spatial variability in predicted yields.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the efficacy of integrating multi-source remote sensing data with machine learning for accurate wheat yield prediction in the arid Qazvin Plain. The RF model outperformed MLR and SVM models, achieving an R² of 0.90 and RMSE of 0.29 ton ha⁻¹ on the testing set during the Anthesis phenological stage. This superior performance is attributed to RF’s ability to capture non-linear relationships and handle high-dimensional datasets, aligning with prior studies that highlight its robustness in complex agricultural systems3,7,47,71. MLR and SVM showed lower accuracy, with greater dispersion in predicted versus observed yields, particularly at the Tillering stage, likely due to their limitations in modeling non-linear dynamics and sparse canopy signals in arid regions8,70. The enhanced accuracy at Anthesis compared to Tillering is likely driven by peak canopy greenness and density, which improve the differentiation of vegetation from soil using indices such as RVI, NRVI, and NDWI3,27,55. At Tillering, sparse canopy cover reduces the effectiveness of these indices, as vegetation signals are confounded by soil background, a common challenge in arid regions8,77.

Variable importance analysis indicated that vegetation indices, particularly RVI and NRVI, were the primary predictors of wheat yield, reflecting their strong correlation with biomass, photosynthesis, and canopy health7,78,79. Hydrological indices like NDWI and WVP also played a critical role, underscoring the importance of water availability in the water-scarce Qazvin Plain80,81. Spectral bands (e.g., Sentinel-2 B02, B03, B04 and B08) were less influential than derived indices, as the latter target specific biophysical properties27. Topographic variables (elevation, slope, aspect) and Aerosol Optical Thickness (AOT) had minimal impact, likely due to the flat terrain and clear atmospheric conditions of the study area82,83. In regions with greater topographic variability or atmospheric interference, these factors may contribute more significantly to yield prediction.

Incorporating Sentinel-1 SAR data with optical indices improved the RF model’s training performance, reducing RMSE from 0.14 to 0.13 ton ha⁻¹, though testing RMSE increased slightly to 0.33 ton ha⁻¹. This suggests potential overfitting or sensitivity to spatial variability in radar data, warranting further investigation42. Optical indices such as TVI, SR, CTVI, NDVI, TTVI, and KNDVI remained dominant, with radar indices providing complementary information on soil moisture and canopy structure, particularly valuable in arid conditions with sparse vegetation40,43. The scatter plot in Fig. 10 confirms the model’s robust linear fit, though slight dispersion indicates areas for refinement in radar integration. These findings align with previous studies that highlight the supportive role of radar data in enhancing model robustness under variable weather conditions41,78,84.

The spatial yield map (Fig. 12), ranging from 3.4 to 8.3 ton ha⁻¹, highlights the model’s ability to capture spatial heterogeneity driven by soil and vegetation indices3,85. High-yield zones (> 7 ton ha⁻¹) correlate with optimal canopy and water conditions, while low-yield zones (< 4 ton ha⁻¹) indicate areas requiring targeted interventions, such as enhanced irrigation or soil management41,42. This map serves as a practical tool for precision agriculture, enabling farmers to optimize resource allocation and improve productivity in arid systems 27. Compared to prior studies, this multi-sensor approach outperforms models relying solely on optical data, such as Qader et al.86 (R² = 0.85) and Raza et al.3 (R² = 0.82), by addressing limitations in sparse vegetation environments through radar-augmented, phenology-specific modeling. Future research should explore scaling this framework to other arid regions, incorporating deep learning to capture additional non-linear patterns, and integrating UAV imagery for higher-resolution yield mapping.

Limitations and future scope

While this study demonstrated the efficacy of integrating Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 features combined with Machine Learning for high-accuracy wheat yield prediction, several limitations should be addressed in future research to enhance the model’s robustness and generalizability. The model relied on data from a single growing season (2020) and only two discrete phenological stages (Tillering and Anthesis). This limited temporal resolution prevents the full utilization of time-series analysis capabilities. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size (N = 189) required the selection of robust, non-parametric models (RF, SVM) and prevented the deployment of data-intensive models. The complexity of advanced machine learning models, such as Gradient Boosting (XGBoost / LightGBM) and Deep Learning (NNs), was deemed inappropriate for the current dataset, as these models are highly susceptible to overfitting when trained with limited samples. Similarly, Temporal Models (LSTM / GRU) were avoided due to the lack of high-frequency time-series data.

Building upon this research, subsequent studies should focus on: (a) Integrating Multi-Year and High-Frequency Data: Expanding the dataset to include multiple growing seasons and a greater number of images across the full phenological cycle (e.g., weekly or bi-weekly acquisitions). (b) Advanced Modeling Techniques: With increased sample size and temporal frequency, researchers should employ advanced machine learning and deep learning algorithms, including XGBoost and LSTM / GRU, to capture the full temporal dynamics of wheat growth and potentially achieve superior predictive accuracy. (c) Incorporation of Auxiliary Data: Future models should incorporate key meteorological variables (such as rainfall, temperature, and evapotranspiration) and detailed soil nutrient data, which were not available for this study, to better explain yield variability and improve prediction robustness. (d) Transferability Assessment: The model’s transferability should be rigorously tested across different semi-arid regions and for various wheat cultivars to validate its wider applicability.

Conclusions

Accurate wheat yield prediction is vital for sustainable agriculture and food security in arid regions like the Qazvin Plain. This study demonstrates that integrating Sentinel-2 optical imagery with vegetation and hydrological indices and RF modeling achieves robust predictions, with R² = 0.90 and RMSE = 0.33 ton ha⁻¹ at the Anthesis stage (15 May 2020), enabling forecasts 50 days before harvest. Incorporating Sentinel-1 SAR data enhanced sensitivity to soil moisture and canopy structure, particularly in sparse vegetation, though testing RMSE increased slightly to 0.33 ton ha⁻¹, suggesting potential overfitting. Key predictors, including vegetation (TVI, SR, CTVI, NDVI, TTVI, KNDVI, RVI and NRVI) and hydrological (NDWI) indices, drove accuracy by capturing canopy greenness and water availability. The yield map (3.4–8.3 ton ha⁻¹) revealed spatial variability, guiding targeted irrigation and fertilization strategies. Compared to prior studies, this multi-sensor approach improved predictive accuracy by 5.9% over Qader et al.86 (R² = 0.85) in semi-arid regions without SAR integration and 9.8% over Raza et al.3 (R² = 0.82) using optical indices alone. While the model excels in wheat yield prediction, its generalizability to other crops requires further validation. These findings support precision agriculture by optimizing resource allocation and informing policy. Future research should scale this framework to other arid regions, explore deep learning for non-linear patterns, and integrate UAV imagery for enhanced resolution, advancing sustainable farming practices.
