Recognizing and Responding to Cardiac Risk With Osimertinib
Authors: Eun Kyoung Kim, Se-Hoon Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: heart failure, lung cancer, pharmacotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitor

The introduction of third-generation epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), particularly osimertinib, has significantly advanced the treatment of EGFR-mutant non–small-cell lung cancer, establishing a new standard of care through improved efficacy and central nervous system penetration over earlier generations.1, 2, 3 However, accumulating evidence has raised concerns regarding its cardiac safety profile, including QT interval prolongation, ventricular arrhythmias, and heart failure (HF). Initial randomized controlled trials, including FLAURA (AZD9291 Versus Gefitinib or Erlotinib in Patients With Locally Advanced or Metastatic Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer) and AURA3 (AZD9291 [Osimertinib] Versus Platinum-Based Doublet-Chemotherapy in Locally Advanced or Metastatic Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer), did not raise concerns about a causal association between osimertinib and HF when analyzed in aggregate.1,2,4 However, these trials were conducted in highly selected populations, often excluding patients with pre-existing cardiovascular conditions, and thus may not have adequately reflected “real-world” cardiovascular risk. Moreover, the relatively low event rates and limited follow-up duration in these studies may have hindered the detection of clinically meaningful cardiac outcomes. In contrast, postmarketing surveillance and real-world data have reported a higher incidence of cardiac events, including symptomatic HF, arrhythmias, and cardiac death.5,6 These observations have led to growing recognition among clinicians of the potential cardiotoxicity of osimertinib, although critical uncertainties remain. First, the causal relationship between osimertinib and cardiac events has not been clearly established, largely because of the absence of prospective observational data and appropriate control groups. Second, evidence guiding the clinical management of patients who develop cardiac dysfunction during osimertinib therapy remains limited.

In this context, the study by Tatebe et al7 reported in this issue of JACC: CardioOncology provides meaningful real-world evidence that helps address the first of these unmet needs. Using a large-scale Japanese claims database, the investigators conducted a retrospective cohort analysis including more than 11,000 patients treated with osimertinib and more than 100,000 control patients who received alternative systemic therapies, including other EGFR TKIs, anaplastic lymphoma kinase inhibitors, and cytotoxic agents. A major strength of the study is its comparative design across multiple treatment classes, including direct comparisons with other EGFR TKIs, which enhances clinical relevance and reinforces the specific association between osimertinib and increased HF risk beyond a class effect. After rigorous adjustment in multivariable Cox regression and propensity score matching, osimertinib use was associated with more than a 2-fold increase in the hazard of HF hospitalization (HFH). This association remained consistent across most subgroups and persisted even when the comparator was restricted to other EGFR TKIs. Although the absolute incidence of HFH was relatively low (∼1%), this is likely reflective of the study’s strict endpoint definition and limited follow-up period. Given that osimertinib is associated with longer treatment exposure compared with earlier generation EGFR TKIs (median duration 20.7 months vs 11.5 months), cumulative cardiotoxic risk may be more pronounced, particularly in long-term survivors.8

Still, some limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design, reliance on claims-based definitions, and lack of baseline echocardiographic data limit the granularity of clinical interpretation. In addition, the focus on HFH as the sole cardiac endpoint likely underestimates the broader cardiotoxic burden. Clinically relevant events such as arrhythmias, myocarditis, outpatient HF, and asymptomatic LV dysfunction were not captured, despite their potential impact on long-term cardiac morbidity and mortality and overall survival.

Importantly, the study also explored risk factors associated with HFH. Consistent with previous retrospective analyses, older age, history of HF, and atrial fibrillation were associated with increased risk.6 The investigators also identified hypertension and chronic kidney disease as additional risk factors among patients receiving osimertinib.

With growing evidence supporting the association between osimertinib and cardiotoxicity, the clinical question is no longer whether the risk exists but how best to identify and manage it. It remains unclear which patients are most appropriate for cardiac monitoring and what a surveillance protocol should entail in terms of timing, frequency, and modality. Similarly, the long-term management of patients who develop cardiac events during osimertinib therapy is poorly defined. Questions regarding the reversibility of cardiac dysfunction, the role of cardioprotective therapies, and the safety of rechallenging with EGFR TKIs remain unanswered. Long-term follow-up data from patients who experience cardiovascular toxicity will be essential to better define the natural history of these events and to inform evidence-based strategies for surveillance and management. Such data will be critical to guiding risk-adapted approaches and improving outcomes in this growing patient population.
