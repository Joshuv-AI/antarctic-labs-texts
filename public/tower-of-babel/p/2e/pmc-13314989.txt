An explainable multi-stage framework for brain tumor classification using hybrid feature fusion and EfficientNetB5 model
Authors: Imran Qureshi, Muhammad Zaheer Sajid, Ayman Youssef, Muhammad Fareed Hamid, Yazeed Alobaidan, Qaisar Abbas, Rashid Jan, Mostafa E A Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate recognition and classification of severity level of brain tumor (BT) is essential for clinical decision making. Manual assessment of brain tumor is a laborious and time consuming task for Experts. Existing deep learning (DL) models have been shown promising classification accuracy with limited features diversity. This study presents a HyFusion-Net model, a novel and explainable DL framework, fuses handcrafted features (SIFT, LBP, HoG, Canny) with deep features derived from DenseNet201, achieving superior accuracy with an EfficientNetB5 classifier. For clinical interpretation, an occlusion map-based explainability is integrated to highlight tumor regions driving classification. In addition, the proposed HyFusion-Net model provides robust performance against MRI variability and ensure a transparent diagnostic rate. Evaluated on a total number of 7023 MRI images using various statistical metrics (AUC, precision, F1-score, and accuracy), the proposed HyFusion-Net outperforms other DL methods while providing heatmaps aligned with radiological markers. Experimental results endorse the suitability of the proposed HyFusion-Net model for clinical use and proves the ability of the model to helps radiologists to understand the classification decision.Keywords: Brain tumor, Deep learning, Feature fusion, Magnetic resonance image, Occlusion mapSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

The brain is an important component of our central nervous system that manages all the human body functionalities using a vast number of connected neurones1. One of the main diseases that affect the brain is brain tumours, which are brain abnormalities that are referred to as tumours in medical terminology. Brain tumor (BT) categorization has crucial role in medical diagnostics, as an early and precise brain tumour classification can significantly increase patient’s survival rate. On the other hand, misclassification of brain tumour will reduce the patient’s chances of survival and prevent them from receiving the proper needed care2. Depending on the tumor’s size, location, and grade, symptoms could range from headaches and cognitive impairments to more severe symptoms and neurological disorders3. The different classes of BT named glioma, meningioma and pituitary have distinct manifestations on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data, as shown in Fig. 1. Hence, brain tumours are usually found and classified using MRI scans. Gliomas arise from the brain tissue and often considered as cancerous, while a meningioma form in the brain outer-covering and are usually non-cancerous. Pituitary tumor come from pituitary gland and lead to symptoms through hormonal effects. MRI is a non-invasive imaging method that gives detailed pictures of the brain4. MRI images help neuropathologists and radiologists to study BT and decide the next steps for treatment. These treatment decisions depend on many factors like the grade of the tumor, its shape and its type. Because of this correct diagnosis and classification of BT is very important for proper and effective treatment5.

A visual example of BT types where (a) shows glioma, (b) meningioma, and (c) pituitary.

In the past few years, there has been growing research interest in developing better techniques for classifying brain tumor MRI images. Manual identification and classification of BT is a slow and error prone process6. This has created a clear demand for automated approaches that can take the place of conventional manual methods. Deep Learning (DL) models especially CNNs have shown very good classification accuracy for different BT types7–9. These DL methods usually follow important steps like image preprocessing feature segmentation and final classification to perform MRI based BT analysis. Deep features extracted from different DL models are usually used for identifying and classifying severity level of BT10–12. This study appears to be the first to present a novel DL-based hybrid framework that accurately classifies the severity level of brain tumours by combining handcrafted features with deep features. The primary novelty of the proposed HyFusion-Net framework lies in its structured hybrid feature fusion strategy, which explicitly integrates complementary handcrafted texture and edge descriptors with deep semantic features extracted using DenseNet201 prior to classification using EfficientNetB5. Unlike conventional approaches that rely solely on deep learning or handcrafted features, the proposed framework combines heterogeneous feature representations to preserve both fine-grained texture information and high-level semantic characteristics, improving feature diversity and classification robustness. In addition, the integration of occlusion-based explainability gives visual interpretation of model predictions, which make the model more transparent and easier to understand. The proposed framework achieves high classification performance with efficient inference and shows good potential for computer-aided brain tumor classification, but still needs more validation on different and diverse clinical datasets.

Related work

Brain tumour classification is considered a hot topic for research that attracts researchers for developing new accurate models for such application. An automated multimodal classification system for brain classification based on DL is presented by the authors in13. There are five primary steps in the proposed methodology. Firstly, the linear contrast stretching function is performed on the input image. This pre-processing step also involves discrete cosine transform and edge-based histogram equalization. Second, DL models are used as feature extractors. In this method, the first stage relies on VGG16 and VGG19 models to obtain feature information from the MRI images. After collecting these features, an extreme learning machine (ELM) and a correntropy-based learning scheme are applied together to filter out the most useful feature sets. A separate step is then used to organize the features created through the partial least squares process into one final matrix. This combined matrix is given to the ELM classifier. The approach was tested on the BraTs2015, BraTs2017, and BraTs2018 datasets and produced accuracy values of 97.8%, 96.9%, and 92.5%.

Another research work14 explores transfer learning strategies for recognizing BT types. In this study, four pretrained deep learning methods are fine-tuned using transfer learning. Different optimization methods such as Adadelta, ADAM, RMSprop, and SGD are used during training. The experiments were carried out using the Figshare dataset which has 3064 T1-weighted MRI images collected from 233 patients. These images cover three tumor groups pituitary, meningioma and glioma. The framework was evaluated using accuracy and AUC. Among the tested models ResNet50 with Adadelta optimization achieved the best performance with a reported accuracy of 99.02%.

Study15 focuses on classifying four different MRI categories including glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumors and healthy cases. The authors apply transfer learning to several pretrained models including Xception, MobileNetV2, InceptionV3, ResNet50, VGG16, and DenseNet121. Data augmentation and image enhancement steps are added to further support the DL models. Among all tested architectures Xception showed the strongest performance with an accuracy of 98.73%. These results show that transfer learning can provide high classification accuracy and also handle challenges like class imbalance in medical images.

Another study2 proposes a classification framework based on GoogLeNet for three common tumor classes glioma, meningioma, and pituitary. The model includes an attention-based inception module which combines spatial and channel attention into the network. The inception part helps extract local structure from the image and the attention mechanism improves the focus on meaningful tumor regions. The model is trained on a publicly available dataset of 3064 MRI scans. It achieved 97.62% overall accuracy while using less parameters compared to standard CNN models. The class-wise accuracy was 99.22% for pituitary, 94.76% for meningioma, and 97.97% for glioma. Additional testing on Kaggle and Harvard datasets also showed consistent and stable performance.

Study5 presents another automatic MRI based brain tumor classification system. First, the MRI images are cleaned in preprocessing step to improve contrast and remove unwanted noise. After that, some pretrained deep learning models are used to extract important features from images. Then partial least squares method is applied to reduce and combine these features into one compact hybrid feature vector. Clustering technique is also used to find important tumor regions, which are resized and given to the classifier. This method achieved accuracy of 98.95%.

A different work16 introduced a BT classification system called RanMerFormer. This model use a pretrained transformer network as the main feature extractor. It adds a token merging strategy in the vision transformer to remove not useful information, which help to reduce the computational cost. A randomized vector functional link layer is used for the final classification step. The model was tested on two well known datasets and the results show that it performs at a strong and competitive level.

Another study4 presented a full DL pipeline that includes a U-Net based CNN for segmenting tumor regions and a classifier that uses transfer learning from a VGG16 network. Both the segmentation and grading parts use FLAIR MRI images. The dataset consisted of MRI scans from 110 patients diagnosed with lower grade glioma. The segmentation model was able to identify tumor regions with an accuracy of 92%. The grading stage separates grade II and grade III gliomas and achieved accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity values of 0.89, 0.87, and 0.92 at the image level and 0.95, 0.97, and 0.98 at the patient level. This demonstrates that DL can be used reliably for automated detection and grading.

In another research study17, a model was developed to classify glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumors. The authors used a U-Net architecture on MRI images and tested different pretrained CNN models such as InceptionV3, EfficientNetB4, and VGG19 through transfer learning. Performance was measured with accuracy, precision, recall, and F score. Their best U-Net based system achieved 98.56% accuracy and a 99% F-score which shows that U-Net is effective for tumor segmentation.

Another method called FusionNet was introduced in study18. This model first produces segmented images using a pretrained network with dual residual blocks. Attention mechanisms and ensemble-based feature selection are used to identify the most important features. The model then blends information from both the original images and the segmented versions to strengthen the classification. FusionNet reported very strong performance with accuracy scores of 99.62%, 99.54%, 99.39%, and 99.57% on the Figshare Kaggle Sartaj and combined datasets. These results show that this approach provides reliable improvements for MRI based tumor classification. In addition to the pre-trained deep learning models, the authors in19–23, showed a promising success for various diseases where the vision transformers with attention modules used for accurate segmentation and classification of disease. For instance, in a study19, the VLOrdinalFormer, a vision language guided transformer used to grade knee osteoarthritis by integrating visual and clinical text features. While in20, the authors used a dense-associated network-based transformer model for cardiac segmentation and obtained superior results. In a study21, the performance of the CNN architectures was improved by integrating attention modules for feature localization and classification. The authors in22 replaced multi-layer perceptron in vision transformer with Kolmogorov–Arnold Networks for improving the learning process and achieved higher accuracy. Similarly, in23, the authors proposed an ensemble-based approach, combined vision transformer with machine learning classifiers for accurate brain tumor classification. Existing DL methods show promising results with a higher cost of computation and limited feature diversity. Although many previous studies explored deep learning-based brain tumor classification with hybrid feature methods, but these approaches use only deep learning models or simple feature concatenation without fully using complementary feature properties. Also, many explainable deep learning models mainly focus on deep feature visualization without handcrafted features. In contrast, the proposed HyFusion-Net framework combines complementary handcrafted and deep features and use ensemble classifier to learn effectively from different type feature representations, while also including explainability. This combination improves feature representation ability, robustness, and interpretability compared to the existing methods.

Methodology

Unlike conventional deep learning methods which only depend on learned features, the proposed HyFusion-Net framework combines both handcrafted and deep features to improve overall feature quality. Handcrafted features capture fine texture, edges, and structural patterns, while deep features provide high-level semantic information. The fusion of these different features helps to provide more complete representation of tumor patterns. In addition, the use of strong classification model improves stability and better generalization compared to traditional single feature methods. The proposed method follows a step-by-step pipeline to achieve reliable brain tumor classification with improved accuracy and interpretability. First, all MRI scans are resized into fixed resolution so that input size remain same for all feature extraction and classification stages. Preprocessing steps such as noise reduction and contrast enhancement are applied to improve image quality and highlight important tumor regions. After preprocessing, dual feature extraction strategy is used where handcrafted features including Local Binary Patterns (LBP)24, Scale Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT)25, Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HoG)26, and edge-based features are extracted to capture texture, structure, and boundary information. At the same time, deep features are extracted using pretrained DenseNet201 model27, which learn hierarchical and high-level semantic representations from MRI images. These handcrafted and deep features are then combined using feature fusion by vector concatenation, resulting in hybrid feature representation with more rich and discriminative information for classification. For classification, EfficientNetB5 model28 is used to learn spatial and semantic patterns from MRI images and perform tumor classification. EfficientNetB5 is selected because of its efficient scaling of depth, width, and resolution, which allows strong feature learning and robust classification performance. In addition, an occlusion-based explainability is used to provide visualization of image regions which influence model prediction, improving interpretability and transparency of the proposed framework. Dataset

The dataset used in this study is Brain Tumor (BT) MRI dataset which is publicly available on Kaggle. This dataset was created by combining three different brain tumor MRI image datasets and arranged into four classes: glioma, meningioma, pituitary tumor, and healthy (no tumor). The final dataset has total 7,023 MRI images collected under different imaging conditions, orientations, and anatomical views. Because dataset is combined from multiple sources, there is a high intra-class variability such as tumor appearance, image contrast, resolution, and spatial differences. This makes classification tasks more difficult but also helps to test the model robustness in better way. This variability allows model to learn more general features and improves its performance on unseen data. It also reflects real clinical imaging conditions where images are different in many ways. All images were properly organized according to their class labels, and same preprocessing steps were applied before training and testing. This ensures consistent input format and gives reliable and fair performance evaluation of the model (Table 2).

Proposed architecture

The architecture used in this study is a hybrid deep learning framework which combine handcrafted feature descriptors with deep convolutional features and then classify them using EfficientNetB5. The main idea behind this design is to take benefit from both types of features. Handcrafted features capture low level texture and structural details, while deep convolutional features learn high level semantic patterns from the MRI images. By combining these two features, the framework create a more rich and powerful feature space which is important to handle the high variation present inside each BT class.

The pipeline begins with a standardized preprocessing stage, where each MRI scan is resized to pixels to ensure compatibility across all feature extraction modules. To enhance the visibility of tumor regions, median filtering is first applied for noise reduction, followed by Contrast Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE) to improve local contrast. In this study, feature level fusion is done by using vector concatenation to combine handcrafted features and deep features. Concatenation keeps all information from each feature source and helps to combine complementary properties in simple and effective way without adding extra learnable parameters or increasing much computational complexity. This method is computationally efficient and also reduce the risk of overfitting compared to more complex fusion methods. In future work, advanced fusion techniques like attention-based fusion and adaptive feature weighting will be explored and utilized to further improves feature integration and overall performance.

To make the feature fusion mechanism more explicit, each handcrafted descriptor is computed as a fixed-length numerical vector before concatenation. The LBP histogram (with P = 8 neighbors and radius R = 1) produces a 256-dimensional vector. HoG features (computed with 9 orientation bins, 8 × 8 pixel cells, and 2 × 2 block normalization) produce a 1764-dimensional vector. SIFT keypoint descriptors are aggregated using a Bag-of-Visual-Words encoding with a 256-words vocabulary obtained by k-means clustering on the training set, producing a 256-dimensional histogram. The Canny edge response is summarized through spatial histogram pooling into a 128-dimensional vector. Each sub-vector is L2-normalized independently before concatenation, so that no single descriptor dominate the fused representation due to scale or magnitude differences. The four normalized sub-vectors are then concatenated along the feature axis to form the handcrafted vector Fhand of dimensionality 2404. The deep feature vector Fdeep is obtained from the global average pooling (GAP) output of the frozen DenseNet201 backbone, which produces a 1920-dimensional embedding. The handcrafted and deep vectors are then concatenated along the feature axis to form the fused representation F = [Fhand || Fdeep] of dimensionality 4324, which is the unified input to the classification stage. This fixed-dimensional fusion strategy ensures consistent input length across all samples and allows fully reproducible training without dataset-specific dimension tuning.

The fused feature vector F is first projected into a spatial tensor of size 7 × 7 × 88 through a learnable dense reshape layer, so that it matches the input format expected by the compound-scaled MBConv blocks of EfficientNetB5. This reshaped tensor is then passed through the convolutional stages of EfficientNetB5, which allow the network to refine the fused representation through its hierarchical convolutional operations. The classification head consists of a global average pooling layer, a dropout layer with rate 0.3 to reduce overfitting, and a fully connected softmax layer with four output neurons corresponding to the glioma, meningioma, pituitary, and healthy classes. During fine-tuning, only the last two MBConv stages of EfficientNetB5 and the classification head are unfrozen, while the earlier convolutional layers retain their pretrained weights to preserve generalizable low-level filters and reduce the risk of overfitting on the relatively small medical imaging dataset. Experimental settings and results analysis

Results analysis

The high classification performance of proposed HyFusion-Net framework is because of strong hybrid feature representation and good classification strategy. Strict dataset partition and separate preprocessing make sure that training and validation datasets are fully separate, so model generalization on unseen data can be evaluated correctly. Several steps are used to reduce overfitting and ensure reliable performance evaluation. All preprocessing and feature extraction done after dataset partition, so no information leakage happens between datasets. Also, validation accuracy remains high and stable during training epochs, and no big differences between training and validation performance. In same way, validation loss remains low and stable, which shows strong generalization ability and low overfitting risk. This section presents experimental evaluation of the proposed HyFusion-Net model for brain tumor classification using MRI scans. In this study, many handcrafted feature extraction methods like Canny edge detection, SIFT, LBP, and HoG used to capture texture, edge, and structure information. Also, deep features extracted using DenseNet201 architecture to get high-level semantic features. Both handcrafted and deep features evaluated using different classification methods to check their effectiveness. For traditional machine learning, classifiers like Support Vector Machine (SVM)29, Random Forest30, Logistic Regression, and AdaBoost31 were used. While for deep learning comparison, architectures like ResNet5032, EfficientNetB5, and Xception33 were considered. All deep learning models trained using same hyperparameter settings to ensure fair and reproducible evaluation. Input image size fixed at 256 × 256 pixels. Models trained for 20 epochs with batch size of 32. Adam optimizer used with learning rate of 0.0001, and categorical cross-entropy used as loss function for multi-class classification. These hyperparameters selected to ensure stable training and reliable performance. All models trained and evaluated on same dataset partition to ensure fair and unbiased comparison. Model performance evaluated using multiple metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC, along with confusion matrix analysis. Complete experimental pipeline includes MRI preprocessing, handcrafted and deep feature extraction, and classification using same training and testing conditions to ensure reproducibility. All experiments performed in Google Collab using NVIDIA L4 GPU. Implementation done in Python 3.12.11 using TensorFlow 2.19.0, CUDA 12.5.1, OpenCV 4.12.0, Scikit-learn 1.6.1, NumPy 2.0.2, Pandas 2.2.2, Matplotlib 3.10.0, and Scikit-image 0.25.2.

To further improve reproducibility, the following implementation details are reported. All random number generators (Python random, NumPy, TensorFlow, and CUDA) are initialized with a fixed seed of 42 before each training run, so that the same data split, parameter initialization, and augmentation order are obtained on every execution. Data augmentation is applied only on the training set and includes random horizontal flipping (probability 0.5), small-angle rotation in the range ± 15°, brightness jittering in the range ± 10%, and width and height shifts up to 5%. The validation and test sets are not augmented or modified. Training uses early stopping with patience of 5 epochs monitored on validation loss, and the best model weights with lowest validation loss are restored before reporting evaluation metrics. The complete end-to-end training run takes approximately 38 min on a single NVIDIA L4 GPU with batch size 32. Pretrained weights are loaded from the official Keras Applications repository for both DenseNet201 and EfficientNetB5. The full source code, preprocessing scripts, and configuration files used to produce the reported results are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request, which allow independent reproduction of every experiment described in this study (Table 3).

Data leakage prevention and training protocol

To make sure the proposed framework is reliable and trustworthy, strict steps were taken to avoid data leakage and overfitting. The dataset was divided into training, validation and testing sets using fixed random seed, so that results can be reproduce again. The test set was separate and never used in model training, feature extraction tuning, or hyperparameter adjustment. All preprocessing steps like resizing, normalization and feature extraction were done after splitting the dataset. This was done to make sure no information from test set go into training process. Handcrafted features like LBP, HoG and Canny edge detection, and deep features using EfficientNetB5, were extracted separately for training, validation and testing sets. Data augmentation was applied only on training set to improve generalization of model. Validation and test sets were not augmented or modified. Hyperparameters were tuned using only training and validation sets. The test set was used only for final performance evaluation. The Random Forest classifier was trained only on features from training data. Then it was evaluated separately on validation and test data. This makes clear separation between training and testing and avoid data leakage. All these steps make sure that reported results truly show the model ability to work on unseen data.

Overview of feature extraction techniques

Canny edge detection

Canny edge detection (CED) is a multi-stage system that detects points, where the intensity changes sharply. It helps to show the boundaries of the object. It is commonly used for image segmentation purposes. In this work, the Canny edge detected features were utilized with Logistic Regression classifier, the model achieved accuracy of 83% with excellent precision, recall and F1-score. These results show that Canny features mainly focus on the boundary of the tumor and contain limited information. Even with simple features, the Logistic Regression model was able to classify the images with reasonable performance. But the models based on Canny features showed variation in results for different tumor classes, as shown in Fig. 5. The misclassification between Glioma and Meningioma was high because of the overlapping edge areas in some MRI slices. The ROC curve for this scheme in Fig. 6, achieved 95.5% of AUC, which shows that the model can still separate the classes to a good level.

Scale-invariant feature transform (SIFT)

SIFT functions as a descriptor that captures distinctive local features within an image that finds key points and captures high contrast areas like corners and blobs. These features are useful to represent the structure of the image with resized and rotation. In this study, the SIFT features were used with the Random Forest classifier, the proposed model obtained 86% of accuracy and 85% of F1-score. Unlike edge-based features, SIFT gives more detailed and local information that keeps the anatomical structure. The confusion matrix in Fig. 9 specifies that the HyFusion-Net model accurately identifies most of the tumor classes with strong accuracy. However, there was still some confusion between identifying Glioma and Meningioma, because of similar structures in some areas. Similarly, the SIFT features were considered with the EfficientNetB5, the accuracy and F1-socre increased to 97%. This improvement shows the strength of the EfficientNetB5 with SIFT features than machine learning classifiers for identified all possible tumor levels. Confusion matrix in Fig. 10 revealed minimal misclassifications, with particularly high precision in distinguishing between tumor classes. These findings confirm that, although SIFT is a traditional feature extractor, its combination with a high-capacity model like EfficientNetB5 can yield to highly valuable findings.

Local binary patterns (LBP)

Local Binary Pattern (LBP) is a texture descriptor that examines the relationship between a pixel and its neighboring pixels to capture local texture information. This makes it especially useful for analyzing medical images. In this work, LBP features were classified using Logistic Regression, where the model reached an accuracy of 84% and an F1-score of 83%. This shows that LBP is not very effective in capturing the small texture differences between different tumor types compared to other feature extraction methods. The confusion matrix in Fig. 11 showed that there were misclassifications mostly between Glioma and Meningioma tumors. But the ROC curve for the LBP model in Fig. 12, showed an average AUC of 0.95 which is still good for a texture-based feature. Similarly, the LBP features were used with EfficientNetB5, the accuracy increased to 94% and the model was able to identify the tumor classes better. The confusion matrix in Fig. 13 also showed strong results for all classes. These results show that integration of the LBP features with EfficientNetB5, still provide good information for accurate tumor classification.

Histogram of oriented gradients (HoG)

Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HoG) is a feature extraction method that captures the direction of gradients in small areas of the image. It helps to represent the shape and contour information and is useful for image classification tasks. In this study, the HoG features were used with Logistic Regression, the proposed model achieved 89% of accuracy and 89% of precision, recall and F1-score. These similar values illustrate that the proposed model obtained a balance classification and HoG features effectively represented the overall shape and structural details of the tumor within the MRI scans. The confusion matrix in Fig. 14, also demonstrates a reasonable performance e of the proposed model. However, there is a problem in separating Glioma and Meningioma. The ROC curve in Fig. 15, showed an AUC value of 98% which proves that HoG features has good discriminative power.

This shows that when HoG features are used with DL model, providing better results, because DL models can learn complex information from the gradient features. The confusion matrix in Fig. 16 showed promising results for all tumor classes and the proposed model was able to better separate Meningioma from Glioma compared to Logistic Regression. This shows that HoG features alone are not very powerful but when combined with DL models like DenseNet201, they become more effective in identifying the shape-based features important for accurate tumor classification.

DenseNet201 features

This hybrid model gave the best performance among all the feature extraction methods with 97% of accuracy. The precision, recall, and F1- score were also 97%, show promising results. The confusion matrix in Fig. 17 showed that almost all the samples were classified correctly. Original MRI images

To assess the classification performance using unaltered input data, We also tested the models using the raw MRI scans, where no handcrafted feature extraction methods were applied. In the machine learning setup, the raw intensities of each image were flattened into one-dimensional vectors and strengthened directly towards the classifiers. The configuration bypasses engineered features and allows us to evaluate how well conventional algorithms perform when relying solely on the intrinsic pixel-level information present in the scans. Among the classical models, the SVM achieved the highest performance, with an accuracy of 89% and F1-score of 89%, outperforming Logistic Regression (88%), Random Forest (87%), and AdaBoost (70%) under the same input conditions. The competitive results of Logistic Regression and SVM indicate that even simple linear or kernel-based models can capture broad intensity-level differences in MRI data, such as the contrast between tumor regions and healthy tissue.

In contrast, EfficientNetB5 was tested to check its ability to learn spatial and semantic features directly from input MRI images. After training, the EfficientNetB5 model achieved around 99% in many evaluation metrics, which shows very strong performance compared to traditional machine learning classifiers. Unlike traditional classifiers that depend on manually designed and fixed features, EfficientNetB5 can automatically learn multi-level spatial and semantic features directly from image pixels. This helps in better feature extraction and improved classification performance. As shown in Fig. 18, the training loss was decreasing continuously over eight epochs and reached very low value at the final epoch, which shows that model converged properly. The validation loss showed some small fluctuations, with slight increase after epoch 4, but overall it stayed low, which indicates stable generalization performance. In same way, the training accuracy increased very fast and went above 99%, while validation accuracy remained high between 97.5% and 98.5%. The confusion matrix in Fig. 19 shows that model achieved very accurate classification for all tumor categories, with very less misclassification between similar classes like glioma and meningioma. These results show that EfficientNetB5 successfully learned both low-level features such as edges and textures, and high-level structural and spatial patterns which are important for tumor classification. Although performance is very promising, more testing on bigger and more diverse datasets is still needed to fully evaluate model generalization ability in real clinical environments.

Occlusion map interpretation

The occlusion map experiment was done to see which parts of MRI images help the model most for classification decision. In this method, small parts of the image were covered one by one, and the change in model prediction confidence was checked. When the prediction confidence goes down after covering some region, it means that area has important information for classification. By doing this for whole image, an occlusion sensitivity heat map was created to show which regions are more important. The visual results of occlusion maps are shown in Fig. 21. In the heat maps, red and yellow color show the regions that have highest effect on model prediction, while blue and dark color show regions with less effect. In most of the cases, the highlighted areas are matching with tumor regions and important structural features. This shows that the proposed HyFusion-Net model is using meaningful parts of the image for classification and not depending on unnecessary background. Also, different tumor types like glioma, meningioma and pituitary tumor are showing different activation patterns in the occlusion maps. This means the model is able to learn different spatial and structural features for each tumor type. This visualization helps to understand the model decision by showing which image areas are influencing the prediction.

Grad-CAM and occlusion sensitivity maps showing that the proposed model focuses on clinically relevant regions. Warmer regions indicate higher contribution to model prediction, demonstrating interpretable and meaningful decision behavior.

However, it is important to note that the occlusion map analysis in this study is qualitative only and was not quantitatively validated using ground-truth tumor segmentation masks or expert clinical annotations. So, these explainability results should be used only as interpretability support, not as clinically validated tumor localization method. In future work, quantitative evaluation using expert annotations and segmentation datasets will be done to further check the clinical reliability of the proposed framework. Occlusion sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the spatial interpretability of the proposed model. This method systematically masks small regions of the input image and observes the corresponding change in prediction confidence, allowing identification of regions that contribute most to the classification decision. The results demonstrate that the model consistently focuses on clinically relevant anatomical structures rather than irrelevant background regions. In addition, Grad-CAM visualization was included to further validate spatial attention behavior. Both methods confirm that the proposed framework learns meaningful and interpretable feature representations. Quantitative localization evaluation was not performed due to lack of pixel-level annotations in the dataset. Future work will include evaluation on expert-annotated datasets using quantitative localization metrics.

Computational complexity and processing-time analysis

To evaluate computational efficiency of the proposed HyFusion-Net framework, training time and inference speed were measured using NVIDIA L4 GPU with TensorFlow 2.19. The average training time per epoch was about 32.7 s, and the full training process for 20 epochs took about 10.9 min. EfficientNetB5 contains approximately 28.5 million trainable parameters and is designed using compound scaling to achieve an effective balance between accuracy and computational efficiency. The inference time of the proposed framework was measured experimentally as approximately 6.8 ms per image on an NVIDIA L4 GPU. Memory usage during inference remained stable and within practical limits. These results demonstrate that the proposed framework maintains efficient computational performance while providing strong classification capability. During inference, the classification stage of HyFusion-Net required about 6.8 ms per MRI image, which shows fast and efficient prediction capability. It is important to note that preprocessing and handcrafted feature extraction steps are very lightweight and add very small extra computation compared to deep learning classification stage. Conclusion

This study proposes a hybrid deep learning framework called HyFusion-Net for brain tumor classification using MRI scans. The proposed method combines different handcrafted features like LBP, SIFT, HoG and Canny edge descriptors with deep features extracted from DenseNet2015. First, image preprocessing is done to improve image quality and remove noise. After that, handcrafted and deep features are extracted and fused together to capture both low-level texture details and high-level semantic information. Then these fused features are used to train the HyFusion-Net model for tumor classification. Experimental results show that the proposed HyFusion-Net achieved very high classification performance and outperformed traditional machine learning and deep learning methods, with around 99% accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score on the test dataset. These results show that hybrid feature fusion improve the discrimination power and robustness of classification. Also, occlusion-based explainability is integrated to give visual interpretation of the proposed model predictions by highlighting tumor regions important for decision, which improve transparency and interpretability of the model. Even though results are very promising, they should be taken with some caution because evaluation is done on a specific dataset only. More validation on larger, multi-institutional and clinically diverse MRI datasets is needed to check generalization ability and real clinical applicability of the framework. In future work, the proposed model needs to be tested on external datasets, explore more explainability methods, and be optimized the framework for reliable use in computer-aided diagnosis systems to support clinical decision making.
