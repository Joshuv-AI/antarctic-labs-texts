Boosting NO Oxidation in the Catalytic Peroxone Process over TiO2: The Key Role of Surface Sulfate
Authors: Weiwei Kong, Shule Zhang, Qin Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Developing facile TiO2 preparation methods is crucial for advancing the industrial implementation of a catalytic peroxone process for NO oxidation. In this study, three types of precursors, including titanium butoxide (Ti-BO), metatitanic acid (Ti-MTA), and titanyl sulfate (Ti-TOS), were used to synthesize TiO2. Remarkably, Ti-TOS and Ti-MTA exhibited enhanced activity compared to that of Ti-BO, with 93 and 87% NO oxidation efficiency (373 K, O3/NO = 0.4, H2O2/NO = 1.2, GHSV = 1.2 × 105 h–1), which is higher than that of Ti-BO (78%). Further investigations revealed that residual sulfate groups (SO4 2–) from precursors enhance Lewis acidity by withdrawing electrons from the Ti4+ sites, thereby facilitating H2O2 deprotonation and Ti-OOH formation. This accelerates radical generation for NO oxidation while inhibiting nitrate accumulation and weakening NO adsorption to optimize reaction kinetics. In addition, sulfates also inhibit the accumulation of oxidation product nitrates on the catalyst surface, thereby improving catalytic stability. This work reveals the promotion mechanism of SO4 2– on the catalytic peroxone process from multiple perspectives.

Introduction

Nitrogen oxides (NO x ), including nitric oxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2), are key contributors to a range of environmental issues such as photochemical smog, acid rain, haze, and the formation of secondary aerosols and PM2.5. These pollutants not only deteriorate air quality but also pose serious risks to human health and ecological systems. In recent years, under the impetus of national policies and advances in pollution control technologies, significant progress has been made in the reduction of NO x emissions in the power sector. By the end of China’s “13th Five-Year Plan” period, for instance, 86% of coal-fired power generation capacity achieved ultralow emission standards, reducing NO x emissions from 7.83 million tons in 2014 to 930,000 tons in 2019. , However, this success has highlighted persistent challenges in nonelectric sectors such as iron and steel, cement, and chemicals, where flue gas is typically emitted at lower temperatures, from smaller and more dispersed sources. These conditions limit the applicability and efficiency of conventional denitration technologies.

Mainstream denitration methods include selective catalytic reduction (NH3–SCR), − selective noncatalytic reduction (SNCR), and advanced oxidation processes (AOPs). NH3–SCR is effective in the range of 200–450 °C, but suffers from reduced efficiency and sulfur resistance at lower temperatures. SNCR, operating above 800 °C, is similarly unsuitable for low-temperature applications. Moreover, these reduction-based technologies rely on NH3, raising concerns about ammonia slip and energy waste under variable operating conditions. In contrast, oxidation-based methods offer several advantages for low-temperature NO x removal, including strong oxidizing potential and reduced temperature dependence. Among these, AOPs have attracted growing interest due to their controllability and ability to generate highly reactive oxygen species (ROS) through activation of precursors such as persulfate (PMS), sodium hypochlorite (NaClO), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) via light, heat, ultrasound, or catalysts.

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is regarded as a clean oxidant, decomposing to H2O and O2 after the reaction. Its HO–OH bond can be readily activated to generate highly oxidative radicals such as •OH and •OOH. However, its activation rate in heterogeneous catalytic systems (e.g., Fe3+/H2O2 (Fenton’s reagent) or UV/H2O2 systems , ) is strongly dependent on the pH values, and its application faces challenges like secondary metal pollution. Consequently, research has shifted toward heterogeneous catalytic systems that activate H2O2 on the catalyst to produce metal-peroxo (M–OOH) species. − The catalytic peroxone process, which synergistically combines H2O2 and ozone (O3), can generate •OH and •OOH radicals with a higher oxidation potential, enabling efficient NO oxidation without secondary pollution. NO oxidation

Particularly, •OOH plays a critical role in the catalytic peroxone process due to its selective reactivity with NO and lower oxidant consumption. Although •OOH has a lower redox potential than •OH, its direct conversion of NO to NO3 – in a single step makes it highly efficient under certain conditions. Nevertheless, the rate-limiting step in catalytic peroxone process is often the deprotonation of H2O2, which is pH-dependent and kinetically slow. − To address this, various metal oxides have been explored as catalysts for H2O2 activation. For example, mesoporous CeO x @SiO2, Mn–N4 single-atom catalysts, and TiO2-based catalysts have demonstrated promising performance. Among them, TiO2 is especially attractive due to its stability, low cost, and tunable surface properties. Nonmetal doping strategies, such as with F, S, or P, have further enhanced the formation of reactive Ti–OOH intermediates and improved NO conversion efficienciesreaching up to 98% in the case of F-doped TiO2 and 94% with P-doping under optimized conditions.

Despite these achievements, many high-performance TiO2 catalysts are synthesized from expensive precursors such as titanium butoxide via complex sol–gel or hydrothermal processes, limiting their scalability and industrial applicability. In real-world settings, TiO2 is more commonly prepared by simply calcining metatitanic acid (H2TiO3) or titanyl sulfate (TiOSO4), owing to the low cost and simplicity of the process.

In the present study, we explore the catalytic potential of TiO2 synthesized from three different commercial precursorstitanium butoxide (Ti-BO), metatitanic acid (Ti-MTA), and titanyl sulfate (Ti-TOS) via direct calcination. Remarkably, Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS exhibited superior performance in the catalytic peroxone process compared to Ti-BO. Through comprehensive characterizations including XRD, FT-IR, and NO-TPD-MS, we reveal that the enhanced activity stems from surface-adsorbed sulfate residues inherited from the precursors. These sulfate groups not only suppress competitive nitrate adsorption (a reaction byproduct) but also increase the Lewis acidity of surface Ti sites, thereby facilitating H2O2 activation and NO oxidation. This work provides new insights into the practical design of Ti-based catalysts and demonstrates that low-cost, scalable preparation methods can yield materials with high catalytic activity, offering a viable pathway toward the implementation of catalytic peroxone process in low-temperature denitration applications for the nonelectric industry.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

Pluronic F-127 (average M n ∼ 10,000–12,000, abbreviated as F-127) is purchased from Energy Chemical (ZeSheng Technology Co., Ltd.). Titanium butoxide (CP, 98%), titanium oxysulfate–sulfuric acid hydrate (93%, abbreviated as titanium oxysulfate), and metatitanic acid (98%) are purchased from Aladdin and used without further refinement.

Synthesis of Catalysts

All of the catalysts were synthesized by simple hydrolysis methods. The catalysts that were synthesized from titanium butoxide, titanium oxysulfate, and metatitanic acid were named Ti-BO, Ti-TOS, and Ti-MTA, respectively.

The Ti-BO was prepared as follows: The F-127 was used to modify the grain size. First, 0.064 mol (21.78 g) of titanium butoxide was dissolved in 70 mL of absolute ethanol. Then, 0.4 g of F-127 was added under continuous stirring until the powder was evenly dispersed in the solution. Next, 40 mL of deionized water is quickly added to the mixture. A milky white solution was obtained under vigorous stirring for 10 min. Under continuous stirring, the mixture was heated to 80 °C and became sticky. Then, the mixture was transferred to the oven and dried overnight under 80 °C. The powder was carefully ground and calcined at 500 °C for 3 h to obtain the final sample, which was denoted as Ti-BO.

The Ti-TOS was prepared by a thermal hydrolysis method. Typically, 0.075 mol (19.35 g) of TiOSO4·xH2SO4·xH2O and 0.15 mol (9.01 g) of urea are mixed and dissolved in 150 mL of deionized water. Then, the mixture was stirred vigorously at 90 °C for 3 h to decompose the urea and form a powder of TiO2. Next, the mixture was filtered and washed by deionized water. Then, the solid was dried in an oven at 120 °C for 12 h, and finally, the dried powder was ground and calcined at 500 °C for 3 h. The as-prepared sample was denoted as Ti-TOS.

The metatitanic acid was calcined at 500 °C for 3 h to directly obtain the Ti-MTA. All of the chemicals and reagents used for the synthesis and other analyses were of analytical grade (Aladdin, no additional refinement).

Experimental Device

The catalytic reaction simulation system consisted of three sections: simulated gas mixing, denitration, and gas composition analysis (Figure ). In the simulated gas mixing section, a gas stream (2.0 L·min–1, 400 ppm of NO) was generated using the following components: Mass Flow Controllers (CS200A, Beijing Sevenstar) regulated flows from a N2 cylinder (99.95%, Wenda Gas Co., Ltd.) and an NO cylinder (5% NO balanced in N2, Wenda). O3 was supplied by an O3 generator (AZCO HTU-500, Canada), with its concentration monitored by an O3 analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific 49i, USA). A 1 mol·L–1 H2O2 solution (diluted from 30 wt % H2O2) was injected into the reactor at 40 μL·min–1 via a peristaltic pump. Both O3 and H2O2 were introduced near the catalyst to prevent premature reaction.

Evaluation device for NO conversion by the catalytic peroxone process. Device for (a) gas distribution, (b) test, and (c) detection.

The denitration reaction section was comprised of a furnace and a fixed-bed reactor. Simulated flue gas was heated to the target temperature in the furnace, where H2O2 vaporized. The quartz tubular reactor (homemade, 8 mm inner diameter) contained 1 g of catalyst (20–40 mesh). The gas composition analysis section included two pathways: a main pipeline to a flue gas analyzer (Testo 350, Germany) and a branch pipeline to the O3 analyzer. Exhaust gas was absorbed in 30 wt % NaOH solution before emission. All reported data were collected after ≥1 h of operation under each reaction condition to ensure steady-state performance.

Catalyst Characterization

The morphology was studied by using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (JEOL, 2100f, Japan). The crystal phase composition of the synthesized catalysts was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a D2 Phaser diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) with Cu Kα radiation, scanning over a 2θ range of 10° to 80°. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was recorded using a Nicolet iS10 (Thermo Scientific, USA) instrument to identify functional groups. Surface chemical states and elemental composition were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) on a PHI-5000C ESCA system (PerkinElmer, America) with Al Kα X-rays (hν = 1486.6 eV); all binding energies were calibrated against the adventitious carbon C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) specific surface area measurements were determined on an automatic aperture analyzer (Micromeritics, ASAP 2460). All of the samples were dried at 150 °C for 12 h and degassed at 300 °C for 10 h before the test. The total specific surface area was determined by a multipoint BET method. The single-point pore volume was estimated from the amount adsorbed at a relative pressure of 0.99.

NO temperature-programmed desorption (NO-TPD) and NH3-TPD experiments were performed on an automated catalyst characterization system (Micromeritics, AutoChem II 2920, America) equipped with a TCD detector. In a typical procedure, about 100 mg of ground sample was added to the tube through a long-necked funnel. The pretreatment procedure heated the sample in argon (Ar) flow (50 mL·min–1) at a rate of 20 °C·min–1 for 30 min and then cooled down to 100 °C. Then, the preparation gas was changed to 2%O2/Ar or 2%NH3/He (50 mL·min–1) for 30 min to achieve full adsorption. Then, the carrier gas was changed to Ar for 30 min. After the TCD signal was stable, the sample was heated to 850 °C at a rate of 10 °C·min–1. In this process, the TCD and mass spectrometry (MS) signals were recorded. The MS signal was recorded using HPR-20 EGA (Hiden Analytical, Britain). A He-TPD&MS program was introduced to investigate the decomposition characteristics of sulfate on the surface. In a typical procedure, 30 mg of ground sample was loaded into the tube. In the pretreatment procedure, the sample was purged under He flow for 30 min. Then, it was heated to 850 °C at a rate of 10 °C·min–1 under a He atmosphere. In this process, the MS signal (m/z = 64) and TCD signal were recorded.

Results and Discussion

Structure Characterizations

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Ti-BO, Ti-TOS, and Ti-MTA are presented in Figure a. The prominent diffraction peak observed at 2θ = 25.2° corresponds to the (101) plane of anatase TiO2, consistent with the standard pattern (JCPDS No. 21–1272). The characteristic peak at 27.4°, which corresponds to the (110) plane of rutile (JCPDS No. 21–1276), was not observed in any of the samples. This absence indicates that all synthesized samples exhibit a pure anatase phase.

Structure characterizations for samples: (a) XRD patterns, (b) size distribution determined by TEM, and (c, e) HRTEM results of Ti-BO, Ti-MTA, and Ti-TOS.

Notably, the intensity of the (101) peak varied with different precursors. Among the three, Ti-BO displays the strongest diffraction intensity and the narrowest full width at half-maximum (FWHM), suggesting a higher degree of crystallinity. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to further estimate the particle sizes of the samples, and the results are summarized in Figure b. Ti-BO exhibited a significantly larger average particle size of 31.0 nm, in contrast to those of Ti-MTA (12.9 nm) and Ti-TOS (12.3 nm).

The representative TEM images shown in Figure c1–e1 reveal that all three samples possess granular morphologies. In agreement with the XRD and size distribution results, Ti-BO particles appear noticeably larger than those of Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS. Furthermore, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images of all samples reveal clear lattice fringes with an interplanar spacing of 0.35 nm, which corresponds to the (101) facet of anatase. This further confirms that all catalysts crystallize in the anatase phase with the (101) plane as the predominant exposed surface.

Catalytic Performance and Verification of the Synergistic Effect

The catalytic performance of the as-prepared TiO2 samples was evaluated, as presented in Figure . All the reactions were conducted under the following conditions: GHSV = 1.2 × 105 h–1 (1.0 g catalyst, total flow rate was 2 L·min–1), 373 K, molar ratio of H2O2/NO and O3/NO, 1.2 and 0.4, respectively. Details of the experimental procedure are provided in Section . Compared with our previous study where the molar ratio of O3 to NO was 0.5, this work further reduces the O3/NO ratio to 0.4. The initial NO concentration was set at 420 ppm, and the O3 flow was adjusted to achieve an approximate NO conversion of 40% in the absence of any catalyst.

Catalytic performance with the O3/H2O2 and Ti-X/O3/H2O2 system.

In the Ti-BO/H2O2/O3 system, the NO oxidation efficiency increased significantly, reaching 78%, indicating the effective role of the catalyst in promoting NO oxidation. Remarkably, when Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS were used instead of Ti-BO, the NO oxidation efficiencies further increased to 87 and 93%, respectively. These differences in the catalytic performance are noteworthy and suggest strong precursor-dependent activity.

In addition, compared with the noncatalytic O3 + H2O2 system, where the outlet NO x concentration was 446 ppm, the introduction of Ti-BO, Ti-MTA, and Ti-TOS led to substantial reductions in total NO x concentrations, reaching 303, 272, and 265 ppm, respectively. Previous studies have demonstrated that Ti–OOH species, formed through the interaction between TiO2 and H2O2, can react with O3 to generate •OH and •OOH radicals, which are key to enhancing NO x removal. These results suggest that Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS facilitate a stronger synergistic interaction between Ti-OOH and O3 compared to Ti-BO, thereby promoting more efficient NO x oxidation.

To further understand the origin of this enhanced catalytic performance, the following section investigates the fundamental factors responsible for the observed differences.

Catalyst Characterization

To evaluate the influence of textural properties on catalytic performance, N2 physisorption measurements were conducted. As shown in Figure a,b, all samples exhibit type IV isotherms with H3-type hysteresis loops, characteristic of mesoporous materials with slit-shaped pores. The corresponding textural parameters are summarized in Table . The BET surface areas follow the order: Ti-MTA (101.04 m2/g) > Ti-BO (68.26 m2/g) > Ti-TOS (48.21 m2/g). Notably, Ti-TOS, which possesses the smallest surface area, demonstrates the highest NO conversion efficiency. Conversely, Ti-BO and Ti-MTA, despite having comparable or larger surface areas, exhibit inferior activity. This clear discrepancy indicates that the specific surface area is not the primary determinant of the catalytic performance in this system. Furthermore, analysis of the pore structure reveals that Ti-TOS has a substantially lower pore volume (0.08 mL/g) and a narrower average pore diameter (6.43 nm) compared to Ti-BO and Ti-MTA. While larger pores are generally believed to enhance reactant diffusion and thus catalytic efficiency, the superior performance of the narrowly porous Ti-TOS further corroborates that factors beyond textural properties govern the activity.

To gain deeper insight into the physicochemical properties, Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analyses were employed. As shown in the FT-IR spectra (Figure c), all samples exhibited a broad absorption band near 1630 cm–1, attributed to the bending vibration of surface hydroxyl groups. A strong Ti–O–Ti stretching vibration band appeared in the 600–900 cm–1 range for all samples. Notably, additional absorption bands at 1050 and 1140 cm–1 were observed for Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS, which are assigned to S–O and Ti–O–S stretching vibrations, respectively, indicating the presence of surface-adsorbed sulfate species (SO4 2–). In the case of Ti-MTA, these residues are inherent to its metatitanic acid precursor, which is synthesized via an industrial sulfate process. The hydrolysis of titanyl sulfate (TiOSO4) leads to sulfate anions that form strong, covalent bonds with surface titanium atoms, resulting in their persistent presence.

XPS analysis was conducted to investigate the surface chemical states of the catalysts (Figure d). The Ti 2p spectra of Ti-BO exhibited two peaks at 458.5 and 464.3 eV, corresponding to Ti 2p3/2 and Ti 2p1/2, respectively. In contrast, Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS showed slight positive shifts (∼0.3 eV) in these binding energies, suggesting a reduced electron cloud density around the Ti4+ sites. This shift is likely due to the electron-withdrawing effect of the coordinated SO4 2– species on the TiO2 surface. The SO groups draw electron density from the Ti–O bonds, thereby enhancing the Lewis acidity of Ti4+ sites. It is worth noting that an increase in Lewis acidity facilitates the deprotonation of H2O2, which is a crucial step in the catalytic peroxone process. The S 2p spectra of Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS exhibited two characteristic peaks at 168.4 and 169.5 eV, corresponding to S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2, respectively.

The O 1s spectrum of Ti-BO could be deconvoluted into two peaks at 529.8 and 531.0 eV, attributed to lattice oxygen (Ti–O–Ti) and surface hydroxyl groups (Ti–OH), respectively. For Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS, these peaks shifted to 530.0 and 531.6 eV, and the relative area of the hydroxyl peak increased significantly, indicating a higher concentration of surface hydroxyl groups. These species may also contribute to the enhanced catalytic activity by providing more active sites for H2O2 activation.

Considering that both titanyl sulfate and metatitanic acid are industrially produced via the sulfate process, the presence of residual SO4 2– is likely due to precursor impurities. Given the enhanced catalytic performance of Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS, it is plausible that surface sulfate species play a critical role in promoting the activity. This hypothesis is further examined in the following sections.

Mechanism Analysis

Promotion Effect Analysis

In the preceding characterizations, the presence of surface sulfate species was confirmed on Ti-TOS and Ti-MTA, whereas no such species were detected on Ti-BO. This difference is likely a key factor contributing to the variation in the catalytic activity among the catalyst samples. In this section, the thermal stability of surface sulfate species was investigated via TPD-MS. As shown in Figure a, no SO2 signal was observed for Ti-BO over the entire temperature range, while a distinct SO2 desorption peak appeared at 451 °C for Ti-MTA, corresponding to the thermal decomposition of surface sulfate. Notably, the desorption peak for Ti-TOS shifted to an even higher temperature of 492 °C, suggesting stronger binding of sulfate species to the Ti-TOS surface compared to Ti-MTA.

Conversion of NO

Based on the TPD results, the effects of surface sulfate on H2O2 deprotonation and NO adsorption are discussed as follows:

As shown in Figure b, the desorption peak below 120 °C corresponds to weakly physically adsorbed NH3, and the peaks above this temperature are attributed to NH3 chemisorbed on Lewis acid sites with an increasing strength. Three broad desorption regions120–220 °C, 220–430 °C, and above 430 °Ccan be attributed to weak, medium, and strong acid sites, respectively. The total desorption intensity for Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS is significantly higher than that for Ti-BO, indicating that SO4 2– markedly enhances the surface acidity of the catalysts, which favors H2O2 deprotonation. Moreover, NO-TPD-MS results show that the notable reduction in total desorption area and the shift of the main desorption peak to a lower temperature for Ti-MTA and Ti-TOS indicate that surface sulfate species effectively weaken NO adsorption, which is conducive to rapid product desorption and active-site regeneration.

Desorption Behavior of the Catalyst before and after Reaction

To elucidate the promotion mechanism, NH3-TPD measurements were conducted on the catalysts before and after the reaction. Specifically, thermal desorption profiles were obtained for the fresh catalyst, the catalyst exposed to NO + O3 + H2O2, and the catalyst exposed to NO + SO2 + O3 + H2O2. The results revealed that when both NO and SO2 were introduced, a portion of SO2 was oxidized to SO4 2– and accumulated on the catalyst surface. A critical difference exists between the sulfate species introduced via the catalyst precursor and those deposited from gaseous sulfate SO2. The TPD-MS in Figure a provides direct evidence: sulfate from precursors generates distinct and sharp desorption peaksat 451 °C for Ti-MTA and 492 °C for Ti-TOSindicating that each precursor creates a specific type of sulfate with a defined bonding structure. In contrast, sulfate deposited from in situ SO2 oxidation yields a broad desorption profile spanning the ranges of both precursor-derived species, indicating a mixture of sulfate configurations. That is, the surface sulfate introduced by the precursor helps to distinguish the promotion of specific species on the reaction, while the sulfate species obtained by SO2 oxidation simulate the situation in real flue gas.

The corresponding NH3-TPD&MS shown in Figure b indicated that NO exposure alone did not significantly alter the acid site distribution of the catalyst, whereas SO2 exposure introduced a greater number of medium-strength acid sites. This enhancement in acidity is likely to facilitate the dissociation of H2O2.

He-TPD&MS (a), NH3-TPD&MS (b), and FT-IR spectra (c, d) of fresh and used catalysts.

Furthermore, FT-IR measurements were performed to investigate the surface species evolution before and after the reaction. As shown in Figure c, a new characteristic peak emerged at 1385 cm–1 after 1 h of reaction, corresponding to the accumulation of surface-bound nitrate species. , After 5 h of reaction, the intensity of this peak increased, indicating the continued accumulation of nitrate on the surface. In a subsequent experiment, NO was introduced during the first 2.5 h of reaction, followed by cofeeding of NO and SO2 for the remaining 2.5 h. Notably, after the introduction of SO2, no further accumulation of nitrate species was observed on the catalyst surface.

These findings suggest that SO2 addition during the reaction promotes the deposition of sulfate species on the catalyst surface. The presence of sulfates not only inhibits the adsorption and accumulation of nitrate but also enhances the acid strength of the Ti sites. This is attributed to a dual mechanism: the electron-withdrawing sulfate species enhance Lewis acidity, which promotes H2O2 deprotonation and accelerates the oxidation and desorption of nitrogen oxides, thereby shifting the pathway from nitrate storage to product release; concurrently, they compete with nitrate precursors for adsorption sites, further inhibiting nitrate stabilization.

Conclusion

In summary, TiO2 catalysts derived from different precursors demonstrate distinct performance in the catalytic peroxone process for NO oxidation, with Ti-TOS achieving superior NO oxidation efficiency (93%) compared to Ti-MTA (87%) and Ti-BO (78%) under identical conditions (373 K, GHSV = 1.2 × 105 h–1, O3/NO = 0.4, H2O2/NO = 1.2). This enhancement is primarily attributed to surface sulfate species (SO4 2–) inherent in sulfate-process precursors (Ti-MTA/Ti-TOS), which elevate Lewis acidity via electron withdrawal from Ti4+ sites. The strengthened acidity facilitates H2O2 deprotonation, which is a critical step for Ti-OOH formation and subsequent radical generation. Surface sulfate further optimizes the reaction dynamics by weakening NO adsorption to accelerate active-site regeneration and inhibiting nitrate accumulation. This work explores a simple synthesis method for Ti-based catalysts, hoping to provide guidance for the industrialization of this technology.
