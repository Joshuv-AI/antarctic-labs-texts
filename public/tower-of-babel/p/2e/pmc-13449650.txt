Identification of serpin B12 as an endogenous inhibitor of epidermal mesotrypsin and its possible role in epidermal desquamation during differentiation
Authors: Masashi Miyai, Mami Yamamoto, Akira Motoyama, Junichiro Hiruma, Yuko Matsumoto, Yukiko Matsunaga, Makiko Goto, Ryoji Tsuboi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The maintenance of stratum corneum homeostasis requires precise regulation of protease activity, particularly within the desquamation pathway. In this study, we identified serpin B12 as an inhibitor of mesotrypsin in stratum corneum extracts obtained from hyperkeratotic heel skin. liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry analysis identified serpin B12 as one of the most abundant candidates with known mesotrypsin-inhibitory potential, and recombinant serpin B12 suppressed mesotrypsin activity in a dose-dependent manner. To evaluate its functional relevance in epidermal tissue, we manipulated serpin B12 expression in reconstructed 3-dimensional skin equivalents. Knockdown and overexpression of serpin B12 resulted in marked alterations in stratum corneum thickness, indicating that serpin B12 influences the structural organization of the upper epidermis. Because mesotrypsin modulates kallikrein-related peptidases, which mediate corneodesmosome degradation and desquamation, these findings suggest that serpin B12 contributes to the modulation of stratum corneum homeostasis through the inhibition of mesotrypsin activity. Our results suggest that serpin B12 functions as an epidermal protease inhibitor and provide insight into the molecular mechanisms governing barrier maintenance.

Introduction

The skin serves to protect the body from the external environment and to maintain a constant internal environment (Eckhart and Zeeuwen, 2018). The outermost layer of skin, the stratum corneum, is constantly exposed to the external environment and undergoes regular renewal, so-called turnover, via the shedding of the outermost layer of corneocytes; this is known as desquamation (Watt, 1989).

Kallikrein-related peptidases (KLKs) are key players in corneocyte desquamation and are regulated by lympho-epithelial Kazal-type inhibitor (LEKTI; also known as serine protease inhibitor Kazal-type 5 [SPINK5])(Brattsand et al, 2005). The importance of the KLK-LEKTI system is supported by the fact that mutations in the SPINK5 gene are associated with Netherton syndrome, a severe skin disorder similar to atopic dermatitis (Fortugno et al, 2012, 2011).

Nakanishi et al (2010) previously cloned a PRSS3 gene product, keratinocyte-specific mesotrypsin, from a cDNA library. The intense immunostaining of mesotrypsin in the upper epidermis, especially in the granular layer, and the specific localization of its activator enzyme, enterokinase, in granular cells strongly suggest that mesotrypsin plays a role in keratinocyte terminal differentiation. Notably, mesotrypsin also activates kallikrein-related peptidases KLK5 and KLK7 (Miyai et al, 2014). Furthermore, mesotrypsin degrades LEKTI, releasing active KLKs from the KLK-LEKTI complex, which itself lacks protease activity. Thus, previous studies indicated that mesotrypsin contributes to desquamation through the activation of at least 2 key zymogens, pro-KLK5 and pro-KLK7, and also through the degradation of the potent multi-domain KLK inhibitor LEKTI. Consequently, once keratinocyte-specific mesotrypsin is activated, it initiates an activation cascade leading to desquamation.

Since the (meso) trypsinogen activator enterokinase is present in the granular layer, active mesotrypsin is expected to be generated in this layer. However, mesotrypsin has been considered resistant to known endogenous trypsin inhibitors, and no epidermal inhibitor with clear mesotrypsin inhibitory activity has been identified (Nyaruhucha et al, 1997). We hypothesized that such an inhibitor would be expressed in human epidermis to maintain homeostasis. To test this idea, we searched for inhibitors of mesotrypsin in corneocytes from stratum corneum extracts. We identified serpin B12 as a candidate inhibitor of mesotrypsin and examined its biochemical activity, its impact on stratum corneum structure using reconstructed 3-dimensional (3D) skin models, and its spatial localization within epidermal tissue.

Results

Identification of a mesotrypsin inhibitor from human corneocyte extract

Preliminary investigations indicated that extract from corneocytes obtained from human heel skin suppresses mesotrypsin activity in a dose-dependent manner. To identify the inhibitory component, the extract was subjected to preparative anion-exchange chromatography with Hiprep Q (Figure 1a), followed by hydroxyapatite CHT2-1 chromatography. This afforded 2 major protein peak fractions, of which the second showed potent mesotrypsin-inhibitory activity (Figure 1b). Cation-exchange chromatography of the latter peak on Mono S revealed that the highest inhibitory activity was present in the unadsorbed fraction (Figure 1c). Subsequent chromatofocusing on Mono P further separated the inhibitory activity into 2 peaks (Figure 1d). We decided to focus on the second peak, which showed higher activity. Superdex 75 gel chromatography afforded a broad protein peak with inhibitory activity (Figure 1e). SDS-PAGE revealed a protein band in the fraction exhibiting the inhibitory activity (Figure 1f). Preparation and characterization of recombinant serpin B12

To confirm that serpin B12 is a mesotrypsin inhibitor, we prepared the recombinant protein as a 70 kDa glutathione S-transferase (GST)-fusion protein (Figure 2a). The peak around 30 kDa is GST. The recombinant protein was purified by glutathione-agarose and anion-exchange Mono Q chromatography. Unfortunately, our attempts to remove GST from the fusion protein using PreScission protease were unsuccessful because the serpin B12 moiety was degraded by this enzyme. Thus, we used the intact GST-fusion protein after confirming that it was recognized by an anti-serpin B12 antibody. The recombinant protein inhibited mesotrypsin activity in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2b). Next, its kinetic properties were examined using the Michaelis-Menten method (Figure 2c). Effect of serpin B12 overexpression and knockdown in skin equivalent models

Skin equivalent models were prepared to examine the effects of serpin B12 on epidermal thickness. Prior to seeding, keratinocytes were transfected with a control vector or pCMV-HA/serpin B12 or treated with nonspecific small interfering RNA (siRNA) (control) or siRNA specific to serpin B12. Quantification of the epidermal layer thickness revealed marked alterations in response to the modulation of serpin B12 expression. The serpin B12-overexpressing model displayed pronounced stratum corneum thickening, reaching approximately 159% of the control value (Figure 3a and b). In contrast, the stratum corneum thickness in the serpin B12 knockdown 3D skin model was profoundly reduced to approximately 23% of that in the control model (Figure 3c and d). Furthermore, the overexpression model showed a modest increase in viable epidermal layer thickness to approximately 108% of the control (Figure 3a and b), whereas the knockdown model exhibited a reduction to roughly 77% of the control thickness (Figure 3c and d). These findings suggest that the modulation of mesotrypsin activity by serpin B12 affects not only the stratum corneum but also the viable cellular layers of the epidermis, with the most striking impact observed in the stratum corneum. Distribution of serpin B12 and colocalization of serpin B12 with mesotrypsin in human epidermis

We examined the distribution of serpin B12 in human epidermis using immunostaining (Figure 5). Serpin B12 immunoreactivity was predominantly detected in the upper layers of the epidermis, particularly around the granular layer. We next used proximity ligation assays (PLA) to explore whether serpin B12 might be positioned in close proximity to mesotrypsin, since serpin B12 suppresses the activity of mesotrypsin (Figure 2b). PLA signals were detectable when serpin B12 was probed together with mesotrypsin (Figure 6). Inhibition of caspase 14 by serpin B12 and their spatial proximity in epidermal tissue

We next investigated whether serpin B12 regulates caspase 14, an enzyme known to contribute to natural moisturizing factor production in the stratum corneum (Kamata et al, 2009). Caspase 14 not only contributes to natural moisturizing factor production but is also a key factor in denucleation during keratinization. Recombinant serpin B12 inhibited caspase 14 activity in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7a), demonstrating that serpin B12 can directly suppress caspase 14 activity. To further explore the spatial relationship between these proteins in the epidermis, we performed a PLA. PLA signals were detected when serpin B12 and caspase 14 antibodies were used in combination (Figure 7b). These qualitative findings support the possibility that serpin B12 and caspase 14 are positioned in close proximity within epidermal layers. Discussion

In the present study, we identified serpin B12 as an endogenous inhibitor of mesotrypsin. Nakanishi et al (2010) have already reported the cloning of keratinocyte-specific mesotrypsin and showed that it is a product of alternative splicing of the PRSS3 gene. Intense immunostaining of mesotrypsin in the upper epidermis, especially the granular layer, and specific localization of its activator enzyme, enterokinase (Light and Janska, 1989), in granular cells strongly suggest a role for mesotrypsin in keratinocyte terminal differentiation. Previous results also indicate that mesotrypsin contributes to desquamation through the activation of at least 2 key zymogens, pro-KLK5 and pro-KLK7 (Caubet et al, 2004), and also through degradation of the potent multi-domain KLK inhibitor LEKTI. Mesotrypsin also degrades LEKTI in the inactive KLK-LEKTI complex, releasing KLK (Miyai et al, 2014). Consequently, once keratinocyte-specific mesotrypsin is activated, it initiates an activation cascade of desquamation even in the presence of LEKTI. It has long been thought that mesotrypsin is not susceptible to endogenous trypsin inhibitors. However, the loss of LEKTI leads to a serious skin disease, Netherton syndrome (Fortugno et al, 2011; Miyai et al, 2014), suggesting that endogenous inhibitors of mesotrypsin should exist.

Serpin B12 has been reported to be expressed in the squamous epithelium and eccrine ducts of the skin (Niehaus et al, 2015). Nevertheless, its functional role within the skin remains largely unclear. In the present study, we identified serpin B12 in stratum corneum extracts as an inhibitor of mesotrypsin activity. Moreover, we found that recombinant serpin B12 suppressed mesotrypsin activity in a dose-dependent manner. In addition, PLA analysis provided qualitative evidence of spatial proximity between serpin B12 and mesotrypsin within epidermal tissue, suggesting that these proteins may be positioned sufficiently close together to support in situ regulation. Because mesotrypsin modulates the activity of KLKs (Miyai et al, 2014), which are essential mediators of corneodesmosome degradation and desquamation, our findings suggest that serpin B12, although acting indirectly, may serve as an important modulator in the corneocyte desquamation process in the epidermis.

Previous studies have reported that the stratum corneum tends to become thicker with age (Boireau-Adamezyk et al, 2014; Tagami, 2008), that trypsin-like protease activity in stratum corneum extracts declines in older individuals (Koyama et al, 1999), and that the amount of mesotrypsin in the stratum corneum decreases after midlife (Miyai et al, 2014). Because trypsin-like activity in the stratum corneum represents the collective contribution of multiple enzymes, including KLKs and mesotrypsin, age-associated reductions in overall protease activity may contribute to impaired desquamation and stratum corneum thickening. Our findings raise the possibility that serpin B12, identified here as an inhibitor of mesotrypsin, may be one of the factors influencing these age-related changes in stratum corneum structure and function.

In addition, we demonstrated that serpin B12 inhibits the activity of caspase 14, an enzyme involved in filaggrin degradation during the final stages of natural moisturizing factor production. Given the essential role of caspase 14 in both natural moisturizing factor generation and keratinocyte enucleation, the ability of serpin B12 to modulate this protease suggests that serpin B12 may also influence epidermal hydration and differentiation processes. Taken together, these observations indicate that serpin B12 could be an important contributor to age-related alterations in stratum corneum homeostasis and could be a factor in conditions such as xerosis in the elderly. Further investigation will be required to clarify the extent to which serpin B12 contributes to these age-associated changes relative to other regulatory factors in the stratum corneum. Caspase 14 has also been implicated in nuclear degradation during keratinocyte enucleation, a key step in the transition from granular cells to corneocytes. In our reconstructed 3D epidermal models, modulation of serpin B12 expression produced not only prominent alterations in the stratum corneum but also subtle changes within the living epidermal layers. These observations raise the possibility that serpin B12 may exert functions beyond the stratum corneum, potentially influencing caspase 14-related processes within the viable epidermis. Further studies will be necessary to clarify the extent to which serpin B12 contributes to both stratum corneum formation and earlier stages of epidermal differentiation.

In conclusion, we identified serpin B12 as a mesotrypsin inhibitor in stratum corneum extracts derived from hyperkeratotic heel skin. Because mesotrypsin governs the activation of KLKs, which drive corneodesmosome degradation and desquamation, these findings suggest that serpin B12 may contribute to the maintenance of stratum corneum homeostasis through modulation of mesotrypsin-dependent pathways. However, there may be additional regulatory factors in the stratum corneum that influence mesotrypsin activity besides serpin B12, and further studies will be required to clarify the relative contribution of serpin B12 among these regulatory components.

Materials and Methods

Preparation of stratum corneum extracts and tissue specimens

Human heel stratum corneum extract was obtained from stratum corneum that had been stripped by rubbing using a beauty instrument, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (Permission number: C01006). Written informed consent was obtained from healthy individuals to publish the results of the experiments using the samples provided. The scraped heels were collected from 14 male and 12 female healthy subjects. The scraped heel stratum corneum extract was prepared by dissolving it in Stratum Corneum Extraction Buffer (0.1 M Tris-HCl [pH 8.0] / 0.14 M NaCl / 0.1% [w/v] Tween20) with a Tenbroeck Tissue Grinder on ice. It was then used in a mesotrypsin inhibitor discovery experiment.

Human skin tissue was obtained from CTIBIOTECH and BIOPREDIC International, with KAC Company Limited as the agent, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (Permission numbers: C10303 and C10304). Written informed consent was obtained from healthy individuals by CTIBIOTECH and BIOPREDIC International, which obtained the samples for the publication of the results of the experiments using the provided samples. Skin tissue samples were obtained from healthy female donors in their 30s to 50s. The cohort consisted of 3 donors in their 30s, 7 donors in their 40s, and 7 donors in their 50s, with a mean age of 48.9 years.

Isolation of endogenous mesotrypsin inhibitor

Extracts of human corneocytes from the heels of healthy individuals were screened for inhibitory activity towards various proteinases, including mesotrypsin. The active extracts were subjected to Hiprep 16/10 Q XL anion-exchange (Cytiva, Tokyo, Japan), CHT2-I hydroxyapatite (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Incorporated Hercules, California), Mono S 5/50 GL cation-exchange (Cytiva), Mono P chromatofocusing (Cytiva) and finally Superdex 75 gel chromatography (Cytiva). The purified inhibitor-containing fraction was analyzed by nano-flow liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry for protein identification, as described below.

Identification of the mesotrypsin inhibitor

The final preparation obtained from Superdex 75 gel chromatography (fraction number 12) was subjected to nano-flow liquid chromatography coupled with electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry analysis after trypsin digestion, as described previously (Motoyama et al, 2007, 2006). Briefly a lab-made nano-flow liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry platform consisting of a Nanospace SI-2 high-performance liquid chromatograph (Shiseido Company Tokyo, Japan), a Yates lab-designed nano-electrospray ionization source, and an LTQ-Orbitrap hybrid mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Corporation, Carlsbad, California, USA) were used to obtain tandem mass spectra of tryptic peptides. The resulting tandem mass spectra were analyzed using the SEQUEST algorithm against a non-redundant reviewed human protein database (UniProtKB/Swiss-Prot, SwissProt_Homo sapiens_review_20210301.fasta; UniProt Consortium, 2021) to identify candidate proteins. Representative search constraints were as follows: precursor mass tolerance, 10 parts-per-million; fragment mass tolerance, 0.6 dalton; target FDR, 0.01, for fully tryptic peptides with no more than 2 missed cleavage sites.

Preparation of recombinant protein

Recombinant serpin B12 was prepared as a GST-fusion protein. Serpin B12 cDNA was PCR-cloned from confluent cultured keratinocytes, inserted into the pGEX-6P1 vector, and expressed in E. coli. The primers used were: forward, 5′- TTTGCTAGCTAGTGCACTCGCACTTAC-3′ and reverse, 5′- GTTGCATGCATGTCCCCTATACTAGGTTA-3′. The recombinant protein was purified by glutathione-agarose (Cytiva) and Mono Q ion-exchange chromatography. Since the GST moiety could not be removed by PreScission protease (Cytiva) without degrading the serpin moiety, we used the fusion protein as recombinant serpin B12.

Enzyme assays

Recombinant active mesotrypsin was incubated alone or with recombinant serpin B12 in the assay buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.1% Tween-20, 1 mM CaCl2) at 37 °C for 30 minutes. A fluorogenic substrate, Boc-Gln-Ala-Arg-MCA, for mesotrypsin (Peptide Institute, Osaka, Japan) was then added to the assay mixture, and the resulting fluorescence was measured using a 2030 Multilabel Reader ARVO X3 (Perkin Elmer, Waltham, Massachusetts) with excitation at 355 nanometers and emission at 460 nanometers.

Culture of keratinocytes

Human keratinocytes derived from normal foreskin (Kurabo, Osaka, Japan) were cultured in EpiLife (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific Corporation, Carlsbad, California, USA) supplemented with epidermal growth factor (0.1 ng ml-1), insulin (10 μg ml-1), hydrocortisone (0.5 μg ml-1), bovine pituitary extract (0.4%), gentamicin (50 μg ml-1)3, and amphotericin B (50 ng ml-1). All experiments were carried out on cells at the third or fourth passage. Keratinocytes were collected at 80% confluence (growth phase) and used to construct skin equivalent models.

Overexpression

To construct the expression plasmid for serpin B12, the serpin B12 open reading frame (NM_080474) was amplified by PCR using primers (5′- CTCAAGCTTCGAATTCATGGACTCTCTTGTTACAGC -3′ and 5′- GTCGACTGCAGAATTCTTAAGGAGAGCAGACCCTG -3′), and the resultant fragment was inserted into the pCMV-HA vector (Clontech, Mountain View, California) after digestion with SalI and XhoI.

Human keratinocytes grown in type I collagen-coated 24-well plates to 80% confluence were transfected with pCMV-HA control plasmid or pCMV-HA/serpin B12 using FuGENE HD transfection reagent (Promega, San Luis Obispo, Caalifornia). Cells were collected at 100% confluence after 24 hours.

siRNA-mediated knockdown

Keratinocytes were transfected with 20 nanomolar siRNA against serpin B12 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific Corporation, Carlsbad, California, USA) and a nonspecific control (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, Texas) using Lipofectamine RNAi MAX (Invitrogen). At 24 hours posttransfection, the cells were used to construct skin equivalent models.

Construction of skin equivalent models

A 3D Keratinocyte Starter Kit (CELLnTEC Advanced Cell Systems AG, Bern, Switzerland) was used to construct the skin equivalent models. First, NHEKs were cultured and suspended in CnT-Prime medium at passaging. The cells were seeded onto the provided cell inserts at a density of 2 × 105 cells per insert. Cultures were grown for 3 days until they were confluent in the insert, after which the culture medium was replaced with 3D Barrier Medium (CnT-PR-3D). The insert was also filled with 3D Barrier Medium for 24 hours, after which the medium was removed, and the surface side was exposed to air for 12 days. During this period, the 3D Barrier Medium was changed every 2 days to maintain the culture.

For serpin B12 overexpression or knockdown in skin equivalent models, transfection was performed on the day before passage in the NHEK preparation stage, and expression constructs or siRNAs were introduced as appropriate. For H&E staining, skin equivalent models were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subsequently stained with H&E.

Real-time RT-PCR analysis

The completed skin equivalent models were collected by separating the membrane from the skin model. Total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Life Technologies) and subjected to reverse transcription using oligo (dT) primers. The resulting cDNAs were analyzed using a LightCycler 480 System (Roche Applied Science, Indianapolis, Indiana). The primer set used for serpin B12 was as follows: forward primer, CTGGGTTGAATGTCAATCCC; and reverse primer, CACCGTGTTTTCATGGTCAA. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase was used as a reference gene. The primer set used for Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase was as follows: forward primer, GAAGGTGAAGGTCGGAGTC; and reverse primer, GAAGATTGGTGATGGGATTTC.

Immunohistochemical procedures

Skin equivalent models and human skin tissue were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin for sectioning. Immunostaining was carried out using antitrypsin, anticaspase 14, antiserpin B12, antikeratin 14, antikeratin 10, and antiloricrin antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). DAPI (4’,6’-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Molecular Probes) was used to visualize nuclei. Alexa Fluor 594 or 488 (Molecular Probes Incorporated, Eugene, Oregon) was used for fluorescence detection. Protein-protein interactions were detected using the Duolink In Situ Fluorescence assay (Sigma-Aldrich), based on the PLA principle, following the manufacturer’s recommended protocol.

Caspase 14 assay

The fraction obtained in a previous report was used as active caspase 14 (Hibino et al, 2010). Caspase 14 activity was measured using Ac-WEHD-7-amino-4-trifluoromethylcoumarin as a substrate. The assay buffer consisted of 0.1 molar HEPES buffer (pH 7.5), 0.06 molar NaCl, 0.01% CHAPS, 5 millimolar DTT, and 1.2 molar sodium citrate (final concentration). The procedure for measuring the activity of caspase 14 was as previously reported (Hibino et al, 2010).
