The Through-&-Through Technique: A Safe Bailout Access Route for Difficult Aortic Arch Anatomy During Thrombectomy
Authors: Johan Wasselius, Emma Hall, Lovisa Sandell, Alex Szolics, Björn Hansen
Published: 2026

Introduction

Endovascular thrombectomy (EVT) for acute ischemic stroke requires prompt, stable access to the precerebral vessels. Procedural success rate has increased over time and the [1] groin-to-reperfusion times has been shortened, but access failure remains a non-negligible cause of procedural failure, reported in up to 5% of cases [2–4]. These cases frequently involve extreme aortic arch elongation, bovine or aberrant origins, or hostile ilio-femoral anatomy [5].

Alternative access routes [6], include direct carotid puncture that carry higher complication rates [7, 8], or radial/brachial access that may not improve access [9], especially in cases with challenging aortic arch anatomy.

We describe a practical through-&-through carotid–femoral access technique, adopted from other fields of interventional radiology [10–14], that combines the safety of micropuncture carotid puncture with the stability of femoral guidance, enabling completion of EVT when conventional access is not otherwise possible.

Technique Description

Two of the procedures were performed with the patient under general anesthesia and one procedure with the patient in conscious sedation and subcutaneous Mepivacaine as local anesthetic at the cervical puncture site.

Retrograde Carotid Puncture

Under ultrasound guidance and in general and/or local anesthesia, the common carotid artery is percutaneously punctured with a 21-gauge micropuncture needle, close to the bifurcation and avoiding carotid plaques. The needle is then exchanged over the wire by the inner-sheath of a micro-puncture introducer set (outer diameter 0.035-inch). An 0.018-inch wire (V-18 ControlWire 0.018-inch, 300 cm length, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, USA) is then advanced retrogradely into the CCA, towards the aortic arch.

Snaring the Wire to Establish Through-&-Through Access

A tri-looped large snare (EN-snare, diameter 12–20 mm, length 120 cm; Merit Medical, South Jordan, USA) with an accompanying 6 French delivery catheter (O.D. 0.083-inch, length 100 cm) is inserted via the 9 French femoral introducer or balloon-guide catheter (Merci BGC, Stryker, Salt Lake City, USA). The tri-looped snare is opened in the aortic arch at the level of the origin of the left CCA by retracting the delivery catheter. The 0.018-inch wire is then advances into the aortic arch, passing through the exposed loops of the snare, and captured by retracting the snare into its delivery catheter, thereby fixating the wire in the snare, creating the through-&-through access. It is possible—but not necessary—to externalize the 0.018-inch wire by gently retracting the snare from the femoral sheath while advancing the wire from the carotid puncture site.

Balloon-Guide Advancement

Over the through-&-through 0.018-inch wire, the 9 French balloon-guide catheter is then advanced to the carotid bifurcation. The ICA is then catheterized using a side-by-side 0.038-inch guide-wire before the through-&-through wire is removed. The balloon-guide catheter is then advanced to the ICA over the 0.038-inch guide-wire. The micropuncture inner-sheath is preferably left in place and removed at the end of the procedure.

Reperfusion

After removal of the through-&-through 0.018-inch wire, EVT is performed as originally planned.

Closure of the Cervical Puncture Site

After completing the EVT, the balloon-guide catheter is retracted to the distal CCA. The micropuncture inner-sheath is then removed from the CCA and gentle manual compression is applied for a couple of minutes to achieve hemostasis. The puncture site is then assessed angiographically to rule out extravasation, dissection, or other complications at the CCA puncture site before ultimately removing the balloon-guide catheter and closing the femoral puncture site.

Patient- and Procedure Characteristics

Patient Characteristics

All three patients (ages: 80–91 years) had severe ischemic strokes with National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale scores of 12–20 points due to occlusions in the left side anterior circulation. Two of the patients had occlusions of the dominant M2-segment of the middle cerebral artery, and one patient had an occlusion of the distal ICA (subterminus/ICA-I).

One patient had a type III aortic arch with a separate origin of the left CCA with a severely stenosis at the origin and a steep curve shortly after the origin (Fig. 2a, e). Two patients had type II aortic arches with common ostium for the left ICA and the brachiocephalic trunk (i.e. bovine arch) and several steep curves in the CCA shortly after its origin (Fig. 2b–d, f, g). Radial or brachial access could have been an alternative access in at least one patient with a type III aortic arch, but that patient had a very thin radial artery and even if radial access had been feasible it does not accommodate 9 French balloon-guide catheters, used as first line strategy in our practice.

The intracranial occlusions were successfully recanalized with a combined stent retriever and direct aspiration thrombectomy using the SAVE-technique in two of the patients. In the third patient we were unsuccessful to pass a tandem lesion in the proximal part of the left ICA, and after multiple navigation attempts the decision was eventually made to end the procedure.

At completion of all procedures the micropuncture inner-sheath was removed from the CCA and manual compression applied for 3–5 min. The puncture site was angiographically controlled from the balloon-guide catheter in all cases without signs of extravasation before ending the procedure and closing the femoral access.

Follow-up

None of the patients had a postoperative complication related to the cervical access site during inpatient care (1–33 days). No complications were detected at the review the regional picture archiving system or electronic patient journals, during the follow-up period after discharge (13–76 days).

Discussion

Principal Findings

The through-&-through technique offers a reliable bailout route for EVT in patients with hostile arch anatomy when conventional access routes fail. Establishing a controlled carotid–femoral through-&-through access route provides stability comparable to conventional transfemoral access while avoiding the risk of morbidity associated with antegrade carotid puncture with 6 French introducers or larger [7, 8].

Comparison with Existing Alternatives

Direct antegrade CCA puncture has been proposed as a last resort, but entails risks of pseudoaneurysm, hematoma, and cranial-nerve injury [6–8] and may be difficult due to the clavicle and neck habitus. Brachial and radial routes typically fail to deliver sufficient support for large-bore systems such as 9 French balloon-guide catheters used by us and many centers. In contrast, our approach allow access from the femoral route by adding only a micropuncture entry at the CCA, minimizing trauma and risk for complications at the neck.

The through-&-through technique may be more complex and time-consuming than direct antegrade sheath introduction into the common carotid artery and the choice of bail-out strategy should therefore be decided on a case-by-case basis considering the unique aspects of the individual patient.

Safety and Reproducibility

All three patients in our series tolerated the procedure without adverse events. While our follow-up was clinical, no delayed complications were noted. With growing operator familiarity, this technique could be adopted as part of the bailout repertoire for EVT, carotid stenting, and other supra-aortic interventions. With further adaptation, alignment to standards for innovation such as the IDEAL framework may be beneficial for rigor [15].

Limitations

Small sample size and absence of systematic imaging follow-up limit generalizability. Further multicenter evaluation could clarify long-term safety and success rates.

Conclusion

The through-&-through technique provides a straightforward, minimally invasive bailout option when conventional endovascular access fails. In selected cases it may overcome otherwise failed access due to challenging vascular anatomies.
