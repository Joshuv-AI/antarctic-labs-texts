Functional property enhancement of walnut flower melanin by lysine modification: Structural characterization, stability, and bioactivity studies
Authors: Wenhui Liang, Bochao Li, Qing Zhou, Jianbing Di
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study utilized an ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction and acid precipitation approach to isolate WFM, which was then modified with Lysine to produce Lysine-modified WFM (L-WFM). The experimental results demonstrated that the maximum extraction yield of WFM reached 16.13 % under optimized conditions: 0.80 mol/L NaOH, 82 W ultrasonic power, 60 min ultrasonic time, 1:25 (w/v) solid-to-liquid ratio, and 75 °C ultrasonic temperature. Lysine modification induced a 3 nm blue shift in the UV–vis absorption maximum of WFM, transformed its morphology from irregular aggregates to smooth crystalline flakes, reduced thermal stability, and significantly increased the color value of WFM. Notably, compared to WFM, L-WFM exhibited a significant increase in color value, along with markedly improved solubility, thermal, light, and pH stability. Additionally, its antioxidant and antimicrobial activities were also significantly enhanced. This study provides a theoretical basis for improving the functional properties of WFM. Introduction

Melanin is a of high-molecular-weight compounds formed through the oxidative polymerization of indole-based monomers or phenolic substances. Featuring a highly conjugated aromatic structure, it exhibits extremely rich structural and functional diversity (Sunil Kumar et al., 2025). In addition, melanin is the most widely distributed and abundant pigment among known biological pigments. It is ubiquitously found in plant seeds, berries, petals, and animal hair, serving as the key determinant of black to dark brown coloration in organisms (Sunil Kumar et al., 2025). Furthermore, it exhibits diverse biological functional activities, including antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, hypoglycemic, liver protective, DNA-protective, lipid-regulating, and antioxidant effects (Sunil Kumar et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023). Based on these properties, purified melanin holds significant promise as a multifunctional food ingredient. It functions not only as a natural pigment that imparts stable coloration to food products, but also provides intrinsic preservation through its inherent antioxidant and antimicrobial activities. This dual functionality positions melanin as a key material for developing clean-label foods, where a single component achieves synergistic effects of coloring, preservation, and quality enhancement. Consequently, it demonstrates distinctive advantages for applications in functional foods and innovative health products.

Walnut (Juglans regia L.), a globally significant tree nut, holds substantial importance in the food industry owing to its rich nutritional composition and broad consumer acceptance. Walnut kernels are extensively incorporated into a variety of food products such as baked goods, snacks, confectionery, and plant-based dairy alternatives (Zhang et al., 2020). Additionally, walnut oil is highly valued in gourmet cooking and premium dressing formulations due to its distinctive flavor and lipid profile (Zhang et al., 2020). Furthermore, the walnut flower is rich in bioactive compounds such as proteins, polyphenols, and melanin, demonstrating significant potential for development as functional food ingredients and natural pigments (Zhang et al., 2020). Nevertheless, in the processing of walnut industry, walnut flowers are commonly discarded as agricultural by-products, resulting in the underutilization of their inherent nutritional value and bioactive properties, which constitutes a substantial waste of valuable biological resources (Yan et al., 2019). Therefore, establishing an efficient comprehensive utilization system for walnut flower resources is imperative. Notably, to date, there have been no reported studies on the extraction and functional characterization of walnut flower melanin (WFM), leaving a critical knowledge gap in the valorization of this agricultural by-product. In this context, the development of efficient strategies to utilize walnut flower melanin resources thus represents an important research direction with significant implications for sustainable food ingredient development.

To address this knowledge gap and facilitate the valorization of walnut flowers, the development of an efficient extraction method for walnut flower melanin (WFM) becomes crucial. The selection of an appropriate extraction technique is paramount, as it directly influences the yield, purity, and ultimately the functional properties of the obtained melanin (Song et al., 2023). The conventional alkaline extraction and acid precipitation method is widely used for pigment extraction due to its simple operation and low cost, but this method has significant drawbacks including low extraction efficiency and inadequate product purity (Song et al., 2023). In recent years, with the advancement of extraction technologies, a series of novel extraction methods, including ultrasound-assisted extraction, have emerged as promising approaches in the field of natural pigments (Zhang et al., 2025). Among these methods, the ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction-acid precipitation technique permeability disrupts plant cell walls via ultrasonic cavitation, significantly improving solvent permeability (Yang et al., 2023). At the same time, the thermal effect of ultrasound can promote the dissolution and release of natural pigments (Yang et al., 2023). However, the application of ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction-acid precipitation for WFM extraction and its process optimization has rarely been reported.

Excellent solubility and stability represent critical factors for melanin to maintain its functional activity during food processing and storage (Yang et al., 2023). However, the unique solubility of natural melanin (soluble in alkaline environment, insoluble in acidic environment, organic solvents and water) greatly limits its application in industry, agriculture and medicine (Li et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023). Interestingly, structural modification and functionality modification of melanin to enhance its chemical properties has emerged as a significant research direction in current food science. Among these approaches, amino acid modification has been demonstrated as an effective strategy to enhance the functional properties of melanin (Ye et al., 2012; Ye et al., 2019). Notably, the -NH2 and -COOH groups in Lysine can form covalent bonds with the quinoid structures and phenolic hydroxyl groups in melanin respectively, while its extended aliphatic side chain effectively enhances the hydrophilicity of melanin molecules (Han et al., 2024). This makes Lysine one of the most commonly used amino acids in melanin modification. A proposed schematic diagram of the possible chemical reactions between Lysine and melanin is shown in Fig. 1A.

Building on this foundation, Lysine-modified melanin holds substantial promise for food applications. The enhanced water solubility of Lysine-modified melanin significantly broadens its application scope in aqueous-based food systems, including beverages, dairy products, and functional liquid formulations (Han et al., 2024). Meanwhile, its improved stability under varying pH conditions enables wider utilization across diverse processed foods (Han et al., 2024;Ye et al., 2012; Ye et al., 2019). Furthermore, Lysine-modified melanin serves as a multifunctional ingredient capable of providing simultaneous coloration, antioxidant protection, and antimicrobial activity within a single component. These integrated functionalities position Lysine-modified melanin as a promising candidate for developing clean-label foods with extended shelf life and enhanced quality attributes, creating new opportunities for sustainable food production (Ye et al., 2012; Ye et al., 2019).

Therefore, this study systematically investigated the optimization of WFM extraction using ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction and acid precipitation, followed by Lysine modification to prepare L-WFM. The physicochemical properties were comprehensively characterized by multiple analytical techniques including UV–vis, FTIR, XRD, TG-DSC, XPS, SEM, and elemental analysis. Furthermore, the color value, solubility, stability, as well as in vitro antioxidant and antimicrobial capacities of L-WFM were evaluated. This work provides fundamental theoretical insights for both extraction process optimization and functional improvement of WFM, while establishing a novel technical approach for the valorization of walnut flower resources.

Material and methods

Materials and reagents

The walnut flowers were harvested from Juglans regia “Jinlong 1” walnut trees (Zuoquan County, Jinzhong City, Shanxi Province, China). Tyrosine, Lysine, Arginine, Leucine, Cysteine, Glycine, Proline, and Cystine were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Other chemical reagents used in this study were of analytical grade and were purchased from China National Pharmaceutical Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Extraction of WFM

The WFM was extracted following the method described by Sunil Kumar et al. (2024) with minor modifications. Specifically, the walnut flowers were oven-dried at 50 °C, pulverized using a high-speed grinder (400Y, Yongkang Baiou Hardware Products Co., Ltd., China), and sieved through a 100-mesh sieve for subsequent use. Then the walnut flower powder was mixed with a certain volume and a certain concentration of NaOH solution, and placed in a numerical control ultrasonic cleaner with a certain ultrasonic temperature and ultrasonic power (KQ5200DE, Kunshan Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., China) for a certain time of ultrasonic extraction. After cooling, 8000 ×g was centrifuged for 15 min to take the supernatant. The collected supernatant was adjusted to pH 2 with 6 mol/L HCl and then bathed at 80 °C for 12 h. After cooling, 8000 ×g was centrifuged for 15 min and the precipitate was collected. After adding 40 mL of 6 mol/L HCl solution into the precipitate, the precipitate was boiled for 1 h, filtered after cooling, and then washed with chloroform: isoamyl alcohol mixed solution (1:5), ethanol, ethyl acetate, petroleum ether and water for 5 times to purify the melanin precipitate. After vacuum freeze-drying for 72 h, the WFM solid powder was obtained and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for use.

Single factor test

NaOH concentration (0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.25, 1.5 mol/L), ultrasonic power (60, 70, 80, 90, 100 W), ultrasonic time (40, 50, 60, 70, 80 min), ratio of walnut flower to NaOH solution (1:20, 1:25, 1:30, 1:35, 1:40), ultrasonic temperature (50, 60, 70, 80, 90 °C) were used as single factors.The fixed conditions of other factors were 1 mol/L NaOH solution, ultrasonic power of 80 W, ultrasonic time of 60 min, solid-liquid ratio of 1:30, and ultrasonic temperature of 70 °C. The yield of WFM was used as a reference index to explore its effect on the yield of WFM.

Screening of amino acids for WFM modification

The types of WFM-amino acid modifications were screened according to the method of Ye et al. (2012). Briefly, precisely weigh 0.01 g each of Tyrosine, Lysine, Arginine, Leucine, Cysteine, Glycine, Proline, and Cystine, along with 0.01 g WFM, and dissolve in 1.5 mL distilled water. After vortexing for 20 min at room temperature, centrifuge at 5000 ×g for 10 min and retain the supernatant. Measure the absorbance at 500 nm to evaluate amino acid-modified melanin solubility. Meanwhile, 0.01 g WFM was weighed and dissolved in 1.5 mL distilled water as the blank control group.

Determination of lysine-WFM modification concentration

Based on the results of amino acid-WFM modification types, Lysine was selected to modify WFM, and the optimal modification concentration of Lysine was determined. Specifically, 0.01 g of WFM was dissolved in 1.5 mL of distilled water, and Lysine was then added at different masses corresponding to WFM:Lysine mass ratios of 1:0.5, 1:0.75, 1:1, 1:1.25, 1:1.5, 1:1.75, 1:2, 1:2.25, 1:2.5, 1:2.75, and 1:3 in sequence. The Lysine-WFM modification solution was shaken for 20 min, centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The absorbance was measured at 500 nm, and the optimal concentration of Lysine for modifying WFM was determined based on the maximum absorbance value (Ye et al., 2012).

Preparation of lysine-walnut flower melanin (L-WFM)

The Lysine-modified walnut flower melanin (L-WFM) was prepared according to the method described by Ye et al. (2012). Briefly, 5 g of purified WFM and 12.5 g Lysine were dissolved in 500 mL distilled water to prepare the Lysine-WFM solution. The mixture was shaken at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 5000 ×g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. The obtained supernatant was then dialyzed against ultrapure water for 48 h (with water change every 4 h). After vacuum freeze-drying for 72 h, the L-WFM solid powder was obtained and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for use.

Characterization of L-WFM

UV–Vis spectral detection (UV–Vis)

The UV–visible spectra of WFM and L-WFM were scanned according to the method of Niu et al. (2024). Specifically, both WFM and L-WFM were separately dissolved in 0.1 mol/L NaOH to prepare 0.1 mg/mL solutions. The absorbance of the solutions was then scanned from 200 to 800 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Lambda 35, PerkinElmer Ltd., MA, USA) in triplicate (n = 3). Their maximum absorption wavelengths (λmax) were determined from the averaged spectra of the three independent replicates.

Detection of fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

The Fourier transform infrared spectra of WFM and L-WFM were scanned according to the method described by Ye et al. (2012). Specifically, WFM and L-WFM powders were separately mixed with potassium bromide (KBr) at a 1:100 (w/w) ratio and homogenized. After pressed into thin sliceswith GK-15 infrared powder press (Cangzhou Yihang Technology Co., LTD., Hebei, China). Then Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Varian 670, Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) was used to scan the spectrum at a wavelength of 4000–500 cm−1. Each sample was scanned three times to ensure reproducibility.

X-ray diffraction detection (XRD)

The X-ray diffraction of WFM and L-WFM was determined according to the method of Cai et al. (2019). In short, 20 mg of WFM and L-WFM samples were placed in an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker AXS GmbH, Germany) and the pattern was measured at a voltage of 50 kV and a current of 50 mA. Under these conditions, the XRD pattern of the sample was scanned at a rate of 4°/min in the range of 5–70°(2θ).

Simultaneous thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimetry analysis (TG-DSC)

The synchronous thermal analysis of WFM and L-WFM was carried out according to the method of Niu et al. (2024). In short, 20 mg of WFM and L-WFM powders were weighed separately, and the thermal stability of the samples was recorded by using a synchronous thermal analyzer (DTG-60, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) under the protection of 20 mL/min nitrogen flow at a speed of 10 °C/min from 30 °C to 800 °C.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy detection (XPS)

The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy measurements of WFM and L-WFM were performed following Niu et al. method with slight modifications (Niu et al., 2025). Specifically, the surface chemical interactions and elemental compositions of WFM and L-WFM were analyzed using an X-ray photoelectron spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The measurement conditions were as follows: a wide-scan survey spectrum was collected over a binding energy range of 0–1200 eV with a pass energy of 200 eV. High-resolution scans were performed for the C 1 s, N 1 s, and O 1 s regions with a step size of 0.1 eV and a pass energy of 50 eV. Charge correction was applied using the C 1 s reference peak at 284.8 eV. The obtained spectra were deconvoluted and optimized using the XPSPEAK 4.1 software for peak fitting.

Observation of scanning electron microscope (SEM)

The WFM and L-WFM were observed by scanning electron microscope according to the method of Niu et al. (2024). Briefly, 5 mg each of WFM and L-WFM were placed on conductive adhesive, followed by gold sputtering. The surface morphology of the samples was then observed under a scanning electron microscope (Gemini SEM 300, Zeiss, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV, with magnifications of 50×, 500×, and 1000×.

Elemental analysis (EA)

Elemental analysis of WFM and L-WFM was performed according to the method described by Ye et al. (2012). Specifically, approximately 10 mg of WFM and L-WFM powder was weighed and loaded into an elemental analyzer (Multi EA 5000, Analytik Jena, Germany) to determine the weight percentages of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S).

Determination of color value

The color values of WFM and L-WFM were determined by the method of Ye et al. (2012). Briefly, WFM solution and L-WFM solution (1 mL, 50 mg/L), and their absorbance values at their respective maximum absorption wavelengths were determined. The color value is E1%1cm (U·g−1) = AB/m, where A is the absorbance value, B is the dilution multiple, and m is the mass of WFM and L-WFM.

Solubility evaluation

The solubility of WFM and L-WFM was evaluated according to the method of Xu et al. (2020). In brief, WFM and L-WFM were separately dissolved in 50 mL of the following solvents: 1 mol/L NaOH, 1 mol/L HCl, anhydrous ethanol, petroleum ether, chloroform, and distilled water. The mixtures were shaken in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min, then centrifuged at 5000 ×g for 5 min. Color changes and solubility were observed in each group.

Stability evaluation

Effect of temperature on the stability of WFM and L-WFM

The temperature stability of WFM and L-WFM was evaluated according to Xu et al. (2020). The 0.001 g WFM and L-WFM samples were dissolved in 10 mL 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution and reacted at 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 °C, respectively. The absorbance values were measured at the maximum absorption wavelengths (λmax) of WFM and L-WFM every 30 min.

Effects of light on the stability of WFM and L-WFM

The light stability of WFM and L-WFM was evaluated according to Xu et al. (2020). The WFM and L-WFM samples (0.001 g each) were separately dissolved in 10 mL of 0.1 mol/L NaOH solution. The solutions were then subjected to reaction under three different conditions: (1) natural light, (2) complete darkness (light-proof), and (3) UV irradiation. The reactions were monitored at 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h time intervals. After reaction completion, the absorbance of both WFM and L-WFM solutions was measured at their respective maximum absorption wavelengths (λmax).

Effects of pH on the stability of WFM and L-WFM

The pH stability of WFM and L-WFM was evaluated according to Xu et al. (2020). The WFM and L-WFM samples (0.001 g each) were individually dissolved in 10 mL of citrate-phosphate buffer solutions at different pH values (2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, and 11). The solutions were allowed to react at room temperature for 2 h under static conditions. After the reaction, the absorbance of each solution was measured at the respective maximum absorption wavelength (λmax) of WFM and L-WFM.

Determination of reducing power

According to the method of Sunil Kumar et al. (2024), the reducing power of WFM and L-WFM was measured and slightly modified. Specifically, a series of WFM and L-WFM solutions with concentrations of (20, 40, 60, 80, 100 μg/mL) were prepared using 0.1 mol/L NaOH. Take 1 mL of sample solution of varying concentrations and thoroughly combine it with 2.5 mL of PBS (20 mmol/L, pH 6.6) and 2.5 mL of a potassium ferricyanide solution (1 %, w/v). Determination of antibacterial ability of WFM and L-WFM

The antibacterial activity of WFM and L-WFM against Gram-negative bacteria (Escherichia coli) and Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus) was determined by filter paper method with reference to Wold et al. The diameter of the inhibition zone (mm) was then measured.

Results and discussion

Optimization of the best extraction conditions for WFM

Effect of single factor experiment on WFM yield

As shown in Fig. 1B∼1F, it can be seen that the yield of WFM showed a trend of increasing and then decreasing with the increase of the NaOH concentration, ultrasonic power, ultrasonic time, solid-liquid ratio and ultrasonic temperature (P < 0.05). At a NaOH concentration of 0.75 mol/L, ultrasonic power of 80 W, ultrasonic time of 60 min, solid-liquid ratio of 1:25, and ultrasonic temperature of 70 °C, the maximum yields of WFM were 11.00 ± 0.1 %、16.60 ± 0.07 %、13.81 ± 0.03 %、10.47 ± 0.06 %、12.59 ± 0.13 %, respectively. This may be due to the appropriate increase of NaOH concentration can provide a suitable alkaline environment for melanin, promote the full swelling of walnut flower cell wall, and thus facilitate the release of melanin (Chen et al., 2025). However, excessively high NaOH concentrations may lead to the degradation of melanin molecular structures and increase the dissolution of impurities, thereby reducing the purity of the extract (Chen et al., 2025). Increasing ultrasonic power can generate moderate cavitation effects and mechanical vibrations, effectively disrupting cell structures and promoting melanin release. However, excessively high power may lead to over-cavitation, producing excessive free radicals that oxidatively degrade melanin structures (Hou et al., 2019). Appropriately extending the ultrasonication time facilitates thorough disruption of cellular structures, enabling complete melanin dissolution. But excessive ultrasonication time may induce degradation of the extracted melanin (Hou et al., 2019). An increased solid-liquid ratio provides sufficient extraction solvent and favorable mass transfer conditions, thereby enhancing extraction efficiency. However, an excessively high ratio may lead to over-dilution of the extract, increase subsequent concentration difficulties, and reduce extraction efficiency per unit volume (Liu et al., 2025). Furthermore, appropriately elevated ultrasonic temperature can enhance molecular mobility and solubility, thereby facilitating melanin release. However, excessively high temperature may induce thermal denaturation of melanin and accelerate its oxidation, ultimately compromising the stability and quality of the extract (Chen et al., 2025; Hou et al., 2019).

The response surface optimization results revealed that the optimal process conditions for maximizing the WFM yield were as follows: NaOH concentration of 0.802 mol/L, ultrasonic power of 82.173 W, ultrasonic duration of 60 min, solid-liquid ratio of 1:25, and ultrasonic temperature of 75.29 °C. Under these conditions, the predicted WFM yield reached 16.14 %. Nevertheless, given the constraints of real-world operational parameters. The optimal extraction process was defined by the following parameters: a NaOH concentration of 0.80 mol/L, ultrasonic power of 82 W, ultrasonic duration of 60 min, solid-liquid ratio of 1:25, and ultrasonic temperature of 75 °C. Three verification tests were carried out under this condition, and the yield of WFM was 16.13 %, 16.14 % and 16.13 %, respectively. The average value was 16.13 %, which was close to the predicted value of 16.14 %, indicating that the optimization results were reasonable. Therefore, the best formula obtained by Box-Behnken test is reliable and has practical application value.

Screening and analysis of amino acids for WFM modification

Melanin is a biological macromolecule composed of various phenolic or indole monomers, exhibiting poor solubility, which limits its applications in the food industry (Hu et al., 2025). In this context, amino acid-modified melanin has emerged as a highly promising strategy due to its ability to significantly improve solubility, enhance stability, and optimize functional properties (Han et al., 2024). The solubility of amino acid-modified WFM was evaluated based on absorbance values, where higher absorbance indicates greater solubility and thus reflects more effective modification (Hu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2012). This approach is well-established in natural pigment modification studies, as enhanced solubility typically results from successful introduction of hydrophilic groups (e.g., amino acid side chains) that improve dispersibility in aqueous systems (Han et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2012).

To identify the most suitable amino acid for modifying WFM, we screened eight different amino acids (Tyrosine, Lysine, Arginine, Leucine, Cysteine, Glycine, Proline, and Cystine) based on their ability to improve WFM's solubility. The absorbance at 500 nm primarily reflects the concentration of chromophores in the supernatant after modification and centrifugation (Hu et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2012). As shown in Fig. 2D, the absorbance value of the Lysine-modified WFM complex was significantly higher than that of the complexes formed with the other seven amino acids (P < 0.05). The significantly higher absorbance of the Lysine-modified group directly indicates its superior effectiveness in enhancing WFM solubility compared to other amino acids. The reason for this phenomenon may be that Lysine is an alkaline amino acid, and its side chain contains amino groups (−NH2), during the modification process, these amino groups can undergo chemical reactions with the active sites in WFM, forming stable covalent or hydrogen bonds. The polarity of WFM molecules was enhanced, thus improving their solubility in aqueous solution (Cao, Wang, Wu, et al., 2025; Han et al., 2024; Shang et al., 2024). Besids, the basic nature of Lysine may also help neutralize the negative charges on the surface of WFM molecules, reducing intermolecular electrostatic repulsion (Han et al., 2024). Thus promoting its dispersion in the solution, which makes the solubility of Lysine-modified WFM significantly enhanced. These findings establish Lysine as the optimal choice for WFM modification, providing a foundation for developing melanin-based ingredients with improved functionality for food applications.

Analysis of lysine-WFM modification concentration

As shown in Fig. 2E, the absorbance of L-WFM first increased and then decreased with the increasing Lysine ratio. The maximum absorbance (0.70) was achieved at a WFM: Lysine of 1:2.5 (P < 0.05). While increased solubility contributes to the initial rise in absorbance, the observed peak and subsequent decrease are primarily governed by the formation efficiency and structural stability of the L-WFM complex itself. In the initial stage (ratios below 1:2.5), the increasing Lysine ratio provides more molecules available to bind with the active sites in WFM, facilitating the formation of a greater number of stable L-WFM complexes. These complexes exhibit enhanced solubility and maintain chromophore integrity, leading to increased absorbance (Cao, Wang, Zhang, et al., 2025). However, beyond the optimal ratio (1:2.5), excess Lysine molecules may induce steric hindrance or engage in non-productive competitive binding (Cao, Wang, Wu, et al., 2025). This can disrupt the optimal chromophore environment within the complex, potentially leading to structural alterations or aggregation that diminish the effective chromophore concentration and light-absorbing efficiency, consequently resulting in decreased absorbance (Zhu et al., 2024). Therefore, the WFM-to-Lysine ratio of 1:2.5 was selected as the optimal modification condition, ensuring the formation of L-WFM complexes with maximal solubility and chromophore stability.

Characterization analysis of WFM and L-WFM

UV–Vis spectral analysis

Both WFM and L-WFM exhibited similar spectral changes in the UV–Vis range of 200–800 nm (Fig. 3A), showing distinct absorption peaks. The mean maximum absorption wavelengths (λmax) for WFM and L-WFM, determined from triplicate measurements (n = 3), were 206.11 ± 0.23 nm and 203.03 ± 0.18 nm, respectively. Compared with WFM, L-WFM exhibits a statistically significant (P < 0.01, independent samples t-test) blue shift of approximately 3 nm. This phenomenon may be attributed to covalent bonding or intermolecular interactions between the amino groups (-NH2) of Lysine molecules and the phenolic hydroxyl (-OH) and carboxyl (-COOH) groups of melanin. Such interactions could alter the conjugated system or electron cloud distribution of melanin molecules, subsequently modifying the molecular conformation and aggregation state of melanin, thereby inducing the observed blue shift in the absorption peak (Cao, Wang, Zhang, et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2012). It is noteworthy that neither WFM nor L-WFM exhibits distinct absorption peaks in the range of 260–280 nm, indicating the absence of protein and nucleic acid components in both purified WFM and dialyzed L-WFM. A similar phenomenon was also observed in the UV–Vis spectra of arginine-modified Lachnum YM-346 melanin, as reported in the study by Ye et al. (2012).

X-ray diffraction analysis

In general, crystalline compounds exhibit sharp and narrow diffraction peaks, while amorphous compounds exhibit wide and weak diffraction peaks (Niu et al., 2024). The X-ray diffraction patterns of WFM and L-WFM are shown in Fig. 3C. Both samples exhibit similar characteristic diffraction features of amorphous compounds, displaying a weak and broad “bump” at 2θ = 24.2°, indicating no significant differences in their spatial structures. This phenomenon may be related to the amorphous nature of Lysine itself and its non-covalent interaction with melanin, which failed to induce the formation of ordered crystal arrangement of melanin molecules (Su et al., 2023). Additionally, the intrinsically complex and randomly stacked polymeric structure of melanin may further suppress the crystallization process, resulting in the maintenance of amorphous characteristics both before and after modification (Sunil Kumar et al., 2025).

Thermal stability analysis

The thermal decomposition of melanin reflects the intermolecular binding pattern and thermal stability of melanin (Zhao et al., 2022). The thermogravimetric (TG) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) of L-WFM are shown in Fig. 3D and Fig. 3E, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3D, both WFM and L-WFM exhibit two similar weight-loss stages. The first stage is at 25 °C ∼ 100 °C, which may be due to the evaporation of free water, bound water and residual solvent in WFM and L-WFM, resulting in slight weight loss (Niu et al., 2024). In the second stage, the material itself was gradually degraded at 100 °C ∼ 800 °C. The weight loss rate of L-WFM (67.81 %) was significantly higher than that of WFM (56.89 %). This suggests that Lysine modification reduces the thermal stability of WFM, likely because the amino group on the Lysine side chain forms additional but weaker hydrogen bonds or electrostatic interactions with the phenolic hydroxyl groups of melanin. These newly formed bonds disrupt the original tightly packed aromatic stacking and strong intermolecular hydrogen-bonding network in melanin (Ji, 2020; Yang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024), ultimately leading to a decrease in its thermal stability.

As shown in Fig. 3E, WFM exhibits endothermic peaks at 88.8 °C and 290.4 °C, while L-WFM displays endothermic peaks at 82 °C and 230.4 °C, indicating the thermal degradation of WFM and L-WFM molecules. In addition, WFM exhibits an exothermic peak at 363 °C, while L-WFM shows exothermic peaks at 177.6 °C and 278.9 °C. This indicates the rupture of intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonds in WFM and L-WFM, leading to thermal decomposition of the components. Notably, both the endothermic and exothermic peaks of L-WFM exhibit a significant blue shift. This phenomenon may be attributed to the introduction of a more readily oxidizable group after Lysine modification of WFM, which leads to the breakdown of melanin molecular chains and disrupts the intermolecular or intramolecular hydrogen bonding interactions in melanin (Yang et al., 2023). In summary, Lysine modification of WFM significantly reduces its thermal stability.

Scanned electron microscopy observation analysis

The scanning electron microscope figures of WFM and L-WFM were shown in Fig. 4. The WFM exhibits a blocky, agglomerated irregular structure with adhesion between samples and a rough surface. The L-WFM displays flaky crystalline morphology, no inter-sample adhesion, and a smooth surface. The reason for this phenomenon may be that during the modification of WFM with Lysine, the amino groups of Lysine interact with both intermolecular and intramolecular bonds of WFM, leading to a reduction in hydrogen bonding within and between molecules (Yang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022).

Elemental analysis

The elemental analysis of WFM and L-WFM was shown in Table 1. As can be seen from Table 1, the contents of C, N, H, and S elements in WFM were 54.946 %, 6.643 %, 5.781 %, and 1.658 %, respectively. The C/N ratio was 8.2709, the C/H ratio was 9.5053, and the S/N ratio was 0.2496. Notably, according to the chemical classification criteria of melanin, melanin species are categorized based on nitrogen (N) content as follows: eumelanin (8–12 %), pheomelanin (6–8 %), melanin polymers (2–4 %), and anthocyanin-derived melanins (< 2 %) (Sunil Kumar et al., 2024). This indicates that WFM belongs to a pheomelanin. At the same time, the C/H ratio (9.5053) in WFM is higher, which indicates that the molecule contains more aromatic structures. Interestingly, compared to WFM, L-WFM exhibits a significantly higher nitrogen (N) content (9.635 %), reaching the range typical for eumelanin. Moreover, the C/H ratio in L-WFM (7.851) was significantly reduced. This phenomenon likely arises from the Schiff base condensation between Lysine amino groups and melanin quinone rings, which introduces additional nitrogen atoms and disrupts partial aromatic conjugation (Yang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022). Consequently, the nitrogen content increased while aromaticity decreased, leading to reductions in both C/H and C/N ratios.

Color value analysis

Quality assessment of natural pigments involves multiple critical parameters, among which color value stands as one of the core indices. Determined by measuring the absorbance (E1%1cm) of a pigment solution at a specific wavelength per unit concentration, the color value directly reflects the chromatic intensity and purity of the pigment, serving as a vital technical parameter for evaluating the commercial value of pigment products (Ye et al., 2012). The color values (E1%1 cm) of WFM and L-WFM were calculated to be 346.27 U·g−1 and 672.14 U·g−1, respectively. The color value of L-WFM was significantly higher than that of WFM (P < 0.05). This enhancement could be attributed to the formation of a more stable conjugated structure induced by Lysine incorporation, which improved light absorption efficiency and consequently enhance the coloration ability of pigment (Zhou et al., 2022). Meanwhile, Lysine is a hydrophilic amino acid (Zhou et al., 2022). This enhancement was achieved by improving WFM dispersibility in aqueous systems and reducing molecular aggregation, thereby effectively increasing its color value. These findings establish a theoretical foundation for the high-value applications of L-WFM in both the food industry and light industrial technologies.

Solubility analysis

As shown in Fig. 5A, WFM and L-WFM exhibited distinct solubility behaviors in various solvents including NaOH, HCl, absolute ethanol, petroleum ether, chloroform, and distilled water. Among them, WFM is soluble only in NaOH solution, whereas L-WFM dissolves in NaOH solution and distilled water and is slightly soluble in HCl. Neither compound is soluble in organic solvents. The observed phenomenon may be attributed to the introduction of Lysine, which significantly enhances the polarity of WFM melanin molecules. Specifically, the free amino and carboxyl groups of Lysine can form hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds in aqueous solution (Zhang & Wu, 2025), thereby conferring a certain degree of water solubility to L-WFM. The isoelectric effect of Lysine enables reversible protonation/deprotonation of L-WFM in acidic/alkaline environments (Zhang et al., 2025), resulting in partial solubility in HCl and complete solubility in NaOH solution. Concurrently, the hydrophilic side chains of Lysine encapsulate the hydrophobic pigment core, effectively shielding it from interaction with organic solvents (e.g., ethanol, petroleum ether, chloroform), thereby insolubility in the organic phases (Zhou et al., 2022).

Stability analysis

Analysis of the influence of temperature on the stability of WFM and L-WFM

At the same heating time, the absorbance of WFM and L-WFM with the increase of temperature, it decreases to varying degrees (See Table 4 in the supplementary material). The possible reason for this phenomenon is that the indole/pyrrole skeleton in melanin undergoes partial degradation at high temperatures, leading to the disruption of the conjugated system and the decrease of light absorption capacity (Yang et al., 2023). Interestingly, under the same heating time, the absorbance of L-WFM was significantly higher than that of WFM, and the decrease of absorbance of L-WFM with temperature was far lower than that of WFM. This phenomenon may occur because the amino group of Lysine can covalently bind to the melanin skeleton, creating a steric hindrance effect. This configuration shields the active sites in the melanin core, reduces bond cleavage caused by molecular thermal vibration, and inhibits radical attacks (Xu et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the positively charged amino group of Lysine may form intramolecular charge interactions with the quinoid structures in melanin, thereby stabilizing the π-electron conjugated system and reducing excited-state energy dissipation (Yang et al., 2023).

Analysis of the influence of light on the stability of WFM and L-WFM

It can be seen from Table 5 (see supplementary material) that the absorbance of WFM and L-WFM decreased with the increase of time under the same light condition. This may be due to the excitation of melanin molecules by ultraviolet and visible light to produce singlet oxygen (1O₂) and superoxide radicals (O₂−•), which attack the phenolic hydroxyl and indole ring structures in melanin, resulting in the cleavage of the conjugated π-electron system and the reduction of chromophore (Huang et al., 2025). In addition, under ultraviolet light irradiation, the absorbance of WFM and L-WFM decreased more, indicating that ultraviolet light had a strong degradation effect on them. This is because the high energy of ultraviolet light can directly break the C-C/C-N bond in the melanin molecule, triggering a free radical chain reaction that leads to the destruction of the conjugated system (Yang et al., 2023).

Analysis of the influence of pH on the stability of WFM and L-WFM

It can be seen from Table 6 (see supplementary material) that the absorbance of WFM and L-WFM increased with the increase of pH, and L-WFM showed higher absorbance, which was mainly due to the deprotonation of the phenolic hydroxyl group of melanin under alkaline conditions to form an extended π-conjugated system (Yang et al., 2023). At the same time, the ε-amino group of Lysine in L-WFM further stabilizes the conjugated structure through intramolecular salt bridge, and the depolymerization of WFM caused by alkaline environment increases the exposure of chromophore (Zhao et al., 2022). Therefore, it can be concluded that WFM remains stable in neutral and alkaline environments, but it is prone to denaturation and precipitation under acidic conditions. The solubility and stability of WFM modified by Lysine under acidic conditions were significantly improved.

Analysis of antioxidant activity in vitro

As shown in Fig. 5B∼5E, with the increase of WFM and L-WFM concentration, the DPPH free radical scavenging activity, ABTS free radical scavenging activity, hydroxyl radical scavenging activity and reducing power of WFM and L-WFM showed an upward trend. The reason for this phenomenon may be due to the formation of a synergistic antioxidant network between melanin molecules at high concentrations, the increase in the range of electron delocalization, and the participation of more phenolic hydroxyl groups in free radical neutralization reactions (Sunil Kumar et al., 2024). Notably, the antioxidant capacity of L-WFM was significantly higher than that of WFM in the same concentration range (P < 0.05). This is attributed to the intramolecular electron transport channel formed by the amino group of Lysine and the quinone structure of melanin, which enhances the electron dissociation ability. At the same time, the new proton-coupled electron transfer site and the stable intramolecular hydrogen bond network synergistically enhanced the free radical scavenging ability of melanin (Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022).

Analysis of antibacterial activity in vitro

It can be clearly observed from Fig. 5F that both WFM and L-WFM exhibit certain antibacterial properties, which may be due to the fact that the phenolic hydroxyl group in WFM destroys the integrity of bacterial cell membrane, interferes with the formation of bacterial biofilm and the activity of key enzymes in cell wall synthesis, and chelates metal ions through redox. Simultaneously, the alteration of membrane lipid phase distribution disrupted calcium and potassium transport, thereby modulating the local microenvironment of calcium channels and ultimately exerting antibacterial effects (Sunil Kumar et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2012). It is worth noting that the inhibition zone size of L-WFM was significantly better than that of WFM (P < 0.05). This may be because the protonation of the amino group of Lysine enhances the electrostatic interaction between melanin and bacterial membrane, and synergistically enhances the metal ion chelating ability and free radical scavenging ability (Li et al., 2025).

Conclusion

The innovation of this study is to extract WFM by ultrasonic-assisted alkali extraction and acid precipitation for the first time, and to improve the physicochemical and functional properties of WFM by Lysine modification strategy. The Lysine modification induced a 3 nm blue shift in the maximum UV absorption wavelength of WFM, transformed its microscopic morphology from irregular aggregates into smooth sheet-like crystals, and significantly reduced its thermal stability. XRD and XPS results show that the crystal structures of WFM and L-WFM are amorphous, and there is no significant difference in surface chemical composition and functional group content between them. It is worth noting that Lysine modification significantly increased the color value of WFM. Furthermore, the nitrogen content of L-WFM (9.635 %) increased into the range typically associated with eumelanin, while its solubility and stability were also markedly improved. Moreover, it substantially enhanced the antioxidant and antibacterial capacities of WFM to a certain extent. This study not only provides theoretical support for the high-value utilization of walnut flower resources, but also lays a technical foundation for the development of natural pigments with excellent water solubility and biological activity. It is expected to achieve industrial application in the fields of functional food, cosmetics and biological materials.
