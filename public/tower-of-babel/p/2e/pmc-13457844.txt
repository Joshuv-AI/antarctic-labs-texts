Thermal processing optimization and modeling for nutritional quality retention in anchote (Coccinia abyssinica) tuber flour
Authors: Abdisa R Hunde, Reddy Prasad DM, Ermias A Tesema, Getachew L Rorissa, Tebelay L Andualem, Metadel K Abera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnchote (Coccinia abyssinica (Lam.) Cogn.) is an indigenous Ethiopian tuber crop that remains underutilized despite its superior nutritional value over commonly consumed tubers such as cassava, potato, and enset. Its high moisture content (70–80%) makes it highly perishable, necessitating optimized post-harvest processing to extend shelf life and preserve nutritional quality. This study aimed to optimize thermal processing conditions for Anchote tuber flour production using Response Surface Methodology (RSM) with Box–Behnken Design (BBD). Three independent variables were investigated: slice thickness (2–6 mm), blanching time (0–10 min), and drying temperature (40–60 °C). Moisture content (MC), crude protein (CP), and Vitamin C (VC) were selected as response variables. Analysis of variance confirmed that all three process parameters had significant effects (p < 0.05) on MC, CP, and VC. The optimal conditions were slice thickness of 2 mm, blanching time of 2.93 min, and drying temperature of 54.26 °C, yielding MC of 4.50%, CP of 9.15 g/100 g, and VC of 45.35 mg/100 g. Experimental validation confirmed predicted values with less than 0.3% deviation. The low drying temperature of 54 °C effectively preserves heat-sensitive Vitamin C, providing a scientific basis for industrial-scale Anchote flour production with retained nutritional quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55168-7.Keywords: Anchote, Thermal processing, Response surface methodology, Box–behnken design, Vitamin C, Tuber flourSubject terms: Agriculture, Biochemistry, Chemistry, Plant sciences

Introduction

A proper diet comprises not only the macronutrients (carbohydrates, proteins, and fats) but also the micronutrients such as vitamins, minerals, and other important trace elements. The main source of energy is carbohydrates and lipids, whereas the main source of tissue repair, maintenance, and building is proteins. Micronutrients are needed in small quantities, yet their deficiency might cause severe health conditions and dysfunction of the organism. Thus, it is important to eat a healthy and balanced diet that will guarantee adequate amounts of these nutrients, especially from vegetable sources. Dietary variety has been shown to be vital in the prevention of malnutrition and enhancement of overall wellness outcomes1.

Root and tuber crops are very common in tropical and sub-tropical areas since they are adapted to the hot and humid climate conditions and can grow in almost all soils. The crops are used as a major staple crop in most of the developing nations and are a major source of daily caloric intake. The most promising but underutilized indigenous Ethiopian tuber is Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica) among these meaningful crops. One such tuber crop is Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica (Lam.) Cogn.), a species of the Cucurbitaceae. Anchote is endemic to Ethiopia and is traditionally grown to produce edible tubers that are starchy and that hold both nutritional and cultural significance3–5. Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica) is a native Ethiopian tuber crop that has been known to have a wide range of nutritional, medicinal, economic, and cultural attributes. Conventionally, Anchote is traditionally used in the healing of fractured or dislocated bones and in strengthening postpartum women. These ethnomedicinal properties have helped it to continue being used in the rural Ethiopian communities6–9.

Although it has culinary and nutritional value, along with other usability, Anchote is among the few indigenous Ethiopian vegetable crops that have ever been traded with little concern of research and production10. Fresh Anchote tubers have a high MC (around 70–80%), therefore, they are very perishable and can be spoiled by microorganisms and fermented, resulting in the development of off-flavors and quality degradation in a few days after harvesting. Therefore, crop preservation is essential. Consequently, preservation is an essential measure for mitigating post-harvest losses and the promotion of food security.

Long boiling has the potential to deteriorate the nutritional value of tubers because of heat and loss of nutrients into the water. Also, Anchote roots, similar to other root crops, have a low shelf life due to their susceptibility to quality deterioration. Although underground storage is often used as the means of minimizing early losses after harvest, it denies farmers the opportunity to use the land for other crops. Additional values have been developed in the form of value-added products of Anchote root, including biscuits and bread besides traditional boiling11–13. Although Anchote is a nutritionally and culturally important crop, it is confronted with serious postharvest challenges that restrict its commercial exploitation.

Due to the scanty literature on postharvest processing of Anchote, especially with regard to nutrient preservation and shelf-life extension, there is an urgent need for scientifically optimized procedures of dehydration and shelf-life stabilization. Although past research work has determined its agronomic and nutritional properties, there have been limited systematic studies on the effects of thermal processing on the nutritional quality of this product.

The originality of this study lies in three aspects. First, multiple critical processing parameters (slice thickness, blanching time, and drying temperature) and their interaction effects on nutritional quality are simultaneously optimised for Anchote, which, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, has not been previously reported. Second, this study specifically investigates lower drying temperatures (40–60 °C), compared to the 80–100 °C range used in prior studies, with the aim of preserving heat-labile Vitamin C. Third, Vitamin C retention is included as a primary optimisation response for Anchote flour, providing a more complete nutritional quality assessment.

Accordingly, this study addresses the following research question: What combination of slice thickness, blanching time, and drying temperature maximises the retention of crude protein (CP) and Vitamin C (VC) while minimising moisture content (MC) in Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica) tuber flour? The specific objectives are: (1) to quantify the individual and interactive effects of slice thickness (2–6 mm), blanching time (0–10 min), and drying temperature (40–60 °C) on MC, CP, and VC; (2) to develop and validate quadratic regression models for each response variable using RSM with Box–Behnken Design; and (3) to identify and experimentally verify optimal processing conditions.

Materials and methods

Materials

The Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica (Lam.)) tubers were prepared and dried at the Bahir Dar University, Mechanical Unit Operation and Food processing laboratory and at the Bahir Dar Institute of technology. Proximate composition studies were done using food chemistry and analysis laboratory, and mineral content studies were done using chemistry laboratory in Peda Campus. The chemicals employed in conducting the research were of analytical grade. The Debre Zeyit Agricultural Research Center provided Fresh Anchote tubers (20 kg) within 24 h of harvest. This accession was selected based on its high nutritional content and superior yield performance. The harvested tubers were taken to the Food Processing Laboratory at the Bahir Dar Institute of Technology within 48 h to minimise post-harvest spoilage and loss of nutrients.

Methods

Sample preparation

Anchote tubers were processed into flour using a laboratory oven dryer (Henan, Model: DHG-9140, China) without relative humidity control. The flour was prepared following the method described by Bikila et al.2 with modifications made to the drying temperature, slice thickness, and pretreatment combinations to optimize the processing conditions. The tubers were washed under running tap water to remove soil and surface contaminants, peeled using stainless steel knives, and washed again to ensure cleanliness. Excess surface moisture was removed by placing the peeled tubers on a sieve (Fig. 1). The clean tubers were then sliced uniformly into thicknesses of 2 mm, 4 mm, and 6 mm, and the slices were prepared for subsequent pretreatment and analysis.

Pretreatment prior to dehydration of Anchote (Coccinia Abyssinica) tuber

The homogenized Anchote slices were categorized into three groups based on the slice thicknesses (2 mm, 4 mm, and 6 mm). Each group was further divided into three pretreatment categories: unblanched and blanched raw sample in hot water at 95–100 °C for 0, 5, or 10 min, and subsequently immersed in a 0.5% sodium chloride (NaCl) solution for 1 min at room temperature. After pretreatment, the samples were prepared for the drying process. The soaking time was selected based on preliminary trials, which showed that longer soaking times resulted in lower lightness (L*) values, indicating increased browning14.

The crude FC was calculated by the weight difference (W1 − W2).

A 2-gram sample was accurately weighed into an ignited, cooled, and pre-weighed ash dish. The sample was then ignited in a furnace (Carbolite, S336RB, England) at 550 °C until only ash remained. After ignition, the dish containing the ash was transferred to desiccators, allowed to cool to room temperature, and then weighed. The ash content was determined by calculating the weight difference of the dish.

Vitamin C determination

Vitamin C quantification can be carried out using various methods, including biological, electrochemical, and chromatographic techniques, though each presents limitations depending on sample type and interfering substances19,20. The 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) method is widely recognized as a simple, accurate, and effective approach for determining total ascorbic acid (AA) in food samples21. In this study, the total VC content (ascorbic acid and dehydroascorbic acid) in eighteen Anchote flour samples was determined using a DNPH-based spectrophotometric method. A standard calibration curve (0–30 µg/mL) was prepared using known concentrations of ascorbic acid. For sample extraction, 5 g of Anchote flour was homogenized in 5% metaphosphoric acid–10% glacial acetic acid (MPA-GAA), centrifuged, and filtered. Ascorbic acid in the extract was oxidized to dehydroascorbic acid using bromine water, followed by thiourea to neutralize excess bromine. The oxidized sample was reacted with DNPH to form osazone derivatives, incubated at 37 °C for 3 h, and treated with cold 85% H₂SO₄ to develop a colored complex. Absorbance was measured using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Cary 60), and vitamin C concentration was calculated from the standard curve. This method effectively quantifies total VC in plant-based foods and is particularly suitable for fresh or processed Anchote tuber samples.

Flour preparation from blanched Anchote tuber

Blanched Anchote flour was prepared following a modified version of the yam flour preparation method described by22, with alterations made to slicing thickness, blanching duration, and drying temperature (Fig. 2). This study differs from Bikila et al.2 in using lower drying temperatures (40–60 °C vs. 80–100 °C) and applying RSM/BBD optimisation. Fresh Anchote tubers (27 kg) were selected, cleaned to remove soil and debris, washed under running tap water, and peeled. A homogenized 3 kg portion of medium-sized tubers was divided into three groups based on blanching conditions and slice thickness. The tubers were sliced into 2, 4, and 6 mm thicknesses (length and width not standardized) and subjected to blanching in 1500–1600 mL of hot water at temperatures ranging from 60 to 100 °C for 0, 5, and 10 min until softened. Raw (unblanched) samples were included as controls. All samples were then dried in a forced-air oven (Henan, Model: DHG-9140, Germany) at 40, 50, and 60 °C with a constant air velocity of 1.5 m/s until constant weight was achieved. Selection of particle size is vital because the size determines the properties of the powder if it is stored, handled and processed23. Larger particles can counteract the extraction process and increase heat treatment, and particle size also affects color, texture, bioactive compound retention, and sensory acceptability24. Dried Anchote tuber flakes were ground into a fine powder (with a laboratory hammer mill (Nima, Model NM8300, Japan)). A sieving process on a 355 μm mesh screen was applied to the resulting powder to achieve uniform distribution of the size of the particles. The flour was subsequently packed in moisture-free polyethylene bags and kept at − 4 °C until further analysis.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

A Box-Behnken Design (BBD) under Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed using Design Expert 11 software to optimize processing parameters for Anchote tuber flour production. This design, efficient for fitting quadratic response surfaces, required relatively few experimental runs. Three independent variables; drying temperature (40, 50, 60 °C), blanching time (0, 5, 10 min), and slice thickness (2, 4, 6 mm), were investigated, with five replicates at the center point to assess experimental repeatability.

Three response variables were selected for optimization based on their critical importance:

Moisture content (MC): Primary determinant of shelf stability, and storage quality. Target: minimize to < 10% for extended shelf life and prevention of microbial growth.

(a) Moisture content (MC): The average MC of the Raw Anchote tubers was 79.2% and this was in close agreement with the literature that reported 73.0% to 74.93%7. MC increased after the processing and peeling steps. The MC of boiled Anchote tubers was 82.43% and that of blanched tubers was 80.28%. Statistical analysis confirmed that the MC of boiled Anchote was significantly higher (P < 0.05) than that of blanched and raw samples. Likewise, blanched Anchote also registered a significantly greater MC (P < 0.05) than raw tubers. This increase, 4.08% in boiled and 1.36% in blanched samples, is probably due to greater retention of water by fibers and other compounds during heat treatment27.

(b)Crude protein (CP): Raw Anchote tubers contained 9.63 g/100 g of CP, a level significantly higher than previously reported values28. After peeling, the mean CP content of boiled Anchote was 8.39 g/100 g, a significant decrease (P < 0.05) of 12.87% compared to raw tubers. In contrast, blanched Anchote showed 9.94 g/100 g, with no significant change compared to raw samples (Table 1). The decrease in measured protein content (determined by Kjeldahl nitrogen) with increasing drying temperature can be attributed to Maillard reactions between reducing sugars and amino acids/proteins at elevated temperatures (> 60 °C). These non-enzymatic browning reactions form melanoidins and other nitrogenous polymers that are not hydrolyzed during Kjeldahl digestion, leading to apparent loss of protein. Additionally, some amino acids may undergo thermal degradation or volatilization at higher temperatures. The structural changes from protein denaturation do not affect total nitrogen content but may reduce protein digestibility and functional properties. The observed reduction in boiled samples can be attributed to protein denaturation and water solubility during heat treatment29.

(c) Crude fat content (CF): Root and tuber crops are generally recognized for their low-fat content, a characteristic also observed in Anchote, which has an ether extract (EE) content of less than 1%. The average CF content of unprocessed Anchote tuber was found to be 0.90 g/100 g. This is notably higher than the findings of Fufa and Urga7(0.17 g/100 g) and26 (0.1 g/100 g), as detailed in Table 1. The mean CF contents of Anchote tubers that were boiled after peeling and blanched after peeling were 0.84 g/100 g and 0.80 g/100 g respectively. A significant difference (P < 0.05) was observed when comparing the average CF content of Anchote boiled before peeling to that of Anchote boiled after peeling. Compared to raw tubers, the CF content decreased by 6.67% in tubers boiled after peeling and by 11.11% in tubers blanched after peeling. These reductions could be attributed to the dispersal of fat into the boiling water during processing31.

(d) Crude fiber content (FC): The mean crude FC of raw Anchote was 2.60 g/100 g, which is higher than previously reported values7. These variations may be attributed to differences in storage duration, soil mineral composition, and agroclimatic conditions17. After peeling, both blanched (4.09 g/100 g) and boiled (3.98 g/100 g) Anchote exhibited significantly higher (P < 0.05) crude FC compared to raw samples. Traditional processing methods increased crude fiber by 56.92% in blanched and 52.67% in boiled Anchote. The increase in crude fiber content from raw to blanched tubers can be attributed to the loss of water-soluble components (sugars, minerals, proteins) during blanching, which concentrates the remaining insoluble fiber fraction when expressed on dry weight basis. Additionally, heat treatment may cause structural changes in cell wall polysaccharides, converting some soluble pectin to insoluble protopectin. Also, the increase is likely due to the loss of water-soluble components during heat treatment, which enhances the relative percentage of macromolecules rich in dietary fiber31. The elevated crude fiber in processed Anchote suggests potential benefits for treating conditions such as obesity, diabetes, cancer, gastrointestinal disorders, and aiding indigestion and colon cancer prevention32.

(e) Total Ash (AC): The amount of ash in a food component indicates its entire mineral content. As pre-treatment progressed from none to boiling, the ash content of the tubers showed a decreasing trend. Compared to fresh (6.67 to 4.46 g/100 g) by 33.13% and (6.67 to 3.87 g/100 g) by 41.98%, respectively. The average total ash concentration of raw Anchote was higher than that reported by7 (2.00 g/100 g) and10(2.19 g/100 g). The small variations in total ash content could be linked to soil types, maturation stage, and agronomic techniques. The reduction in ash content (minerals) during blanching is primarily due to leaching of water-soluble minerals (particularly potassium, sodium, and magnesium) into the blanching water. The degree of mineral loss depends on blanching time, temperature, and water-to-sample ratio. A reduction in the ash content of various tubers after the blanching process is reported by different authors33,34. The same trends were reported for boiled Dioscorea dumetorum, and cocoyam tuber flours29,35. The reduction could be ascribed to mineral component leaching and water absorption during the boiling process29,35 which damages the flour’s mineral content.

(f)Total carbohydrate content (CH: In root and tuber crops, carbohydrates are among the most significant nutrients36. Table 1 shows the amount of CH in Anchote flour as a function of blanching and boiling, and the mean value was calculated by difference. Fresh Anchote contained 2.30 g/100 g of total carbohydrate. Boiling and blanching Anchote tubers yielded 4.46 and 5.79 g/100 g of utilizable carbohydrate, correspondingly. The result for raw Anchote was lower than the values reported by7,28 of their value 16.86, 22.5 and 21.1 g/100 g, respectively.

Blanching and boiling significantly enhanced the carbohydrate (CH) content of the Anchote tuber. This observation aligns with Shebabaw H13, who reported increased carbohydrate levels in two Anchote accessions (Nekemte: 77.31% to 78.72%; Dembidolo: 77.95% to 79.35%) after cooking.

However, these findings contrast with Fekadu28 who reported reduced carbohydrates in boiled Anchote. The increase in carbohydrate content due to blanching and boiling has also been documented in other root and tuber crops, including Dioscorea dumetorum, cocoyam, and bitter yam flours29,35,37. Variations in total carbohydrate content (calculated by difference) reflect the combined changes in moisture, protein, fat, fiber, and ash. The method of calculation (100 - sum of other components) means that errors in any component measurement are compounded in the carbohydrate value. Overall, Anchote flour derived from blanched tubers yields a higher carbohydrate content. Given its high carbohydrate levels, this flour presents a viable alternative energy source.

Raw Anchote has a mean energy value of 56.20 Kcal. Anchote tubers had an average gross energy content of 58.98 Kcal and 69.85 Kcal, respectively, when boiled and blanched. Boiled Anchote tuber had a lower mean gross energy content than blanched Anchote tuber. However, the average raw Anchote tuber was compared to the average boiled Anchote tuber.

Raw Anchote value was found to be lower than that given by EHNRI (98.10 Kcal/100 g) and7 (103.5 Kcal/100 g), which could be attributed to ecological factors such as rain fall, temperature, and soil type. The gross energy content of blanched and cooked Anchote tubers increased by 24.29 and 4.95%, respectively, as compared to raw tubers.

Statistical analysis of Anchote flour production

Model fitting and response surface methodology

Response Surface Methodology (RSM) is a powerful statistical tool used to model and optimize complex processes by evaluating the effects and interactions of multiple variables. In this study, RSM was applied using Design Expert software (v11.1.2.0) to analyze the influence of slice thickness, blanching time, and drying temperature on MC, CP, and VC levels in Anchote flour. Among several model options (linear, 2FI, quadratic, cubic), the quadratic model was selected based on its superior statistical performance, significance of added terms, and lack of aliasing. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirmed the model’s adequacy, with non-significant lack-of-fit (p > 0.05) and significant model terms (p < 0.05), indicating strong predictive capability. The Box–Behnken Design effectively captured the response behavior across three levels of each factor: slice thickness (2–6 mm), drying temperature (40–60 °C), and blanching time (0–10 min). The generated quadratic regression Eqs. (8–10) in coded terms provide a reliable basis for optimizing processing parameters to improve the nutritional quality of Anchote flour.

The sign of the regression coefficients in the quadratic model indicates the nature of their effect: a positive sign suggests a synergistic effect that increases MC, CP yield, and VC (VC) concentration, while a negative sign implies an antagonistic effect, reducing these responses26. Positive coefficients were observed for A, C, AC, A², B², and C² on MC and for A, C, B², and C² on CP yield, indicating a positive linear or quadratic influence. However, only A² showed a positive effect on VC concentration. Conversely, increasing values of these variables eventually led to a decline in MC, CP, and VC, reflecting diminishing returns at higher levels.

Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) were used to assess multicollinearity. Values of 1 for factors A, B, C, and interactions AB, AC, and BC confirmed orthogonality. Slightly higher VIFs (1.02) for quadratic terms A², B², and C² indicated minimal multicollinearity. ANOVA results (Tables S1–S3) showed highly significant model F-values of 113.15, 4.135 × 10⁵, and 6.888 × 10⁵ for MC, CP, and VC, respectively, with corresponding p-values < 0.0001, confirming strong model significance.

All model terms had p-values < 0.05, indicating statistical significance, except for AB and B² in the MC model. The lack-of-fit p-values for MC (0.97), CP (0.63), and VC (0.71) were all > 0.05, demonstrating non-significant lack of fit and thus a good match between the model and the experimental data. These findings confirm the validity and predictive strength of the quadratic model for optimizing Anchote flour processing conditions.

Model adequacy checking

Evaluating the adequacy of the developed model to describe variations in the proximate composition of Anchote flour, specifically MC, CP, and VC is critical. Model validity was assessed using key statistical indicators: the coefficient of determination (R²), adjusted R², predicted R², and adequate precision (signal-to-noise ratio), derived from ANOVA results. A high R² value (close to 1), minimal difference (< 0.2) between adjusted and predicted R², and adequate precision above 4 indicate strong model reliability and predictive accuracy25. In this study, R² values of 1.00 for CP and VC and 0.99 for MC demonstrate excellent model fit, with over 99% of the variation in responses explained by the independent variables. Only 0.78% of the variation in MC could not be accounted for, indicating a minimal prediction error. The model’s reliability is further supported by low standard deviations (0.0008 for CP, 0.179 for MC, and 0.012 for VC) and low coefficients of variation (CVs) of 0.0088% (CP), 2.93% (MC), and 0.032% (VC), indicating high precision and consistency. Importantly, the lack-of-fit was statistically insignificant (p > 0.05), confirming that no key terms were excluded and the residuals were within acceptable limits. Collectively, these metrics affirm the model’s robustness and suitability for predicting and optimizing thermal processing parameters in Anchote flour production (Fig. 3).

Diagnostic plot for the proposed model.

The strong agreement between adjusted R² values (1.000, 1.000, and 0.9834) and predicted R² values (1.000, 1.000, and 0.9841) for VC, CP, and MC, respectively, confirms the robustness and predictive accuracy of the model. Additionally, the high values of Adequate Precision: 2318.43 for VC, 2551.86 for protein, and 37.49 for moisture exceed the minimum threshold of 4, further indicating that the model provides a strong signal relative to noise and is suitable for navigating the design space.

The near-perfect R² values (1.00 for VC and CP, 0.99 for MC) might initially raise concerns about potential overfitting. However, these values are valid and reliable for the following reasons: (a) The full quadratic Box-Behnken Design includes replication at center points (n = 3), which allows estimation of pure error and verification of model adequacy; (b) The high R² is accompanied by low predicted residual error sum of squares (PRESS) and adequate precision (> 4), indicating genuine predictive power rather than data fitting artifacts; (c) The lack of fit is non-significant (p > 0.05) for all models, confirming that the quadratic model adequately describes the relationship without unexplained systematic variation; and (d) Diagnostic plots (normal probability, residuals vs. predicted) show no patterns or outliers, supporting model validity. These statistical indicators collectively demonstrate that the models capture true relationships rather than overfitting noise38.

Effect of process variables on proximate properties of Anchote flour production

Effects of process parameters on moisture content

According to statistical analysis, slice thickness (ST), blanching time (BT), drying temperature (DT), and their interactions had a significant effect (p < 0.05) on the MC of Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica) flour. MC varied from 4.00% to 9.02% across the experimental ranges: ST (2–6 mm), BT (0–10 min), and DT (40–60 °C). For effective storage under ambient conditions typical of 27–29 °C, a MC below 12.55% is considered acceptable, while levels below 10% are recommended for extended shelf life39,40. Two-dimensional response surface plots illustrated the individual and combined effects of ST, BT, and DT on MC, with the subsequent section detailing the specific influence of each processing factor.

Interaction effects of slice thickness and drying temperature on MC

Moisture level is a critical factor determining the shelf life of food products, with lower MC extending storage duration. In our study, Anchote flour exhibited a low MC due to the drying process. Across all drying temperatures 40–60 °C, MC ranged from 4.0% to 9.7%. The lowest MC (4.0%) was achieved in 2 mm thick slices dried at 60 °C and blanched for 5 min. However, increasing slice thickness beyond 2 mm reduced moisture removal efficiency.

Statistical analysis confirmed significant differences (P < 0.05) in MC across various drying temperatures. Specifically, for a constant slice thickness of 2 mm, MC decreased as drying temperature increased, from 6.5% at 40 °C to 4.0% at 60 °C. This is because higher drying temperatures enhance moisture migration to the surface and evaporation rates (Fig. 4). While increased drying temperatures improve moisture removal, excessively high temperatures risk burning and ashing the slices. Conversely, lower temperatures result in higher moisture, accelerating chemical and microbiological deterioration during storage39,40.

Effects of slice thickness and drying temperature on MC (at BT = 5 min).

Furthermore, the slice thickness significantly influenced MC during the falling rate drying period. Increasing slice thickness directly correlated with higher moisture content. For instance, the MC ranges were 4.40%−8.25% for 2 mm, 4.41%−9.01% for 4 mm, and 5.57%−9.77% for 6 mm thicknesses. Samples with a 2 mm thickness dried at 60 °C consistently achieved the lowest moisture 4.4%, whereas 6 mm slices blanched at 60 °C retained a higher moisture of 5.57% at a constant 5-min blanching time. This trend of decreasing MC with decreasing slice thickness was observed across all blanching times. Our recorded MC range was slightly below those observed by Bikila et al. (2020)2, further supporting that moisture removal increases with higher drying temperatures and on thinner Anchote slices.

Blanching is a gentle heat treatment that is used to change the texture, enzyme activity, microbial reduction, taste preservation, nutritional value, and flavor of foods. Blanching involves the use of hot water at temperatures ranging from 60 to 100℃ for an amount of time determined by the materials used. The usage of hot water, on the other hand, can result in a change41. Figure 5 compares the MC of raw and blanched Anchote flour samples dried at different temperatures. At 40 °C, no significant difference (p > 0.05) in MC was observed between raw flour 6.54% and flour from tubers blanched for 5 min 6.85%. The MC was examined at 50 °C for both raw and blanched samples. Notably, a comparatively higher MC of 7.35% was observed for flour obtained from tubers blanched for 10 min.

At 60 °C, the effects of pretreatments were significant. Flour blanched for 10 min showed the highest MC, followed by that blanched for 5 min, and then the raw tubers. This suggests that heat treatment may alter the physicochemical properties of tuber starch, potentially modifying its water-holding capacity compared to flour from raw samples. The MC values observed in this study were comparatively higher than those reported for raw 3.25% and blanched 4.48% potato flours16.

Interaction effects of sample thickness and drying temperature

Anchote tubers are good sources of protein45,46. Figure 7 demonstrates the interaction effects of temperature and slice thickness at the middle blanching time (5 min) on the protein content. The protein content of the Anchote flour samples decreased with an increase in drying temperatures and slice thickness. Nevertheless, when temperature increases beyond 50 °C decrease in CP was observed which is probably because of heat on protein (denaturation) as described by47,48. Interaction influence of slice thickness and blanching time on protein content

The mean CP contents of raw, 5,10 min blanched Anchote flours were analyzed relative to drying temperature and tuber slice thickness. Results showed no significant difference (P > 0.05) in protein content between raw and 5 min blanched Anchote flour. However, protein was significantly reduced in the 10 min blanched sample (Fig. 8). This decrease, approximately 7.87 g/100 g, was notably higher (twice the amount) than reductions reported by Bikila et al. (3.27 g/100 g) and Fekadu (3.14 g/100 g)2,34, likely due to the range of drying temperatures selected in our study. Such decreasing trends in CP content after blanching are consistent with observations in other root and tuber crops35,49, and can be attributed to the water solubility of proteins during extended blanching times The selectivity of protein leaching during blanching is governed by molecular weight and solubility: low-molecular-weight proteins, free amino acids, and peptides are most susceptible to diffusion across the softened, heat-treated cell walls into the aqueous blanching medium. At 5 min of blanching, heat treatment sufficiently inactivates endogenous proteases that would otherwise cause post-harvest protein degradation, partially offsetting solubilisation losses. Beyond 5 min, cumulative leaching becomes dominant and net CP declines significantly. This mechanistic understanding justifies the optimal blanching time of 2.93 min identified in this study29.

Drying temperature also significantly affected the flour samples’ protein content (P< 0.05). Raw and blanched Anchote dried at 40 °C and 50 °C retained more protein than samples subjected to other treatments. Protein content consistently decreased as drying temperature increased, a finding echoed in studies on orange-fleshed sweet potato tubers. This reduction is attributable to thermal denaturation of proteins at higher drying temperatures Beyond 55–60 °C, Maillard reactions between reducing sugars and free amino acid side chains (particularly lysine ε-amino groups) generate melanoidins that resist Kjeldahl hydrolysis, causing an apparent reduction in measurable nitrogen. Thermal denaturation also reduces protein digestibility and functional properties even when total nitrogen is unchanged. These mechanisms collectively explain why maintaining drying temperature at or below 54 °C is critical for preserving the bioavailable protein fraction48.

As a result, Anchote flour that has been pre-drying treated and dried has a higher protein content compared to common root and tuber crops, including sweet potato (3.40 g/100 g), cassava (1.5 g/100 g), and potato (1.70 g/100g)16,50. Due to its superior protein content and complementary amino acid profile, Anchote flour can be blended with conventional tuber flour at 20–30% substitution levels to enhance the overall protein quality and nutritional value of traditional foods such as injera, bread, and porridge, without compromising sensory acceptability.

From these findings, we conclude that Anchote tubers, particularly those blanched in hot water then immersed in sodium chloride (to reduce non-enzymatic browning and drying time) and dried at temperatures between 50 °C and 60 °C, can serve as a valuable protein source, potentially replacing other root and tuber crops often deficient in protein.

Interaction effects of drying temperature and blanching time on CP content

Statistical analysis indicated that a higher drying temperature significantly influenced the CP content of blanched Anchote flour. As depicted in Fig. 9, initial blanching times showed no significant difference in CP between raw (8.82 g/100 g) and blanched (9.05 g/100 g) Anchote flours. Nevertheless, CP content sharply declined to 8.35 g/100 g after 10 min of blanching. This approximately 5.28% decrease in CP following extended blanching corroborates the report by Fekadu34. The consistent reduction in CP across various root and tuber crops after prolonged blanching is often linked to the leaching of water-soluble proteins during extended processing Importantly, even at the observed CP range of 8.35–9.35 g/100 g across all treatment combinations, Anchote flour consistently outperforms common staple crops including cassava (1.0–2.0 g/100 g), potato (2.0 g/100 g), and taro (6.43 g/100 g), confirming its nutritional superiority as a protein source among root and tuber crops29, a trend also observed in another research35,49,51.

The CP content exhibited significant differences (P < 0.05) across all drying temperatures, as evident from Table S2 and Fig. 9. Dried Anchote flour at 40 °C yielded the highest CP (9.20 g/100 g), surpassing levels observed at 50 °C (9.05 g/100 g) and 60 °C (8.69 g/100 g) at the central blanching time. This finding suggests that optimal drying temperature is crucial for preserving Anchote flour’s protein content. Although protein content generally decreases irrespective of drying temperature, raw and 5 min blanched Anchote samples dried at 40 °C and 50 °C maintained higher protein levels compared to those subjected to extended hot water blanching and 60 °C drying. Comparable results have been noted in orange-fleshed sweet potato tubers52.

The decrease in protein content with increasing drying temperature was likely due to protein denaturation caused by heat48. Despite this, blanched and dried Anchote flour exhibits higher protein levels than common root and tuber crops like sweet potato (3.40 g/100 g), cassava (1.5 g/100 g), potato (1.70 g/100 g), yams (purple: 6.84 g/100 g, yellow: 5.62 g/100 g), and taro (6.43 g/100 g)16,33,49,50. These findings suggest that Anchote flour, specifically from blanched tubers dried at 50 °C, could effectively replace other root crops as a protein source.

Effects of independent variables on vitamin C content

Playing a central role in both bodily development and disease prophylaxis, VC offers numerous vital functions. These include its well-known antioxidant properties, involvement in protein synthesis for structural components like tendons, ligaments, and blood vessels, support for wound healing and scar tissue development, and its contribution to the health of cartilage, bone, and teeth. Iron absorption is another critical function attributed to VC53. Experimental data were analysed using RSM/BBD. Determination of vitamin C using UV-visible spectrophotometer

In this work, VC in flour made from dried raw and blanched Anchote tuber slices was determined by considering drying temperature 40–60 °C, blanching time 0–10 min and slice thickness 2–6 mm as important process variables. The maximum VC content (45.35 mg/100 g) was observed at drying temperature of 50 °C, slice thickness of 4 mm and blanching time duration of 5 min, respectively while the lowest value was found as (24.13 mg/100 g) for 10 min blanched sample with 4 mm thickness dried at 60 °C. A negative relationship was observed between VC retention and drying time. Shorter drying times resulted in greater vitamin degradation.

Effects of different temperature and thickness levels on vitamin C

The influence of varying levels of temperature and thickness on VC is discussed below. The VC content decreased progressively as drying temperature increased from 40 °C to 60 °C, with the notable exception that VC retention at 50 °C (44.33 to 45.35 mg/100 g) was higher than at 40 °C (34.32 to 36.58 mg/100 g). This non-linear response can be explained by the opposing effects of temperature and drying time: at 40 °C, the longer drying duration required to achieve the target moisture content results in prolonged thermal exposure of the Vitamin C, leading to greater cumulative degradation. At 50 °C, the shorter drying time compensates for the slightly higher temperature, yielding net higher VC retention. Beyond 50 °C, thermal degradation becomes dominant and VC declines sharply to 23.13 mg/100 g at 60 °C. These results demonstrate that 50–55 °C is the optimal drying temperature range for Anchote flour to preserve Vitamin C, which distinguishes this study from Bikila et al.2, who did not investigate VC retention at temperatures below 60 °C. This non-linear temperature–VC relationship can be understood through competing degradation kinetics. Ascorbic acid (AA) degrades via two principal pathways: aerobic oxidation catalysed by ascorbate oxidase and metal ions, and anaerobic thermal degradation producing furfural and CO₂. At 40 °C, prolonged drying time (8–10 h) results in extended oxygen exposure, favouring cumulative oxidative losses despite the lower temperature. At 50 °C, shorter drying time (5–6 h) reduces this oxidative burden and the temperature is insufficient to substantially accelerate thermal degradation kinetics, yielding the highest net VC retention. At 60 °C, the activation energy threshold for rapid thermal degradation of AA is exceeded, and losses occur faster than the reduced drying time can compensate. This interplay explains why 50–54 °C is the optimal drying window for VC preservation in Anchote flour.

Optimization of flour production from Anchote tuber slice variables

The primary purpose of optimization is to identify the best combination of process variables based on the fitted model to maximize response variables (moisture removal, CP, and VC). Characterization of raw and blanched Anchote flour

Proximate composition

The proximate composition of raw and optimally processed Anchote flour is examined and presented in Table 5. All values are expressed on a dry matter (DM) basis to allow direct comparison with published flour composition data and to eliminate the confounding effect of varying moisture content across samples. This is distinct from Table 1, where fresh and thermally treated tuber data were reported on a wet weight basis. Raw Anchote flour had moisture, CP, crude fiber, ash, and fat concentrations of 5.39, 9.13, 2.61, 6.67, and 0.90, respectively, which were similar to those reported by Yenenesh46. The total carbohydrate content of this flour was 77.2% in the experiment, which was consistent with the values of 73–84.51%reported by10,46, respectively. While the proximate moisture, protein, fat, fiber, ash, carbohydrate, and energy value of blanched flour were 4.50, 9.15, 0.91, 4.37, 3.31, and 82.11 g/100 g and 373.29 Kcal, respectively.

A sample’s MC is a good indicator of its preservation quality, and consequently of its shelf life. As indicated in Table 5, the oven dried Anchote flour sample at OPV had a lower MC(4.50%) than raw flour (5.39%), a decrease of 16.43%, which, due to ion solvation, results in faster and better dehydration produced from the combination as a result of the solute flowing into the products while moisture is transferred from the interior to the NaCl solution62.

The protein content of four blanched Anchote tuber slice samples was 9.15 g/100 g, which was determined to be 0.27% different from raw (9.13 g/100 g) values. The drying of Fritillaria thunbergii Miq flours63 and lily bulb flours64 also showed similar protein reduction patterns with increasing drying temperatures. The protein value in this study for both raw and blanched flour at optimum conditions is higher than that reported by2, with values of 3.76 and 4.13 g/100 g for raw and blanched Anchote flour dried at 100 °C respectively. Protein denaturation due to increased drying temperatures in Anchote slices explains this finding48. Thus, Anchote flour, prepared from blanched tubers at OPV, presents a promising protein source for replacing protein-deficient root and tuber crops.

Even though sample contamination might result in a higher ash content, ash content is an indicator of the mineral content in food. When compared to raw flour (6.66 to 3.31 g/100 g), the flour made from blanched Anchote tuber slices had a 50.31% lower ash content (6.66 to 3.31 g/100 g). Different authors33,34 have found a decrease in the ash content of certain tubers after blanching. The raw flour had a higher ash content than the blanched flour, indicating that minerals were partially leached into the blanching water during processing.

On the other hand, blanching at a higher drying temperature, as shown in Table 5, increases the FC of Anchote flour. At optimum conditions, blanched Anchote flour had a FC of 4.37 g/100 g, which was 67.86% greater than raw flour (2.60 g/100 g), indicating that flour at OPV is a rich source of fiber. This emphasizes its benefits in terms of protecting against certain health issues. The findings of2 on Anchote flour, whose FC increased from 7.54 to 7.63 after blanching, further corroborated the upward trend. The increase could be related to the starch gelatinization and retro-degradation that occurs after heat treatment. As a result, blanched Anchote flour can help to increase dietary fiber by adding it into other foods or by assisting in the development of diets for diabetics and other health-conscious people. The fat content of raw (0.9%) and blanched (0.91%) flour dried at 54 °C did not differ significantly. This result, however, was higher than that reported by2on raw (0.60%) and blanched (0.52%) Anchote flour dried at 80 °C air temperature. An increase in the drying temperature may be to blame for the decreasing variation in lipid content.

Furthermore, higher carbohydrate content at OPV is also noted in Table 5 than raw flour which increased by 6.35%. Generally, Anchote flour obtained from the blanched tuber provides a higher CH content. Because flour contains a high amount of carbohydrate, it can be used as an alternative source of energy, resulting in a higher energy value from raw to blanched flour of (355.77 to 373.29) Kcal, an increase of 4.92% when raw flour is used as a reference.

Ensuring a sufficient intake of specific, metabolically active nutrients and minerals can effectively prevent diseases. Root and tuber crops stand out as key mineral sources, associated with preventing common deficiencies such as anemia and rickets, thus promoting their regular consumption66,67. Among these, dried Anchote slices are particularly rich in minerals, proteins, and vitamins, possessing abundant nutrient components that confer considerable healing properties3,7,11.

Conclusion

This study optimised thermal processing of Anchote (Coccinia abyssinica) tuber flour using RSM/BBD. Optimal conditions slice thickness of 2 mm, blanching time of 2.93 min, and drying temperature of 54.26 °C yielded MC of 4.50%, CP of 9.15 g/100 g, and VC of 45.35 mg/100 g, experimentally validated with less than 0.3% deviation from predicted values. All three parameters significantly affected (p < 0.05) the responses, with slice thickness being the most influential. The low drying temperature of 54 °C is a notable finding, effectively preserving heat-sensitive Vitamin C while achieving safe moisture levels for ambient storage. These results confirm that the RSM/BBD framework provides a reliable approach for developing evidence-based processing protocols for Anchote flour production.

The flour’s superior protein content (9.15 g/100 g) surpasses cassava (1.0–2.0 g/100 g) and potato (2.0 g/100 g), making it suitable for fortifying traditional food products such as injera, bread, porridge, biscuits, and infant foods at 20–30% substitution levels, thereby supporting nutritional quality, food security, and economic development in Ethiopia. The protocol is scalable using tunnel or solar dryers at 50–55 °C with pre-slicing to 2–3 mm and 3–5 min blanching, making it practical for small- and medium-scale processing enterprises.

Key limitations include the absence of microbiological safety testing, sensory evaluation, and industrial-scale validation. Results are based on a single accession and laboratory-scale conditions.

Future research should address: (i) microbiological safety and storage stability; (ii) sensory evaluation and consumer acceptability; (iii) industrial-scale drying validation; (iv) functional properties (WAI, WSI, gelation); (v) bioactive compounds and anti-nutritional factors; and (vi) economic and life-cycle assessment of the processing protocol.
