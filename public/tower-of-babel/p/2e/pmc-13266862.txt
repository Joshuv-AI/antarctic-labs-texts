Long-term outcomes of lung transplantation in the elderly: an analysis using the UNOS database
Authors: Osnat Shtraichman, J Sam Meyer, Arielle Zur, Gil Leor, Dan Aravot, Yury Peysakhovich, Yael Shostak, Mordechai R Kramer, Yaron D Barac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung transplantation (LT) remains a life-saving intervention for patients with end-stage lung disease, but recipient age continues to be a key determinant of long-term outcomes. As the candidate population ages and allocation policies shift, understanding how age and donor-recipient variables impact survival is increasingly critical. This study aimed to evaluate the association between recipient age and long-term survival after LT, and to characterize age-specific differences in mortality risk factors in a national cohort.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 30,494 adult LT recipients using data from the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) from 2006 to 2022. Recipients were stratified by age at transplant into three groups: 18–64, 65–69, and ≥70 years. Primary outcome was all-cause mortality; secondary outcomes included graft dysfunction, rejection rates, and cause of death. Kaplan-Meier and Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate survival risk factors.ResultsSurvival declined progressively with increasing age, with recipients aged ≥70 years showing significantly lower 5-year survival. Multivariable analysis identified treated rejection, low body mass index (BMI), pre-transplant intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and post-transplant dialysis as independent predictors of mortality. The impact of risk factors varied by age: rejection had less effect in older recipients, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) predicted mortality only in younger patients, and dialysis was significant mainly in the 65–69 years group. Importantly, donor age and sex did not significantly affect survival.ConclusionsAdvanced recipient age is independently associated with reduced long-term survival after LT, and this association persists after adjustment for donor and procedural factors. The relative importance of risk predictors varies across age groups, highlighting distinct, age-dependent risk profiles that may inform candidate selection and counselling. Together, these findings underscore the need to further refine selection strategies to support the ethical and effective allocation of scarce donor lungs.Keywords: Lung transplantation (LT), post-transplant survival, frailty, allocation policy, elderly patients

Highlight box

Key findings

• Long-term survival after lung transplantation (LT) decreases progressively with advancing recipient age.

• Predictors of mortality shift by age group: rejection and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support are strongest in younger adults, whereas systemic factors—low body mass index, pre-transplant intensive care unit stay, and post-transplant dialysis—predominate in older recipients.

• Donor age and donor sex do not significantly influence survival, supporting the safe use of older donor lungs.

What is known and what is new?

• Older recipients have worse long-term outcomes after LT. Prior studies have focused mainly on procedural factors or single-center experiences, limiting the ability to delineate age-dependent risk patterns.

• Using a national cohort of 30,494 recipients, this study provides the largest contemporary analysis of age-specific risk profiles in LT. We demonstrate that the major drivers of mortality differ by age, with systemic physiologic vulnerability outweighing graft-related determinants in older adults. Donor age does not adversely affect long-term survival, challenging historical assumptions about the suitability of older donor lungs.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Chronological age alone should not exclude patients from LT. Candidate assessment should incorporate frailty, physiologic reserve, and comorbidity burden to improve risk stratification in elderly recipients.

• Donor acceptance practices can be broadened to include older donor lungs without compromising survival.

• These findings support refining age-informed selection criteria to ensure more ethical and effective lung allocation.

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LT) has become an increasingly effective treatment for patients with end-stage lung disease. However, ensuring fair and optimal allocation of limited donor lungs remains a central challenge. The United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) first addressed this issue in 1990 with a system based on ABO blood group compatibility and time on the waitlist (1). While simple, this “first-come, first-served” approach failed to account for medical urgency, contributing to high waitlist mortality. A major shift occurred in 2005 with the introduction of the Lung Allocation Score (LAS), which prioritized candidates based on both disease severity and predicted post-transplant survival (2). This change reduced waitlist mortality and reshaped the transplant landscape (3).

According to the 2022 Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) report, the demographics of LT candidates have undergone a significant shift. Candidates aged 65 years and older now represent 36.9% of the waitlist—an increase of 63.5% since 2011—while the number of younger candidates (18–34 years) has dropped by over 50%. There has also been a surge in transplants for patients with restrictive lung disease, now comprising over two-thirds of the waitlist. Concurrently, LAS scores have trended higher, with more patients scoring above 60, reflecting an increase in medical severity among candidates (4).

In 2023, UNOS introduced the Continuous Distribution (CD) framework and the Composite Allocation Score (CAS), further refining the system. CAS balances waitlist urgency and post-transplant survival equally and incorporates geographic proximity into allocation decisions to improve equity and access (5,6).

Against this evolving backdrop, age remains a critical factor. Although the 2015 International Society of Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) guidelines listed age >65 years as a relative contraindication (7), the 2021 update now categorizes age 65–70 years as a risk factor and age >70 years as high risk (8). These revisions acknowledge improved short-term outcomes in older recipients but do not fully address their poorer long-term survival (9).

Given the increasing number of older LT candidates and persistent organ scarcity, a deeper understanding of long-term outcomes across recipient age cohorts and how donor characteristics (including donor age) relate to outcomes is essential.

In this study, we assess post-transplant survival across recipient age groups and, importantly, characterize age-stratified mortality risk factors in a national cohort to clarify how the relative impact of recipient, donor, and early post-transplant factors differs by recipient age. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2473/rc).

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the SRTR files provided by the UNOS (https://hrsa.unos.org/data/view-data-reports/request-data/). The dataset included de-identified patient-level and center-level data on lung transplant recipients between January 2006 and June 2022. This study adhered to guidelines for the use of registry data, and all analyses were conducted in compliance with relevant ethical standards. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board of Rabin Medical Center (IRB No. 172 Pro00073879), and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Study population

We identified lung transplant recipients during the study period and applied inclusion and exclusion criteria. Patients who had lung re-transplantation or multiple-organ transplants were excluded. The final cohort (n=30,494) was divided into three age groups: 18–64 years (n=20,885, 68.5%), 65–69 years (n=6,675, 21.9%), and ≥70 years (n=2,934, 9.6%).

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, as recorded in the SRTR registry. Secondary outcomes included the incidence of primary graft dysfunction (PGD), length of hospital stay (LOS), treatment for rejection within the first year post-transplant, renal failure requiring hemodialysis, chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD)/bronchiolitis obliterans syndrome (BOS), and the primary cause of death. When examined as predictors of longer-term mortality, early post-transplant events were analyzed using a 1-year landmark design as described below.

Variable definitions (SRTR)

“Treated rejection within the first year” was defined as any reported episode of acute rejection requiring treatment during post-transplant days 0–365, as captured in SRTR follow-up reporting. Post-transplant dialysis and stroke were defined as SRTR-reported dialysis requirement and stroke occurrence within the first post-transplant year. CLAD BOS, reported in the SRTR as chronic rejection or BOS, was defined using the SRTR follow-up indicator for chronic rejection/BOS as documented in follow-up records. Ischemic time was modelled as the SRTR-reported total allograft ischemic time in hours (procurement/cross-clamp to reperfusion).

Variables and comparisons

Recipient, donor, and transplant-related characteristics were pre-selected for comparison among the three age groups. Key recipient variables included demographics, pre-transplant health status, and diagnosis. Donor characteristics included age, cause of death, and other relevant factors. Transplant-related variables encompassed ischemic time, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) mismatch, and support requirements such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO).

Statistical analysis

Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier estimates to determine survival probabilities at 30 days, 3 months, 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years for each age group. The log-rank test was used to compare survival distributions across age categories.

A multivariate Cox model identified predictors of 1- and 5-year mortality using a complete case approach. Recipients with missing covariate data and under 1-year follow-up that caused non-convergence were excluded. We report covariate-level missingness and resulting exclusions in Table S1 and summarize cohort derivation in Figure S1. Because several covariates of interest occur after transplantation (e.g., treatment for rejection within the first year, post-transplant dialysis, post-transplant stroke, and LOS), we evaluated associations between these early post-transplant events and subsequent mortality using a 1-year landmark design to mitigate immortal-time and reverse-causality bias. Specifically, landmark analyses were restricted to recipients alive/at risk at 1-year post-transplant [post-transplant time (PTIME) ≥365 days], and mortality was modeled from day 365 forward. To assess potential selection bias, we compared baseline characteristics of included vs. excluded landmark-eligible recipients (Table S2).

The model included recipient variables: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), transplantation indication, diabetes history, and waitlist status, and donor variables: age, sex, smoking history, hypertension, cocaine use, donor cause of death, ischemic time, ABO blood type, and HLA mismatch level, selected via backward stepwise inclusion at a P value threshold of 0.25. Hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to assess the strength of each predictor. Our analysis examined these effects across age groups (18–64, 65–69, and ≥70 years) and included an overall analysis without age stratification. Given major U.S. lung allocation changes during the study period, we adjusted all Cox models for transplant era (pre- vs. post-2017-11-25) and included transplant year as a continuous covariate to account for secular trends.

Recipient characteristics

The youngest cohort (18–64 years) accounted for 76.8% of the total recipients, followed by 15.6% in the 65–69 years age group and 7.6% in the ≥70 years age group. Male recipients comprised 56.4% of the youngest group, 65.3% of those aged 65–69 years, and 74.7% of recipients aged ≥70 years (P<0.05). The mean BMI increased slightly with age, and BMI <18.5 kg/m2 was most prevalent in the youngest cohort (8.1%), compared to 2.7% in the 65–69 years group and 1.6% in recipients aged ≥70 years.

Pulmonary diagnoses varied significantly across age groups (P<0.05). Interstitial lung disease (ILD) was predominant in older recipients, comprising 58.0% of those aged 65–69 years and 73.6% in recipients aged ≥70 years. In contrast, cystic fibrosis was seen almost exclusively in younger recipients, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)/emphysema was more common in the 65–69 years cohort. Intensive care unit (ICU) hospitalization prior to transplant occurred in 13.8% of recipients aged 18–64 years but decreased to 6.4% in the ≥70 years group (P<0.05). Procedure type differed markedly by age. Double-LT was performed in 80.8% of recipients aged 18–64 years, compared with 57.7% in those aged 65–69 years and 39.7% in recipients aged ≥70 years (Table 1). Outcomes by age cohort

The median LOS was 17 days (IQR, 12–29 days) for recipients aged 18–64 years, 16 days (IQR, 11–27 days) for those aged 65–69 years, and 16 days (IQR, 11–27 days) for recipients aged ≥70 years (P<0.05). Treated rejection within the first year post-transplant occurred in 20.1% of recipients aged 18–64 years, compared to 18.1% in the 65–69 years group and 16.2% in recipients aged ≥70 years (P<0.05). Chronic rejection rates also decreased with age from 51% in the 18–64 years group, to 48.5% in the 65–69 years group, and 37% recipients aged ≥70 years (P<0.05). PGD occurred at similar rates across all age groups and was not significantly different.

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significant differences in survival probabilities across age groups at key time points (30 days, 3 months, 1 year, 3 years, and 5 years). The probability of survival decreased with age, particularly over longer follow-up periods, as shown in Table 3 and Figure 1.

Cause of death analysis

Cause of death varied significantly by age group, with a global chi-square test confirming a statistically significant difference in cause of death distribution across recipient age categories (χ2=161.9, degrees of freedom =18, P<0.001). Graft failure or a pulmonary-related event, followed by infections, were the most common causes of death in all age groups. Graft failure per se was the most common cause of death in the 18–64 years group (19%), and its frequency declined progressively with increasing age (15% in 65–69 years and 12% in ≥70 years; P<0.001). In contrast, cardio or cerebrovascular event-related deaths increased with age above 65 years, accounting for 10% of deaths in the 18–64 years group and 12% for those aged above 65 years (P=0.001). Malignancy-related deaths were also significantly more frequent in older recipients, occurring in 10% of recipients aged 18–64 years, 12% in those aged 65–69 years, and 14% in the ≥70 years group (P<0.001) (Figure 2).

Multivariate analysis

Recipient variables

In 1-year landmark multivariable Cox models (mortality modeled from 1-year post-transplant forward) identified several predictors of post-transplant mortality, some of which were consistently significant across all age groups, while others showed age-dependent effects. ECMO at transplant, treated rejection within the first year, low BMI (<18.5 kg/m2), post-transplant dialysis, and donor-recipient HLA mismatch were significant predictors of increased mortality in the overall cohort analyses (HR =1.54, P=0.007), (HR =1.31, P<0.001), (HR =1.24, P<0.001), (HR =1.2, P=0.004), and (HR =1.18, P=0.03), respectively. Notably, the effect of post-transplant treatment for rejection declined with increasing age, with no clear association observed among recipients aged ≥70 years. Procedure type differed markedly by age, with double-LT performed in 80.8% of recipients aged 18–64 years, 57.7% of those aged 65–69 years, and 39.7% of those aged ≥70 years. Given the strong association between procedure type and post-transplant outcomes, double-LT was included as a key covariate in all multivariable models. In the adjusted landmark model, double-LT was independently associated with lower mortality (HR =0.65, 95% CI: 0.62–0.68, P<0.001); however, recipient age remained a strong predictor of mortality among both single- and double-lung transplant recipients (age 65–69 years: HR =1.37, 95% CI: 1.31–1.43; age ≥70 years: HR =1.56, 95% CI: 1.46–1.67; both P<0.001). In sensitivity analyses stratified by procedure type, older recipient age was associated with worse survival within both single-lung (age 65–69 years: HR =1.30, 95% CI: 1.21–1.40; age ≥70 years: HR =1.48, 95% CI: 1.35–1.62) and double-lung transplant recipients (age 65–69 years: HR =1.40, 95% CI: 1.32–1.50; age ≥70 years: HR =1.71, 95% CI: 1.55–1.89). An age-by-procedure interaction was statistically significant (interaction P=0.003) (Table S3).

In contrast, several variables demonstrated significance only within specific age groups. ECMO at transplant was the strongest overall predictor of mortality (HR =1.54, P=0.007), but this association reached significance only in the 18–64 years cohort (HR =1.41, P<0.001) and not in older groups. Post-transplant dialysis showed a significant association with mortality in the 65–69 years age group (HR =1.4, P=0.008) but not in younger recipients. Additionally, an ischemic time of ≥6 hours was unexpectedly associated with reduced overall mortality (HR =0.91, P=0.002). Transplant year was independently associated with mortality (HR =1.014 per year, 95% CI: 1.007–1.020, P<0.001), likely reflecting temporal changes in recipient risk profile and increasing clinical complexity over time, whereas the post-2017-11-25 allocation era indicator itself was not significantly associated with survival (HR =1.021, 95% CI: 0.958–1.088, P=0.53).

Several baseline recipient variables were not independently associated with post-transplant mortality in multivariable analyses. Recipient sex was not a significant predictor in any age group, with HRs close to 1 (Figure 3). Similarly, the pulmonary diagnostic category did not significantly influence survival. Renal function, assessed by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), was not associated with mortality in any cohort (HR =1.00, 95% CI: 1.00–1.01, P=0.47 in the overall model).

Donor variables

Donor age and sex were not significantly associated with post-transplant mortality in any recipient age group. Donor smoking history, however, was linked to increased mortality in recipients aged 18–64 years (HR =1.24, P<0.001) and 65–69 years (HR =1.14, P=0.049), but not in those ≥70 years. Donor diabetes was a significant risk factor only in the 18–64 years cohort (HR =1.10, P=0.048). Interestingly, donor history of heavy alcohol use was associated with lower mortality in recipients aged 18–64 years (HR =0.88, P<0.001), but this effect was not observed in older groups and reversed in those ≥70 years. Donor death due to anoxia was associated with improved survival in the younger age group (below 70 years), but not in the older age group (HR =0.75, P<0.001 in 18–64 years; HR =0.81, P<0.001 in 65–69 years; HR =0.89, P=0.13 in ≥70 years). Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of survival outcomes across different recipient age cohorts, highlighting a clear disparity in long-term survival rates among older LT recipients. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have identified age as a significant determinant of post-transplant mortality (10-12). Notably, our findings show that this disparity extends not only to recipients aged ≥70 years, currently defined as high-risk in the updated 2021 ISHLT consensus statement for candidate selection (8), but also to those aged 65–69 years, previously considered a relative contraindication to transplant (7). While advances in surgical techniques and postoperative management have led to improved short and long-term survival following LT over time (13,14), and similar survival within the first year for older recipients (9), this population, particularly those aged ≥70 years, continues to experience diminished long-term survival compared to younger age groups.

Although elderly candidates undergo rigorous selection processes, they often carry a greater burden of comorbidities that are not fully captured in registry-based analyses. Conditions such as hypertension, atherosclerosis, coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, valvular disease, depression, and obesity are more prevalent in this age group (15,16). While each may not individually preclude transplantation, their cumulative or “stacked” presence exerts a synergistic effect, compromising recovery and long-term survival (17,18). Frailty further compounds this risk. Although frailty is not directly measured in SRTR/UNOS, its prevalence increases beyond age 60 years, and when superimposed on existing comorbidities, it significantly worsens post-transplant survival. Studies have consistently demonstrated its prognostic importance in LT (19,20). Despite this, a standardized approach to frailty assessment remains lacking. The recently developed Lung Transplant Frailty Scale offers a promising, transplant-specific tool to quantify physical frailty and may help identify high-risk candidates, guiding targeted prehabilitation efforts (21). These findings collectively suggest that long-term outcomes in older recipients maybe shaped more by systemic health status and physiologic reserve than by graft function alone.

Our multivariable models support this interpretation. Among recipients aged 65–69 years, global health indicators—such as pre-transplant ICU stay, post-transplant dialysis, and low BMI—were stronger predictors of mortality. While these factors did not reach statistical significance among recipients ≥70 years, this is likely due to the smaller number of high-risk individuals in that age group, limiting statistical power. These findings underscore the increased vulnerability of the aging population to non-graft-related complications. Procedure type varied markedly by age, with older recipients substantially less likely to undergo double-LT. Although double-LT was independently associated with improved survival, adjustment for procedure type did not attenuate the strong association between advancing age and mortality. Moreover, age remained a significant predictor of worse outcomes within both single- and double-lung transplant recipients, indicating that age-related survival differences are not explained by procedure selection alone. The significant age-by-procedure interaction further suggests that while double-LT confers an overall survival benefit, this benefit may be attenuated in older recipients. Importantly, the attenuated association between treated rejection and long-term survival in older recipients further suggests that mortality in this population is increasingly driven by age-related comorbidities and diminished physiological reserve rather than graft failure alone. Several alternative explanations may also contribute to this finding, including survivorship bias and potential age-related differences in the recognition or reporting of rejection episodes. However, because rejection events are captured using the same reporting mechanisms across age groups, differential reporting alone is unlikely to fully account for the observed differences. In parallel, lower observed BOS/CLAD rates in older recipients should be interpreted in the context of competing mortality, as diagnosis requires sufficient survival and longitudinal follow-up. Taken together, these findings suggest that post-transplant outcomes in older lung transplant recipients are significantly influenced by systemic vulnerability rather than by graft-specific factors alone, underscoring the need for a more comprehensive approach to candidate assessment that extends beyond traditional graft-centered metrics. In a cohort with a relatively young donor population (median donor age 33 years), recipient age remained associated with post-transplant survival after adjustment for donor age, suggesting that donor age alone is unlikely to account for the observed age-related differences in outcomes. Historically, an age below 55 years has been considered the ideal donor age (22). Several studies suggested a negative impact of donor age >55 years on long-term outcomes following transplantation (23,24). Yet, numerous studies challenge the assumption that chronological age alone determines graft viability (25-27), including the 2020 ISHLT Registry Report, which demonstrates that the effect of older donor age on survival is modest and largely limited to the first year post-transplant (28). Most notably, a recent study employed a propensity score-matched design to rigorously account for confounding factors and found no difference in post-transplant outcomes between donors aged ≥70 years and younger donors (29), highlighting that previously observed risks may have been driven by confounding factors such as comorbidities, recipient selection, or center-level variation.

In contrast, specific donor characteristics showed age-dependent effects. In our analysis, donor smoking history was associated with increased mortality in younger recipients (HR =1.24 in 18–64 years, P<0.001; HR =1.14 in 65–69 years, P=0.049), but not in recipients ≥70 years. These findings are in line with earlier studies linking donor smoking to reduced allograft function and slightly lower survival (24,30). However, evidence from a multicenter prospective cohort study by the Lung Transplant Outcomes Group (LTOG) adds nuance to this relationship. In that study, donor smoking, defined using both clinical history and urinary biomarkers, was associated with a modest but measurable increase in the risk of PGD, with absolute risk increases of up to 11.5%. Yet, it was not associated with differences in long-term recipient survival, regardless of the exposure definition (31). This discrepancy highlights the limitations of retrospective studies, which show associations but not causality, whereas prospective designs, such as the LTOG study, better capture true clinical effects.

Donor diabetes was an independent risk factor in the 18–64 years group (HR =1.10, P=0.048), consistent with ISHLT Registry findings (28). Although the ISHLT report lacked multivariable modeling due to sample size limitations, these results align with prior adjusted analyses, which have shown reduced survival (32). Interestingly, our study found that longer donor ischemic time was associated with better survival—a counterintuitive finding that has also been reported in the ISHLT Registry (33). This counterintuitive association should be interpreted with caution and likely reflects residual confounding or selection and utilization effects rather than a true protective biological mechanism. Similarly, the observed association between donor history of heavy alcohol use and lower mortality, an unexpected direction of effect, can be due to potential misclassification in donor history reporting and unmeasured confounding. These unclear mechanisms likely reflect the inherent limitations of retrospective registry data, which reveal associations but cannot fully account for their full complexity. In our study, donor death from anoxia was consistently associated with improved survival across all recipient age groups. Prior studies have raised concerns regarding the impact of traumatic brain injury (TBI) as a donor cause of death, speculating that neurohormonal disturbances following TBI could impair lung graft quality (34). However, more recent evidence, also using a propensity score–matched design, demonstrated comparable 5-year survival rates between recipients of lungs from TBI and non-TBI donors, thereby challenging this notion (35).

Taken together, these findings highlight the multifactorial and time-dependent nature of donor-related influences on transplant outcomes. They also underscore the limitations of relying solely on retrospective registry data to guide donor selection. Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter investigations that can better account for the interplay of donor, recipient, and procedural factors. Until then, donor evaluation should be individualized, balancing short-term risk with long-term benefit, and informed by a comprehensive assessment of donor characteristics.

While this study provides valuable insights into survival outcomes among elderly lung transplant recipients, several limitations should be acknowledged. As a retrospective analysis of registry data, it is inherently subject to unmeasured confounding and reporting bias. The dataset lacks important clinical variables such as frailty, cognitive function, functional status, and quality-of-life measures, all of which are known to influence outcomes in older adults. Accordingly, any discussion of frailty and physiologic reserve is inferential and intended to contextualize observed age-associated risk patterns rather than establish causality.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study confirms that age, especially ≥70 years, is a key predictor of reduced long-term survival after LT. While advances in surgical care and updated ISHLT guidelines reflect growing confidence in offering transplantation to older adults, the long-term benefits remain limited due to a greater comorbidity burden, reduced physiologic reserve, and an increased risk of age-related complications. Frailty, a powerful yet underused prognostic factor, should be systematically incorporated into candidate evaluation. Importantly, donor age did not significantly affect post-transplant mortality, supporting the safety of accepting older donor offers. Given ongoing organ scarcity, transplant decisions for elderly candidates must go beyond chronological age and consider frailty, functional status, and overall health. Refining age-informed selection criteria is essential to improve outcomes and ensure ethical, effective use of donor lungs.
