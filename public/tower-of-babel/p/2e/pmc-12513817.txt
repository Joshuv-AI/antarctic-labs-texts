Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome: A Case Report of the Oldest Man in Literature

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA diagnosis of neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) was made in a 96‐year‐old man, representing the oldest documented case in the existing medical literature. Even at very low doses of antipsychotic medications, older adults are more likely to develop NMS. Introduction

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rare and idiosyncratic but serious and potentially life‐threatening adverse reaction to antipsychotic medications, characterized by a sudden onset and unpredictable course [1]. It typically occurs in reaction to dopamine receptor antagonists, such as typical and atypical antipsychotics, metoclopramide, and even following the abrupt discontinuation of dopaminergic agents like levodopa and amantadine. The underlying mechanism is believed to involve blockade of central dopamine receptors, leading to a critical reduction in dopamine activity in the brain [2, 3]. Patients who have recently started using antipsychotic medications or experienced dose changes are at risk of NMS. Patients on neuroleptic drugs have incidence rates of NMS ranging from 0.01% to 3.2% [4, 5].

According to the review, there are few reports of NMS in patients taking long‐term sustained doses of typical antipsychotics [6, 7].

Risk factors identified for the development of NMS include male gender (with a male‐to‐female ratio of 2:1), age under 40, neurological disorders, certain medical conditions, use of antipsychotics in the postpartum period, extrapyramidal symptoms, intellectual disability (ID), restlessness, dehydration, physical limitations, iron (Fe) deficiency anemia, long‐term use or dose escalation of antipsychotics, high ambient temperature, and a prior history of NMS [8, 9]. A retrospective chart review from a tertiary neuropsychiatric facility on patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of a tertiary health‐care center between 2017 and March 2023 was examined by Sen et al. Of the 21 patients diagnosed with NMS, complete medical records were available for 12 individuals. Among these cases, the majority were men (76%), with an average age of approximately 36 years. Given that younger patients are typically prescribed lower doses of antipsychotic medications, their outcomes—assuming no significant comorbidities—may be relatively more favorable [10].

In this context, Lindeman et al. (2020) [11] reported a case of a man over 60 years old with schizoaffective disorder and oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) who was treated with oral psychotropic medications, including perphenazine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, and valproate. They described his experience with NMS, suggesting that the concurrent use of multiple medications may have triggered or accelerated the onset of the syndrome [11].

Additionally, Sanger et al. [7] reported the case of an 82‐year‐old woman who had developed NMS after being treated with high‐dose antipsychotics—specifically zuclopenthixol—for 7 years prior to the onset of the syndrome. Additionally, Eroğlu et al. [8] described an 82‐year‐old man who had atypical NMS brought on by low‐dose quetiapine. After being diagnosed with dementia, he was treated with donepezil (10 mg/day). Notably, another clinic had also prescribed quetiapine (12.5 mg/day) for 3 months to address complaints of irritability and insomnia [8]. Although numerous case reports of NMS in older adults exist in the literature, this report is the first to describe the condition in the oldest known patient, who had been treated with low‐dose haloperidol for 1 year.

Reports in the literature indicated that NMS can develop in older adults even when very low doses of typical antipsychotics are administered. This case further demonstrates that minimal doses of common antipsychotic medications may still trigger NMS in elderly patients. Additional risk factors—such as preexisting neurological disorders or the concurrent use of cholinesterase inhibitors—may exacerbate the severity of the syndrome. It is exceedingly rare for patients with coexisting conditions such as advanced age, Alzheimer's disease (AD), and opium use disorder (OUD) to develop NMS as a result of stable, low‐dose haloperidol treatment. The scientific knowledge of such situations can be greatly enhanced by this case. Here, we report a rare case of NMS in a 96‐year‐old man with AD and opium use disorder, making it the oldest documented case of NMS in the literature.

Case Presentation

Case History/Examination

The case involved a 96‐year‐old man with a history of opium use disorder (OUD) and (AD), who was brought by his family to the emergency room of a teaching hospital in northern Iran in 2023. The patient was hospitalized immediately following presentation to the emergency room, and after diagnosis of aspiration pneumonia, he was admitted to the internal medicine ward. He presented with a decreased level of consciousness (LOC), which did not improve after receiving intravenous naloxone at a dose of 0.4 mg, up to a total of 10 mg. Initially, due to decreased LOC and the possibility of opiate overdose, naloxone was administered; however, the patient did not respond.

He had been taking oral opiates at a dose of 0.25 mg/day over the past year and had a history of smoking opium for approximately 10 years.

After being diagnosed with aspiration pneumonia, the patient was transferred to the internal medicine department for further care. At the time of admission to the emergency room, his vital signs were as follows: a temperature of 38.7°C, a pulse rate (PR) of 105 beats per minute, a respiratory rate (RR) of 33 breaths per minute, an arterial blood pressure (BP) of 125/98 mmHg, and an oxygen saturation of 93% while receiving oxygen via a reservoir bag mask. Although his GCS score was 14, indicating mild impairment, the patient showed clear clinical evidence of confusion, disorientation, and stupor during psychiatric evaluation. The complete blood count (CBC) revealed a leukocyte count of 16.5 × 103/μL (Table 1).

Serum electrolyte levels and cardiac enzymes, including troponin, were within normal limits. Routine urinalysis revealed no abnormalities except for proteinuria (albuminuria) and glycosuria. A chest X‐ray showed infiltration consistent with aspiration pneumonia (Figure 1). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for COVID‐19 and influenza was negative. The electrocardiogram (ECG) demonstrated normal sinus rhythm (NSR). Brain computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed no evidence of ischemic or hemorrhagic lesions. The echocardiogram showed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 55%, and normal left ventricular systolic function (LVSF). Intravenous (IV) Meropenem (500 mg) was administered twice daily, and vancomycin (1 g) was given every 48 h.

The patient had been taking 0.5 mg of haloperidol daily for 1 year to manage dementia‐related agitation, with no recent changes in dosage. Due to a history of haloperidol use, a psychiatric consultation was requested. According to the assessment by the consultant‐liaison psychiatrist, the patient exhibited stupor, confusion, and restlessness. Mental status examination revealed a lack of cooperation, disorientation to time and place, and inattention. Neurological examination for muscle rigidity showed resistance to eye opening (REO), negativism, and positive gegenhalten.

Therefore, an emergency blood test was ordered to measure creatine phosphokinase (CPK) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels. The patient's serum iron (Fe) level and total iron‐binding capacity (TIBC) were 33 μg/dL (reference range: 60–180) and 206 μg/dL (reference range: 220–420), respectively, suggesting iron deficiency likely related to changes in acute phase reactants (APR).

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

In addition, the patient exhibited muscle rigidity, a body temperature above 38.5°C, altered mental status, refusal to eat and drink, leukocytosis, increased CPK levels, and signs of autonomic dysfunction symptoms such as fluctuating blood pressure and an increased respiratory rate (RR). Notably, serotonin syndrome was ruled out due to the absence of hyperactive bowel sounds, diarrhea, chills, mydriasis (dilated pupils), hyperreflexia, clonus, and myoclonus. A diagnosis of haloperidol‐induced NMS was made based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5) criteria and a Naranjo Adverse Drug Reaction (ARD) Probability Scale score of +9 (Table 2) [12, 13].

Although there was no increase in the haloperidol dosage, factors such as the patient's male sex, age over 65, cognitive decline, and limited physical activity prior to admission may have contributed to the development of NMS. The likelihood of dehydration could not be determined. After the diagnosis of NMS, haloperidol was discontinued, and supportive care was initiated. The patient's vital signs were closely monitored. His elevated body temperature was managed, fluid therapy (FT) was administered, and electrolyte imbalance was corrected. Subsequently, bromocriptine (2.5 mg) was given three times daily via gavage, followed by lorazepam (1 mg) administered intramuscularly (IM) twice daily, along with continuous monitoring of partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) and oxygen monitoring.

Due to the favorable therapeutic response to the prescribed doses and medications, the dosage was not increased, but was instead gradually tapered over the course of a week and discontinued by the tenth day. Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

CPK levels and leukocyte counts gradually decreased over the following days. Muscle rigidity and decreased LOC gradually improved within 10 days. Since the low serum iron levels were likely due to an acute phase reactant, it was decided to follow up 2 weeks after discharge and, if necessary, refer the patient to a gastroenterologist.

Discussion

This report described the oldest patient diagnosed with NMS reported in the literature to date—an individual with comorbid AD who had been treated with low‐dose haloperidol for 1 year. A study conducted up to 1987 on NMS in older adults included 18 patients aged 60 and above, with the oldest being 71 years old [14]. Nicklason et al. [15] identified five NMS patients among nearly 13,000 geriatric care admissions over a 2‐year period. Additionally, Ryken and Merrell [16] reported the case of a 67‐year‐old woman who developed NMS after being treated with haloperidol and was subsequently admitted for dementia assessment. She had previously received low doses of haloperidol with no complications, and there was a clear temporal relationship between a dose increase and the onset of her symptoms [16].

Some laboratory abnormalities—key characteristics of NMS—may be associated with the APR changes observed in the older patient described in this case report. APRs are nonspecific inflammatory markers that fluctuated significantly in response to inflammation [17, 18]. Numerous studies have reported elevations in various APRs during episodes of NMS. Several proposed mechanisms for APR induction in NMS include autoantibody production, virus‐drug interactions, heat stress, rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown), and psychological stress [17].

The acute phase response is a complex physiological reaction that typically occurs in response to tissue injury. In this case, there was an increase in CPK, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), CRP, interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), alpha‐1 antichymotrypsin (ACT), and fibrinogen levels, along with a decrease in iron (Fe) and albumin (AL) levels. Negative acute phase reactants are substances that decrease during inflammation, such as iron, albumin, and transferrin. The role of acute phase reactants in inflammation is multifaceted and, in some cases, not fully understood. These findings were consistent with activation of the APR [19].

Furthermore, certain medical conditions and the concurrent use of medications in older adults may slow the metabolism of antipsychotics, leading to their accumulation in the body [1]. Although NMS has been reported in patients who had been taking neuroleptics for an extended period prior to symptom onset—as was the case in the patient described here—it most commonly develops following a change in medication or an abrupt increase in dosage. In older adults, organic brain disease has been identified as a risk factor for the development of NMS [16]. Additionally, some case reports have linked NMS to the use of cholinesterase inhibitors, particularly donepezil, either alone or in combination with antipsychotics [8, 20, 21].

In patients with dementia, confusion may go unnoticed, making the diagnosis and management of NMS in older adults particularly challenging. In addition to the diagnostic difficulties, there is an increased risk of complications and higher mortality rates in this population [15]. In this context, Shea and Chu [22] reported a case of quetiapine‐induced NMS in a 69‐year‐old man with Lewy body dementia.

Similarly, Isik and Soysal [23] described a 75‐year‐old woman with corticobasal degeneration (CBD), normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH), cognitive impairment, and Parkinsonism who also developed NMS. Since primary and secondary Parkinsonian dementia syndromes often co‐occur in older patients—and dopaminergic agents appear to be potential risk factors for NMS—such medications should be used with caution in this population [23].

Overall, NMS is challenging to diagnose in older adults, as respiratory infections can cause acute confusion in this population [24], as was observed in the present case. Increased muscle rigidity may also be overlooked in individuals with Parkinson's disease [15]. Consequently, the mortality rate in older adults may be higher due to the presence of additional complications, such as infections, acute renal failure, or hypotension. Despite the presence of aspiration pneumonia and an acute rise in CPK levels in this patient, early detection and appropriate treatment led to a successful recovery.

In light of this, it is recommended that a comprehensive assessment—including detailed neurological and radiological evaluations—be conducted for optimal management of older patients in order to minimize diagnostic errors and avoid ineffective treatments.
