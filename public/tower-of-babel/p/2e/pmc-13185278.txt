Computed tomography in ARDS, from morphological insights to AI-powered multi-modal analysis: a narrative review
Authors: Zirui Xu, Yongran Wu, Azhen Wang, You Shang, Le Yang, Xiaojing Zou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a critical clinical condition characterized by acute respiratory failure and high mortality. It poses considerable challenges in both diagnosis and management. Imaging constitutes a central element of the conceptual framework for ARDS, with computed tomography (CT) being an essential technical tool for studying the morphological and pathological mechanisms of lung tissue in ARDS.Main textCT imaging has provided profound insights into the respiratory mechanics in ARDS and has informed the optimization of ventilation strategies. It is widely used to characterize the typical pathophysiological manifestations of ARDS in the lungs and can quantify the distribution of ventilation, perfusion, and pulmonary edema. Moreover, CT-based morphological classification of ARDS constitutes a significant component of ARDS subphenotypes research. However, given the heterogeneity in both its diagnosis and response to treatment, a single assessment model is insufficient to meet the management needs of patients with ARDS. The widespread application of artificial intelligence (AI) has greatly facilitated the quantitative analysis of CT imaging, enabling the integration of multidimensional data, such as CT imaging, pulmonary functional data, and laboratory tests.ConclusionThis narrative review adopts a CT-centric viewpoint, delineating the progressive shift in the diagnosis, phenotyping, and management of ARDS from qualitative to quantitative analysis and from unimodal to multimodal evaluation, propelled by ongoing advances in AI. Looking forward, CT-based multimodal fusion analysis holds promise for identifying more precise therapeutic biomarkers and advancing the development of individualized treatment strategies for ARDS.

Background

CT is one of the most commonly employed imaging modalities in the diagnosis and management of ARDS. According to the current definition, the imaging criterion of ARDS includes the presence of bilateral opacities on CT, which cannot be fully explained by effusions, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules [1]. Although conventional CT is inherently static and qualitative, limiting its utility for comprehensive functional evaluation [2], recent advances in computational power have facilitated the quantitative characterization of dynamic changes in pulmonary pathology using CT imaging [3]. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) has substantially expanded the scope of imaging applications [2]. AI supports automated, quantitative assessment of pathological lesions, thereby enhancing the information available for differential diagnosis. Furthermore, AI-driven multimodal analytical approaches combine CT-based radiomic features with functional imaging techniques—including electrical impedance tomography (EIT) and respiratory mechanics monitoring—alongside multi-omics data. Building upon structural information derived from CT, these integrated methods deliver more comprehensive and real-time physiological insights.

Literature search and selection

This review synthesizes the latest evidence on the application of CT imaging and artificial intelligence in ARDS, aiming to comprehensively understand their significant roles in quantitative imaging analysis, subphenotype classification, and multimodal data fusion for ARDS. The literature review was conducted using an extensive search of the PubMed database, employing Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text search terms such as “acute respiratory distress syndrome,” “computed tomography,” “quantitative computed tomography,” “artificial intelligence,” “machine learning,” “subphenotype,” “multimodal,” and “deep learning”. Filters were applied to focus on studies involving adult populations with ARDS undergoing CT imaging. Articles were limited to those published in English to ensure accessibility and relevance. The review primarily focused on literature published between 2010 and 2025, ensuring the inclusion of contemporary research on AI-driven imaging analysis and emerging multimodal approaches.

Main text

Application of conventional CT in ARDS

Early imaging features of ARDS on CT

The primary CT appearances of early ARDS include ground-glass opacities (GGOs), atelectasis, and consolidation [4]. GGOs reflect the presence of inflammatory exudate. With progression of pulmonary edema, atelectasis and consolidation become increasingly prominent in gravity-dependent lung regions, producing a characteristic ventral-to-dorsal gradient in parenchymal density [4]. The combination of diffuse GGOs and this dorsoventral attenuation gradient serves as a key imaging feature for the early diagnosis of ARDS [4, 5].

Temporal evolution of ARDS on CT

CT enables visualization of the dynamic temporal progression of ARDS in the lungs, from the exudative phase through to the fibrotic phase. In the early stage, CT findings may be normal or demonstrate only initial pathological features such as infectious infiltrates [5]. Typically, between days 2 and 7 of disease progression—corresponding to the exudative pathological phase—CT imaging predominantly exhibits bilateral, diffuse GGOs and consolidations, often accompanied by air bronchograms [5]. These abnormalities generally present with an asymmetric, gravity-dependent density gradient along the ventrodorsal axis. In supine patients, ventral lung regions often appear normally aerated or even hyperinflated, likely due to mechanical ventilation, whereas dorsal regions—subject to gravitational effects—are predominantly atelectatic. In severe cases, exudative changes may advance to near-complete opacification of bilateral lungs [6]. Traditionally, the fibroproliferative phase of ARDS is considered to begin around the second week of illness and subsequently progresses into the fibrotic phase [5]. During this period, in the absence of superimposed pulmonary infections or additional complications, partial resolution of exudative changes typically becomes evident. GGOs and consolidations tend to stabilize and gradually regress, whereas diffuse fibrosis increasingly emerges as the predominant finding [7]. Key manifestations of this fibrotic evolution include progressive traction bronchiectasis, residual GGOs, reticular opacities, honeycombing, and diminished lung volumes. In addition, predominantly ventral cystic changes occur in patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation [8].

However, more comprehensive investigations suggest that fibrotic alterations may appear earlier in the course of the disease than previously recognized [9]. A recent study further demonstrates that pulmonary fibrosis is already detectable at admission in patients with COVID-19-associated ARDS and is associated with long-term residual pulmonary fibrosis and compromised lung function [10].

Predictive value of CT in ARDS prognosis

The extent of pulmonary fibrosis observed on CT is an important indicator for assessing the prognosis of patients with lung diseases, and it has been shown to correlate with prognostic outcomes such as forced vital capacity (FVC) decline and transplant-free survival in fibrotic interstitial lung disease (fILD) [11]. Although CT alone is not yet sufficient for accurately predicting the short-term mortality in patients with ARDS [12], the fibroproliferative changes identified on HRCT scans are associated with multiple organ failure, barotrauma, and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), all of which influence the long-term survival of ARDS patients [13]. These fibrotic changes also contribute to persistent pulmonary function impairment and reduced quality of life among survivors [14].

Limitations of conventional CT in ARDS

Limited quantitative diagnostic capability of conventional CT

Imaging criteria have long served as a cornerstone in the diagnosis of ARDS [1]. However, the reliability of current qualitative imaging standards has been increasingly debated, largely due to the inherent heterogeneity of ARDS. This concern is further exacerbated by the persistent absence of objective, quantitative CT-based diagnostic criteria. Although CT imaging can aid in distinguishing certain forms of atelectasis unrelated to ARDS, such as obstructive, compressive, and perioperative atelectasis, radiological features including consolidation, GGOs, and atelectasis are non-specific. In particular, for patients lacking typical imaging manifestations, conventional CT alone may be insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis.

Discordance between CT features and oxygenation index

Atelectasis of alveoli with protein-rich fluid severely hinders oxygenation in patients with ARDS. The oxygenation index (PaO₂/FiO₂), a fundamental component of the ARDS definition, plays a critical role in evaluating disease severity and patient prognosis [1]. However, current research indicates a lack of correlation between the pulmonary aeration status as assessed by CT and the oxygenation index [15–17]. Furthermore, during PEEP titration and other recruitment maneuvers, the volume of recruited lung quantified by CT frequently shows significant discordance with actual improvements in oxygenation [16, 17].

Inability of conventional CT to assess pulmonary perfusion

Conventional CT provides a static description of pulmonary tissue density and morphology at a given time point, offering clear visualization of anatomical structures such as the lung parenchyma, tracheobronchial tree, and major pulmonary vessels. However, this modality is inherently limited in its ability to capture and quantify dynamic pulmonary perfusion, such as the flow and distribution of blood within the pulmonary microvascular bed.

To address these limitations, quantitative CT employs objective metrics to more precisely characterize ventilation, perfusion, and treatment responses, thereby enhancing the standardization of imaging-based quantification.

Application of quantitative CT in ARDS

Quantitative CT relies on Hounsfield Unit (HU) values—a standardized measure derived from the attenuation of X-rays traversing different materials. By applying the well-established linear correlation between tissue density and HU values [18], the density or composition of a region of interest can be quantified using a reference standard, such as water, defined as 0 HU [4]. This approach allows for quantitative assessment of ventilation, perfusion, and pulmonary edema in patients with ARDS.

Quantification of pulmonary ventilation

For ventilation assessment, the HU values of entire lung CT voxels can be employed as an indicator of lung aeration. Lung tissue is commonly classified into distinct aeration compartments according to predefined HU thresholds [17]: non-aerated (HU range: + 100 to − 100), poorly aerated (− 101 to − 500 HU), normally aerated (− 501 to − 900 HU), and hyperinflated (− 901 to − 1000 HU). The weight or volume of each compartment is subsequently quantified. Additionally, generating frequency distribution maps of lung CT values offers an intuitive visualization of the relative proportions of aerated and collapsed lung regions [4, 17]. Consequently, the lungs exhibit regional segmentation with differential ventilation, enabling quantitative characterization of pulmonary ventilation status at the structural level. By performing CT at different PEEP levels, the recruitment response can be quantitatively evaluated based on the volume or mass of recruited lung tissue [17]. Moreover, calculating the dynamic relative regional strain (DRRS) [19] provides insight into how PEEP adjustments modulate regional lung strain in patients with ARDS, thereby supporting the design of individualized ventilation strategies and mitigating the risk of ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI).

Quantification of pulmonary perfusion

SPECT/CT serves as an essential imaging modality for evaluating pulmonary perfusion, revealing the ventilation-perfusion matching, and plays a significant role in quantitative assessment of abnormal perfusion distributions resulting from vascular constriction or microthrombosis [20]. Additionally, dynamic contrast-enhanced CT (DCE-CT) and dual-energy CT (DECT) can also be used for the quantitative analysis of pulmonary perfusion [21]. DCE-CT generates time–density curves for specific lung regions by acquiring multi-phase continuous lung scan data, allowing for the tracking of the dynamic distribution of the contrast agent within the pulmonary vasculature, thereby enabling perfusion quantification. DECT conducts simultaneous scans at two different X-ray energy levels and uses material decomposition algorithms to generate iodine density maps, which are then used to calculate pulmonary blood volume (PBV).

The application of DECT in studies involving COVID-19 patients has revealed numerous perfusion defects in the absence of overt pulmonary embolism, suggesting the presence of microvascular pathology [22]. More recently, a study quantified a hyperperfusion coefficient by DECT, which reflects the extent of hyperperfusion in well-ventilated regions [23]. When integrated with physiological monitoring, this coefficient demonstrated a negative correlation with the oxygenation index (R2 = 0.66) and a positive correlation with the exacerbation of pulmonary edema (R2 = 0.84). These findings deepen our understanding of the mechanisms underlying pulmonary ventilation–perfusion mismatch and hypoxemia in ARDS.

Quantification of pulmonary edema

Voxel-by-voxel accumulation of HU from CT scans enables calculation of total lung weight. This calculated value can be compared with the estimated normal lung weight to assess the severity of pulmonary edema and predict the responsiveness to mechanical ventilation [24]. The quantitative assessment of pulmonary edema holds significant guidance for fluid management in patients with ARDS [25]. Specifically, the extravascular lung water index (EVLWI), measured by transpulmonary thermodilution (TPTD), reflects the severity of pulmonary edema and serves as a crucial reference parameter for fluid management [25]. Although CT-based assessment of pulmonary edema is generally less quantitatively precise than EVLWI [26], it is substantially easier to perform. Furthermore, CT provides the distinct advantage of visualizing the spatial distribution of pulmonary edema while simultaneously revealing the influence of other thoracic pathologies, such as infectious atelectasis and pleural effusions.

Right ventricular and pulmonary vascular features on CT

Right ventricular (RV) dysfunction is a common complication of ARDS and an independent risk factor for mortality [27]. Although primary systolic dysfunction may pre-exist in some patients [28], ARDS typically leads to RV dysfunction (RVD) through several mechanisms. It causes an increase in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) and pulmonary artery pressure, which elevates RV afterload, and the compensatory hypercontractility of RV can also impair its own coronary perfusion [28, 29]. These factors ultimately culminate in RVD. Key contributors to this pathophysiology include hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (HPV), hypercapnia, endothelial injury with thrombosis, and the effects of positive pressure ventilation [28, 29]. A systematic review reported that patients with RV injury had a 1.45-fold increased odds of mortality compared to those without [30]. Therefore, the early identification of deteriorating RV function is paramount for guiding therapeutic adjustments and improving patient outcomes.

However, due to resource limitations, high costs, and the inherent risk of complications associated with invasive procedures, right heart catheterization (RHC), the gold standard for assessing right heart hemodynamics [31], is often restricted in clinical application. Moreover, ultrasound assessment of RV function is frequently hindered by suboptimal cardiac acoustic windows in ARDS patients. In such circumstances, CT imaging may offer a potentially effective alternative for detecting right heart dysfunction.

Previous research has indicated that a set of CT-derived parameters, including the main pulmonary artery diameter and the right-to-left ventricular diameter ratio, can effectively identify and screen for pulmonary hypertension [32]. Furthermore, in patients with severe ARDS, the CT-measured right-to-left ventricular area ratio has demonstrated a strong correlation with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), making it a viable alternative assessment when acoustic windows are suboptimal [33]. A multicenter study focusing on non-congenital pulmonary hypertension identified the main pulmonary artery diameter as an independent predictor of prognosis in patients with pulmonary hypertension [34]. Recently, a study on COPD found that the ratio of pulmonary artery to aorta diameter (PA/Ao) and the indicator for small vessel pruning (aBV5/aTBV) were strongly associated with the risk of acute exacerbation and mortality, respectively [35]. In pulmonary diseases, early alterations in pulmonary vascular characteristics indicate changes in pulmonary perfusion and vascular resistance [35]. Therefore, the CT parameters of pulmonary vasculature may hold significant importance for investigating the heterogeneity of pulmonary vascular pathology in ARDS, as well as for the early identification and prevention of right ventricular dysfunction in these patients.

The influence of lung volume status on quantitative CT

Although the use of quantitative CT has substantially reduced subjective interference compared to conventional CT, it must be emphasized that its findings are highly dependent on voxel-based analysis of lung tissue density, which is notably influenced by the instantaneous state of lung inflation. Consequently, such studies, especially multicenter studies, necessitate rigorous standardization of the patient’s PEEP level and inspiratory breath-hold maneuver, as well as comprehensive documentation of postural parameters during image acquisition [36]. In differentiating subphenotypes such as “wet lung” and “dry lung” [37], it is imperative to fully account for factors related to “lung volume and posture at the time of image acquisition” to prevent misinterpreting increased density resulting from lung hypoinflation as pulmonary edema or consolidation.

Furthermore, it should be recognized that the results of quantitative CT scanning essentially reflect density measurements rather than direct physiological states of pulmonary ventilation and gas exchange. In interpreting the metrics derived from quantitative CT, full consideration must be given to the pathophysiological alterations in ventilation and perfusion distribution within the lung itself.

CT-based ARDS subphenotyping

ARDS is a critical clinical syndrome characterized by significant heterogeneity in etiology, endotypes, phenotypes, and treatment response [1]. Therefore, to achieve individualized treatment, it is imperative to elucidate subphenotypes of ARDS [38]. CT imaging has already served as a foundational tool for subphenotyping.

The focal and diffuse subphenotypes

Based on pulmonary morphology visualized by CT, ARDS can be categorized into focal and diffuse types [39]. Focal ARDS is characterized by the lobar distribution of attenuation in the lower part or gravity-dependent regions of the lung, with relatively preserved aeration in the upper lobes, whereas diffuse ARDS is marked by widely distributed lesions [39, 40]. These two types may involve relatively distinct pathophysiological processes. Diffuse ARDS generally demonstrates more severe ventilatory impairment, lower lung compliance, and a higher 28-day mortality rate [41]. Coppola’s study, utilizing quantitative CT and recruitment maneuvers, confirmed that diffuse ARDS patients, compared to those with focal morphology, exhibited higher lung elastance and lung weight, along with better recruitability and a more favorable response to higher PEEP levels [40]. Regarding ventilatory variables, one study found that driving pressure may be more strongly associated with mortality in diffuse ARDS than in focal ARDS [41].

Nevertheless, this morphological subphenotyping relies on rigorous imaging-based discrimination and is inherently challenged by the heterogeneity of ARDS itself. For instance, in the LIVE study, 21% of patients were misclassified radiologically, potentially contributing to the absence of a statistically significant difference in 90-day mortality for personalized treatment guided by CT morphological subphenotyping [42]. Furthermore, this dichotomous classification inherently lacks a comprehensive quantitative analysis of pulmonary lesion characteristics.

The dry, wet, and fibrotic subphenotypes

A recent multicenter retrospective study, which integrated CT findings in patients with severe pulmonary ARDS, further proposed three subphenotypes: dry, wet, and fibrotic types [37]. Lung CT scans of these three subphenotypes revealed distinct proportions and distributions of ground-glass opacities, consolidation, and fibrosis, with differential responsiveness to PEEP. Notably, the fibrotic type showed a higher incidence of right ventricular dysfunction and was associated with a worse prognosis. This phenotyping highlights the crucial role of fibrosis in the disease progression, addressing the limitations of previous morphological classifications by integrating laboratory findings and other clinical data with morphological features. The study reported classification results for patients requiring ECMO support. However, further research is needed to determine how to identify and capture the early signs of these three subphenotypes, particularly the fibrotic type, in the early stages of disease progression.

Overall, CT-based subphenotyping is inherently limited by its inability to provide real-time bedside monitoring, and initial classifications may lack stability as the disease progresses and therapeutic interventions evolve. Relying solely on CT-based morphological classification is insufficient to capture the heterogeneity in the underlying pathophysiological processes among different subphenotypes of ARDS. Therefore, the future development of bedside monitoring tools, such as EIT, lung ultrasound (LUS), and respiratory mechanics, integrated with AI-driven multimodal subphenotyping, may represent an effective strategy to overcome the limitations of CT-based classification.

Artificial intelligence and multimodal data fusion

AI-assisted image interpretation

Neural network architectures have been applied for automated segmentation of lung CT images in ARDS, facilitating rapid computation and output of quantitative metrics—including GGOs, consolidation, non-aerated tissue fraction, and the lung inhomogeneity index [43].

Multimodal data fusion

Unimodal assessment methods are insufficient for achieving individualized treatment of ARDS. While CT offers high-resolution anatomical visualization of pulmonary structures, it remains insufficient for capturing real-time lung ventilation–perfusion function. In contrast, bedside functional imaging, such as EIT and LUS, and respiratory mechanics provide non-invasive, real-time assessments of pulmonary functional status. In the field of respiratory medicine, self-supervised multimodal pre-training models have demonstrated extensive utility, ranging from disease diagnosis to therapeutic planning, thereby substantially improving both clinical workflow efficiency and diagnostic accuracy [44]. Multimodal subphenotyping

ARDS can be classified based on multiple criteria [38], including its etiology, morphological patterns of lung injury, and the host inflammatory response.

Advances in automated segmentation have enabled the automatic quantification of the mass or volume of recruitable lung tissue at different PEEP levels, a capability that has been applied in a study exploring recruitability-based subphenotyping [46]. Machine learning models [47, 48] can now predict hyper-/hypo-inflammatory phenotypes based on various clinical data, including ARDS risk factors, vital signs, and laboratory findings. These models are more flexible and can be rapidly updated, making them suitable for quick bedside identification. The AI-quantified CT imaging features further enhance the existing clinical data model [49]. A recent large-scale proteomic study combined CT radiomics and clinical information to further explore inflammatory subphenotypes of ARDS, revealing differential responses to corticosteroid therapy and PEEP across these subphenotypes [49]. The imaging characteristics representing texture, intensity, and pulmonary edema distributions exhibit differences across various inflammation phenotypes. Patients with high inflammation may have greater lung recruitability, while patients with low inflammation are more prone to issues of over-distension when subjected to relatively high PEEP [49]. Therefore, different ventilation strategies should be implemented for patients with different types of inflammation.

Multimodal trajectory prediction

In recent researches, deep learning models (e.g., XGBoost + CNN) enable the effective fusion of imaging and clinical data for early ARDS prediction [43, 45]. Although certain studies have not incorporated radiographic data, they use AI to predict the severity of ARDS progression, response to therapy, and mortality based on clinical data such as ventilation parameters, laboratory findings, and demographic information [48, 50], thereby further affirming the value of multimodal applications.

For unimodal prediction models, increasing the frequency of CT scans may potentially enhance model accuracy. However, this approach concomitantly elevates radiation exposure and transport-associated risks, and may raise ethical concerns regarding patient necessity. Current limitations and challenges

Although artificial intelligence and multimodal analysis demonstrate promising application prospects in the field of CT and ARDS, significant limitations and challenges remain that cannot be overlooked.

Current AI-related research predominantly focuses on observational cohorts, lacking exploration into the impact of different interventions on prognostic outcomes with AI integration. Multiple studies [43, 45, 50] are still confined to small-sample, single-center, retrospective designs or lack external validation. A multicenter prospective phenotyping study [49] based on proteomics and machine learning further indicates that combining quantitative CT information and conducting randomized controlled trials is essential for exploring personalized treatment in ARDS.

Furthermore, translating research models into bedside tools faces practical hurdles. There are requirements for clinical environment equipment, necessitating integration into hospital systems and optimization for clinician-friendly operation. Meanwhile, many deep learning toolkits remain commercially protected and are not freely accessible for use. Moreover, regarding the interpretation of machine learning models, although SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) has partially addressed the “black box” issue and improved interpretability, this does not equate to transparency. Clinicians must still approach model results with caution, especially critical care physicians, who should rely primarily on first-hand clinical information as the basis for decision-making. It should be emphasized that the primary role of large models is to provide early warnings and optimize resource allocation, rather than to serve as the principal basis for clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

CT has long been a cornerstone in the understanding and management of ARDS, evolving from traditional qualitative morphological assessment to precise quantitative analysis of pulmonary ventilation, perfusion, and edema.

The advancement of machine learning offers an effective means for integrating diverse types of data. We should further explore more prospective study designs that incorporate CT-derived radiomic features with bedside functional imaging, respiratory mechanics parameters, and even multi‑omics data. The role of CT is expected to evolve from an independent imaging modality into a central hub for multimodal data integration, with artificial intelligence serving as a key driving force. This integrated strategy holds significant potential for building more precise models of ARDS subphenotyping, responsiveness to treatment, and prognostic prediction [43], and is expected to propel the therapeutic paradigm for ARDS from a ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach towards individualized medicine. However, the clinical translational value of these advanced models must be validated through rigorously designed, large-scale prospective studies to ultimately improve patient outcomes.
