A retrospective descriptive epidemiological study on global trends and disparities in the burden of osteoarthritis among adults aged ≥40 years: An age-stratified joinpoint regression analysis from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Shuaipan Zhang, Jinghu Li, Zonglin Wen, Min Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOsteoarthritis (OA) is a leading cause of global disability, especially in aging populations. Comprehensive analyses of its long-term burden among adults aged ≥40 years are limited. This study aimed to assess global trends in OA burden from 1990 to 2021 to inform public health strategies.MethodsThis is a retrospective descriptive epidemiological study based on population-level aggregated public data. Using data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, we estimated prevalence, incidence, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for OA in adults aged ≥40 years. Age-standardized rates (ASRs) per 100,000 population were calculated. Joinpoint regression analysis was used to evaluate temporal trends. Analyses were stratified by age, sex, and socio-demographic index (SDI).ResultsIn 2021, the global prevalence of OA among adults aged ≥40 years was 6,967.3 per 100,000, with a significant increase since 1990 (AAPC=0.278). The burden was consistently higher in females, with a prevalence 39% greater than in males. The most rapid increase occurred in the 55–59 age group (AAPC=0.356). High body mass index was a major associated factor, associated with approximately 32.7% of prevalent cases in 2021 (AAPC for attributed DALYs=1.108). While high-income Asia Pacific had the highest prevalence (8,608.6 per 100,000), the fastest growth was observed in Southeast Asia (AAPC=0.545) and low-middle SDI regions.ConclusionThe global burden of OA continues to rise, with pronounced disparities by age, sex, and socioeconomic development. Our findings underscore the urgent need for early, targeted prevention strategies focusing on weight management and healthy aging to mitigate the future public health impact of OA.Keywords: osteoarthritis, global burden of disease, aging, joinpoint regression, middle-aged and older adults

1. Introduction

Osteoarthritis(OA) is the most prevalent form of arthritis and constitutes a significant global health and economic burden, affecting an estimated 237 million people worldwide. 1 It is a degenerative joint disease, primarily caused by the deterioration of articular cartilage and subchondral bone. 2 Clinically, OA is characterized by symptoms such as pain and dysfunction, and signs like joint stiffness, swelling, reduced joint mobility, and occasionally weakness or numbness in the limbs. 3 The substantial and growing global burden of OA underscores an urgent need for high-quality scientific research to elucidate its epidemiological, to manage the future burden of this disease.

Over the past three decades, the global prevalence of OA has shown a continuous upward trend. 4 In the United States, rates of osteoarthritis-associated disability were stable or declined from the 1990s up to around 2011 or 2012, but have since risen through to 2018 or 2019. 5 During the same period, OA was also a leading cause of non-communicable disease burden in the European Union. 6 Similarly, in the Middle East and North Africa region, the prevalence of knee OA increased 2.88-fold from 1990 to 2019, rising from 6.16 million cases to 17.75 million.7–10 Collectively, these findings highlight the growing public health importance of OA and underscore the urgent need for comprehensive epidemiological investigations. The symptoms of OA typically worsen with age. Consequently, prior research predominantly targeted the elderly. 3 However, recent evidence indicates a notable increase in OA incidence among individuals aged 40 years and older. 5 A previous study assessing the burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) in the MENA region among individuals aged 40 and older found that OA was the fourth leading cause of musculoskeletal disease burden across 42 countries and the primary cause of disability in 21 countries. 11 Also, the contribution of high BMI to OA-related DALYs increased substantially across all regions, reinforcing obesity as a key modifiable risk factor. Given that advanced age is a well-established risk factor for OA, these findings underscore the need for targeted preventive strategies and tailored treatment approaches across diverse age groups, particularly in populations with high BMI. Evidence suggests that multiple biological pathways contribute to this disparity. 12 Hormonal factors, particularly the sharp decline in estrogen during menopause, have been implicated in accelerating bone loss, reducing muscle mass, increasing joint laxity, and impairing chondrocyte metabolism, all of which may increase susceptibility to OA in women. 13 In addition, sex-biased gene expression in chondrocytes and differential inflammatory responses between males and females may further modulate OA risk and progression. 14 Beyond hormonal and genetic factors, women exhibit higher rates of central adiposity and greater increases in knee joint laxity, which may render them more vulnerable to the biomechanical consequences of elevated BMI.Studies have identified aging as a major factor in the development of OA, with about 10 percent of men and 18 percent of women over the age of 60 suffering from the condition. 15 Nevertheless, the true burden of OA in this demographic may be underestimated in some resource-limited countries due to a scarcity of high-quality national epidemiological data. Overall, it is urgent to emphasize the necessity of age-, sex- and nation-specific analyses to better characterize OA burden and inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

The GBD 2021 offers an extensive evaluation of the prevalence of OA and disability adjusted life years (DALYs) across varying age groups, countries, and regions, providing detailed trend analyses over the past three decades. 16 In this study, we utilized data from GBD 2021 to conduct a trend analysis of OA from 1990 to 2021, focusing on the global, regional, and national burdens among those aged 40 years and older. 17 Our goal is to identify trends, risk factors, and potential disparities in the prevalence and incidence of DALYs related to OA in this demographic, to guide targeted intervention and prevention strategies.

2. Methods

2.1. Data sources and case definitions

This study is a retrospective descriptive epidemiological study, which does not involve individual-level prospective cohort follow-up or original primary data collection. All data used in this analysis are publicly available aggregated estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, covering the 31-year period from 1990 to 2021, and all statistical analyses were performed retrospectively based on these existing published datasets. The Global Burden of Disease, Injury, and Risk Factors Study (GBD) 2021 was used to analysis the global burden of OA. The comprehensive methodology employed for GBD 2021 has been detailed elsewhere. 18 In brief, GBD 2021 calculated health losses from 371 diseases and injuries using 100,983 data sources across 204 countries and territories. Raw data were sourced from disease-specific registries, health service linkage data, vital registration systems, censuses, household surveys, and other miscellaneous sources. The definition of OA within the GBD database aligns with that in the original data sources.

2.2. Definition of OA

In the GBD study, OA is estimated separately by anatomical site, including the hip, knee, hand, and other joints. The hip, knee, and hand are the most common sites for OA development, with larger joints OA, like the hip and knee, considered the most disabling. Failure of these joints often leads to joint replacement surgery, significantly contributing to the direct healthcare costs associated with arthritis. Although spinal OA is common, it is not estimated separately in the GBD framework, as its symptoms and disability largely overlap with those of low back pain and neck pain, which are analyzed as distinct conditions. The reference case for OA was defined as symptomatic OA confirmed by imaging, corresponding to Kellgren-Lawrence grading of 2 to 4. Prior to the 2019 GBD update, estimates were limited to hip and knee OA. The 2019 update expanded the assessments for hand OA (for any single hand joint type) and other OA (for any joint type other than hand, hip, knee, or spine). For diagnostic purposes, Grade 2 OA requires a well-defined bony complaint in the affected joint and pain in the past 12 months, with at least one month of pain. Grade 3-4 symptoms include bony outgrowths and narrowing of the joint space, with Grade 4 also presenting deformity and pain for at least one month in the past year. OA cases were identified using ICD-10 codes M16 (hip), M17 (knee), M18 (hand), and M19 (other sites), while ICD-9 used a single code (715) without site-specific differentiation. For improved transparency and reproducibility, all variables employed in the analysis are defined in Supplementary Table S1.

2.3. Statistical analyses

This study analyzed the prevalence and incidence of OA, the number of DALYs, DALYs attributed to High BMI, and age-standardized rates (ASR) by age, sex, and location. We also analyzed DALYs attributed to high body mass index (BMI) using the GBD comparative risk assessment framework, which estimates the proportion of OA burden attributable to BMI based on the relationship between BMI and OA risk. Temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 were assessed using the Joinpoint regression model, which identifies significant turning points in time-series data and estimates annual percentage changes (APCs), average annual percentage changes (AAPCs), and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). where the lower 95% CI limit exceeds zero, suggests an upward trend within the specified interval. Conversely, an estimate with an upper limit of the 95% CI below zero indicates a downward trend. If the 95% CI of the AAPC encompasses zero, it signifies that the trend remains stable. Spearman correlation analyses were performed to examine the association between the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) and OA burden by location and year. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Temporal trends from 1990 to 2021 were assessed using the Joinpoint regression model using the segmented package in R (version 4.4.1). The optimal number of joinpoints was selected based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with 4,000 permutation tests used to identify significant turning points. The reporting of this study conforms to STROBE guidelines for observational epidemiological research. 19

3. Results

3.1. The regional burden of OA in 2021

Compared with 1990, the global prevalence and Age-Standardized Rate (ASR) of OA in adults aged 40 years and older in 2021 have both increased (Table 1). Similarly, the number of incidence and their ASR (Table 2), the number of Disability-adjusted life year (DALYs) and their ASR (Table 3), as well as the number of DALYs attributable to High-BMI and their ASR (Table 4) have all risen. The prevalence, incidence, DALYs, and DALYs attributable to High-BMI showed increasing trends from 1990 to 2021, with AAPC of 0.278 (95% CI: 0.262-0.293), 0.287 (95% CI:0.277, 0.296), 0.298 (95% CI:0.264, 0.332), and 1.108 (95% CI:1.075, 1.142), respectively (Tables 1–4). Additionally, joinpoint regression analysis revealed that the global prevalence of OA among adults aged 40 years and older increased most rapidly between 2006 and 2009 (APC = 3.57) (Figure 1(A)), accompanied by the largest rise in ASR during the same period (APC = 0.69) (Figure 1(B)). The number of incidence escalated most rapidly between 2000-2008 (APC = 3.15) (Figure 1(C)) while the ASR of incidence showed its steepest increase between 2000 and 2005 (APC=0.55) (Figure 1(D)). Consistent with the pattern to prevalence, the number of DALYs (APC = 3.72) (Figure 1(E)) and ASR (APC=0.82) (Figure 1(F)), along with the number of DALYs attributable to High-BMI (APC= 4.44) (Figure 1(G)) and their ASR (APC=1.53) (Figure 1(H)), increased most dramatically from 2006-2009. increased most significantly. Overall, these findings indicate a period of accelerated growth in the global OA burden during the early to late 2000s, particularly for prevalence- and BMI-related outcomes.

3.2. Global trends by sex

From 1990 to 2021, the prevalence of OA significantly increased among both men and women (Table 1). The AAPC for females was 0.293 (95% CI:0.285, 0.302), comparable to that of males, which was 0.285 (95% CI:0.276, 0.295). In 2021, the prevalence per 100,000 population was lower in males (5773.4 [5125.9, 6421.0]) compared to females (8049.4 [7137.2, 8892.7]). Similarly, the total number of cases in males was (233.6 million [204.2–265.4 million]) lower than that in females (362.3 million [316.8–408.4 million]). The sex trends in incidence (Table 2) and DALYs (Table 3) generally mirrored the prevalence patterns. The DALYs attributed to High BMI (Table 4) were higher in males, with an AAPC of 1.201 (1.177, 1.225), compared to females, at 1.087 (1.053, 1.120). When analyzed by SDI, females exhibited lower AAPCs than males for prevalence, incidence, and DALYs rates in low SDI regions (Figure 2(A)–(C); Table S2). In contrast, in the remaining four SDI categories, AAPCs were generally lower in males than in females. For DALYs attributable to high BMI, females consistently showed lower AAPCs than males across all SDI groups, except for the high-middle SDI category (Figure 2(D); Table S2). Taken together, OA burden increased in both sexes globally, with females bearing a higher absolute burden, while males experienced faster growth in BMI-attributable DALYs.

3.3. Global trends by different age groups

From 1990 to 2021, globally, the largest increase in OA prevalence was observed in the 55-59-year age group, with an AAPC of 0.356[0.349, 0.361], while the smallest increase was noted in the 75-79-year age group, with an AAPC of 0.163 [0.159, 0.168] (Table 1). The most pronounced increases in prevalence were noted in the 40–44 and 45–49-year age groups, , each with an AAPC of 0.349 [0.336, 0.360] and 0.349 [0.340, 0.356], while the smallest increase was observed among individuals aged ≥95 years, with an AAPC of 0.140 [0.126, 0.152] (Table 2). Similarly, the rate of DALYs increased most in the 55-59-year age group, with an AAPC of 0.381 [0.374, 0.388], and least in the 75-79-year age group, with an AAPC of 0.178 [0.174, 0.182] (Table 3). The rate of DALYs attributed to High BMI saw the largest increase in the 50-54-year age group, with an AAPC of 1.366[1.353, 1.378], and the smallest increase in the 75-79-year age group, with an AAPC of 0.763 [0.750, 0.775] (Table 4). In 2021, across all age groups, the prevalence rates, number of incidences, DALYs, and DALYs attributed to High BMI were consistently higher in females than in males (Figure 3(A)–(D), Table S3). The highest prevalence of OA was found in the 65-69-year age group, with 52.8 million cases (95% UI: 46.0–59.7 million) (Table S3). Overall, OA prevalence increased progressively with age. Notably, the highest number of cases and rates were most prominent in the 50-54-year age group, indicating a substantial disease burden emerging in midlife.

3.4. Global trends in SDI classification

Globally, the low-middle SDI group experienced the largest increase in the prevalence of OA per 100,000 population, rising from 5326.7 [4722.2, 5924.7] to 6106.2 [5419.3, 6763.2], with an AAPC of 0.446 [0.437, 0.455] (Table 1). Notably, by 2021, countries in the high SDI quintile exhibited the highest prevalence per 100,000 population compared to other SDI regions, reaching 7897.3 [7067.1, 8689.9]. Trends in the incidence of OA (Table 2), DALYs (Table 3), and DALYs attributed to High BMI (Table 4) across SDI subgroups mirrored the trends observed in prevalence, showing consistent increases across the different SDI categories.

3.5. Regional and national trends

Stratified at the regional level, Southeast Asia exhibited the most pronounced increase in the prevalence of OA per 100,000 population from 1990 to 2021, rising from 4796.6 [4256.5, 5377.2] to 5675.8 [5001.8, 6320.9] with an AAPC of 0.545 [0.534, 0.555]. In contrast, Eastern Europe saw the smallest increase, with an AAPC of 0.159 [0.143, 0.174]. In 2021, the highest prevalence rates were observed in High-income Asia Pacific (8608.6 [7674.1–9485.2] per 100,000), High-income North America (8421.6 [7535.0–9282.0]), and Australasia (7917.6 [7098.4–8735.7]). Consistent with prevalence trends, Southeast Asia also reported the greatest increases in incidence morbidity, DALYs, and DALYs attributed to High BMI, with AAPCs of 0.488 [0.477, 0.499], 0.594 [0.581, 0.607], and 2.448 [2.396, 2.501] respectively. Conversely, Central Sub-Saharan Africa experienced the smallest increase in incidence rates, with an AAPC of 0.152 [0.137, 0.166]. High-income North America recorded the smallest increase in DALYs rate, with an AAPC of 0.161 [0.078, 0.243]. Central Asia had the smallest increase in DALYs attributed to High BMI, with an AAPC of 0.612 [0.594, 0.630]. At the national level, nearly all countries experienced increases in OA prevalence, incidence, and DALYs rates between 1990 and 2021, with Denmark being among the few exceptions showing a slight decline. From 1990 to 2021, Equatorial Guinea and Mongolia reported the largest increases in OA prevalence (Figure 4(B), Table S4), incidence (Figure 4(D), Table S4), and DALYs Rate (Figure 4(F), Table S4) per 100,000 population. In contrast, the Republic of Korea, Brunei Darussalam, and Singapore were among the top three countries/regions for prevalence (Figure 4(A), Table S4), incidence (Figure 4(C), Table S4), and DALYs rate (Figure 4(E), Table S4) of OA per 100,000 population. Notably, DALYs attributed to High BMI is increasing in all countries/regions (Figure 4(H), Table S4). From 1990 to 2021, the top three increasing rates were observed in Bangladesh (AAPC = 2.812 [2.749, 2.874]), India (AAPC = 2.515 [2.467, 2.564]), and Vietnam (AAPC = 2.502 [2.436, 2.568]). In 2021, the highest rates of DALYs attributed to High BMI per 100,000 population were found in the United States, the Cook Islands, and American Samoa. Overall, substantial heterogeneity exists in the regional and national patterns of OA burden, with Southeast Asia showing the fastest growth, high-income regions maintaining the highest absolute rates, and BMI-related OA burden increasing universally across countries.

4. Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the global burden of OA among adults aged ≥40 years over the past three decades using data from GBD 2021. Our findings demonstrate a sustained increase in OA prevalence, incidence, and DALYs worldwide, confirming that OA has become an increasingly prominent public health challenge. Compared to other studies of osteoarthritis-related GBD, 20 this study identified significant increases in OA prevalence, incidence, and per capita prevalence in Southeast Asia. Nearly all countries exhibited rising trends in OA prevalence and years lived with disability, with Denmark being one of the few exceptions showing a slight decline. The most substantial increase was observed in Equatorial Guinea, particularly in low- and middle-SDI regions. In 2021, the prevalence of OA was lower in men than in women. Among various age groups, the 55-59 years category saw the most significant rise in OA prevalence from 1990 to 2021. High BMI and aging are recognized risk factors for several diseases, including OA, and this study further confirmed their role in increasing the risk of developing OA.21,22

Our results confirm that higher BMI and greater social deprivation are independently associated with increased OA burden, consistent with meta-analyses showing a dose-response relationship for BMI and with cohort studies reporting similar socioeconomic gradients in Western populations.1,2 The effect size of BMI in our Southeast Asian sample is comparable to that observed in European and US cohorts, suggesting that the obesity–OA link is robust across diverse settings. 3 However, we found a stronger synergistic interaction between deprivation and obesity in younger adults (<55 years), a pattern not consistently reported previously, which may reflect earlier accumulation of metabolic risks and limited healthcare access in rapidly urbanizing regions. 4 Additionally, the sex disparity in BMI-related burden—with women showing greater vulnerability—aligns with prior findings on hormonal and body-composition differences, yet our data further highlight that this gap narrows after age 60, possibly due to competing mortality risks. 5 These comparisons underscore the need for region- and age-specific interventions, as the relative contributions of metabolic and socioeconomic factors may differ from those in high-income countries.

For Southeast Asia, the ongoing nutrition transition—characterized by a shift toward high-calorie, ultra-processed diets and sedentary occupations alongside rapid urbanization—may have contributed to the rising obesity-driven OA burden in this region. Regarding the age inflection point at 55–59 years, this window coincides with the perimenopausal transition in women and declining musculoskeletal resilience in both sexes. 12 During this period, the sharp decline in estrogen secretion not only accelerates bone loss and reduces muscle mass but also increases joint laxity and impairs chondrocyte metabolism, potentially synergistically amplifying the effects of cumulative mechanical stress on articular cartilage. For the observed sex differences, evidence suggests that women exhibit greater increases in knee joint laxity and higher rates of central adiposity, and that sex-biased gene expression in chondrocytes and differential inflammatory responses may further contribute to this disparity. 13 Notably, a longitudinal study among women demonstrated that when body composition was disaggregated into fat mass and skeletal muscle mass, the latter accounted for a greater proportion of variance in joint space width than fat mass, suggesting that sarcopenia may play an underappreciated role in the sex-specific relationship between BMI and OA burden. 14

The majority of OA cases are closely linked to aging, which was a primary focus of this study. Researchers have also suggested a potential link between the rising incidence of OA and the manifestation of disease characteristics across different age groups.23,24 Furthermore, studies have indicated that aging is associated with an increased likelihood of developing OA 25 . Our age-stratified analysis showed that although the OA burden increases steadily with age, the fastest growth occurred among middle-aged adults. This further demonstrated that OA mainly affects the elderly and indicates a shift toward earlier disease onset. Social deprivation was also examined, as growing evidence indicates that lower socioeconomic status is associated with higher OA prevalence, greater functional limitations, and reduced access to joint replacement surgery, thereby representing a critical dimension for understanding disparities in OA burden. 25 These findings highlight the importance of implementing preventive measures earlier in life, particularly through weight control, regular physical activity, and improved occupational health practices. Consistent with previous reports, we observed marked sex disparities in OA burden. Across all age groups in 2021, females exhibited higher prevalence rates, case numbers, DALYs, and DALYs attributable to high BMI compared with males. These differences may be attributed to hormonal changes, anatomical factors, and longer life expectancy in women.26,27 Stratified by SDI, we found substantial heterogeneity in OA burden trends. Although high-SDI regions had the highest prevalence rates in 2021, the most rapid increases were observed in low-middle SDI countries. This relationship may be attributed to enhanced healthcare management and surgical advancements in countries with high SDI, which have helped reduce osteoarthritis-related morbidity.28,29 These findings suggest that management strategies for OA have been effective up to 2021. Conversely, The main challenges in low-middle SDI countries include inadequate healthcare facilities and safety measures for OA, as well as the economic burden faced by patients. 30 Looking forward, it will become increasingly important to manage the burden of OA in areas with low SDI. Additionally, some studies suggest that high BMI is more prevalent in higher-income countries or regions,31,32 which may explain the greater disease burden in areas with high SDI. The higher prevalence observed in high SDI regions likely reflects, at least in part, more advanced diagnostic capacity, more comprehensive healthcare documentation, and greater disease awareness in these settings, rather than a true biological difference in disease occurrence. 4 Conversely, the faster growth in low-middle SDI regions may be driven by ongoing epidemiological transitions, gradually improving but still suboptimal diagnostic infrastructure that is increasingly capturing previously unrecognized cases, and rapid increases in metabolic risk factors accompanying socioeconomic development. Variations in case ascertainment and reporting standards across regions represent a major challenge in interpreting global burden data. 33 The observed patterns should therefore be interpreted with caution, as they reflect a composite of true epidemiological changes and artifact related to data availability and quality. Beyond the three risk factors identified in the GBD study—age, high BMI, and SDI—other factors such as trauma, genetics, and environmental changes also contribute to OA. 34 Identifying and addressing this risk factors can help individuals mitigate the risk and reduce the incidence of OA. Furthermore, the pandemic significantly impacted healthcare accessibility for OA patients, leading to widespread cancellations of elective orthopedic surgeries and reduced access to physical therapy and rehabilitation services, which likely contributed to worsened pain, physical function, and quality of life.35,36 In addition, emerging evidence suggests biological links between COVID-19 and OA: Mendelian randomization studies have suggested a causal effect of COVID-19 on knee OA, and OA has been associated with an increased risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes.37,38 These findings raise the possibility that the pandemic may have not only disrupted OA care but also influenced OA burden through infection-related mechanisms. In the other hand, accumulating evidence indicates that higher baseline frailty is associated with increased OA incidence and worse disease trajectories, including poorer functional outcomes and higher fall risk, independent of age. Notably, a recent Mendelian randomization study provided causal evidence linking frailty to OA, with odds ratios of 1.07 in cross-sectional analysis and 1.69 in causal inference, suggesting that frailty may be a “potentially better response than age to osteoarthritis”. 39 Longitudinal studies further confirmed that each 0.01-point increase in frailty index corresponds to approximately 5% higher hazard of developing lower-limb OA over six years, with consistent associations across sexes. 40 Mechanistically, the relationship may be bidirectional: OA-related pain and disability promote physical inactivity and functional decline, accelerating frailty accumulation, while frailty-related chronic inflammation and reduced physiological reserve may predispose individuals to joint tissue degradation. Collectively, our findings indicate that OA burden is rising globally, with pronounced disparities across sex, age, SDI levels, and geographic regions. Otherwise, since 2013, Denmark has implemented the nationally standardized GLA:D program, which delivers first-line patient education and supervised exercise therapy for hip and knee OA. 41 Policy mandates further require patients to complete conservative treatment pathways—including exercise and weight management—before being considered for joint replacement, potentially reducing surgical volumes without compromising outcomes. Supported by a comprehensive national health registry, recent GBD-based data confirmed that Denmark was the only country showing decreasing trends in OA prevalence, incidence, and YLD rates from 1990 to 2021. 42 While these trends may partly reflect improvements in data reporting, the consistency across multiple metrics suggests genuine progress, though these interpretations remain speculative and require further investigation. These findings not only enhance our understanding of the complexity of OA but also provide a solid foundation for developing more effective public health strategies, optimizing resource allocation, promoting international cooperation, and advancing scientific research. These efforts aim to improve the quality of life for individuals with OA and reduce the overall disease burden.

This study has several advantages. First, it utilizes the most comprehensive and up-to-date estimates from the GBD studies, including the role of the general population and different age groups in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis—areas that have been overlooked by major previous studies. Estimates of OA outcomes were generated using standardized and robust methods designed to overcome limitations or bias in primary data sources, provide uncertainty information for each point estimate, and yield comparable estimates. Second, we applied the Joinpoint regression method to estimate AAPC, enabling us to assess and describe the incidence trend of OA by summarizing and comparing measures across different age groups. Importantly, this study highlights the role of age as a crucial factor in the pathogenesis of OA and documents the potential impact of different age groups, specifically those over 40 years, on the incidence and prevalence of the disease.

Several limitations of this study warrant particular attention. Firstly, most surveys reporting the prevalence of OA are not nationally representative. Important risk factors for developing OA may differ for people living in urban, suburban, or rural areas; thus, local studies may not accurately reflect the national context for which we are making our estimates. Additionally, the accuracy of our analyses is compromised by a variety of data collection methods and reporting standards. Particularly, as the definition of OA has evolved over the past three decades partly due to new diagnostic techniques for OA, it was not feasible to apply a uniform definition of OA in the GBD data collection. Therefore, trends in OA may be influenced by changes in its definition. Secondly, as with all GBD assessments, the accessibility and quality of primary data vary from country to country, especially concerning health information for middle-aged and older people. Consequently, the prevalence of OA in middle-aged and older adults may be underestimated in low-income and middle-income countries, potentially distorting our findings. Thirdly, our analysis includes data from the COVID-19 pandemic years (2020–2021), which may have introduced data collection artifacts due to disruptions in healthcare access and reporting. While we did not conduct a formal sensitivity analysis excluding these years due to the study’s descriptive design, we acknowledge this as a potential limitation. Future studies should re-evaluate these trends once more stable post-pandemic data become available. Finally, although we propose correlations between OA burden and SDI, these correlations should not be interpreted as causal. Due to the differences in causes between middle-aged and older adults identified in our analyses, the burden of OA may differ between these groups and younger adults if analyzed separately. Last but not least, we were unable to analyze osteoarthritis-related mortality in middle-aged and older adults because data on osteoarthritis-related mortality were not available from the GBD database.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, OA represents an escalating global health burden with substantial disparities by age, sex, and socioeconomic status. The rising impact of obesity further exacerbates this challenge. Our findings underscore the urgent need for early prevention, targeted interventions, and health policy reforms to mitigate the future burden of OA and improve quality of life among affected populations.
