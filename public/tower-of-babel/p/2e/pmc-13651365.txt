TRIM47 catalyzes MAVS SUMOylation to safeguard chronic viral infections and tissue inflammation
Authors: Wenting Lu, Junji Xing, Junying Wang, Laurie J. Minze, Xian C. Li, Zhiqiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic tissue inflammation causes progressive tissue damage, organ dysfunction, and increased susceptibility to inflammatory diseases. Viral infections are major drivers of this process, but the molecular mechanisms linking antiviral immune responses to persistent inflammation and tissue pathology remain poorly understood.MethodsThe function of TRIM47 was examined using primary macrophages and mouse models of RNA virus infection. Alveolar macrophages, peritoneal macrophages, and bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDM) from wild-type and Trim47-deficient (Trim47-KO) mice were treated with RNA viruses or RNA mimics, and antiviral cytokine production was quantified. Sublethal reovirus and influenza A virus infection mouse models were used to evaluate survival, cytokine responses, and viral titers in vivo. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) was used to identify TRIM47-interacting proteins. Molecular and biochemical assays were used to examine post-translational modification, aggregation and activation of mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS).ResultsTRIM47 was highly expressed in macrophages, and TRIM47 deficiency in BMDM markedly reduced type I interferon (IFN-I) production following RNA virus infection or RNA mimics stimulation. In sublethal RNA virus infection mouse models, Trim47-KO mice exhibited reduced survival, impaired IFN-I responses, higher viral titers, increased inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue inflammation including myocarditis compared to wild-type mice. Mechanistically, TRIM47 interacted with MAVS and promoted SUMO1-mediated MAVS SUMOylation at K297 and K348, thereby enhancing MAVS aggregation and activation and driving robust IFN-I, IL-1β and IL-18 production in macrophages.ConclusionsThese findings establish TRIM47 as a molecular switch of MAVS activation and antiviral cytokine responses in macrophages, highlighting its role in restricting RNA virus infection and mitigating inflammation. This study provides mechanistic insight into host pathways that may be therapeutically leveraged in chronic viral inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12964-026-03066-w.

Introduction

Persistent viral infections occur when the virus evades clearance, remaining latent or active within specific cells of the infected individual. Chronic tissue infection, a subset of these persistent infections, is marked by the ongoing presence of the virus after initial infection and may manifest as chronic or recurring disease [1]. Despite their significance, the mechanisms underlying chronic tissue infections remain poorly understood. Innate immunity is the body’s first line of defense and pivotal in combatting viral infections [2–7]. Among the cells involved, macrophages play a central role by engulfing viral particles, producing inflammatory cytokines, and activating dendritic cells (DCs) [8], which in turn release large amounts of type I interferons (IFN-I) [9–12]. Beyond cellular responses, innate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), are critical for initial infection detection [2, 13–17]. Upon RNA virus detection, RLRs, such as retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), engage mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS) which is an essential adaptor protein localized on mitochondria, peroxisomes, and mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum membranes (MAMs) [18–20]. The activation of MAVS induces its aggregation, forming a signaling platform known as the MAVS signalosome which facilitates the recruitment of several key signaling molecules, including TNFR1-associated death domain protein (TRADD), tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factors (TRAFs), and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) [21, 22]. These molecules form a complex that activates downstream signaling cascades crucial for antiviral responses. The MAVS signalosome initiates two key cytosolic kinases: TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) and IκB kinase epsilon (IKKε) [23, 24]. Activated TBK1 and IKKε subsequently phosphorylate and activate interferon regulatory factors IRF3 and IRF7, as well as nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), which then translocate to the nucleus to promote transcription of IFN-I and type III interferons, as well as proinflammatory cytokines [23, 25, 26]. These cytokines are essential for coordinating immune responses to eliminate the virus and infected cells [27–29]. Additionally, MAVS also plays a vital role in inflammasome activation, specifically in the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, which amplifies inflammatory responses and contributes to chronic tissue inflammation [30–32]. MAVS recruits NLRP3 to mitochondria, where this proximity facilitates inflammasome assembly. NLRP3 interacts with ASC (apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD), recruiting and activating pro-caspase-1 [33, 34]. Upon activation, caspase-1 cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms, IL-1β and IL-18, which are secreted and drive robust inflammatory responses [35, 36]. In addition to cytokine maturation, NLRP3 activation via MAVS can lead to pyroptosis which further exacerbates tissue inflammation and creates a feedback loop sustaining chronic inflammation [31, 37]. The MAVS-mediated inflammasome activity is thus a critical component of the immune system’s response to RNA viruses, where sustained MAVS signaling may perpetuate inflammation, contributing to the persistence of chronic tissue inflammation.

E3 ubiquitin ligases are enzymes central to the ubiquitination process, transferring ubiquitin to lysine residues on target proteins [38]. This post-translational modification influences protein’s stability, activity, subcellular localization, and molecular interactions [39, 40]. In addition to ubiquitination, E3 ligases (E3s) can catalyze the addition of small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) proteins, thereby further modulating protein functions. Although only a limited number of SUMO E3s have been identified, members of the tripartite motif (TRIM) superfamily are increasingly recognized as SUMO E3s [41, 42]. These TRIM proteins enhance SUMO transfer by binding both the SUMO-conjugating enzyme (Ubc9) and specific substrates, thereby influencing numerous cellular processes crucial for immune responses [41]. TRIM proteins share a characteristic structure with three domains: the RING-finger domain, one or two B-box domains, and a coiled-coil region [43, 44]. TRIM proteins are pivotal in processes such as cell growth, DNA repair, intracellular signaling, and immune responses, with several members directly implicated in antiviral immunity [45, 46]. We previously reported that TRIM18 modulates responses to viral myocarditis and other viral inflammations by stabilizing the phosphatase PPM1A via K63-linked ubiquitination, thereby dampening excessive immune responses to both RNA and DNA viruses [47]. We also found that TRIM29 regulates antiviral innate immunity against respiratory RNA and DNA viruses in respiratory tract by targeting and degrading the adaptor protein NEMO and STING via K48-lined ubiquitination [48, 49]. Likewise, TRIM60 is reported to promote SUMOylation of TAB2 to regulate septic shock induced by LPS and bacteria [50]. TRIM47, a protein within the TRIM family, is recognized for its role in promoting cancer progression [51, 52] and regulating lung injury and inflammation induced by LPS and allergens [53, 54]. However, the role of TRIM47 in virus-induced tissue chronic inflammation is completely unknown.

In this study, we found that TRIM47 is highly expressed in macrophages, a critical cell type in orchestrating immune responses to viral infections. Macrophage-derived TRIM47 controls chronic tissue inflammations, including intestinal inflammation, lung inflammation, and myocarditis, induced by RNA viruses by promoting MAVS SUMOylation and aggregation. These findings identify TRIM47 as a key regulator of RNA virus-induced innate immune signaling and suggest that the TRIM47-MAVS axis may be a potential therapeutic target for chronic viral inflammation.

Methods

Mice

Trim47 knockout (Trim47-KO) mice were acquired from the Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Centers at the University of Missouri. All animals were on the C57BL/6 genetic background and maintained in the specific pathogen-free facility at Houston Methodist Research Institute in Houston, Texas. Animals were housed under the following conditions: temperatures of 68–72 F, 30–70% humidity, 10–15 fresh air exchanges hourly, and a 12:12 h light: dark cycle (lights were on from 07:00–19:00). Mice were housed in sterile individually-ventilated cages (Techniplast S.p.A., Buguggiate, Italy) containing autoclaved Bed-o’Cobs 1/4” bedding (The Andersons, Inc.), a sterile cotton nesting square or sterile crinkle nesting material, and received approximately 75 air changes hourly [47–49, 55–59]. Mice were housed at a density of up to five mice.

In vivo virus infection

For reovirus infection, 2- to 3-week-old mice were intragastrically inoculated with 1 × 108 PFU of the reovirus type 3 Dearing (T3D) strain in PBS [55, 57, 58], obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (VR-824). For survival analysis, the mice were monitored daily. On day 2 post-inoculation, mice were euthanized, and intestinal tissues were excised, homogenized in PBS, and analyzed for cytokine production via ELISA. By day 4 post-infection, mice were euthanized, intestines and mesenteric lymph nodes were collected for flow cytometry, and their intestinal tissues were harvested to determine infectious viral titers using standard plaque assay [58].

For IAV infection study, 6-week-old mice were infected intranasally with IAV (sub-lethal dose: 1 × 102 PFU/mouse, lethal dose: 1 × 104 PFU/mouse) in 40 µl PBS after anesthesia. The survival of mice was monitored daily after Flu PR8 infection [47, 48]. The viral titers in the lung were quantified by plaque assay [48]. Cytokine concentrations in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) were quantified using ELISA.

Plaque assay

Infectious viral titers from targets (intestines, lungs, and hearts) were standardly determined by plaque assay to provide gold-standard infectious quantification. Briefly, harvested organ samples were weighed precisely and homogenized. Serially diluted supernatants were inoculated onto confluent monolayers of permissive host cells in 12-well culture plates. After a 1-hour incubation for viral adsorption, the inoculum was standardly replaced with an agar nutrient overlay. Cells were incubated at 37 °C until active plaques developed. Cells were then fixed with 4% formaldehyde and stained with crystal violet to manually enumerate plaque-forming units (PFU). Final infectious loads are standardly presented as PFU per gram of tissue mass (PFU/g organ) [42, 48, 55, 57, 58].

Reagents

LPS (tlrl-pglps), 5’ppp RNA (tlrl-3prna), poly I: C (HMW, tlrl-pic), ATP (tlrl-atpl), Nigericin (tlrl-nig), and poly dA: dT (tlrl-patn) were from InvivoGen. Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, ThermoFisher) was used to transfect the stimulus into cells. The following antibodies were used for immunoprecipitation: anti-MAVS (1:100; 4983 S; Cell Signaling Technology) and anti-TRIM47 (1:100; A17803; Abclonal). The following antibodies were used for immunoblot analysis: anti-MAVS (1:1000; 4983 S; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-TRIM47 (1:500; A17803; Abclonal), anti-ubiquitin (1:1000; sc-8017; Santa Cruz), K63-specific anti-ubiquitin (1:1000; 05–1313; Millipore), anti-GAPDH (1:10,000; clone GAPDH-71.1, G9295; Sigma), anti-HA (1:5000; clone HA-7, H6533; Sigma), anti-Myc (1:5000; ab1326; Abcam), anti-β-actin (IB:1:10,000; A3854; Sigma), peroxidase affinipure goat anti-mouse light chain specific IgG (IB:1:10,000; 115–035–174, Jackson ImmunoResearch), and peroxidase mouse anti-rabbit light chain specific IgG (IB:1:10,000; 211–032–171, Jackson ImmunoResearch). The IFN-β, IFNλ, IL-1β and IL-18 ELISA kit was from R&D systems. Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910) was from Promega. Influenza A virus (PR8 A/Puerto Rico/8/1934(H1N1)) and Reovirus T3D strain were purchased from ATCC (ATCC® VR-95™ and ATCC® VR-824 ™).

Peritoneal macrophage isolation and culture

Wild-type (WT) and Trim47-KO mice were then euthanized, and the peritoneal cavity was carefully exposed, ensuring the integrity of the peritoneum was maintained. A 20G needle connected to a 5-mL syringe filled with cold harvest medium was inserted into the peritoneal cavity, and 5 mL of medium was injected. The abdomen was gently massaged, and peritoneal fluid was slowly aspirated. The collected fluid was centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and the cell pellet was resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C to allow macrophages to adhere. Non-adherent cells were removed by washing with PBS, and the remaining adherent cells, predominantly macrophages, were cultured for further use.

Alveolar macrophage isolation and culture

To isolate alveolar macrophages, WT and Trim47-KO mice were euthanized, and the trachea was exposed and cannulated using a 20G catheter. The lungs were lavaged with 1 mL of cold, sterile PBS, and the lavage fluid was gently aspirated using a 1 mL syringe. This process was repeated five times to ensure efficient recovery of alveolar macrophages. The collected BALF was centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to pellet the cells. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was resuspended in complete RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were seeded into culture dishes and allowed to adhere for at least 4 h at 37 °C. After incubation, the dishes were washed with PBS to remove non-adherent cells. The attached cells, which were predominantly alveolar macrophages, were retained for further use.

Bone marrow derived macrophage isolation and culture

WT and Trim47-KO mice were euthanized and their bodies were sterilized with 70% ethanol. The skin was removed to expose the lower limbs. The femur and tibia were excised and cleaned of surrounding tissues, taking care not to damage the bones. Both bones were placed in ice-cold PBS. The epiphyses were cut to expose the bone marrow, which was accessed by inserting a 23G needle into the bone cavity. Bone marrow was flushed out with 2–3 mL of PBS per bone into a 15 mL tube, avoiding backflow to prevent contamination. The suspension was placed on ice and centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer and mixed thoroughly and then incubated on ice for 2 min. The cells were washed, centrifuged again, and resuspended in 5 mL of bone marrow culture medium. The medium consisted of RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 10 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor. The suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer, counted, and seeded into petri dishes at equal concentrations for both WT and Trim47-KO mice. On day 3, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium. Bone marrow-derived macrophages could be collected on day 6.

Isolation of lamina propria lymphocytes from intestine

Small intestines were sectioned into 1-cm pieces and incubated in RPMI 1640 medium containing 0.5 mmol/L EDTA for 20 min at 37 °C to remove epithelial cells and mucus. The tissues were then washed 4 to 5 times with prewarmed PBS, minced, and enzymatically dissociated in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 0.5 mg/mL collagenase D, and 100 µg/mL DNase I for two 30-minute intervals at 37 °C with shaking. Lamina propria lymphocytes were subsequently enriched using 40% and 75% Percoll gradients, followed by centrifugation without braking (2200 rpm for 20 min at room temperature) [55, 57].

Cell stimulation

For reovirus and influenza A virus infection, change the culture medium of peritoneal macrophage, alveolar macrophages and BMDM into no serum medium. Then cells were cultured with reovirus or influenza A virus at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 2 for 18 h. Complete medium was restored 4 h after viral infection. For 5’ppp RNA and Poly I: C stimulation, cells were transfected with 5’ppp RNA at 0.4 µg/ml or Poly I: C at 2 µg/ml delivered by Lipofectamine 3000. For all stimulation used for IL-1β detection, cells were first primed with LPS at 200 ng/ml for 4 h, followed by stimulation with ATP at 2 and 5 mM for 30min [56], or Nigericin at 2 and 5 µM for 2 h, or poly dA: dT at 2.5 and 5 mM for 18 h, or Poly I: C at 2 and 10 µg/ml for 18 h.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatants, BALF and intestinal tissue homogenates were collected for cytokine measurements. For BALF collection, tracheas were cannulated following exsanguination, and lungs were lavaged with 1 mL sterile PBS. The lavage volume was kept identical across all experimental groups to ensure accurate volumetric comparisons. BALF samples were centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min to remove cells, and the resulting supernatants were further clarified by centrifugation at 13,000 × g for 1 min. For intestinal cytokine measurements, intestinal tissues were harvested, weighed, and homogenized in PBS at a ratio of 50 mg tissue per 200 µL PBS. Tissue homogenates were centrifuged to remove debris, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. The concentrations of IFN-β, IFN-λ, IL-1β, and IL-18 were determined using commercially available mouse ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cytokine concentrations in BALF and cell culture supernatants were expressed as pg/mL. Cytokine levels in intestinal tissue homogenates were normalized to tissue weight and expressed as pg/g organ.

Flow cytometry

Cells isolated from mesenteric lymph nodes and lamina propria lymphocytes were first stained with the Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend) for 10 min to assess cell viability. This was followed by staining with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies on ice for 20 min. After staining, cells were washed twice with PBS/BSA and then subjected to flow cytometry analysis. Data acquisition was performed using an LSR-II flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson), and the data were analyzed with FlowJo v10 software (Tree Star) [42, 48, 55, 57, 58]. The following antibodies were used for flow cytometry: PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD45 (103116, BioLegend), PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD4 (100528, BioLegend), PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-mouse CD8a (100734, BioLegend), APC anti-mouse CD19 (152410, BioLegend), and PE anti-mouse NK-1.1 (108708, BioLegend), APC anti-mouse CD64 (139306, BioLegend), PE anti-mouse Ly6G (127608, BioLegend), Brilliant Violet 785 anti-mouse/human CD11b (101243, BioLegend), PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse Ly6C (128018, BioLegend).

Quantitative qRT-PCR

RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated RNA was used to synthesize cDNA with the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad). iTaq SYBR Green Supermix with ROX (Bio-Rad) was used for quantitative qRT-PCR. qRT-PCR were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as relative expression values normalized to the corresponding housekeeping genes and expressed relative to WT controls. The following primers were used: IFN-β forward, 5′-CCCTATGGAGATGACGGAGA-3′ and reverse, 5′-TCCCACGTCAATCTTTCCTC-3′; IFN-α forward, 5′-AGTGAGCTGACCCAGCAGAT-3′ and reverse, 5′-CAGGGGCTGTGTTTCTTCTC-3; GAPDH forward, 5′-AACTTTGGCATTGTGGAAGG-3′ and reverse, 5′-ACACATTGGGGGTAGGAACA-3′; PR8 NS1 forward, 5′-TGTCAAGCTTTCAGGTAGATTG-3′ and reverse, 5′- CTCTTAGGGATTTCTGATCTC-3′;Ppia forward, 5’- CGCTTGCTGCAGCCATGGTC-3’ and reverse 5’- CAGCTCGAAGGAGACGCGGC-3’.

Histology

For intestine histology, intestines were harvested from wild-type and Trim47-KO mice following reovirus infection. After removal, the intestines were thoroughly washed with PBS and subsequently fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature. The fixed tissues were then embedded in paraffin and processed using standard histological techniques. Longitudinal sections of 5 μm thickness were prepared, stained with Haematoxylin & Eosin (H&E), and examined using a digital inverted light microscope (EVOS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) as described in previous studies [48, 49]. The histology score was determined based on the degree of intestinal inflammation and tissue damage. Inflammation was evaluated by the presence of infiltrating mononuclear cells, polymorphonuclear cells, and lymphocytes, with scores ranging from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe). Tissue damage was assessed through scores assigned to crypt hyperplasia, epithelial injury, and epithelial cell death, also on a scale from 0 (absent) to 3 (severe) [55].

For heart and lung histology, a 20-gauge angiocatheter was inserted into the trachea, and the heart and lungs were excised together as a single unit. The lungs were carefully inflated with up to 0.8 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). The heart and lungs were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 °C. Following fixation, the tissues were processed, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm, and stained with H&E. The severity of pneumonia in the lungs of mice infected with Flu PR8 was evaluated on a 5-point scale [60], where 0 indicates no or minimal damage, 1 denotes mild damage, 2 represents intermediate severity, 3 indicates moderately severe damage, and 4 reflects severe and widespread lesions. Lesions were defined as regions exhibiting inflammation, cell necrosis, or tissue loss.

Echocardiography measurement

Cardiac function was assessed using a Vevo 2100 ultrasound system (VisualSonics, Toronto, Canada) with 2D and M-mode imaging, following previously described protocols [42]. Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation, and body temperature was maintained at 37 °C throughout the procedure. Following hair removal and application of ophthalmic lubricant, the left ventricle (LV) long-axis view was acquired. The transducer was then rotated 90° clockwise to obtain the LV short-axis view, visualizing the papillary muscles. M-mode images were subsequently acquired. Ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were analyzed using Vevo LAB 5.6.0 software. Post-procedure, residual ultrasound gel was removed, and mice were allowed to recover.

In vitro pull-down and immunoblot analysis

For the endogenous immunoprecipitation assay, BMDM from wild-type mice were cultured with reovirus for 6 h and then lysed in IP lysis buffer (87788; Fisher Scientific) containing a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail for 30 min. Pre-cleaned protein A/G agarose beads were incubated with primary antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C, followed by three washes with PBS. The lysates were then centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatants. These supernatants were subsequently added to the antibody-coated protein A/G agarose beads and incubated with continuous rotation for 2 h at 4 °C. The resulting precipitates were washed three times with cold IP lysis buffer, and the final bead-bound complexes were resolved by SDS-PAGE.

For the preparation of purified MAVS and TRIM47 proteins, HEK293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216) were transfected with expression plasmids encoding either full-length or truncated versions of Myc- or HA-tagged MAVS or TRIM47. These plasmids were constructed using the pCMV-Myc or pCMV-HA vectors (Catalog: 631604, Clontech). Cell lysates were collected post-transfection and incubated with anti-HA or anti-Myc beads to capture the tagged proteins. After extensive washing with PBS (six times), the proteins were eluted from the beads. For co-immunoprecipitation experiments, purified Myc-tagged full-length MAVS or its truncations were incubated with purified HA-tagged TRIM47 (or vice versa) for 2 h. Following incubation, the protein complexes were captured by adding beads and then pelleted by centrifugation. The bound protein complexes were subsequently analyzed by immunoblotting using anti-HA or anti-Myc antibodies [61].

Luciferase reporter gene assay

HEK293T cells were plated in 48-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and subsequently transfected with a combination of reporter plasmids, including IFN-β-firefly luciferase (100 ng) and Renilla luciferase (1 ng), along with an expression vector for either wild-type (WT) MAVS or MAVS mutants (50 ng), with or without an expression vector for TRIM47 (300 ng). An empty control vector was used to ensure that a total of 450 ng of vector DNA was transfected into each well. After 24 h of post-transfection, cells were harvested, and luciferase activity was measured in total cell lysates using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay.

Immunoprecipitation–mass spectrometry assay

D2SC cells were transfected with cytosolic VACV-70 dsDNA (5 µg/ml) using Lipofectamine 3000 for 8 h. Cells were lysed with RIPA lysis buffer (sc-24948, Santa Cruz). After spinning down, supernatants were collected and immunoprecipitated by anti-TRIM47 antibody. The immunoprecipitation samples were sent out for protein sequencing by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry at Mass Spectrometry - Proteomics Core Laboratory in Baylor College of Medicine [42, 48, 49, 58].

Semi-Denaturing Detergent Agarose Gel Electrophoresis (SDD–AGE)

SDD–AGE was conducted following a previously published protocol with slight modifications [62]. Cells were lysed in Buffer A (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.25 M D-mannitol and a protease inhibitor cocktail) through repeated douncing. The homogenate was centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 30 min at 4 °C to isolate the crude intact mitochondria (P5) as a pellet. The P5 fraction was resuspended in 1× sample buffer containing 0.5× TBE, 10% glycerol, 2% SDS, and 0.0025% bromophenol blue, and subsequently loaded onto a vertical 1.5% agarose gel. Electrophoresis was carried out at 4 °C for 35 min in running buffer (1× TBE and 0.1% SDS) under a constant voltage of 100 V. Proteins were then transferred to an Immobilon membrane (Millipore) at 4 °C using a constant voltage of 20 V for 1 h. The membrane was probed with anti-MAVS or anti-Myc antibodies following standard western blotting protocols.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Exact p values are reported in the corresponding figures.

Results

TRIM47 deficiency impairs the production of IFN-β and IL-1β in macrophages in response to RNA stimuli and RNA viruses

TRIM47 was highly expressed in macrophages, including peritoneal macrophages, alveolar macrophages, and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) (Figure S1A). To define the role of macrophage-expressed TRIM47 in RNA virus infection, we isolated peritoneal macrophages, alveolar macrophages, and BMDM from wild-type (WT) and Trim47-deficient (Trim47-KO) mice. These cells were infected with RNA viruses, including influenza A virus (Flu) and reovirus, or stimulated with RNA sensor agonists, including 5’ppp RNA and Poly I: C, and IFN-β levels in the supernatants were measured by ELISA. Trim47-KO peritoneal macrophages produced 2- to 3-fold less IFN-β than WT counterparts in response to Flu virus and reovirus (Fig. 1A and B). Similarly, stimulation with 5’ppp-RNA or Poly I: C yielded approximately one-third the IFN-β levels in Trim47-KO peritoneal macrophages compared to WT macrophages (Fig. 1C and D). In alveolar macrophages, TRIM47 deficiency also led to a significant reduction in IFN-β production following Flu virus and reovirus infection (Fig. 1E and F) and after 5’ppp RNA or Poly I: C stimulation (Fig. 1G and H). Likewise, BMDM lacking TRIM47 produced significantly lower IFN-β across multiple doses of RNA viruses and RNA sensor agonists (Fig. 1I-L). These findings suggest that TRIM47 positively regulates IFN-β production in macrophages in response to RNA virus infection and RNA mimics.

During infection, IL-1β is released at the earliest stage of the immune response, initiating a cascade of proinflammatory cytokines dependent on inflammasome activation [63], with the NLRP3 inflammasome being the most established pathway [64]. Since RNA virus infection can drive chronic tissue inflammation [65] and TRIM47 promotes antiviral IFN-β production (Fig. 1A-L), we investigated whether macrophage-expressed TRIM47 also regulates IL-1β production in RNA virus induced inflammatory responses. First, we treated BMDM with NLRP3 agonists (ATP and nigericin) to assess the role of TRIM47 in inflammasome-mediated IL-1β production. Trim47-KO BMDM produced 5-fold and 10-fold less IL-1β than WT BMDM in response to low and high doses of ATP and nigericin, respectively (Fig. 1M and N). As a control, stimulation with the AIM2 inflammasome agonist poly dA: dT resulted in comparable IL-1β levels in WT and Trim47-KO BMDM (Fig. 1O), indicating that TRIM47’s inflammasome regulation is specific to NLRP3. Next, we used Poly I: C, an RNA mimic that activates RNA-sensing pathways akin to RNA viruses, to evaluate IL-1β production. Similar to NLRP3 agonist treatment, Poly I: C stimulation led to significantly lower IL-1β levels in Trim47-KO BMDM compared to WT BMDM (Fig. 1P). These results indicate that TRIM47 is also essential for NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent IL-1β production in macrophages during RNA virus infections.

TRIM47 is essential for controlling intestinal inflammation induced by enteric reovirus infection in vivo

Given that macrophage-expressed TRIM47 enhances antiviral and proinflammatory cytokine production in response to RNA viruses and RNA mimics ex vivo (Fig. 1), we further investigated the role of TRIM47 in response to RNA viruses in vivo. Reovirus, a double-stranded RNA virus known to infect gastrointestinal tracts, was chosen due to its capability to establish persistent or chronic infections through viral mutations or host cell factors [66]. We intragastrically infected age- and sex-matched 2- to 3-week-old WT and Trim47-KO mice with enteric reovirus at 1 × 108 PFU per mouse and monitored survival over time. Upon reovirus infection, WT mice survived the observation period, indicating the development of chronic tissue inflammation (Fig. 2A), whereas Trim47-KO mice exhibited significantly lower survival rates (Fig. 2A). Reovirus infection became lethal in Trim47-KO mice by day 3 post-inoculation, with nearly 80% mortality by day 5 (Fig. 2A). Thus, TRIM47 is essential for survival during RNA virus reovirus infection, as its absence markedly increases mortality in a sublethal reovirus challenge.

Given the essential role of IFN-I in antiviral defense and the involvement of IL-1β and IL-18 as early inflammatory cytokines, we measured these cytokines expression at the local infection site to evaluate both antiviral and inflammatory responses. Intestines were harvested on day 2 post-inoculation and then homogenized. Protein levels of IFN-β, IFN-λ, IL-1β and IL-18 were quantified by ELISA and normalized to tissue mass, whereas IFN-α and IFN-β mRNA transcripts were assessed by qRT-PCR and presented as fold change relative to WT controls. Trim47-KO mice showed 2- to 3-fold lower intestinal expression of IFN-β, IFN-λ, IL-1β and IL-18 than WT mice (Fig. 2B-E). In addition, Trim47-KO mice exhibited a prominent 5-fold less IFN-α and IFN-β expression at RNA levels than WT mice (Fig. 2F and G). Infectious viral titers measured by plaque assay on day 4 post-inoculation were significantly higher in the intestines of Trim47-KO mice than in WT mice (Fig. 2H). H&E staining of intestines collected on day 4 post-inoculation showed intact and compact villus architecture in mock-infected mice but more severe villus damage in reovirus-infected Trim47-KO mice than in infected WT mice (Fig. 2I), consistent with higher histology scores in Trim47-KO mice (Fig. 2J).

Because immune cell infiltration contributes to persistent inflammation, we analyzed neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, T cells, B cells, and NK cells in the intestines and mesenteric lymph nodes (mLN) on day 4 post-inoculation by flow cytometry (Figure S2). Trim47-KO mice exhibited increased recruitment of CD11b⁺Ly6G⁺ neutrophils (Fig. 2N) and Ly6Chigh inflammatory monocytes (Fig. 2K) into the infected intestinal microenvironment compared with WT controls. Trim47-KO mice also showed increased CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells accumulation in both intestine and mLN compared with WT mice (Fig. 2L and M). Conversely, B cells numbers were higher in both intestine and mLN of WT mice than in Trim47-KO mice (Fig. 2O and Q), whereas NK cell infiltrations were comparable in both intestine and mLN between WT mice and Trim47-KO mice (Fig. 2P and R). These results indicate that TRIM47 limits inflammatory myeloid cells and T cell infiltration in the intestine and mLN while promoting B cell presence in the mLN, modulating immune cell dynamics during reovirus infection. Collectively, these in vivo data demonstrate that TRIM47 is essential for antiviral host defense against enteric RNA virus infection and for limiting intestinal inflammation.

TRIM47 is essential for controlling lung inflammation induced by influenza A virus in vivo

Given the high expression of TRIM47 in lung tissue (Figure S1B), we investigated its role in antiviral inflammation using Flu virus, an RNA virus that primarily targets the respiratory tract. WT and Trim47-KO mice were intranasally infected with a sublethal dose of IAV (1 × 102 PFU per mouse), and survival was monitored. All WT mice survived the entire observation period, whereas Trim47-KO mice showed progressive mortality, with complete mortality by day 12 (Fig. 3A). Body weight changes mirrored this pattern: WT mice recovered after transient weight loss, whereas Trim47-KO mice continued to lose weight and reached maximum loss by day 11 (Fig. 3B). On day 6 post-inoculation, infectious viral titers in the lungs of Trim47-KO mice were markedly higher than those in WT mice (Fig. 3C). Consistently, BALF from Trim47-KO mice contained significantly lower levels of IFN-β and IFN-λ (Fig. 3D and E) as well as approximately threefold lower IL-1β and IL-18 levels than BALF from WT mice (Fig. 3F and G). Histopathological analysis further showed more severe inflammatory cell infiltration in Trim47-KO lungs, accompanied by significantly higher histology scores (Fig. 3H and I). These results demonstrate that TRIM47 is required for antiviral protection and control of RNA virus-induced chronic lung inflammation.

To determine whether TRIM47 also regulates acute tissue inflammation caused by RNA viruses, WT and Trim47-KO mice were infected with a lethal dose of Flu virus (1 × 104 PFU per mouse), and survival and body weight changes were monitored. Trim47-KO mice exhibited significantly lower survival than WT mice, with mortality beginning on day 4 and reaching 100% by day 5, whereas WT mice showed a more gradual decline and reached 100% mortality by day 8 (Figure S3A). Trim47-KO mice also experienced more pronounced weight loss beginning on day 2 post-inoculation and continuing until death (Figure S3B). On day 4, infectious viral titers in Trim47-KO lungs were higher than those in WT lungs (Figure S3C), and BALF from WT mice contained 2- to 4-fold higher levels of IFN-β, IFN-λ, IL-1β, and IL-18 than BALF from Trim47-KO mice (Figure S3D-3G). TRIM47 deficiency exacerbates virus-induced myocarditis

Given that even a sublethal dose of reovirus or Flu virus can cause mortality in Trim47-KO mice and both reovirus and Flu virus are cardiotropic viruses, we further examined other organs and found significant cardiac involvement upon infection. To assess this, we performed echocardiography, histological analysis, and viral titer quantification on day 4 post-infection with a sublethal dose of reovirus. Echocardiography revealed impaired cardiac function in Trim47-KO mice (Fig. 4A), with significantly reduced ejection fraction (EF) (Fig. 4B) and fractional shortening (FS) (Fig. 4C) compared to WT mice. EF measures the percentage of blood ejected from the left ventricle per heartbeat, while FS reflects the percentage reduction in left ventricular diameter during systole which both parameters commonly used to evaluate cardiac function. Gross morphological examination of Trim47-KO hearts showed larger white plaque areas, suggesting inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4D). Consistently, H&E staining confirmed normal cardiac morphology in WT mice, whereas Trim47-KO mice exhibited extensive inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 4E). Additionally, the infectious viral titer determined by plaque assay in Trim47-KO hearts was significantly higher than that of WT hearts (Fig. 4F). A similar trend was observed on day 6 post-infection with a sublethal dose of Flu virus. Trim47-KO mice exhibited impaired cardiac function (Fig. 4G), with decreased EF (Fig. 4H) and FS (Fig. 4I) compared to WT mice. White plaque areas were more extensive in Trim47-KO hearts (Fig. 4J). H&E staining confirmed increased inflammatory infiltration in Trim47-KO hearts (Fig. 4K). Consistently, the viral titers in Trim47-KO hearts were significantly higher than in WT hearts (Fig. 4L). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that TRIM47 deficiency exacerbates virus-induced myocarditis, contributing to increased mortality in Trim47-KO mice.

TRIM47 binds directly to MAVS to enhance IFN-β promoter activation

To further investigate how TRIM47 regulates RNA virus-induced inflammation, we sought to identify its potential target proteins involved in the RNA-sensing signaling pathway. Using a TRIM47-specific antibody, we immunoprecipitated TRIM47-associated proteins from D2SC cell lysates and analyzed them via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry. Among the identified proteins, MAVS, a key adaptor in RNA virus sensing, was prominently enriched (Fig. 5A). To verify downstream pathway activation and capture protein expression kinetics after virus infection, immunoblot analysis demonstrated that reovirus infection triggered robust downstream IRF-3 phosphorylation (p-IRF3). Importantly, the overall expression lines of total MAVS and TRIM47 proteins increased over the infection time course, verifying that their binding occurs independent of changes in baseline protein stability (Fig. 5B). Next, we assessed the interaction between TRIM47 and MAVS in BMDM under endogenous conditions. Co-immunoprecipitation with anti-TRIM47 antibody, but not control IgG, revealed a direct interaction between TRIM47 and MAVS in reovirus-infected BMDM (Fig. 5C). In contrast, no interaction was observed in the absence of reovirus infection (Fig. 5C), confirming that TRIM47 and MAVS interact specifically during RNA virus infection.

To further map the binding sites between MAVS and TRIM47, we generated HA-tagged full-length and truncated TRIM47 mutants (Fig. 5D), Myc-tagged full-length and truncated MAVS mutants (Fig. 5F). We then transfected HEK293T cells with Myc-tagged full-length MAVS and either HA-tagged full-length or truncated TRIM47 mutants. After immunoprecipitation with anti-HA antibody, we observed that full-length TRIM47 and all truncation mutants, except for the N-terminal domain, bound MAVS, indicating that the PRY/SPRY domain of TRIM47 is responsible for this interaction (Fig. 5E). Similarly, we co-transfected HEK293T cells with HA-tagged full-length TRIM47 and either Myc-tagged full-length or truncated MAVS mutants to map the MAVS domain interacting with TRIM47. Immunoprecipitation assays revealed that full-length MAVS and its central domain, but not the N-terminal CARD, proline-rich, or C-terminal regions, bound TRIM47, indicating that the central domain of MAVS mediates the interaction (Fig. 5G). These results suggest that TRIM47 binds directly to MAVS, highlighting a potential mechanism in RNA virus-induced inflammation.

Given that MAVS activation leads to IFN-β production, we next examined whether the interaction between TRIM47 and MAVS is essential for IFN-β induction. In a luciferase reporter assay using the IFN-β promoter (IFN-β-Luc) in HEK293T cells, we observed that overexpression of MAVS alone significantly activated the IFN-β promoter (Fig. 5H). However, overexpression of full-length TRIM47 (T47-a) and its MAVS-binding truncation mutant (T47-d) further enhanced MAVS-induced IFN-β promoter activity (Fig. 5H). In contrast, TRIM47 truncation lacking the MAVS-binding domain (T47-f) failed to exert this positive regulatory effect (Fig. 5H). Consistently, functional ELISA confirmed that overexpressing full-length TRIM47 significantly increased IFN-β level after reovirus challenge (Fig. 5I). Collectively, these data demonstrate that TRIM47 directly interacts with MAVS, and this interaction is essential for enhancing MAVS-driven IFN-β production during RNA virus infection.

TRIM47 promotes MAVS SUMOylation to maintain downstream activation

Building on the observed interaction between TRIM47 and MAVS (Fig. 5A and G) and its role in activating the MAVS signaling pathway (Fig. 5H and I), we further explored the molecular mechanisms by which TRIM47 promotes MAVS activation. Notably, our proteomic analysis identified Ubc9, a SUMO-conjugating E2 enzyme, among TRIM47-interacting proteins (Fig. 5A). Given the role of SUMOylation in regulating protein interactions and signaling and the function of certain TRIM family members as E3 SUMO ligases with Ubc9 [42, 50], we hypothesized that TRIM47 may promote MAVS activation through MAVS SUMOylation.

We first demonstrated that full-length TRIM47 and its PRY/SPRY domain-containing mutant interacted with Ubc9, whereas the mutant lacking this domain did not (Fig. 6A). To identify the specific SUMO subtype involved, we transfected HEK293T cells with His-tagged SUMO1, SUMO2 and SUMO3 together with Flag-tagged Ubc9, Myc-tagged MAVS, and HA-tagged TRIM47. We then performed Ni⁺-mediated pull-down assays to isolate SUMO-conjugated complexes and analyzed the samples by immunoblotting with an anti-Myc antibody to determine which SUMO subtype is associated with TRIM47 and MAVS. The results showed that TRIM47 specifically facilitated SUMO1, but not SUMO2 or SUMO3, modification of MAVS (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, full-length TRIM47, but not the mutant lacking the PRY/SPRY domain, promoted SUMO1-mediated MAVS SUMOylation (Fig. 6C). To identify MAVS SUMOylation sites regulated by TRIM47, we generated SUMOylation site mutants by substituting lysine residues with arginine at ten candidate sites (K136R, K270R, K297R, K325R, K331R, K348R, K371R, K420R, K461R, and K500R). Mutagenesis analysis revealed that K297R and K348R significantly reduced MAVS SUMOylation (Fig. 6D) and the K297RK348R double mutant nearly abolished SUMO1 modifications of MAVS (Fig. 6E). To assess the functional requirement of these sites, we compared WT MAVS with the K297RK348R double mutant during reovirus infection. The SUMOylation-deficient MAVS mutant failed to enhance downstream IFN-β production as efficiently as WT MAVS (Fig. 6F), supporting the functional importance of TRIM47-mediated MAVS SUMOylation at K297 and K348. Together, these results indicate that TRIM47 acts as a SUMO E3 ligase that promotes MAVS SUMOylation and antiviral signaling.

TRIM47 promotes MAVS aggregation

MAVS forms prion-like aggregates that amplify downstream signaling in response to RNA virus infection [62, 67]. To investigate whether TRIM47-induced SUMOylation enhances MAVS aggregation and promotes its activation, we assessed MAVS aggregation using semi-denaturing detergent agarose-gel electrophoresis (SDD–AGE). To investigate MAVS aggregation at the endogenous level, BMDM from both WT and Trim47-KO mice were infected with reovirus, and MAVS aggregation was subsequently assessed. In WT BMDM, reovirus infection robustly induced MAVS aggregation, while Trim47-KO BMDM exhibited significantly reduced MAVS aggregation than WT counterparts (Fig. 7A). To determine if the interaction between TRIM47 and MAVS is critical for this aggregation, HEK293T cells were transfected with Myc-tagged full-length MAVS, along with HA-tagged vector, full-length TRIM47 or a TRIM47 mutant lacking the MAVS binding domain, followed by MAVS aggregation analysis. Without reovirus infection, MAVS aggregation could barely be detected in all groups (Fig. 7B). However, upon reovirus infection, cells expressing full-length TRIM47 exhibited markedly enhanced MAVS aggregation, whereas cells transfected with the HA-tagged vector or TRIM47 mutant showed minimal MAVS aggregation (Fig. 7B). To directly evaluate the role of TRIM47-induced SUMOylation in MAVS aggregation, HEK293T cells were transfected with HA-tagged full length TRIM47 and Myc-tagged wild-type MAVS or a double SUMOylation-site mutant of MAVS (K297RK348R), or Myc-tagged vector. MAVS aggregation was analyzed following reovirus infection. Wild-type MAVS expressed in the presence of TRIM47 displayed robust aggregation, whereas cells expressing the SUMOylation-site mutant (K297RK348R) showed significantly reduced aggregation, similar to vector controls (Fig. 7C). Collectively, these data demonstrate that TRIM47 promotes MAVS aggregation after RNA virus infection in a manner that depends on TRIM47-MAVS interaction and MAVS SUMOylation.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the role of macrophage-expressed TRIM47 in reovirus and influenza virus-induced chronic tissue inflammation. Our findings demonstrate that TRIM47 binds to MAVS, promotes SUMO1-mediated MAVS SUMOylation, and amplifies MAVS-dependent downstream signaling. This signaling program enhances production of IFN-I, IL-1β and IL-18 following RNA virus infection, positioning TRIM47 as a positive regulator of macrophage antiviral immunity and a protective factor against RNA virus-induced chronic tissue inflammation (Fig. 8).

Macrophages are critical in defending against infectious microbes but are also implicated in driving acute and chronic inflammation, which can lead to significant tissue pathology [68–70]. Their presence in diverse tissues across various diseases highlights their role in sustaining inflammation. In rheumatoid arthritis, CD14low/CD16++ non-classical monocytes increase in patient blood, with their levels correlating with disease markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein [71–73]. Lower levels of these monocytes are associated with therapeutic response, suggesting that their abundance reflects inflammation severity. In atherosclerosis, a chronic inflammatory condition of large and medium-sized arteries, macrophages accumulate within vessel walls, contributing to lesion progression and plaque destabilization, thereby promoting cardiovascular disease [74, 75]. Similarly, in asthma, a chronic inflammatory airway disorder marked by hyper-responsiveness and tissue remodeling, macrophages show dysfunctional behavior, contributing to enhanced immune responses, airway remodeling, and reduced pathogen clearance, all of which can drive persistent inflammation and exacerbate asthma severity [76]. Another reason macrophages are crucial in chronic tissue inflammation is their abundance of PRRs and complex signaling pathways. RLRs are cytosolic PRRs that specifically detect RNA viruses. Upon viral infection, RLRs bind viral RNA and activate the adaptor protein MAVS, which triggers the induction of inflammatory and antiviral genes, including IFNs and IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) [77]. These IFNs serve to inhibit viral replication and prevent viral spread. MAVS is also essential for activating the NLRP3 inflammasome, as it recruits NLRP3 in response to RNA viruses [64]. During infection with RNA viruses, such as influenza virus and encephalomyocarditis virus, the mitochondrial outer membrane protein mitofusin 2 (MFN2) interacts with NLRP3, further inducing IL-1β and IL-18 secretion through NLRP3 inflammasome activation [78]. These IL-1 family cytokines are early-response cytokines, meaning they are released in the initial stages of the immune response and initiate a cascade of proinflammatory cytokines. IL-1β stimulates IL-6 and IL-17a, while IL-18 drives the production of IFN-γ, IL-2, and IL-12 [79–82]. The downstream cytokines, including IL-6 and IFN-γ, support proinflammatory T-helper 1 and T-helper 17 immune responses. Deletion of both Il1β and Il18 genes has been shown to completely abolish inflammation across tissues, highlighting their central role in immune-driven inflammation [83]. In this study, we observed high TRIM47 expressions across macrophage types, including BMDM, alveolar macrophages, brain microglia, and peritoneal macrophages. Trim47-KO mice exhibited significantly reduced survival rate, IFNs and IL-1 family cytokine levels compared to WT mice. The in vivo flow cytometry data (Fig. 2K and R) consistently support our ex vivo cellular findings. Although Trim47-KO macrophages show impaired cytokine production in vitro (Fig. 1), Trim47-KO mice exhibited a massive influx of neutrophils, monocytes, and T cells into the infected intestine. This apparent contradiction is driven by the early collapse of the type I IFN primary shield (Fig. 2B, C, F and G), which allows unchecked viral replication (Fig. 2H) and severe epithelial cell necrosis (Fig. 2I). The resulting tissue damage triggers secondary and compensatory recruitment of inflammatory myeloid and adaptive cells. However, this delayed and disorganized cellular infiltration fails to clear the virus and instead drives chronic, tissue-destructive immunopathology, leading to the accelerated mortality of Trim47-KO mice. Therefore, TRIM47 is required to orchestrate a timely, protective antiviral response and prevent secondary inflammatory storms. Additionally, when the binding domain between TRIM47 and MAVS was deleted, TRIM47 failed to induce MAVS SUMOylation and aggregation, leading to reduced IFNs, IL-1β and IL-18 production. Furthermore, our ex vivo datasets (Fig. 1M and P) show that Trim47-KO BMDM exhibit markedly reduced IL-1β maturation after stimulation with canonical NLRP3 agonists, including ATP and nigericin, even in the absence of viral infection. These findings suggest that TRIM47 may regulate NLRP3 inflammasome activation through additional MAVS-independent mechanisms that operate in parallel with the mitochondrial MAVS signaling network. Deciphering the molecular crosstalk and specific components involved in this MAVS-independent TRIM47-NLRP3 axis will be an important direction for future studies. These findings underscore the role of macrophage-expressed TRIM47 in driving RNA virus-induced chronic tissue inflammation through MAVS signaling modulation, establishing TRIM47 as a critical factor in the macrophage-mediated inflammatory response.

The MAVS signaling pathway plays a central role in host defense against RNA virus infection. MAVS-deficient mice exhibit increased apoptosis, reduced survival, impaired IFN production, and increased viral replication after Ebola virus infection [84]. TRIM family proteins regulate MAVS expression and activation through diverse post-translational mechanisms. For example, TRIM25 binds to MAVS, and promotes its degradation after RLRs activation [85]. Notably, residual MAVS detection in both wild-type MEFs and HEK293T cells transfected with the TRIM25-V5 plasmid suggests that TRIM25 does not fully degrade MAVS [85]. Conversely, TRIM31 facilitates MAVS aggregation and activation via Lys63-linked polyubiquitination [62], while TRIM21 promotes innate immunity against RNA viruses through MAVS ubiquitination [86, 87]. Several studies, including ours, highlight the context-dependent regulatory influence of TRIM47 on MAVS-related signaling. TRIM47 promotes cytokine secretion, inflammation, and pyroptosis in bronchial epithelial cells by activating the NF-κB/NLRP3 pathway in HDM-induced asthma [54]. It also enhances K63-linked TRAF2 ubiquitination in endothelial cells, thereby activating NF-κB and MAPK signaling and triggering pulmonary inflammation and acute lung injury [53]. Moreover, TRIM47 enhances NF-κB signaling through PKC-ε and PKD3 stabilization via lysine 27-linked polyubiquitination, contributing to tamoxifen resistance and cell proliferation [52]. In hematopoietic stem cells, TRIM47 prevents exhaustion under stress by modulating MAVS-mediated immunity through K48-linked ubiquitination [88]. In contrast, our study identifies TRIM47 as a positive regulator of MAVS signaling in macrophages during RNA virus infection through SUMO1-mediated MAVS modification and aggregation. These apparently divergent findings may reflect cell-type-specific and stimulus-specific functions of TRIM47, distinct post-translational modification outputs, and different immune contexts. The relationship between TRIM47-mediated SUMOylation and K48-linked ubiquitination of MAVS remains an important question for future studies.

Conclusions

Our study identifies that TRIM47 is a key regulator of macrophage antiviral innate immunity that protects against virus-driven chronic tissue inflammation. TRIM47 acts as a SUMO E3 ligase to catalyze MAVS SUMOylation at K297 and K348, thereby promoting MAVS aggregation and inducing robust antiviral and inflammatory cytokine responses required for effective viral control. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that TRIM47-mediated MAVS SUMOylation coordinates antiviral defense and limits RNA virus-induced chronic tissue inflammation, highlighting the TRIM47-MAVS axis as a potential therapeutic target for RNA virus-associated inflammatory diseases.
