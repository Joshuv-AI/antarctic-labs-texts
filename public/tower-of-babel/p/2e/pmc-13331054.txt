Disruptions in perinatal outcomes across Manitoba, Canada: before and over 2 years of the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Laila Aboulatta, Kaarina Kowalec, Lisa M Lix, Mina Tadrous, Joseph A Delaney, Christine Leong, Jamie Falk, Silvia Alessi-Severini, Christina Raimondi, Katherine Kearns, Lara Haidar, Payam Peymani, Qier Tan, Sherif Eltonsy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic disrupted healthcare service utilisation, but evidence on its impact on perinatal outcomes is conflicting. We investigated the impact of the pandemic public health measures (eg, lockdowns, reduced healthcare access, altered health-seeking behaviour) on preterm birth (PTB), stillbirth, low birth weight (LBW), small for gestational age, caesarean delivery, breastfeeding initiation (BFI) and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions.MethodsUsing linked administrative health databases, we conducted a population-based study of pregnancies before (January 2008–February 2020) or during (March 2020–March 2022) the pandemic; for the latter period, they were categorised by exposure duration. We conducted interrupted time series analyses using season-adjusted generalised linear models, and analyses were stratified by income.ResultsAmong 222 636 pregnancies (190 694 prepandemic and 31 942 during pandemic), PTB rates increased from 8% prepandemic to 9.1% during the pandemic, with a 19.0% relative increase overall (p<0.01). In stratified analyses, PTB rose by 27.1% in the lower-income group (8.8% to 10.1%, p=0.01) and by 19.7% in the higher income group (7.3% to 8.2%, p=0.02); however, effect modification by income was statistically non-significant. Pregnancies 100% exposed during the first and second year of the pandemic experienced higher rates of PTB by 15.0% (p=0.01) and 11.6% (p<0.01) compared with the prepandemic period, respectively. The pandemic measures were associated with no changes in stillbirth (0.7‰ vs 0.61%; p=0.37) or NICU admission rates (8.3% vs 8.2%; p=0.69). LBW rates increased from 5.5% to 6.3% (13.9% relative increase, p=0.05). Pregnancies spanning the entire first year of the pandemic experienced higher rates of caesarean deliveries (24.6% relative increase, p<0.01). BFI rates decreased from 82.5% to 81.8% (3.9% relative decrease, p=0.01) compared with prepandemic, followed by a slight increase (p=0.02) during the pandemic period.ConclusionsThe COVID-19 pandemic measures were associated with increased PTB and caesarean delivery rates, particularly among pregnancies fully exposed to pandemic measures. Over the 2-year pandemic period, stillbirth, NICU admissions and BFI rates were stable.

Introduction

Perinatal outcomes—including preterm birth (PTB), stillbirth, low birth weight (LBW) and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admissions—are considered key indicators of maternal and infant health.1 Recent reports from Canada have described rates of PTB at approximately 8%2 3 and stillbirth rates around 8.9 per 1000 total births.4 The rate of caesarean deliveries, Canada’s most common surgical procedure, has steadily risen, exceeding the WHO’s recommended level.5 6 COVID-19 public health measures exacerbated social inequalities and impacted the health of vulnerable individuals and their access to routine care.79 Reduced access to prenatal services, fear of contracting the novel coronavirus infection and gathering and transportation restrictions may have created an obstacle for pregnant women to receive optimal care, thus potentially increasing the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes.1017

While the effects of COVID-19 pandemic measures on rates of adverse perinatal outcomes have been widely documented across countries,1826 the precise impact remains less certain. A study based in a hospital in Northeastern Italy reported that changes in hospital policies and prenatal care adopted due to the pandemic were associated with reduced rates of breastfeeding initiation (BFI) and increased psychological distress among women who delivered there.27 Public health measures in the Netherlands and Denmark were associated with a decrease in PTB rates during the early months of the pandemic.22 23 However, pandemic measures in the UK were not associated with significant changes in PTB, stillbirths, caesarean deliveries and NICU admissions.2426 A meta-analysis of 36 studies found no difference in PTB, stillbirths and LBW in both national and single-centre studies before and during the pandemic.28 A meta-analysis of 17 studies found an association between the pandemic-related measures and stillbirths but not PTB and LBW.29

Reports from Canada on the effects of COVID-19 pandemic measures on perinatal outcomes have been conflicting. While some studies showed no significant changes in NICU admissions, stillbirths and PTB during the examined pandemic period compared with prepandemic period,1821 other studies reported increases in stillbirths and PTB and a decline in caesarean deliveries.30 31

Using the Manitoba administrative health databases, our team previously examined the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic measures on PTB and stillbirth during the first year of the pandemic (October 2016–March 2021).31 That study identified an immediate increase in PTB rates among lower-income groups that returned to baseline within three quarters. However, the analysis was limited to one pandemic year, relied on quarterly data and could not assess long-term trends or the effects of varying durations of exposure to pandemic measures during pregnancy.31

While the inconsistent findings across Canadian provinces and pandemic periods highlight the varying impact of the pandemic measures on pregnant women, important gaps remain. There is limited evidence with respect to the length of exposure to pandemic measures, that is, during how much of their pregnancy were pandemic measures in place, and how this relates to prenatal care and associated outcomes. In addition, pandemic measures—such as restrictions on healthcare access, reduced social support, changes in hospital policies, shifts in hygiene practices and physical distancing—may have differentially impacted perinatal outcomes across different regional and socio-economic settings. Overall, findings from Canadian studies suggest heterogeneous impacts of COVID-19 pandemic measures on perinatal outcomes, with no consistent patterns observed across outcomes or provinces.

Building on our prior work,31 the current study extends the follow-up to two full years of the pandemic and applies monthly interrupted time series (ITS) with exposure-level modelling across seven perinatal outcomes. This approach allowed the assessment of both the immediate and longer-term cumulative effects of pandemic measures. This study, therefore, aimed to examine the immediate and lagged impact of COVID-19 measures on population-based rates of seven perinatal outcomes: PTB, LBW, small for gestational age (SGA), NICU admissions, BFI, caesarean deliveries and stillbirth. In addition to investigating how different lengths of exposure to pandemic measures affected rates of perinatal outcomes, we examined impacts across different maternal income groups in the province of Manitoba.

Methods

Study design and period

This population-based open cohort study used data from 1 January 2008 to 31 March 2022. We examined the monthly rate of each outcome over two periods: (1) pre-COVID-19, from 1 January 2008 to 29 February 2020 and (2) during COVID-19, from 1 March 2020 to 31 March 2022. More than 10 years of data from 2008 to 2019 allowed us to account for the underlying trends in the perinatal outcomes of interest during the prepandemic period.

Data source

We used the Population Research Data Repository hosted at the Manitoba Centre for Health Policy (MCHP), a secure provincial health and administrative database containing de-identified individual health information for the entire province’s population eligible to receive health services. The reliability and validity of the MCHP Repository for epidemiological research have been documented in prior studies.32 33 Our study included multiple databases to capture demographic, clinical and socioeconomic information. The hospital discharge abstract database captured perinatal outcomes, age and comorbidities, with diagnoses coded using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) system, 10th Revision, with Canadian enhancements (ICD-10-CA). Medical claims and services database provided physician records to identify comorbidities, with diagnoses reported using ICD-9-CM codes. The Manitoba Health Insurance Registry provided information on age and area of residence, while the Drug Program Information Network (DPIN) was used to capture prescription drug dispensations. Statistics Canada census data provided information on household income.

Study population

An established mother–infant linked data cohort from Manitoba was used to create pregnancy cohorts pre and during the pandemic. We included all pregnant women giving birth, either live birth or stillbirth, in hospitals in Manitoba during the study period. The delivery date was defined as the date associated with procedure code of delivery as recorded in the hospital discharge abstract database.34 We excluded out-of-hospital deliveries, pregnancies ending in miscarriage (pregnancy loss prior to 20 weeks gestation), and ectopic pregnancies. The following variables were examined: maternal age at delivery, income quintile, parity (primipara or multipara), pre-existing comorbid diseases (validated definitions of diabetes and hypertension as well as maternal psychological distress), and area of residence (urban or rural). Maternal psychological distress in our population before and during the pandemic is defined as mood and anxiety disorders with a broad spectrum of conditions that range from anxiety state to anxiety disorders, phobic disorders, obsessive–compulsive disorders, depressive disorders, affective psychoses and neurotic depression. Maternal psychological distress was identified if diagnosed within the 8 months before birth using ICD Codes from hospital abstracts and medical claims databases and Prescription Drug Data (DPIN).

Within Manitoba, there are five regional health authorities (RHA) responsible for organising and delivering healthcare services within specific geographic regions to which individuals are assigned based on address of residence: Winnipeg (includes the city of Winnipeg and the town of Churchill), Interlake-Eastern (Interlake, North Eastman), Prairie Mountain (Assiniboine, Brandon, Parkland), Southern (Central, South Eastman), and Northern (Burntwood, NOR-MAN).35 Variations in healthcare services and access exist across Manitoba’s RHAs, which might contribute to disparities in maternal and neonatal care and an increased risk of adverse outcomes. As a proxy for maternal socioeconomic status, we used the classification based on the median neighbourhood income quintile: lower income (lowest: first and second median quintile), higher income (third to highest fifth median quintiles), and income unknown (individuals who cannot be assigned a neighbourhood income). Race and ethnicity are not available in our database, thus we could not examine differences across racial and ethnic groups.

Pandemic measures and exposure levels

Pregnancies exposed to pandemic measures between March 2020 and March 2022 were compared with those occurring in the decade prior to the pandemic. Manitoba’s first COVID-19 case was reported on March 12, 2020, and public health measures began to influence clinical practices by the end of that month.36 37 In March 2020, the provincial government implemented strict measures, including lockdown, physical distancing, limiting the numbers at gatherings, changes to healthcare services, non-urgent medical care restrictions and limiting supply of drug prescriptions, followed by a temporary relaxation of some restrictions between June and July 2020. Additional restrictions were reinstated in August 2020, and Manitoba reached its peak levels of restriction measures in November 2020 and May 2021.36 37

We examined the impact of the duration of exposure to COVID-19 public health measures on perinatal outcomes. To reflect this, delivery dates were categorised into groups based on the approximate fraction of time that each pregnancy was under COVID-19 measures. Categories include unexposed (birth between January 2008 and March 2020); 25% exposed (birth in April or May 2020); 50% exposed (birth between June and August 2020); 75% exposed (September to November 2020); and fully exposed (December 2020 to March 2022). However, there were differences between the first and second years of the fully exposed period; COVID-19 vaccination for pregnant women began in Manitoba in 2021, and in the second year, gathering and healthcare restrictions were eased as the province announced phase re-opening plans based on immunisation targets.36 37 Therefore, pregnancies occurring entirely during the pandemic were divided into 100% exposed year 1 (December 2020 to March 2021) and 100% exposed year 2 (April 2021 to March 2022).

Perinatal outcomes

We examined seven perinatal outcomes, including PTB, stillbirth, LBW, SGA, NICU admission, caesarean delivery and BFI. The definitions and corresponding codes used are summarised in online supplemental table 1.

Analyses

The demographic and clinical characteristics of pregnant women were described before and during the pandemic using percentages and their 95% CIs for categorical variables and means and SD for continuous variables. For descriptive analyses, pregnancies were classified as prepandemic or pandemic based on the date of delivery; therefore, some pregnancies categorised as occurring during the pandemic may have been partially exposed to pandemic measures. Monthly incidence rates of perinatal outcomes were estimated, and crude and adjusted relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs were calculated, adjusting for maternal age, parity, hypertension, diabetes, psychological distress, income and season. The denominator for PTB, LBW, SGA, NICU admissions and BFI outcomes included the total number of live births in Manitoba for each month. For caesarean delivery and stillbirth, the denominator included the total number of pregnancies, such as live births, stillbirths, multiple births and singletons, in each month.

The effect of the pandemic measures on the outcomes was examined using ITS analysis through generalised linear models, with March 2020 chosen as the intervention point for the pandemic measures. We used the monthly rates in our analyses when the number of reported cases in each month was ≥80 events, while bimonthly or quarterly rates were used otherwise. This recommended cut-off was chosen as a sufficient number of outcome events per time point to achieve desired efficiency for ITS models.38

We fit two models for each outcome using the full time series, defining the prepandemic period as January 2008 to February 2020 and the pandemic period as March 2020 to February 2022, with March 2020 specified as the interruption point. Model 1 (immediate and lagged changes) was used to estimate the abrupt change in the outcomes of interest following the onset of pandemic measures and the changes during the 2-year pandemic period. Model 1 included an indicator variable for the pandemic measures (COVID-19: 1/0), a continuous time variable representing monthly observations to account for underlying secular trends, a postintervention time variable for the COVID-19 period (24 monthly time points, April 2020=1 to March 2022=24), and season (fall/winter/spring/summer) to account for seasonality. Model 1 provided estimates for the abrupt effect using immediate change in level (βimmediate effect) from pre-COVID-19 to post-COVID-19.39 40 The change during the pandemic was estimated using the change in slope (βlagged effect) from preinterruption to postinterruption in the monthly outcomes rates while accounting for any secular trend in the prepandemic period.41 42 Model 2 (change by exposure duration) estimated changes in outcomes according to varying durations of exposure to pandemic measures, rather than postpandemic temporal trends. We specified model 2, informed by ITS principles (prepandemic and postpandemic structures), but not by formal ITS, for trend estimation. This approach assessed whether prenatal outcomes differed by cumulative exposure during pregnancy, estimating differences in average outcome rates for each exposure-duration category (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100% year 1, 100% year 2) relative to 0% exposure, rather than modelling postpandemic slope changes. Because exposure to pandemic measures accumulated gradually over gestation, no immediate or abrupt change in outcome level at the onset of the pandemic was assumed or modelled. Model 2 included the exposure variable and season to account for seasonality. The relative immediate percentage changes were derived from exponentiating the model estimate. Subgroup analyses were conducted by income groups (higher and lower) and used generalised linear models to assess potential effect modification by maternal income through multiplicative interaction terms between income and pandemic exposure. All models fit were evaluated using the deviance and Bayesian information criterion. The full model equations are provided in online supplemental file 1. Missing data for gestational age, birth weight and parity were minimal (<0.1%) and similarly distributed across prepandemic and pandemic periods. We conducted complete-case analyses because missingness was negligible and non-differential by different periods. We conducted the primary analyses (models 1 and 2) separately within each income stratum to investigate pandemic-related changes in outcome levels and trends.

Given the relatively low outcome rates within individual regions across the five RHAs in Manitoba, ITS analyses were not feasible at the regional level. Accordingly, RHA-specific analyses were conducted descriptively. For the RHA descriptive analyses only, we compared average outcome rates between a 2-year prepandemic period and a 2-year pandemic period to estimate relative changes. This approach was used for descriptive purposes and does not represent the full time series used in the ITS analyses for the main analyses.

A nominal α=0.05 was used to assess statistical significance. Analyses were conducted using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

Population characteristics

Table 1 shows the characteristics of pregnant women who gave birth during the study period from January 2008 to March 2022. We identified pregnant women who gave birth before (n=1 90 694) and during (n=31 942) the COVID-19 pandemic. Among those, there were 189 538 live births before the pandemic and 31 717 live births during the pandemic. The average age of women was 28.6 years (SD=5.8) before the pandemic and 29.9 years (SD=5.6) during the pandemic. Around half of the pregnant women lived in urban areas and were in the higher-income group in both periods. The prevalence of psychological distress increased from 13.1% before the pandemic to 20.0% during the pandemic.

Low birth weight

During the pandemic period, the percentage of live births with low weight increased compared with the prepandemic period, with a relative increase of 18% (adjusted RR=1.18, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.24, figure 2). A similar pattern was observed across all regions in Manitoba. During the pandemic period, the Northern region showed a relative increase of 16.1%, while Winnipeg showed a smaller relative increase of 5.8% (online supplemental table 2, figure 1).

The pandemic measures were associated with an immediate rise in LBW occurrence of 13.9% (βimmediate effect=0.13, p=0.05) among all live births, with an increase of 24.6% (βimmediate effect=0.22, p=0.01) in the lower-income group (table 2). Rates of LBW peaked for pregnancies with 50% pandemic exposure compared with prepandemic, with an increase of 29.7% (β50%exposed=0.26, p=0.01) among all pregnancies and 39.0% (β50%exposed=0.33, p=0.01) in the lower-income group. Although LBW rates for pregnancies that spanned the full term during the second year of the pandemic showed a reduction compared with those during the first year, the rates remained elevated and did not return to the prepandemic level (table 2, figure 1B, online supplemental figure 2).

Small for gestational age

During the pandemic period, the percentage of SGA infants among live births increased by 5% relative to prepandemic period levels (adjusted RR=1.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.09, figure 2). During the pandemic period, average SGA rates differed by region, with Winnipeg having the highest average SGA rate before and during the pandemic period (10.02% and 10.49%, respectively, online supplemental table 2, figure 1). Among all pregnancies, the pandemic was not associated with significant changes in SGA monthly rates (table 2). We found an increase in the incidence of SGA rate among the higher income group for pregnancies exposed 100% during the second year (β100%Y2=0.14, p<0.01) (table 2, figure 1C and online supplemental figure 2).

NICU admissions

NICU admissions among live births rose by 5% relative to prepandemic levels (adjusted RR:1.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.10, figure 2). However, across regions of Manitoba, the Prairie Mountain region showed an 11.5% relative increase, while the other regions showed reductions (online supplemental table 2, figure 1). NICU admission rates by region, income groups and exposure groups did not differ significantly from the prepandemic period. For pregnancies that were fully exposed during the second year of the pandemic period, NICU admission rates returned to prepandemic period levels among all pregnancies and different income groups (table 2, figure 1D, online supplemental 2).

Breastfeeding initiation

During the pandemic period, rates of BFI in hospital were similar with an adjusted RR of 0.97 (95% CI 0.96 to 0.98) (figure 2) and with the largest reduction in the Northern region (6.9%, online supplemental table 2, figure 1). Pandemic measures were associated with an immediate decline in BFI by 3.9% among all pregnancies (βimmediate effect=−0.04, p=0.01), followed by a minor increase in breastfeeding monthly rates during the 2 year pandemic period (βlagged effect<0.01, p=0.02, table 2). The ITS analyses of BFI rates by exposure duration showed no statistically significant changes (table 2, figure 3A, online supplemental 2).

Caesarean delivery

During the pandemic period, 28% of all pregnancies were caesarean deliveries compared with 23% prepandemic, representing a relative increase of 19% (adjusted RR=1.19, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.21) (figure 2). Caesarean delivery rates were notably higher in higher income groups compared with lower-income groups (figure 3B and online supplemental figure 2). The increase occurred across most regions in Manitoba during the pandemic, with the exception of Prairie Mountain (online supplemental table 2, figure 1). Following the onset of pandemic measures, an immediate increase of 22.1% in caesarean delivery rates was observed among all pregnancies (βimmediate effect=0.20, p<0.01), 20.9% (βimmediate effect=0.19, p<0.01) in the higher-income group, and 24.6% (βimmediate effect=0.22, p<0.01) in the lower-income group (table 2). The pandemic period was associated with a significant relative increase in monthly rates among those exposed to restrictions for 50% of their pregnancy, with a peak increase of 27.1% (β50%exposed=0.24, p<0.01) among all pregnancies. Pregnancies occurring entirely within the first or second year of the pandemic period experienced higher rates of caesarean deliveries by 24.6% (β100%Y1=0.22, p<0.01) and 20.9% (β100%Y2=0.19, p<0.01), respectively (table 2 and figure 3B).

Stillbirth

The average prepandemic period stillbirth rate among all pregnancies was 0.6% and rose to 0.7% during the pandemic period (figure 3C, online supplemental figure 2). The adjusted RR of stillbirth increased to 1.2 (95% CI 1.04 to 1.38) during the pandemic period (figure 2). Stratified by income, these results became non-significant but in a similar direction. We observed no change in the stillbirth quarterly rates following the onset of the pandemic period (p=0.1) or over the 2-year pandemic period (p=0.37, table 2). Given the relatively low rate of stillbirths in Manitoba, it was not feasible to conduct monthly rate analyses and ITS analyses by exposure duration (figure 3C).

Income effect modification

We assessed the interaction between COVID-19 pandemic and income on the overall incidence of perinatal outcomes. Our models suggested no evidence of effect modification for the overall PTB (p=0.11), LBW (p=0.08), caesarean delivery (p=0.44), NICU admission (p=0.41), SGA (p=0.95) or stillbirth (p=0.24). Evidence of effect modification was observed for BFI (p<0.01) (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

In this study of 222 636 pregnancies, we observed significant effects for the pandemic measures on perinatal outcomes, including PTB and LBW, with differences across income groups; however, there was no evidence of effect modification by income. The exposure duration under the pandemic measure was also a significant determinant for perinatal health, where increased risks of PTB, caesarean delivery and LBW were observed among pregnancies that spanned in its entirety during the pandemic. Our analyses suggest that the short and long term measures had minimal effect on BFI, NICU admissions and SGA rates during the study period.

Previous studies have not included the influence of duration of exposure to pandemic measures on prenatal care and the associated perinatal and delivery outcomes. A US study categorised pregnancies based on exposure to multimodal prenatal healthcare (combined in-person and virtual visits) between July 2018 and October 2021.43 Their findings showed a decrease in NICU admissions among the partially exposed group (change/4 week: −0.91%; 95% CI –1.77% to −0.03%), but an increased risk in fully exposed pregnancies (change/4 week: 1.75%; 95% CI 0.49% to 3.02%), with no significant changes in PTB, caesarean delivery or SGA rates.43 In contrast, our study found that NICU admission rates by exposure did not differ from the prepandemic period, whereas we found a significant increase in PTB and caesarean delivery rates among those exposed to 50% of their pregnancy during the pandemic. The COVID-19 pandemic has strained the healthcare system across the province. Factors such as staff shortages, the redeployment of nurses, the return of retired nurses to the workforce, and challenges in maintaining continuity of care while supporting the staff well-being could explain the observed changes in perinatal outcomes. The observed non-significant changes among pregnancies with 75% exposure could be attributed to the heterogeneity in the intensity of pandemic measures during this period, including phases of eased or partially lifted measures. This mixed exposure may have attenuated disruptions to healthcare access, social support, and maternal stress, resulting in no changes in perinatal outcomes compared with the prepandemic period. Previous studies have defined exposed cohort of pregnancies based on any period during the pandemic.192143 In our view, this carries the risk of inaccurate exposure assignments and potentially biased and conflicting results. For instance, women who gave birth shortly after the onset of pandemic measures likely had different pregnancy experiences compared with those who spent a longer time under the pandemic measures, experiencing altered prenatal care due to changes in healthcare access. When categorised as a dichotomous measure, this difference could account for the conflicting results seen across studies.

Several studies and meta-analyses of early-pandemic studies have reported reductions in PTB, particularly during the initial phase of the pandemic. A meta-analysis of 31 studies suggested pandemic measures were associated with an increase in stillbirths and a reduction in PTB (<37 weeks of gestation), particularly in high-income European countries.46 However, no significant differences were found in LBW, NICU admissions or caesarean delivery rates.46 A larger meta-analysis examined the first 9 months of the pandemic and found a reduction in PTB (<34 weeks) in countries with stringent implemented measures (OR=0.71, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.88), while no changes were observed for PTB (<37 weeks of gestation), NICU admissions, stillbirths or LBW.47 Studies covering 12 months or more during the pandemic found either no change or an increase in adverse perinatal outcomes compared with prepandemic periods.4853 However, these studies did not address the differential impact of the duration of pregnancy that was spent under pandemic measures. In contrast, our findings indicate an increase in PTB and caesarean delivery, particularly among pregnancies with greater exposure to pandemic measures. These discrepancies could be explained by pandemic exposure definition where most prior studies defined exposure dichotomously and did not account for the duration of exposure during pregnancy. In our study, incorporating exposure duration revealed a dose–response relationship, suggesting cumulative impacts of prolonged exposure. In addition, many studies were limited to the early months of the pandemic, whereas our analysis captured 24 months of the pandemic. The use of ITS accounted for prepandemic trends, which were not considered in the analyses of many previous studies.

Socioeconomic disparities, including fears of contracting COVID-19 at healthcare facilities, transportation restrictions, reduced social support, exacerbated financial insecurity, stress and mental health challenges, could have negatively impacted maternal health and contributed to higher rates of PTB and stillbirths.54 In addition, more strict pandemic measures, such as travel restrictions and 14-day isolation requirements, in northern and remote communities may have disproportionately affected pregnant women by limiting their access to timely prenatal care in Manitoba. The shift to virtual prenatal care during the pandemic may have added further challenges for these communities. However, a recent study in the Canadian province of Ontario that examined the first 21 months of the pandemic found a 15% reduction in PTB (adjusted OR=0.85, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.87) compared with the prepandemic period.48 Differences in findings between the Ontario study and the current study may be due to differences in the pandemic measures implemented across provinces or differences in the analytical methods applied. Specifically, the Ontario study did not account for the prepandemic trend nor the exposure duration to pandemic measures, which may explain their reported reduction in PTB.

In our study, caesarean delivery rates were increasing in the prepandemic period, consistent with national trends showing a rise in caesarean deliveries in Canada from 17.6% in 1995 to 26.9% by 2010. This upward prepandemic trend was likely driven by changes in maternal and obstetric characteristics, such as increasing maternal age and higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, obesity and multiple gestation, as well as evolving clinical practice and preferences including improved surgical techniques and greater acceptance of caesarean delivery among both clinicians and patients.55 In our ITS models, we observed an increase in caesarean delivery rates associated with the introduction of pandemic measures, while accounting for the prepandemic secular trend. Several previous studies noted higher rates of caesarean deliveries during the pandemic period compared with the prepandemic period, which is consistent with the findings of the current study.48 56 57 A cohort study conducted in the Canadian province of Ontario reported increased odds of caesarean deliveries during the pandemic period compared with the prepandemic period (adjusted OR of 1.09, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.11).48 The observed increase in rates may reflect the efforts of obstetricians to provide optimal care and ‘best services’ adapting to changes in guidelines aimed at better neonatal outcomes during the pandemic.57 58 Additionally, the rise in caesarean deliveries may have been driven by concerns over adverse events such as respiratory complications, placental infections, potential vertical transmission and neonatal death associated with vaginal delivery.59 The increase might also be attributed to the rise in maternal requests for caesarean deliveries, which might have been influenced by anxiety over restrictive hospital policies and limited support networks during the pandemic.54 58 These factors may also explain the increased rates associated with longer exposure durations.

Our study found no changes in NICU admissions and SGA rates, consistent with evidence from Denmark, Botswana and other Canadian studies.182160 Other studies have shown either an increase61 or a decrease in NICU admissions48 62 during the COVID-19 pandemic. The current study found that the BFI rate was largely unchanged over time, which may be attributed to the short follow-up period as we only captured the initiation recorded after delivery in hospitals and not after discharge. The slight initial decline during the early pandemic, especially among lower income and rural populations, highlights the need for targeted support to address these disparities. Varying access levels to breastfeeding support, reduced face-to-face professional support or differences in maternal preferences across different regions during the pandemic could have led to minor changes in breastfeeding rates in Manitoba.6366 Studies from Spain and the UK reported increased breastfeeding rates at discharge for term infants, while others from Italy and Canada observed decline or no change in breastfeeding rates during the pandemic.626770 A UK survey conducted during early pandemic, between May and June 2020, found two contrasting experiences: around 41% of mothers felt that breastfeeding was supported, while 27% struggled with support and subsequently stopped breastfeeding.71

The current study has several strengths. First, our province-wide database captures all births in the province of Manitoba over 2 years during the pandemic and 12 years prior. Second, the use of ITS analysis by exposure level provided the temporal detail to distinguish the immediate effect of the pandemic from its cumulative long-term effects. Third, we examined the pandemic differential effect across income groups and different regions within Manitoba. Limitations of this study should be acknowledged. A causal interpretation of the impact of pandemic measures cannot be inferred, as pregnant women were also exposed to the novel coronavirus and restrictions to healthcare access; thus, the results should be interpreted cautiously. While BFI was defined as any exclusive or partial breastfeeding within the first 3 days postpartum, we did not capture BFI after hospital discharge. Although this is unlikely to significantly affect the reported initiation rates, assessing the continuation of breastfeeding after discharge would be important for understanding longer-term breastfeeding patterns. Administrative data does not include variables such as race and ethnicity, limiting our ability to fully investigate disparities and inequities in perinatal outcomes. In addition, we did not examine the effect of COVID-19 infection on perinatal outcomes, though the small number of COVID-19-positive pregnancies (n=225) in our cohort during the first wave makes it unlikely that this would strongly influence the results of our immediate effect models. Despite the increase in stillbirth rates with more prolonged exposure, rates were relatively low, and our ITS analysis was therefore underpowered. Additionally, while we did not investigate variations in prenatal care, the changes to maternal healthcare were implemented consistently across the provincial healthcare system, reducing differential variability. However, rural regions and lower-income areas may have experienced a disproportionate impact compared with urban centres. Our study did not capture out-of-hospital births, including those attended by midwives or at nursing stations, as these are not included in medical services or hospital abstract databases. We therefore cannot determine whether out-of-hospital birth rates changed during the COVID-19 pandemic. During the pandemic, national data from Statistics Canada indicated a slight decline in hospital births, with a corresponding increase in out-of-hospital deliveries, rising from approximately 1.7%–1.8% of all births prepandemic to about 2.0–2.6% during the pandemic period.72 Given that planned home and birth centre deliveries are more common among lower-risk pregnancies, their exclusion from our hospital-based cohort may have resulted in a relatively higher-risk population during the pandemic, potentially biasing the estimates leading to an overestimation of pandemic effect on adverse outcomes. In Manitoba, fewer than 1% of births occur at home, suggesting that any shift to out-of-hospital settings is unlikely to fully explain the observed changes in perinatal outcomes. Moreover, we could not account for vaccination rates among pregnant women. We also did not examine other maternal outcomes such as morbidity, death and hospital admissions. We could not distinguish between spontaneous and medically indicated PTBs, nor between planned and emergency caesarean sections, which may reflect different underlying mechanisms. Clinically, spontaneous PTB is more often linked to infection, inflammation, psychosocial stress and chronic maternal or placental disease, whereas medically indicated PTB and planned caesarean usually is a consequence of complications such as preeclampsia, fetal growth restriction or suspected fetal compromise. As a result, we cannot determine whether the observed changes are driven primarily by altered obstetric practice or by changes in spontaneous onset of labour, and our findings should be interpreted as overall population level changes rather than specific causal pathways. Finally, this study was restricted to pregnancies ending in live birth or stillbirth and did not include miscarriages. Since our primary objective was to examine perinatal outcomes defined among births (PTB, LBW, SGA, NICU admission, BFI, caesarean delivery and stillbirth), the findings should be interpreted as capturing the impact of pandemic measures on outcomes among births rather than on all pregnancies.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that changes around the COVID-19 pandemic, including both mitigation measures, policy changes and direction effect of the pandemic, were associated with increased rates of PTBs and caesarean deliveries, particularly among pregnancies in which the full term occurred during the pandemic. These observations can be attributed to changes in access and adequacy of care, greater socioeconomic vulnerability among certain groups or an unknown mechanism. While the pandemic measures were not associated with significant effects on stillbirths, the rates increased with greater pandemic exposure, followed by a drop to prepandemic levels in the second year. Over the 2-year pandemic period, we observed no change in NICU admissions and BFI. Further investigation into regional and socioeconomic disparities is warranted to better understand the underlying mechanisms and to guide evidence-based maternal and neonatal healthcare policies for vulnerable populations in future public health crises.
