Sleep quality among pregnant women: a prospective longitudinal study of trajectories and bidirectional associations with physical activity
Authors: Yupeng Wang, Yi Li, Rong Zhang, Minna Mao, Weiqian Zheng, Rulan Qiu, Jingning Xu, Suwen Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the trajectory patterns and influencing factors of sleep quality and verify the bidirectional association between physical activity and sleep quality during pregnancy.DesignThis was a prospective longitudinal study.SettingThe study was conducted at the obstetric clinic in a tertiary maternity hospital in Zhejiang Province, mainland China.ParticipantsA total of 645 pregnant women were selected through simple random sampling and completed follow-up assessments during the first, second and third trimesters of pregnancy from January to December 2024.Outcome measuresThe desired data were collected using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form. Latent class growth analysis was applied to identify sleep quality trajectories, multiple logistic regression was used to determine the influencing factors, and cross-lagged panel analysis was used to explore the bidirectional association between sleep quality and physical activity.ResultsThree distinct sleep quality trajectories were identified: the constantly poor sleep quality group (21.40%), the constantly decreasing sleep quality group (50.70%) and the constantly good sleep quality group (27.90%). Multiple logistic regression analyses indicated that being underweight (OR=3.013, 95% CI: 1.308 to 6.942) and having insufficient physical activity levels during the first (OR=3.346, 95% CI: 2.009 to 5.573) and second phases (OR=37.515, 95% CI: 20.215 to 69.620) were associated with an increased risk of exhibiting a consistently declining pattern. Being underweight (OR=2.679, 95% CI: 1.041 to 6.893), passive smoking (OR=11.433, 95% CI: 2.220 to 58.883) and having insufficient physical activity levels during the first (OR=12.624, 95% CI: 6.285 to 25.356) and second phases (OR=23.773, 95% CI: 11.745 to 48.118) were associated with an increased risk of exhibiting a consistently poor pattern. Additionally, results from the cross-lagged panel analysis revealed a bidirectional association between sleep quality and physical activity.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates heterogeneity in changes in sleep quality among pregnant women. Interventions aimed at promoting maternal and infant health during pregnancy should consider both sleep quality and physical activity. Earlier interventions are associated with better outcomes.Trial registration numberChiCTR2300078952.Keywords: Pregnancy, Sleep Medicine, Health, Nursing Care, Pregnant Women

Introduction

Improving maternal and child health is both the foundation and prerequisite for human survival, sustainable development and a critical global social issue.1 The Chinese government has consistently prioritised maternal and child health management. Following the implementation of the three-child birth policy, maternal and child healthcare has become a key component of health promotion in China.2 Among these, healthy lifestyle behaviours are especially important as they can significantly enhance both the physiological and psychological well-being of pregnant women, improve their sense of control over health and reduce the incidence of adverse pregnancy outcomes.3 In studies on the health-promoting lifestyles of pregnant women, sleep quality and physical activity are two critical factors to be considered.4

Sleep has a direct impact on the health of pregnant women.5 Pregnancy is a period of significant physiological, psychological and social change, which can easily affect sleep quality. Pregnancy-related sleep disorders are characterised by a decrease in sleep quality during pregnancy, primarily manifested as nocturnal insomnia, increased awakenings and daytime drowsiness.6 A meta-analysis of 24 studies involving 11 002 pregnant women found that 45.7% experienced poor sleep quality.7 Poor sleep quality can increase the prevalence of gestational diabetes, hypertension, prenatal depression and the risk of caesarean section, preterm birth and stillbirth.8 It is important to note that human behaviour is a dynamic process. As gestational weeks progress, the sleep quality of pregnant women changes, primarily due to increased visceral compression, abdominal enlargement and hormonal fluctuations, which contribute to emotional distress.9 As research progresses, scholars have observed considerable heterogeneity in the trends of sleep quality changes among perinatal women.10 Latent class growth analysis (LCGA) can be used to explore the trajectories of group characteristics over time. Its advantage lies in simultaneously considering developmental trends, heterogeneity and sources of variation across different classes, thereby providing a more accurate depiction of changes in individual characteristics over time.11 Tomfohr’s study followed 293 women from the second trimester of pregnancy to 6 months postpartum and identified four trajectories using LCGA: consistently high sleep quality, mild decline in sleep quality, significant decline in sleep quality and persistently poor sleep quality.12 Sedov identified trajectories of pregnancy insomnia symptoms through a follow-up study from early pregnancy to early postpartum involving 142 women, revealing three trajectories: no insomnia, subclinical insomnia and clinical insomnia.13 Due to variations in sample sizes, follow-up durations and the sleep issues investigated, the identified trajectories varied. However, current studies in China on sleep quality among pregnant women are predominantly cross-sectional in design, failing to capture longitudinal relationships and developmental trends over time. Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to complement and expand on the conclusions of previous research.

Physical activity is associated with health benefits for individuals of all ages, particularly pregnant women, and has a positive impact on preventing pregnancy complications and enhancing quality of life.14 The WHO guidelines recommend 150 min of moderate physical activity per week for all pregnant women without medical contraindications.15 However, physical activity in women declines from prepregnancy to pregnancy.16 The promotion of physical activity during pregnancy has been a significant focus of scholarly attention. Sleep quality and physical activity, as key elements of healthy lifestyle behaviours, are likely to be particularly correlated.17 First, physical activity influences sleep quality.18 From a physiological perspective, regular physical activity helps to increase total sleep time by improving insulin sensitivity, altering concentrations of inflammatory markers, regulating circadian rhythms and promoting the release of brain-derived neurotrophic factors.19 Beyond that, a cohort study demonstrated that a high physical activity level was beneficial to improve the sleep quality of pregnant women in the third trimester.20 Second, sleep quality also affects physical activity.21 One study involving the elderly indicated that individuals with excessive daytime sleepiness tend to participate less in exercise.22 Another study involving pregnant women showed that sleep disorders lead to reduced physical activity, which in turn results in declines in physical performance.23 The studies mentioned above, which examine sleep quality as a predictor of physical activity, employ cross-sectional designs and, therefore, cannot establish causality. Currently, cross-lagged panel analysis is commonly used to investigate the bidirectional nature of the relationship between two variables.

Based on the preceding discussion and literature, we propose the following research hypothesis: H1: There is substantial heterogeneity in sleep quality trajectories during pregnancy; H2: There is a bidirectional association between sleep quality and physical activity. Hence, the objectives of this study were to investigate the trajectory patterns and influencing factors of sleep quality and to verify the bidirectional association between sleep quality and physical activity in pregnant women using a longitudinal survey, which provides effective insights into practical intervention strategies.

Methods

Design and participants

This prospective longitudinal study was conducted in the outpatient department of a grade-A tertiary obstetrics and gynaecology hospital in Zhejiang Province from January to December 2024, with a total of 645 participants. Pregnant women were recruited during their first antenatal care visit in early pregnancy, with follow-up assessments conducted at mid-pregnancy and late pregnancy. The inclusion criteria for the study population were as follows: (1) age ≥20 years; (2) natural conception; (3) singleton pregnancy; (4) 10–13+6 weeks of gestation; (5) planning to undergo prenatal examinations until delivery at this hospital; (6) no communication barriers and (7) no adverse lifestyle habits, such as smoking or drinking. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) restriction of regular activities due to mental or physical limitations; (2) heart, liver or blood system disorders during pregnancy and (3) women required to work night shifts or experience jet lag during pregnancy. Elimination criteria included: (1) limited physical activity or pregnancy termination due to obstetric contraindications as the pregnancy progressed,24 such as premature rupture of membranes, cervical insufficiency, unexplained persistent vaginal bleeding, placenta previa, pre-eclampsia, preterm labour and threatened abortion; (2) taking drugs or medications that could affect sleep; (3) loss to follow-up or voluntary withdrawal; incompletely filled questionnaires and (4) experiencing major life events during pregnancy, such as the sudden death of a friend or family member or an emotional breakup between the couple.

Sample size determination

The latent class growth model (LCGM) was the primary analysis method used in this study. The target sample size was set to be at least 200, based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) as the model fit indicator.25 Additionally, the sample size for the structural equation model should range from 200 to 500.26 Considering a 20% lost-to-follow-up rate, the required minimum sample size was 240. Therefore, this study ultimately included 645 pregnant women, all of whom completed the follow-up assessments, meeting the sample size requirement. Notably, high participant engagement and institutional support allowed us to exceed the originally expected sample size.

Measures

Demographic and obstetric characteristics

Demographic information was collected from the participants, including age, prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), education, occupation, residency, household monthly income (RMB), marital status, family structure of pregnant women (one-child family or not), prepregnancy exercise habits, exposure to passive smoking and primary caregivers. Obstetric data were obtained from the medical records of the pregnant women, including whether the pregnancy was planned or unplanned, history of adverse pregnancy or childbirth outcomes, childbirth experience and pregnancy complications (eg, gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, hypothyroidism, etc).

Sleep quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), developed by Buysse et al, was used to assess the sleep quality of pregnant women during the early, mid and late stages of pregnancy. 27The Chinese version of the PSQI, comprising 18 items, was adapted cross-culturally by Liu28 and structured into seven dimensions, with a reported Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.73, indicating acceptable internal consistency. These dimensions include subjective sleep quality, sleep duration, sleep disturbance, sleep latency, habitual sleep efficiency, use of sleep medication and dysfunction. Each dimension is scored on a scale from 0 to 3, with the total score ranging from 0 to 21. Higher scores indicate poorer sleep quality. Clinically significant poor sleep quality is defined as a total score greater than 5.27 Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.71.

Physical activity

The level of physical activity in pregnant women during the three gestational periods was assessed using the Chinese version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Form (IPAQ-SF), which is recommended as a cost-effective tool for assessing physical activity and has an intra-examiner correlation coefficient of 0.89.29 The IPAQ-SF comprises seven questions covering four types of activities: sedentary activity, walking, moderate-intensity physical activity and vigorous-intensity physical activity. MET-minutes per week were calculated using the standard IPAQ scoring protocol: 3.3 METs for walking, 4.0 METs for moderate activity and 8.0 METs for vigorous activity. Total physical activity was derived by summing the MET-min/week for each domain. Participants were then categorised into low, moderate or high physical activity.

In this study, moderate or high levels of physical activity were defined as ‘sufficient physical activity’, while low levels were defined as ‘insufficient physical activity’ in accordance with the WHO physical activity guidelines.15 30 Cronbach’s α in this study was 0.82.

Procedure

Standardised training was provided to investigators prior to the investigation, covering the study design, follow-up protocols and communication skills. Participant recruitment occurred from January to April 2024, with follow-up continuing through December. Three investigators explained the purpose, significance, follow-up procedures, methods of questionnaire completion and confidentiality principles to pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria during their first obstetrical visit (T1: 10–13+6 weeks of gestation). Consenting pregnant women were provided with paper-based questionnaires. All questionnaires were completed and collected onsite and were checked for missing, omitted or incorrect items. The investigators then conducted two follow-up visits, during which participants were provided with the next follow-up appointment, including those for the second (T2: 14–27+6 weeks of gestation) and third (T3: ≥28 weeks of gestation) trimesters. Each visit was spaced at least 8 weeks apart. Sleep quality and physical activity were assessed at each visit, while demographic and obstetric data were collected during the final visit.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS V.26 and Mplus V.8.3. All tests were two-tailed, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed on the full available data set, and no imputation of missing data occurred.

Descriptive statistics

Continuous variables were expressed as means±SD, and comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons were performed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between sleep quality and physical activity.

Latent class growth analysis

The number of trajectories of sleep quality during pregnancy was determined using LCGM analysis conducted with Mplus V.8.3 software. The analysis began with one latent class, progressively increasing the number of classes. Model fit indicators, including Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), BIC and sample size-adjusted BIC (aBIC), were compared, with smaller values indicating a better fit.31 To ensure clinical meaningfulness, each class was asked to include at least 5% of the participants. The best-fitting model was determined based on both practical significance and statistical criteria. Significant p-values for the likelihood ratio test (LMR) and bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) for the k-class model indicated that the k-class model was superior to the (k−1)-class model. Additionally, entropy, which reflects classification accuracy, ranges from 0 to 1, with values above 0.8 indicating 90% classification accuracy.32 Higher entropy values indicate higher classification accuracy. Subsequently, unordered multiclass logistic regression was used to assess the influencing factors of sleep quality trajectory patterns.

Cross-lagged panel model analysis

Finally, a cross-lagged panel analysis was performed using Mplus V.8.3 software to examine the causal relationship between sleep quality and physical activity after adjusting for education, occupation, household monthly income and prepregnancy BMI33 34 Based on the aforementioned hypothesis, a reciprocal-causation model incorporating both autoregressive and cross-lagged paths was developed. Weighted least squares mean and variance-adjusted estimation was used, treating physical activity as a categorical variable (1=sufficient physical activity, 0=insufficient physical activity). An acceptable model fit was assessed using the chi-square test of model fit/df (χ²/df) < 5, comparative fit index (CFI)>0.90, Tucker-Lewis index (TLI)>0.90, root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA)<0.08 and standardised root mean square residual (SRMR)<0.05.35

Results

Descriptive statistics of the study sample

A total of 813 pregnant women in the first trimester (mean gestational week 10.36, SD 0.98) were enrolled in this study; 724 completed follow-up in the second trimester (mean gestational week 23.21, SD 3.13), and 645 completed it in the third trimester (mean gestational week 31.23, SD 2.76), yielding a dropout rate of 20.66% (figure 1). The age range of the pregnant women was 23–45 years old. Other demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in online supplemental table S1. When PSQI scores were analysed as categorical variables, approximately 27.91%, 34.26% and 77.67% of participants met the criteria for clinically significant poor sleep quality in the first, second and third trimesters of pregnancy (χ2=380.290, p<0.001), defined as a PSQI score greater than 5. When PSQI scores were considered as continuous variables, the mean scores at the three time points were 4.07±2.36, 4.66±2.37 and 8.16±3.64 (F=386.170, p<0.001), respectively. LSD post-hoc test results also showed significant pairwise differences (all p<0.001). According to the IPAQ-SF, the proportion of pregnant women engaging in sufficient physical activity increased from 38.14% to 44.65% from early to mid-pregnancy. However, by late pregnancy, this proportion decreased to 21.86% (χ2=78.260, p<0.001). Furthermore, the results of the correlation analysis for all variables at each time point are presented in online supplemental table S2.

Flow chart of pregnant women included in the study.

Latent class growth model of sleep quality trajectories

The PSQI scores during the first, second and third trimesters were used as observation indicators, and 645 eligible participants were included in the model analysis. LCGM was applied, with time treated as a freely estimated parameter, to extract one to five latent classes sequentially. When increasing from one to two classes, AIC, BIC and aBIC all decreased, both the LMR and BLRT were significant (p<0.05), but entropy did not reach its highest value. When increasing from two to three classes, AIC, BIC and aBIC continued to decrease, and the LMR and BLRT remained significant (p<0.05), and entropy increased to 0.821. When increasing from three to four or five classes, the proportions of classifications were unreasonable, and the LMR was not significant (p>0.05), indicating no further improvement in model fit beyond three classes. Therefore, three distinct latent classes were identified (online supplemental table S3). Latent class 1 comprised 138 participants (21.40%), latent class 2 comprised 327 participants (50.70%) and class 3 comprised 180 participants (27.90%). The three sleep quality trajectories derived from the model are shown in figure 2.

Developmental trajectories of sleep quality (PSQI scores). PSQI, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index.

The latent classes were named based on the characteristics and trends of sleep quality changes during pregnancy. Based on the sleep disturbance thresholds recommended by Buysse, the PSQI scores for pregnant women in class 1 were greater than 5 at all three time points, and the average slope (β) for class 1 was 0.877 (SE=0.139, t=6.289, p<0.001), indicating that the sleep quality was constantly poor and worsened throughout pregnancy in class 1. Therefore, class 1 was named the constantly poor sleep quality group.

In class 2, pregnant women had low PSQI scores in early pregnancy, with scores increasing during mid and late pregnancy. The average slope (β) for class 2 was 2.099 (SE=0.147, t=14.271, p<0.001), indicating a significant upward trend in PSQI scores over time. This suggests that sleep quality deteriorated as pregnancy progressed, justifying the designation of class 2 as the ‘constantly decreasing sleep quality’ group.

In class 3, the PSQI scores for pregnant women in class 3 were lower than 5 at all three time points, and the average slope (β) for class 3 was 1.186 (SE=0.106, t=11.184, p<0.001), indicating that pregnant women in class 3 did not reach the established sleep disturbance cut-off scores throughout pregnancy, so they were named the constantly good sleep quality group.

Logistic regression analysis of influencing factors of sleep quality trajectory categories

Descriptive characteristics of pregnant women were compared across different trajectory categories of sleep quality. As shown in online supplemental table S1, significant differences were observed in prepregnancy BMI (p=0.030), exposure to passive smoking (p<0.001) and physical activity levels during the first (p<0.001), second (p<0.001) and third (p<0.001) trimesters.

To explore factors associated with different sleep quality trajectories, we conducted an unordered multinomial logistic regression. The dependent variable was the sleep quality trajectory group. The primary exposure variables were physical activity levels during the first, second and third trimesters (sufficient=1, insufficient=0). Covariates included prepregnancy BMI (underweight=1, overweight/obese=2, normal weight=3) and exposure to passive smoking during pregnancy (yes=0, no=1).

Compared with pregnant women in the consistently good sleep quality group, those who were underweight before pregnancy (OR=3.013) and those with insufficient physical activity during the first (OR=3.346) and second (OR=37.515) trimesters were more likely to be in the constantly decreasing sleep quality group. Similarly, being underweight (OR=2.679), exposure to passive smoking (OR=11.433) and insufficient physical activity in the first (OR=12.624) and second (OR=23.773) trimesters were associated with higher odds of being in the constantly poor sleep quality group (online supplemental table S4).

Autoregressive cross-lagged standardised path model of sleep quality and physical activity

As presented in online supplemental table S5, the model demonstrated an acceptable fit, with the following indices: χ²/df=3.543, CFI=0.976, TLI=0.936, RMSEA=0.063 (95% CI: 0.043 to 0.084) and SRMR=0.034. As shown in figure 3, the standardised model indicated that the PSQI score at T1 significantly and negatively predicted physical activity at T2 (β =–0.355, p<0.001), and the PSQI score at T2 significantly and negatively predicted physical activity at T3 (β =–0.209, p<0.001). Additionally, physical activity at T1 significantly and negatively predicted the PSQI score at T2 (β =–0.073, p<0.05), and physical activity at T2 significantly and negatively predicted the PSQI score at T3 (β =–0.591, p<0.001). These findings suggest a significant bidirectional negative relationship between PSQI scores and physical activity over time.

Autoregressive cross-lagged standardised path model of sleep quality and physical activity. PSQI, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index.

Discussion

Our longitudinal study found that sleep quality progressively worsened as pregnancy advanced, with the incidence of poor sleep quality reaching 27.91%, 34.26% and 77.67% in the first, second and third trimesters, respectively. These findings are consistent with previous reports.36

We also found that the proportion of pregnant women engaging in sufficient physical activity was 38.14%, 44.65% and 21.86% in the first, second and third trimesters, respectively. A significant decline was observed in the third trimester, consistent with findings from the study by Zhang et al.37

In this study, we identified three distinct latent classes with different trajectories of sleep quality: the constantly poor sleep quality group (class 1), the constantly decreasing sleep quality group (class 2) and the constantly good sleep quality group (class 3), indicating a population-specific pattern of sleep quality changes in pregnancy. Class 1, comprising 21.40% of participants, showed persistent poor sleep quality throughout pregnancy, aligning with the findings of Sedov et al,13 which indicate that poor sleep quality can persist at all stages of pregnancy. This may be attributed to pregnant women in this group experiencing more intense pregnancy-related symptoms at each stage, leading to increased somatic complaints, more severe negative sleep-related cognitions and greater disruptions in sleep hygiene behaviours. These factors collectively contribute to sustained sleep disturbances. Given the persistence of poor sleep in this group, early identification and targeted intervention are warranted to reduce potential risks such as perinatal depression or pregnancy complications. Class 2, which accounted for 50.70% of the participants, demonstrated good sleep quality in early pregnancy that progressively deteriorated as pregnancy advanced, reaching poor sleep quality in the late stages. As pregnancy progresses into the second and third trimesters, hormonal changes—such as increased oestrogen and progesterone levels—lead to elevated respiratory secretions, an expansion of the chest’s transverse diameter, and frequent nocturia due to uterine pressure on the bladder.12 38 Gastro-oesophageal reflux also contributes to the decline in sleep quality during this period.39 Importantly, class 2 represented the majority of pregnant women in the sample, and the clinical significance of this group should not be overlooked. Although these women began pregnancy with relatively good sleep, the steady decline across trimesters may signal a risk trajectory for negative outcomes. Class 3, comprising 27.9% of participants, maintained consistently good sleep quality throughout pregnancy. According to Plancoulaine et al10 women in this group are more likely to benefit from strong family support, higher self-efficacy and healthier lifestyle habits. These protective factors may buffer against common pregnancy-related sleep disturbances, suggesting that strengthening psychosocial resources could be a valuable strategy in prenatal care. However, the sleep quality trajectory identified in Liu’s study showed a slight improvement from early to mid-pregnancy, followed by a deterioration in late pregnancy.40 The discrepancies in findings between our study and Liu’s may be attributed to variations in factors such as maternal education levels, economic conditions and the criteria used to assess poor sleep quality across different regions.41

The type of sleep quality trajectory has been found to be related to prepregnancy BMI, with pregnant women with prepregnancy underweight more likely to be in the constantly decreasing or poor sleep quality group. Throughout the first, second and third trimesters of pregnancy, the body composition of pregnant women with prepregnancy underweight was consistently lower in comparison to those with prepregnancy normal weight, such as muscle mass, carbohydrates, protein and bone mineral, which are essential for maintaining high sleep quality, were key nutrients.42 As pregnancy advanced, the body’s demand for these nutrients progressively increased. Pregnant women with prepregnancy underweight during pregnancy fail to meet these nutritional requirements, leading to a gradual decline in sleep quality. The results of this study showed that pregnant women exposed to passive smoking were more likely to be categorised into the constantly poor sleep quality group than into the constantly good sleep quality group, which aligns with the findings of Liu.40 The stimulation of nicotine in cigarettes leads to the release of sleep-regulating neurotransmitters, including dopamine and serotonin. It also stimulates nicotine–acetylcholine receptors in the brain, causing disruptions in the central nervous system functions that regulate sleep cycles, thereby triggering sleep disorders.43 The results of this study also showed that insufficient physical activity in the first and second trimesters was the risk factor for development into constantly decreasing or poor sleep quality groups. Lower limb muscle strength and balance are closely related to the quality of sleep.44 Adequate physical activity is conducive to the improvement of cardiorespiratory function, muscle strength and limb flexibility of pregnant women.33 Therefore, lack of physical activity tends to affect the quality of sleep during pregnancy.

As shown in the autoregressive cross-lagged path model, the PSQI score negatively predicted the level of physical activity (βT1–T2 = –0.355, p<0.001; βT2–T3 = –0.209, p<0.001). Conversely, the level of physical activity also negatively predicted the PSQI score (βT1–T2 = –0.073, p<0.05; βT2–T3 = –0.591, p<0.001). While these findings support the hypothesised bidirectional relationship, it is important to note that the effect size from physical activity at T1 to sleep quality at T2 was modest. Such small coefficients are not uncommon in longitudinal behavioural research, especially when investigating complex constructs influenced by multifactorial physiological and psychosocial factors. Prior studies have similarly reported small but consistent bidirectional effects between sleep and activity among human populations.20 45

Previous research on the causal relationship between sleep quality and physical activity has yielded inconsistent findings. For example, a longitudinal study conducted in the USA reported that better baseline sleep quality predicted increased physical activity over time, whereas baseline physical activity did not predict later sleep outcomes.46 In contrast, a study from the UK found a bidirectional relationship, showing that improvements in sleep patterns contributed to increased physical activity levels, while declines in physical activity negatively affected subsequent sleep.47 Other studies, however, have reported no significant associations between the two variables.48 49 These inconsistencies may reflect the complex, multifactorial mechanisms underlying the sleep–activity relationship, which likely involve a range of physiological and psychological processes—such as circadian rhythm regulation, metabolic and immune function, thermoregulation, vascular health, mood and endocrine responses.19 In this context, the present study adds to the literature by providing longitudinal evidence for a bidirectional association between sleep quality and physical activity in a pregnant population, a group with unique physiological and psychosocial characteristics that may modulate this dynamic relationship.

This longitudinal and prospective study, with its large sample size, well-designed follow-up procedures and comprehensive data analysis, provides reliable conclusions that contribute to the theoretical understanding of this relationship and offer clinical guidance. Therefore, healthcare practitioners should emphasise the importance of healthy sleep and appropriate physical activity for overall well-being in pregnant women. Keeping a normal prepregnancy BMI and establishing a smoke-free environment for pregnant women are essential. Additionally, behavioural interventions targeting pregnant women should be developed to promote regular sleep patterns and encourage moderate physical activity throughout the day, which may further enhance sleep quality. It is important to note that the levels of physical activity in the first trimester and second trimester would influence the sleep quality trajectory during pregnancy, so the earlier an intervention promoting physical activity is delivered, the better the sleep quality. Today, physical activity is well recognised to have positive health benefits, such as enhancing muscle strength, promoting natural childbirth, minimising complications of pregnancy and alleviating emotional distress, except for improving sleep hygiene.

This study has several limitations. One limitation is that both sleep quality and physical activity were assessed through self-reported questionnaires, which are subject to reporting bias. Future research could consider utilising wearable devices to quantitatively monitor physiological data, thereby providing more objective and accurate measurements. Another limitation of the present study is the attrition rate of 20.66%. Although most cases of attrition appeared to be due to health-related reasons, the potential for attrition-related bias cannot be entirely ruled out and should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. The third limitation is the absence of data on potential psychological confounders such as depression and anxiety, which may influence the observed associations. Future studies should consider incorporating these variables to enhance the robustness and interpretability of the findings. Additionally, the generalisability of our findings may be limited, as the sample was drawn from a single tertiary hospital in China, which may overrepresent women with relatively high access to healthcare and health literacy. Future research should adopt multicentre designs across different regions and healthcare settings, including rural and low-resource environments, to examine whether similar associations hold in more socioeconomically and culturally diverse populations. Finally, the cross-lagged panel analysis indicated a bidirectional association between sleep quality and physical activity, although these findings should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal due to the observational nature of the data.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study enhances our understanding of sleep quality and physical activity among pregnant women. Both sleep quality and physical activity exhibited significant changes over time. The LCGA identified three distinct sleep quality trajectories: the constantly poor sleep quality group, the constantly decreasing sleep quality group and the constantly good sleep quality group, and the levels of physical activity in the first trimester and second trimester would influence the sleep quality trajectory. Additionally, the cross-lagged panel analysis indicated temporal associations in both directions between sleep quality and physical activity. These findings suggest that any interventions aimed at improving the lifestyles of pregnant women should address both sleep and physical activity, as doing so may positively influence both maternal and infant health. The earlier the intervention stage, the better the results.
