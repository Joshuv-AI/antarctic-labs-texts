Large Pheochromocytoma Presenting as Refractory Cardiogenic Shock and Multiorgan Failure: A Case Report
Authors: Pui Man Chung, King Shing Yung, Dominic So, Stephen Ka Hon Wong, Lik Fai Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report a case of a large pheochromocytoma in a middle-aged woman with a good past health record. She presented with pheochromocytoma crisis, complaining of acute shortness of breath, and quickly deteriorated into refractory cardiogenic shock with multiorgan failure. Multi-axial CT showed a large mass in the left suprarenal region. Elevated serum catecholamines confirmed the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma. Left distal transradial adrenal artery embolization under local anesthesia was performed because of limited femoral access and very high perioperative risk. The aim was to devascularize the tumor and reduce catecholamine secretion. Her labile blood pressure improved after embolization. She gradually recovered and underwent adrenalectomy three weeks later.Keywords: pheochromocytoma, cardiogenic shock, multiorgan failure

Introduction

Pheochromocytomas are catecholamine-producing tumors of chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla or of a paraganglion. Typical presentations include sustained or paroxysmal hypertension, severe headaches, palpitations, and sweating resulting from hormone excess [1]. They are also described as “10% tumors”, in which 10% of them are bilateral, extra-adrenal, familial, or malignant. A large proportion of cases (69%) present with an incidentally detected adrenal mass, especially in the older patients [2]. Standard curative treatment of symptomatic pheochromocytomas involves blood pressure control followed by surgical excision.

Case Report

A 46-year-old previously healthy woman presented to the emergency department with acute shortness of breath and a few months' history of coughing, headache, and palpitations. She denied any abdominal pain and was afebrile. Her initial blood pressure was 100/73 mmHg, pulse was 134 bpm, and SpO2 was 82% on 4 L of oxygen. She quickly deteriorated and developed refractory hypotension, unresponsive to vasopressor support.

She was intubated and transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU). Her electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia of 160 bpm and high-sensitivity troponin I >10,000 ng/L. Bedside ultrasound showed impaired left ventricular contraction with an ejection fraction of only 15%. There was no valvular regurgitation or pericardial effusion. Her left kidney could not be localized and was replaced by a 9-cm mass located superior to the expected location of the left kidney.

A CT scan of the abdomen revealed a 9.7 × 9.1 × 9.5 cm mixed cystic-solid mass at the left suprarenal region closely related to the left adrenal gland. The right adrenal gland was normal (Figure 1).

Coronal CT image showed a 9.7 × 9.1 × 9.5 cm mixed cystic solid mass at the left suprarenal region closely related to the left adrenal gland. The right adrenal gland was normal.

Markedly elevated plasma metanephrine and normetanephrine levels, at 1,560 pmol/L and 8,210 pmol/L respectively, confirmed the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma. Her presentation was consistent with a pheochromocytoma crisis.

She progressed to refractory cardiogenic shock and multiorgan failure with acute pulmonary edema, ischemic hepatitis, and acute renal failure. Blood tests showed worsening renal function with creatinine up to 328 μmol/L, white blood cell count of 30 × 109/L, severe metabolic acidosis with lactate of 12.5 mmol/L, and elevated alanine aminotransferase of 5,658 U/L.

Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and an Impella device (combinedly termed “ECPella”) were inserted for mechanical cardiopulmonary support. Continuous renal replacement therapy was also initiated.

She was started on α (i.e. phenoxybenzamine) and β (i.e. propranolol) blockers for blood pressure control.

Coronary angiography and endomyocardial biopsy were normal. Myocarditis workup was negative. The cardiac findings were attributed to Takotsubo cardiomyopathy.

Her hemodynamics and echocardiographic parameters seemed to improve, and she was taken to the operating theater for liberation of ECMO support. However, her vitals again became extremely unstable upon anesthetic induction, thus decannulation was abandoned. She remained dependent on mechanical circulatory support as pharmacological treatment failed to control her labile hemodynamics.

The option of surgical adrenalectomy was initially discussed but eventually turned down by the surgeons due to excessive bleeding risk while the patient was receiving heparin for ECMO.

After multidisciplinary team discussion, tumor embolization was chosen to first devascularize the tumor and reduce its catecholamine production in order to stabilize the patient. Definitive operation could then be performed after the patient was liberated from ECPella support.

Embolization was performed under local anesthesia via the left distal transradial approach at the anatomical snuffbox, using Embosphere as the embolic agent (Figures 2-6). Conventional femoral access was unavailable as both femoral arteries were occupied by ECMO and the Impella device. A dose of 3,000 iu of heparin and 200 mcg of glyceryl trinitrate was injected through the distal radial artery to prevent thrombosis and vasospasm. The patient's hemodynamics remained unstable throughout the procedure, with labile blood pressure and sinus tachycardia, while being supported with ECPella. An intensive care specialist was present during the embolization to manage her hemodynamics.

Left renal angiogram was performed using 5 Fr Performa Catheter and 0.035” 150 cm Terumo Guidewire. It showed a single left renal artery and a mildly vascular tumor at the left suprarenal region.

Pre and post embolization left renal angiograms showed significantly reduced vascularity of the tumor.

The tumor was mainly supplied by the left adrenal artery arising from the proximal left renal artery.

Left adrenal angiogram was performed using 2.0 Fr Progreat microcatheter and 0.016” Meister microguidewire. It outlined the large pheochromocytoma.

The left adrenal artery was embolized with 0.4 ml 300–500 μm Embosphere (prepacked in a 2 ml vial, and diluted with Omnipaque 300 into 20ml in total volume). The aim was to achieve permanent and as distal as possible embolization.

Better blood pressure control was achieved after embolization. Her systolic blood pressure was maintained at 120-140 mmHg. Twenty-four-hour urine metanephrine and normetanephrine were within the normal range one week after embolization.

Unfortunately, she developed right lower limb gangrene due to vascular complications arising from the arterial limb of the right femoral ECMO. Multiple surgical and endovascular procedures were attempted to salvage the limb but they were unsuccessful. She eventually required a right above-knee amputation during her ICU stay.

Her condition gradually improved, and she was transferred to the general ward for rehabilitation. Her cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, and renal functions gradually returned to normal. Open adrenalectomy was performed three weeks later. The pathology report revealed pheochromocytoma with necrosis.

On her latest follow-up 1.5 years later, she was asymptomatic and walking with a right lower limb prosthesis. A follow-up CT scan showed no tumor recurrence. She was not on any antihypertensive drugs and had satisfactory blood pressure.

Discussion

There are a few cases reported in the literature on the embolization of pheochromocytomas. Kako et al. [3] reported a case of a patient with a 2.8 cm pheochromocytoma that underwent transarterial embolization with iodized oil injection and gelatin sponge, followed by CT-guided radiofrequency ablation as the definitive treatment. Blood pressure was very labile during embolization and ablation, requiring invasive monitoring and drug treatment. The tumor shrank on follow-up CT, with absence of tumor enhancement. Blood pressure normalized after treatment. Ichikawa et al. [4] reported a case of a patient with a 5.7 cm pheochromocytoma with spontaneous rupture. Blood pressure was very high initially and then suddenly dropped due to tumor rupture. The patient was treated with transarterial embolization using gelatin sponge particles and coiling. Serum catecholamine levels normalized gradually after embolization, and blood pressure stabilized. Serial follow-up CT showed gradual shrinkage of the tumor to 1.3 cm. Laparoscopic adrenalectomy was performed 6 months later. Some case reports did not specifically mention catecholamine levels after embolization. In view of the limited studies on urgent treatment of patients with pheochromocytoma, management options are not yet standardized and depend on the expertise and experience of each center. In general, these cases are managed with a combination of medical, surgical, or interventional radiological treatments.

We can consider transarterial embolization as an effective and safe procedure in hemodynamically unstable patients until surgery can be performed. It could also be used as a last-resort treatment. However, cases should be carefully considered on an individual basis. The rationale for the decrease in hormone levels is the blockage of the adrenal blood supply and, hence, its function, including hormonal production. The procedure can allow time for patient optimization before definitive surgery. In some cases, the tumor shrinks after embolization. Tumor necrosis or adrenal manipulation during or immediately after embolization could trigger a pheochromocytoma crisis. However, in our case, because the patient was already in pheochromocytoma crisis, the chance of further increase in catecholamine levels was relatively low. Close monitoring in the ICU was provided for our patient during the critical period. Fortunately, the patient stabilized shortly after embolization. The immediate improvement in blood pressure was likely due to the embolization, while the best medical treatment was provided during her hospital stay.

The adrenal gland is classically supplied by three sources: the inferior phrenic artery, the aorta, and the renal artery. Ideally, all the branches supplying the tumor should be embolized. In our case, only a portovenous-phase CT scan was performed preoperatively. Hence, the vascular anatomy was not well delineated. During angiography, the aorta did not show supply to the tumor. The inferior phrenic artery, which branched from the left gastric artery, did not show supply to the tumor. The only supply to the tumor, which was not very vascular on angiography, came from an adrenal branch of the proximal left renal artery. We therefore performed embolization of that artery with Embosphere until stasis. Embosphere was chosen as the embolic agent because we aimed to achieve permanent and as distal as possible embolization. Coils were not used. Glue was also not used due to the difficulty in controlling it and the risk of microcatheter blockage. Different methods of embolization are used on a case-by-case basis, depending on vascular anatomy and the center's experience and expertise. Apart from embolization, other interventional radiology treatment options include image-guided ablation. However, the large tumor size in our case made it unsuitable.

The right femoral access is usually chosen in emergency abdominal embolization as it is more direct and associated with greater clinical experience. However, in our case, this approach was not feasible, as both femoral arteries had been used for ECMO and Impella support. A distal transradial approach, instead of the conventional transradial approach, was used. This technique has no statistically significant difference in terms of access site complications [5]. In the case of vessel occlusion, the proximal part of the radial artery can still be used. Easier hemostasis, along with patient and operator comfort, are the other advantages of the distal transradial approach. The transbrachial approach could be considered as an alternative, but it carries a higher risk of upper limb ischemia in the event of thrombosis.

It is better to be familiarized with different sites of vascular access because conventional options may not always be available, as demonstrated in our case.
