An Unusual Initial Manifestation of Pulmonary Embolism: Aseptic Cavitary Infarction With Pulmonary Hypertension—Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Vinay Venugopal, Saurabh Karmakar, Rajesh Kumar Yadav, Abdul Raouf Wani, Debapriya Maji, Gaurav Kumar Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is a rare but serious consequence of acute pulmonary embolism occurring in about 0.4%–6.2% of cases. Pulmonary infarction is an infrequent complication due to the lung's dual blood supply. Cavitary pulmonary infarction without infection is an exceedingly rare manifestation in CTEPH. We are reporting a 44‐year‐old woman who presented with lower limb swelling, cough, haemoptysis, and dyspnoea. Evaluation confirmed CTEPH with an aseptic cavitary infarct in the context of a heterozygous Factor V Leiden (1691G > A) mutation. She responded well to anticoagulation and riociguat, showing marked clinical improvement and complete resolution of the cavity on follow‐up imaging. Family screening showed that both children carried the same mutation, highlighting the value of genetic testing in patients with unexplained CTEPH.Keywords: chronic thromboembolism, haemoptysis, pulmonary hypertension, thrombophilia

Introduction

Pulmonary infarcts are rare due to dual pulmonary and bronchial arterial circulations. Cavitation after pulmonary infarcts may result from either aseptic necrosis of the infarcted lung (bland infarct) or from superimposed bacterial infection [1]. The prevalence of cavitation in pulmonary embolism (PE) related pulmonary infarction is 3.4%–7% [2]. In CTEPH, cavitation usually indicates secondary infection; sterile cavitary infarction is extremely uncommon [3]. Studies have also found no prior history of PE in approximately 25%–35% of CTEPH patients [4]. We report a rare case of a middle‐aged woman with no previous history of PE diagnosed with chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) with aseptic cavitary lung infarct in the context of heterozygous Factor V Leiden mutation (FVL) (1691G > A). We emphasise the need for thrombophilia genetic testing in unexplained CTEPH, as identifying these mutations can influence long‐term management.

Case Report

A 44‐year‐old woman with hypothyroidism on l‐thyroxine presented with bilateral lower limb swelling for 1 year and 1 week of cough, haemoptysis, and breathlessness (mMRC grade III). She had no history of fever, syncope, hospitalizations, trauma, surgery, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, DVT, prior PE, or hormonal therapy. On arrival, vitals were: oxygen saturation 95% on 2 L/min via nasal prongs, BP 130/90 mmHg, pulse 108/min, temperature 38°C, and respiratory rate 22/min. Examination revealed bilateral pitting pedal oedema up to the ankles, without pallor, clubbing, lymphadenopathy, raised JVP, telangiectasia, Raynaud's phenomenon, joint swelling, skin ulcer, or erythema. Crepitations were present over bilateral basal regions and S2 was loud. The rest of the systemic examination was normal. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis on FiO2 of 28% revealed pH 7.519, PaO2 54.7 mmHg, PaCO2 25.6 mmHg, A‐aO2 63 mmHg, and SpO2 of 93%.

Chest radiograph is shown in Figure 1A. Blood counts and other routine investigations were normal. Laboratory investigations including d‐dimer (8.87 μg/mL), BNP (2780 pg/mL), and hs Troponin‐I (0.136) were elevated. Modified Wells score for PE was 5.5. Anticoagulation with enoxaparin was initiated. Bilateral venous Doppler was normal. Contrast‐enhanced CT (CECT) of thorax is shown in Figure 1B–D. Blood and urine cultures were sterile. BAL was obtained from the left upper lobe. BAL GeneXpert, acid‐fast bacilli (AFB), periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS), and methamine silver stains were negative, including bacterial and fungal cultures. Lung biopsy was inconclusive. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed dilated right atrium and ventricle, and mild pericardial effusion with no regional wall motion abnormality. All the blood investigations and echocardiography findings are summarised in Table 1. Ventilation–perfusion (V/Q) scan showed multiple bilateral wedge‐shaped perfusion defects. Quantitative perfusion analysis showed that the left lung contributed 40.2% of total perfusion (7.4% upper zone, 21.4% middle zone, and 11.4% lower zone), while the right lung contributed 59.8% (16.0% upper zone, 32.3% middle zone, and 11.5% lower zone). These findings were consistent with a high probability for pulmonary thromboembolism (Figure 1G–N), and pulmonary angiogram (Figure 1E,F) findings were suggestive of chronic PE with pulmonary hypertension. The patient was unable to perform spirometry, 6MWD, or other lung function tests. Autoimmune disorder evaluation including vasculitis, anti‐nuclear antibody (ANA), rheumatoid factor, complement levels, and extractable nuclear antigen (ENA) profile were inconclusive. The sole abnormality in thrombophilia workup was heterozygous Factor V Leiden mutation [guanine substitution to adenine at 1691 nucleotide position (1691G > A)]. Complete blood investigations are shown in Table 1. A final diagnosis of CTEPH with aseptic cavitary pulmonary infarction in the context of Factor V Leiden mutation was made. The patient was initiated on warfarin and riociguat. Thrombophilia screening revealed both children were heterozygous carriers for FVL mutation (1691G > A) and referred for genetic counselling. The patient declined right heart catheterization and surgical interventions. At 3‐month follow‐up, 6MWD improved to 360 m, d‐dimer decreased to 1.41 μg/mL, BNP to 2097 pg/mL, spirometry showed predicted FVC of 81% predicted (2.39 L), FEV1 of 75% predicted (1.87 L), FEV1/FVC of 93%, and DLCO of 74%. ABG analysis (on room air) showed pH 7.437, PaO₂ 82.3 mmHg, PaCO₂ 19.2 mmHg, A‐aO₂ 43 mmHg, and SpO2 of 98%. A follow‐up chest radiograph and CT at 6 months showed complete cavity resolution (Figure 2), and she has been doing well for the past 1 year.

Discussion

CTEPH is a potentially treatable yet underdiagnosed cause of pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure, arising in patients with acute pulmonary embolism (APE) who fail to achieve resolution despite therapeutic anticoagulation for 3 months. Risk factors for CTEPH include recurrent venous thromboembolism (VTE), elevated post‐PE pulmonary artery pressures, malignancy, splenectomy, staphylococcal infections, non‐O blood groups, and thrombophilic conditions [5]. Reported incidence of CTEPH varies across studies. Ende‐Verhaar et al. found it in 0.56% of all PE patients and up to 3.2% among PE survivors [6]. Recently, Luijten et al. estimated a pooled incidence of 2.5% using updated WSPH definitions, with 1.5% in all‐comers and 2.7% among survivors (with and without major comorbidities) [4]. In a real‐world cohort, Yongping et al. observed cumulative incidences rising from 11% at 3 months to 14.5% at 3 years [7].

The prevalence of congenital thrombophilia in CTEPH is found to be 9.8% [8]. FVL mutation is a recognised common genetic variant for VTE, but it is relatively uncommon in CTEPH (5%–8%) compared to APE [9]. In evaluating the genetic predisposition, the FVL variant is less common in CTEPH compared to APE, and FVL was strongly associated with younger PE patients at first VTE (< 50 years; 21.3%) [10]. The CTEPH registry from Europe reported an FVL mutation prevalence of around 7.7% [11], while the CTEPH registry from China [8] and Japan [12] did not detect any FVL mutation. Among patients with VTE, protein C deficiency, protein S deficiency, and antithrombin III deficiency are more frequent in Asian populations, while FVL and prothrombin G20210A mutations are extremely rare compared to Caucasians (~5% of the general population and up to 16%–21% of VTE patients) [8, 13]. A recent East‐Asian meta‐analysis of 44 studies, including more than 6400 VTE patients, identified only 5 carriers of FVL (0.1%), while no cases were reported in large CTEPH registries from China and Japan [13]. Cavitation in infarct is reported in 3.4%–7% of acute PE cases [2] and in 10.6% of CTEPH cases [3]. Cavitation typically occurs when infarcts exceed 4 cm [14]. Other risk factors for infarction with cavitation include older age, heart failure, and chronic lung disease [15]. Cavitation after pulmonary infarcts may result from either bland infarction or superimposed bacterial infection [1, 3, 16, 17]. A cavity with irregular margins and no fluid level usually suggests aseptic infarction, while the presence of an air‐fluid level, fever, and purulent sputum points to infection [18]. The upper lobes are more frequently affected than the lower lobes, while the non‐cavitary infarcts are typically found in lower lung zones due to higher blood flow. Cavitation in CTEPH reflects disease severity rather than impacting mortality [3]. The differentials of cavities usually include infectious aetiologies like tuberculosis and fungal infections, vasculitis, and malignancy.

Most patients present with exertional dyspnoea and progressive exercise intolerance; haemoptysis is less common [19]. CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) remains the diagnostic gold standard (revealing pulmonary arterial filling defects and vascular pruning), while V/Q scans aid in identifying perfusion defects [2, 19]. The average time of initial detection of consolidation to cavitation is 2 weeks (range 6–40 days) for infected emboli and 4 weeks for bland emboli (range 7–120 days) [20]. Treatment of CTEPH‐related cavitary infarcts follows usual CTEPH management protocols, with pulmonary endarterectomy (for operable cases), balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA), and riociguat therapy for inoperable cases or persistent post‐PEA pulmonary hypertension [19]. Patients should undergo comprehensive evaluation, especially in unprovoked thromboembolism, as they have a higher rate of recurrence with an annualised event rate of 7.9% per patient–year [21]. Pulmonary cavitation is a rare complication of CTEPH. It requires exclusion of infection, vasculitis, and malignancy. While it reflects advanced vascular occlusion, it does not predict mortality. Unlike Western cohorts, where FVL mutation is a major risk factor, congenital thrombophilia in Asians is typically driven by protein C, protein S, and antithrombin deficiencies. The identification of heterozygous FVL mutation in our patient represents an exceptional finding, underscoring the importance of considering uncommon genetic risk factors in Asian populations and the value of family screening in such cases.
