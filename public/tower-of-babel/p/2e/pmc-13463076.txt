An intelligent assessment method for English translation teaching model based on improved BERT and attention mechanism
Authors: Wenting Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A two-step interactive fusion of the improved BERT and attention mechanism is proposed as an intelligent assessment method for English translation teaching. The adaptive Fusion BERT Network (AFBNet) model is designed to address the difficulty of effectively utilizing complex linguistic knowledge in the English translation tasks using only the fine-tuning method. The specific procedures involve extracting BERT’s multilayer representation, building the mask knowledge matrix, and applying the pre-training knowledge contained in BERT to the encoding word embedding layer. Secondly, the attention mechanism module interactively extracts useful knowledge in the multilayer representation of BERT and adaptively fuses with our English translation teaching model. As a result, AFBNet improves BLEU by approximately 2.6 to 4.4 points over the vanilla Transformer baseline across the five translation tasks, which exact gains vary by task. AFBNet is outperformed by BERT-JAM on the German-related tasks in our experiments, while achieving stronger results on several other directions. In conclusion, this two-step method integrating improved BERT and attention mechanism model proposed in this paper reduces the differences between pre-trained models and English translation teaching models due to different training goals. AFBNet model can effectively use pre-trained linguistic knowledge to improve English translation teaching model. The proposed method is particularly suitable for English translation teaching scenarios, where fine-grained semantic assessment and robust evaluation under limited data conditions are required.

Introduction

Language is a primary way to communicate and plays an important role in global interaction. As a main language in the world, English is widely used in business, culture, news, and association all over the world. An accurate, efficient, and intelligent method for English translation has gained tremendous attention. Deep learning-based language representation models and attention mechanisms open up new perspectives and methods for English translation teaching. Machine translation is widely used for the translation of a language into English, which has applications in natural language processing (NLP)1,2. Some specific models include rule-based machine translation3, statistical machine translation4, neural machine translation5, etc. But the performance of this models still relies on large-scale bilingual data. Moreover, generative artificial intelligence (GAI) models have gained widespread attention in terms of dealing with NLP tasks6. As a subset of artificial intelligence, these models can successfully perform language understanding, reasoning, and text generation7. However, these models have disadvantages such as requiring large amounts of pre-trained data, imprecision, and computational complexity8.

Therefore, proposing an intelligent, low-cost and efficient method for English translation teaching is of great significance. Since the monolingual data in nature is easier to access and lower cost, it is important to study how to utilize the existing large-scale monolingual data. Firstly, models trained on large-scale monolingual data, like BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representation from Transformers), are promising on natural language processing9. Fine-tuned BERT models have been shown to excel in comprehension tasks. The fine-tuning approach is characterized by its simplicity and accessibility, with the pre-trained model being a common subject of treatment. Nevertheless, for the sequential English translation task, the approach to initialize and tune parameters using pre-trained language models is not very successful according to the deviation in training goals. It is therefore important to investigate how BERT can be improved to fit English translation tasks. Nevertheless, the optimal utilization of BERT knowledge in English translation teaching remains an unsettled matter.

In translation pedagogy, assessment emphasizes semantic adequacy, lexical appropriateness, and structural alignment with the source text, rather than solely optimizing end-to-end translation accuracy. Student translations often exhibit characteristics such as partial semantic loss, literal transfer, collocation errors, and inconsistent discourse coherence, which require fine-grained evaluation beyond sentence-level fluency. Existing neural machine translation models are mainly optimized for large-scale industrial translation scenarios and do not explicitly consider these pedagogical characteristics. In contrast, an intelligent assessment model for English translation teaching should be capable of capturing subtle semantic deviations, filtering noisy or misleading lexical information, and providing reliable quality evaluation under limited or imperfect input conditions, which are common in student-generated translations.

Herein, an intelligent assessment model, Adaptive Fusion BERT Network (AFBNet), is proposed to effectively integrate the improved BERT model and attention10 mechanism in an interactive way. To improve language characterization capabilities of AFBNet model, different layers of BERT information are fused with the AFBNet model through the mask knowledge matrix and the adaptive fusion module. Unlike existing BERT-based NMT approaches that directly fuse the final-layer representations or apply uniform attention across layers, the proposed AFBNet introduces a two-step interactive fusion strategy that explicitly separates feature aggregation and feature selection. This design enables more controlled integration of pre-trained knowledge and reduces redundancy in representation learning. It is important to note that the proposed AFBNet model is not solely a translation system but is designed as a translation-quality–driven assessment framework. In this study, translation outputs are used as measurable indicators of students’ translation competence, particularly in terms of semantic adequacy, lexical selection, and structural alignment. Therefore, the model serves as an intelligent tool to support English translation teaching assessment by providing automated and consistent evaluation signals. Related work

BERT-based model

In 2018, Google AI proposed BERT, a deep learning-based language representation model11, which significantly outperforms traditional models such as Sent WordNet, logistic regression, and LSTM on natural language processing (NLP) tasks12. The Transformer encoder is the main model structure of BERT, offering significant advantages in text representation and computational efficiency over long-short-term memory networks (LSTMs) and recurrent neural networks (RNNs), which are traditionally used for NLP tasks13. In order to achieve further enhancement in the performance of BERT, researchers have put forth the following four primary avenues for improvement. One is to improve the model’s capacity for text feature learning through enhancing the pre-training goals of BERT. For example, ERNIE 2.0 enriches the semantic feature learning of inter-sentence relations by extending sentence relations to document location relations, rhetorical relations, retrieval phase relations, etc.14; and the ELECTRA model, named as Efficiently Learning an Encoder that Classifies Token Replacements Accurately, proposed by Facebook optimizes the pre-training process through the Replacement Word Detection (RTD) task15. Second, the text features learned by the model are further enriched by fusing external knowledge bases. For example, LIBERT introduces a syntactic relation classification training target, which specifically targets the syntactic relations between words in the text to enable the model to represent better linguistic knowledge16. Third, the internal structure of the Transformer is improved to adapt to more application scenarios. For example, the advent of Transformer-XL that introduces a segmented RNN structure breaks through the problem of limitation in long text processing by allowing cached segments of text to be revisited within the model, realizing model information with ultra-long-term dependencies17. UniLM designs different MASK matrices for the two sentences in the input data to complete the learning of the generative model, improving the multi-task modeling capability9. Improves the multi-task modeling capability18. Fourth, pre-trained model compression is utilized to improve computational efficiency and usability of the model. TinyBERT significantly reduces the model parameter size by Transformer distillation19, while Q-BERT optimizes the storage and computational performance of the model by using Hessian-based mixed-precision quantization20. RuBERT adapts pre-trained models for the Russian language, transfers learning from a multilingual model to monolingual model, and highlights notable improvements in key NLP tasks21. These improvements not only enhance the performance of BERT but also make it more efficient and easier to use in practical applications. However, these approaches are primarily designed for general NLP or translation tasks and do not explicitly consider the pedagogical requirements of translation teaching assessment, such as error sensitivity and semantic adequacy evaluation.

Research and application of attention mechanism

As a core technique of deep learning, attention mechanism has been widely used in feature extraction and model optimization22. For example, SE-Net (Squeeze-and-Excitation Networks) adjusts channel weights through global average pooling and fully connected layers, thus improving the ability of models to capture global information23. The ECA (Efficient Channel Attention) module further optimizes SE-Net by reducing the complexity of cross-channel interaction computation, thus improving efficiency24. In addition, the improved design of the normalization module replaces the global average pooling operation by introducing a gating mechanism to dynamically adjust the inter-channel relationships, and although this design curtails the parameter count, the intricacy of hyperparameters increases the training difficulty25. To further improve the model performance, the CBAM (Convolutional Block Attention Module) combines the channel attention and spatial attention mechanisms, which significantly improves the model capability in feature extraction, but has a higher demand for training data26. The BAM (Bottleneck Attention Module), on the other hand, optimizes the fusion of channel and spatial features through the concatenated structure, which improves the feature representation capability of the model27. In addition, CAM mechanism (Coordinate Attention Mechanism) is able to capture the relationship between different positions by introducing position coding, which is especially superior in small target recognition28. SimAM module, on the other hand, combines channel and spatial attention weights to optimize the extraction capability of 3D features, but it may lead to a decrease in global consistency due to the deep fusion29. The core mechanism of assigning attention weights to different dimensions can also be used for other data such as the extraction of contextual dependencies in text representations by optimizing feature interactions in the embedding and encoding layers. These innovative modules provide effective means for model performance improvement, and also provide important insights for subsequent research. Despite their effectiveness in improving feature representation, these attention mechanisms are not specifically designed to distinguish between pedagogically relevant and irrelevant linguistic features, which is critical in translation assessment scenarios.

Theories and methods of English translation teaching assessment

Translation quality assessment is an important field in translation teaching research, and a variety of models and methods have been proposed. As early as 1977, House proposed the first complete translation quality assessment model, but it was limited by its subjectivity30. Reiss suggested that qualitative assessment based on text type is more suitable for published translations31. Williams proposed a new approach combining quantitative and textual analysis, emphasizing the need for uniform standards, but the support for more complex assessment needs is still insufficient32. In recent years, multidimensional quality metrics (MQM) models have gradually become standardized assessment methods. The translation evaluation method based on functional and target audience characteristics provides richer feedback dimensions, nevertheless, the scope of application of the model still needs to be further expanded33. In addition, the introduction of machine translation evaluation criteria provides platform support for automated evaluation research34. O'Brien classifies errors into three levels, namely, minor, major, and serious errors, which provides a detailed reference categorization for evaluation, making the type of error directly correlated with its weight, thus enhancing the objectivity of scoring35. Although the field of translation quality assessment has long lacked a unified and standardized method, this problem has prompted the close cooperation between the translation academia and the translation industry to jointly propose a more perfect translation quality assessment system. Taken together, although these theories and methods provide important guidance for translation activities, they still need to be further optimized and developed in practical application. Recent advances in automatic translation evaluation have introduced neural-based metrics such as BERTScore36 and COMET37, which leverage contextual embeddings to better capture semantic similarity and adequacy. Compared to traditional metrics like BLEU, these approaches demonstrate higher correlation with human judgments, particularly in evaluating meaning preservation and fluency.

While these methods provide valuable theoretical frameworks, they are largely manual or semi-automatic and lack scalability, making them insufficient for intelligent and automated assessment in modern educational environments. With the continuous progress of the BERT model and attention mechanism, the evaluation of translation teaching no longer relies solely on manual scoring but gradually develops in the direction of automation and intelligence, and becomes a cutting-edge trend in the field of translation research.

Methodology

Given limited bilingual data, it is difficult for translation quality assessment models to generate appropriate sentence context representations, and pre-trained linguistic models can improve the effective linguistic complement. However, the method of translation quality assessment model parameter initialization and fine tuning using pre-trained language models is not suitable for English translation teaching tasks. Therefore, a two-step interactive fusion of pre-trained models, AFBNet, for English translation teaching is proposed in this paper. In the word embedding stage, a mask knowledge matrix is built, and the linguistic knowledge in the BERT representation that is useful for the English translation teaching task is adaptively merged at the word embedding layer. In encoder output stage, the valid information in the BERT is filtered by the adaptive fusion module, improving the contextual representation of source sentences. The overall framework of AFBNet model is depicted in Fig. 1. AFBNet model follows a structured pipeline consisting of three key stages including extraction and aggregation of multi-layer BERT representations, filtering of irrelevant linguistic knowledge via the mask knowledge matrix at the embedding level, and adaptive integration of BERT features with encoder representations through attention-based fusion. This stepwise design enables controlled incorporation of pre-trained knowledge while reducing noise and redundancy.Fig. 1The overall workflow of AFBNet model.

The overall workflow of AFBNet model.

Specifically, a multi-layer fusion module aggregates BERT representations, and a mask matrix filters out irrelevant information. The refined features are processed with single-head attention and fed into the encoder blocks. The decoder incorporates standard transformer components with cross-attention to align target tokens with the encoded source features. This design enhances translation quality by integrating deep contextual knowledge from BERT while maintaining efficient attention processing.

In translation teaching, assessment often relies on evaluating the quality of translated outputs. Therefore, modeling translation performance provides a practical and scalable approach to automated assessment. The AFBNet model leverages this principle by learning to generate and evaluate high-quality translations, where improvements in translation accuracy reflect improvements in underlying linguistic competence.

Adaptive fusion modules

The adaptive fusion modules are composed of two parts: the single-head attention mechanism and the selective fusion module. The former employs the output information from the coding end of AFBNet model to identify valid information in the BERT. It subsequently reduces the BERT output and that of AFBNet model encoder to create an output, which differentially interferes with AFBNet model. The selective fusion module, as the name suggests, is tasked with the responsibility of fusing the information output by the aforementioned single-head attention mechanism with the information output from the coding side of the AFBNet model. This fused information is then transmitted to the decoding side.

Experiments and analysis

Datasets and setup

AFBNet model is evaluated on three low-resource translation tasks and the languages translated into English are: German, French, and Russian. The high-resource translation tasks include German and Chinese. We classify languages based on the availability of parallel corpora, pre-trained models, and linguistic tools. Resource-rich languages have extensive datasets and well-established NLP frameworks, while resource-poor languages have fewer publicly available resources, making translation tasks more challenging. To extract contextual representations of the source language, BERT is used as a pre-trained language model. The parameters of BERT are frozen during the extraction of the contextual representations in order to decrease training time and numbers of parameters. The models used in this paper are based on Fairseq40. For example, we used bert-base-german-uncased to process German task. Transformer Transformer-Multi-Language-Translator is used as the base model. The number of layers of the encoder-decoder is 6, the word vector dimension is 512, the dimension of Feed-Forward Hidden Layer is 1,024, and the number of attention heads is 4. Adam is used as the optimizer, where the parameters β1 = 0.9, yield β2 = 0.98. The weight decay is 0.0005, the number of warm-up steps is 4,000, the learning rate (lr) is 0.0001. The loss function is the cross-entropy, the dropout of the encoder-decoder is set to 0.3, and that of the single-head attention mechanism is set to 0.2. According to the length of the sentence, it is divided into different batches, and the max-token parameter is set to 4,096. All experiments in this paper are performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 graphics card with 24GB memory. All experiments were conducted using Python 3.9.0 and Pytorch 2.6.0 deep learning framework.

BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy) was used to assess translation quality by measuring n-gram overlaps between the machine-generated output and reference translations. BLEU incorporates a brevity penalty to account for overly short translations and provides a standardized way to compare model performance across tasks. In addition to BLEU, we incorporate BERTScore and BLEURT as complementary evaluation metrics to better assess semantic quality. BERTScore measures the similarity between candidate and reference translations using contextual embeddings, enabling more accurate evaluation of semantic adequacy and lexical appropriateness. BLEURT, on the other hand, is a learned metric trained on human judgments and captures both semantic and fluency aspects of translation quality.

In addition to the current ablation settings, further analysis can be conducted on the impact of different BERT layer combinations. For example, selectively using lower, middle, or higher layers may reveal how different linguistic features contribute to translation performance. Moreover, alternative attention mechanisms, such as multi-head attention or lightweight attention variants, could be explored to evaluate their influence on adaptive fusion. These directions are left for future work due to computational constraints.

Comparative models

The work includes comparisons with other base models for the same task, including models enhanced with pre-trained knowledge and other models.

First, models that are augmented with the trained knowledge include C-MLM41, BERT-fused42, AB-NET43, XLM-R_ENC44 and BERT-JAM45. C-MLM is a conditional mask language model that use the fine-tuned BERT as additional supervision to improve the traditional sequence-to-sequence model. The output of the last layer in BERT-fused model is fused with each layer of the model separately by attention mechanism to fully exploit the BERT knowledge. Through the introduction of a light-weight adapter module in AB-NET, two pre-trained BERT models from the source language domain and the target language domain are set up into a sequence-to-sequence model. Application of XLM-R cross-language pre-trained model to the source language side of XLM-R_ENC model is used to improve the quality of translations. BERT-JAM is proposed to merge the BERT output with each layer of the model using the joint attention mechanism and cross-attention mechanism, respectively, and training BERT model using a three-level optimization strategy to moderately unfreeze the different components.

Besides, other models includes DynamicConv46 and Tied Transformers47 on the same task. For DynamicConv, lightweight dynamic convolutional neural networks are constructed to solve the problem of computationally excessive attention mechanisms due to long input sequences. Meanwhile, the translation effect of Tied Transformers is improved by sharing the weights of the codecs.

Our method shows consistent improvements over the standard Transformer model in multiple translation tasks, with the enhancement on BLEU scores ranging from 2.5 to 4.5. In comparison with other models that use pretrained knowledge, AFBNet outperforms several listed baselines on multiple tasks, but it does not outperform every strong pretrained-fusion model in every setting. For example, notably BERT-JAM is better than AFBNet on the German-related tasks in Tables 3, 4 and 5. Thus, this result supports consistent gains over our vanilla Transformer and many reported baselines, with task-dependent competitiveness against the strongest alternatives. Thus, our intelligent assessment method is capable of making effective use of the trained language knowledge. In contrast to the BERT-fused model, which fuses the BERT knowledge at each layer of encoding and decoding, AFBNet model only fuses the BERT knowledge at the encoding, which reduces training parameters. On the German translation task, AFBNet model does not outperform the BERT-JAM model. One reason is that BERT-JAM’s three-stage optimization strategy releases different modules at different training stages to achieve the best performance. The joint attention mechanism is also more effective for German translation since English and German both belong to the Germanic language family, sharing linguistic features, core vocabulary and some grammatical similarities. For these languages, BERT-JAM has a better learning ability. But for translation tasks with greater linguistic differences, such as the Chinese translation task, the result is not as good.

In addition, it is important to note that the current evaluation is conducted on standard machine translation datasets rather than authentic student-generated translation data. While these datasets provide a controlled and widely accepted benchmark for model comparison, they do not fully reflect the characteristics of learner translations in educational contexts. Real student translations often contain systematic errors such as semantic omission, literal translation, lexical misuse, grammatical inconsistency, and discourse-level incoherence. Therefore, future work will focus on validating the proposed AFBNet model on annotated student translation corpora to better assess its effectiveness in practical teaching scenarios.

Furthermore, the model can be extended to better handle typical student errors by incorporating error-aware learning mechanisms. For example, integrating error-type classification modules could enable the model to explicitly identify and weight different categories of translation errors. In addition, combining AFBNet with pedagogically annotated datasets or feedback-oriented training objectives may allow the model to generate more informative evaluation signals, such as diagnostic feedback on lexical choice, syntactic structure, and semantic adequacy. These extensions would enhance the applicability of AFBNet as an intelligent assessment tool in real-world translation teaching environments.

Conclusion

In our work, we investigated how to optimize and improve BERT’s language model and fuse attention mechanism in AFBNet encoding. At the encoder word embedding, the mask knowledge matrix is elicited, and the multi-layer language knowledge of BERT is used to guide the generation of word embeddings. In the output stage, to improve the sentence characterization ability, the effective information in the BERT model for the English translation teaching task is filtered by the adaptive fusion module including single-head attention mechanism and selective fusion modules. Extensive experiments on standard parallel MT benchmarks show that AFBNet achieves better BLEU, BERTScore, and BLEURT scores than our vanilla Transformer baseline across the reported tasks, with task-dependent comparisons against strong BERT-fusion baseline. Although motivated by teaching-oriented assessment, the quantitative evidence reflects MT benchmark performance rather than validated rubric-based scoring of learner translations. The qualitative examples illustrate typical differences relative to our Transformer baseline but do not substitute for human evaluation.
