Longitudinal patterns of penile human papillomavirus detection among Ugandan men: A prospective sampling study
Authors: Xinyi Feng, Eshan U Patel, Godfrey Kigozi, Patti E Gravitt, Ronald M Galiwango, Molly R Petersen, Fred Nalugoda, Andrew D Redd, Steven J Reynolds, Thomas C Quinn, M Kate Grabowski, Aaron A R Tobian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman papillomavirus (HPV) is a highly prevalent sexually transmitted infection, yet data on HPV dynamics in African male populations remain limited. We conducted a prospective study of 300 human immunodeficiency virus‐negative, sexually active Ugandan men enrolled at circumcision and followed every 6 weeks for 6 months. Penile swabs were collected by clinical staff, and HPV DNA was detected using the Roche polymerase chain reaction‐based linear array assay at six time points. We estimated both point and cumulative prevalence of any and high‐risk HPV, and assessed empirical and bootstrapped absolute and relative differences to quantify bias from relying solely on baseline testing. The baseline prevalence of any HPV was 49.7%, increasing to 66.0% when cumulative results were considered. Among 27 men with swabs with amplifiable viral or cellular DNA at all six visits, 100% tested positive at least once. HPV detection was often transient: only 6.1% of men with any HPV were consistently positive at all visits. Men who were ever HPV positive reported a younger age at first sex and more sexual partners. Single‐timepoint testing significantly underestimated HPV prevalence, with relative biases of 24.7% for any HPV and 40.3% for high‐risk HPV. Bootstrap analyses showed that reducing absolute bias to below 5% required two visits for any HPV and three for high‐risk HPV, whereas achieving relative bias below 5% required three and five visits, respectively. The results remained consistent in sensitivity analyses that excluded pre‐circumcision visits. These findings highlight the limitations of cross‐sectional HPV testing and emphasize the importance of repeated sampling for accurate surveillance and vaccine impact assessments in male populations.Keywords: HPV prevalence, human papillomavirus (HPV), longitudinal study, penile infection, Ugandan men

INTRODUCTION

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide. High‐risk HPV types are causally linked to multiple cancers, such as cervical, anal, penile, vaginal, vulvar, and oropharyngeal cancers. 1 Cervical cancer, driven primarily by HPV‐16 and HPV‐18, is the most common HPV‐associated cancer. 2

Africa bears a disproportionate burden of HPV infection and HPV‐related cancers. Two meta‐analyses in 2010 and 2023 estimated the pooled prevalence of any genital HPV infection at 24% in men and 37% in women, both substantially higher than global averages of 11.7% and 31%, respectively. 3 , 4 Another meta‐analysis in 2014 among only African men reported a pooled genital HPV prevalence of 78.2% in men with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and 49.4% in men without HIV. 5 Africa also has the highest cervical cancer incidence and mortality globally. 6 Despite the availability of highly effective prophylactic vaccines that protect against multiple oncogenic HPV types, vaccine coverage in Africa remains limited, with full‐dose coverage at 32% among women and less than 1% among men. 7

While HPV infection has long been studied in women, men play a critical role in HPV transmission and are also susceptible to HPV‐related diseases. 8 Up to 75% of women whose male partners test positive for HPV had HPV DNA detected in their cervix. 9 , 10 Male circumcision decreases HPV prevalence and incidence and also decreases transmission to female partners in studies with up to 24 months of follow‐up. 11 , 12 , 13 Men with HPV are at increased risk for genital warts, anal and penile cancers, oropharyngeal cancers, and acquisition of other sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. 9 , 10 Yet, longitudinal dynamics of penile HPV infection among men, especially in African populations, remain understudied.

HPV infections are often transient and controlled by the host immune system. 14 Among men, the median time to clearance is approximately 5.9 months, with 75% of infections resolving within 1 year. 15 However, most HPV studies rely on single‐point testing or long testing intervals (e.g., 6 months or more), which may miss transient infections and underestimate true prevalence. 16 , 17 In 2013, a study among women showed that baseline testing underestimated HPV prevalence by 24.2% compared to cumulative testing over a 16‐week period. 18 To date, no comparable study has evaluated short‐interval testing and transient HPV infections in men.

To address this gap, we conducted a prospective study among 300 sexually active Ugandan men followed at 6‐week intervals to assess longitudinal patterns of HPV detection and point prevalence estimates based on a single baseline versus cumulative period prevalence based on repeated testing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and population

This prospective longitudinal study was conducted in Rakai, Uganda, between June 25, 2011, and September 19, 2012. We enrolled 300 sexually active men without HIV, unvaccinated for HPV, aged 18 years and older, who were undergoing circumcision.

Participants completed a study visit at enrollment before circumcision and were followed at 6‐week intervals for 6 months post‐surgery, resulting in six total visits. Wound healing is typically completed within 6 weeks in men without HIV following circumcision. 19 At each visit (week 0, weeks 6, 12, 18, 24, and 30), participants underwent a genital examination for the collection of three penile swab specimens (from the coronal sulcus, scrotum, and penile shaft) and completed interviewer‐administered questionnaires. The questionnaires captured self‐reported sociodemographic characteristics and sexual behaviors. Pre‐moistened Dacron swabs were rotated around the full circumference of the penis at the coronal sulcus and glans and stored in Roche Amplicor specimen transport media at −80°C until assay.

HPV detection and genotyping

We evaluated all penile coronal sulcus swabs within 12 months of study completion for HPV DNA using a polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‐based Roche HPV Linear Array assay (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN), which can identify 37 HPV genotypes. These included 14 high‐risk types—HPV 16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, and 68—as well as 23 low‐risk types. 2 , 20

Specimens were evaluated for the presence of the human β‐globin gene to confirm sample adequacy. Samples that were β‐globin positive with or without HPV detection were considered valid. As previously defined, those that were β‐globin negative but HPV positive were also considered valid, indicating the presence of viral DNA despite low levels of human DNA. 11 , 12 , 13 , 21 Samples that were negative for both β‐globin and HPV were considered invalid due to insufficient DNA or poor specimen collection.

The primary outcomes of interest were any HPV infection, defined as the detection of one or more of the 37 HPV genotypes, and high‐risk HPV infection, defined as the detection of at least 1 of the 14 oncogenic types. We also examined the detection of low‐risk HPV and HPV 16/18.

Statistical analysis

Participants' first study visit with an amplifiable viral or cellular DNA result was defined as the baseline visit. Baseline sociodemographic and sexual behavior characteristics were summarized and compared between participants who were ever versus never detected with any HPV infection during the study period using descriptive statistics and Pearson's chi‐squared tests.

We estimated both point prevalence (HPV prevalence at each visit) and cumulative 6‐week period prevalence (proportion of participants with at least one HPV detection during follow‐up) for any HPV and high‐risk HPV. Cumulative prevalence was also calculated by the number of visits with swabs having detectable DNA. Among those ever detected with any HPV during the study period, we visualized longitudinal patterns in HPV status across visits, categorizing each time point as HPV positive, negative, invalid (no amplifiable viral or human DNA), or missing (no swab collected at that time point).

To quantify potential bias introduced by relying solely on baseline testing, we compared baseline prevalence to cumulative prevalence using both absolute and relative differences. 18 Absolute difference was calculated as: baseline prevalence − cumulative prevalence, while relative difference was calculated as: ((baseline prevalence/cumulative prevalence) − 1) × 100%. The statistical significance of differences between baseline and cumulative prevalence was evaluated using McNemar's chi‐squared test. To assess uncertainty in the magnitude of bias, we conducted a resampling analysis in which a single visit was randomly assigned as the “baseline” for each participant, and the absolute and relative differences in prevalence were bootstrapped over 400 iterations. We also performed 1000 bootstrap iterations to identify the minimum number of sequential swabs required per participant to reduce the median absolute bias to below 5%.

Finally, because most baseline visits occurred prior to circumcision, we conducted a sensitivity analysis redefining baseline as the first post‐circumcision visit with a swab having detectable DNA; and repeated the bias assessment accordingly. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis among the 27 men who had all six swabs with detectable DNA.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.2.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). A two‐sided p‐value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline and follow‐up characteristics

There were a total of 300 HIV‐negative, sexually active men with at least one swab with detectable DNA included in the analysis, contributing to 1524 person‐visits, with 1096 person‐visits having swabs with detectable DNA (median: 4 person‐visits per participant [interquartile range (IQR): 3–5]). Characteristics of visits with and without swabs with detectable DNA were similar (Table S1).

Among the study participants, 198 (66.0%) tested positive for any HPV genotype at least once during follow‐up, while 149 (49.7%) were positive at baseline. The baseline sociodemographic characteristics were similar between men who were ever (n = 198) versus never HPV positive (n = 102) during the study period. However, notable differences were observed in sexual behaviors (Table 1). Men who have ever tested positive for HPV reported a significantly younger age at sexual debut, as well as a higher number of lifetime and past‐year sexual partners. Interestingly, this group also reported more frequent condom use and penile washing, which may reflect more sexually active behavior patterns rather than protective practices alone.

Detection pattern and HPV prevalence

Across six study visits, the point prevalence of any HPV ranged from 48.8% to 62.7%, and high‐risk HPV ranged from 28.3% to 38.5% (Figure S1). Figure 1A shows substantial variation in detection patterns, with intermittent positivity across visits. Among the 198 men with any HPV detected during follow‐up, only 12 (6.1%) consistently tested HPV positive at every time point with swabs having detectable DNA, while 82 (41.4%) tested positive at fewer than three visits. Among the 198 men, the mean number of unique HPV genotypes detected over the study period was 3, with a maximum of 13 genotypes, while the mean number of genotypes detected per visit was 2.

(A) Patterns of human papillomavirus (HPV) status among Ugandan men ever testing positive for any HPV during the study period (N = 198) and (B) cumulative HPV prevalence by number of swabs with detectable DNA (N = 300).

Cumulative HPV prevalence increased significantly with the number of visits with swabs with detectable DNA (p‐trend <.001) (Figure 1B). Notably, among the 27 participants who had swabs with detectable DNA at all six visits, all tested positive at least once during follow‐up (cumulative prevalence: 100%), while only 88.9% (24/27) were HPV positive at baseline (Table S2).

Bias of HPV prevalence using single and multiple tests

Using baseline testing alone underestimated the absolute cumulative HPV prevalence by 16.3% for any HPV (49.7% vs. 66.0%) and 19.3% for high‐risk HPV (28.7% vs. 48.0%). The relative bias was 24.7% for any HPV and 40.3% for high‐risk HPV (Table 2). To explore the robustness of these findings, we randomly reassigned a single visit as baseline and repeated prevalence calculations 400 times. The distribution of estimated biases confirmed substantial underestimation with single‐timepoint testing. In a separate bootstrap analysis of 1000 iterations, we found that the minimum number of consecutive visits every 6 weeks needed to reduce the median absolute bias to below 5% was two for any HPV and three for high‐risk HPV. To reduce the relative bias to below 5%, the minimum number of visits needed was three for any HPV and five for high‐risk HPV (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study provides one of the most detailed longitudinal assessments of penile HPV infection dynamics among men. We observed a high cumulative prevalence of HPV (66.0%), with nearly half of the participants testing positive at baseline. HPV infection was frequently transient, with only a small proportion of men showing persistent detection across all study visits. Men who tested positive for HPV were more likely to have a history of engagement in high‐risk sexual behaviors, including younger age at first sex and a greater number of sexual partners both in their lifetime and in the past year. Importantly, relying on single‐timepoint testing significantly underestimated the true burden of HPV infection; our findings suggest that multiple sampling points are necessary to accurately assess penile HPV infection prevalence in men, as infection may be present yet undetectable given the sensitivity limits of the nucleic acid detection method.

The baseline prevalence of any HPV was 49.7%, which increased to 66.0% when all follow‐up visits were considered. These estimates are consistent with prior studies in sub‐Saharan Africa, including a meta‐analysis reporting a pooled prevalence of 49.4% among HIV‐negative men, 5 and a large study from Rakai, Uganda, which found a prevalence of 60.9% in HIV‐negative men. 16 Although baseline sociodemographic characteristics were similar between men who were ever HPV positive and those who were never HPV positive, significant differences were observed in sexual behavior—factors well‐established as risk factors for HPV acquisition. 22 Interestingly, they also reported higher rates of condom use and penile washing. These findings may reflect a behavioral profile of higher overall sexual exposure, rather than effective risk reduction, and underscore the complex relationship between self‐reported preventive practices and actual infection risk. Condom use and penile washing may also increase the risk of microtrauma to the penile epithelium, which could trigger HPV reactivation through wound healing. 14 , 23

Our findings demonstrate that HPV infection in men is often intermittently detectable. Among men who tested positive at any point, only 6.1% remained consistently positive at all six time points, while more than 40% were positive at fewer than three visits. Similar results were reported from the multinational HPV in men Study, showing a median duration of 7.5 months for any HPV infection and approximately 12 months for HPV‐16, with most infections becoming undetectable within a year. 15 Similar results were found in Ugandan men, with rates of loss of detection for high‐risk HPV exceeding 170 per 100 person‐years among HIV‐negative individuals. 21 These findings suggest that intermittent detectability is a key feature of HPV natural history in men. However, it is impossible with current molecular technologies to determine if this represents transient detection and re‐detection of the same infection over time, or transient acquisition/clearance/re‐infection. 24

We found that single‐timepoint testing significantly underestimated active or recent infections and cumulative HPV prevalence. Relative bias reached 24.7% for any HPV and 40.3% for high‐risk HPV. This aligns with findings from a short‐interval sampling study among women, where a single HPV test missed nearly 25% of infections detectable over a 16‐week window. 18 In addition, a global review of male HPV prevalence noted substantial variability, driven in part by the number of anatomic sites sampled and the frequency of testing. 25 These data reinforce that detection bias is a major challenge in HPV surveillance and that studies using only data with a single time point for sampling are likely to misclassify many infected individuals as negative. Our bootstrap analysis further revealed that to reduce absolute bias to below 5%, a minimum of two visits was required for any HPV and three for high‐risk HPV. To achieve a relative bias below 5%, three and five visits were needed, respectively. These results provide quantitative guidance for researchers and public health programs on the value of repeated sampling. Incorporating serial sampling into surveillance or intervention trials could markedly improve detection of transient infections and yield more accurate burden estimates.

This study has several strengths, including its prospective design, high‐frequency follow‐up, and the application of robust analytical methods to quantify detection bias. Conducted within a well‐characterized cohort of HIV‐negative men in sub‐Saharan Africa, it addresses a critical gap in the literature, as most prior studies on HPV dynamics have been designed to test HPV status with >6 months intervals. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, transient infections occurring between study visits may have gone undetected. Second, our findings may not be generalizable to older men, uncircumcised men, or men living with HIV. Additionally, differences in specimen collection methods, including swab type and anatomical site, may also affect the comparability of our results with other studies. 26 , 27 Third, because most baseline visits occurred prior to male circumcision, which can increase clearance of HPV, 13 the potential for measurement bias exists; however, sensitivity analyses excluding the pre‐circumcision visit yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of our findings. Lastly, we did not evaluate type‐specific persistence or HPV viral load. Prior studies suggest that high‐copy number penile HPV infections—particularly with high‐risk types—are associated with reduced clearance and prolonged persistence, indicating greater clinical relevance. 28 , 29

In conclusion, our findings highlight the high prevalence and transient detection of HPV infection in men. Single‐timepoint testing underestimates the true HPV infection burden, and testing intervals greater than 6 months may misrepresent within‐individual infection dynamics. These insights are critical for informing HPV vaccine policy, particularly in male populations in sub‐Saharan Africa, where vaccination rates and data have been sparse. Future studies and public health programs should prioritize repeated sampling to ensure an accurate assessment of HPV prevalence and vaccine impact.
