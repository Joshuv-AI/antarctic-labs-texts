Hexagonal Boron Nitride Quantum Simulator: Prelude to Spin and Photonic Qubits
Authors: Antonio Cobarrubia, Nicholas Schottle, Dilon Suliman, Sebastian Gomez-Barron, Christopher R Patino, Boris Kiefer, Sanjay K Behura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe quest for qubit operation at room temperature is accelerating the field of quantum information science and technology. Solid state quantum defects with spin-optical properties are promising spin- and photonic qubit candidates for room temperature operations. In this regard, a single boron vacancy within hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) lattice such as VB– defect has coherent quantum interfaces for spin and photonic qubits owing to the large band gap of h-BN (6 eV) that can shield a computational subspace from environmental noise. However, for a VB– defect in h-BN to be a potential quantum simulator, the design and characterization of the Hamiltonian involving mutual interactions of the defect and other degrees of freedom are needed to fully understand the effect of defects on the computational subspace. Here, we studied the key coupling tensors such as zero-field splitting, Zeeman effect, and hyperfine splitting in order to build the Hamiltonian of the VB– defect. These eigenstates are spin triplet states that form a computational subspace. To study the phonon-assisted single photon emission in the VB– defect, the Hamiltonian is characterized by electron–phonon interaction with Jahn–Teller distortions. A theoretical demonstration of how the VB– Hamiltonian is utilized to relate these quantum properties to spin- and photonic-quantum information processing. For selecting promising host 2D materials for spin and photonic qubits, we present a data-mining perspective based on the proposed Hamiltonian engineering of the VB– defect in which h-BN is one of four materials chosen to be room temperature qubit candidates.Keywords: hexagonal boron nitride, quantum defect, single photon emission, qubit, quantum simulator, quantum information science

Introduction

Qubit engineering is an emerging research area for designing quantum information (QI) devices for quantum sensing,1 quantum cryptography,2,3 quantum networking,4 and quantum computing.5−7 Current state-of-the-art quantum computers are built on superconducting qubits with fast gate operations but suffer from the lack of completely stable and noise-free energy transitions needed for large-depth quantum algorithms.8 Further, the coherence time, i.e., the average time a qubit takes to relax to the ground state from the excited state, is sufficiently prolonged. Other qubit systems such as semiconductor spin qubits and ion qubits can retain longer coherence (stable) times (T1 > 100s) and higher gate fidelity.5,9 However, ion based qubits are difficult to scale up as the architecture has to account for strong coupling regimes when more ions are packed together closely at cryogenic temperatures.10 Alternatively, quantum defects in solid-state materials are potential candidates for coherent room temperature qubits since defect states can enable both spin and photonic computational states (|0⟩ and |1⟩).11

Solid state two-level system-based spontaneous emission is the conventional mechanism to produce single photons from the homogeneous environment of single molecules, quantum dots, and color centers in wide bandgap materials such as diamond, silicon carbide, and zinc oxide. Single photons originating from optically active solid state quantum defects are promising qubit candidates for room temperature quantum processes such as the case for NV-diamond qubits.12,13 At this temperature, large band gap materials isolate degenerate energy transitions from environmental noise to allow single photon emissions. Stable spin–spin interactions translate gate interactions between each single photon emitter’s computational subspace.1,12,14 However, the quality of a photonic qubit depends more on than just stable transitions between vibrational states: (a) brightness, (ii) purity, (iii) indistinguishability, and (iv) reproducibility.12,15 Further, there are several challenges that need to be addressed for the development of bright, fast, stable, and controllable quantum sources of light at room temperature including: low collection efficiency and quantum yield with three-dimensional quantum emitters, long intrinsic lifetime of the electronic excited states, and challenges in fabricating nanostructures and integration into quantum photonic circuits. Creating spin-defect-based quantum sources of single photons hosted on the surface of the 2D material is promising for quantum information science and technology in contrast to the defects buried within the bulk of the 3D material. In this regard, atomically thin 2D materials are just surfaces that host spin defects and are compatible with integrated photonic circuits.14 Hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN), a 2D wide band gap material, hosts optically active defects while displaying spin-optical properties at room temperature1−3,5,16−22 suitable for photonic quantum simulations.

A quantum simulator is a quantum state that simulates and/or emulates another quantum phenomena through coherent and stable quantum control protocols.23 This is a subrequirement for Fermionic and Bosonic problems based on the basis state description of the QI devices,24 which are facilitated through controllable pulse sequences that rotates the state of the qubit to digital logic operations. These processes are potentially compatible with solid-state defect qubits in h-BN.

The VB– Defect

Hexagonal boron nitride has a triangular Bravais lattice with a two-atom basis with boron and nitrogen atoms covalently bonded in sp2 hybridization as presented in Figure 2a. Each nitrogen atom surrounding the boron vacancy contributes 2 electrons, which are spin-paired in a 2pz orbital, resulting in 6 electrons.27 Moreover, with the removal of the boron atom, the three nearest-neighbor (NN) nitrogen atoms surrounding the defect contribute 3 electrons each due to dangling bonds.27 This brings the total number of electrons in a single VB– defect to 9, where the band gap energy is ∼6 eV. Here, single photons are emitted by the transitions between degenerate eigenstates within the band gap due to the boron vacancy, as shown in Figure 2b. Since the vacant boron atom would have had 3 valence electrons, the defect may accept up to 3 electrons.

From density functional theory (DFT), the case where the defect accepts no additional electrons, VB0, forms C2v symmetry28 where two of the NN nitrogen atoms are distorted closer to each other, as shown in Figure 2c. The formation energy (∼7.8 eV at ground Fermi level) in-bulk scenarios makes VB0 configuration an energetically favorable symmetry for cryo-temperatures relative to other charge states.28 For few-layer h-BN at room temperature, the VB0 configuration becomes energetically less favorable to retain the stability of its symmetry. The case where the defect accepts an electron, VB–, form a D3h symmetry where each NN nitrogen atom is equidistant from the other (Figure 2c) and a stable NN N–N bond is formed.28,29 The boron vacancy with 11 electrons, VB–2, forms C2v symmetry.27 However, the VB–2 defect needs more energy to be a favorable defect state, as highlighted in Figure 2c. The case where the defect accepts all 3 electrons, VB–3, is completely unstable from a DFT analysis.29 Thus, for few-layered h-BN at room-temperature, the boron vacancy defect VB– is the energetically favorable configuration to retain D3h symmetry due to its additional acceptance of electron, which makes the total defect electron count 10.30,31

Under room temperature operation, phononic assisted single photon (PA-SP) emissions from defect electron–phonon coupling, enhance the photostability of color center in h-BN.39,40 PA-SP emissions have been observed at resonant frequency of 45 THz phonon mode associated with multiple defect centers in h-BN.39 In-plane acoustic phonon modes have the potential of distorting sp2 bonding to sp3 from the interaction with out-of-plane distortions.40 Out-of-plane static distortions in the h-BN defect indicate that only in-plane phonons dynamically contribute to VB– PA-SP emissions. Longitudinal acoustic phonons may carry energy away from single photon emission to the thermal environment and increase the coherence noise within the h-BN emitter impacting the stability of quantum control protocols.41 The main PA-SP emission is due to in-plane longitudinal optical phonons. Photonic Qubits

Bosonic codes, such as bosonic sampling, in single photonic quantum computers require efficient indistinguishable emission of single photons for high fidelity and scalable architectures.58 In much the same way as discussed in the spin-qubit perspective, generating single photons and encoding information in the time domain are solutions to eq 1. However, unlike the spin-qubit where gate operations are driven by an external magnetic field, a single gate operation on a single photon is processed through integrated photonic circuits; and their bosonic modes allow for higher-dimensional logic operations beyond qubits–qudits.59 Under this case, phonon contribution may play an important factor in assisting higher dimensional logic gates as described in eq 17 as well as the emission of single photons in VB– h-BN defect at room temperature.60 Characterization and modeling of similar behavior in optical cavities are well established in open quantum models such as quantum input-output network (QION) theory and SLH theory.26,61 Currently, photonic models for solid-state defect qubits are studied for quantum network devices,32 but they lack the necessary QION theory for larger qubit devices. In the subsequent work, we plan to study photonic qubits using the QION theory.

Data Mining for Promising SPE 2D Materials

The Hamiltonian described in eq 8 provides a complete description of the electronic structure of (charged) point defects and has been used to describe electronic defect states in the band gap of the otherwise pristine 2D- and 3D-host materials.12,27,62 Similarly, DFT studies at various levels of theory have explored the structural stability of “deep-level” point defects and their electronic, magnetic, and optical signature, within h-BN (see, for example, Liu et al.49 and Baber et al.).63 While 2D materials have emerged as a promising materials platform in support of quantum technologies, a significant remaining challenge is to find SPE materials that operate in the telecom frequency bands (1.25–1.55 nm or 0.992–0.800 eV) for linking up with existing fiber optics communication technology. Several different strategies are currently pursued to achieve this goal, strain engineered 2D materials such as WTe2 where the band gap is driven toward the telecom window by an applied strain;64 an alternative is to identify suitable point defects and/or dopants that realize the complete SPE optical system within a wide band gap host material.46 However, it is desirable to provide an assessment of 2D materials based on the applications for optimizing quantum technologies.

Here we present a data analysis of DFT predicted properties within the computational 2D database, C2DB.65,66 At the time of writing, the database contained 15733 2D materials. DFT-PBE band gaps were available for 7208 of these materials, and DFT-HSE06 band gaps (including spin–orbit coupling) were available for 1599 materials. The DFT-PBE band gap is well-known to be generally smaller than the experimental band gap.67 This deficiency is addressed through exact exchange-correlation functionals, such as HSE06,68 and quasi-particle GW methods,69 that generally lead to better agreement between predicted and experimentally observed band gaps such that “deep-level” point defects continue to attract significant attention.11,70 Guidance for selecting suitable host materials for deep-level spin qubits, including the need of low spin–orbit coupling and isotopes with nuclear spin I = 0, both requirements decrease unwanted interactions between defect and host in which the defect is isolated from its environment. To identify suitable materials in the database, we screen the DFT-HSE06 materials by requiring that the most abundant isotopes of each elemental constituent have a nuclear spin I = 0 (singlet) leading to 381 materials. Further screening for a low (atomic) spin–orbit coupling constant (SOC) of <130 cm–1 (Si) results in two ungapped materials, silicene and graphene, and a tighter constraint, such as SOC < 80 cm–1, leads to no results at all. Relaxing the isotope constraints to match the isotope signature of h-BN: B: I = 3/2 and N: I = 1, for the most abundant isotopes, increases the number of promising materials to 1016 (∼64% of the DFT-HSE06 material set), using again SOC < 130 cm–1 leads to 11 materials, further requiring a band gap of >2 eV, leads to 5 materials (h-BN, H2Si2, MgB2H8, C2H2, and CH2Si), but only h-BN is on the convex hull, suggesting relative stability to other competing phases, consistent with its experimental synthesizability. Allowing for a higher SOC < 200 cm–1, leads to 32 materials, 22 of which have a band gap >2 eV, but only 4 compounds are on the convex hull (Figure 4): h-BN (Eg(HSE06) = 5.7 eV), K2C2H2O6 (Eg(HSE06) = 6.2 eV), Na2H2O2 (Eg(HSE06) = 4.5 eV), and Li2H2O2 (Eg(HSE06) = 5.6 eV). All four 2D materials are predicted to show high thermodynamic, vibrational, and elastic stability. We emphasize that investigated materials for SPE, such as WS2,46 are in the DFT-HSE06 database, both W and S have dominant isotopes with I = 0, but the compound did not pass the screening test due to high SOC of W and S.

This indicates that each selective material of the data search would lead to promising single photon emitter candidates. Conclusion and Perspectives

The VB–-defect’s spin-optical properties are fully characterized by the total Hamiltonian presented in eq 8. In order to extract a stable computational subspace for digital quantum information processing, the spin-1 states have to be chosen in relation to the SU(2) algebra while filtering unwanted matrix element transitions. The main control mechanisms to generate and control stable spin-1 states are due to key coupling tensors: zero-field splitting, Zeeman effect, and hyperfine splitting.

While we have explored the fundamental physics of VB– defect, the stress and strain contributions to ZFS75 and HFS tensors may impact the spin states of the defect. For example, silicon carbide color centers show enhanced ODMR sensitivity for finer tuning in quantum control.76 Phonon-assisted photostability of h-BN defects, beyond Jahn–Teller distortions, may behave similarly to shifted frequency readouts much like the role of superconducting quantum resonators for nondemolition measurements.6−8 Coherent control for VB– centers due to spin-optical coupling with a coherence time of T1 ∼ 16 μs and a decoherence time of T2 ∼ 40 ns are influenced by the rate of nuclear spin flip-flops (|ms = −1⟩ ↔ |ms + 1⟩).19,52 An alternate strategy of VB– design is to use h-BN with different atomic isotopes to stabilize the rate of nuclear spins along with efficient decoupling pulse schemes, such as Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse distribution, to suppress magnetic noise.19 Optimized pulse protocols in a spin bath system lead to increased decoherence time (T2) from standard echo pulse schemes for VB– from ∼100 ns to ∼103 ns.53 Employing similar decoupling schemes onto the defect site such as CPMG could lead to continued promising coherence times and thus a great opportunity to isolate noise for quantum control of an h-BN defect qubit.

While VB– spin states are well investigated through ODMR characterization and DFT analysis, the origin of the 10th electron accepted by the defect remains ambiguous. The 10th electron originates from the environment surrounding the h-BN, which can be from the lattice,14 substrate, or external process from electron transfer during growth. However, currently none of these cases are theoretically or empirically well documented to rule them out. If we consider the interpretation that the accepted electron originates from the h-BN lattice itself, then a hole in its electronic configuration would change the electron density and symmetry of the defect. Current DFT analysis assumes that the VB– forms a perfect periodic lattice (aside from the defect), which gives rise to D3h symmetry in VB– and an energetically favorable formation energy; changing the geometry of the lattice changes the point group of the lattice and its overall formation likelihood. Alternatively, during preparation of VB– defect, the positive ions break the B–N bond such as ion implantation,18,30,77 which may accept additional electrons. Probing this electron during the preparation of VB– defects may be significant in selectively designing single- and multidefect sites.30 Therefore, the origin of the 10th electron will provide an important explanation on the symmetry of VB– defect and coupling tensor parameters.

Furthermore, the band gap and spin–orbit coupling constraints eliminate all but ∼0.3% of the materials in the C2DB DFT-HSE06 database, which highlights a likely small material space for SPE spin-qubit 2D hosts. However, our physics-guided database search also confirms that h-BN belongs to a short list of promising spin-qubit-layered host materials. The ZPL in a VB– defect in h-BN is 1.65 eV, higher than the telecom frequency window, likely requiring additional tuning beyond defect formation, such as an interface strain that will occur during quantum emitters integration and/or more complicated defect motifs. In addition, the relatively large band gap of these selected materials hinders the quality of the luminosity, i.e., brightness, of the emitted photons such as the case with h-BN.14 Defect-plasmonic coupling from metallic reflectors such as gold enhances the brightness of photons emitted from h-BN defect hosts.78 This phenomenon occurs due to surface plasmons on the metallic reflector underneath the h-BN defect host creating plasmonic quasi-particles, which enhances photon-plasmon interactions.14,79 However, for h-BN, the large band gap obstruct the electrons to move across the surface of the h-BN lattice,25,80−82 which is why plasmons without a supporting plasmonic interface have negligible contributions to the total Hamiltonian in eq 8.79 Yet, the theoretical formulation of plasmonic effects on h-BN defects and the overall impact on QI devices is not well understood. Deep learning networks can actively predict defects in materials with the targeted band gap energies of >2 eV and associated SOC conditions from ab inito calculations. This leads to 1000 point defects in 2D materials including h-BN, which are assigned a machine learning (ML) metric called defect score to assess the quality of the defect as a stable qubit.83 Further, the defects in h-BN have high defect scores corresponding to longer emission lifetimes (defect score of >0.9). However, these ML models only focus on material properties under unperturbed conditions. In addition, the influence of quantum control techniques needs to be integrated for a full predictive analysis to find efficient quantum defects for single photon emitter candidates.

A full description of h-BN’s role as a qubit requires a time evolution simulator that incorporates these additional complex defect phenomena as described previously. A simulator framework that can extend to 2D defects would give the capability for first principle analysis, characterization, and prediction of quantum information protocols at the defect level including, but not limited to, gradient-based pulse optimization integrated with in-house experiments such as Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate (DRAG) schemes.8 In the perspective of a numerical integration approach, to build a h-BN quantum simulator framework, the required classical computation is fundamentally a coupled dynamical system of equations that scales with the dimensions of the qubit’s (and other coupled systems) Hilbert space. The h-BN qubit can directly target coherence, decoherence, gate times, and qubit’s feature vectors (such as entanglement ratio, parallelism, program communication, critical depth, liveness, and measurement).84 However, for qubits in h-BN to scale to the demands of quantum technology requires solving the curse-of-dimensionality beyond the current capability of supercomputers.85 Fortunately, h-BN defects have a multitude of selectively tunable interactions that can be exploited as prime candidates for current and future quantum technologies, quantum computing, quantum sensing, and quantum communication.

In terms of practical development, there has been significant progress in integrating probabilistic sources of quantum light with photonic circuitry, and less progress has been made using the more advantageous deterministic sources. This is because the current efforts to create quantum defects in h-BN mostly rely on h-BN films prepared through exfoliation from bulk crystals followed by defect creation using techniques such as thermal annealing, ion irradiation, ion implantation, and electron beam irradiation. The ability to develop deterministic sources of quantum light in large-area h-BN films will play an important role in the advancement of quantum information science and technology. Catalytic chemical vapor deposition (CVD) approach has been used to synthesize large-area h-BN films on various metal surfaces.86−88 However, for defect creation and studies related to the photophysics of single photon emitters and their integration with photonic circuitry, the metal-supported h-BN layers need to be transferred onto various insulating and/or dielectric substrates via chemical or electrochemical methods. Such methods introduce additional defects as well as polymer and metal impurities to the transferred h-BN films.89 It is envisioned that by synthesizing large-area h-BN layers directly on dielectric or photonic substrates such as SiO2/Si, Si3N4/Si, and Al2O3, while controlling the defect density, defect type, crystallinity, and thickness will enable deterministic sources of quantum light. Transfer-free and direct CVD growth of h-BN layers on both silicon-based and nonsilicon-based dielectric substrates have been performed. Few to multilayered h-BN films were grown via CVD on SiO2/Si and quartz substrates by using ammonia borane (NH3BH3) as B and N sources.90 Further studies focused on describing the reaction kinetics for the formation of h-BN layers on SiO2/Si, Si3N4/Si, Si, and Al2O3 substrates91−95 using low-pressure CVD at a temperature range of 700–1100 °C. Future studies with a focus on controlling the defect density in the large-area CVD grown h-BN layers are critical.
