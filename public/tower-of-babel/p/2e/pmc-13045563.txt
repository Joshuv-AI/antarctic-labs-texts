Electroanatomical Mapping May Identify Concealed Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathy in Endurance Athletes With Complex Ventricular Arrhythmias
Authors: Boris Delpire, Dagmara Dilling-Boer, Olivier Ghekiere, Pieter Koopman, Peter Peytchev, Tom De Potter, Hein Heidbuchel, Andre La Gerche, Rik Willems, Guido Claessen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHighly trained endurance athletes can develop complex ventricular arrhythmias despite normal structural cardiac imaging, creating major diagnostic challenges. This case series describes 3 young cyclists presenting with symptomatic ventricular arrhythmias from the right ventricle (RV), in whom conventional investigations, including transthoracic echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance, revealed no structural abnormalities. Prolonged rhythm monitoring prompted electroanatomical mapping, which revealed discrete to diffuse low-voltage areas across the RV outflow tract, RV inferior wall, and apex; findings compatible with an arrhythmogenic substrate and raising the possibility of early or concealed arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. All 3 athletes had long-standing exposure to high-intensity endurance training, which may be relevant in susceptible individuals, although no causal relationship can be inferred from these cases. These observations highlight the limitations of relying solely on conventional imaging in athletes with persistent symptomatic arrhythmias and suggest that, in carefully selected cases, prolonged rhythm monitoring and electroanatomical mapping may offer complementary diagnostic insights when concealed arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy is suspected.Key words: athletes, arrhythmias, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, HRM spikes, rhythm monitoring, sports cardiology

Athlete 1

Presentation

A 15-year-old highly trained male cyclist (15 hours/week of cycling) was referred after premature ventricular beats (PVBs) were detected during preparticipation screening. The initial report described ectopic beats that did not suppress with exercise.

Upon detailed questioning, the athlete acknowledged intermittent palpitations associated with chest discomfort that forced him to stop training. He had noticed irregular “spikes” on his chest-worn heart rate monitor (HRM) corresponding to these episodes but denied syncope. Family history revealed his paternal grandfather had received an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) at 75 years of age for tachyarrhythmia and syncope.

Diagnostic work-up

Resting electrocardiogram (ECG) was normal (Figure 1A). Exercise testing showed frequent (98) monomorphic premature ventricular beats (PVBs), most prominent during mild to moderate workloads. The ectopic beats appeared as isolated and monomorphic couplets with right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) morphology (Figure 1B), with slight variation in morphology during exercise.

(C) A persistently high burden of PVBs confirmed by a 7-day ambulatory single-lead ECG monitor (14,324 PVBs, 134 couplets, and 1 triplet). ECG = electrocardiogram; PVB = premature ventricular beat.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed an enlarged RV with mildly reduced systolic function (global strain: −16.2%, free wall strain: −17.2%, fractional area change: 24%) and regional hypokinesia in the subtricuspid valve region.

Given the combination of symptoms, frequent PVBs with couplets during exercise, and TTE findings, the athlete was temporarily withdrawn from competition. Beta-blocker therapy was initiated for symptomatic control, and further diagnostic testing was pursued.

Follow-up

A 48-hour Holter monitoring recorded 4,597 PVBs (2.3% over 24 hours) of multiple morphologies, often in bigeminal and trigeminal patterns, and 14 triplets. The predominant morphology was RVOT origin, consistent with the exercise ECG findings.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) demonstrated slight asymmetric RV enlargement (RV end-diastolic volume: 129 mL/m2 vs LV end-diastolic volume: 113 mL/m2) with normal RV ejection fraction (51%). No late gadolinium enhancement or structural abnormalities fulfilling diagnostic criteria for arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy were identified. A combined cardiopulmonary exercise test with echocardiography confirmed preserved RV contractile reserve (RV end-systolic pressure-area ratio: >2).1 Signal-averaged ECG was normal.

Given the athlete's young age, the unexpectedly high ectopic burden, and the asymmetrical RV enlargement, an electrophysiology study was performed but failed to induce supraventricular or ventricular arrhythmias (VAs). Genetic testing was initiated, and follow-up rhythm monitoring was arranged.

Diagnosis and management

Follow-up Holter monitoring 6 weeks later confirmed a persistently high ectopic burden (2,047 PVBs, 134 couplets, and 1 triplet, 2% over 24 hours) (Figure 1C). Given the ongoing ectopy and concern for an underlying arrhythmogenic substrate, a repeat electrophysiology study with detailed electroanatomical mapping (EAM) (Carto, Biosense Webster) and PVB ablation was performed.

EAM demonstrated multifocal PVBs originating from 4 RV regions: the predominant morphology originated from the anterior RVOT just beneath the pulmonary valve, with additional foci in the anterior and posterolateral RVOT and the inferior RV wall. Bipolar mapping demonstrated discrete low-voltage areas, while unipolar mapping showed broader abnormalities, suggesting a more extensive epicardial substrate (Figure 2). A small zone of late potentials was identified along the inferior wall. Radiofrequency ablation of the dominant PVB focus was performed, after which no sustained or nonsustained VAs were inducible during programmed ventricular stimulation (PVS).

Genetic analysis identified a heterozygous desmoglein-2 (DSG2) variant (c.458A>C; p.Asn153Thr, exon 5), classified as a variant of uncertain significance (class 3/5). DSG2 mutations are associated with arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy. Familial segregation testing was recommended.

Given the presence of an arrhythmogenic substrate, high ectopic burden, and evolving phenotype, the athlete was advised to abstain from competitive sports, and an implantable loop recorder was inserted. Within 1 month, the implantable loop recorder documented a 29-second episode of nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) at 232 beats/min (Figure 3). After multidisciplinary discussion, an extravascular ICD was implanted for secondary prevention, balancing the elevated arrhythmic risk against the athlete's young age and preference to avoid transvenous leads. Ongoing follow-up includes regular rhythm monitoring, exercise testing, and echocardiographic assessment of RV function. Maintenance therapy consists of a beta-blocker.

Athlete 2

Presentation

A 16-year-old highly trained male cyclist (>17 hours/week of cycling) was evaluated for recurrent palpitations associated with abrupt “spikes” on a chest-worn HRM, reaching up to 214 beats/min. The episodes had sudden onset and offset, recurred several times over recent months, and were not accompanied by syncope, chest pain, or dyspnea. Medical history was notable for exercise-induced asthma, and there was no relevant family history.

Diagnostic work-up

A resting ECG demonstrated early repolarization, T-wave inversion in leads V1 and V2, and a rightward axis (Figure 4). TTE and exercise testing were completely normal. A 7-day ambulatory single-lead ECG subsequently recorded 1,718 isolated PVBs (0.25% over 24 hours), 6 couplets, and 1 monomorphic triplet.

ECG = electrocardiogram.

Given the burden of symptomatic ventricular ectopy, CMR was performed, demonstrating normal biventricular systolic function (LV ejection fraction: 56%, RV ejection fraction: 51%), no edema, and no late gadolinium enhancement. Coronary computed tomography angiography was unremarkable for coronary anomalies. Because of the presence of complex VAs and the athlete's high training level, an electrophysiology study was performed, which failed to induce supraventricular tachycardia or VAs. An ajmaline provocation test was negative. After these assessments, sports restrictions were lifted, and a gradual return to training was allowed.

Follow-up

Five months later, the athlete experienced cardiac arrest after exercise. Prompt bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation and application of an automated external defibrillator restored spontaneous circulation. Subsequent interrogation of the automated external defibrillator revealed 6 shocks for ventricular fibrillation, with successful restoration of sinus rhythm after 15 minutes. Urgent coronary angiography showed no significant coronary artery disease. Repeat CMR after the collapse showed acutely reduced ventricular function (LV ejection fraction: 45%, RV ejection fraction: 38%) and myocardial edema consistent with changes after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest.

Diagnosis and management

Because of frequent PVBs and recurrent NSVT progressing to VT storm despite optimal medical therapy (Figure 5), the athlete underwent repeat electrophysiological study with EAM (Carto). This confirmed diffuse epicardial involvement, prompting catheter ablation. EAM showed areas of fragmented and late potentials in the RVOT, basal posterior tricuspid annulus, and RV apex (Figure 6). Spontaneous ectopy matched the morphology of the clinical VT. PVS induced short runs of identical NSVT, but sustained VT could not be provoked despite isoproterenol and adrenaline infusion. Pace mapping localized the focus to a broad RVOT region (anterior to posteroseptal), which was successfully ablated. No VT was inducible afterward.

An extravascular ICD was implanted for secondary prevention. On the day of discharge, the patient experienced recurrent VT, resulting in 2 ICD shocks, necessitating readmission to the intensive care unit, where recurrent long runs of spontaneous NSVT were documented. A repeat ablation revealed diffuse epicardial involvement of the RV with marked electrogram fragmentation in the anterior RVOT. Combined endocardial and epicardial ablation of this region was performed and resulted in elimination of ectopy during a 45-minute waiting period, including after anesthesia withdrawal and pharmacologic stimulation with isoproterenol and adrenaline. Genetic testing identified no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants.

Overall, findings were consistent with early arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy, and cessation of competitive sports was advised. Ongoing management includes beta-blocker therapy and regular ambulatory follow-up with periodic Holter monitoring, exercise testing, and imaging.

Athlete 3

Presentation

A 23-year-old highly trained male mountain biker (elite level; >20 hours/week of cycling) was referred for evaluation of exercise-associated palpitations accompanied by abrupt “spikes” recorded on his chest-worn HRM. During detailed history taking, he also reported several syncopal episodes without prodromal symptoms.

He was able to continue cycling during the palpitations, albeit at reduced intensity, and reported feeling markedly unwell upon cessation of exercise. He had no significant personal medical history. Family history was notable for a grandfather who underwent pacemaker implantation at age 65 for unspecified reasons.

Diagnostic work-up

Resting ECG showed multiple PVBs with left bundle branch block morphology in V1, an inferior axis, and QRS duration <130 ms, with slightly variable morphology, suggesting an RVOT origin (Figure 7A).

Exercise testing revealed frequent PVBs with similar morphology, including couplets at peak exercise and during recovery (Figure 7B). TTE demonstrated LV size and systolic function within the expected range for a highly trained athlete (4-dimensional ejection fraction: 56%) and normal RV size and function (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion: 23 mm; RV global longitudinal strain: 18%; free-wall strain: 21.4%). Biochemical analysis was unremarkable.

Because of the combination of palpitations and VAs during exercise, a 24-hour Holter and CMR were performed. The 24-hour Holter showed frequent PVBs (3,262, 3.3% over 24 hours) with left bundle branch block morphology in V1, multifocal, short coupled, 130 couplets, 42 triplets, and 1 polymorphic run of NSVT of 4 beats at 193 beats/min (Figure 7C). CMR showed normal biventricular systolic function (LV ejection fraction: 57%, RV ejection fraction: 51%), with no edema or late gadolinium enhancement.

Follow-up

Given the complex VAs and persistent palpitations despite a negative CMR, an electrophysiology study with EAM (Carto) was planned. During the first procedure, isoprenaline induced complex multifocal ventricular ectopy with 12 distinct PVB morphologies from the RVOT and inferior RV wall, and short NSVT runs (≤14 beats). PVS provoked a fast, sustained monomorphic VT (left bundle branch block morphology, V5 transition, inferior axis). Bipolar and unipolar mapping revealed reduced voltage around the RVOT (Figure 8).

Although no sustained VT was inducible postablation, the patient was readmitted 1 day later with recurrent palpitations and long NSVT runs (≤30 beats). Repeat EAM induced 3 VT morphologies, including the one observed in procedure 1. A stable VT (cycle length: 395 ms, left bundle branch block, V5 transition, inferior axis) showed a pseudo–focal activation pattern consistent with an epicardial re-entry circuit. Ablation targeted the endocardial exit and best pace-map sites.

Despite beta-blocker (10 mg) and flecainide (200 mg), sustained VT and NSVT recurred, prompting epicardial ablation. PVS again induced the same VT, with the full circuit localized to the epicardial RVOT. Bipolar mapping demonstrated extensive patchy low-amplitude areas without late or decremental potentials. After epicardial ablation, no further arrhythmias were observed.

Diagnosis and management

The combination of recurrent symptoms, complex VAs, and abnormal EAM findings in this symptomatic athlete supports the diagnosis of underlying arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. After epicardial ablation, all VAs ceased, and no further ectopy was documented during follow-up, including during maximal exercise testing. Given the complete arrhythmic suppression, implantation of an ICD was deferred, and an implantable loop recorder was placed. The athlete was advised to permanently refrain from competitive sports and to continue beta-blocker therapy for secondary prevention. Genetic testing is pending and may influence future management.

Discussion

This case series illustrates the diagnostic and management challenges encountered in highly trained endurance athletes presenting with complex VAs despite normal conventional imaging. A summary of the characteristics of the included athletes is presented in Table 1. All 3 athletes reported palpitations or abrupt heart rate “spikes,” and exhibited ventricular ectopy originating from the RVOT region. A further common element was the tendency for greater epicardial substrate, with all cases requiring progression to epicardial ablation for arrhythmia control. While exercise-induced NSVT is often considered benign in athletes,2 a subset of individuals may harbor an arrhythmogenic substrate that predisposes them to life-threatening arrhythmias.3 Distinguishing physiological athletic remodeling from early or concealed arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy therefore remains a critical clinical challenge.

In all 3 cases, structural evaluation with TTE and CMR was unremarkable. Although gadolinium-enhanced CMR is the gold standard for noninvasive myocardial tissue characterization,4 subtle or localized RV abnormalities may fall below its spatial resolution. Prior work has shown that CMR may underestimate early or small-volume RV scar,5 and that high-resolution late gadolinium enhancement imaging can occasionally uncover abnormalities even when task force criteria are not met.6 High-resolution late gadolinium enhancement was not systematically available in our cohort, which represents a limitation. Nevertheless, the combination of persistent symptoms and a high arrhythmic burden despite normal CMR findings prompted more detailed electrophysiological evaluation.

This limitation underscores the potential complementary role of EAM in detecting subtle myocardial abnormalities that may elude conventional imaging.7 EAM revealed discrete to diffuse low-voltage abnormalities across the RV in all 3 athletes, extending beyond the RVOT to the anterior and posterolateral RV, the inferior RV wall, the apex, and the tricuspid annulus. These findings provided the first objective substrate correlating with the complex VAs. The broader distribution of abnormalities suggests a process beyond isolated RVOT tachycardia and raises the possibility of early arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy.

A further common feature was the long-standing exposure to high-intensity endurance exercise, with ≥15 hours of training per week beginning in early adolescence and continuing for 5 to 10 years. Whether this exposure accelerated a pre-existing arrhythmogenic substrate, unmasked an underlying genetic susceptibility, or is unrelated to the observed myocardial abnormalities cannot be determined from this case series.

Interpretation of EAM findings requires caution, as normative data on voltage patterns in endurance athletes without arrhythmias are limited, and subtle voltage abnormalities may reflect benign remodeling. To mitigate overinterpretation, all cases underwent multidisciplinary review involving electrophysiology, cardiogenetics, radiology, and sports cardiology, ensuring appropriate indication and contextualized interpretation of the mapping findings.

Management decisions similarly required individualized assessment. In athlete 3, sustained arrhythmic suppression after epicardial ablation allowed deferral of ICD implantation, highlighting the importance of longitudinal evaluation, including maximal exercise testing to confirm electrical stability. Genetic testing did not reveal pathogenic variants in athletes 1 and 2, and testing in athlete 3 is pending. These findings align with prior reports demonstrating a relatively low genetic yield in endurance athletes with complex VAs.8

Diagnostic Workflow Summary

Three highly trained cyclists presented with palpitations and heart rate “spikes.” Despite normal conventional imaging (TTE, CMR), symptoms and ventricular arrhythmias persisted, prompting electroanatomical mapping, which revealed concealed arrhythmogenic substrates. The combination of clinical findings and mapping results ultimately led to a diagnosis of ACM in all 3 athletes, underscoring diagnostic gaps in current ACM criteria. ACM = arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy; CMR = cardiac magnetic resonance; HRM = heart rate monitor; PVB = premature ventricular beat; SCA = sudden cardiac arrest; TTE = transthoracic echocardiography; VA = ventricular arrhythmia.

Together, these cases emphasize that, in a small subset of endurance athletes, symptoms and complex VAs may reflect early or concealed arrhythmogenic remodeling that remains undetectable by standard imaging. When used selectively and interpreted within a multidisciplinary framework, EAM can provide critical diagnostic information and guide management.9 Larger studies are needed to clarify the prevalence and significance of subtle voltage abnormalities in athletes, to disentangle the interplay between training load and arrhythmogenic susceptibility, and to refine the diagnostic criteria for this challenging population.
