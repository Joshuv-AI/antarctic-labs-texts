Work environment of nurses and technicians in Brazilian Intensive Care Units during the COVID-19 pandemic: a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Liana Amorim Corrêa Trotte, José Luís Guedes Dos Santos, Marluci Andrade Conceição Stipp, Graciele Oroski Paes, Gabriela Marcellino De Melo Lanzoni, Etiane Oliveira Freitas, Silviamar Camponogara, Danielle Saraiva Tuma Dos Reis, Alexandre Pazetto Balsanelli, Maria Gefé da Rosa Mesquita, Fernando Henrique Antunes Menegon, Alacoque Lorenzini Erdmann, Allison Squires
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFavorable nursing work environments are associated with better outcomes and well-being, yet the COVID-19 pandemic intensified workload and stress in intensive care settings. This exploratory descriptive study examined associations between sociodemographic and work-related factors and nursing work environment scores among registered nurses and nursing technicians working in exclusive COVID-19 ICUs in 10 public university hospitals.MethodsAn exploratory descriptive multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between April and December 2021. The Portuguese version of the Practice Environment Scale of the Nursing Work Index (PES-NWI) was administered, along with sociodemographic and work-related items, which were analyzed as potential explanatory factors of work-environment perceptions. Data were analyzed using descriptive and comparative statistics (t-tests, Mann–Whitney tests, χ² tests). Reliability was assessed with Cronbach’s α.ResultsOf 996 eligible professionals, 431 responded (43.3%): 194 nurses (45%) and 237 nurse technicians (55%). Participants were predominantly female (78%) with a mean age of 40.9 ± 9.1 years. Technicians were older (42.3 ± 9.8) than nurses (39.2 ± 8.0; p < 0.001). Race/color distributions differed (White: nurses 58.8% vs. technicians 41.8%; Black: 37.6% vs. 57.4%; p < 0.001). Most received institutional COVID-19 training (80%) and reported feeling protected at work (71.7%). PES-NWI internal consistency was excellent (total α = 0.94). All subscale means exceeded 2.5; technicians rated four subscales— participation in hospital affairs, foundations for quality, staffing/resources, and nurse/physician relations —higher than nurses did, while leadership support showed no difference. The total PES-NWI score was higher for technicians (2.98 ± 0.60 vs. 2.83 ± 0.58; p = 0.013).ConclusionsWork environments in COVID-19 ICUs of Brazilian public university hospitals were generally perceived as favorable. Differences between categories likely reflect variations in job roles and expectations rather than causal effects. As an exploratory descriptive study, findings should be interpreted as associative and hypothesis-generating, supporting future research on equity, management practices, and workforce well-being in critical care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14309-4.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic had a significant impact on the nursing workforce worldwide. Studies report that, although nurses played and continue to play a central role in the pandemic response, they experienced uncertainty, fear, psychological distress, and emotional exhaustion [1–4], as well as anxiety and sleep disorders [5, 6].

In Brazil, the first confirmed case occurred in February 2020, with São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro as the most affected cities at the onset [7]. The country faced three waves of mortality: the first between February and July 2020; the second, longer and more lethal, between November 2020 and April 2021; and the third, between December 2021 and May 2022 [8]. By the end of the pandemic, Brazil had the second-highest mortality rate worldwide.

As part of the emergency response, hospitals rapidly expanded their capacity, particularly in ICUs. It is noteworthy that, in addition to this measure, not only structural adjustments were made in Brazil and worldwide, but also the introduction of emergency teams prepared for the pandemic, specific clinical protocols for COVID-19, separation of workflows, training on the use of personal protective equipment, and mental health support programs [9–12].

Nonetheless, the shortage of experienced nurses and nursing technicians led to the hiring of professionals without prior ICU expertise. This incorporation of inexperienced professionals, who cared for highly complex patients may have resulted in elevated mortality, which subsequently raised concerns about the quality of care and nursing practice environments [13]. Evidence indicates that less experienced nurses are more likely to miss critical changes in patients’ conditions, while their unfamiliarity with ICU routines can lead to hypervigilance and increased occupational stress [14–16].

The structural characteristics of the Brazilian nursing workforce also shaped the pandemic response. Regulated by Law No. 7.498/1986, nursing is organized into two main categories: registered nurses, who receive university-level education and are responsible for care management, clinical decision-making, team coordination, and often teaching and research, and nursing technicians, trained at the secondary level, who deliver intermediate-complexity procedures under nurses’ supervision [17]. In Brazil, nurses who work in intensive care units are usually specialists in intensive care, although this is not a legal requirement. During the pandemic, this measure, previously implemented by hospitals, had to be relaxed due to the necessary rapid expansion of ICU capacity and the shortage of specialized personnel.

The nursing work environment is defined by organizational attributes that sustain professional practice, including participation in institutional decision-making, team composition, and resource availability [18, 19]. The ICU environment itself poses inherent challenges. The high complexity of patients demands advanced knowledge of pathophysiology, mastery of sophisticated technologies, and emotional intelligence to manage dynamic interactions with physicians, multidisciplinary colleagues, and families. These factors contribute to overlapping physical and mental workloads [20].

In this regard, the American Association of Critical-Care Nurses (AACN) has emphasized the importance of healthy work environments since 2001, establishing six standards: skilled communication, true collaboration, effective decision-making, adequate staffing, meaningful recognition, and authentic leadership [18]. Studies confirm that favorable environments, adequate staffing, and workloads compatible with patient needs are associated with lower mortality, shorter hospital stays, higher patient satisfaction, and reduced nurse exhaustion [21–23]. The Brazilian critical care nursing community has also adopted these principles, adapting their implementation to local contexts and resources.

Evidence consistently shows that trust, mutual respect, and collaboration among healthcare teams strongly contribute to care quality, particularly in crises [9]. Meta-analyses further demonstrate that favorable nursing practice environments are associated with better safety and quality-of-care outcomes, as perceived by both nurses and patients [24, 25]. Key factors identified include effective communication between nurses and physicians, strong team relationships, and managerial support [26–29].

While the benefits of healthy work environments for patient safety and nurse well-being are well established [23, 25, 30], the pandemic added unprecedented strain. Reports from different countries highlighted negative impacts on ICU nurses, including excessive workloads, staff shortages, and compromised safety routines [9, 11, 31, 32].

In Brazil, these pressures were compounded by structural vulnerabilities that led to growing concerns about how the rapid expansion of ICUs during the pandemic was affecting the professional environment, nurses’ and nursing technicians’ performance, and the quality of care. In this context, it is crucial to analyze how Brazilian nurses and nursing technicians, who have different roles, evaluated their practical environment in the ICUs dedicated to COVID-19, considering the impact on patient safety and work organization during a global health emergency.

Method

Aim

To examine associations between sociodemographic and work-related factors and nursing work environment scores among registered nurses and nursing technicians working in exclusive COVID-19 ICUs in 10 public university hospitals.

Design & setting

An exploratory, descriptive, cross-sectional multicenter study was conducted in 10 Brazilian public university hospitals, ensuring representation of all regions. (two hospitals per region: Professor Polydoro Ernani de São Thiago University Hospital; University Hospital of Santa Maria; São Paulo Hospital; Clementino Fraga Filho University Hospital; Onofre Lopes University Hospital; Professor Edgard Santos University Hospital; João de Barros Barreto University Hospital; Getúlio Vargas University Hospital; Maria Aparecida Pedrossian University Hospital; Júlio Müller University Hospital).

University hospitals serve as hubs for training healthcare professionals and advancing technology within the Brazilian healthcare sector. They offer free services to the population and operate under the direct auspices of the Ministry of Education, facilitating ongoing development and refinement of technical protocols across various medical conditions. These hospitals exhibit significant diversity in their infrastructure, technological capabilities, and scope of care. Each one holds a prominent position within the community it serves.

Sample

Nurses and nursing technicians working in intensive care units during the study period were eligible to participate. The inclusion criteria were established as follows: Participants had to be nursing professionals with at least three months of experience in the direct care of patients admitted to the ICU due to COVID-19. Nursing professionals who were on leave from their work during the data collection period were excluded. Across the 10 hospitals and ICUs, 312 nurses and 684 nursing technicians were eligible to participate in the study.

The umbrella project’s sample size calculation (WINPEPI; proportion estimation) applied to the broader study. For the present article, which compares mean PES-NWI scores (total and subscales) between registered nurses and nursing technicians, we conducted a post-hoc detectable effect size (MDE) calculation for two independent means. With α = 0.05 and 80% power, the achieved group sizes (n = 194 and n = 237) provide sensitivity to detect a standardized mean difference of approximately d = 0.27, corresponding to ≈ 0.16–0.21 points on the PES-NWI assuming SD ≈ 0.60–0.80 (as observed).

Variables

Primary outcomes were the PES-NWI total and subscale scores. The main explanatory variable was professional category (registered nurse vs. nursing technician). Consistent with the exploratory descriptive design, sociodemographic and labor variables were examined as potential explanatory correlates rather than pre-specified hypotheses.

Sociodemographic and labor variables

Sociodemographic and labor data included biological sex, age, race/color, time working in direct care for patients with COVID-19, years of professional experience, institutional tenure (years), contract type, weekly workload in the unit, presence of other employment, institutional training on COVID-19, perceived protection against COVID-19 in the workplace, staffing adequacy, adequacy of material/technological resources, job satisfaction, intention to leave within one year, and perceived patient safety.

Job dissatisfaction was measured by dichotomizing responses to the survey question “Overall, how satisfied are you with your job?” to contrast nurses who were ‘very/moderately dissatisfied’ and ‘very/moderately satisfied’. Intention to leave was measured by responses to the question: ‘Do you plan to be with your current employer one year from now?’. The level of patient safety in relation to the work environment was evaluated by responses to the question “How would you rate your work environment in terms of patient safety?”. The last two questions were answered on a scale from 0 to 10. These variables were analyzed descriptively and in pre-specified bivariate comparisons as potential explanatory correlates of work-environment perceptions; no causal inferences were intended. Data were collected using a study-specific form developed from prior research [33, 34]. (see Supplementary Files).

Nursing work environment

The nursing work environment was assessed using the Practice Environment Scale of Nursing Work Index (PES-NWI), originally derived from the Nursing Work Index-Revised (NWI-R) [35]. The instrument comprises 31 items distributed into five subscales: nurse participation in hospital affairs (9 items); nursing foundations for quality of care (10 items); nurse manager ability, leadership, and support (5 items); staffing and resource adequacy (4 items); and collegial nurse–physician relations (3 items).

The PES-NWI has demonstrated reliability and validity in multiple clinical settings and has been applied in different countries [36, 37]. In Brazil, the instrument was psychometrically validated for registered nurses in 2017 [38] and subsequently adapted and validated for nursing technicians in 2019 [39].

The Brazilian version of the PES-NWI has demonstrated strong psychometric properties. Internal consistency for the total scale was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.90), and the subscales showed good reliability (α ranging from 0.76 to 0.87). Composite reliability (CR) values ranged from 0.86 to 0.90, further confirming the instrument’s internal consistency. Regarding construct validity, confirmatory factor analyses indicated that all subscales presented adequate factor loadings (> 0.50), and the minimum average variance extracted (AVE) reached 0.50, supporting satisfactory convergent validity [39].

The PES-NWI uses a 4-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree) to measure the extent to which organizational attributes of the work environment support professional nursing practice and the provision of high-quality care. Mean scores above the midpoint of 2.5 indicate a favorable practice environment, while those at or below 2.5 are considered unfavorable [35]. This cutoff criterion is based on specific prior studies [36–39].

Data collection

Data were collected by convenience sampling between April and December 2021. Potential participants were informed about the study by department leaders and were invited to enter and then given access links to the online questionnaire. All participants (nurses and nursing technicians) were asked to complete the questionnaires through the online research platform “Google forms”. Participants could complete the questionnaire on a computer or a smartphone. When accessing the website, participants were first directed to a page containing information about the study’s purpose, anonymity, and confidentiality. Then, they had access to the informed consent form. After reading it, if they agreed to participate, they clicked the “affirmative” option. Then they were directed to the data collection page.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Given the exploratory descriptive design, we summarized distributions and conducted pre-specified bivariate comparisons across professional categories. Categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies; continuous variables as mean±standard deviation (if normally distributed) or median [interquartile range] otherwise. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons used independent-samples t tests (normal distributions) or Mann–Whitney tests (non-normal); categorical variables used chi-square tests. Two-sided α = 0.05 was used for all tests. Internal consistency of the PES-NWI (total and subscales) was assessed with Cronbach’s alpha.

To account for the multicenter design, multilevel linear models (random intercepts for hospital) were estimated with PES-NWI total as the dependent variable and professional category as the primary explanatory variable. Exploratory multivariable linear regressions were also conducted using the same dependent variable: Model 1 adjusted for sex, age, and years of professional experience; Model 2 additionally adjusted for race/color.

Results

Sociodemographic and work-related characteristics

Of 431 respondents (194 registered nurses [45%] and 237 nursing technicians [55%]), 78% were female and the mean age was 40.9 years (SD 9.1). Technicians were older than nurses (42.3 vs. 39.2 years; p < 0.001). Self-declared race/color differed across categories, with a higher proportion of Whites among nurses and Blacks among technicians (p < 0.001). Across participating public university hospitals, 996 eligible participants were invited, and 431 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 43.3%. Most participants reported COVID-19 training (80%) and feeling protected at work (71.7%), and 84.7% were satisfied or very satisfied with their job. Groups disagreed about staffing adequacy (RNs: 65.3% vs. technicians: 53.8%; p = 0.023). All other descriptive details are shown in Table 1.

Comparison of the work environment between groups

All PES-NWI subscale means exceeded 2.5. Technicians reported higher scores on four subscales —participation in hospital affairs, foundations for quality, staffing/resources, and nurse–physician relations —while manager support did not differ. The total PES-NWI score was also higher among technicians (2.98 ± 0.60) than nurses (2.83 ± 0.58; p = 0.013). The highest item means (≈ 3.3–3.4) were for working with clinically competent nurses, writing and updating care plans for all patients, positive nurse–physician relations, and supportive unit management. Multilevel analysis of factors associated with the PES-NWI total score

The results of the multilevel linear regression models are summarized below, with full model specifications provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. The null model, which included only a random intercept for hospital, indicated that 17.1% of the total variance in PES-NWI total scores was attributable to differences between hospitals, supporting the use of a multilevel modeling approach.

In Models I and II, which included professional category, sex, age, years of professional experience, and race/color as fixed effects, age and years of professional experience were independently associated with PES-NWI total scores. Increasing age was associated with higher scores (B = 0.019; 95% CI: 0.013–0.024; p < 0.001), while greater professional experience was associated with lower scores (B = − 0.015; 95% CI: −0.021 to − 0.009; p < 0.001), holding other variables constant.

For sex, respondents who selected “prefer not to declare” had significantly lower PES-NWI scores compared with the reference category (female) (B = − 0.465; 95% CI: −0.751 to − 0.178; p = 0.002). No statistically significant differences were observed between men and women in either model.

Professional category (registered nurse vs. nursing technician) was not significantly associated with PES-NWI total scores after adjustment in either Model I (p = 0.751) or Model II (p = 0.852).

In Model II, race/color was associated with PES-NWI scores, with participants classified as “Other” (including Asian and Indigenous) reporting higher scores compared with the reference category (White) (B = 0.110; 95% CI: 0.038–0.182; p = 0.003), while no statistically significant difference was observed between Black and White participants.

Discussion

This study is one of the first assessments of the nursing practice environment in intensive care units dedicated solely to the care of COVID-19 patients in Brazil. The results suggest a favorable work environment for nurses working in public hospitals, despite the recognized challenges of Brazil’s public system. By analyzing the research results from the characteristics of the sample to the questionnaire used, the literature provides interesting insights that may be useful for the development and evaluation of nursing management in university hospitals.

Mean scores on the PES-NWI indicated that the overall nursing practice environment is perceived as favorable, even amid pressures created by the health crisis. Although this finding suggests a positive outlook, it is important to emphasize that characterizing a healthy environment is not limited to emergency measures. Instead, it depends on structuring elements such as high-quality communication, effective team collaboration, and reliable leadership [18, 23, 24].

The better ratings may be related to the combined adoption of care protocols, expanded ICU capacity, strengthened managerial support, and clearer communication routines reported by university hospitals during the pandemic [10, 40, 41]. Another perspective is that successive waves of cases accelerated organizational learning and favored the adaptation of workflows and the redefinition of expectations by teams, similar to what was reported in Portugal after the third wave [42].

On the other hand, caution is needed when interpreting these results. Taken together, the link with resource constraints and long-standing precarious conditions indicates limited generalizability to similar public university settings and may not reflect sustained changes over time [43–47].

Most respondents reporting being satisfied with their work is a finding that is consistent with evidence that a more developed practice environment is associated with lower levels of burnout and higher staff retention [21, 23, 30, 48–50]. Thus, self-reported satisfaction may be consistent with a dynamic equilibrium under specific managerial practices, without demonstrating persistence over time or resolution of structural deficits [1, 5, 51]. A plausible explanation for this coexistence is that professional purpose and symbolic recognition served as temporary protective factors that could cushion daily pressures, yet did not address deeper structural deficits [9, 52]. Satisfaction should therefore not be taken as evidence that risks to patient well-being or safety have been eliminated; instead, it may reflect an unstable equilibrium, maintained by specific management practices during persistent constraints.

Some institutional measures were associated with this scenario, with training and preparedness aligned with higher assessments of practice fundamentals and perceived safety [10, 53, 54]. Visible leadership and management support were consistent with frequent communication and at-the-bedside problem solving [18, 21, 29]. The expansion of bed capacity and the introduction of standardized protocols may have increased workload; however, in conjunction with effective collaboration and reliable information flow, these measures have already been put in place to maintain perceived quality of care [9, 10, 55].

Professional recognition and interprofessional collaboration have been associated in prior studies with higher satisfaction and retention, which is consistent with participants’ ratings in our sample [21, 23, 24]. On the other hand, the negative assessment of perceived insufficient supplies reveals a significant weakness; this finding is consistent with other studies that identified material and technical deficiencies as critical barriers to quality of care in the context of the pandemic [12, 44, 56].

Perceptions also varied between professional groups. Technicians rated four of the five subscales (participation in hospital affairs, foundations for quality of care, availability of staff/resources, and relationships between nurses and physicians) more positively. There was no significant difference in the leadership/management support domain, whereas nurses reported more adequate staffing of their teams. Although these differences were found, it is not possible to establish causal relationships from the data. One possible interpretation, supported by the literature, is that expectations and the characteristics of each role shape the way professionals perceive the environment. Nurses, who are responsible for coordinating care and mediating interprofessional processes, tend to be more sensitive to structural and organizational gaps [18, 23]. Technicians, who focus on direct patient care, often experience more peer support and collaborate with physicians daily, which may explain the more favorable ratings on these subscales [24, 28, 29].

Differences in age and length of service (with nursing technicians being older on average) may have influenced perceptions of organizational climate and tolerance for fluctuations in workload [23, 25]. As the pandemic progressed, training and clinical procedures became more standardized [40, 41]. In this context, technicians may have perceived faster progress in operational aspects, such as routinization of practices. At the same time, nurses remained more attentive to systemic constraints, such as staffing and interface with management [21, 25, 30].

Nonetheless, it is important to emphasize that more precarious practice environments have been associated in the literature with a higher risk of adverse events and higher levels of burnout [20, 22, 24]. Therefore, differences in perceptions between professional groups should not be interpreted as merely complementary views, but rather as indicators of misaligned needs and priorities that require management attention.

The sample analyzed reflects both the feminization of nursing and differences in self-reported ethnicity/color between professional categories, with a higher proportion of nurses identifying as White and a higher proportion of technicians identifying as Black. Although the results suggest a possible relationship between hierarchical position and socio-demographic profile, the study did not subjectively examine socio-structural dimensions. One likely interpretation is that the differences in perceptions are related to occupational stratification by sex and race/color, a phenomenon historically associated with unequal access to resources, recognition, and career advancement opportunities in diverse societies [45–47]. These patterns should be interpreted in light of possible selection effects of voluntary participation in university hospitals and a moderate response rate, which may bias estimates upward toward more favorable work-environment perceptions relative to less resourced contexts.

To advance this analysis, future studies should include psychosocial variables, such as social support, alongside experiences of discrimination and indicators of workplace equity to determine whether these factors influence the observed differences across professional categories.

Limitations

This study has several limitations, including a cross-sectional design that precludes causal inference and the convenience sampling of 10 university hospitals, which limits generalizability outside of these types of facilities. Participation was voluntary and restricted to university hospitals; staff from better-resourced/supportive settings may be overrepresented, shifting perceptions toward a more favorable environment. The overall response rate was 43.3% and incomplete sampling frames may have introduced nonresponse bias, likely favoring respondents in more stable organizational conditions; these factors constrain generalizability beyond similar settings.

Data were collected after the acute pandemic phase and are therefore susceptible to recall and social desirability effects. Psychosocial constructs relevant to the pandemic (stress, social support, burnout) were not measured, and several outcomes (job satisfaction, intention to leave, patient safety) were based on unvalidated measures, highlighting the need for cultural and psychometric validation. Cross-cultural variability in the factor structure of the PES-NWI may also affect international comparability [36, 37].

Implications

From a practical standpoint, structured feedback mechanisms, meaningful recognition, and clear development trajectories for both professional and managerial roles may help sustain job satisfaction beyond crisis periods [21, 52]. Measures that increase contractual transparency, reduce precarious employment, and align incentives with care complexity have the potential to strengthen retention and improve organizational climate [45–47, 57]. Maintaining briefing/debriefing routines and ensuring active managerial presence during and after demand peaks can help preserve communication gains associated with better practice-environment ratings [18, 29].

Future research should use longitudinal, multicenter designs with probability sampling, document response rates, include validated psychosocial scales, link surveys to objective indicators (e.g., adverse events, absenteeism, turnover), and plan model specifications and sample sizes to allow for adjusted estimates.

Conclusion

This study has shown that in 10 Brazilian public university hospital ICUs dedicated to COVID-19 care, nurses perceived the practice environment as generally favorable on the PES-NWI. Higher ratings were observed for clinical competence, updated care plans, collegial nurse physician relations, and visible managerial support. These patterns may be associated with organizational features typical of university hospitals (e.g., standardized protocols and closer management clinical interface) and are best interpreted within similar settings. Overall, the findings highlight actionable priorities protect staffing/resources, sustain interprofessional coordination, and maintain manager presence at the point of care while avoiding causal interpretation.
