Low risk of severe COVID-19 in vaccinated people with multiple sclerosis: a nationwide Norwegian study
Authors: Tilde Harridsleff Rasmussen, Stine Schikora-Rustad, Åslaug Rudjord Lorentzen, The Trung Tran, Mathias H Øverås, Kjell-Morten Myhr, Stig Wergeland, Jan Aarseth, Lars Bø, Tone Berge, Einar August Høgestøl, Hanne F Harbo, Anne Therese Tveter, Kristin Kaasen Jørgensen, Celiné Cunen, Øivind Torkildsen, Trygve Holmøy, Elisabeth Gulowsen Celius, John Torgils Vaage, Gro Owren Nygaard, Fridtjof Lund-Johansen, Marton König
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to assess antibody responses and COVID-19 outcomes after vaccination in people with multiple sclerosis (pwMS) receiving disease-modifying therapies.MethodsNevroVAX is a Norwegian multicentre cohort study including 3559 pwMS and 449 healthy controls (HCs) who received at least one dose of BNT162b2 (Pfizer-BioNTech), mRNA-1273 (Moderna) or ChAdOx1 (AstraZeneca/Oxford) between January 2021 and December 2022. Data from records, registries, questionnaires and blood samples collected June 2021–November 2023 were analysed. Primary outcomes were humoral immune responses assessed by seroconversion (anti-spike and anti-receptor-binding domain (RBD) IgG ≥5 binding antibody units/mL). Secondary outcomes included quantitative anti-RBD IgG levels, breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection, COVID-19-related hospitalisation and death. Associations with breakthrough infection were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression.ResultsAmong 3559 pwMS, 1201 received anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies and 324 sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor (S1PR) modulators. After three vaccine doses, seroconversion was observed in 43.0% of anti-CD20-treated patients and 48.4% of S1PR-treated patients. In multivariable analysis, seroconversion was associated with lower risk of breakthrough infection (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.78). During follow-up, 58 pwMS (1.6%) were hospitalised for COVID-19; 4 (0.1%) required non-invasive ventilatory support, no patients required invasive ventilation and no deaths occurred.ConclusionsHumoral vaccine responses were impaired in pwMS receiving anti-CD20 or S1PR therapies, but severe COVID-19 was rare. These findings support continued vaccination programmes and tailored protective measures for immunomodulated populations.Keywords: MULTIPLE SCLEROSIS, COVID-19, HEALTH POLICY & PRACTICE, IMMUNOLOGY

Introduction

People with multiple sclerosis (pwMS) have an increased risk of infections compared with the general population, a vulnerability further amplified by disease-modifying therapies (DMTs).1 This is particularly pronounced with B-cell-depleting therapies such as anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies (anti-CD20 mAbs), which in pwMS have been associated with higher infection rates and more severe infectious outcomes.2 3

DMTs vary in their mode of action. High-efficacy treatments in MS include anti-CD20 mAbs (rituximab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab), natalizumab, alemtuzumab, cladribine and sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor (S1PR) modulators.4 5 Anti-CD20 mAbs exert their effect by depleting CD20-expressing B cells, thereby reducing B cell-mediated inflammation and autoimmunity. S1PR modulators, such as fingolimod, siponimod and ozanimod, prevent lymphocyte egress from lymphoid tissue, leading to peripheral lymphopenia and reduced central nervous system (CNS) infiltration.6 Whereas natalizumab blocks leucocyte migration into the CNS via α4-integrin antagonism, cladribine and alemtuzumab induce lymphocyte depletion through cytotoxic mechanisms.6

The arrival of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines in late 2020 offered hope of protection but also raised major concerns for pwMS on immunotherapy. Early immune correlates analyses from pivotal vaccine trials showed that protection against symptomatic COVID-19 was strongly associated with antibody levels, with little attention given to cellular immunity.7 8 This created a widespread alarm: if antibodies were required for protection, then persons unable to mount them would remain vulnerable. COVID-19 vaccination in pwMS therefore represented a critical natural experiment, testing whether T-cell responses could compensate when humoral immunity was absent.

Early reports confirmed the basis for this concern, showing that pwMS on anti-CD20 therapies or S1PR modulators often failed to mount antibody responses after vaccination, leading to speculation that vaccination might provide little or no benefit in these groups.9 We and others confirmed markedly reduced antibody responses in pwMS on anti-CD20 mAbs and S1PR modulators and demonstrated that additional vaccine doses only partly restored immunity.10 11 Similar findings were reported in patients with lymphoma treated with rituximab, who showed blunted antibody responses but often retained T-cell responses.12 Subsequent analyses clarified that SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination elicit both B-cell-mediated antibody production and T-cell-mediated immunity, with the latter contributing particularly to protection against severe disease. In later work from this same cohort, we demonstrated that T-cell responses to SARS-CoV-2 vaccination were generally preserved even in pwMS receiving immunomodulatory treatments.13

Norway’s universal health coverage and comprehensive national health registries, linked through personal identifiers,14 provide a unique framework for large-scale research. Within this system, the NevroVAX cohort was established as a registry-linked study of pwMS, enabling integration of vaccination records, serological data and clinical outcomes.

The aim of this study was to evaluate antibody responses, breakthrough infections and severe COVID-19 outcomes in vaccinated pwMS, stratified by DMT and to compare these findings with a healthy control (HC) group. The study also examined how timing of sample collection and vaccine dose number influenced observed antibody responses.

Methods

Inclusion

Between June 2021 and December 2023, pwMS who had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine were invited to participate in the NevroVAX study (online supplemental figure S1). Recruitment occurred via website advertisements and direct invitations from Oslo University Hospital and the Norwegian MS Registry and Biobank (NMSRB).

HCs were recruited from a parallel study on vaccine responses in immunosuppressed individuals with rheumatic or gastrointestinal conditions.15 HCs were healthcare workers from hospitals in the Oslo area with similar vaccination schedules and served as a reference group for antibody and breakthrough infection analyses.

Data sources and definitions

Self-reported variables included comorbidities, lifestyle factors, height and weight. HC data included age, sex and antibody levels. Serum samples were analysed for antibodies against the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein and receptor-binding domain (RBD), with sampling date recorded.

Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) scores and MS treatment dates were obtained from the NMSRB. Vaccine type, date and dose number were retrieved from the Norwegian Immunisation Registry. PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 cases were identified through the Norwegian Surveillance System for Communicable Diseases, which recorded all positive cases until December 2021; thereafter, only cases linked to outpatient or hospital care were reported.16

COVID-19 diagnoses, hospitalisations, outpatient visits, ventilation procedures and deaths were identified through the Norwegian Patient Registry (NPR) and Cause of Death Registry using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes for MS (G35) and COVID-19–related conditions (U07.1, U07.2, U09, U10). Procedures were identified using Norwegian Classification of Medical Procedures (NCMP), Nordic Medico-Statistical Committee Classification of Surgical Procedures (NCSP), and Norwegian Classification of Radiological Procedures (NCRP) codes GXAV20, GXAV01, GXGX00 and GXAV24. Deaths included both direct and indirect causes. The timing and overlap of data sources and observation periods are shown in online supplemental figure S2.

PwMS were grouped by self-reported current DMT: anti-CD20 mAbs (rituximab, ocrelizumab, ofatumumab), S1PR modulators (fingolimod, ozanimod), other high-efficacy DMTs (natalizumab, alemtuzumab, cladribine) and other DMTs (glatiramer acetate, dimethyl fumarate, interferons, teriflunomide). Four participants with remote (>10 years prior) mitoxantrone exposure were retained in the ‘other DMTs’ category. Current DMT use was based on patient report without systematic verification against treatment dates; therefore, the ‘No current DMT’ group may have included individuals with prior DMT exposure.

Severe COVID-19 was defined as hospitalisation with a relevant ICD-10 code in NPR. Breakthrough infections were defined as PCR-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection occurring ≥14 days after the most recent vaccine dose.17

Vaccines

Participants received one or more of the following SARS-CoV-2 vaccines: BNT162b2 Comirnaty (Pfizer–BioNTech), mRNA-1273 Spikevax (Moderna), ChAdOx1 (AstraZeneca/Oxford) or the bivalent boosters Comirnaty BA.4–5 and Spikevax BA.4–5. ChAdOx1 was discontinued in Norway on 11 March 2021 due to rare but severe adverse events after approximately 6 weeks of use.18

Antibody measurement

Antibodies against the spike protein and RBD of the Wuhan-Hu-1 strain were measured using a validated multiplex bead-based flow cytometry assay.11 Binding antibody units per millilitre (BAU/mL) were calculated from median fluorescent intensity (MFI).

Seropositivity was defined as an MFI value of ≥5 for both the spike protein and RBD targets.19 Seroconversion was defined as BAU/mL ≥5 for both antigens at the same time point. All available samples were included, regardless of prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, reflecting both vaccine-induced and hybrid immunity.

To capture peak post-vaccination responses, only samples collected 10–50 days after a recorded vaccine dose were included. BAU/mL values were log₁₀-transformed for visualisation and regression analyses. Antibody responses were compared across DMT groups, vaccine dose number and disease group (pwMS vs HCs).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was vaccine-induced seroconversion. Secondary outcomes included antibody concentrations, SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection, COVID-19-related hospitalisation and severe disease.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.2920 and R V.4.3.3.21 Continuous variables are presented as means with SD or medians with IQR, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s χ² tests. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used for continuous variables across more than two DMT groups.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify independent predictors of breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection and COVID-19-related hospitalisation. Covariates included age at vaccination, sex, EDSS, DMT group, seroconversion status (binary), smoking, body mass index, vitamin D use and comorbidities. Overall predictors of breakthrough infection were assessed in a multivariable model including all vaccinated pwMS (online supplemental table S6). Dose-specific associations after the second, third and fourth vaccine doses were examined in separate multivariable models (online supplemental table S7).

Correlations between age at vaccination and antibody levels were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation, including all eligible postvaccination samples for the relevant dose. COVID-19-related hospitalisation was analysed using Firth penalised logistic regression owing to sparse events. Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs are reported. All tests were two-sided, and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. Adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied only for predefined pairwise comparisons of antibody levels between pwMS and HCs using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate method, as detailed in online supplemental table S4.

Results

Study population

Of 5496 consenting pwMS, serum samples and complete registry linkage were available for 3675 participants (71.5%). After excluding 116 participants enrolled in the hematopoetic stem cell treatment (HSCT) arm of the same study, 3559 were available for inclusion (online supplemental table S1). Key differences between treatment groups included sex, age, EDSS score and comorbidities. EDSS scores were available in 3150 pwMS (88.5%) and ranged from 0.0 to 9.0. Individuals with no current DMT were significantly older than the DMT-treated groups (median age 58.5 years; p<0.001). Vaccination data are summarised in table 1, showing that nearly all participants (99.9%) had received two vaccine doses and 95.2% had received three doses.

Immunogenicity

After the second SARS-CoV-2 vaccine dose, seroconversion was markedly reduced in pwMS receiving anti-CD20 mAbs or S1PR modulators (43.0% and 48.4%, respectively), compared with other high-efficacy DMTs, other DMTs and no current DMT (all ≥88%; p<0.001; online supplemental table S3). After the third dose, seroconversion improved but remained below 60% in the anti-CD20 (47.7%) and S1PR (58.8%) groups, while exceeding 97% in all others.

Antibody levels followed a similar pattern to seroconversion rates. After the second dose, median antibody levels were 1 BAU/mL (anti-CD20) and 4 BAU/mL (S1PR) compared with 6656 BAU/mL in HCs, and medians ranging from 3007 to 5243 BAU/mL in the other pwMS groups (online supplemental table S4). After the third dose, levels remained low in the anti-CD20 (1 BAU/mL) and S1PR (6 BAU/mL) groups, whereas the other groups had medians between 3779 and 6777 BAU/mL (online supplemental table S4, figure 1). Group-level differences were statistically significant across most vaccine doses (overall Kruskal-Wallis p<0.01), except for the sixth dose, where sample sizes were very small (online supplemental table S4). No association was observed between age and antibody levels in pwMS (ρ=0.00; p=0.98), while a weak inverse correlation was present in HCs (ρ = –0.14; p=0.02, online supplemental table S9).

Breakthrough infections

Breakthrough infections occurred in 22.3% of participants overall (793/3559) but were more frequent in pwMS receiving anti-CD20 mAbs (31.2%) or S1PR modulators (23.9%) than in those receiving other DMTs (14.9%) or no current DMT (13.0%). The rate in HCs was comparable to that in the overall cohort (22.7%) (table 1). The ‘other high-efficacy DMT’ group consisted predominantly of natalizumab and cladribine users, and subgroup effects were not assessed separately (online supplemental table S2).

Anti-CD20 mAbs carried the highest adjusted risk of breakthrough infection (OR 3.44, 95% CI 2.49 to 4.84), followed by other high-efficacy DMTs (OR 1.88, 95% CI 1.29 to 2.77), whereas S1PR modulators were not significantly associated with increased infection risk (OR 1.39, 95% CI 0.96 to 2.02). PwMS on no current treatment had reduced risk (OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.80). Seroconversion was independently associated with a reduced risk of breakthrough infection (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.78, p<0.001) (figure 2; online supplemental table S6). Dose-specific multivariable analyses for vaccine doses 2–4 yielded broadly similar associations (online supplemental table S7).

Other protective factors included older age (OR 0.98 per year, 95% CI 0.97 to 0.99), higher EDSS (OR 0.91 per point, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.95), vitamin D use (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.86) and smoking (OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.53) (all p<0.001, (figure 2, online supplemental table S6). Higher antibody levels after the second SARS-CoV-2 vaccine dose were associated with lower infection risk in pwMS (OR 0.76 per log₁₀ BAU/mL increase, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.82, p<0.001), but not in HCs (OR 1.14, 95% CI 0.59 to 2.41, p=0.72) (online supplemental table S8).

Low risk of severe COVID-19

In 2022, 58 pwMS (1.6%) were hospitalised with COVID-19, with the highest rates among those treated with anti-CD20 mAbs (3.3%), followed by pwMS without current DMT (2.1%) and those receiving S1PR modulators (0.9%); rates were substantially lower in other high-efficacy DMTs (0.2%) and other DMTs (0.5%) (table 2). In adjusted Firth logistic regression, higher EDSS was independently associated with increased hospitalisation risk (OR 1.33 per point, 95% CI 1.18 to 1.49; p<0.001). Anti-CD20 treatment conferred markedly increased odds of hospitalisation (OR 8.79, 95% CI 3.41 to 32.10; p<0.001), and pwMS without DMT also had higher odds (OR 3.30, 95% CI 1.05 to 13.30; p=0.041), whereas no significant associations were observed for S1PR modulators, other high-efficacy DMTs, age or sex (online supplemental table S5).

Discussion

Anti-CD20 mAbs and S1PR modulator therapies were associated with significantly lower antibody levels and lower seroconversion rates compared with both other pwMS and HCs, confirming earlier results.22,24 Antibody levels against SARS-CoV-2 spike and RBD in those receiving other DMTs were slightly lower than in HCs but otherwise preserved. Despite these differences, clinical outcomes were favourable: breakthrough infections were mostly mild, only 1.6% required hospitalisation and no COVID-19–related deaths or invasive ventilation occurred. Importantly, the category of other high-efficacy DMTs comprises treatments with heterogeneous mechanisms of action, and the observed association with breakthrough infection should therefore not be interpreted as uniform across all agents in this group.

These findings are notable given early pandemic expectations. Based on immune correlates from pivotal vaccine trials, antibody levels were initially assumed to be a key determinant of protection and early reports from pwMS receiving anti-CD20 therapy or S1PR modulators suggested absent or minimal antibody responses.9 23 24 This created widespread concern that these persons would remain unprotected despite vaccination. However, accumulating evidence indicates that the relationship between antibody responses and clinical protection is less direct than initially assumed.25 Our findings show that, despite profoundly reduced antibody responses in some treatment groups, clinical outcomes in vaccinated pwMS were generally mild, with very low hospitalisation rates and no deaths. This suggests that protection against severe COVID-19 is not solely dependent on antibody responses, and that cellular or other immune mechanisms can provide substantial protection even when humoral immunity is compromised.

Although early pandemic studies raised concerns about COVID-19 severity in pwMS, they were limited by data collected before vaccines were available.26 In our vaccinated cohort, overall infection rates in pwMS were comparable to those in the general population, but risks varied by treatment group. Multiple studies confirm increased breakthrough infection and increased risk of severe disease in pwMS on anti-CD20 mAbs, whereas data for other high-efficacy DMTs remain more heterogeneous.27,31

Higher EDSS scores and comorbidity burden have previously been identified as predictors of hospitalisation.32 In our cohort, higher EDSS scores were paradoxically associated with lower infection rates, likely reflecting reduced exposure among individuals with greater disability, while simultaneously representing the strongest independent predictor of hospitalisation. Anti-CD20 therapy was also associated with increased hospitalisation risk, whereas the association for no current DMT was weaker. Although these patterns mirrored unadjusted hospitalisation rates, the small number of severe cases resulted in wide CIs.

By mid-2022, only 27% of individuals in Norway were officially registered with a confirmed COVID-19 diagnosis, despite widespread seroprevalence in the adult population due to vaccination and/or prior infection.19 In a national survey from the same period, between 40% and 70% of Norwegian adults reported a prior positive COVID-19 test.33 This discrepancy suggests under-reporting of asymptomatic or mild cases, which may have influenced observed infection rates in our cohort. Notably, lower infection rates were observed in pwMS with no current DMT (15%) and those on other DMTs (14%) compared with HCs and the overall pwMS cohort (22–22.3%). These differences may reflect behavioural factors such as heightened self-isolation in higher-risk individuals. Conversely, increased care-seeking and testing among individuals aware of their immunosuppressed status may have resulted in higher detection rates in some treatment groups, as well as higher hospitalisation rates due to a low threshold among GPs for referring individuals receiving anti-CD20 therapy with symptomatic COVID-19.

Seroconversion was observed across all groups but remained incomplete in individuals receiving anti-CD20 mAbs or S1PR modulators, consistent with their markedly reduced antibody levels. In contrast, antibody responses in other pwMS groups were comparable with those in HCs. COVID-19 outcomes in MS are broadly similar to those reported in other autoimmune conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease and rheumatic diseases, where severe illness in vaccinated individuals remains uncommon despite immunosuppressive therapy.34 By contrast, populations with more profound immunosuppression, such as kidney transplant recipients, have shown substantially higher rates of mortality and intensive care admission following COVID-19.35

Our findings further support the concept that individuals treated with anti-CD20 mAbs may retain cellular immune responses despite impaired humoral immunity. In contrast, S1PR modulators reduce both antibody production and circulating T cells, although reduced peripheral T-cell counts may partly reflect lymphocyte sequestration rather than absent immunity.13 36 Notably, despite these immunological alterations, pwMS receiving S1PR modulators had a lower risk of breakthrough infection than those on antiCD20 mAbs, suggesting that residual effector T cell subsets or innate immune pathways may still contribute to clinical protection.37

Smoking was associated with a reduced risk of breakthrough infection after the second and third vaccine doses, resembling the so-called ‘smoker’s paradox’ described in COVID-19. The inverse associations observed for smoking, no current DMT and higher EDSS scores are more plausibly explained by behavioural or exposure-related factors rather than biological protection and should not be interpreted as evidence of a protective effect.38

Our study benefits from high-quality, national-level data of PCR-confirmed COVID-19 cases linked to hospitalisation outcomes, within a well-characterised longitudinal cohort spanning a broad range of disability levels. While only vaccinated individuals were included, which prevented direct comparison to unvaccinated pwMS, the very high vaccine uptake in Norway (91% of individuals aged ≥ 16 had received at least one dose by mid-July 2022)19 39 limits the utility of such a comparison. Limitations include possible under-reporting of mild infections and exclusion of hospital data from 2021. HCs were primarily healthcare workers and may have had higher occupational exposure to SARS-CoV-2. Differences in exposure patterns or smoking prevalence between HCs and pwMS may therefore influence comparisons of breakthrough infection rates. In addition, information on duration of current DMT use and prior DMT exposure was incomplete and could not be incorporated into multivariable analyses, which may have influenced estimates of infection risk.

In summary, this large, vaccinated cohort of pwMS experienced consistently mild COVID-19 outcomes, even among those receiving immunosuppressive therapies. Despite profoundly reduced antibody responses in individuals treated with anti-CD20 mAbs or S1PR modulators, severe disease was rare, indicating that vaccination provides meaningful clinical protection through immune mechanisms beyond humoral responses.
