RNAi targeting LMAN1-MCFD2 complex promotes anticoagulation in mice
Authors: Siqian Ma, Boyan Liu, Hong Du, Fei Yang, Jingjing Han, Xinqi Huang, Minyang Zhang, Shundong Ji, Miao Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombined deficiency of coagulation factor V (FV) and factor VIII (FVIII) is a rare bleeding disease caused by variants in either lectin mannose binding 1 (LMAN1) or multiple coagulation factor deficiency 2 (MCFD2) gene. Reducing the level of FVIII by inhibiting the LMAN1-MCFD2 complex may become a new anticoagulant approach. We aimed to find a new therapeutic option for anticoagulation by RNA interference (RNAi) targeting LMAN1 and MCFD2. siRNA sequences with cross-homology between mice and humans were designed based on LMAN1 or MCFD2 transcripts in NCBI and were screened with the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay. The optimal siRNAs were chemically modified and conjugated with three N-acetylgalactosamine molecules (GalNAc-siRNA), promoting their targeted delivery to the liver. The expression of LMAN1 and MCFD2 in cell lines or mice was examined by RT-qPCR and western blotting. For the mice administered with siRNA, we assessed their coagulation function by measuring APTT and the activity of FVIII factor. After administration, siRNAs GalNAc-LMAN1 and GalNAc-MCFD2 demonstrated effective and persistent LMAN1 and MCFD2 inhibition. 7 days after injection of 3mg/kg GalNAc-LMAN1, the LMAN1 mRNA levels reduced to 19.97% ± 3.78%. MCFD2 mRNA levels reduced to 32.22% ± 13.14% with injection of 3mg/kg GalNAc-MCFD2. After repeated administration, APTT was prolonged and the FVIII activity was remarkably decreased. The tail bleeding test of mice showed that the amount of bleeding in the treated group did not significantly increase compared with the control group. Our study confirms that therapy with RNAi targeting LMAN1-MCFD2 complex is effective and can be considered a viable option for anticoagulation drugs. Introduction

LMAN1 (lectin mannose binding protein (1) forms a complex with MCFD2 (multiple coagulation factor deficiency, (2) that facilitates the transport of coagulation factors V (FV) and VIII (FVIII) from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus via an endoplasmic reticulum Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) [1]. Mutations in LMAN1 or MCFD2 genes cause combined deficiency of FV and FVIII (F5F8D) [2, 3], a rare autosomal recessive bleeding disorder characterized by mild to moderate bleeding and coordinate reduction in plasma FV and FVIII levels [4].

However, according to literature reports, the degree of FV/FVIII levels in F5F8D patients caused by LMAN1/MCFD2 gene defects is usually between 5 and 30%, which is higher than the FVIII levels in typical moderate or severe hemophilia A patients [5–7]. Even in cases where homozygous LMAN1/MCFD2 gene mutations result in complete loss of LMAN1 or MCFD2 protein, the FVIII level in patients remains above 5%. Therefore, targeting LMAN1/MCFD2 to reduce FV/FVIII levels may be a safe and effective anticoagulant strategy. Since LMAN1/MCFD2 is only expressed in liver cells and not secreted outside, it is impossible to neutralize the LMAN1/MCFD2 proteins with antibodies [8, 9]. Using small molecule reagents or RNA molecules may be an achievable approach to reduce intracellular LMAN1/MCFD2 protein levels [10–13].

In this study, we first examined clinical manifestations of some F5F8D patients. We designed small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) specifically targeting LMAN1/MCFD2 mRNA based on human and mouse LMAN1/MCFD2 gene sequences. These siRNAs have undergone glycosylation modifications, enabling them to specifically enter liver cells. We characterized their pharmacological properties in human liver cell lines and mice, including the reduction of LMAN1/MCFD2 expression and the resulting changes of coagulation functions.

Materials, methods, and patients

Plasma preparation

Mouse blood was collected from orbital venous plexus or inferior vena cava, with 3.8% sodium citrate (1:9) added. The samples were then centrifuged at 5000g for 10 min to obtain platelet-poor plasma (PPP) which was stored at – 70 °C. Assay results with the combined plasma of WT mice was taken as standard for FVIII activity.

Synthesis of siRNAs

The siRNA sequences were designed based on transcripts in NCBI (LMAN1-Homo NM_005570, Lman1-Mouse NM_027400.3, MCFD2-Homo sapiens NM_139279.6, MCFD2-Mouse NM_176808). Then they were screened with the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay. The sense and antisense single strands of the optimal ones were chemically modified at selected sites with either 2′-O-methyl or 2′-F nucleosides, and sense strand was conjugated to an asialoglycoprotein receptor ligand derived from N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc), promoting targeted delivery to the liver. The GalNAc-siRNAs were synthesized with the help of the GenePharma Company (Suzhou, China).

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay and cell immunofluorescence assay

293T cells were purchased from cell bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). Fragments containing the binding sites of the siRNAs were synthesized and sub-cloned into the psiCHECK-2 plasmid (Promega, USA; C8021). Cells were seeded in 96-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well. After 24 h, cells were transfected with the siRNAs using lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA; 53,566) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reporter plasmids were transfected into the cells simultaneously. Luciferase activity was determined at 24 h post-transfection using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA; E2940). For tracking the GalNAc-siRNAs, HepaG2 cells (Procell, China, CL-0103) were transfected with FAM-labeled GalNAc-siRNAs and examined 24 h later using a confocal fluorescence microscope at a wavelength of 488nm.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR)

To detect the transcripts of LMAN1 or MCFD2, total RNA was extracted from mice liver or transfected HEPAG2 cells using TRIzol (BBI, China; B511311). The RNA was converted to cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan; RR036A) to amplify various partial fragment of LMAN1 or MCFD2 mRNA. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China; Q511-02) with the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Results were normalized with GAPDH RNA, and the fold change was determined using the 2-ΔΔCT method.

Western blotting

To assess the levels of LMAN1 and MCFD2, proteins were isolated from mouse livers using Protein Extraction kit (BBI, China; B510005). Total cellular lysates (30 mg/lane) were separated on 10% or 15% SDS-PAGE gels. The membranes were blocked and incubated overnight with primary antibodies, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies for 2 h. The target bands were visualized in an imaging analyzer, and the gray value of each band was quantified by an ImageJ software. The expression level of GAPDH was used as reference. Primary antibodies are as follows: anti-LMAN1 rabbit monoclonal (Cell Signaling Technology, USA; clone E2B6H), Anti-MCFD2 rabbit polyclonal (Absin, China, abs151162), Anti-FVIII mouse polyclonal (Abcam, USA, ab171825), anti-GAPDH antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, USA, clone 14C10). Secondary antibodies are as follows: rabbit anti-mouse IgG (BBI, China; D110098) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (BBI, China; D110058).

Coagulation assays

Collected mouse plasma was tested for prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) using assay kits (Sysmex, China; R9055 and R9051, respectively) and four-channel semi-automated coagulation analyzer STart (Diagnostica Stago, France). The activity of FVIII in mouse plasma was detected using Coamatic Factor VIII kit (K822585, Chromogenix, Italy) following the protocol provided with the kit.

Pharmacokinetics of siRNAs in mice

Female mice (C57BL/6, 6–8 weeks old, n = 5/group) supplied by Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd (Zhejiang, China) were subcutaneously administered with GalNAc-LMAN1 or GalNAc-MCFD2 at 10 mg/kg. At 12 h and on days 7 and 14 post administration, the mice were sacrificed and tissue samples, including liver, heart, spleen, lung, muscle, kidney, and serum, were collected. The drug levels in these organs were accessed using Stem Loop-qPCR assays [14–16] (GenePharma, Suzhou, China) according to the provided instruction. The mice were perfused with saline before tissue collection. The sequences of the primers used for the Stem-Loop qPCR assays are shown in Fig. 3B. Because the GalNAc of each siRNA acts similarly, LMAN1-181 and MCFD2-36 were tested as examples.

Pharmacodynamics of siRNAs in mice

Female mice (C57BL/6, 6–8 weeks old, n = 6/group) were subcutaneously administered with either 0.9% saline or GalNAc-siRNAs at 3mg/kg or 10 mg/kg. 7 days after the 3mg/kg single dose injection, plasma samples from inferior vena cava were collected to measure FVIII activity. Livers were collected, and the expression of LMAN1 and MCFD2 mRNA was examined by RT-qPCR, and protein levels were examined by western blotting. In a separate set of experiment, mice received three doses of siRNA at 10mg/kg on days 1, 4 and 7. Plasma samples from retro-orbital venous plexus were collected at various times, and the FVIII and were measured with APTT assay (see Fig. 5A for the schedule of injection and blood collection).

Tail bleeding test

The mice in the drug administration group and the normal control group were subjected to a mouse tail bleeding test 10 days after the first administration. Mice (male, 8–10 weeks) were anesthetized with 1% sodium pentobarbital solution and then placed in the prone position. The 2-mm distal portion of the tail was removed with a scalpel and the rest of the tail was immediately immersed in a 15 ml centrifuge tube containing 0.9% saline that was preheated to 37 °C in a water bath. The tail was kept vertical and below the body level. Each animal was monitored for 20 min. Blood loss was determined by hemoglobin content after red cell lysis with 2% acetic acid and was expressed in microliters of blood per gram (g) mouse body weight assuming a hematocrit of 46%. If bleeding did not stop within 20 min, the experiment was terminated within 20 min according to the approved animal protocol to avoid death during the procedure.

Statistical analysis

All values were expressed as means plus or minus standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between the groups were performed using independent-samples t-test and two-way ANOVA test with GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. P values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Patients

All F5F8D patients reported in this study were from hematology clinic of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. The recently identified proband and her family members were sampled for genetic analysis. The DNAs were extracted using a whole blood DNA reagent kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Genomic DNA analysis using a gene sequencing platform for hemostasis and thrombosis (HAT platform) that included 199 genes related to hemorrhage, thrombi, and platelets [17]. This study was approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of Soochow University. Informed consent was obtained from all patients and healthy controls in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Identification of a novel splicing variant in MCFD2 in a Chinese family with F5F8D

The proband was a 34-year-old Chinese woman born into a healthy consanguineous family. She had no history of bleeding manifestations such as menorrhagia, epistaxis, or gingival bleeding. However, abnormal blood clotting and decreased FVIII levels were detected before she underwent a cesarean section. Laboratory tests showed prolonged APTT and PT with lowered clotting activities of factor V (FV:C) and factor VIII (FVIII:C) (8% and 9% of the normal level, respectively) in the blood (Fig. 1A). Her parents, older brother, and son had no coagulation abnormalities or spontaneous bleeding, while her daughter had mild epistaxis. The APTT and PT values of her brother and children were not obtained. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) revealed a homozygous splice variant of MCFD2 [c.149 + 1G > C] in the proband (Fig. 1B). To verify the distribution of this variant in the pedigree, we sequenced all four exons and intron–exon boundaries of MCFD2 among all family members using Sanger sequencing. The results confirmed the heterozygous variation in her parents, brother, and two children (Fig. 1C,1D). This variant is located at the classical donor splicing site between exon 2 and flanking intron 2 of MCFD2 (Fig. 1B). It was predicted to be splicing influencing by “Ada” (scores = 0.9999). Therefore, this variant is likely to affect normal splicing, resulting in abnormal protein expression.

The FV and FVIII levels in F5F8D patients with LMAN1/MCFD2 variants

We retrospectively analyzed data of 171 patients with LMAN1 or MCFD2 variant reported by previous studies (Supplementary Table S2, S3). Available clinical data including symptoms, FV/FVIII levels and coagulation indexes were recorded (Fig. 1A, 1E). Although the serum FV and FVIII levels of most F5F8D patients have decreased to varying degrees, the bleeding symptoms are mostly mild, mainly manifested as nosebleeds and excessive menstruation. Usually, these patients do not seek medical attention.

Design and generation of siRNAs reagents

Because human and mouse LMAN1/MCFD2 transcripts are different, and target site accessibility highly impacts the effectiveness of siRNA [18], we designed 12 siRNAs (Supplementary Table S4, S5) against various target sites of the coding sequences in the mRNAs, including eight specifically targeting LMAN1 and four targeting MCFD2 (Fig. 2A). All of them target the homologous regions between human and mice mRNAs. To improve the stability and specificity, 12 optimal siRNAs, selected with the Dual-Luciferase reporter assay in 293T cells, were chemically modified with 2′-O-methyl (2′-OMe) and 2′-deoxy-2′-fluoro (2′-F) at various sites (Fig. 2B). Phosphonothioate (PS) was used for backbone modifications. Since GalNAc can recognize and bind to the asialoglycoprotein receptor abundantly expressed on hepatocytes, the 3’-positive-sense strands of the siRNAs were conjugated with three GalNAc molecules for the siRNAs’ specific delivery to hepatocytes. Luciferase activity assay indicated that most of the chemically modified siRNAs are more effective than unmodified ones (Fig. 2C). To validate mRNA targeting, we transfected HepaG2 cells with FAM-labeled siRNAs and observed the siRNAs inside the cells at 24 h post transfection with confocal fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2D). To evaluate the inhibition effects of the siRNAs on the expression of LMAN1 or MCFD2, we transfected HEPAG2 cells with seven LMAN1-targeting and three MCFD2-targeting siRNAs 72 h later, we measured the transcripts level of the target genes using RT-qPCR (Supplementary Table S1). The results showed that several siRNAs, such as LMAN1-73, LMAN1-181, LMAN1-190, LMAN1-491 and MCFD2-36, could reduce the mRNA level of the target genes to below 50% (Fig. 2E).

Pharmacokinetics and liver targeting of GalNAc-siRNA in mice

To access pharmacokinetics and liver targeting of the GalNAc-siRNAs, we used Stem Loop-qPCR (Fig. 3A, 3B) to evaluate their concentration in major organs of female mice at various times after the mice were subcutaneously administered with a single dose of the siRNAs at 10mg/kg. As shown in the figure (Fig. 3c), 12 h after injection, siRNA has already diffused to all organs, but its level is highest in the liver. It remained at high level in the liver for days (2 × 105 pmol/kg at day 7), and its level was significantly reduced after 14 days. All other tested organs had much lower amount of the siRNAs except kidney, indicating that the GalNAc-siRNA possesses satisfactory liver specificity.

GalNAc-siRNA demonstrated effective and persistent LMAN1 and MCFD2 inhibition in mice.

We’ve shown that the siRNAs exhibited inhibition effect on LMAN1 and MCFD2 mRNAs in HepaG2 cells (Fig. 2E). We further conducted pharmacological analysis of the GalNAc-siRNAs in mice. Female mice (C57BL/6, 6–8 weeks old, n = 6/group) were subcutaneously administered with either PBS or GalNAc-siRNAs at 3mg/kg. After 7 days, we measured the mRNA levels of LMAN1 and MCFD2 in mouse livers via RT-qPCR. The GalNAc-siRNAs demonstrated effective inhibition effects in vivo, reducing the LMAN1 and MCFD2 mRNA levels to as low as 19.97% ± 3.78% and 32.22% ± 13.14%, respectively (Fig. 4A). Consistently, liver levels of LMAN1 and MCFD2 proteins were significantly reduced (to 30 and 50% respectively) (Fig. 4B). The highest inhibition of FVIII protein expression in mouse livers is about 60% by LMAN1-181 (the specific siRNA). As expected, GalNAc-siRNA reduces protein expression through targeted mRNA degradation in mice’s liver. Interestingly, contrary to expectations, FVIII protein expression in mouse livers is decreased. Perhaps because it is not properly modified to bind to the antibody.

GalNAc-siRNAs prolonged APTT through blocking the secretion of FVIII

Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) is an important and sensitive evaluation index of the hemostasis and coagulation function. We used APTT to evaluate if RNAi targeting LMAN1-MCFD2 complex promotes anticoagulation through blocking the secretion of FVIII. Female mice (C57BL/6, 6–8weeks old, n = 6/group) received multiple subcutaneous injection of either 0.9% NaCl or various GalNAc-siRNAs at 10 mg/kg (Fig. 5A). From day 0 to day 31 after administration, plasma samples from retro-orbital venous plexus were collected to measure FVIII activity and APTT. The greatest anticoagulant potency appeared on day 13, then APTT gradually returned to normal (Fig. 5B-D). The FVIII activities of plasma samples with APTT > 120s (total sample number = 12) were significantly decreased compared with the activity of plasma from mice treated with NaCl (Fig. 5E). During the process of blood sample collection and operation, no bleeding symptoms were observed in any mice.

Tail bleeding test

Mice administered with siRNA-LMAN1-73, siRNA-LMAN1-181, and siRNA-MCFD2-36, as well as control mice, underwent a tail bleeding test 10 days after administration. The results showed that the amount of bleeding in each group of mice was as follows: siRNA-LMAN1-73 (28.5 ± 6.9 μL/g, n = 4), siRNA-LMAN1-181 (33.6 ± 4.3 μL/g, n = 6), and siRNA-MCFD2-36 (32.1 ± 4.1 μL/g, n = 5). Compared with the control group of mice (28.2 ± 3.7 μL/g, n = 6), the amount of bleeding in the siRNA-LMAN1-181 group showed a slight increase (P < 0.05), while there was no statistical difference between the other two groups.

Discussion

Thrombosis is a major cause of death and disability leading to increasing global burden and estimated to account for 1 in 4 deaths worldwide [19, 20]. Anticoagulation therapy is the cornerstone of the prevention and treatment of thromboembolism. Despite the emergence of oral anticoagulants (DOACs) directly targeting factor X and factor II, the search for more effective and safer antithrombotic strategies continues. Some studies have shown that factors XI and XII have the potential to become targets for novel anticoagulant drugs [21].

There are currently no anticoagulant drugs targeting FVIII. Because FVIII is a sensitive factor in the intrinsic coagulation pathway, excessive reduction in FVIII levels may lead to bleeding. However, a rare coagulation deficiency disease, F5F8D, provides us with a controllable pathway to reduce FVIII levels. F5F8D is caused by LMAN1 or MCFD2 gene defects [1, 2, 5]. LMAN1 and MCFD2 form a complex and act as a specific receptor for the transport of FV and FVIII from the endoplasmic reticulum to the Golgi apparatus [22, 23]. The characteristic of F5F8D patients is a decrease in plasma FV and FVIII levels, with an average decrease to 9% -15% of normal levels (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Tables S2, S3). However, F5F8D patients present no serious spontaneous bleeding manifestations despite low plasma levels of FV and FVIII. This phenomenon makes the LMAN1/MCFD2 complex a potential attractive and safe target for anticoagulant therapy.

siRNA can mediate targeted mRNA degradation in a sequence-specific manner, which may be an alternative method for interfering with target gene expression [24–26]. RNA interference-based therapy has been used for genetic diseases [27], cardiovascular diseases [28, 29], and infectious diseases [30]. In our study, chemically modified and GalNAc-modified siRNA showed good stability and liver targeting specificity. Drug distribution data showed that after 7 days of administration, siRNA was mainly concentrated in the liver and kidney. Furthermore, GalNAc siRNA targeting LMAN1 or MCFD2 exhibited effective and persistent LMAN1 and MCFD2 inhibitory effects in the liver of mice. After 7 days of administration, the coagulation index APTT began to increase, reaching its peak on day 13 after administration, and gradually returning to normal after 27 days of administration (as shown in Fig. 5B). However, there were individual differences in the changes in APTT in mice, with about one-third of the mice not showing significant APTT prolongation. The mouse tail bleeding test showed that although there were significant changes in coagulation indicator APTT and FVIII levels, the amount of bleeding in mice did not significantly increase compared to the control. The data indicate that GalNAc-siRNA targeting LMAN1 or MCFD2 may potentially serve as an efficacious and safe anticoagulant agent.

Conclusion

In both cellular models and normal mouse models, our study has confirmed for the first time that GalNAc-LMAN1 and GalNAc-MCFD2 siRNA are effective and safe candidates for anticoagulant therapy. It is imperative to further evaluate the anticoagulant efficacy and bleeding risk of these siRNAs in thrombophilic mice.
