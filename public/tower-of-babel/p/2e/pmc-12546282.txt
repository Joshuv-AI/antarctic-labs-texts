6-year clinical performance of prefabricated and clear template-formed resin composite veneers
Authors: Hakan Bilen, Lezize Sebnem Turkun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the clinical performances of prefabricated and direct resin composite veneers used for diastema closure and tooth reshaping over a period of 6 years.Materials and methods21 patients (1 male, 20 females; mean age: 35) received a total of 74 anterior restorations. Group 1 was treated with Edelweiss prefabricated veneers (10 patients, 38 teeth) while Group 2 received Ceram-X Duo SphereTec resin composite using U-veneer clear templates (11 patients, 36 teeth). All procedures were performed according to manufacturer’s instructions without further mechanical polishing. The veneers were evaluated at baseline, 1, 2, 4, and 6 years by two blinded examiners using modified USPHS criteria. Statistical analysis was performed with Chi-square and Cochrane Q tests (p < 0.05).ResultsSeven patients for the Edelweiss group and eight for the direct resin composite veneer group were evaluated after 6 years. Both techniques were clinically successful/sufficient. Slight polishing loss after the first year was visible in the direct group (p < 0.05). Direct composites showed more marginal discoloration and chipping, while two Edelweiss cases had repairable partial fractures.ConclusionDespite their slight polishing loss over the years and local marginal discolorations/chipping, prefabricated resin composite veneers and direct resin composite veneers performed with U-veneers were found to be successful after 6 years. However, prefabricated resin composite veneers can be applied in less time and can offer esthetic advantages for smokers and heavy coffee drinkers.Clinical relevanceLong-term effectiveness was possible with direct and semi-direct resin composite veneer techniques. However, fine polishing and maintenance were needed after yearly recalls for achieving persistent esthetic results especially for direct resin composite veneers.

Background

With the increased influence of social media and evolving societal norms, and the widespread demand for esthetic smiles have significantly increased the popularity of anterior esthetic restorations. Irregularities in the shape, size, and color of anterior teeth may lead to social and psychological challenges for affected individuals. On the other hand, the esthetic outcomes of dental treatments have been shown to positively impact self-esteem and quality of life [1, 2].

Emphasis is increasingly placed on conservative treatment modalities that prioritize the preservation of natural dental structures with a minimally invasive approach [3]. Among minimally invasive techniques, laminate veneer restorations have a long history, originating in the 1930 s, and have gained prominence for their ability to achieve esthetic enhancement while preserving tooth structure. These restorations are favored due to their adhesive bonding capabilities, biological compatibility, and capacity to produce highly esthetic results [3]. Laminate veneers may be applied using direct, semi-direct, or indirect techniques.

The direct technique is completed intraorally in a single session and is particularly influenced by clinician skill, material characteristics, and patient cooperation. Advances in resin composite materials have enhanced the feasibility of their use in anterior restorations. These materials are widely preferred due to advantages such as cost-effectiveness, reparability, and ease of application. Recent developments, including the introduction of nanohybrid resin composites, have significantly improved esthetic outcomes, mechanical strength, and color stability compared to earlier formulations [4]. These improvements enable more accurate replication of the natural translucency, texture, and shade gradation found in dental tissues [5].

The indirect technique, while more costly and reliant on laboratory support, offers enhanced durability, esthetics and superior mechanical strength. Ceramic-based indirect restorations allow for precise control of morphological features, color harmony, and surface characteristics, contributing to their longstanding clinical use [6–8]. Nevertheless, these restorations have limitations, including the need for multiple appointments, lower fracture toughness, increased tooth preparation, potential wear on opposing dentition, lack of reparability, and higher costs [7, 9].

To integrate the benefits of both direct and indirect techniques, prefabricated composite laminate veneers—classified as a semi-direct approach—have been developed over the past two decades. These restorations are polymerized under high pressure and temperature during manufacturing, resulting in superior surface properties and enhanced performance compared to direct composite applications [10]. Their surfaces are often treated via vitrification or laser sintering, which improves color stability and resistance to degradation. This technique offers a practical and cost-efficient single-visit option with outcomes comparable to those of indirect techniques in terms of marginal adaptation, surface quality, and clinical longevity [3, 11, 12]. Furthermore, the semi-direct technique offers a more accessible learning curve for clinicians who are new to esthetic dentistry, providing ease of application and time efficiency [13]. Nevertheless, their long-term clinical success depends heavily on their proper marginal adaptation and strong adhesive bonding between them and tooth structures [14]. Despite their clinical utility, there remains a limited number of long-term clinical studies evaluating the performance of this technique [3, 5, 8, 10, 11, 15].

Comparative clinical studies have indicated that both direct and indirect techniques can yield successful outcomes when applied with appropriate patient selection and treatment indication [16–18]. The direct method presents notable advantages, including lower cost, elimination of the need for a resin cement, minimal wear on opposing dentition, and ease of reparability [4, 6, 9]. However, they may exhibit inferior long-term color stability, greater polymerization shrinkage, increased microleakage, and reduced wear resistance [6, 19].

Several key factors influence the clinical success of resin composite veneers, including clinician expertise, the physical and optical properties of the material, and effective isolation during the procedure [11, 20]. Recent innovations have introduced adjunctive tools—such as prefabricated veneers and clear templates—to improve procedural standardization and reduce the dependence on clinicians. Clear templates are designed to optimize esthetic and functional outcomes by standardizing form, ensuring symmetry, and reducing treatment time in direct composite restorations [11]. Evidence suggests that for less experienced clinicians, the use of prefabricated or template-guided techniques may result in improved esthetic outcomes compared to conventional direct methods [11, 20].

The success of esthetic dental procedures is influenced by a multitude of factors, including material quality, adhesive protocols, application precision, clinician experience, and the patient’s oral hygiene and dietary habits [21, 22]. Proper selection and application of adhesive systems contribute to enhanced marginal integrity and reduced microleakage [23]. Modern esthetic evaluation is influenced not only by the clinician’s judgment but also by the patient’s perception and individual expectations. Accordingly, incorporating patient-centered esthetic goals during treatment planning is essential for achieving optimal outcomes [24, 25].

Considering the aforementioned situations, the present study aimed to evaluate the 6-year clinical performance of direct resin composite veneers applied using clear templates versus prefabricated resin composite veneers applied via the semi-direct technique. The clinical performance of both groups was assessed using United States Public Health Service (USPHS) criteria. The null hypothesis (H0) of this study was that there will be no statistically significant difference in clinical performance between the two veneer application techniques.

Methods

Study design

This in vivo randomized clinical trial aimed to compare direct and semi-direct techniques for resin composite laminate veneers over a 6-year follow-up period. The study was designed in accordance with the 2010 Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines (Table 1). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine at Ege University (Approval No: 18-11.1T/15; Date: 28.11.2018) and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05984472). Sample size determination

Sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power 3.1 software, employing a 95% confidence level and 90% statistical power. The effect size for the Chi-square test was set at 0.40 based on the primary outcome, which was restoration retention. Assuming a maximum of five degrees of freedom, the required minimum total sample size was calculated to be 70 (n = 35 per group).

Patient selection

A total of 21 patients were recruited from the Department of Restorative Dentistry, Ege University School of Dentistry, (20 female patients and 1 male). The age range of the patients was 18–60 with an average of 35 years. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, good oral hygiene, absence of systemic diseases, no pregnancy, absence of heavy bruxism behaviors or occlusal dysfunction, in normal overbite and overjet occlusion and indication for anterior resin composite veneer restorations due to diastema, peg lateral incisors, enamel hypoplasia, or other aesthetic concerns. Teeth with extensive caries, mobility, or loss of vitality, patients with temporo-mandibular joint disorders, orthodontic therapy or eccentric occlusion with overjet more than 2 mm and overbite exceeding 3 mm were excluded. Comprehensive dental and medical histories were obtained from the patients, followed by intraoral examinations to assess tooth morphology, relation with neighboring teeth, occlusion, and tooth proportions. Treatment options, including ceramic veneers and prefabricated/direct resin composite veneers, were thoroughly explained to eligible patients, including their clinical protocols, longevity, advantages, and limitations. Patients expressing preference for prefabricated or direct resin composite veneers were informed about the study and invited to participate. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. All patients received a dental scaling and polishing session and received oral hygiene instructions prior to treatment.

Randomization process

Randomization was performed by an independent clinician not involved in the study. Participants were allocated to treatment groups using a random number table generated through the ‘Research Randomizer Program’ (http://www.randomizer.org/form.htm).

Restorative procedures

Pre-operative intraoral photographs (smile, maximum intercuspation, and open-mouth views) were taken prior to the treatment session and repeated at the end of the restorations and at each follow-up sessions using standardized angles. All restorations were performed by a single experienced operator (HB).

Semi-direct technique (Edelweiss)

For the semi-direct group, shade selection was performed using the “Button Try” technique with Edelweiss nanohybrid resin composite. Suitable prefabricated veneer sizes were determined using the manufacturer’s clear guide based on tooth morphology. Isolation was achieved with a disposable mouth retractor (OptraGate, Ivoclar Vivadent, Liechtenstein), ensuring moisture control and application access.

Initial cervical margin adaptation was verified by placing the prefabricated veneers without adhesive system applications on the teeth. Margins were refined using Super-Snap Rainbow polishing discs (SHOFU, Kyoto, Japan) without water cooling. The same adaptation was performed for the mesio-distal width. Internal retention was enhanced by micro-roughening the inner veneer surfaces with fine-grit yellow ring diamond burs.

Tooth surfaces were etched with 34% phosphoric acid gel (Caulk tooth conditioner gel, Dentsply Sirona, Konstanz, Germany) for 30 s, rinsed, and dried with oil-free air. A universal adhesive system (Prime&Bond Universal, Dentsply Sirona, Konstanz, Germany) was rubbed for 20 s on tooth surfaces, air-thinned, and light-cured (D-Light Duo, GC-Europe, Leuven, Belgium).

After cleaning the veneer’s internal surface with alcohol, the manufacturer-specific Veneer Bond adhesive (Edelweiss Dentistry, Wolfurt, Austria) was applied. The veneer was loaded with Edelweiss nanohybrid resin composite and seated on the tooth. Initial pre-light-curing was performed at the incisal edge to fix the position then it was followed by removal of excess resin and final curing of all surfaces for 40 s each with an LED device (D-Light Duo, GC).

In the final stage, the cervical adaptation of the restoration was re-evaluated using the finishing and polishing discs from the Super-Snap Rainbow Technique Kit (SHOFU). Any premature contacts were checked using articulation paper in all restorations and then adjusted and finished simultaneously using low-speed fine-grit diamond finishing stones (Komet, Germany) and copious water. Adjustments in the proximal surfaces were performed using interproximal finishing strips to ensure optimal contact and contour.

Direct technique (U-Veneer and Ceram-X Duo)

In the direct group, shade selection for dentin and enamel was performed using the “Button Try” technique with Ceram-X Duo SphereTEC composite (Dentsply Sirona). After proper selection, clear U-Veneer templates (Ultradent, South Jordan, Utah, USA) were trial-fitted to determine appropriate sizes.

Isolation was achieved with OptraGate retractors. Enamel surfaces were etched with 34% phosphoric acid (Dentsply Sirona, Konstanz, Germany) for 30 s, rinsed, and dried. Prime&Bond Universal adhesive (Dentsply Sirona) was applied and light cured. Dentin shade composite was layered onto the labial surfaces with a fine spatula (LM Modella, LM-Dental, Parainen, Finland) and light cured. Then, enamel-shaded resin composite was applied inside the U-Veneer template, which was positioned and adapted on the tooth under light pressure) and light-curing was completed for 20 s per surface (D- Light Duo, GC). Excess material after curing was removed with a fine-tipped scaler (LM-Arte Eccesso, LM-Dental). Final polishing and adjustments followed the same protocol as for the semi-direct group.

Clinical evaluations

A total of 74 veneer restorations—38 on maxillary central incisors and 36 on maxillary lateral incisors—were evaluated at baseline (1 week), and at 1, 2, 4, and 6 years post-operatively. Clinical evaluations were performed by two experienced, blinded examiners under natural daylight for color assessment, with the aid of a mirror and explorer for the other parameters. The modified United States Public Health Service (USPHS) criteria were used to evaluate color match, marginal discoloration, marginal integrity, anatomical form, secondary caries, retention, surface roughness, polishing retention, and postoperative sensitivity [26] (Table 3). Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM Corp., SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Frequency tables were generated for each parameter. Intragroup comparisons over time were conducted using Pearson’s Chi-square test. Changes in evaluation scores from baseline to follow-up intervals were analyzed using Cochran’s Q test, followed by Dunn’s post-hoc test where applicable. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Throughout all recall periods, no instances of color mismatch, secondary caries formation, or postoperative sensitivity were observed in either group (p > 0.05). However, restorations performed using the direct technique with U-Veneer clear templates exhibited a higher incidence of minor marginal discoloration (p < 0.05) and slight marginal adaptation deficiencies in four patients (Fig. 1). These findings were first noted during the 1-year recall and remained stable through subsequent evaluations. The most prominent esthetic issue reported—also recognized by patients—was the gradual loss of surface gloss in all restorations within the direct veneer group. In the semi-direct Edelweiss group, minor marginal adaptation discrepancies were detected in single restorations of three patients. Additionally, one patient experienced a partial veneer fracture involving a restored maxillary incisor at the 6-year recall (Fig. 3). Since both fractured segments could be precisely re-adapted to the tooth and the patient expressed a desire to retain the original restoration, a conservative approach was chosen. Considering the cumulative USPHS scores and patient-reported satisfaction, both the direct resin composite veneers and the prefabricated Edelweiss veneers demonstrated clinically acceptable and successful performance. While both groups experienced minor polish retention loss—beginning as early as the first year in the direct group (p < 0.05), and from the fourth year in the semi-direct group—these changes were minor. The dullness was noticeable only upon air drying and did not negatively impact the patients’ esthetic perception.

Discussion

Clinical trials with a longer observation period are essential to better understand the performance of the material in the oral cavity and during function. These studies indicate the main causes of restoration failure considering factors related to the patient, operator and materials [27–30]. However clinical trials are very difficult to perform starting from patient selection to application techniques and recall sessions [31]. Moreover, the clinical success of anterior restorations depends on many factors such as caries risk of the patient, quality of the material, accuracy of the technique employed, extent of the defects and experience/skillfulness of the operator. Thus, studies evaluating the clinical performance of restorative materials in challenging clinical situations and including different application techniques can be considered “high-risk and difficult studies” [31].

Our study compared the long-term clinical performance of a newer semi-direct prefabricated resin composite veneer system, Edelweiss, versus a nano-hybrid resin composite Ceram-X Duo applied with clear templates U-Veneer. Both systems were in the framework of minimal invasive restorative procedures. To investigate the performance of both materials in the permanent dentition, the present study was performed on adult patients with an average age of 35 years. Our results indicate that both systems are suitable for anterior restorations and, despite minor esthetic problems, gave satisfactory results as long-term restoratives under the conditions of this study. To the best of our knowledge, very few data has been reported in the literature comparing the short or middle-term clinical success of the semi-direct and direct resin composite veneers and this study is the longest one reporting these comparisons.

Different clinical criteria were used for evaluating the clinical performance of dental restorations. The United States Public Health Service (USPHS) criteria, also known as the Ryge criteria, is the most widely used over the years [26]. However, as different modifications of these criteria have been proposed over the years to allow for a more detailed analysis of esthetic restorations, the comparison of data among different studies presents always a big challenge. For a more detailed and objective analysis of the observed drawbacks in esthetic restorations, Hickel et al. [32, 33]. proposed in 2007, and elaborated in 2010, a new and very detailed clinical evaluation system, mainly for resin composite restorations, and based on three fundamental properties: esthetical, functional and biological properties by attributing scores that ranges from 1 to 5. The first 3 scores stand for successful restorations while score 4 and 5 are considered as failures. These new criteria were then approved by the World Dental Federation (FDI) and were considered today as a golden standard [31–33] for evaluation clinical studies. However, as there were very few case reports or clinical data available, for comparing our results with the literature, we chose to use the USPHS criteria for evaluating our restorations.

Since the development of nanohybrid composite resins in 2002, the application of composite laminate veneers has gained increased popularity. In addition to their mechanical and aesthetic advantages, their reparability, remodeling capacity, and relatively lower costs compared to indirect techniques have made them a preferred treatment option among patients as well [27, 30, 34]. Although advancements in highly filled nanohybrid composite resins have aimed to minimize polymerization shrinkage, this issue remains one of the primary disadvantages of these materials [35]. Due to the spherical filler particles within their formulation, nanohybrid composite resins offer optimal optical, physical, and mechanical properties. These include low polymerization shrinkage, high polishability, long-term gloss retention, and color stability, making them well-suited for long-term aesthetic restorations [35–38]. A study by Lempel et al. demonstrated that nanohybrid composites pose a lower chipping risk compared to their microhybrid counterparts [39]. However, a systematic review by Kaizer et al. reported no significant difference in polishing retention and color stability between microhybrid and nanohybrid composites [40]. In our study, we opted to use a nanohybrid composite resin, Ceram-X Duo, for direct veneer applications to achieve optimal long-term clinical outcomes.

The success of directly applied, freehand composite resin veneer restorations depend not only on the material itself or patient-related factors, but also heavily on the clinician’s skill in shaping, achieving color harmony, and forming surface anatomy [41]. The technical sensitivity and complexity of such procedures may deter some practitioners, leading them to prefer indirect restorations fabricated by dental technicians. To address these challenges and simplify direct applications, several auxiliary tools have been developed. Among the most basic are U-veneer clear templates, which allow for the creation of shape and surface morphology in a single step, enabling symmetry and expediency. However, the manufacturer recommends a single layer/color application, limiting the possibility for layering. Moreover, these templates can present adaptation challenges in the interproximal regions and may result in overhangs sometimes very difficult to remove [3]. Although manufacturers claim that surfaces formed with these templates are free of the oxygen inhibition layer and do not require polishing, in vitro studies have shown that polishing is essential for achieving long-term durability and maintaining color stability [3, 11, 42, 43].

With advancements in material technology, prefabricated composite resin veneers have become a viable option in aesthetic dentistry. These are thin, pre-shaped, factory-polished composite shells available in various sizes [12, 44, 45]. Currently, three main commercial brands exist: Edelweiss (Edelweiss Dentistry, Wolfurt, Austria), which was used in our study, Componeer (Coltène, Altstätten, Switzerland), and Visalys Veneer Chairside (Kettenbach, Germany). While Componeer veneers are polymerized using only light curing, Edelweiss veneers undergo polymerization under high temperature and pressure, resulting in stronger and more polished/glossy surfaces [10, 12]. Given their standardized form and surface morphology, and reduced dependence on the clinician’s skill for marginal adaptation, prefabricated veneers offer more predictable outcomes in terms of color stability and aesthetic surface quality [11, 45]. These systems are thus accessible to less experienced clinicians, yielding consistent and reliable results in a shorter chair time [10, 12, 46].

Clinical studies evaluating the longevity of prefabricated composite veneers used in semi-direct techniques remain limited, with most being of short duration or in the form of case reports [3, 11, 12, 20, 44, 46]. Therefore, this clinical study aimed to compare the long-term performance of prefabricated composite veneers with that of composite resin veneers fabricated using clear templates.

Coelho-de-Souza et al. [38] reported satisfactory clinical performance in their in vivo study of composite resin restorations followed for 3.5 years. While no survival differences were observed among different brands, microfill composite resins were deemed more successful in terms of aesthetics. Gresnigt et al. [18] compared indirect resin composite resin and ceramic veneers over a period of 10-year and found that ceramic veneers performed significantly better after a decade for color match, surface roughness, partial fracture and wear of the restorations. Our study aligns with these findings, as the most frequent failures observed were marginal discolorations, polishing loss and marginal chippings in composite resin laminate veneers. Similar results were reported by Peumans et al., who observed a 5-year survival rate of 89% for composite resin veneers, with 7% exhibiting unacceptable marginal discoloration and 5% requiring incisal chipping repairs [47, 48].

In a 5-year in vivo study performed by Demirci et al. involving diastema closure, inadequate surface preparation was shown to compromise adhesion, leading to marginal discoloration over time [49]. Korkut and Türkmen recently published a 4-year clinical evaluation of diastema closures and recontouring with nanohybrid, microhybrid, and nanofilled composites, finding comparable longevity across all materials [36].

After 6 years of clinical follow-up, both techniques yielded generally successful and similar outcomes leading to the acceptance of our H0 hypothesis. According to our findings, and similarly to previous studies [18, 38, 47–49] composite resin veneers fabricated using the direct technique and U-veneers, exhibited more instances of polish loss, marginal discoloration, and marginal chipping. In contrast, prefabricated veneers occasionally showed repairable partial fractures in single teeth and some loss of surface luster at the last year check-up. Marginal discolorations were superficial and could be removed through repolishing, not necessitating complete restoration replacement. The consumption of staining substances such as coffee, tea, and tobacco by patients can contribute to the discoloration of composite resin surfaces over time. Although not officially recommended by manufacturers, we suggest a fine polishing procedure at the end of treatment to minimize long-term gloss loss. Our recommendation is supported by numerous studies demonstrating the critical role of surface polishing in restoration longevity [42, 43].

In a toothbrushing abrasion simulation study, Karveli et al. [50] evaluated surface properties of a prefabricated resin composite veneer and a laboratory resin composite. They concluded that there was weak evidence that the prefabricated veneer material exhibited less change in surface and concluded that prefabricated composite veneers did not retain their gloss as well as laboratory resin composite. Kocak and Turkun [42] conducted an in vitro study comparing the color stability, gloss, and surface roughness of one semi-direct and three direct composite resin systems. Their findings revealed that even indirect restorations can suffer from gloss and color stability. Additionally, unpolished composite resin surfaces exhibited significantly more discoloration.

In 2021, a review by Albuquerque et al. [13] highlighted the short- and medium-term clinical success of prefabricated composite veneers, noting their cost-effectiveness, ease of application, and high patient satisfaction. A subsequent review reported that composite resin veneers remained clinically successful even beyond 5 years, emphasizing that, alongside material quality, clinician skill and patient compliance significantly impact treatment success [14]. Our findings support and align with these reviews.

A comprehensive review by Turkun [3] discussed minimally invasive techniques for the aesthetic rehabilitation of anterior teeth. Prefabricated composite veneers were noted for their short chair time, consistent form stability, and aesthetic outcomes, with lower operator dependence compared to direct composite resins. However, it was also emphasized that maintaining their glossy surfaces over time requires regular polishing and maintenance.

In our study, both prefabricated composite veneers and clear templates proved effectiveness in mitigating the challenges associated with direct restorations, minimizing technique-sensitive variables. This reduces the necessity for highly experienced clinicians to achieve esthetic anterior restorations. When comparing direct composite resin veneers created with clear templates to semi-direct prefabricated veneers, both offer benefits such as lower elastic modulus, smoother surfaces, higher wear resistance, and reduced polymerization shrinkage—characteristics traditionally associated with indirect restorations [3, 5, 8, 10, 12, 13, 15].

When comparing these two techniques in terms of application time, both allowed for efficient achievement of aesthetic outcomes. Moreover, both methods are relatively easy to learn, requiring minimal experience after a few applications.

Although both materials yielded satisfactory clinical outcomes, there were certain limitations in our data analysis. The first concerns the number of participants. While long-term studies are more informative than short-term ones, retaining participants over extended periods presents challenges, and not all restorations can be re-evaluated over time. The loss of follow-up in clinical studies is a known and widespread issue [9, 36, 47, 48]. The recall rate over the six-year follow-up was 70% for Edelweiss and 72.72% for Ceram-X Duo, slightly below the threshold determined in the power analysis. It is widely acknowledged that longer follow-up periods are associated with increased dropout rates. Another limitation was the impossibility of blinding the operator due to the visually distinguishable nature of the materials and their application techniques.

Considering these findings, we believe further long-term in vivo and in vitro studies are needed to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the performance and characteristics of these materials and techniques.

Conclusion

Despite their slight polishing loss over the years and local marginal discolorations/chipping, prefabricated resin composite veneers and direct resin composite veneers performed with U-veneers were found to be successful after 6 years.

Both materials experienced a loss of polishing retention, sooner or later, over the period of our study. Therefore, contrary to manufacturer recommendations, it would be advisable to fine polish composite resin veneers fabricated using clear templates at baseline and at each recall. However, as polishing procedures may compromise the vitrified surface of prefabricated veneers, these procedures may be more appropriate to patients with high exposure to staining agents (e.g., coffee, tea, tobacco). In all other cases, the surfaces should be left untreated.
