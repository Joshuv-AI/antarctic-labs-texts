Development of a physical activity promotion intervention for colorectal cancer survivors who are physically inactive: A Delphi study integrating the analytic hierarchy process
Authors: Jiayu Mao, Xiaoke Qiu, Can Wang, Xueli Yang, Yi Lin, Jie Zhao, Qiuping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePhysical activity (PA) confers multiple health and rehabilitation benefits for colorectal cancer (CRC) survivors; however, many CRC survivors do not meet recommended PA levels following curative-intent treatment. This study aimed to develop a comprehensive, evidence-based PA promotion intervention program for CRC survivors who are physically inactive.MethodsA preliminary program was developed based on the COM-B model, a literature review, clinical practice guidelines, and a retrospective case analysis. Between August and October 2025, 15 experts with expertise in clinical nursing, clinical medicine, rehabilitation medicine, and nursing management participated in two rounds of Delphi consultation. Experts completed the questionnaires independently in each round, and aggregated feedback was used to refine the program through an iterative consensus process. The analytic hierarchy process (AHP) was used to derive indicator weights and establish a hierarchical framework.ResultsOf the 20 experts invited, 15 completed Round 1 (response rate, 75%); all 15 Round 1 participants completed Round 2 (response rate, 100%). The expert authority coefficients were 0.840 and 0.857 in Rounds 1 and 2, respectively. The coefficients of variation for all indicators were below 0.25 in both rounds, and Kendall's coefficients of concordance were 0.188 and 0.228, respectively (both P < 0.001), indicating low but statistically significant inter-expert concordance. The finalized program comprised three first-level indicators, ten second-level indicators, and 25 third-level indicators, with AHP-derived weights of 0.49, 0.31, and 0.20 assigned to motivation, capability, and opportunity components, respectively.ConclusionsThe program developed in this study provides a theoretically grounded, expert-consensus-based framework for promoting PA among CRC survivors who are physically inactive. Its feasibility, acceptability, safety, and preliminary efficacy should be evaluated before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies of the digestive tract, accounting for approximately one in ten cancer diagnoses and cancer-related deaths worldwide.1 With global population growth and population aging, the number of new CRC cases is projected to reach 3.2 million by 2040.2 Standard treatment strategies for CRC include surgical resection, chemoradiotherapy, and immunotherapy.3 Although these treatments can improve disease control and survival, they are associated with a wide range of side effects that impair patients’ physical and psychological well-being.4 Consequently, the quality of life (QoL) of CRC survivors may remain impaired during survivorship, reflecting the cumulative toxic effects of both the disease and its treatments.3

A growing body of evidence indicates that maintaining consistent and adequate physical activity (PA) can alleviate treatment-related side effects, reduce cancer-specific mortality, and improve overall QoL among CRC survivors.5,6 Specifically, PA is broadly defined as any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure,7 and encompasses leisure-time, occupational, domestic, and transportation domains.8 To achieve optimal health benefits, current guidelines recommend that CRC survivors engage in 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week, supplemented by muscle-strengthening exercises on at least two days per week.6 Consistent with the World Health Organization 2020 Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour, the present study operationally defined physical inactivity as not achieving at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic PA, 75 min of vigorous-intensity aerobic PA, or an equivalent combination of moderate- and vigorous-intensity activity per week.8 This threshold was used to define the target population for the intervention and is proposed as an eligibility criterion for future feasibility studies. Despite the well-documented benefits of PA, a recent cross-sectional study reported that only 25.6% of Chinese CRC survivors met current PA recommendations, whereas the remaining 74.4% were physically inactive after primary treatment.9 As a risk factor for adverse health outcomes among cancer survivors, physical inactivity not only elevates the risk of disease-specific and all-cause mortality but also imposes an estimated annual burden of $47.6 billion on global health care systems.10 Developing a targeted PA promotion intervention program for CRC survivors who are physically inactive and enhancing their long-term adherence to PA recommendations is therefore of considerable clinical importance.

Recent studies have examined the determinants of PA engagement among CRC survivors: treatment-related side effects and time constraints have been identified as primary barriers, whereas social support and self-efficacy serve as key facilitators.11, 12, 13 Notably, CRC survivors face a distinct set of disease- and treatment-specific barriers that distinguish them from other cancer populations. Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy and treatment-related fatigue substantially impair physical capacity and exercise motivation, and these effects frequently persist beyond active treatment.14 CRC survivors with a stoma commonly report concerns about appliance security and body image disturbance, which are significant deterrents to structured PA participation.15 In addition, bowel dysfunction associated with low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), including stool urgency and fecal incontinence, can severely restrict participation in community-based exercise settings.16 Together, these challenges underscore the need for PA interventions tailored to the unique clinical profile of CRC survivors. Translating this evidence into effective intervention design requires a structured theoretical framework that systematically maps each determinant onto actionable intervention strategies.17 However, existing PA programs have not adequately accounted for these population-specific factors, which may limit their effectiveness,18 and, to our knowledge, few PA promotion programs that systematically integrate these determinants have been developed for this population.

The multidimensional nature of these barriers calls for a theoretical framework that simultaneously addresses individual, social, and environmental determinants and provides an explicit mechanism to translate these determinants into targeted intervention components. The Capability-Opportunity-Motivation-Behavior (COM-B) model, a central element of the Behavior Change Wheel, conceptualizes behavior as the product of three interacting components: capability (physical and psychological capacity), opportunity (environmental and social conditions), and motivation (reflective and automatic processes).19 This model is particularly well suited to oncology rehabilitation, because CRC-specific barriers map systematically onto its components: physical deconditioning, fatigue, and peripheral neuropathy impair capability; limited facility access, insufficient professional guidance, and stoma-related stigma constrain opportunity; and reduced self-efficacy, fear of symptom exacerbation, and negative emotional states reflect deficits in motivation.20 Critically, compared with theories that focus more narrowly on selected behavioral determinants, such as the Theory of Planned Behavior and Social Cognitive Theory, the COM-B model explicitly links each behavioral determinant to a corresponding intervention function, providing the structure needed to translate barriers and facilitators into concrete, theoretically justified strategies.21 Although the COM-B model has been applied in older adults and patients with cancer,21,22 its systematic application to PA promotion among physically inactive CRC survivors remains limited. The present study adopts the COM-B model as its overarching framework to ensure that each intervention component is grounded in the specific barriers and facilitators that characterize this population.

To address this gap, this study aimed to develop a theoretically grounded, population-specific PA promotion intervention program for CRC survivors who are physically inactive. Grounded in the COM-B model, the program was developed through a systematic literature review and retrospective case analysis, refined through two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, and weighted using the analytic hierarchy process (AHP). The resulting program is intended to provide evidence-based guidance for clinicians and to serve as a reference framework for the design of future intervention trials in this population.

Methods

The Delphi method was used to develop an intervention program promoting PA participation among physically inactive CRC survivors who had undergone curative-intent surgical resection, with or without adjuvant therapy. This structured, iterative approach facilitates consensus development through successive rounds of expert consultation.23 The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Guidance on Conducting and REporting DElphi Studies (CREDES) recommendations.24 The overall research workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Research process of the Delphi study for developing a PA promotion intervention program for CRC survivors who are physically inactive. Establishment of the research group

The research group comprised eight members: one postgraduate nursing supervisor, three nursing postgraduate students, one oncology chief nurse, one oncology nurse, one oncology chief physician, and one oncology physician. The supervisor oversaw the study design, quality control, and overall research process. The postgraduate students conducted the literature review, distributed and collected the expert questionnaires, and analyzed the consultation data. The chief nurse and chief physician formulated the expert selection criteria and drafted the initial questionnaire, and the remaining members conducted the retrospective case analysis. All members jointly reviewed and interpreted the consultation results.

Development of the initial draft of the program

Theoretical foundation

The COM-B model conceptualizes behavior as the product of three interacting components: capability, opportunity, and motivation. The model links each behavioral determinant to corresponding intervention functions, providing the structural mechanism needed to translate identified barriers and facilitators into theoretically justified strategies.19 The three core components served as the three first-level indicators (intervention components), each decomposed into subdimensions: capability into physical and psychological capability, opportunity into physical and social opportunity, and motivation into automatic and reflective motivation. These six subdimensions constituted the organizing framework for the second-level indicators (intervention themes), within which all subsequent evidence was systematically mapped to generate the third-level indicators (intervention content), ensuring direct traceability from each component to its behavioral determinant.

Literature review

The research group previously published a peer-reviewed systematic literature review identifying barriers and facilitators to PA participation among CRC survivors.20 That review was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024562668) and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. The database-specific search strategies, the PRISMA flow diagram, and the dual-reviewer screening with third-reviewer adjudication are reported in that publication.20 Briefly, six electronic databases were searched from inception through May 2024, yielding 32 eligible studies that were appraised using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool25 and synthesized using the Joanna Briggs Institute convergent integrated approach.26 For the present study, two researchers independently re-extracted data on study design and sample characteristics and identified barriers, facilitators, and their corresponding COM-B subdimensions, with disagreements resolved by a third senior researcher. The extracted items underwent inductive content analysis, merging semantically similar items into a nonredundant pool of candidate items. These candidates were then deductively mapped onto the six COM-B model subdimensions, with each subdimension assigned a second-level indicator. Barriers were translated into strategies intended to reduce their effects, whereas facilitators were translated into strategies intended to strengthen PA engagement. All items were refined through iterative consensus, yielding nine preliminary second-level indicators.

Clinical practice guidelines for PA among CRC survivors were also identified to inform the third-level indicators. Guideline repositories, professional association websites, and electronic databases were searched from inception through August 2025 using the following search strategy (‘physical activity’ OR ‘exercise’ OR ‘aerobic exercise’ OR ‘resistance exercise’) AND (‘colorectal cancer survivors’ OR ‘colorectal cancer’ OR ‘colon cancer’ OR ‘rectal cancer’) AND (‘clinical guideline’ OR ‘guideline’). Six eligible guidelines were identified from 1417 records and appraised using the AGREE II instrument,27 of which four were rated as Grade A6,28, 29, 30 and two as Grade B.31,32 Recommendations regarding PA frequency, intensity, type, duration, progression, and safety precautions were independently extracted and cross-validated against the literature-derived items. Items with guideline support were retained and refined, and gaps were supplemented with guideline recommendations to ensure clinical actionability. Exercise prescription parameters, namely moderate-intensity aerobic activity three to five times per week for 30 min per session, together with resistance training on at least two nonconsecutive days per week, with gradual progression, were directly informed by Grade A guidelines. This process yielded 22 preliminary third-level indicators.

Retrospective case analysis

Medical records of patients treated between January 2024 and January 2025 were retrieved from the hospital electronic medical record system between March and August 2025. Inclusion criteria were histopathologically confirmed CRC, curative-intent surgical resection with or without adjuvant therapy, age 18 years or older, and complete clinical records. Cases were excluded if the medical records were incomplete, if another primary malignancy was present, or if the required follow-up data were unavailable. Of the 480 medical records screened, 220 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the analysis. These criteria reflect the population of CRC survivors following curative-intent treatment for whom the program is intended, and the analysis served to enrich intervention content rather than define program eligibility. Two researchers independently extracted sociodemographic characteristics, tumor-related information, treatment modalities, and cancer-related physical conditions relevant to PA participation from the included cases. Case findings were integrated with literature-derived candidates: the observed occurrence of stoma formation and cancer-related fatigue informed stoma-specific contraindication screening (Ⅲ-1) and fatigue-adapted exercise prescription (Ⅲ-2); documented gaps in structured PA counseling supported multidisciplinary supervision (Ⅲ-9) and self-monitoring mechanisms (Ⅲ-10); and the age and comorbidity profiles reinforced the personalized, progressive exercise prescriptions (Ⅲ-7).

Preliminary formulation of program contents and items

Integrating the three evidence streams through iterative panel discussions and expert review, the research group established a preliminary draft comprising three first-level, nine second-level, and 22 third-level indicators. The COM-B model provided the theoretical architecture, the literature review and guideline synthesis supplied the empirical and clinical evidence base, and the retrospective case analysis improved the clinical relevance and contextual applicability of the program. Preparation of expert consultation questionnaire

The consultation questionnaire comprised four sections. The first was a cover letter outlining the study background, objectives, significance, and completion instructions. The second collected expert demographics, including name, sex, age, contact details, highest educational attainment, professional title, specialty, and years of work experience. The third was the core evaluation form, in which each indicator was rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = least important, 5 = most important), with an open-text field for suggested revisions. The fourth was the expert authority assessment, capturing each expert's familiarity with the topic and the basis of their judgments regarding the consultation topic.

Selection of experts

Experts were eligible if they had at least 10 years of clinical or research experience in oncology nursing, clinical medicine, rehabilitation medicine, or nursing management; held at least a bachelor's degree and an intermediate or senior professional title; and were willing to participate actively and provide substantive feedback throughout the consultation.

Implementation of expert consultation

To establish a target expert panel of 15, a total of 20 eligible experts were invited to participate. Questionnaires were distributed by email, and responses were requested within two weeks. Experts completed the questionnaires independently, and individual responses were not disclosed to other panelists. The research team retained identifying information solely for questionnaire administration and follow-up. After Round 1, the research group synthesized the experts’ feedback and revised the questionnaire for Round 2 through group discussion. An indicator was retained when its mean importance score exceeded 4.0, and its coefficient of variation (CV) was below 0.25.33 Items that did not fully meet the predefined retention criteria but were considered clinically important were discussed individually before a final decision was made.34 Consensus was achieved after two rounds, and the Delphi consultation was concluded.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0) and Microsoft Excel 2019, with P < 0.05 as the significance threshold. Expert panel characteristics were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Expert engagement was assessed using the questionnaire response rate, and panel authority was quantified using the authority coefficient Cr = (Ca + Cs)/2, where Ca denotes the judgment coefficient and Cs denotes the familiarity coefficient. Inter-expert consensus was evaluated using the CV and Kendall's coefficient of concordance (Kendall's W). Indicator importance was summarized using the mean, standard deviation, and proportion of ratings of 5. Indicator weights were determined using the AHP within a three-tiered hierarchical structure comprising first-level, second-level, and third-level indicators.35 Pairwise comparison matrices were constructed from mean importance scores derived from the second-round consultation. Expert judgments were first aggregated by arithmetic mean, and the resulting means were converted to integers on the Saaty nine-point scale using a predefined mapping rule. Each matrix entry Aij represented the relative importance of indicator i over indicator j, with reciprocal values Aji = 1/Aij ensuring reciprocity of the pairwise-comparison matrix. Local and global weights were calculated in Yaahp (version 10.0) using the geometric mean method. The consistency of each matrix was verified by computing the consistency ratio CR = CI/RI, where CI denotes the consistency index and RI denotes the random index for the matrix order. A CR below 0.10 was considered indicative of acceptable consistency.

Results

Characteristics of the Delphi panel

A total of 15 experts who met the eligibility criteria participated in the Delphi study. All panel members had relevant expertise in the diagnosis, treatment, nursing care, or rehabilitation of CRC survivors. The panel was predominantly female (80.00%), and the majority were aged 40–49 years (53.33%). Regarding educational attainment, 40.00% of the experts held doctoral degrees, and an equal proportion held bachelor's degrees. With respect to professional titles, 73.33% were at the associate senior or senior level, and 46.67% had more than 20 years of clinical experience. Most experts (73.33%) were affiliated with tertiary Grade A hospitals. Expert engagement, authority, and consistency

Response rates were 75% and 100% in the first and second rounds, respectively, both exceeding the 70% threshold considered indicative of adequate panel engagement in Delphi studies.36 The Cr values were 0.840 and 0.857 in the two rounds, both exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.70 and confirming a high level of panel authority.37 The mean importance scores ranged from 4.13 to 5.00 in the first round and from 4.47 to 5.00 in the second round, with full-score rates of 20%–100% and 60%–100%, respectively, indicating greater convergence of ratings in Round 2. The CV remained below the 0.25 criterion in both rounds, ranging from 0.00 to 0.21 and from 0.00 to 0.17, respectively. The Kendall's W values were 0.188 and 0.228 (both P < 0.001), indicating low but statistically significant inter-expert concordance. Collectively, these results indicate stable ratings and statistically significant, although limited, concordance among the experts. Complete statistics for both consultation rounds are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Development and refinement of the PA promotion intervention program

Through two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, this study systematically refined a PA promotion intervention program for CRC survivors who are physically inactive. Based on the predefined indicator-retention criteria and expert feedback, the research group made structured modifications to the program indicators. In the first round, all indicators met the predefined retention criteria, and the experts provided 26 written comments. After duplicate comments were removed, 19 actionable recommendations remained: seven additions, three deletions, one merger, and eight modifications. The detailed changes based on the first-round consultation are presented in Supplementary Table S3. The research group then developed a revised questionnaire for the second round, containing 37 indicators (three first-level, ten second-level, and 24 third-level).

In the second round, the 15 experts provided four written recommendations. Expert 6 recommended replacing sports rehabilitation specialists with rehabilitation physicians as the intervention providers for item Ⅲ-6, and the provider designation was revised accordingly. Expert 9 suggested specifying the resistance training prescription and anchoring progression to guideline targets. Item Ⅲ-9 was therefore revised to prescribe an individualized initiation-phase plan of moderate-intensity aerobic PA (60%–70% of maximum heart rate) three to five times per week, 30 min per session, together with resistance training on at least two nonconsecutive days per week. The plan was designed to progress toward the recommended target of 150–300 min of moderate-intensity aerobic PA per week. Expert 12 recommended incorporating personalized time management education to reduce scheduling barriers to PA participation. Accordingly, item Ⅲ-11 was added to provide structured guidance on organizing treatment, work, and daily responsibilities through flexible, conflict-free scheduling. Expert 13 noted that paper-based PA diaries may impose documentation burdens and yield illegible records. Item Ⅲ-17 was therefore revised to establish an online activity tracking system that enables timely digital diary uploads and systematic monitoring of adherence to the PA plan.

After two rounds of Delphi consultation, the finalized PA promotion intervention program comprised three first-level, ten second-level, and 25 third-level indicators. The addition of item Ⅲ-11 accounted for the increase of one third-level indicator relative to the second-round questionnaire. Indicator weights

The AHP was adopted in this Delphi study to translate the expert judgments into quantitative priority weights for the indicator system.38 Pairwise-comparison matrices were generated from the second-round mean importance scores using the predefined mapping rule detailed in Supplementary Table S5, and priority weights were calculated for each indicator at all three hierarchical levels. Consistency testing confirmed that all CR values were below the accepted threshold of 0.10, indicating acceptable consistency of all pairwise-comparison matrices. At the first level, motivation received the highest priority weight (0.49), followed by capability (0.31) and opportunity (0.20). At the second level, the highest-weighted indicators within the capability, opportunity, and motivation domains were Ⅱ-1, Ⅱ-6, and Ⅱ-10, with local weights of 0.54, 0.50, and 0.43, respectively. At the third level, Ⅲ-25 (Assessing primary and secondary outcomes at baseline and post-intervention) recorded the highest global weight (0.16). The weights of all second- and third-level indicators are detailed in Supplementary Table S6.

Discussion

Main findings

Guided by the COM-B model, this study developed an evidence-based, theoretically grounded PA promotion intervention program for physically inactive CRC survivors. The program was developed through a systematic literature review, a retrospective case analysis, and two rounds of Delphi consultation, with indicator weights established using the AHP. Across both consultation rounds, expert authority coefficients exceeded 0.70, the CV remained below 0.25, and Kendall's W reached statistical significance (both P < 0.001), indicating stable ratings and low but statistically significant inter-expert concordance. The finalized program comprised three first-level, ten second-level, and 25 third-level indicators. Weighting identified the motivation domain as the highest-weighted component, underscoring the central role of motivation in sustaining PA participation in this population. This program will undergo pilot feasibility testing to assess its acceptability, safety, and preliminary efficacy. The methodological approach may offer a replicable framework for developing complex behavioral interventions targeting cancer survivor populations.

Physical inactivity is highly prevalent among Chinese CRC survivors and is associated with disease- and treatment-specific barriers, including stoma-related concerns, chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, and LARS-related bowel dysfunction.39 Although PA confers substantial benefits by attenuating metabolic dysregulation and chronic inflammation and improving physical function and health-related QoL,40 clinically feasible, theoretically grounded interventions tailored to this population remain scarce. The Delphi method has been used to build expert consensus on exercise interventions in oncology, but existing Delphi-based work has notable methodological limitations. Boereboom et al. reported one of the first CRC-specific exercise consensus studies; however, their study was confined to perioperative prehabilitation and omitted both a behavior change framework and quantified indicator weighting.41 Wei and He likewise developed a nurse-led exercise program for patients with breast cancer undergoing chemotherapy; however, the program was restricted to the active-treatment setting and lacked theoretical grounding and evidence-based prioritization.42 The present study addresses these gaps in three substantive respects. First, both CRC-specific barriers and facilitators were systematically identified and used to design intervention themes, an approach rarely adopted in prior Delphi-based exercise programs. Second, these themes were deductively mapped onto the COM-B model subdimensions, ensuring theoretical traceability from behavioral determinants to intervention strategies. Third, AHP integration yielded quantified weights within a three-tiered hierarchical structure, with motivation as the highest-weighted domain, providing clinicians with evidence-based priorities for resource allocation at a level of methodological detail less commonly reported in comparable work. This barrier-informed, theoretically coherent, and rigorously weighted approach aligns with the patient-centered paradigm in oncology rehabilitation.

The initial indicator pool was derived from three complementary evidence sources: a systematic review of 32 eligible studies across six databases, six AGREE II–appraised clinical practice guidelines, and a retrospective analysis of 220 CRC survivors. Iterative integration of these streams yielded a preliminary framework of three first-level, nine second-level, and 22 third-level indicators, which two rounds of Delphi consultation refined into a finalized structure of three first-level, ten second-level, and 25 third-level indicators. The 15-member expert panel comprised specialists in clinical nursing, clinical medicine, rehabilitation medicine, and nursing management; 73.33% held associate senior or senior professional titles, and 46.67% had more than 20 years of clinical experience. Panel response rates of 75% and 100% exceeded the recommended 70% engagement threshold,36 and authority coefficients of 0.840 and 0.857 confirmed high panel credibility.37 The lower bound of the importance-score range increased from 4.13 to 4.47, the maximum CV decreased from 0.21 to 0.17, and Kendall's W rose from 0.188 to 0.228 (both P < 0.001), indicating low but statistically significant inter-expert concordance.34 All AHP consistency ratios fell below the accepted threshold of 0.10, indicating acceptable consistency of the pairwise-comparison matrices. Collectively, these findings support the methodological consistency of the program-development process, providing a structured, evidence-based framework to guide nursing practice and inform future research on reducing physical inactivity among CRC survivors in China.43

The program's development explicitly incorporated the distinctive barriers and facilitators of PA engagement among CRC survivors, drawing on the COM-B model, factors influencing PA participation, and clinical guideline recommendations to develop intervention indicators and derive indicator weights. The resulting distribution ranked motivation highest (0.49), followed by capability (0.31) and opportunity (0.20), consistent with our prior systematic review based on the COM-B model, which identified a lack of motivation as the primary barrier to PA participation in this population.20 These findings provide additional support for the relatively high priority assigned to the motivation domain. The higher weight assigned to motivation highlights the importance of incorporating motivational strategies throughout the intervention. CRC survivors frequently report reduced motivation and a marked dependence on clinician guidance to initiate and sustain behavior change.44 These barriers call for population-specific strategies, including fatigue-adapted graded activity for survivors with treatment-related exhaustion, structured psychoeducation to address recurrence-related exercise anxiety, and individualized goal-setting that acknowledges post-surgical limitations such as bowel dysfunction and body image concerns among stoma patients.9 Within the motivation dimension, Ⅱ-10 (local weight = 0.43) was the only indicator with a full-score rate of 100%, reflecting unanimous expert recognition that systematic knowledge consolidation at program completion is critical for sustaining long-term behavioral change. Furthermore, Ⅲ-25 (global weight = 0.16) emerged as the third-level indicator with the highest overall weight, affirming expert consensus on the importance of rigorous outcome evaluation for evidence-based programs.

Regarding capability, the high priority assigned to Ⅱ-1 (local weight = 0.54; global weight = 0.17) aligns with clinical guidelines recommending individualized pre-exercise fitness appraisal and referral to trained personnel for survivors with heterogeneous post-surgical impairments.28 The timing and level of supervision should be individualized according to treatment status, symptoms, comorbidities, and functional capacity.45 Within the opportunity domain, Ⅱ-6 (local weight = 0.50; global weight = 0.10) received the highest weighting. Structured process supervision delivered via digital behavioral cues, such as mHealth reminders and wearable activity monitors, has demonstrated feasibility and acceptability among CRC survivors and offers a scalable mechanism to sustain PA beyond face-to-face contact.46 The comparatively lower weight assigned to opportunity does not diminish its clinical relevance; rather, it suggests that environmental and structural facilitation functions as a necessary but insufficient condition for PA engagement and should be integrated with capability- and motivation-focused strategies.47 The weight distribution also carries implications for future research. The predominance of the motivational domain suggests a need to develop and validate motivation-specific assessment instruments for CRC survivors, as existing tools have inadequately captured the motivational profiles that govern sustained PA engagement.48 Future studies should examine whether the relative importance of opportunity differs among CRC survivors with a stoma, older survivors, and those who have completed adjuvant chemotherapy.49 Future trials should also extend follow-up beyond program completion to determine whether the motivational and knowledge-reinforcement strategies emphasized here translate into durable PA behavior change and sustained improvement in patient-reported outcomes.

Implications for nursing practice and research

To support clinical uptake, the program was designed to integrate into the existing CRC survivorship pathway rather than be a stand-alone service. Supplementary Table S4 details each component's weekly schedule, assessment plan, and division of labor. The six face-to-face sessions can be anchored to routine surveillance appointments, with baseline and endpoint assessments aligned to two scheduled visits and the remaining sessions delivered through nurse-led survivorship clinics or group education. The six mobile application sessions are delivered asynchronously between visits, shifting routine education and adherence monitoring to the mobile application to conserve in-person resources. Delivery draws on existing oncology team roles rather than additional staff positions. The oncology nurse acts as a case manager and provides continuity throughout all 12 weeks, while the treating oncologist oversees clinical safety by applying predefined clinical and hematologic safety thresholds to determine when PA sessions should be paused and when participants should be referred for further clinical evaluation. The rehabilitation physician and psychologist contribute at defined time points, and the stoma care nurse manages stoma-specific precautions during routine stoma follow-up. This pragmatic structure provides nurses with a practical blueprint for embedding the combined in-person and mobile intervention into routine survivorship care and supports subsequent feasibility testing.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, experts in sports science and clinical psychology were not represented on the Delphi panel, which may have limited the breadth of expert input into the exercise prescription and behavior change components. Although the intervention team itself includes rehabilitation physicians and psychologists, specialists in these two fields will be consulted to refine these components before feasibility testing. Second, Delphi consensus is based on expert judgment rather than empirical testing. Although questionnaires were completed independently and controlled feedback was provided, expert judgment remains inherently subjective. Third, the program targets physically inactive CRC survivors following curative-intent resection and does not extend to patients receiving palliative care or those with advanced or recurrent disease or other complex clinical conditions. Within this scope, the lack of subgroup stratification may limit its applicability to survivors who differ in stoma status, treatment history, and comorbidity. Fourth, cultural and regional variation in health care infrastructure, attitudes toward PA, and available community resources may limit generalizability, underscoring the need for cultural adaptation and contextual validation prior to broader implementation. Fifth, the feasibility of this program in its intended target population has yet to be established, leaving its real-world acceptability and deliverability unknown. Future research should therefore prioritize a pilot feasibility study assessing recruitment, retention, and adherence, with intervention content adapted to disease stage and stoma status.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study developed a PA promotion intervention program for CRC survivors who are physically inactive through a systematic literature review, a retrospective case analysis, and two rounds of Delphi consultation, with indicator weights assigned using AHP. Grounded in the COM-B model, the program comprises three first-level, ten second-level, and 25 third-level indicators, with motivation identified as the highest-weighted domain. The methodological approach may be reproducible when developing complex behavioral interventions in cancer survivor populations. However, as the program has not yet been implemented or empirically evaluated, it currently provides a theoretically grounded and expert-consensus-based framework that requires feasibility, acceptability, safety, and preliminary efficacy testing among CRC survivors who are physically inactive.
