Conditional Deletion of Linked, Noncontiguous Genes Using the Cre-Lox System
Authors: Guoli Zhu, Avital Swisa, Hannah M. Kolev, Eric L. Waite, Mark A. Tigue, Hilana El-Mekkoussi, Catherine Lee May, Klaus H. Kaestner
Published: 2026

Conditional gene ablation in mice using the Cre-lox system has revolutionized the genetic analysis of mammalian genes by enabling cell type–specific and/or inducible gene deletion. This technology has been employed to study the role of thousands of single genes, and occasionally of 2 unlinked genes, where the lox-flanked alleles can be combined into a single animal through breeding. In addition, 2 or more closely spaced genes of similar function can be ablated simultaneously by placing lox sites 5′ and 3′ to the gene cluster of interest. However, a difficulty arises when attempting to delete 2 noncontiguous genes at the same locus. Mammalian genomes contain multiple gene clusters where the chromosomal arrangement of genes with similar function is interrupted by interspersed, nonrelated genes. A prime example is the major histocompatibility complex, which, in humans, spans 3.6 megabase pairs containing hundreds of genes, not all of which are involved in immunity.1 Deriving lox-flanked alleles for 2 closely spaced genes separately and then breeding the 2 alleles to homozygosity is not possible due to the very low meiotic recombination frequency of tightly linked genes.

Here, we employed 2 sets of noncompatible lox sites to overcome this problem. We targeted the Fox gene minicluster on chromosome 8, where 2 mesenchymally expressed genes, Foxf1 and Foxl1, are interrupted by Foxc2 and Mthfsd (Figure 1A). Foxf1 and Foxl1 were shown by single null alleles to both affect the development of the gut tube by regulating mesenchymal to epithelial signaling.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 For simultaneous conditional ablation of Foxf1 and Foxl1 without perturbing the intervening genes, we flanked the 2 genes of interest with orthogonal, noncompatible lox sites. We employed a gene-targeting strategy with high targeting efficiency.9,10 Four different CRISPR RNA–containing ribonucleoprotein complexes were preassembled with the Cas9 ribonucleoprotein and injected with 4 lox-site–containing repair templates into mouse zygotes (Figure 1B). Notably, no toxicity was observed when all 4 single-stranded DNA repair templates were injected simultaneously. The 20 resulting pups were screened by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with primers flanking the 4 lox sites, followed by restriction digest and gel analysis (see Methods; data not shown). Unfortunately, only 2 of the 4 lox sites, flanking the Foxl1 gene, were correctly placed in the targeted mice. Therefore, we bred these Foxl1loxN mice to homozygosity, and repeated CRISPR/Cas9-assisted gene targeting in zygotes from these mice with redesigned guide RNA and repair templates for the 2 Foxf1-flanking loxP sites. Of 7 offspring screened, 2 were positive by PCR (Figure 1C), and the correct placement of all 4 lox sites was further confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure 1D). To ascertain that the 4 lox sites are in cis, we employed long-read sequencing of genomic DNA (Figure 1E). Correctly targeted mice were bred to homozygosity and are phenotypically normal and healthy. Summary information on gene targeting and outcomes is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

To assess if the 4 lox sites can be targeted by Cre recombinase as intended, with both Foxf1 and Foxl1 deleted but the intervening genes maintained, we treated mice heterozygous for the double-floxed Foxf1loxP/Foxl1loxN allele with a liver-targeting adeno-associated virus (AAV) expressing Cre. Ten days following the injection, genomic DNA from liver was prepared. We employed real-time PCR to assess the abundance of the DNA fragments containing the 4 genes in question (Figure 2A and B). The AAV-Cre treatment caused a dramatic increase in the amplicons detecting the recombined Foxf1 and Foxl1 genes, with no loss of the intervening Mthfsd PCR amplicon (Figure 2C). To directly show the final, post-Cre configuration of the locus, we employed long-read sequencing of genomic DNA of hepatocytes isolated from homozygous Foxf1loxP/loxP/Foxl1loxN/loxN mice treated with the AAV-Cre virus. The locus was recombined as intended, with single lox sites remaining at the Foxf1 and Foxl1 genes, whereas the sequences of the intervening Foxc2 and Mthfsd genes were retained (Figure 2D). Of note, the expected deletion was observed in ∼73% of the alleles. This finding suggests that, in all cells that receive the AAV-Cre virus, deletion of both lox-flanked genes is highly efficient. Analysis of the whole-genome long-read data did not reveal evidence of unintended lox site insertions elsewhere in the genome. To validate gene deletion at the protein level, we performed immunofluorescence staining on embryonic (E15.5) duodenum from Pdgfra-Cre; Foxf1loxP/loxP/Foxl1loxN/loxN mice (cDKO mice). Costaining for PDGFRA, FOXF1, and FOXL1 demonstrated a marked loss of both proteins in PDGFRA+ mesenchymal cells compared with littermate controls (Figure 2E), confirming efficient deletion. Notably, FOXF1 expression was preserved in PDGFRA− cells, consistent with the expected cell type–specific activity of Pdgfra-Cre. In sum, we present a novel strategy for the simultaneously conditional removal of 2 closely linked but noncontiguous genes. This methodology enables the genetic analysis of functionally related and/or partially redundant genes not previously possible. This approach can also be used to derive double null alleles for noncontiguous genes by simple breeding of the double-floxed alleles to a ‘Cre deleter’ strain, which effects lox site recombination in the germline.
