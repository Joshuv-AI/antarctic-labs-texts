Endoscopic resection of lumbar intradural spinal tumors: A case series on feasibility, safety, and preliminary outcomes
Authors: Facundo Van Isseldyk, Vincent Hagel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntradural spinal tumors are traditionally managed with open microsurgical approaches that provide effective tumor control but involve substantial soft tissue disruption. Although full endoscopic spine surgery is well established for degenerative pathology, its use for intradural tumors remains limited due to challenges related to dural opening, cerebrospinal fluid management, and intradural manipulation.Research questionCan fully endoscopic resection of lumbar intradural extramedullary tumors be performed safely and effectively, and what technical adaptations are required?MethodsA retrospective case series of 10 consecutive patients undergoing uniportal full endoscopic resection of lumbar intradural extramedullary tumors between 2020 and 2024 was analyzed. All procedures were performed via an interlaminar approach by experienced endoscopic surgeons. Operative data, complications, extent of resection, and neurological outcomes were collected. Functional status was assessed using the modified McCormick scale, and postoperative magnetic resonance imaging evaluated extent of resection.ResultsThe cohort included 6 women and 4 men with a mean age of 58.3 years. Pathology revealed schwannoma (n = 7), meningioma (n = 2), and neurofibroma (n = 1). Gross total resection was achieved in all cases. Mean operative time was 96.5 min, and mean hospital stay was 1.5 days. No intraoperative, cerebrospinal fluid–related, or new neurological complications occurred. Modified McCormick scores improved in 6 patients (60%) and remained stable in 4 (40%).ConclusionsFully endoscopic resection of selected lumbar intradural extramedullary tumors is feasible and safe when performed by experienced surgeons. Favorable early neurological outcomes can be achieved with careful irrigation control and adapted dural closure techniques.

Introduction

Intradural spinal tumors constitute a relatively uncommon yet clinically significant subset of spinal neoplasms, representing approximately 5–10% of all spinal tumors. Most are intradural-extramedullary lesions-such as meningiomas, schwannomas, and neurofibromas-that produce progressive neurological impairment due to compression of the spinal cord or nerve roots (Liu et al., 2024). The primary goal of surgical treatment is complete tumor resection with maximal preservation or improvement of neurological function (Roehrkasse et al., 2024).

Open microsurgical techniques via laminectomy or laminotomy have established an excellent standard of care for intradural-extramedullary spinal tumors, consistently achieving high rates of gross total resection and favorable neurological outcomes (Tigre et al.). These approaches remain the gold standard and are associated with low rates of major complications in experienced hands. Nevertheless, the surgical access required - including paraspinal muscle dissection and varying degrees of bony removal - is associated with well-documented approach-related morbidity, including postoperative pain, prolonged hospitalization, risk of muscle atrophy, and, in some cases, epidural fibrosis or segmental instability (Ottenhausen et al., 2019). These factors are particularly relevant in elderly or medically frail patients, in whom minimizing surgical trauma may have meaningful implications for recovery. It is in this context - not as a replacement for microsurgery, but as a complementary option for selected patients - that fully endoscopic resection warrants systematic investigation.

Over the past two decades, endoscopic spine surgery has transformed the management of degenerative spinal pathologies. Through small incisions and the use of working channels under continuous endoscopic visualization, these techniques offer equivalent neural decompression while minimizing tissue trauma, reducing blood loss, and shortening hospital stay (Van Isseldyk et al., 2024). Despite these advantages, their application to intradural spinal pathology has remained extremely limited, primarily due to the technical complexities unique to intradural work (Hagel and Isseldyk, 2024; Dolas et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2023; Suvithayasiri et al., 2023).

Performing intradural tumor resection using a fully endoscopic approach introduces a series of technical considerations that are distinct from those encountered in extradural endoscopic surgery. These include achieving a controlled dural opening under continuous irrigation, maintaining visualization without elevating intracranial or intradural pressure, carefully manipulating tumors near critical neural structures within a confined working corridor, and closing the dura securely to prevent cerebrospinal fluid leakage (Hagel and Isseldyk, 2024). Addressing these challenges requires not only technical precision but thoughtful adaptation of established endoscopic maneuvers (Wu et al., 2024). To date, these challenges have limited the systematic adoption of endoscopy for intradural tumor surgery, and standardized technical frameworks remain lacking.

Nevertheless, the potential benefits of reduced tissue disruption, smaller surgical footprints, and accelerated recovery make endoscopic resection an appealing frontier for selected cases of intradural tumors. We hypothesized that, with careful adaptation, the same endoscopic techniques employed for degenerative pathology could be extended to intradural surgery-offering similar oncological and neurological outcomes to traditional open approaches, while preserving the advantages of minimally invasive access (Isseldyk et al., 2022).

In this study, we present our initial experience with fully endoscopic resection of intradural spinal tumors. We describe the technical aspects of the procedure-including endoscopic dural opening, tumor manipulation, and closure techniques-and report clinical and surgical outcomes in a retrospective case series of 10 patients. A supplementary video is provided to illustrate key procedural steps and highlight the technical adaptations required for safe and effective execution of this novel approach.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

We conducted a retrospective analysis of 10 consecutive patients who underwent endoscopic resection of lumbar intradural spinal tumors between 2020 and 2024 at HPR-Asklepios. This initial case series was limited to lumbar intradural tumors, a decision intended to reduce anatomical variability and procedural risk during the early experience with the endoscopic approach.

During the same 5-year period, a total of 51 lumbar intradural tumor resections were performed by the operative group. Of these, 10 patients (approximately 20%) met criteria for the endoscopic approach and constitute the present series. The remaining 41 cases were managed via open microsurgical techniques, reflecting the deliberate and selective application of endoscopy during the early adoption phase of this technique.

Demographic, radiologic, surgical, and clinical data were collected from medical records. This study involved a retrospective review of fully anonymized clinical and surgical data collected as part of routine medical care. In accordance with the institutional and national research ethics guidelines, ethical approval was not required for this type of study. No identifiable patient information was used, and all data were analyzed in compliance with principles of patient confidentiality and the Declaration of Helsinki.

All procedures were performed by surgeons with extensive prior experience in uniportal endoscopic spine surgery for degenerative lumbar pathology, with more than 1500 cases each.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia with the patient placed in the prone position on a radiolucent table. Neuromonitoring (somatosensory and motor evoked potentials) was employed when available.

A uniportal endoscopic approach was performed via an interlaminar window. A 0.8–1.0 cm skin incision was made over the target level under fluoroscopic guidance. Blunt dilators and a working channel were sequentially introduced into the interlaminar space. After enlarging the interlaminar window with a high-speed drill, the ligamentum flavum was removed under endoscopic visualization to expose the dura.

Prior to dural opening, meticulous hemostasis was performed to ensure a clear endoscopic field. The irrigation pressure was then reduced to its minimum, allowing venous capillary bleeding to become visible. Subsequently, the pressure was gradually increased until the bleeding ceased. This maneuver enabled identification of the minimum effective irrigation pressure required to proceed safely with the intradural phase of the procedure.

Dural opening was carried out longitudinally using an endoscopic scalpel or microsurgical scissors and a specialized dural hook, creating a window sufficient for tumor exposure. The tumor was dissected from the arachnoid and neural structures using a combination of endoscopic microdissectors, bipolar coagulation, forceps, and working sheath maneuvers. Tumor removal was performed either piecemeal or en bloc depending on its size and consistency. Hemostasis was secured with bipolar cautery. Dural closure was performed under endoscopic visualization using interrupted 5-0 Prolene sutures with an endoscopic needle holder, or by applying a dural patch over the defect. In all cases in which a dural patch was applied (n = 7), closure was performed without the use of additional sealants. No CSF-related complications were observed in either closure group. The wound was closed in layers, and no drains were placed. Immediate postoperative ambulation was encouraged.

Outcome measures

Data collected included age, sex, tumor type, level, size, operative time, estimated blood loss, dural closure technique, intraoperative and postoperative complications, length of hospital stay, and time to return to work.

Neurological status was assessed preoperatively and at discharge using the modified McCormick scale. The modified McCormick scale was selected as the primary functional outcome due to its widespread use in intradural spinal tumor surgery and its sensitivity to clinically meaningful neurological change. Postoperative MRI (within 72 h) confirmed the extent of resection. Postoperative MRI was formally assessed and reported by a staff radiologist independent of the surgical team. Gross total resection was defined as the absence of residual tumor on contrast-enhanced MRI, as determined by this independent radiological evaluation. For benign intradural-extramedullary tumors, particularly schwannomas and meningiomas, our institutional follow-up protocol includes MRI at 3 months, 12 months, and annually thereafter for a minimum of 5 years, given the known potential for late recurrence in these histological subtypes.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (range). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages.

Results

Patient and tumor characteristics

Ten patients (6 female, 4 male) with a mean age of 58.3 years (range: 46–72) underwent endoscopic resection of lumbar intradural tumors. All tumors were intradural-extramedullary and located between L2 and L5. Histopathological diagnoses included schwannoma (n = 7), meningioma (n = 2), and neurofibroma (n = 1). Tumor size ranged from 10 to 40 mm, with a mean diameter of 24.5 mm (SD ± 9.8 mm). Presenting symptoms varied and included radicular pain (n = 6), neurogenic claudication (n = 2), and motor deficits (n = 2).

Surgical outcomes

Mean operative time was 96.5 ± 19.7 min (range: 70–125 min). No intraoperative complications were observed. Dural closure was performed using either endoscopic suturing (n = 3) or a dural patch technique (n = 7). Endoscopic suturing was performed using a specialized endoscopic needle holder with interrupted 5–0 Prolene stitches placed under continuous visualization. The dural patch technique involved positioning a collagen-based inlay graft over the durotomy defect, followed by overlay reinforcement, without the use of additional sealants. Both techniques resulted in zero CSF-related complications. Postoperative MRI confirmed gross total resection in all cases. No patient developed new neurological deficits.Mean hospital stay was 1.5 ± 0.5 days (range: 1–2 days).

Modified McCormick scores improved in 6 patients (60%), remained stable in 4 (40%), and worsened in none. Preoperative motor deficits were present in 2 patients, both of whom showed postoperative improvement.

Statistical results

Wilcoxon signed-rank testing demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in modified McCormick grades from preoperative to postoperative evaluations (p = 0.014). Subgroup analysis showed that all three patients who received endoscopic dural suturing achieved McCormick grade 1 postoperatively, although this group also had slightly longer operative times (mean: 113 min) compared to the patch group (mean: 91.7 min). No differences were observed in hospital stay or complication rates between the groups.

Illustrative case example (case number 2)

A 68-year-old female presented with progressive left L5 radicular pain associated with dorsiflexion weakness. Preoperative magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated a left-sided intradural extramedullary lesion at the L5 level, extending from the midline toward the L5–S1 foraminal region and producing significant compression of the left L5 nerve root.

Surgery was performed using a uniportal full endoscopic interlaminar approach at L4–L5 with a 3.7-mm working channel endoscope. A tailored L5 laminectomy was carried out to optimize the angle of endoscopic visualization, taking advantage of endoscope tilting to access the foraminal extension of the lesion while preserving surrounding posterior elements. Following longitudinal dural opening under reduced irrigation pressure, the tumor–neural interface was identified after arachnoid dissection. Circumferential dissection and internal debulking allowed safe mobilization and gross total resection of the lesion under continuous endoscopic visualization.

Dural closure was achieved using a dural patch with an inlay–overlay technique, obtaining a watertight seal. Postoperatively, the patient experienced significant improvement in motor function, with resolution of dorsiflexion weakness. No wound-related or cerebrospinal fluid–related complications were observed. Follow-up magnetic resonance imaging at 24 months confirmed sustained gross total resection without evidence of tumor recurrence.

Discussion

This study presents a preliminary experience with the endoscopic resection of lumbar intradural tumors, demonstrating the technical feasibility, safety, and favorable short-term outcomes of adapting uniportal techniques-traditionally employed in degenerative spine surgery-for the management of intradural pathology. To our knowledge, only a limited number of publications have documented the use of a fully endoscopic approach for intradural tumor resection, and most available evidence remains confined to isolated case reports or small series.

Recent literature has demonstrated the feasibility of percutaneous full-endoscopic techniques for the management of spinal foraminal schwannomas, particularly for small, well-selected epidural lesions. In the largest reported series to date (Hu et al., 2023), Hu et al. analyzed 25 patients with epidural foraminal schwannomas treated using full-endoscopic interlaminar or extraforaminal approaches, achieving high rates of gross total resection with minimal morbidity. Importantly, intradural tumors were explicitly excluded from that cohort, and no dural opening or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) management was required. Consequently, critical aspects inherent to intradural surgery-such as dural opening, dural closure strategies, intradural pressure dynamics, and CSF-related complications-remain largely unaddressed in the existing endoscopic literature. The present series specifically focuses on intradural pathology, thereby extending the application of endoscopic spine surgery beyond epidural lesions and addressing a distinct set of technical and physiological challenges that have not been systematically reported.

Gross total resection (GTR) was achieved in all patients, with no postoperative neurological deterioration, no cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)–related complications, and low perioperative morbidity. These results compare favorably with historical microsurgical series, which report GTR rates of 90–100% and new postoperative deficits in 5–15% of patients, depending on tumor characteristics and surgical complexity (Czyżewski et al., 2025; Randhawa et al., 2024). The absence of complications and significant functional improvement observed in 60% of our patients-as measured by the modified McCormick scale-suggests that endoscopic resection may offer oncological and neurological outcomes comparable to those of conventional microsurgical approaches.

The minimally invasive nature of the endoscopic approach likely contributed to the low morbidity and accelerated recovery observed in this series. By avoiding extensive paraspinal muscle dissection and minimizing bony removal, this technique reduces surgical trauma and facilitates early mobilization. Mean hospital stay was 1.5 days, notably shorter than the 4–7 days typically reported following open resection (Wong et al.). Although postoperative pain and analgesic use were not formally recorded, patients consistently reported minimal incision-related discomfort and a rapid return to daily activities-observations that echo prior findings in endoscopic decompression for degenerative pathology (Liu et al., 2023).

One of the key conceptual contributions of this series is the pragmatic approach to irrigation pressure management during intradural endoscopy. While irrigation is essential for maintaining a clear endoscopic view, it poses the risk of elevating intradural pressure once the dura is opened. This may theoretically result in transient neurological or hemodynamic effects (Farshad et al., 2024; Vargas et al., 2023). In our experience, careful modulation of irrigation flow, combined with brief pauses to vent the dural sac, successfully mitigated these risks. We propose estimating the minimum indispensable irrigation pressure as the lowest pressure sufficient to suppress venous capillary bleeding and maintain a stable endoscopic field, while avoiding visible dural sac distension, excessive nerve root displacement, or tension on intradural neural elements. Rather than applying a fixed irrigation pressure, this pressure was dynamically titrated intraoperatively based on real-time visual feedback, allowing safe progression of intradural dissection while minimizing the risk of elevated intradural pressure and its potential neurological or hemodynamic consequences.

The concern regarding potential tumor cell dissemination via continuous irrigation deserves direct attention. The histologies encountered in this series - schwannomas, meningiomas, and neurofibromas - are encapsulated benign tumors with no established propensity for CSF seeding, distinguishing them biologically from lesions such as ependymomas or other CSF-disseminating neoplasms. As the preoperative diagnosis may not always be confirmed histologically, this consideration should inform patient selection: cases with preoperative imaging or clinical features suggestive of a non-encapsulated or potentially seeding lesion should be excluded from the endoscopic approach until further evidence is available. No patient in the present series developed distant intradural tumor manifestations at follow-up imaging; however, longer follow-up and larger series will be required to provide definitive reassurance on this point.

Dural closure represents another critical step. In open surgery, watertight suture closure is standard. Endoscopically, however, the restricted working corridor and continuous irrigation make suturing technically demanding. In our series, dural closure was achieved via two strategies: endoscopic suturing (n = 3) and dural patch placement (n = 7), with both approaches resulting in successful closure. No patient developed postoperative CSF leakage, pseudomeningocele, or wound complications. Although subgroup analysis showed that the suturing group had slightly longer operative times, all patients in this group reached McCormick grade 1 at follow-up, suggesting excellent clinical outcomes. These findings indicate that both closure techniques can be effective when properly executed, though further research is needed to refine and standardize endoscopic dural repair techniques. Regarding the two strategies employed: endoscopic suturing provides direct anatomical coaptation and was associated with excellent functional outcomes in our series, with all three patients in the suturing group achieving McCormick grade 1 postoperatively. However, it remains technically demanding due to limited instrument maneuverability within the working channel and the challenge of knot-tying under irrigation. The dural patch technique is technically more accessible and was the preferred strategy in our series, particularly for durotomies not amenable to primary suture closure due to size or location. The absence of CSF-related complications in both groups suggests that each approach can achieve adequate dural sealing when correctly executed.

The learning curve for endoscopic intradural tumor surgery should not be underestimated. Key steps-including dural opening, tumor manipulation within a narrow corridor, and dural suturing under continuous irrigation-require a high degree of technical skill. In our cohort, all procedures were performed by surgeons with extensive experience in uniportal endoscopic spine surgery for degenerative conditions. The operative maneuvers-bone drilling, ligamentum flavum removal, dura mobilization-are adaptations of techniques used routinely in cases such as lumbar stenosis or juxtafacet cyst resection (Balain et al., 2023). This continuity likely enabled a smoother transition to intradural applications and may reduce the number of cases needed to achieve proficiency. Nevertheless, for surgeons without a background in endoscopy, the adoption of this approach would necessitate dedicated training, mentorship, and careful patient selection. We recommend that surgeons approaching intradural endoscopy for the first time follow a stepwise progression. A solid foundation in uniportal endoscopic decompression for degenerative pathology - including routine proficiency in interlaminar approaches, ligamentum flavum resection, and dural mobilization - is a prerequisite. The next step should involve endoscopic management of epidural lesions, such as juxtafacet cysts or foraminal schwannomas, to develop familiarity with neural handling within the endoscopic corridor. Only thereafter, with dedicated mentorship and in carefully selected cases, should surgeons attempt intentional durotomy and intradural work. Formal cadaveric training and observation of live cases are strongly encouraged before independent practice. (Training and credentialing for endoscopic, 2026).

Appropriate patient selection was central to our early positive results. We restricted our cohort to lumbar intradural-extramedullary tumors smaller than 4 cm, avoiding multisegmental lesions and extensive laminectomies. This cautious strategy reflects the deliberate introduction of a novel technique in a controlled clinical context. Future studies should assess whether these outcomes are replicable in more complex cases, including cervical or thoracic tumors, or those requiring multi-level exposure.

Beyond size and location, certain tumor characteristics represent current contraindications that surgeons should consider carefully. Calcified tumors impose mechanical demands that exceed the current capabilities of instruments within the uniportal corridor and should be reserved for open resection. Highly vascularized tumors represent another contraindication in the current form of the technique, as hemostasis within the intradural endoscopic corridor relies primarily on bipolar coagulation and irrigation pressure modulation, without the tactile control and tamponade options available in open surgery. For meningiomas with a broad dural base, complete dural excision - as required for Simpson Grade I resection - cannot be reliably performed endoscopically at this time. In our two meningioma cases, the dural attachment was managed with bipolar coagulation of the insertion site, equivalent to a Simpson Grade II resection, which carries an acknowledged risk of late recurrence compared to formal dural excision. This limitation must be discussed with patients during informed consent, and long-term imaging surveillance is particularly important for this histological subtype.

Direct nerve stimulation (DNS), routinely used in open microsurgery to identify and preserve functional nerve fibers adherent to schwannomas, was performed in all cases using a DNS probe adapted for use within the endoscopic working channel. This capability addresses one of the most frequently cited concerns regarding the oncological safety of endoscopic intradural tumor surgery, allowing real-time identification of eloquent nerve fibers before and during tumor dissection. In addition, all patients underwent continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring with somatosensory evoked potentials, motor evoked potentials, and free-running electromyography. The combination of DNS and continuous neuromonitoring provided a multi-layered neurophysiological safety framework comparable to that available in open microsurgery.

Ultrasonic aspiration was employed in selected cases using the Söring Endoscopic Neurosurgical Pen (ENP), a neuroendoscopic ultrasonic aspirator specifically designed to be guided through the working channel of an endoscope, allowing simultaneous fragmentation and aspiration of tumors of varying consistency. Originally developed for intraventricular neurosurgical procedures, its sonotrode profile proved compatible with the uniportal spine endoscope working channel, extending its potential application to the intradural spinal setting. This instrument proved particularly useful for larger or firmer tumors, facilitating internal debulking and safe mobilization of the tumor away from adherent neural structures. Intraoperative ultrasound was not employed in this series and warrants prospective evaluation as a complementary tool for real-time resection control in future studies.

From a functional standpoint, outcomes were favorable and statistically significant. The modified McCormick grades improved in 60% of patients and remained stable in the rest. Wilcoxon signed-rank testing confirmed a significant difference between pre- and postoperative grades (p = 0.014). All patients who underwent endoscopic dural suturing achieved grade 1 functionally, further supporting the clinical efficacy of this approach. Fig. 2 clearly illustrates this trend, highlighting consistent improvements across most cases.

Finally, endoscopy may offer additional benefits beyond clinical outcomes. The combination of reduced skin incision, muscle preservation, and decreased blood loss may lead to enhanced patient satisfaction, reduced resource utilization, and improved cosmetic outcomes-advantages well-documented in the degenerative literature (Muthu) and now increasingly relevant in tumor surgery.

Study limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the small sample size and retrospective design limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, the absence of a concurrent open microsurgery control group is a fundamental limitation of this study. No direct statistical comparison with conventional approaches can be drawn, and claims of superiority over open techniques are not supported by the present data. The results should be interpreted exclusively as a feasibility and safety report within a carefully selected patient cohort.Third, functional outcomes such as pain scores and return to work were not formally assessed. Fourth, the relatively short follow-up prevents analysis of long-term tumor recurrence, spinal instability, or delayed CSF-related complications. Finally, inter-surgeon variability in closure technique introduces a degree of heterogeneity that could affect results. Nonetheless, the consistent achievement of gross total resection, the absence of complications, and the functional improvements observed support the feasibility of the approach in carefully selected patients.

Perspectives for future research

Future investigations should prioritize multicenter, prospective studies comparing endoscopic and open techniques using standardized outcome measures, longer follow-up, and larger cohorts. Specific areas of interest include the refinement of endoscopic dural closure instruments, the integration of real-time intradural pressure monitoring systems, and the development of training modules for safe adoption of the technique. Additionally, expanding indications to include cervical, thoracic and multilevel lesions-once safety parameters are established-could further broaden the applicability of this approach. Ultimately, defining the optimal patient population, technical steps, and outcome benchmarks will be essential for establishing the role of endoscopy in intradural spinal tumor surgery.

Conclusion

In this preliminary case series, we demonstrated that endoscopic resection of intradural spinal tumors is a feasible and safe technique in selected patients, achieving gross total resection and neurological outcomes comparable to those reported with open microsurgical approaches. By adapting endoscopic maneuvers traditionally used in degenerative spine surgery, we were able to apply a minimally invasive corridor to intradural pathology without compromising oncological or functional goals. The endoscopic approach may offer additional benefits, including reduced soft tissue disruption, shorter hospitalization, and earlier recovery. However, this technique remains technically demanding and requires prior experience with endoscopic spine surgery, particularly for dural suturing and intradural neural handling. Careful patient selection-including consideration of tumor size, location, and surgeon experience-is critical for safe implementation during the early adoption phase. While these initial results are encouraging, larger studies with longer follow-up are necessary to validate these findings, further refine technical aspects, and better define the role of endoscopic surgery in the management of intradural spinal tumors.
