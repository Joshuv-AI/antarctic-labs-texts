Bronchial rheoplasty for chronic bronchitis: real-world evidence of safety and performance
Authors: Judith Brock, Felix Herth, Kaid Darwiche, Ralf-Harto Hübner, Dirk Skowasch, Björn Schwick, Wolfgang Gesierich, Christian Schumann, Arschang Valipour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBronchial rheoplasty (BR) addresses mucosal inflammation and hypersecretion associated with chronic bronchitis (CB) by ablating goblet cell hyperplasia with nonthermal pulsed electric fields. This study assessed if BR continues to demonstrate a safe profile and symptom burden reduction in CB patients.MethodsThis observational, post-market, single-arm registry was conducted at eight centres in Austria and Germany. Patients with CB and high symptom burden (COPD assessment test (CAT) score ≥7 points in the first two questions) were treated with two bronchoscopic BR procedures. The primary outcomes were safety and symptom burden at 12 months, evaluated by (serious) adverse events ((S)AEs) and questionnaire scores (CAT and St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ)). Subgroup analyses were performed to identify treatment responders.Results106 BR procedures were performed in 54 patients (mean forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) 57.8±25.3% predicted, residual volume 166.2±39.6% predicted, CAT 24.8±6.8) with a follow-up time of up to 12 months (n=40). The procedure could be performed safely, with only two SAEs (thoracic pain) and 10 respiratory AEs (mainly COPD exacerbation) deemed to be related to the device or procedure over 12 months. Patients experienced statistically significant questionnaire score improvements in CAT (−5.5±8.0 at 6 months, −4.2±7.4 at 12 months) and SGRQ at 6 and 12 months. CAT responders (improvement ≥the minimal clinically important difference of 2 points) had more preserved lung function and better exercise capacity at baseline than CAT nonresponders.ConclusionThe real-world clinical experience demonstrates that BR is a relatively safe and promising treatment option for CB patients with significant symptom burden.

Introduction

COPD is a complex, heterogeneous disease characterised by chronic airway inflammation with destruction of adjacent alveoli and vasculature causing persistent and progressive airflow obstruction. Symptoms range from debilitating dyspnoea to chronic productive cough, wheezing and fatigue [1]. Primarily caused by cigarette smoking, COPD currently ranks third worldwide in terms of disease burden and is a major cause of mortality [2]. Emphysema and chronic bronchitis (CB) are the traditional phenotypes; however, additional phenotypes are increasingly being recognised such as the frequent exacerbators and those with rapid lung function decline. Moreover, these clinical categories often overlap with one another and patients often present with different combinations and intensities of symptoms [2].

The predominant treatment option for CB patients is smoking cessation. Pharmacological therapies are similar for a vast majority of COPD patients depending on the stage of disease and primarily include inhaled medications such as long-acting β-agonists, long-acting muscarinic antagonists, anticholinergics and inhaled corticosteroids [2]. The goal of pharmacotherapy in this context is primarily to reduce symptom burden as well as the frequency and severity of exacerbations. CB is characterised by increased and more viscous sputum and cough, which is associated with multiple clinical consequences, including poorer health-related quality of life, increased lung function decline, risk of exacerbations and increased all-cause mortality [3–6]. Inhalation and respiratory therapy are recommended for CB patients. However, the currently available therapy does not adequately target the mucus hypersecretion associated with CB, with many patients with CB remaining symptomatic despite optimal medical therapy.

Bronchial rheoplasty (BR; RheOx, Galvanize Therapeutics, Redwood City, CA, USA) was designed to treat the chronic inflammation and mucus hypersecretion associated with CB through the application of a specific form of nonthermal pulsed electric fields to the airway mucosa [7, 8]. BR reduces the hyperplastic mucus-producing cells, including goblet cells, while preserving the extracellular matrix, allowing for re-epithelialisation with a more normal distribution of epithelial cells [8]. The therapy has demonstrated safety and clinically meaningful improvements in symptom burden through 12 months, as well as significant reduction in goblet cell hyperplasia on histology in a multicentre 30-patient study [8]. Efficacy and safety were confirmed in a further observational study of 21 patients [9].

This registry study was designed to prospectively evaluate performance, safety and efficacy of BR in patients with moderate to severe CB in a typical clinical setting.

Materials and methods

This study is a prospective, observational, multicentre, open-label, single-arm registry (clinicaltrials.gov NCT04182841). It was designed to collect real-world clinical experience across multiple European centres regarding the performance, safety and efficacy of BR when used in typical clinical practice. The study protocol and informed consent form were approved by the local ethics committee at each study centre. All patients provided written informed consent prior to screening. The study was conducted in concordance with the Declaration of Helsinki in its current version.

Study subjects

Participating sites were selected based on their expertise in pulmonology and advanced bronchoscopic techniques. Study inclusion criteria were based on the CE mark instructions for use (IFU). Patients treated in the study met all of the requirements of the indication statement and none of the contraindications listed in the RheOx IFUs. RheOx with the RheOx catheter is indicated for the treatment of symptoms due to CB in patients with moderate to severe CB in the European Union. Moderate to severe CB was defined as a sum of the first two questions of the COPD assessment test (CAT) questionnaire of ≥6 points, in the absence of masquerading conditions (e.g., chronic rhinosinusitis, uncontrolled gastroesophageal reflux disease). Because the application of electric current in synchronisation with the heart is not feasible when irregular rhythms are present, contraindications for treatment included patients with an implantable cardioverter defibrillator, pacemaker or any other implantable electronic device, patients with a history of ventricular tachyarrhythmia, any clinically significant atrial tachyarrhythmia (i.e., abnormality with vital signs) and/or history of second- or third-degree AV block. Additionally, patients with airway stent(s), valves, coils or other lung implant/prosthesis were excluded. There were no exclusions related to lung function or emphysema (e.g., computed tomography-based densitometry).

Study design

Technical details of the BR procedure have been previously described [8]. Patients received BR treatment in two bronchoscopic sessions, the first to treat the right lung and the second to treat the left lung 4–6 weeks later. All accessible lobes and airways were treated; however, in several patients, the catheter could not be deployed in the apical segments of the left upper or right upper lobe due to a steep angle. The number of activations was dependent upon patient anatomy.

Post-procedure hospital stay and follow-up visits were scheduled per individual institution standard of care and as deemed necessary per the medical judgement of the investigator. End-point assessments were recommended to be conducted at 1, 3, 6, 12 and 24 months following completion of the final BR procedure.

Data collection

The primary outcomes were safety and efficacy at 12 months. Safety was assessed through the collection and monitoring of respiratory adverse events (AEs), and adverse device effects. Events were summarised using a standard medical coding dictionary (MedDRA) and analysed by frequency, seriousness, severity, and relatedness to the device or to the BR procedure(s) within discrete time periods including the treatment recovery period defined as the 30 days following each treatment.

Efficacy was assessed via respiratory specific questionnaires at baseline and follow-up including St. George's Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) and CAT. Body plethysmography (post-bronchodilator (BD) forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), lung volumes (residual volume (RV), total lung capacity (TLC), vital capacity (VC)), 6-min walking distance (6MWD) and device performance were also evaluated.

Analysis

Formal sample size calculations were not undertaken for this study. As this was an open label registry study, descriptive statistics were utilised. Categorical data were reported using summary statistics such as frequency distributions. Certain dichotomous data (e.g., subjects with SAEs) are also presented as rates. Continuous data were summarised using mean±SD, medians, minimums, maximums and interquartile ranges where applicable. Changes in CAT and SGRQ were also reported as dichotomised variables representing improvement above or below the threshold of minimal clinically important difference (MCID), with a change of 2 points for the CAT score [10] and a change of 4 points for SGRQ [11] as MCID. CB scores for cough and phlegm were further analysed separately. Further subgroup analysis was undertaken for patients that were identified as CAT responders (change of ≥2 points) versus non-CAT responders (change of <2 points). Additionally, a post hoc medical review of the data identified 12 patients who appeared to exhibit a mixed phenotype with CB symptoms accompanied by severe air-trapping. In the absence of radiologic assessment, these mixed-phenotype patients were characterised by an RV/TLC ≥0.65 [12]. These “mixed-phenotype” patients were compared to “CB-dominant” patients for efficacy analyses.

All calculations were based on available data; no imputations or extrapolations were made to replace missing values. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

Results

A total of 54 patients (33 men, 21 women) were enrolled and treated at eight centres in Austria and Germany between February 2020 and August 2022. 52 of the 54 patients received both BR treatments and two patients received one treatment for a total of 106 procedures performed in the study. 40 patients completed a 12-month visit, two patients withdrew, one patient died prior to 12 months and 11 patients did not complete a visit (figure 1).

Mixed-phenotype patients were older with lower FEV1 (39% versus 65% predicted), lower 6MWD (221 versus 388 m), higher smoking history (46 versus 30 pack-years) and more hyperinflation (RV 200% versus 152% predicted) despite similar symptom scores.

Safety

Patients tolerated the BR procedures well with no serious procedural complications reported. A mean±sd of 56.3±15.3 activations were applied per lung ranging from 16 to 102 activations based on patient anatomy and airway accessibility. Patients were discharged a median of 1 day following the procedure with 85% of procedures (90 out of 106) resulting in a hospital stay of 2 days or less.

A total of 67 respiratory AEs were reported. The most frequently reported nonserious respiratory AEs (table 2) were COPD exacerbation (14 events), coronavirus infection (seven events), pneumonia (six events), common cold (three events), and haemoptysis (three events). Nine nonserious respiratory adverse events were deemed possibly, probably or definitely related to the device only (two) or the device and the procedure (seven), which included COPD exacerbation (four), haemoptysis (two), cough (one), dyspnoea (one), and lung scar (one). Pneumonia (one event) was deemed possibly related to the procedure only.

Overall, COPD exacerbation rates in the study were low. Through 12 months the severe exacerbation rate was 0.05 when excluding the treatment recovery period. Similarly moderate exacerbation rates were 0.16 when excluding the treatment recovery period.

No unanticipated adverse events were reported in the study. In addition, despite delivering electrical current in close proximity to the heart, no device-related and only two procedure-related cardiac events (bigeminy and supraventricular extrasystoles) were reported in the study.

Efficacy

Significant mean±sd changes from baseline in total CAT score were −5.5±8.0 and −4.2±7.4 at 6 months and 12 months, respectively (figure 2, table 4). The MCID was reached in 35 of 49 patients (71.4%) at 6 months, and 22 of 40 patients (55.0%) at 12 months. CAT cough and phlegm scores also improved significantly at months 6 and 12 (table 4). Mean±sd changes from baseline in total SGRQ score were −9.5±17.5, and −12.4±16.3 at months 6 and 12, respectively (figure 3). The MCID was reached in 30 of 47 patients (63.8%) at 6 months, and 26 of 39 patients (66.7%) at 12 months. CAT responders at 6 months (n=35) had significantly less RV (154.0 versus 188.2% predicted, p=0.007), higher FEV1 (66.8 versus 39.6% predicted, p=0.014) and greater 6-min walk distance (393 versus 289 m, p=0.006) at baseline compared to nonresponders. No clinically meaningful changes were observed in pulmonary function tests or 6MWD following treatment.

Discussion

We present results from a prospective, observational, multicentre, open-label, single-arm registry, designed to collect real-world clinical experience regarding the performance, safety and efficacy of BR when used in typical clinical practice for the treatment of CB symptoms in adult patients. 54 patients were treated in two separate procedures and followed for 12 months. BR was shown to be a technically feasible, safe procedure with few unexpected side-effects while providing benefit through symptom reduction.

The study population presents a diverse group of patients with varying degrees of lung disease but with equally significant CB symptoms (cough and phlegm). As described previously, the study enrolment criteria were based on the RheOx IFUs and no other inclusion or exclusion criteria were included in the protocol. As a result, FEV1 ranged from <30% predicted to well over 100%, and RV from 108% predicted (relatively normal) to nearly 300% (severe). Though each of these patients presented with symptoms of CB (e.g., productive cough), we now know that COPD phenotypes encompass a more heterogeneous mix of airway inflammation, mucus production and airflow limitation, [13] which is also evident in this patient group. Although on average patients exhibited mild airway obstruction, both patients with advanced pulmonary emphysema and severe airway obstruction and patients without any obstruction in lung function were included in this study. This real-life patient selection must be considered when interpreting the results from this study.

Consistent with results from previous studies, BR was shown to be technically feasible and safe [8]. Only two SAEs (both thoracic pain) were deemed to be related to the device or procedure. In both cases, the symptoms were relieved with analgesics and may have resulted from narcotics used for general anaesthesia. Nevertheless, this remains a side-effect that needs to be monitored. Of note, nonserious COPD exacerbation adverse events were deemed related to the device in four patients, which was expected in this patient population undergoing two separate bronchoscopies [14].

The secondary outcome of clinical utility demonstrated that clinically meaningful improvement in quality of life, as evidenced by CAT and SGRQ scores, can be achieved in the majority of patients. These results are consistent with those found in previous studies, [8, 9, 15] and demonstrate that similar outcomes as well-controlled clinical studies can be achieved in a real-world setting.

Due to the heterogeneous study population, an attempt was made to identify the patient cohort that would benefit most from BR. Both the analysis of CAT responders and the categorisation of mixed-phenotype versus CB-dominant patients showed that patients with less impaired lung function and reduced air trapping are likely to benefit more from BR. This may be the group of patients with less predominant pulmonary emphysema, although CT analyses are lacking to confirm this hypothesis. Patients with advanced emphysema may suffer more from the effects of hyperinflation than secretion burden, thus BR alone may not provide sufficient improvement of symptoms. Whether a combination of lung volume reduction procedures and BR is possible in these mixed phenotypes cannot be said at this time, nor can any statement be made from this cohort as to whether emphysema patients generally benefit from BR or not. However, a recently published case study of five patients with emphysema and prior placement of lung volume reduction valves who subsequently developed worsening clinical issues, including complaints of increased and thickened mucus, along with exacerbations in the setting of a loss of some valve-associated benefit, demonstrated the BR procedure can be performed safely in these patients [16].

While the CAT cough and phlegm score tended to increase slightly between 6 and 12 months, the improvements in the SGRQ remained constant through 12 months. A feasibility study of BR in the USA demonstrated improvements in symptom scores in 21 CB patients for up to 2 years with a similar trend of a slight symptom increase after 12 months. The question of how long the effects of BR last and whether the procedure can or must be repeated after some time remains to be answered in future studies with a longer observation period and larger patient population. One case of retreatment after symptom recurrence has already been reported [17].

In addition to BR, two other procedures are currently being investigated in the treatment of CB, namely karakoca resector balloon desobstruction device and metered cryospray therapy [18].

Karakoca resector balloon desobstruction was shown to be feasible and safe in a pilot study on 10 patients and a case series on 188 patients. In contrast to BR, the case series included mainly patients with COPD GOLD IV, and significant improvements in lung function and 6MWD were demonstrated after 1 month [19, 20]. Due to the wide range of patients, including those with noninvasive ventilation and tracheostomy, and the short follow-up period, it is not possible to fully evaluate or compare this treatment with BR [20].

The cryospray method uses a different technique, the application of liquid nitrogen, with the same aim as BR, namely ablation of the goblet cells, and is performed in three bronchoscopies. The reported side-effects were mainly COPD exacerbation and pneumonia, and the improvements in symptom scores after 6–9 months were similar to BR. There is no current direct comparison of these two procedures, and the experiences seem to be similar for the treatment of CB patients. In addition, the association between CB and COPD exacerbations is well known and BR has already shown a slight reduction in exacerbation frequency in the US feasibility study [9]. The overlapping and concurrent effects with targeted lung denervation, which is mainly targeted at patients with COPD exacerbations, should be evaluated in the future [21].

Primary limitations of this study include the absence of strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, withdraw rates and missing data between 6 and 12 months, lack of evaluable CT scans and data on bacterial colonisation, and lack of a control group, which would allow for characterisation of any potential placebo effect on patient-reported outcomes. A randomised, sham-controlled pivotal trial of BR is ongoing and will hopefully close this gap.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that BR can be performed safely and results in meaningful improvements in patient-reported symptoms through at least 12 months in a real-world setting. Further, this study demonstrates that BR remains safe in a group of patients with complicated and progressed lung disease.
