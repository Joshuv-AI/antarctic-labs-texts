Elbow flexion training with blood flow restriction improves strength, proprioception, and performance in healthy individuals: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Sevgi Sevi Yeşilyaprak, Deniz Dere
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEvidence on the effects of exercise training with blood flow restriction (BFR) on upper extremity proprioception and performance is limited. The purpose of this study was to investigate the effects of 6 weeks of low-load elbow flexion exercise training with BFR on upper extremity strength, joint position sense (JPS), and functional performance.MethodsSixty healthy individuals were randomized into the experimental group (n = 30) which received low-load training with BFR or the control group (n = 30) which received no training. Elbow muscle strength, shoulder and elbow JPS, and upper extremity functional motor performance [Closed Kinetic Chain Upper Extremity Stability Test (CKCUEST), Functional Throwing Performance Index (FTPI), Single Arm Shot Put Test (SASPT), and Modified Pull-Up Test (MPUT)] were assessed. For normally distributed data, Two-way ANOVA [2 × 2, group (between-participant) X time (within-participant), repeated measures] was used to determine the intervention effects on dependent variables. F value was used based on sphericity assumed. We considered the time-group interaction term in analyses. For non-normally distributed data, we used the Wilcoxon Signed-rank Test to examine the changes in individual groups for the relevant results and the Mann-Whitney U Test to compare changes between two-time intervals between the groups.ResultsTime by Group showed significant effects for the results of flexor strength, CKCUEST, FTPI, and MPUT in favor of the experimental group (all p < 0.001). The extensor strength, JPS, and SASPT results also improved in the experimental group compared to the control group (p < 0.05). The only change in the control group was a decrease in the FTPI percentile (p = 0.017).ConclusionsThis study adds new information about the effects of BFR on proprioception and performance. Six-week low-load elbow flexion training with BFR improves elbow muscle strength, shoulder and elbow proprioception, and upper extremity functional motor performance.Clinical trial registrationNCT03401567 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier) (Registration Date:23/12/2017).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-025-08768-9.

Introduction

Strength training with blood flow restriction (BFR) via cuff attached to the extremity, which has become prominent in recent years, safely improves strength under various conditions [1–5]. Although high-load (HL) resistance training is generally considered a better option to optimize maximum muscle strength in the scientific literature [6], a very recent meta-analysis revealed that both HL training and low-load (LL) training with BFR were equally effective in producing gains in muscle strength [7]. Moreover, both exercise regimens increase muscle mass [6]. The proposed mechanism of BFR is to restrain venous flow in the applied extremity and stimulate protein synthesis. In addition, with low mechanical stress and minimal muscle damage [5, 8], BFR exercises do not require a long regeneration process [2, 5, 6, 8, 9]. In this respect, the risk of injury associated with BFR exercises is considered to be lower than that associated with HL resistance exercises and the technique can be advantageous in particular populations (e.g., the nonathletic general population and elderly individuals) where HL exercises may not be suitable [2, 6, 7].

The perception of the position of a limb is a specialized proprioceptive function and plays an essential role in performing complex movements. Joint position sense (JPS), the most commonly used measure of proprioception, reflects the accuracy of joint-angle replication [10] and is reduced in physically inactive individuals than in physically active individuals [11]. It has also been proven that poor proprioception leads to injury [10]. HL resistance exercises contribute to proprioception because of the increased sensitivity of muscle spindles [12, 13]. As effective as HL exercises for improving muscle strength, LL-BFR exercises can also improve proprioception. Furthermore, as techniques that apply pressure to soft tissue may increase the activity of type Ib afferents, change muscle spindle length, and improve proprioceptive feedback to the central nervous system (CNS) [14, 15], LL-BFR exercises with a cuff that creates pressure on muscle and fascia structures can contribute to proprioception.

Adequate muscle strength and proprioception are essential for motor control, dynamic stability, and neuromuscular function. Performance essentially depends on strength and neuromuscular control [10, 11, 13, 16, 17]. Therefore, performance can be improved as strength, proprioception, and neuromuscular control improves. BFR technique may contribute to performance [18, 19] like traditional strengthening. The effects of LL-BFR exercise training on muscle strength have been well researched [1, 2, 6, 7], however, there is limited information about the effects of the technique on proprioception and performance in the upper extremity. Moreover, evidence is conflicting concerning whether resistance exercises combined with BFR can be applied as part of traditional exercise training programs to increase performance gains [19, 20]. A very recent meta-analysis on this topic recommended more high-quality randomized controlled trials [20]. Understanding whether LL-BFR exercise training is beneficial for developing functional motor performance will help to improve skills, functionality, and sporting success [13, 16, 17], as well as injury prevention [10].

This study aimed to examine the effects of a 6-week LL elbow flexion exercise with BFR on strength, JPS, and functional motor performance in healthy individuals. We hypothesized that, compared with the control intervention, LL-BFR elbow flexion training would improve elbow muscle strength, shoulder and elbow JPS, and upper extremity performance in healthy individuals.

Materials and methods

Patients and study design

Between September 2017 and May 2019, 60 healthy university students from X University Campus participated in this prospective, two-arm, randomized controlled study conducted at X University, X Department. Because there were no relevant studies, a priori sample size calculation for ANOVA: repeated measures, time‒group interaction was performed via G*Power software using medium effect size “f ” as 0.25 (95% power, 5% Type-I error level). A sample size of 27 participants per group was determined, and we decided to include 30 participants in each group, with a 1:1 ratio, in case of dropouts (10%).

The primary author (SSY) screened participants for eligibility, generated the random allocation sequence, and assigned them into groups. A computer-generated list of random numbers was used to allocate (concealed by envelopes) the participants into the experimental group (EG) or control group (CG) (Fig. 1). The EG performed elbow flexion exercises with a BFR exercise set attached on the dominant side 3 times a week [1] for 6 weeks. The CG received no exercise but rather an informational brochure about exercise and injury prevention. Elbow and shoulder JPS, elbow flexion and extension strength, and upper extremity functional motor performance were measured on the dominant upper extremity in random order, at the beginning and after 6 weeks. Any learning effect was excluded by performing a previous familiarization session for all tests.

Instruments

The primary outcome measurement was muscle strength. We measured elbow muscle strength while the participants were in the supine position, with a neutral shoulder, elbow 90˚ flexion, and forearm in supination for elbow flexion and in neutral for the elbow extension test. Stabilization was achieved by being held onto the upper part of the humerus. At the end range, we placed a hand-held dynamometer (HHD) (MicroFET®3; Hoggan Inc, UT, USA) just proximal to the medial and lateral styloid processes to test the flexors and extensors, respectively. We asked the participants to perform a gradually increasing maximum voluntary contraction in 2 s (until their effort matched that of the examiner) and then maintain it for 5 s. Three test results were averaged. We allowed a rest period of 20 s between repetitions and 1 min between tests [21]. On the basis of the test-retest results for 10 participants, our reliability was [ICC(3, k)] ICCflex=0.92, ICCext=0.96.

Secondary outcome measurements were proprioception and functional motor performance. For all JPS measurements, the absolute value of the difference between the target angle and the angle where the participant stopped was recorded. We allowed a rest period of 10 s between repetitions and 1 min between tests [22]. The use of a bubble inclinometer (Baseline®, Fabrication End Inc, NY, USA) shoulder JPS (A) for 100° shoulder elevation in the scapular plane was evaluated while the participant was sitting on a chair, (B) for 45° internal rotation (IR) and 75° external rotation (ER) while the participant was lying supine. In the rotation test, the elbow was at 90° flexion, the shoulder was at 90° abduction, and the participant’s palm indicated the face. The IR test ranged from 90° ER to 45° IR. The ER test ranged from the neutral position to 75° ER. The test positions were taught to the participants 3 times (when the target angle was reached, the participants held their arms for 3 s and then returned to the starting position), and afterward, they were asked to match the target angle 3 times (the results were averaged) [23] (ICCABD=0.78, ICCER =0.70, ICCIR=0.70.) For elbow JPS measurement, the participants sat on the chair with their backs supported and their hips flexed approximately 90°. The forearm was placed on the arms of the chair, covered with a foam pad, in a semi-prone position with the elbow fully extended at the starting position. The participants bent their elbow to the target angle (45°, 60°, and 75° flexion) controlled with a digital inclinometer (Baseline®, Fabrication End Inc, NY, USA), maintained this position for 5 s to learn the angle, returned to the initial position, and rested for 5 s. The protocol was repeated 3 times while their eyes were open and closed. In the actual test, we applied the same protocol 3 times (eyes were closed) and recorded the deviation from the target angle (the results were averaged) [22] (ICC45°=0.84, ICC60°= 0.89, and ICC75°=0.78).

The functional motor performance tests used in this study are the up-to-date, reliable, and essential assessment tools for upper extremity musculoskeletal/sports injury prevention, screening and return to sport after injury. They allow for a comprehensive evaluation of upper extremity function by emphasizing different aspects of performance [24–26]. All performance tests started with a warm-up activity from submaximal to maximal. For a high intensity effort, we verbally encouraged the participants throughout the tests. The tests were repeated 3 times, and the scores were averaged. We allowed a 45-sec rest period between repetitions in individual tests. The rest period was 60 s between tests [24]. The closed kinetic chain upper extremity stabilization test (CKCUEST) is used to assess upper extremity neuromuscular dynamic stability, strength, and endurance. In this test, we marked 2 lines (width = 3.8 cm) parallel to each other at a distance of 90 cm on a floor. The starting position for the test is one hand on each line while the body is in a pushup position. The participant moved both hands back and forth from each line as many times as possible in 15 s. Men were in the push-up position and women were in the modified push-up position (kneeling). The average number of lines touched, the score, and power were recorded (ICC = 0.87) [Score = (average of the number of lines touched / height), power = (68% weight * average number of lines touched) / 15] [27]. The modified pull-up test (MPUT) assesses an individual’s ability to perform movements which are essential for various sports and functional activities, strength, and endurance. Moreover, activity performed in the MPUT closely resembles the elbow flexion exercise, which is the primary exercise in the present research. The test was performed while the participants were positioned on their backs. We adjusted the metal frame above the head to be just above the shoulder level. Men perform the pull-up test with the support from their heels, and women perform the test with a step just below their knees [24]. To perform the test at full range, the participants started the test by holding the metal frame with their arms in full extension, pulling it parallel to the floor, and finally lowering their bodies with their elbows fully extended. During the test, we warned the participants to limit the movement of the head and trunk and to maintain the smoothness as much as possible. The participants performed the test with as many pulls as possible within 15 s during the 3 maximal tests (ICC = 0.85) [24]. The single arm shot put test (SASPT) is used to assess upper extremity power, strength, and coordination. The test was performed while the participants were seated in a chair with no arm support, with yellow tape affixed to the chair’s front legs, and the participant’s body was fixed to the chair via a velcro belt. The participants extended their legs to another chair so that their hips, knees, and ankles were in a straight line and parallel to the floor. The nonthrowing arm was placed on the opposite shoulder and a 3 kg medicine ball was launched from chest distance. We gave them 2 min rest after 4 warm-up shots with 25, 50, 75, and 100% effort from submaximal to maximum. Three maximal efforts were tested. The closest point and midpoint of the mark that the ball left when it touched the ground were measured in cm from the appropriate point of the tape affixed to the front legs of the chair (ICC = 0.98) [24]. Finally, we determined the functional throwing performance index (FTPI). The FTPI is an objective assessment tool used to evaluate upper extremity accuracy, power, consistency, and coordination. The test was performed by a 50.8 cm plastic ball that was thrown into a square area drawn on the wall which was 1.22 m above the ground and its edge width was 30.48 cm. The shooting distance from the wall was 4.57 m. First, the participants performed 4 controlled warm-up throws from submaximal to maximal. For testing, participants made a controlled maximum number of accurate throws in 30 s (FTPI = Number of shots within the target / Total number of shots * 100) (ICC = 0.79) [27].

Exercise protocol

The exercise was performed in a total of 75 repetitions, with the first set being 30 repetitions followed by 3 sets of 15 repetitions. A 30-sec rest was given between sets [28]. The exercise rhythm was determined via a metronome and the duration of contraction was set to 2 s for concentric contraction and 2 s for eccentric contraction. 1 RM was calculated using free weights. Free weights of 4 kg, 6 kg, or 8 kg were used according to the physical appearance of the individual. The number of repetitions they were able to lift in the relevant position was measured. Then, via the Holten diagram, a kg value equivalent to 20% of 1 RM was calculated. The elastic exercise band (Theraband, The Hygenic Corporation, ABD) was selected by considering the equivalent of kg in the elastic band force table. Since 20% of 1 RM does not always have the exact equivalent in the elastic band force table, a 10–30% range of 1 RM was taken into account, as suggested [29, 30]. In the first 4 weeks, exercise progression was achieved by increasing the pressure of the BFR cuff, whereas in weeks 5 and 6, exercise progression was achieved by changing the color of the elastic band. Elastic band color progressed so that ratings of perceived exertion during exercise ranged from 7 to 9 according to the OMNI Perceived Exertion Scale for Resistance Exercise with Elastic Bands (OMNI-RES EB) [31] Pain during exercise was less than “2” according to the numerical rating scale. The participants sat in a chair for the elbow flexion exercise, and the angle of the elbow joint during the exercise was adjusted in flexion from approximately 45˚-140˚ [30]. The BFR pressure band (cuff) (B Strong, Park City, Utah, ABD) was attached to the uppermost part of the arm. The pressure of the BFR cuff was maintained throughout the exercise. The pressure was adjusted to 80 mmHg in the first week, 90 mmHg in the second week, 100 mmHg in the third week, and 120 mmHg in the fourth week [31]. When starting each exercise session, the pressure was first set at 40 (± 5) mmHg and gradually increased to the desired pressure of the relevant week to avoid sudden pressure increase [31]. During the fifth and sixth weeks, the cuff pressure was 120 mmHg [9, 31]. After a careful review of previous studies’ protocols [9, 28, 30–32], this restriction pressure was selected because our population was not familiar with the technique and because excessive cuff pressure may augment the cardiovascular response, induce discomfort, and have negative effects on the sensorimotor system [2].

Control protocol

Controls received an educational brochure on the effects of exercise on different systems in the human body and its importance for health and protection from injuries. We asked them not to start any exercise program during the study and not to change their daily life routines, and we monitored their compliance by phone weekly.

Statistical analysis

We used SPSS v24.0 (IBM Corporation, NY, USA)” for data analyses. We determined the normality of the distribution of continuous data via the Shapiro-Wilk Test. We used the independent samples t-test to compare height, body weight, BMI, and age, and χ2 test to compare the sex and dominant extremity distributions of the groups. Two-way ANOVA [2 × 2, group (between-participant) x time (within-participant), repeated measures] was used to determine the intervention effects on the dependent variables. The F value was used based on sphericity assumed. We considered the time-group interaction term in the analyses. Between-group differences at the end of the intervention were also investigated. Pairwise comparisons were used to determine differences from the baseline to the completion of the intervention between groups and investigate whether any between-group differences in change scores were statistically significant. For nonnormally distributed data, we used the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to examine the changes in individual groups for the relevant results and the Mann-Whitney U Test to compare changes between two-time intervals between the groups. The significance level was p < 0.05.

Discussion

To our knowledge, we are the first to investigate the effects of elbow flexion exercise with BFR on JPS and performance. Muscle strength, JPS, and functional motor performance improved after 6 weeks of LL-BFR.

We found improvements in the muscle strength of both elbow flexors and extensors after elbow flexion training with BFR. This result is important for addressing clinical meaningfulness [13]. The increased strength after BFR exercise was attributed to a decrease in venous return in the applied extremity; an increase in protein synthesis, the concentration of nNOS, the water content of muscle cells, and GH release; and a decrease in myostatin mRNA expression, resulting in the development of muscle fibres and mass [1, 33]. Neural adaptations and the beginning of hypertrophy can be other reasons [6, 33, 34]. Yasuda et al. investigated the effects of low-intensity bench press with BFR on hypertrophic responses in restricted upper arm and non-restricted chest muscles. They reported an increase in 1-RM bench press strength, the triceps brachii hypertrophy, and surprisingly, a greater increase in pectoralis major hypertrophy after weightlifting with a BFR cuff attached to the arm [32]. In our study, the pressure applied to all muscles wrapped up by the cuff during exercise may have led to similar metabolic changes [32].

The improvement in JPS that we obtained may be due to an increase in the activity of electrical inputs relayed to the CNS by muscle spindles and skin and joint mechanoreceptors, the Golgi tendon organ, and the increase in proprioceptive input in the primary motor cortex and spinal levels [13, 35]. Although closed kinetic chain (CKC) exercises are generally recommended to improve JPS, open kinetic chain (OKC) exercises also contribute to proprioception as a result of improvements in the sensitivity of muscle spindles and hence better neuromuscular control [12, 13]. The amount of improvement in JPS that we obtained was close to the gains after HL OKC strength training achieved in previous studies [12, 13]. Differences in exercise types, the number of exercises and repetitions, measurement techniques, and relatively normal and/or lower baseline JPS values compared with those reported in those studies [12, 13] may explain the slightly lower gains in JPS after our LL-BFR protocol. Nevertheless, improvement in JPS after LL training is a clinically noteworthy finding. Techniques that include pressure applied to soft tissue may increase the activity of type Ib afferents, cause changes in muscle spindle length, and cause proprioceptive feedback to the CNS in related muscles [14, 15]. The BFR cuff creates pressure on the muscle and fascia structures. In addition to the elongation of the muscle spindle, pressure may also have been effective on the fascia, which contains up to tenfold more receptors than the muscle and contributes to proprioception [36]. Furthermore, the presence of biarticular muscles in the area where we applied the BFR cuff may have produced a successful result in both shoulder and elbow proprioception.

The effect of BFR exercises on performance is not clear. It was reported that 30-m sprint running time improved after LL-BFR, but jumping performance did not [18]. Researchers have based these results on the absence of plyometric exercises in the protocol and inadequate tendon stiffness. We found improvement in functional motor performance after our LL-BFR protocol. As strength and proprioception are the main physical properties of performance, improved strength and JPS can explain performance improvement, especially in performance tests where dynamic stabilization and skill are prioritized. The CKCUEST is performed in a weight-bearing position and incorporates full upper-extremity and trunk stability [24]. The better the proprioception during motor performance is, the better the perception of distance, force, and timing is expected; thus, optimal functional motor performance can be achieved [10]. As biceps brachii muscle activity occurs throughout the MPUT [37], the increase in biceps brachii strength may have improved our MPUT results. Moreover, the increase in power and strength may have been enabled by the fact that more motor units and rapidly contracting muscle fibres came into play to overcome the strength deficit that occurred together with the lack of oxygen formed by the occlusion created in the BFR technique [38]. The elbow flexion exercise used in our study is a somewhat similar activity to the MPUT. The improved motor learning and skill gained throughout the study may have also improved the MPUT results [39].

It has been reported that upper extremity proprioception affects free-throw performance in basketball since joint stabilization is important in this movement [17]. While strength is important for the FTPI, this test is more of a skill test. Therefore, proprioception, hence, dynamic stabilization of the shoulder and elbow joints, and accurate target shooting skills can be considered to play a role in improving the FTPI. Lust et al. noted that the combination of six-week OKC, CKC, and core stabilization exercise improved the FTPI likely due to increased proprioception and neuromuscular control [16]. Their suggestion supports our explanation. Furthermore, the improvement in the FTPI we obtained was greater than that in their results. Biceps brachii muscle activation is maximal in the arm cocking phase of the overhand throw and it is active in all phases after beginning [40]. In addition, the triceps brachii muscle also plays an important role in throwing performance [41]. Moreover, the elbow and wrist extensor muscles have greater effects than other muscle groups do on the shot rate [42]. Thus, the increase in both elbow flexion and extension strength may also have had a positive effect on the FTPI results.

Mayhew et al. reported that SASPT performance was associated with upper extremity power and muscle size [43]. This relationship may be based on a combination of neuromuscular skill, muscle mass, neural activation, and fibre type distribution, as in many other anaerobic power tests. The increase in muscle strength and proprioception may partly explain the improvement in the SASPT score. However, while the SASPT results do not consider distance and calculate power, the test performance is based on explosive power rather than skill [24]. Our initial average shooting distance in the SASPT for the LL-BFR training group was close to the reference values reported for healthy individuals [24]. Nevertheless, our results revealed a statistically significant increase. Strength and proprioception development, as well as greater power generation, may have improved the SASPT results. On the basis of the knowledge that there is a positive relationship between muscle power and strength, our gain in strength may have also contributed to the increase in power.

The first limitation of this study is the unblinded examiner, as it was a thesis study. Therefore, we took strict precautions to avoid bias: the examiner did not observe the first evaluation results while the subsequent results were recorded, and the researcher who performed the data analyses was unaware of the group assignment of the participants. The second limitation could be that long-term follow-up of the results was outside the scope of the study. Finally, the use of absolute pressure during BFR exercise could be another limitation of the present study. The amount of pressure required to cease blood flow to an extremity may be related to multiple factors, such as individual extremity characteristics, cuff size, and individual blood pressure [2]. Recently, it was recommended to individually set the pressure relative to the arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) [2]. Although some researchers set the pressure relative to brachial systolic blood pressure (SbP) [28], this method has been criticized because it may not provide a consistent reduction in blood flow and SbP has been determined to correlate poorly with measurements of AOP [2]. Nevertheless, the majority of early studies [18, 32], including our study, applied the same absolute pressure to each participant and reported beneficial muscular adaptations [2]. We used suitable cuff sizes for each individual according to the manufacturer’s descriptions [4]. The reasons for selecting this method (absolute pressure set at 80–120 mmHg) were as follows: first, our population was not familiar with the technique; second, with the absence of a clear consensus on ideal individualized pressures during exercises with BFR [2, 3], we considered that excessive cuff pressure may augment the cardiovascular response, induce discomfort, and have negative effects on the sensorimotor system [2, 4], and finally, from a clinical point of view, it is not possible to have a handheld Doppler to determine AOP in every clinic.

Conclusions

This randomized controlled study provides important and new information about the effects of BFR on proprioception and performance. Our results provide evidence that in healthy individuals, six weeks of low-load elbow flexion exercise training with BFR is beneficial for improving shoulder and elbow proprioception, elbow flexion and extension muscle strength, and upper extremity functional motor performance. In terms of proprioception and performance, the effects of the technique on different upper extremity pathologies and overhead athletes should be investigated. Additionally, further studies are warranted to explore long-term effects.
