Association Between Maladaptive Cognitive Emotion Regulation and Fear of Cancer Recurrence in Breast Cancer Patients With a History of Suicide Attempts: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: XinWei Zhang, Hui Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and fear of cancer recurrence in breast cancer patients with a history of suicide attempts.MethodsA cross‐sectional study was conducted on 289 breast cancer patients from January to April 2025. The cognitive‐emotional regulation questionnaire was used to assess maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation, and the cancer recurrence fear scale was used to assess fear of cancer recurrence. Demographic, clinical, and psychosocial data were collected. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted, and smooth curve fitting was used to explore nonlinear relationships.ResultsThe women in this group were aged 20–79 years. Non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation was associated with cancer recurrence fear in a non‐linear positive association with a mid‐range convexity relationship (p < 0.001). Fear of cancer recurrence increased with higher scores on non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation until reaching a plateau around 69 points (β = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.38–0.52, p < 0.001 below the threshold; β = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.15, p = 0.03 above the threshold). Age, educational level, and time since diagnosis significantly moderated this relationship (all interaction terms p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study identified a non‐linear positive association with a mid‐range convexity correlation between adaptive and maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and fear of cancer recurrence, highlighting a threshold effect. These findings emphasize the importance of addressing maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation processes in psychological interventions targeting this vulnerable population.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignant tumor among women worldwide. As the number of survivors increases, there is growing concern about long‐term psychosocial outcomes [1]. Among survivors, fear of cancer recurrence (FCR) is a significant psychological issue, affecting approximately 50%–70% of this population and typically persisting for several years after treatment [2]. Research indicates that FCR may become particularly pronounced and debilitating in individuals with a history of suicidal ideation, exacerbating psychological distress and reducing quality of life [3]. Persistent FCR may stem from survivors' interpretations of their cancer narrative, leading them to engage in maladaptive cognitive processes that may further impair their mental health and overall well‐being [4].

Non‐cognitive emotional regulation encompasses adaptive cognitive emotional regulation and maladaptive emotional regulation, such as rumination, catastrophizing, and self‐blame, which are associated with adverse psychological outcomes in cancer survivors [5]. Previous research has shown [6] a direct link between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and elevated FCR levels, but the dynamics of this relationship remain poorly understood, particularly in vulnerable populations such as breast cancer survivors with a history of self‐harm [7, 8]. The self‐regulatory executive function model assumes that maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation maintains a high level of perceived threat and psychological distress by continuously activating the threat‐evaluation system, thereby strengthening the attention bias to the FCR, and ultimately aggravating the psychophysical susceptibility of cancer survivors to disease progression [9]. For individuals with a history of suicidal behavior, emotional responses may already be heightened because of cognitive distortions, and the impact of maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation on FCR may be particularly pronounced [10, 11]. Conversely, FCR itself may lead to maladaptive cognitive and poor mood.

This study aims to investigate the complex relationship between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and FCR in breast cancer survivors with a history of suicide attempts. Specifically, this study focuses on three objectives: (1) describing the nature of the relationship between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and FCR, (2) identifying any potential threshold effects, and (3) exploring various demographic and clinical factors that may moderate this relationship.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This cross‐sectional study recruited 289 breast cancer survivors with a history of suicide attempts from 10 cancer centers in China between January and April 2025. The Chinese version of the Beck Inventory of Suicide Ideation was used to assess thoughts about life and death, and the severity of suicidal ideation and suicide risk [12]. A history of attempted suicide was verified. Psychological assessments were performed at a consistent time after diagnosis or treatment to avoid temporal variation affecting the results. Inclusion criteria: (1) female breast cancer survivors aged ≥ 20 years; (2) completion of primary tumor treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy) at least 3 months prior; (3) A history of at least one suicide attempt post‐diagnosis documented in medical records; (4) Ability to complete the questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Active mental disorders (e.g., psychosis, active substance abuse); (2) Cognitive impairments preventing questionnaire completion; (3) Cancer recurrence or metastatic disease.

Data Analysis

The flowchart of the selection process for the research population is shown in Figure 1. The data distributions of each covariate were compared between the exposed and unexposed groups. Appropriate statistical tests were used on the basis of the type of variable, including t‐tests for continuous variables with normal distributions, Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum tests for continuous variables with non‐normal distributions, and chi‐square tests for categorical data (see Table 1). Subsequently, univariate logistic regression analysis (see Table 2) and multivariate logistic regression models (see Table 3) were used to assess the association between covariates and the outcome of interest. All analyses were performed using R (http://www.R‐project.org) and Empower Stats software (www.empowerstats.com, X&Ysolutions Inc., Boston, MA).

Results

The flowchart outlines the inclusion and exclusion criteria used to determine the study sample of patients diagnosed with breast cancer in thoracic surgery, resulting in a final cohort of 289 participants. Initially, 5010 patients were excluded from a total of 17,050 patients for not meeting the primary thoracic surgery criteria. An additional 6751 patients were excluded on the basis of the criterion of a hospital stay of at least 48 h. Age restrictions excluded 1000 patients under 18 years of age, and an additional 20 patients were excluded because of incomplete medical records. Finally, 4000 patients who self‐referred to thoracic surgery were also excluded. The meticulous application of these criteria ensured the selection of a relevant and manageable sample for subsequent analysis, enhancing the validity of the study results.

Table 1 compares the demographic characteristics and psychological scores of participants across different age groups (20–30 years, 40–59 years, 60–79 years). The results showed that there were no significant differences between groups in terms of nationality, place of residence, marital status, educational attainment, type of medical insurance, or household income (p values: 0.704, 0.627, 0.216, 0.767, 0.206, respectively), but employment status significantly influenced patient distribution (p = 0.006). The proportion of employed individuals significantly decreased in the 60–79 age group. There were statistically significant differences in household income between groups (p = 0.025), with the low‐income group (< 2000 yuan) being more prominent in the elderly group. The distribution of the number of children also changed with age, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.062). In terms of mental health indicators, patients aged 60–79 exhibited higher levels of FCR (p < 0.001), non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation (p < 0.001), and lower mental health scores, suggesting that older patients demonstrate better psychological adaptation. Additionally, there were statistically significant differences between groups with different stages of breast cancer (p < 0.001), and the proportion of elderly patients increased because of later disease progression, suggesting that disease management strategies for elderly patients should be prioritized.

Table 2 describes the univariate analysis of FCR, where age in the 60–79 age group was significantly associated with FCR (β = −5.2, p < 0.0001), with the 20–30 age group serving as the reference group. Additionally, there were no significant differences in fear levels between ethnic groups (p = 0.5621). Geographically, there were no significant differences between urban and rural residents (p = 0.3989). The impact of marital status was minimal, with a β = 1.6 (p = 0.1356) for the divorced group, whereas employment status showed a slight decrease in fear levels among job seekers (β = −2.1, p = 0.0550). Education significantly reduced the fear of college students (β = −3.5, p = 0.0175). Economic income was negatively correlated with FCR, and participants with monthly incomes between 4000 and 6000 yuan showed a significant reduction in FCR (β = −3.3, p = 0.0449). In terms of satisfaction with family support, the fear level of the dissatisfied group significantly increased (β = 4.1, p = 0.0022). Finally, cancer stage, surgical method, and chemotherapy history were all significantly associated with FCR, especially in Stage3 and Stage4 patients (p < 0.0001). Overall, there were significant associations between multiple factors such as age, economic status, chemotherapy history, family support satisfaction, and FCR, suggesting that these factors should be considered when managing patients' mental health.

Table 3 shows the relationship between non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and FCR. The results showed that in Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for marital status, employment status, and educational attainment), and Model 3 (further adjusted for health insurance type, personality self‐assessment, family income, number of children, satisfaction with family support, and surgical approach), non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation scores were significantly positively correlated with FCR, with a consistent β value of 0.3 (p < 0.0001). Regarding the quantiles of non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation, both the moderate‐level group (β = 2.9, p = 0.0167) and the high‐level group (β = 6.6, p < 0.0001) exhibited significantly higher levels of fear than the reference group, particularly the high‐level group. Additionally, cancer stage was significantly negatively correlated with fear of recurrence, particularly in Stage3 (β = −10.3, p < 0.0001) and Stage4 (β = −11.5, p < 0.0001). Chemotherapy history was also significantly associated with fear of recurrence (β = 6.2, p < 0.0001), indicating that chemotherapy history has a significant impact on patients' psychological state. These findings highlight the key predictive value of non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation of fear responses to cancer recurrence.

Figure 2 illustrates the relationship between “non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation scores” and “FCR.” The red solid line represents the trend of fear of recurrence as a function of emotion regulation scores, whereas the blue dashed line indicates the 95% confidence interval. Analysis shows that as non‐adaptive cognitive emotion regulation scores increase, there is a significant upward trend in FCR, with scores rising from 20 to 70, and from approximately 10 to nearly 50. Notably, there are fluctuations along the upward trajectory, indicating variability in fear levels, particularly at higher emotional regulation scores. These findings suggest a potential link between non‐adaptive cognitive emotional regulation and heightened fear of recurrence, emphasizing the need for targeted psychological interventions to address these emotional issues in cancer patients.

Non‐adaptive cognitive emotion regulation score smoothed curve fitting plot.

Figure 3 shows the influence of various demographic and psychosocial factors on FCR, with clear and statistically significant results. Factors such as age, race, region, marital status, work conditions, and educational attainment are significantly associated with FCR, with urban residents and those with higher educational attainment exhibiting lower levels of fear, with p < 0.0001. Additionally, married individuals exhibited significantly lower levels of fear than divorced individuals, highlighting the significant impact of marital status on mental health. The number of children, satisfaction with family support, self‐assessment of personality, and psychosocial variables all exerted significant influences. Among these, high satisfaction with family support and an extroverted personality had positive effects on health outcomes, whereas breast cancer stage and surgical type also influenced fear levels, with non‐breast‐conserving surgery significantly associated with higher fear levels. These results suggest that non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation scores may influence FCR through multiple factors, emphasizing the importance of integrating demographic and psychosocial factors into psychological interventions for cancer patients.

Discussion

Table 1 lists the baseline characteristics of the study participants, including 289 individuals divided into three age groups: 20–30 years (81 individuals), 40–59 years (97 individuals), and 60–79 years (111 individuals). The results showed that there were no statistically significant differences between the different groups in terms of nationality, geographical location, marital status, and educational attainment (p < 0.05) [17]. However, the employment rate in the 60–79 age group showed a significant decline (p = 0.006). This trend aligns with the literature, indicating that employment outcomes often deteriorate with age, particularly for cancer survivors, who face unique barriers in the workplace because of the lingering effects of chronic health conditions and treatment [18, 19].

Interestingly, a higher proportion of respondents aged 60–79 reported monthly incomes between 4000 and 6000 yuan (p = 0.025). These financial outcomes can be understood through socioeconomic factors, as income disparities significantly impact cancer survivors' quality of life and employment opportunities [20, 21]. Additionally, it has been established that economic stress and fear of income loss influence decisions to return to work after cancer treatment, further complicating employment prospects for this population [22].

Furthermore, statistical analysis revealed significant differences in cancer recurrence fear (FCR) and non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation scores across age groups (p < 0.001). Higher FCR in younger populations may be attributed to transitional life stages, typically involving family planning and career establishment, which may increase anxiety about cancer recurrence. In contrast, late‐stage cancer patients (Stages 3 and 4) exhibited significantly lower levels of fear of recurrence compared to early‐stage patients (p < 0.001) [23]. This finding may contradict the general perception that anxiety intensifies in late‐stage patients; instead, it suggests that patients facing the reality of a late‐stage diagnosis may be more accepting of their situation, thereby reducing anxiety about recurrence [24].

In summary, these data highlight the complexity of employment status, income levels, and psychological factors among cancer survivors across different age groups. This underscores the need for targeted support systems to address the psychological and economic challenges faced by cancer survivors at various stages of life [25, 26, 27].

Univariate analysis revealed significant correlations between specific psychological characteristics of FCR and family dynamics. Specifically, higher levels of extraversion are associated with increased FCR, whereas lower family support satisfaction is a key factor in elevated fear levels [28, 29]. These findings validate the theoretical framework linking mental health indicators to intrinsic psychological characteristics and external family support structures within the cancer patient population. This underscores the importance of addressing mental health variables in cancer treatment management and paves the way for future research exploring these relationships further [30].

Table 3 shows a positive correlation between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and FCR, revealing consistent associations across different analytical models, with a β coefficient of 0.3 (95% CI: 0.2, 0.3; p < 0.0001). Such a significant figure indicates that patients exhibiting high levels of maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation dysfunction have a markedly higher risk of developing FCR, particularly those scoring 6.6 (95% CI: 4.3, 9.0; p < 0.0001). This aligns with similar studies indicating that maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation exacerbates anxiety and fear in cancer survivors, further impairing their mental health [31, 32]. In contrast, older age (60–79 years) and advanced‐stage cancer (Stages 3 and 4) were associated with lower FCR scores, suggesting that older patients may undergo a potential adaptive process when facing advanced‐stage cancer, leading to a reordering of concerns and reduced focus on FCR [33, 34].

Additionally, studies have found that a history of chemotherapy is associated with increased fear. This observation reinforces the notion that trauma associated with chemotherapy experiences significantly contributes to psychological distress, including FCR among survivors. Longitudinal studies suggest that this distress may stem from persistent concerns about the potential impact of treatment outcomes on survival [32, 35]. In summary, these findings highlight the complex interplay between psychological factors and clinical characteristics in shaping cancer patients' FCR, emphasizing the necessity of comprehensive psychosocial support systems aimed at addressing the emotional and clinical needs of these individuals [36, 37].

This study aims to explore the impact of maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation on FCR among breast cancer patients with a history of suicide attempts. The focus of this study is on the potential mediating role of these maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation strategies between major life events and psychological outcomes in breast cancer patients. Using a cross‐sectional design with a large sample size of 289 participants, the study examined the associations between various psychological and clinical variables.

The results indicate that maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation significantly influences FCR; patients who employed strategies such as rumination and catastrophizing were more likely to report higher levels of FCR [38]. This underscores the importance of addressing non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation in psychological care programs for breast cancer patients, particularly those with suicidal behavior. Interventions targeting these non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation strategies can significantly improve the emotional well‐being of these vulnerable groups, thereby reducing FCR.

In addition, the results of this study support the theoretical hypothesis of the role of maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation in FCR [39]. Therefore, FCR manifestations among breast cancer survivors can be viewed as closely related to individual differences in emotional regulation abilities, particularly among those with a history of emotional crises such as a history of suicide attempts.

Given these insights, future research should consider adopting longitudinal study designs to better establish the causal relationships between these variables. Additionally, exploring targeted interventions aimed at enhancing maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation may help alleviate FCR, potentially improving psychological outcomes and thereby enhancing the quality of life for breast cancer survivors [40].

This study, which focused on breast cancer patients with a history of suicidal ideation, found a non‐linear positive association with a mid‐range convexity relationship between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and FCR, with a critical threshold effect at certain scores. This finding is noteworthy as it aligns with other studies that have used cross‐sectional designs and statistical analyses to explore psychological impacts in specific populations. For example, a study assessing maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation in breast cancer survivors emphasized the mediating role of disease perception and mental health in FCR. However, unlike studies that primarily examine different aspects of stress responses or well‐being, this study explicitly highlighted the psychological complexity faced by breast cancer survivors [41].

The differences in research findings—such as the non‐linear positive association with a mid‐range convexity curve observed in this study, rather than the previously described linear or mediating relationships—can be attributed to differences in sample characteristics, measurement tools, and cultural contexts. For example, psychological distress associated with the FCR fundamentally differs from general quality of life or addiction indicators, which may elicit unique cognitive responses among patients [42]. Additionally, the observed threshold effect may reflect the complex interplay between anxiety and cognitive regulation, particularly for cancer survivors [43]. Understanding how maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation paradoxically modulates different levels of fear requires further exploration.

Theoretically, the relationship between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and FCR may stem from the cognitive amplification of threats; individuals who employ maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation such as rumination or catastrophizing may focus on the worst‐case scenario, thereby exacerbating their fear. Notably, when cognitive distortions reach a certain threshold, patients may experience a degree of desensitization related to these threats, leading to a paradoxical reduction in fear levels [44, 45].

Overall, while methodologically similar to previous studies, this study's unique focus and specific population provide valuable insights into the complex dynamic relationship between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and cancer‐related fear. Future research should continue to explore these pathways and potential interventions aimed at improving maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation to reduce FCR and improve the psychosocial outcomes of breast cancer survivors [46, 47].

Emotional dysregulation is a significant issue, often leading to increased anxiety and feelings of helplessness, which in turn may exacerbate patients' FCR. In some cases, extreme emotional dysregulation can manifest as psychological numbness or detachment, where patients dissociate from their emotions as a defensive mechanism. This detachment may sometimes reduce fear, as patients reduce their emotional investment in the environment [48]. This emotional response is particularly relevant to cancer survivors, as the threat of recurrence is a persistent source of anxiety.

Current studies employ cross‐sectional designs, which inherently limit the ability to draw causal inferences or address potential selection biases, especially since participants are breast cancer survivors willing to disclose their suicide attempts [49]. This limitation may distort the study results, as it only captures a small portion of experiences and reactions related to fear and emotional regulation. Therefore, future longitudinal studies are necessary to explore how changes in maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation influence the trajectory of cancer recurrence fear over time. Such research could provide important insights into the dynamic nature of these relationships and help identify effective interventions to mitigate the negative emotional impacts associated with cancer survival [50].

Previous longitudinal studies support the view that emotional dysregulation is associated with elevated anxiety levels and the development of emotional disorders, suggesting that individuals' ways of experiencing and expressing emotions evolve over time and significantly impact their mental health [51]. Additionally, the focus on suicide attempters highlights a unique population that may experience these emotional dynamics more intensely, thereby offering a more nuanced understanding of [52] non‐cognitive emotional processes.

Overall, the complex interplay between emotional dysregulation and FCR underscores the necessity of comprehensive psychological assessments and targeted interventions to support breast cancer survivors' emotional coping with maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation, particularly among those with a history of suicide attempts. Conclusion

This study identified 289 patients with breast cancer who had attempted suicide. The findings revealed a nonlinear dose–response relationship between maladaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation and FCR. Non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation is associated with cancer recurrence fear in a curvilinear manner. Higher or lower levels of non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation are associated with increased FCR. These findings emphasize the importance of personalized, cognition‐focused psychological interventions for this vulnerable population, particularly for younger patients and those exhibiting moderate patterns of non‐adaptive cognitive‐emotional regulation. Study limitations also exist; the cross‐sectional design limits causal inference, and we do not know whether maladaptive emotion regulation contributes to fear of relapse or vice versa. Longitudinal confirmation is therefore required.
