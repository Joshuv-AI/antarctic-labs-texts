Social determinants of access to health care among deaf people in the Dominican Republic: a qualitative study
Authors: Shazia Siddiqi, Pablo Lemuel Taveras Cueto, Zahira Quiñones Tavarez, Wyatte C Hall, Sachiko Flores, History Estill-Varner, José Javier Sánchez, Cristobalina Betemit, Alan Jesurum, Timothy De Ver Dye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHearing loss is comparatively frequent in the Latin America and Caribbean (LAC) region, where the prevalence of disabling hearing loss is higher than in many areas of the world. The current societal infrastructure in the Dominican Republic (DR) for Deaf people is insufficient: services such as sign language interpreters are lacking, as are accessible and widespread educational and health care services or access to emergency services. This project was formed through four partnerships, including United States- and Dominican Republic-based academic institutions and community organizations serving Deaf communities, to identify lived experience of Deaf Dominicans in accessing health care.MethodsWe recruited 102 people in the DR self-identifying as Deaf to participate in 51 qualitative interviews. All interviews were conducted in Dominican Sign language (“LSRD”) by Deaf interviewers and were visually recorded. Interview transcripts were analyzed using thematic analysis informed by grounded theory to identify categories explaining participants’ experiences.ResultsThree themes emerged in our grounded theory showing that Deaf Dominicans (1) rely heavily on hearing people as facilitators and navigators for the Deaf person to access health care; (2) often as a result of this dependence, Deaf Dominicans frequently experience lack of health care information in transactional health-related encounters and, more broadly, in general health knowledge; and (3) communicate frequently with the hearing world (including health care providers and workers) through gestures other than an established sign language. Further, Deaf Dominicans routinely experience structural audism, defined as systemic discrimination and prejudice against Deaf indivdiuals, when they are unable to fully access health care.ConclusionsUrgent attention is needed to address these systemic barriers. Deaf Dominicans are an underserved linguistic and cultural minority population who face barriers and challenges in accessing the health care system. Background

Although the United Nations (UN) affirms that health is a fundamental right of every human being, health inequities among global populations with hearing loss persist despite protections such as the UN Convention on the Rights of People with Disabilities (CRPD). Hearing loss is a major global health issue, ranking third worldwide in years lived with disability (YLD), a metric that quantifies the impact of non-fatal health conditions on daily functioning and underscores the enduring burden hearing loss imposes on overall quality of life [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that 430 million people worldwide have hearing loss [2], including 32 million children [3], for whom this condition may particularly affect key developmental processes such as language acquisition and cognitive development [4]. People with hearing loss are often considered to have an “invisible” disability and face numerous barriers to health, including difficulty obtaining adequate care, accessing timely and accurate health information (e.g., during medical consultations or emergencies), and maintaining sufficient health literacy [5–7]. These challenges reflect broader social determinants of health that affect Deaf people, who experience many of the same factors as the general population, such as disparities in health care access and quality, education, social and community context, economic stability, and the neighborhood and built environment [8]. Communication and social barriers are primary factors separating Deaf people from the hearing community, regardless of other disabilities. As a result, Deaf individuals experience greater disparities in employment, education, and both mental and physical health [9–11].

Hearing loss is particularly prevalent in the Latin America and Caribbean (LAC) region, where the prevalence of disabling hearing loss is higher than in many other parts of the world [12]. For Deaf populations living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these disparities are compounded by persistent educational challenges. Historical data from 2009 estimated that more than 80% of the world’s 70 million Deaf people live in these regions with limited access to formal education, and many have no formal education at all [13]. Data collection on the global Deaf population remains significantly limited and fragmented. The only known population-based Deaf census - which was conducted in the Dominican Republic (DR)- reports that 80,000 people identify as Deaf, hard of hearing, or cannot speak, including 23,200 people identifying as Deaf, DeafBlind, or Deaf with disabilities [14]. According to this census, almost 40% of Deaf Dominicans had no access to a sign or spoken language and used “gestures” to communicate; few had access to sign language before 10 years old; over 75% had no secondary education; and about half were unemployed or lacked health insurance [14]. Deaf culture has been examined in various global contexts [15], including in Latin America [16–19]; however, it has not been previously documented in the DR. While many Deaf cultures around the world share common elements—such as the prioritization of sign languages and the significance of collective identity—each national context presents distinct cultural and historical intersections that shape the experiences and identities of local Deaf communities. In this paper, we capitalize “Deaf” to refer specifically to self-identified Deaf people in the DR. In the DR and elsewhere, the capitalization of “D” in Deaf (or “S” in Sordo) is somewhat inconsistent; however, our team concluded that Sordo (with a capital “S) is commonly used to refer to the Deaf community and culture in the DR, and we adopt that convention here when referring to Deaf/Sordo culture.

The current societal infrastructure in the DR for Deaf people is insufficient: services such as qualified sign language interpreters are lacking, as are accessible and widespread educational and health care services, including access to emergency services. Access to education and health resources for Deaf individuals is shaped by limited official data and uneven service provision, with pronounced disparities between urban and rural areas. Unlike in the United States (US), the Ministry of Education does not make systematic information on Deaf schools or programs publicly available, and the quality of services is inconsistent. Current estimates suggest the existence of approximately 21 state-run Deaf schools and seven mixed schools serving both Deaf and hearing students. Educational settings vary widely: some full-time Deaf schools are staffed primarily by hearing teachers, often supported by “Deaf monitors,” while mainstream schools typically lack interpreters. Mixed programs frequently provide little or no sign language access. In Santo Domingo, students benefit from three dedicated sign language schools and one bilingual (oral and sign) school, whereas rural schools—though more numerous—are characterized by lower-quality instruction and highly variable sign language proficiency, depending on the teacher.

Beyond schools, Deaf people in the DR engage in community life through regional and national deaf clubs and associations, particularly the Asociación Nacional de Sordos de la República Dominicana (ANSORDO). While laws exist to support the inclusion of Deaf students in mainstream schools, the quality of integration is highly uneven. Health services present further gaps: there are no Deaf-specific clinics, no Deaf clinicians, and no dedicated resources to support accessible health care for Deaf patients. In the post-COVID-19 context, video conferencing has become an important tool for social interaction, education, and even limited clinical care among Deaf individuals. Overall, access to quality education and health services remains fragmented, with better resources concentrated in Santo Domingo and significant inequities persisting in rural communities. Captions on television news broadcasts and in other forms of public communication are virtually non-existent, and may not be accessible even when available due to literacy concerns.

Although the DR has an established three-tiered health care system (public, social security-based, and private), there are significant gaps in specialized services and accessibility accommodations for Deaf patients. Access to hearing aids and cochlear implants is limited for most Deaf Dominicans due to restricted public insurance coverage, inadequate health care infrastructure, and a shortage of trained professionals. Seeking services in health care settings is often challenging, especially in an LMIC context with limited resources or infrastructure to support Deaf people. There is no national policy mandating the provision of sign language interpreting services, and health care workers often have no training in Deaf culture or basic communication strategies. Health care institutions in the DR frequently provide inadequate, inaccessible, and sometimes discriminatory care for Deaf individuals [20].

The Deaf population, in general, is often overlooked and marginalized within health-related research. To our knowledge, prior to this study, no qualitative research about the lived experiences of the Deaf community in the DR has been published.

Although much of the data orginates from research conducted in the US, it is widely acknowledged globally and historically that over 90% of Deaf individuals are born to hearing parents, the majority of whom do not use nor know sign language [21].

Deaf individuals who do not acquire sign language during the neurocritical period of development experience significant difficulties in acquiring morphological and grammatical features compared to native signers [22]. This highlights the critical importance of first language exposure for the 90% of Deaf children born to hearing parents who may face delayed language acquisition [15].

Due to the lack of early screening in the DR, many families do not realize they have a Deaf child until the child is older and exhibiting developmental delays. This vulnerable population, using sign language as their main mode of communication, often experiences “language deprivation”—that is, not having an established first-language foundation before the critical neurodevelopmental window for language acquisition closes, typically within the first 5 years of life [22, 23].

Educational institutions for Deaf students are few in number and largely concentrated in urban areas. Most schools rely on oralist approaches or mainstreaming strategies, often without trained interpreters or sign-fluent teachers [24]. Non-governmental organizations and religious missions play a substantial role in providing Deaf education, but their quality and sustainability vary. As a result, Deaf people experience higher rates of low health literacy [7], stigmatization, and ineffective communication, which may lead them to avoid health care providers (HCPs) and health settings [25], as well as limited communication with HCPs who do not know sign language [11].

In general, health disparities in the DR are driven by intersecting factors such as poverty, rural isolation, racial and ethnic discrimination, and limited access to quality health care. Marginalized groups, including low-income individuals and rural residents, face systemic barriers that result in poorer health outcomes, limited insurance coverage, and exclusion from essential services. Deaf adults have significantly lower health-related vocabulary knowledge compared to hearing adults [6]. In an audio-centric environment, Deaf people are often excluded from incidental contextual information during conversations with hearing family members who do not sign, a phenomenon known as the “Dinner Table Syndrome” [26], as such exclusions are often most pronounced around the family dinner table. This is particularly impactful in cultures where family and community relationships play a central role in daily life. The consequences of limited and indirect family communication include missed health-related contextual information, which can negatively affect Deaf people’s lives and contribute to poorer health outcomes [27].

Deaf people may experience “audism”—a form of discrimination and prejudice based on an individual’s inability to hear—which can negatively affect their self-esteem and trust in hearing society [28]. In a systematic review of health care access among Deaf people, Kuenburg et al. identified consistent themes of communication challenges, greater difficulty accessing health information compared to hearing peers, and an increased risk of marginalization [29].

Such experiences of language deprivation, inconsistent patient-provider communication access, and poor health literacy compound existing gaps in access to care, information, and education. While the DR ratified the UN’s Convention on the Rights of People with Disabilities in August 2009, it was not until 2023 that it officially recognized its local sign language, known as “Lengua de Señas Dominicana” (LSRD) [30]. Although this recognition is a significant milestone and a point of national strength—especially when compared with many countries that still do not officially recognize their local sign languages—the delayed recognition of LSRD reflects the historical marginalization of sign language users and contributes to ongoing health disparities by limiting access to linguistically appropriate health information and services.

To address the persistent health inequities faced by the Deaf community in the DR, it is essential to understand the broader sociocultural and infrastructural challenges that hinder access to quality health care. Despite the international protections of the UN Convention on the Rights of People with Disabilities, the absence of a well-established support system for the Deaf population in LMICs often leads to marginalization. In many instances, access to qualified health care providers trained in sign language is limited, resulting in miscommunication, misunderstandings, and delayed or inadequate treatment. Additionally, cultural attitudes toward disability, including “audism” and stigmatization, exacerbate barriers to care, making it more difficult for Deaf individuals to seek help when needed. A lack of early hearing loss screening and insufficient educational resources further compound these disparities, particularly when families remain unaware of a child’s hearing loss until later stages of development, leading to missed opportunities for early intervention.

Therefore, this study aimed to identify salient issues and factors relating to Deaf Dominicans’ lived experience with health-related care and the social determinants of health in both urban and rural areas. The findings from this study might inform efforts to formulate equitable opportunities, interventions, and policies to (1) improve Deaf Dominicans’ access to health care, (2) equip health care settings to deliver high quality care to Deaf individuals and communities, and (3) ultimately enhance health outcomes among Deaf people.

Methods

Study design

We designed a qualitative study to examine Deaf Dominicans’ health experiences and access across the entire country of the DR in 2021–2022. Our partnerships included both urban and rural areas, with no geographical restrictions. No systematic research had been previously conducted with the Deaf community in the DR. After discussions with team partners, all concurred that an open-ended, qualitative methodology supplemented with some semi-structured elements, would be the most appropriate approach to optimize identification of cultural and lived-experience constructs among Deaf Dominicans, whether or not these were anticipated a priori.

Two research priorities (discussed below) drove the methods for this project: language and Dominican Deaf-led data collection, and appropriate ethical and methodological preparation.

Language and Dominican Deaf-led data collection

This project involved complex language relationships. The primary study population—Deaf Dominicans—communicate mainly through signing. Census data indicate that about 40% of Deaf Dominicans use “gestures,” 30% use LSRD, and 30% use spoken or written Spanish [14]. To honor these local communication practices, interviews were conducted by Deaf Dominicans using their own languages and approaches. All data collectors in the DR self-identified as Deaf and were experienced with local, indigenous communication. Other project team members’ primary languages included American Sign Language (ASL), Spanish, and English.

Interviews were initially planned to be conducted in person but shifted to Zoom due to the COVID-19 lockdown. Most interviews were ultimately held online, allowing Deaf Dominican participants to engage in real-time, face-to-face conversations in LSRD. ASL- and LSRD-fluent interpreters provided interpretation among ASL, English, LSRD, and Spanish, ensuring seamless communication across all languages. This arrangement prioritized uninterrupted conversation in LSRD between interviewers and participants, while allowing non-LSRD-fluent team members to observe (and if necessary, contribute) in English, ASL, and Spanish. This flexible format supported participant comfort, respected local communication preferences, and enabled unobtrusive participation by other researchers. Primary interactions occurred between Deaf Dominican interviewers and participants, fostering clear exchange of concepts and experiences.

Ethical and methodological preparation

Given the emphasis on language and the Deaf-led data collection, it was essential to make human-subjects research training accessible to interviewers with no prior research experience. Community-based data collectors—mainly members of ANSORDO, the representative body for Deaf Dominicans—completed a customized training plan based on the Spanish version of Family Health International’s Bioethics Training for Community Leaders. Training was delivered live in LSRD and Spanish, interpretation into ASL and English, and supplemented by case examples from University of Rochester (UR) researchers. All Dominican team members completed this training individually or as a group.

The entire project team undertook more than 20 h of instrument review, role-playing, and mock interviews to familiarize themselves with the content of each question and with qualitative interviewing techniques. As data collection progressed, the team debriefed after interviews, refining procedures and wording as needed.

Research team and reflexivity

This project was formed through four partnerships. The Deaf Dominican team is organized through ANSORDO, a longstanding community organization that advocates for Deaf Dominican interests and provides space for socialization and community organization. ANSORDO is the primary organization for Deaf people in the DR. Its leadership met regularly with external team members, provided guidance on methodology and logistics, participated as interviewers, and contributed to the creation of scholarly research outputs, including this manuscript.

At the beginning of this project, ANSORDO had an existing relationship with Discovering Deaf Worlds (DDW), later known as Deaf Worlds, a Deaf-led US-based NGO that provides advocacy, capacity building, and training for Deaf organizations in the US and globally. DDW also had structures for managing US federal funds and complying with contractual relationships.

The UR project team—including doctorally-prepared Deaf/ASL-fluent investigators and hearing English-/Spanish-fluent investigators—had prior long-term research partnerships in the DR with Pontificia Universidad Católica Madre y Maestra (PUCMM), a substantive research focus with Deaf communities in the US, and a professional connection with DDW. UR served as the primary recipient of funding, and has managed projects throughout Latin America and the Caribbean, including the DR, for more than 20 years.

Nine interviewers comprised the Deaf Dominican interview team, all of whom identified as Deaf and Dominican, with four identifying as male and five as female. All interviewers completed IRB and interviewing training alongside the UR-, DDW-, and PUCMM-based teams.

Participants

We recruited 102 people to participate in 51 individual or group qualitative interviews, averaging 72 min each. Inclusion criteria included self-reported childhood deafness (before age five), 18 years or older, self-identification as a Deaf community member and sign language user, and the ability and willingness to consent and participate. Exclusion criteria included late-deafened (after age five), hard-of-hearing or hearing individuals, those who did not identify as Deaf community members or sign language users, individuals under 18, and those unable to consent and participate. While DeafBlind participants were included in our data collection, interpretation of those interviews was uncertain, and, as a result, we do not report those results in this paper.

Participants were recruited via flyers, social media posts, community presentations, email invitations, vlogs, and through interpersonal communication. Consent was obtained in person using an information letter and visually signed consent form, also provided in written Spanish, and when necessary, visually in LSRD. A waiver of documentation of written consent was granted due to unclear written literacy among Deaf Dominicans and the typical restriction of formal document signing to legal and contractual contexts. Participants viewed a recorded video consent, followed by visually signed approval in LSRD to proceed. An information sheet was provided, outlining participants’ rights, study details, and contact information for questions.

Data collection

We used a semi-structured key informant and focus group interview guide that emphasized participants’ lived experiences across various social contexts (see Additional File 1). Discussions explored Deaf Dominicans’ experiences with health barriers, risk factors, protective factors, and early language experiences. All interviews were conducted in LSRD and visually recorded. Focus group discussions were limited to five participants or fewer. Participants represented all regions of the country, including urban and rural areas. Video-recordings ensured accurate coding and thematic analysis while capturing essential non-verbal cues. Visual data were transcribed into written text through interactive interpretation by LSRD-fluent Deaf and hearing Dominicans, along with ASL-fluent investigators. Written transcripts were collaboratively reviewed by Deaf and hearing Dominican team members to cross-check linguistic and cultural nuances.

Data analysis

Interview data were first open-coded for participant-generated constructs, then deliberately coded for emerging themes [31]. Themes were iteratively organized through participatory report-back mechanisms with partners and community members to generate grounded models for subsequent elaboration and testing [32]. Data processing and analyses were conducted using DEDOOSE software, cloud-based mixed-method data analysis software, by team members fluent in LSRD and ASL. Coding was completed using an emic-etic comprehensive codebook (Additional File 2), and analyses were performed on the written English transcripts after translation from sign language [33]. Grounded theories were finalized through iterative discussion among researchers, data collection staff, and community participants [34]. While the Biopsychosocial Model [35] provided the initial framework, the iterative and inductive analysis allowed for localized models to emerge from the data that may support, extend, or diverge from the original framework [36].

We report our findings in adherence to the COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative research) Checklist (see Additional File 3) [37].

Results

In total, 102 individuals from across the DR (55 males, 47 females; median age 33 years) participated in 51 interview sessions during the study period.

Three consistent themes emerged from analyses of focus groups and interviews: Deaf Dominicans (1) depend on hearing people as facilitators and navigators to access health care, (2) often experience limited health care information—both in transactional health encounters and more broadly regarding general health knowledge—due to this dependence, and (3) frequently communicate with the hearing world, including health care providers, through gestures rather than through an established sign language.

Reliance on hearing people as gatekeepers and facilitators

As shown in Table 1, the predominant theme regarding Deaf Dominicans’ lived experiences with health care is reliance on hearing people as gatekeepers and facilitators. Deaf experiences spanned doctors’ offices, clinics, hospitals, and occasional emergencies in public or private spaces. Participants reported a clear dependence on family members—often mothers or grandmothers—as primary caregivers assisting them in health care settings. This reliance is largely due to the absence of legal requirements for hospitals and health care providers to offer sign language interpreting services, forcing Deaf individuals to pay out of pocket, which is frequently inaccessible. Additionally, there is a general shortage of qualified sign language interpreters in the DR, with even fewer possessing expertise in interpreting complex medical concepts.

The involvement of mothers and grandmothers specifically—and family more generally—is consistent with the DR’s family-centered and collective culture. For Deaf individuals, this involvement often continues throughout their lives, well into adulthood. Many participants reported that they could not navigate clinical settings without a familiar hearing family member and would avoid hospitals or doctor’s offices if such support was unavailable, sometimes resulting in uncomfortable or awkward situations for the Deaf person.

Also illustrated in Table 1, another co-occurring factor alongside Deaf Dominicans’ dependence on hearing people to access health care is a lack of common language for communication. Sign language is the primary mode of communication through which Deaf people communicate and understand one another. When a health provider does not know sign language, the Deaf individual must rely on alternative communication methods. Participants repeatedly stated that they have no choice but use gestures when communicating with a doctor or family member in a dependent interaction, such as when the family member acts as interpreter. Other strategies include writing back and forth or using gestures with family members, such as pointing to the area of pain (e.g., the throat) or acting out events like a fall.

Lack of accessible heath care information

The second most common—and closely related—theme that emerged regarding Deaf Dominicans’ health care experiences is the dearth of health care information, as shown in Table 2. Because of their dependence on hearing people for information, Deaf Dominicans are vulnerable to receiving incomplete information or none at all. Without direct means of communication, they rely on others to guide them and have limited insight, agency, or autonomy in managing their own health or caring for their bodies.

Discussion

The grounded theory that emerged from this analysis shows that Deaf Dominicans’ lived experience with health and health care are characterized by an overwhelming reliance on hearing people for services, information, and communication with health professionals. This dependence stems from inaccessible societal structures that assume full hearing and spoken language skills are necessary to navigate health systems. Our model demonstrates that most Deaf Dominicans live in an audio-centric environment where few family members or others use sign language, forcing them to rely on non-sign language gestures to communicate with providers and the wider community.

In a collective culture like that in the DR, where family ties are highly valued, Deaf individuals often depend on their parents—especially mothers and grandmothers— to manage interactions with the world, including with health care. Family-mediated disclosure of health information is common in the DR [38]. Parents frequently continue as caregivers for their Deaf children into adulthood. Participants often described mothers and grandmothers as their primary escorts to clinics and hospitals. This lifelong dependence fosters an internalized lack of autonomy regarding their own health and well-being, paralleling barriers documented among older Deaf adults in the US [39]. Importantly, our theory highlights that the absence of sign language interpreters, poor communication with providers, limited Deaf cultural competence among professionals, and systemic inaccessibility of health information all contribute to feelings of disempowerment and diminished autonomy among Deaf Dominicans.

As most Deaf people are born into hearing families, they are often—as Smith and Chin describe—a “visual minority within their own auditory-dominant family” [40]. If there is no bidirectional visual communication, they cannot fully communicate with their relatives. Deaf Dominicans report relying on hearing family members to access health services; without them, they often have no way to obtain care. Because most of these families do not fully know sign language, communication typically occurs through gestures, lipreading, or writing. This interaction is often inconsistent and limited, reflecting the alternate communication modalities Deaf people frequently use with hearing individuals. Our findings show that, in the DR, Deaf people commonly depend on “lipreading” and “gestures”—particularly in health care settings—rather than their native sign language, a pattern also documented in other Deaf communities [41, 42].

Not having full language acquisition from a young age can have detrimental effects on quality of life across the lifespan, impacting cognitive, socioemotional, and overall development, with lasting consequences into adulthood [23, 43, 44]. Language deprivation, not auditory deprivation, explains behavioral problems that may arise in Deaf children [45]. It is also likely a key social determinant underlying disparities in health literacy, advocacy skills, and the ability to receive effective medical care. Early experiences of limited language access thus have a disabling effect, contributing to the reliance many Deaf adults have on their family members.

As shown in Table 2, the paucity of health care information that Deaf Dominicans receive also stems from interactions within the family home [26, 46]. Deaf individuals often lack access to information shared among hearing family members, including incidental details about a family member’s health, or even their own, when conversations occur in spoken language [26]. This deprivation of incidental learning opportunities affects their understanding of personal health and family health history. Years of conscious and unconscious exclusion at the “dinner table” can lead to feelings of isolation, neglect, ignorance, and resignation [26]. Research demonstrates that early communication barriers and experiences of language deprivation constitute adverse childhood communication experiences, which are associated with increased risk for chronic diseases and mental health disorders in Deaf and hard-of-hearing adults [47]. This study identifies language deprivation and communication neglect as forms of early-life toxic stress with potential long-term health effects, establishing a direct link between formative dinner table experiences and adult health trajectories [47].

Regarding the social implications of these challenges, the hearing population plays a crucial role in mitigating such barriers. Our findings highlight the urgent need for family members, friends, and society at large to actively learn LSRD to facilitate communication and improve daily interactions with Deaf individuals. Learning LSRD not only enhances social and health care engagement but also fosters an inclusive and respectful environment that embraces diversity. The analysis of communication challenges in the DR reveals factors that amplify the vulnerability of Deaf individuals, particularly in the health care context. Notably, the lack of effective communication with health professionals contributes to heightened anxiety and uncertainty, which negatively affects overall well-being. Inadequate interaction in these settings can lead to substandard care and increased emotional distress.

Our grounded theory shows that the Deaf Dominican community experiences lower health literacy—the ability to access, understand, and use health information for their own well-being [6]—consistent with literature showing that people with hearing loss often have lower health literacy than those without hearing loss [48]. Deaf individuals frequently have limited access to health care information, restricted knowledge about medical conditions, inadequate awareness of disease prevention and screening tests, and face barriers with HCPs [49]. McKee et al. found that that 48% of participants with hearing loss had inadequate health literacy and were 6.9 times more likely than hearing individuals to have poor health literacy [7]. Pollard et al. found that even among people with hearing disabilities who had a college degree, health-related vocabulary knowledge was lower than that of hearing peers [6]. Deaf Dominicans are therefore at high risk for poor health literacy, highlighting the need for timely, accessible materials and health information.

As shown in Table 3, when Deaf Dominicans are forced to use communication modalities other than their native sign language, effective patient-provider communication often breaks down. Deaf individuals frequently face stigmatization, impatience, and negative attitudes, which can discourage them from returning to HCPs [25]. Such miscommunication can lead to misdiagnosis and inadequate follow-up regarding their medical conditions [50]. As noted earlier, Deaf patients with language-concordant health care providers are more likely to utilize a broader range of preventive services [11].

Deaf Dominicans repeatedly reported how they did not understand HCPs at all, consistent with research elsewhere showing that even Deaf individuals who frequently use health services often have greater difficulty communicating with HCPs [51]. In the US, ASL interpreters observed that patients did not fully understand HCP instructions in nearly half of appointments [52]. Communication difficulties with HCPs often lead Deaf adults to feel distrustful, fearful, and frustrated with medical care [42]. Deaf individuals also report that access to care in sign language or with a live interpreter would increase their satisfaction with medical care [42].

Iezzoni et al. examined methods for effective communication, including lipreading, writing notes, and using sign language interpreters [41]. However, lipreading and writing notes may not always be effective, particularly in regions where sign language education and resources are limited. Their effectiveness depends on factors such as the individual’s education level and familiarity with these tools. One study of Deaf ASL users’ understanding of prescription medicines in the US found that complex written English led to misunderstandings and negative attitudes from pharmacy team staff [53].

As our findings show, similar aversive attitudes are common in Deaf Dominicans’ experiences with HCPs, who often exclude patients from shared decision-making about their own health. Breakdown of patient-provider communication can be frustrating, confusing, and even threaten patient safety, such as not knowing proper medication protocols. These experiences are emotionally taxing, exhausting, and detrimental to well-being. Furthermore, misaligned communication can compromise confidentiality, increasing the risk of marginalization and discrimination if medical or personal information is misinterpreted or inappropriately disclosed. Protecting privacy and ensuring respect for confidentiality are therefore fundamental to promoting equitable health care for Deaf individuals.

We document that Deaf Dominicans experience structural audism when hearing individuals—whether in the home, community, or health care settings—dismiss or ignore their perspectives, needs, and linguistic identity. Addressing structural audism in health care requires urgent attention to overcome systemic barriers, including providing professional, qualified sign language interpreters trained to uphold ethical standards and maintain patient confidentiality; training health care professionals to work effectively with Deaf patients; offering linguistically appropriate health education; and supporting to Deaf Dominicans to self-advocate. These changes are critical, as many Deaf individuals lack access to health insurance due to documentation or employment barriers, or are forced to pay out-of-pocket. Many rely on public health insurance (Seguro Nacional de Salud—National Health Insurance). Unemployment or informal employment further limits access to contributory insurance, and even when eligible for subsidized coverage, Deaf individuals face barriers to enrollment, such as inaccessible government forms, forms only in Spanish, or lack of interpreters.

In addition to the challenges Deaf Dominicans face in accessing health care, it is vital to emphasize the need for enhanced education and awareness of Deaf culture and communication needs across the health care system. Integrating Deaf awareness training for health care providers is essential to improve their understanding of the unique needs of Deaf patients and how to communicate effectively with them. Hoang et al. developed educational training for medical students about people with hearing loss, exposing prospective doctors to Deaf culture and increasing their preparedness to work with Deaf patients in the future [54]. Providing similar sensitivity training and communication strategies in academic institutions in the DR could help Deaf patients feel less frustrated or confused when visiting hospitals or clinics. Such training should cover not only the fundamentals of sign language but also the cultural factors that influence how Deaf individuals engage with their environment. Furthermore, establishing clear communication protocols and ensuring consistent access to professional sign language interpreters would help reduce barriers in health care settings. These initiatives could foster a more inclusive environment where Deaf patients feel understood and supported, ultimately improving health outcomes and patient satisfaction. Additionally, involving the broader community—particularly through incorporating Deaf education into public health campaigns—can help normalize communication access for all.

Deaf people generally face delayed access to timely health information, which is often shared in an audio-centric environment through auditory means such as phone or radio. Lack of access to emergency preparedness information can have severe implications, and urgent attention is needed at all levels to ensure full accessibility for Deaf individuals in disaster situations [55]. In South Africa, Haricharan et al. demonstrated that Deaf people can use short message service (SMS) as a strategy to improve health education information among peers, incorporating images and sign language-based drama [56]. Similar approaches could enhance health awareness and disseminate information among Deaf Dominicans. Improving communication modalities can also be simple: providers can face the Deaf individual with good lighting, speak clearly without obstructing their mouth (important if lipreading is needed), use visual or tactile alerting devices in waiting rooms, avoid relying solely on telephones, and know how to access and effectively use qualified LSRD interpreters when necessary [57]. Ultimately, the goal is for Deaf Dominicans to participate authentically in shared decision-making within a fully inclusive environment.

Deaf people generally experience poorer health outcomes than their hearing peers, including in mental health [10], reproductive care [58], and lower rates of preventive screenings such as cervical cancer [59]. To reduce these health inequities, attention should be given to the sociocultural context of the DR, embracing the strong family-oriented structure. The government could provide support groups, such as parent-to-parent networks, and social services for early intervention for parents with Deaf children [60]. Early newborn hearing screening should also be ensured within the first few months of life so that intervention services can begin during the critical early years, supporting appropriate language and socioemotional development [61].

As part of early intervention, parents should be exposed to LSRD to support language development and communication, rather than relying solely on hearing devices. For optimal language access, the whole family should learn sign language alongside the Deaf child to minimize the negative effects of “Dinner Table Syndrome” [62]. One limitation of this study was the inability to include interviews from individuals outside established networks who experience greater health care disparities, such as DeafBlind participants. Significant communication barriers and lack of appropriate modalities during data collection meant that these interviews could not be fully comprehended or validated, limiting data reliability and depth. Future research should prioritize inclusion of DeafBlind individuals by employing alternative and accessible methods such as tactile sign language, protactile interpreting, and other individualized accommodations. By providing targeted resources and culturally accessible support, Deaf Dominicans would be better equipped to navigate the health care system independently and empowered to achieve equitable access to quality care.

To alleviate the emotional burden Deaf Dominicans experience while interacting with hearing family members and peers, ANSORDO has provided educational workshops for hearing family members on how to communicate effectively with their Deaf children. This current study highlights the importance of strong familial support and suggests that these workshops could be expanded to include information about the health of Deaf family members and strategies to increase foundational health knowledge. While ANSORDO successfully led the movement for nationwide recognition of LSRD after this work was completed [30], expanded advocacy and policy efforts are still needed at both national and local levels to ensure full health care access for Deaf Dominicans, with targeted interventions in rural and urban areas. This includes providing fully qualified sign language interpreters, visual aids throughout health care settings, and early intervention and screening services. Deaf individuals in rural areas may face additional barriers to timely health care, including reduced quality and frequency of services and less access to information compared to urban families [63]. Community-based outreach efforts leveraging ANSORDO’s expertise could help alleviate Deaf Dominicans’ anxiety when interacting with HCPs.

Conclusions

Deaf Dominicans are an underserved linguistic and cultural minority facing substantial barriers in accessing the health care. Their reliance on hearing family members and others, limited access to sign language, and systemic audism contribute to reduced autonomy, lower health literacy, and poorer health outcomes. Ensuring full communication and comprehension through accessible sign language interpretation, culturally competent care, and community- and family-based support is essential to achieving equitable health care access. Targeted interventions, policy changes, and broader Deaf awareness initiatives are critical to empower Deaf Dominicans to navigate the health system independently and improve overall well-being.
