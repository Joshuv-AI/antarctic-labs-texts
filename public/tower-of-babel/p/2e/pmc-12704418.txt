Concomitant use of medical cannabis and drugs associated with risks of interaction in older patients: a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Zoumana Cheick Bérété, Arvis Abraham Sebgo, Dean T Eurich, Cerina Dubois, Jason R Dyck, John G Hanlon, Arsene Zongo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCannabinoids interact with multiple drugs, with some interactions having significant clinical effects.ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate amongst older people (i) the trends in the concomitant medical use of cannabis and drugs associated with a risk of significant clinical interaction with cannabinoids (DARSCIC), including those with a narrow therapeutic index (drugs with a narrow therapeutic index; DNTI) such as warfarin, and (ii) the risk of bleeding, drug-related intoxication, thyrotoxicosis and major cardiovascular events.MethodsFor objective 1, we conducted an interrupted time series study amongst 12 599 seniors who received an authorised cannabis prescription in Ontario from 2014–2019. Using clinical and medico-administrative data, dispensations of DARSCIC were assessed pre-post cannabis prescription. For objective 2, longitudinal cohort studies were conducted in patients concomitantly exposed to cannabis and warfarin (bleeding) or to DNTI (intoxication) versus controls.ResultsFor objective 1, the trends of DARSCIC/DNTI dispensations were similar in the year before and after cannabis prescription. For objective 2, amongst 378 patients exposed to cannabis and warfarin, the risk of bleeding was 1.19, 95%CI (0.71–1.98), compared to 1646 controls. The risk of drug-related intoxication was 2.61, 95%CI (1.42–4.79) amongst 3926 patients exposed to cannabis and DNTI compared to 12 223 controls. Patients exposed to cannabis and levothyroxine (n = 2499) had a significantly higher risk of heart failure but not thyrotoxicosis, acute coronary syndrome or stroke.ConclusionThese findings indicate that prescribing practices for cannabis may not adequately consider the implications of DARSCIC/DNTI. Enhancing prescriber awareness of these interactions could mitigate the risk of adverse effects such as drug-related intoxication.Keywords: medical cannabis, cannabinoids, drug interaction, time series analysis, bleeding, intoxication, older patients

Introduction

Cannabis is increasingly used for medical purposes in North America and other countries around the world [1]. In the United States, a national survey shows a 333% increase in cannabis use amongst adults aged 65 and older between 2002 and 2014 [2]. In Canada, the proportion of older adults (65+) using cannabis rose from 1% in 2012 to 7% in 2019 [3], reflecting one of the fastest-growing demographics for cannabis use. Most importantly, survey data suggest that cannabis is mainly used for medical purposes by older persons [3]. Indeed, according to the 2019 Canadian Cannabis Survey, amongst older adults who reported cannabis use, 76% used cannabis for medical purposes (52% for exclusive medical use and 24% for both medical and recreational use), representing >350 000 people who consumed cannabis for medical purposes in 2019 [4]. This usage occurs either through medical prescriptions or self-medication to treat conditions such as pain, anxiety and sleep disorders [5].

In the body, the main cannabinoids, notably delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD), are metabolised by cytochrome P450 enzymes, a group of enzymes involved in the metabolism of ~90% of drugs used in humans [6]. These pharmacological mechanisms lead to interactions between cannabinoids and certain drugs [7–9]. A review of the literature indicates that some of these interactions have clinically significant effects, ranging from mild to severe, including simple disruptions of biological markers to the risk of severe gastrointestinal bleeding [7, 8].

A total of 21 such drugs, have been identified, including warfarin (full list in Appendix 1a) [7, 8]. Recently, another list of 16 drugs with a narrow therapeutic index (DNTI), also presenting clinically significant interaction risks with cannabinoids, was published (Appendix 1b) [9]. Their particularity lies in the fact that a minimal change in their concentration can lead to serious adverse effects or treatment inefficacy [9].

However, the concomitant medical use of cannabis products and these drugs, and the associated risks are not well known and could potentially expose patients to undue risks.

Therefore, this research aimed (i) to describe the trends in the use of drugs associated with a risk of significant clinical interaction with cannabinoids (DARSCIC) in patients aged 66 and older who received authorised prescriptions of cannabis in Ontario between 2014 and 2019, (ii) to assess factors associated with the concomitant use of DARSCIC and cannabis products and finally (iii) to estimate the risk of bleeding in patients concomitantly exposed to cannabis and warfarin (objective 3a), as well as the risk of drug-related intoxication amongst patients concomitantly exposed to cannabis and DNTI (objective 3b). Warfarin was specifically assessed as it is the only DARSCIC in the category of severe interaction and the most dispensed DARSCIC in our data. For objective 1, we hypothesised that practitioners have a low awareness of the risks of interaction between cannabinoids and certain drugs and would not adjust the medication accordingly when prescribing DARSCIC to older patients on cannabinoids. For objective 3, we hypothesised a higher risk of bleeding and intoxication.

Methods

Study design

For our first objective, we conducted an interrupted time series analysis to assess the trends in DARSCIC dispensations. To this end, a follow-up of 365 days before and 365 days after the authorised prescription cannabis date (i.e. index date) was considered for each patient. Each 365-day period was then subdivided into four periods of 91 days in which the dispensation of DARSCIC was repeatedly assessed. In secondary analysis, 24 30-day time windows (12 before and 12 after the index date) were also considered.

An interrupted time series analysis was also conducted amongst controls to appreciate how the effect of potential time-varying factors could have influenced the trends of DARSCIC dispensation independently of the cannabis prescription [10].

For the second objective, we conducted a cohort study within the cannabis sample. For this study, each patient was followed for 365 days to identify those with a dispensation of any DARSCIC (outcome). The factors potentially associated with DARSCIC were assessed in the 365 days before or at the index date.

For the third objective, we conducted a cohort study comparing patients concomitantly exposed to cannabis and warfarin (objective 3a) to controls using warfarin. The first warfarin dispensation date in the year following cannabis prescription (or control index date) was used as a new index date. For each patient, the outcome (i.e. bleeding) was assessed from this new index date and up to the end of the 365-day period following cannabis prescription. The same approach was used for objective 3b by considering the date of the first dispensation of DNTI as the new index date (see details in Appendix 2).

Study population

This study included individuals who received an authorised prescription of cannabis and individuals selected from the general population of Ontario (control group).

Patients who received an authorised prescription of cannabis (Figure 1) are patients of all ages with various health conditions (i.e. pain, anxiety, depression, etc.) who consulted in cannabis clinics.

Selection of study population.

At the request of our research team, the Institute for Clinical Evaluative Sciences of Ontario (ICES) selected and matched three controls to each cannabis patient using the Ontario Health administrative data. The matching was based on propensity scores (details in [11]). An index date was assigned to each control based on the distribution of the exposed group’s index dates.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

For the present study, objectives 1 and 2, we included patients aged 66 and older covered by the Ontario public drug insurance plan in the 365 days before and 365 days after their index date. Exclusion criteria are presented in Fig. 1.

For objectives 3a and 3b, we selected from patients included for objective 1, those who had a dispensation of warfarin (or DNTI for objective 3b) following cannabis prescription or the control index date.

Data sources

Clinical data from cannabis clinics and Ontario medico-administrative data (anonymized) provided by ICES were used. The cannabis clinic data come from all adult patients authorised to use medical cannabis, between June 2014 and January 2019 [12]. These data were collected and recorded during patients’ initial visits to the clinics and follow-up consultations. They include sociodemographic characteristics; reasons for consultation; comorbidities; consumption of medications, drugs, tobacco, and alcohol; past and current cannabis use; and the duration of this use [12].

The medico-administrative data cover the period from January 2011 to March 2020 and include drug dispensation data, emergency visits, hospitalisation and outpatient visits data (details in Appendix 3 [13]).

Outcomes

The outcome for objective 1 was the dispensation of at least one of 18 DARSCICs available in the Canadian market (excluding caffeine) (Appendix 1a), evaluated from the drug dispensation data. Within each 91-day interval before and after the date of the authorised cannabis prescription, the DIN codes of each DARSCIC were searched. Then, the identified DARSCICs were classified based on the severity of the interaction. The proportion of patients exposed to DARSCIC in each 91-day interval was assessed.

For the third objective, bleeding that occurred after the dispensation of warfarin and up to the end of the 365-day period following cannabis prescription was assessed [14] (detailed definition in Appendix 4). These events were identified using ICD-10 codes in hospitalisation and emergency department data. Only the first event was considered. As with bleeding, drug-related intoxication events were assessed in the period following the dispensation of a first DNTI and up to the 365-day post cannabis prescription date. Specifically, intoxication events related to the main therapeutic classes of the 16 DNTI listed in our study were assessed (See list and ICD-10 codes in Appendix 4) [15].

Covariates

At the study’s index date, the following sociodemographic characteristics of the patients, such as age, sex, neighbourhood income quintiles and place of residence (rural vs urban), were evaluated. In the year before the index date, the number of doctor visits and hospitalisations, and different comorbidities (see list in Table 1), were also evaluated in both groups using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes in the administrative data. In addition to these variables, we also evaluated other variables in the exposed group using their electronic clinical records (data provided by the cannabis clinics) (see list in Table 1).

Statistical analyses

For objective 1, the proportion of patients with any dispensation of DARSCIC within each 91-day period was calculated, as well as the type of DARSCIC.

To compare the trend of DARSCIC dispensation 1 year before and 1 year after the cannabis prescription, we conducted segmented linear regressions [10]. We modelled the proportion of DARSCIC use within each 91-day interval during the year before and after the authorised cannabis prescription date, which is the segmentation factor of the periods. The results of the segmented linear regression were stratified by sex. Autocorrelation was tested using the Durbin-Watson test [10]. The 24-month follow-up period allowed for an equal number of measurement points before and after the index dates to account for the possible seasonal variation in the trend of DARSCIC dispensation [10].

The same analyses were conducted in the sample of controls.

For the second objective, factors associated with DARSCIC dispensation were assessed using logistic regression. All variables were entered in the model and backward selection was used to retain variables associated with a P-value ≤.20. This first step aimed to minimise the number of variables to include in the final model. From the final model, only variables associated with the outcome with a P-value <5% were considered statistically significant and regarded as factors associated with DARSCIC dispensation.

For the third objective, to estimate the risk of bleeding in patients concomitantly exposed to cannabis and warfarin versus controls exposed to warfarin, we used Poisson regressions. To minimise confounding, we calculated propensity scores (i.e. probability of being exposed to cannabis + warfarin) using sociodemographic variables, comorbidities, healthcare utilisation and use of drugs (including all other anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs available in Canada) before the date of warfarin dispensation (a total of 30 variables presented in Appendix 4). Next, inverse probability weights were calculated with the propensity scores and stabilised (Appendix 5) [16]. Finally, we used weighted Poisson regression to estimate the risk of bleeding. The same approach was used for assessing the risk of intoxication by assessing the covariates before the date of the first DNTI dispensation.

The significance threshold was 5%. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Secondary analysis

We assessed the trends of dispensation of a second list of 16 DNTI (list in Appendix 1b). The same methodology as for objective 1 was used.

The trends in DARSCIC dispensation were also evaluated for patients who received their first prescription of cannabis in 2018 and 2019, i.e. the period around or post-legalisation of cannabis for recreational use in Canada.

We also assessed the trend of DARSCIC in 30-day time windows (12 before and 12 after index dates).

Finally, as levothyroxine was the most frequently dispensed DNTI in the cohort, we also evaluated the risk of thyrotoxicosis and major adverse cardiovascular events, including acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or stroke (composite outcome), and heart failure in patients concomitantly exposed to cannabis and levothyroxine (details in Appendix 6) [17].

Results

A total of 12 599 exposed individuals and 48 651 controls were included for objectives 1 and 2 (Fig. 1). The age group 66–70 years was the most represented amongst the exposed (36.05%) and the controls (31.05%) (Table 1). The majority of participants were female, 60.98% amongst the exposed and 59.16% amongst the controls.

Overall, 6.8% of the exposed and 6.4% of the controls had at least one DARSCIC dispensation in the year following their index dates. The three most dispensed DARSCICs amongst patients who received at least one DARSCIC in the exposed group were warfarin (44.92%), ketoconazole (23.80%) and tacrolimus (12.60%) (Appendix 7).

For objective 1, the ITS results for the exposed group showed that, compared to the period before cannabis prescription, a non-significant reduction of 3 patients on DARSCIC/10,000 cannabis-exposed patients was observed in the first 91-day period following cannabis prescription (immediate change) (P-value = .84) (Table 2). In the year following the prescription, a non-significant increase of 7 patients on DARSCIC/10 000 cannabis-exposed patients per 91-day period was observed (temporal change) (P-value = .35) (Table 2).

For individuals concomitantly exposed to medical cannabis and levothyroxine, the risks of ACS, stroke and thyrotoxicosis were not significant. However, a significantly higher risk of heart failure was observed (Appendix 6).

Discussion

In this study, no significant variation in the dispensing trends of DARSCICs or DNTI was observed in the year following cannabis prescription compared to the year before. Epilepsy, bipolar disorders, cardiovascular diseases requiring anticoagulants (other than congestive heart failure) and a high number of doctor visits were associated with an increased likelihood of DARSCIC dispensing. Finally, the concomitant exposure to medical cannabis and DNTI was associated with a significantly higher risk of drug-related intoxication. Additionally, a significantly higher risk of ED visit or hospitalisation for heart failure (incident or not) was observed in individuals concomitantly exposed to medical cannabis and levothyroxine. However, the risks of ACS/stroke and thyrotoxicosis were not significant in this sub-sample. A non-significant risk of bleeding was also observed in patients concomitantly exposed to cannabis and warfarin.

Warfarin was the most dispensed DARSCIC in the cohort. This is concerning as minor disturbances in the International Normalized Ratio (INR), a marker measuring blood fluidity, can lead to serious adverse effects [18]. In their systematic review, Nachnani et al. reported that in six of seven clinical cases reporting interactions between warfarin and cannabinoids, 71.4% of patients required warfarin dose adjustments, and 28.5% experienced adverse effects such as gastrointestinal bleeding [9].

The results showing no change in the dispensing trends of DARSCIC raise concerns about patients’ safety. Indeed, the results suggest that prescribers may not be adjusting their DARSCIC prescriptions, including those with a narrow therapeutic index, for older patients vulnerable to drug interaction risks whilst receiving medical cannabis. The scientific literature on the clinical interaction risks of cannabinoids with other drugs is recent and sparse [7–9]. Moreover, there is a lack of clinical guidelines to guide the prescription of cannabinoids and drugs with a potential for interaction [19]. Additionally, a lack of communication between practitioners, particularly those prescribing cannabis and those providing follow-up care, due to the absence of a shared electronic patient record, could also explain the observed results [20]. The absence of pharmacists in the cannabis care chain in Canada, who could analyse cannabis interaction profiles with DARSCICs and provide real-time recommendations to prescribers, should also be considered [21].

Although a non-significant increase in the risk of bleeding was observed in individuals concomitantly exposed to medical cannabis and warfarin, it remains a concern regarding the seriousness of the event and the fact that a statistical power issue could not be excluded. For drug-related intoxication, our results suggest that concomitant exposure to medical cannabis and DNTI should be avoided. The significantly higher risk of ED visit or hospitalisation for heart failure associated with the concomitant exposure to medical cannabis and levothyroxine reinforces this recommendation.

Our study has limitations. First, data on the type of cannabinoids and their route of use that would allow for a more precise assessment of the potential risk of interaction were missing. THC and CBD are the main cannabinoids used for medical purposes [22]. As shown in Appendix 8, both THC and CBD inhibit at least one of the cytochrome or glucuronidation enzymes that are mainly involved in the metabolism of 17/18 DARSCIC [23–25]. Therefore, the risk of cannabinoid-drug interaction remains plausible for patients exposed to cannabis, regardless of the type of cannabinoids used. Moreover, some controls may have used non-medical cannabis for recreational or self-medicating purposes, potentially leading to a slight underestimation of the observed associations. Residual confounding due to unmeasured confounders is also possible.

Regarding the generalisability of the results, the medical cannabis cohort may be made up of patients whose characteristics may somewhat differ from the general population. Finally, since the data cover the period from 2014 to 2019, the observed trends in DARSCIC/DNTI dispensations may not accurately reflect the current trends, given the rapid evolution of the cannabis landscape.

Conclusion

Our study showed that older patients with cannabis prescriptions may be exposed to DARSCICs, as no change in the trend of their dispensation was observed. Several factors may contribute to this exposure. Finally, concomitant exposure to medical cannabis and DARSCIC/DNTI was associated with an increased risk of drug-related intoxications and heart failure. Interestingly, no significant risk was observed for bleeding, ACS/stroke and thyrotoxicosis.
