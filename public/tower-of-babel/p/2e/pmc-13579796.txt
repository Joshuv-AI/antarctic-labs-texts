Gene co-expression networks related to intramuscular fatty acid composition across different pig genotypes
Authors: Mariah Castro Durval, Luiz F. Brito, Simara Larissa Fanalli, Fernanda Nery Ciconello, Camila Sabino Oliveira, Lucas Echevarria Nascimento, Bruna Pereira Martins da Silva, Aline Silva Mello Cesar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe nutritional quality of pork, a vital source of protein in the human diet, is being increasingly studied due to health concerns regarding pork fatty acid (FA) composition. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to identify the relationship between muscle gene expression and the composition of different FAs in pigs, focusing on the two prevalent genotypes (AA and AB) from hotspot expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) regions within the study population. This was achieved through the application of weighted gene co-expression network analyses (WGCNA) based on RNA-Seq data from 72 pigs to construct gene co-expression clusters and correlate them with the FA profile of skeletal muscle in pigs. The WGCNA was constructed using RNA-Seq data normalized to transcripts per million (TPM) across three hotspot eQTL regions: SSC3 (102,983,783 bp), SSC2 (67,926,641 bp), and SSC6 (93,742,864 bp).ResultsFor the hotspot located in SSC3, the AA genotype exhibited five significant co-expressed gene modules, while the BB genotype had only one. At the hotspot on SSC2, the AA genotype had four significant gene modules compared to five in the AB genotype. Notably, at the hotspot located in SSC6, 15 significant gene modules were identified for the AA genotype, whereas none were observed for the AB genotype. The co-expression networks revealed differences in gene connectivity, functional enrichment of identified modules, and hub genes, highlighting significant variations between the genotypes.ConclusionsThe observed differences in co-expression related to FA composition in the AA and AB genotypes suggest that the genotypes influence gene co-expression. Furthermore, we identified genes co-expressed with metabolic processes indicative of diseases such as diabetes, alongside genes related to immunity and inflammatory response, including MAPK8, TFG, CCL17, TBX21, ELP2, and GYS1. These results enhance our understanding of the mechanisms in which genetic variations located in specific genomic regions impact the co-expression of genes related to metabolic regulation, thereby influencing relevant traits in pig breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13086-y.

Introduction

Pork is a key component of the human diet due to its nutritional quality [1, 2]. More recently, consumers are increasingly focusing on the nutritional properties and healthiness of meat [3]. Fatty acids (FA), especially monounsaturated (MUFA) and polyunsaturated (PUFA), are essential for human and animal health [4–6]. Different FAs can have favorable or unfavorable effects on human health. For instance, some saturated FA (SFA) are implicated in the progression of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes mellitus [7–9]. In contrast, MUFA are considered effective for controlling and preventing obesity [10]. A high consumption of unsaturated FA (UFA), such as PUFA and MUFA, has been demonstrated to lower the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus [11, 12].

In addition to the nutritional value of pork to the human diet, pigs (Sus scrofa) are an excellent human biomedical model [13–15]. For instance, pigs have been used for studying metabolic diseases such as obesity, diabetes, processes related to inflammation and the immune system, and for the development of new health therapies [2, 15, 16]. Fatty acids contribute to the regulation of transcription in tissues such as muscle and adipose tissue [17, 18]. This regulation occurs either through direct binding of FA to the transcription factor (TF) or via indirect mechanisms. For instance, FAs can modulate signaling pathways that control the expression of TF or influence their phosphorylation, ubiquitination, or proteolytic cleavage [19]. Consequently, FAs affect gene expression and modulate TF, which in turn influence metabolic and inflammatory processes [6, 20, 21].

The identification of expression Quantitative Trait Loci (eQTL) variants that regulate gene expression and contribute to phenotypic variation is fundamental to understanding the biological mechanisms that influence complex traits such as FAs composition [22, 23]. A previous analysis of this dataset [24] identified regions in the genome referred to as eQTL hotspots, where genetic polymorphisms regulate the expression of numerous genes. These hotspots can act as key regulatory hubs, potentially modulating metabolic pathways [22, 25].

Weighted gene co-expression network analyses (WGCNA) are widely used to analyze gene expression patterns by constructing co-expression networks [26]. These networks are modular, with each module representing a cluster of co-expressed genes [26]. Each gene module comprises genes that share functional relationships and participate in similar biological processes, while hub genes act as key regulators within a module, reflecting its overall function and potential biological significance. WGCNA has been applied to analyses exploring groups of highly correlated genes related to specific traits [13, 26, 27] and has also been used across various species to explore gene networks related to phenotypic traits and genetic variability [13, 28], including studies investigating the influence of genotype on gene co-expression patterns [23]. However, its application in pigs remains limited despite its potential to uncover regulatory mechanisms at a systems level.

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying gene co-expression across different genotypes, the main objective of this study was to identify key pathways and co-expressed hub genes involved in FA deposition in intramuscular fat (IMF) of Large White pigs. For this purpose, we focused on the genes located within three eQTL hotspot regions identified by Durval et al. [24]: SSC3 (102,983,783 bp), SSC2 (67,926,641 bp), and SSC6 (93,742,864 bp). These regions were selected due to the presence of TF and genes previously associated with lipid metabolism.

Methods

All animal procedures were approved by the “Luiz de Queiroz” College of Agriculture Animal Care and Use Committee (University of São Paulo, Piracicaba, SP, Brazil; protocol number CEUA 2018-28) and were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Agricultural Animals in Agricultural Research and Teaching [29].

Sample collection

A total of 72 Large White pigs homozygous negative for the halothane gene (NN) were used in this study. The animals had an average final body weight of 133.9 ± 9.4 kg at 155 days of age. Pigs were slaughtered at a commercial processing facility following standard industry procedures and in accordance with the Brazilian animal welfare and slaughter regulations, including the Regulation of Industrial and Sanitary Inspection of Products of Animal Origin (RIISPOA; Decree No. 9,013/2017, https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2017/decreto/d9013.htm). All experimental animals were rendered unconscious by electrical stunning and subsequently exsanguinated. After slaughter, Longissimus lumborum (LL) muscle samples were collected, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction. The population, experimental design, and sample preparation are described in detail in Almeida et al. [30].

RNA-Seq and phenotypic data

Total RNA was extracted from the LL muscle samples, using the RNeasy® Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The quantity, purity, and integrity of the total RNA were evaluated using both the Nanodrop 1000 and Bioanalyzer platforms, with each sample achieving an RNA Integrity Number (RIN) of 7 or higher. For library preparation, 2 µg of total RNA from each sample was utilized, following the protocol specified in the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation kit v2 guide (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to determine the average size of the libraries. KAPA Library Quantification kit (KAPA Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) was used for quantification via quantitative PCR.

Pooled libraries were sequenced across five lanes of a sequencing flow cell using the TruSeq PE Cluster kit v4-cBot-HS (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). TruSeq SBS Kit v4-HS (200 cycles) was used for clustering and sequencing on the HiSeq2500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. All the sequencing analyses were performed at the ESALQ Genomics Center located in the Animal Biotechnology Laboratory at ESALQ–USP (Piracicaba, SP, Brazil).

Data quality assessment was first conducted using the FastQC software (version 0.11.8) [www.bioinformatics.bbsrc.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/] [31]. The Trim Galore package (version 0.6.5) [www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/trim_galore] was then used to remove adapters and low-quality bases. Reads with a minimum length of 70 bases and a Phred score exceeding 33 were retained. The assembly provided in Ensembl Release 102 [www.ensembl.org/Sus_scrofa/Info/Index] was used to align and map the reads to the pig reference genome (Sus scrofa 11.1). Alignment and mapping were carried out using the Bowtie2 version 2.4.3 package [32].

Expression values were calculated using the software RNA-Seq by Expectation Maximization (RSEM) version 1.3.1 [33]. We analyzed the composition of various FA, including palmitoleic, oleic, linoleic, alpha-linolenic, MUFA, PUFA, omega-3 (n-3), and omega-6 (n-6), all expressed as percentages (%) of total identified FAs. Total lipids were extracted from 100 g of LL samples using the cold extraction method previously described for lipid extraction [34], followed by methylation according to the American Oil Chemists’ Society (AOCS) protocol (Method AM 5–04) [35].

The total proportion of MUFA was calculated as the sum of palmitoleic acid (C16:1), oleic acid (C18:1 n-9), and eicosenoic acid (C20:1 n-9). Similarly, total PUFA was determined by summing linoleic acid (C18:2 n-6), alpha-linolenic acid (C18:3 n-3), eicosapentaenoic acid (C20:5 n-3), and docosahexaenoic acid (C22:6 n-3). The total n-6 PUFA was derived from linoleic acid (C18:2 n-6), while the total n-3 PUFA included alpha-linolenic acid (C18:3 n-3), eicosapentaenoic acid (C20:5 n-3), and docosahexaenoic acid (C22:6 n-3). Further details on the analytical procedures are provided in Almeida et al. [30].

Identification of eQTL hotspot regions

The hotspots on SSC3 (102,983,783 bp), SSC2 (67,926,641 bp), and SSC6 (93,742,864 bp) were identified by Durval et al. [24]. SNPs related to the expression levels of 277 or more genes, defined as those affecting more than the average number of genes influenced by all eQTL plus three times the standard deviations, were considered eQTL hotspots. These hotspots were primarily selected based on the number of genes they regulated. For example, the hotspot located on SSC3 (102,983,783 bp) regulates 4,018 genes, SSC2 (67,926,641 bp) regulates 1,044 genes, and SSC6 (93,742,864 bp) regulates 633 genes.

Additionally, they were chosen because each contained at least 15 animals with the AA allele and 15 animals with the AB allele and included previously annotated TF. Another important criterion was the presence of genes related to lipid metabolism, such as Acetyl-CoA Carboxylase Alpha (ACACA), Fatty Acid Binding Protein 4 (FABP4), and Lipase A, Lysosomal Acid Type (LIPA) [36–38]. Fifteen is the minimum number of samples recommended for use in WGCNA analyses [39, 40].

Weighted Correlation Network Analysis (WGCNA)

Our study aimed to identify differences in gene co-expression patterns between the two most frequent genotypes in eQTL hotspots: AA (homozygous reference) and AB (heterozygous), using gene co-expression analysis with WGCNA. Gene co-expression networks were defined independently for each genotype using WGCNA. For each genotype, the gene expression data were examined to identify modules of genes with high co-expression, enabling the assessment of genotype-specific variations in network structure. Following the centering and scaling of phenotypic data [33, 41], a linear model was fitted in R version 4.4.2 [42], before performing WGCNA. The fitted model can be described as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Y}_{ijk}=\mu+\text D_{i}+ B_{j}+\varepsilon_{ijk}$$\end{document}

Where yijk represents the phenotypic records, 𝜇 is the general mean of the phenotypes, di and bj are the fixed effects of diet i and block j, respectively, and 𝜀ijk is the residual effects. The experimental design involved a block defined by the weight of the pigs at the end of the nursery phase and the beginning of the growth phase. For 98 days, during the growth and finishing phases, the pigs were fed a six-phase diet. These diets consisted of corn-soybean meal formulations supplemented with different types of oils [30].

Gene co-expression networks were constructed with the WGCNA R package version 1.74 [26] based on transcript per million (TPM) values. Genes with zero counts across all samples or expressed in less than 50% of samples were excluded. The data were clustered to identify the outliers. We evaluated the similarities between the gene expression profiles within each genotype by constructing a similarity matrix. The n × n similarity matrix S = [Sij] was initially constructed based on pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between gene expression profiles for each sample pair [43]. Next, the adjacency function aij = power (Sij, β) =| Sij |β was computed, where β is the soft-thresholding power [43]. The value of β was determined based on the scale-free network property, which is characterized by the connectivity distribution p(k)∼k-γ [43]. The soft-thresholding power (β) was set to 20 and applied consistently across all networks to ensure comparability between genotypes. The similarity matrix was then transformed to the n × n adjacency matrix A = [aij], applying a β exponent to maintain the scale-free topology [43]. The scale-free topology index (R²) was then assessed and found to be greater than 0.8, confirming the presence of a scale-free network structure [41, 44, 45]. Mean connectivity was also evaluated to ensure appropriate network sparsity, and the corresponding values are provided in Additional file 1.

We opted for signed networks, which retain information on the sign of co-expression as a metric, as they are more effective in capturing gene expression trends, such as upregulation and downregulation, and in classifying co-expressed gene modules [26]. To define modules based on dissimilarity in the topological overlap matrix (TOM), we used (1 - TOM) [26]. To achieve this, we converted the adjacency matrix into a TOM, a method used to quantitatively assess the similarity between nodes by comparing the weighted correlations of two nodes with all other nodes in the network [26, 46]. Gene modules were identified and subsequently merged based on module eigengene (ME) dissimilarity, using a threshold (MEDissThres) below 0.25, corresponding to a correlation of 0.75 [47], while ensuring a minimum module size of 30 genes [26, 47]. Genes not assigned to any clusters were clustered in the “grey” module, as previously done in other studies [44]. Module-trait associations were evaluated by correlating the ME, which represents the first principal component of gene expression within a module, with each FA profile parameter, allowing the identification of modules associated with specific traits [48]. Gene significance (GS), defined as the correlation between gene expression and the trait, and module membership (MM), defined as the correlation between gene expression values and the ME [49, 50], were used to validate module- trait correlation [45, 48, 51]. A strong correlation between GS and MM indicates that genes are central elements of the module and are strongly associated with the trait [52]. Modules with a p-value < 0.05 were used for functional enrichment analysis and identification of hub genes, defined as those with the highest connectivity within the module [49].

Functional enrichment analyses

Functional enrichment analyses were conducted for the genes in the constructed modules that were associated with at least one FA trait with a p-value < 0.05, using the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) version 6.8 [53]. DAVID enrichment analyses were conducted for Biological Processes (BP), Cellular Components (CC), Molecular Functions (MF), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Gene Ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathways with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) below 5% were considered significantly enriched. To minimize redundancy among the enriched GO terms and enhance the clarity of the functional categories, we utilized the web-based tool REVIGO (https://revigo.irb.hr/) with the default settings.

Detection of hub genes involved in FA deposition in IMF

The STRING app plugin (Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins), with the STRINGFy function, was used to explore and visualize functional protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks using default parameters. The genes that showed the most significant correlation with a WGCNA edge weight were visualized using Cytoscape version 3.9.1 [54], considering the edges exported by the WGCNA “exportNetworkToCytoscape” function. We obtained annotation information by utilizing the STRINGIFY network function within the STRING app version 2.2.0. The top one gene hub was selected based on the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm [55], which was accessed through CytoHubba version 0.1 [45, 55].

Results

Co-expression networks

We used WGCNA to further investigate three eQTL hotspot regions identified in a previous study [24], which contain TF, to assess their effects on gene expression and phenotypic variability in two genotypes (AA and AB) that are more frequent in the study population. There were not enough BB animals to include them in the analyses [23]. The WGCNA results enabled us to understand the relationship of co-expressed genes allocated to different co-expression modules for the AA and AB genotypes, correlating them with the FA composition of IMF of LL. Generally, a correlation value greater than 0.80 is considered strong, while a value less than 0.5 is considered weak [56].

In the eQTL hotspot SSC3 (102,983,783 bp), we observed five modules for the AA genotype (n = 43 animals with the genotype AA) and one module for the AB genotype (n = 22 animals with the genotype AB) (Figures S2A and S2B; Additional file 2). In the eQTL hotspot SSC2 (67,926,641 bp), we identified four modules for the AA genotype (n = 49 animals with the genotype AA) and five modules for the AB genotype (n = 18 animals with the genotype AB) (Figures S3A and S3B; Additional file 3). Finally, in the eQTL hotspot SSC6 (93,742,864 bp), we found 15 modules for the AA genotype (n = 36 animals with the genotype AA) and no modules for the AB genotype (n = 28 animals with the genotype AB) (Figure S4; Additional file 4).

Co-expressed gene modules correlated with hotspot SSC3 (102,983,783 bp)

The AA modules labeled “darkgreen”, “red”, and “darkred” showed negative correlations with the palmitoleic acid trait, with correlation coefficients of r = -0.37, r. = -0.31, and r = -0.40, and p-values of 0.013, 0.044, and 0.0084, respectively. Meanwhile, “darkorange2” and “white” displayed negative and positive correlations with total SFA, with correlation coefficients of r = -0.37 and r = + 0.37, respectively, both having p-values of 0.014, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. For the AB module, only “sienna3” exhibited a significant positive correlation with the palmitoleic acid trait, with a correlation coefficient of r = + 0.46 and a p-value of 0.031, as shown in Fig. 1B.

For better understanding, we highlighted the KEGG pathways related to lipids, FA, and immunity. Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first WGCNA analysis conducted on different genotypes in pigs for complex phenotypes, such as FA composition in intramuscular fat. The WGCNA study in pigs, using the composition of different FA as a trait and the co-expressed genes, shows significant differences between the two most frequent genotypes in the population analyzed, reflecting the influence of genotype on gene co-expression.

There was notable variation in the number and composition of modules identified for the AA and AB genotypes, revealing distinct patterns of modules and their relationships with phenotypic traits. These findings suggest that genotypes can modulate the structure of co-expression networks, thereby influencing gene interactions and functional organization. In addition, differences in gene connectivity, functional enrichment, and hub gene profiles further highlight the impact of genotype on the regulatory architecture of these networks.

Previous studies have demonstrated that genetic variation contributes to shaping gene expression patterns across tissues. For example, Farhangi et al. [58] identified thousands of eQTL regions and regulatory hotspots across multiple porcine tissues, showing that gene expression is strongly influenced by genotype and enriched for metabolic and immune-related biological processes. Similarly, single-cell eQTL studies have shown that genetic variants can have cell-type-specific regulatory effects on gene expression, leading to genotype-dependent differences in transcriptional activity associated with metabolic pathways [59]. In addition, several studies in pigs have reported associations between gene expression and FA composition, highlighting the importance of transcriptional regulation in lipid metabolism [24, 60, 61]. Co-expression network approaches have also been applied to identify gene modules and regulatory pathways associated with FA traits [45, 62]. However, these studies have generally focused on expression–phenotype relationships and have not explored how different genotypes influence gene co-expression patterns.

Consistent with these observations, our study revealed genotype-dependent differences in gene expression patterns. In contrast to previous studies, our approach integrates co-expression network analyses with phenotypic data related to FA composition while explicitly considering genotype-specific effects. This enabled us to demonstrate that genotype not only influences individual gene expression but also reshapes gene network architecture and functional interactions. Therefore, our results provide a more comprehensive, systems-level understanding of the relationship between genotype, gene regulation, and complex metabolic traits.

As observed in the study by Cesar et al. [23], conducted in cattle, which compared AA and AB genotypes by performing co-expression analysis using the expression profiles in eQTL hotspot regions, our study also identified differences in gene co-expression patterns and functional enrichment across genotypes. These differences may be driven by regulatory variation or mutations in TFs located within these hotspots [23]. The findings reported by Cesar et al. [23] support the hypothesis that the co-activation of gene expression influences the pathways affecting the traits under investigation, explaining the difference in module content and functional enrichment between the different genotypes. These results highlight the complexity of gene regulation and emphasize the involvement of multiple genes and pathways in FA deposition.

Hotspot SSC3 (102,983,783 bp)

In the “darkred” module for AA genotype, we found TBC1 domain family member 1 (TBC1D1), a gene known for its high expression in muscle tissue [63–65]. TBC1D1 regulates glucose uptake in skeletal muscle by controlling Glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) translocation [65, 66], a process essential for insulin- and contraction-stimulated glucose utilization [67]. Given the central role of insulin in regulating both glucose and lipid metabolism, including inhibition of lipolysis, stimulation of lipogenesis, and modulation of FA oxidation [68], TBC1D1 may be associated with FA metabolism through insulin-mediated pathways. This suggests that genes within this module may be linked to variation in FA composition by influencing glucose uptake and downstream metabolic processes. Mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 3 (MAP2K3) was identified as the hub gene of this module. MAP2K3 is associated with IMF and is involved in adipogenesis [69], as well as insulin-related signaling pathways that regulate energy metabolism. Previous studies have indicated a connection between the MAP2K3 gene and human body mass index [70], fat-related traits in pigs [71]. Together, these findings suggest that MAP2K3 and TBC1D1 may contribute to FA composition by integrating glucose metabolism and lipid storage pathways in pig muscle tissue.

The “darkred” module showed enriched pathways related to diabetes and lipid metabolism, including genes such as MAPK8 and GYS1 [72–74]. MAPK8 has been reported to be associated with hyperglycemia, insulin resistance [72, 74], lipid metabolism, and diabetes [72, 75], and has also been linked to increased tenderness and altered fat distribution in pigs [74]. In humans, MAPK8 influences lipolysis in adipose tissue [76]. Importantly, MAPK8 has been shown to regulate FA metabolism by controlling lipolysis, the release of non-esterified FA, and their re-esterification [77]. In addition, FA released during lipolysis can directly activate MAPK8, suggesting that this kinase acts as a metabolic sensor linking intracellular FA levels to gene expression and metabolic regulation [78]. In this context, the negative correlation observed between the “darkred” module and palmitoleic acid indicates that genes within this module, including MAPK8, are associated with biological processes related to insulin signaling and lipid metabolism that may be relevant to variation in FA composition. These findings from studies in humans and pigs support the link between MAPK8 and lipid metabolism and reinforce the potential role of FA in modulating these pathways. GYS1 is involved in glycogen synthesis and non-oxidative glucose metabolism and has been associated with insulin resistance [73, 79, 80]. Its presence in this module further supports the role of glucose metabolism in regulating FA-related traits.

Elongator acetyltransferase complex subunit 2 (ELP2) was identified as the hub gene of the “red” module and has been associated with anti-inflammatory responses in pigs [81]. Fatty acid may promote an anti-inflammatory profile by influencing the production of key mediators and regulators of inflammation and immune responses [82]. The “darkred” and “red” modules were negatively correlated with palmitoleic FA. Palmitoleic acid, described as a lipokine, participates in the regulation of several metabolic processes, including improving insulin responsiveness in muscle tissue, stimulating β-cell proliferation, alleviating endoplasmic reticulum stress, and decreasing lipogenic activity in white adipocytes [83]. Also, palmitoleic acid has been characterized as an anti-inflammatory FA [84]. In contrast, palmitic acid has been shown to promote inflammatory response in macrophages by activating various members of the Toll-like receptor (TLR) family, particularly TLR4 [85]. In this context, the negative correlation between the “darkred” and “red” modules and palmitoleic acid suggests an inverse relationship between palmitoleic acid levels and the co-expression of genes within these modules.

In the “sienna3” module for the AB genotype, UPP1 was identified as the hub gene. In a study involving humans, UPP1 was significantly associated with FA biosynthesis and coenzyme A metabolism [86], as well as immune and inflammatory processes [87, 88]. Given the central role of coenzyme A in FA metabolism, these findings suggest that UPP1 may contribute to metabolic processes related to lipid metabolism. In addition, its involvement in immune-related pathways indicates a potential link between metabolic and inflammatory processes within this module. In this context, the identification of UPP1 as a hub gene in the AB genotype suggests that gene co-expression patterns in this module may be associated with biological processes integrating lipid metabolism and immune responses, which may be relevant to the FA composition traits evaluated in this study.

Several chemokine genes, such as C-C motif chemokine ligand 22 (CCL22), C-C motif chemokine ligand 17 (CCL17), C-C motif chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7), C-C motif chemokine ligand 3 like 1 (CCL3L1), and C-C motif chemokine ligand 8 (CCL8), have also been identified. Chemokines are proteins that belong to the cytokine family. They participate in the chemoattraction of immune cells and are involved in a wide range of functions, according to the type of cell [89]. Several immune-related pathways were also found in this module “sienna3”, which contains chemokine genes such as CCL8, CCR7, and CCL3L1.

Understanding swine chemokines and cytokines is crucial as it enables us to comprehend porcine immunity, which is fundamental for ensuring a safe and reliable food supply. This knowledge is pivotal for preventing and treating infectious diseases, developing effective vaccines and treatments, and conducting biomedical research [90]. FA has a fundamental influence on the regulation of inflammation, impacting both acute and chronic inflammatory processes [91]. Furthermore, this module was positively correlated with palmitoleic FA, which has been studied for its potential anti-inflammatory effects [83]. This positive correlation suggests that palmitoleic acid levels are linked to the expression of genes within this module, such as CCL8, CCR7, and CCL3L1. This correlation implies that palmitoleic acid may be involved in modulating immune responses by promoting the expression of immune-related genes in the “sienna3” module.

For hotspot SSC3 (102,983,783 bp), the AA genotype was observed to be associated with a greater number of genes involved in diabetes and lipid metabolism. In contrast, the AB genotype was linked more noticeably to immune response. These findings suggest that while the AA genotype is more involved in pathways related to metabolic disorders, the AB genotype displays a stronger connection to immune system-related processes, highlighting the distinct functional roles of these genotypes at the hotspot SSC3 (102,983,783 bp).

Hotspot SSC2 (67,926,641 bp)

In the “darkorange” module for the AA genotype, we identified fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase (FBP1) as a hub gene. FBP1 is essential for regulating gluconeogenesis, and a deficiency in this enzyme can lead to hypoglycemia [92, 93]. Furthermore, FBP1 is an important regulator of insulin signaling, preventing excessive insulin activity and ensuring a proper balance between glucose and lipid metabolism [93–95]. The “darkorange” module was correlated with palmitoleic acid, a FA known for its beneficial effects on hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and insulin sensitivity [96]. This association suggests that genes within this module, including FBP1, are involved in pathways related to glucose metabolism and insulin regulation. These findings may reflect the interaction between glucose metabolism and lipid regulation, where insulin-mediated pathways influence FA composition in muscle tissue. However, more detailed gene expression studies are needed to understand the functions of these genes within the co-expression network and potential regulatory roles.

The “cyan” module for the AA genotype did not show significant KEGG pathways related to lipid metabolism or immunity, suggesting that genes within this module may not be directly associated with these biological functions. However, Trk-fused gene (TFG), identified within this module, is known to be involved in glucose homeostasis and insulin secretion [97]. Alterations in TFG function may affect insulin secretion, contributing to the development and progression of diabetes. Given that FA are closely associated with insulin resistance and metabolic disorders [98], and that omega-3 FA improve insulin sensitivity and metabolic health [99–101], genes within this module, including TFG, may be involved in biological processes linking glucose metabolism and FA-related traits.

In the “darkorange” module for the AB genotype, we identified the gene TBX21. This gene is involved in adaptive immunity by controlling the Th1/Th2 balance and regulating cytokine production [102–104]. Fatty acids are known to influence immune cell function, including T lymphocyte differentiation and inflammatory responses [105, 106]. This module “darkorange” was negatively correlated with alpha-linolenic acid, an omega-3 FA associated with immune modulation and antibody production [107, 108]. This negative correlation suggests an inverse relationship between alpha-linolenic acid levels and the expression of genes within this module. It may indicate that alpha-linolenic acid is associated with immune-related processes, potentially influencing the activity of genes in the “darkorange” module. According to the results obtained for hotspot SSC2 (67,926,641 bp), the AA genotype was noted to be related to insulin secretion and diabetes, highlighting the potential influence of palmitoleic acid and omega-3 FA on metabolic health. In contrast, the AB genotype demonstrated a relationship with immunity processes. These findings suggest that the AA genotype may influence metabolic disease, whereas the AB genotype is more strongly linked to immune functions.

Hotspot SSC6 (93,742,864 bp)

In the “plum1” module for the AA genotype, we identified the hub gene ankyrin repeat domain 2 (ANKRD2). Silencing ANKRD2 alters the expression of genes involved in signaling pathways such as TGFβ [109]. In skeletal muscle, TGFβ is associated with suppression of cell differentiation, modulation of inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodeling [110, 111]. In addition, TGFβ has been linked to decreased expression of mitochondrial regulators and components of β-oxidation, along with adverse effects on insulin response [110]. The module “plum1” was negatively correlated with alpha-linolenic acid. The negative correlation between the “plum1” module and alpha-linolenic acid suggests an inverse relationship between alpha-linolenic acid levels and the co-expression of genes within this module. Linolenic acid can be converted into eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) [112]. EPA and DHA can improve insulin sensitivity by remodeling the phospholipid composition of mitochondrial membranes [113], reducing the accumulation of reactive lipid intermediates in muscles [114], and enhancing the transcription of genes related to mitochondrial biogenesis and FA oxidation [115, 116]. Therefore, the observed correlation suggests that genes within the “plum1” module may be associated with pathways involved in mitochondrial function, FA oxidation, and insulin signaling, all of which are relevant to FA-related traits.

In the “orange” module, we identified the hub gene cluster of differentiation 14 (CD14). CD14 is expressed in immune cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells [117]. A study in mice showed that CD14 influences the inflammatory response in adipose tissue and contributes to insulin resistance [118]. The “orange” module was correlated with total SFA, which are known to activate inflammatory pathways and contribute to metabolic disorders [9, 119]. This suggests that genes within this module may be associated with inflammatory processes and immune signaling pathways influenced by FA profiles.

In the “mediumpurple3” module, we identified the gene neuropeptide Y receptor Y5 (NPY5R). NPY5R may be involved in regulating energy balance, making it a potential candidate gene for susceptibility to obesity and related conditions like insulin resistance syndrome and type 2 diabetes mellitus [120]. These modules, “mediumpurple3” and “orange”, were correlated with total SFA. SFA have been associated with impaired insulin signaling and increased inflammation, which are key mechanisms linking lipid metabolism to metabolic dysfunction [121, 122]. These findings suggest that genes within these modules may be associated with pathways integrating energy balance, inflammatory signaling, and FA metabolism.

Skeletal muscle functions as an endocrine organ, releasing myokines that enable communication with other organs, such as adipose tissue, the liver, and the pancreas, providing insights into how muscles interact with the rest of the body [123, 124]. In conditions such as obesity, lipids accumulate in adipose tissue and can be deposited ectopically in skeletal muscle [125, 126], affecting insulin sensitivity and metabolic regulation. Lipids in skeletal muscle are strongly associated with insulin sensitivity; however, excessive lipid accumulation can impair muscle maintenance and regeneration in obesity. eQTL hotspots have been observed in multiple genetic studies [22–128], suggesting that when multiple gene expressions map to the same locus, it may indicate the presence of a genetic regulator. In our study, we utilized eQTL hotspots to conduct a co-expression network analysis of three genomic regions across different genotypes. This approach enabled the identification of distinct network structures, functional enrichment patterns, and hub genes associated with FA composition in the LL muscle of pigs. These findings provide a biological framework for understanding how genetic variation can influence gene regulation and metabolic processes, which are further explored through the comparative analysis across hotspots presented in the following section.

Comparative analyses across eQTL hotspots

Overall, a consistent pattern was observed across the three hotspots, where the AA genotype was predominantly associated with metabolic-related pathways, while the AB genotype showed a stronger association with immune-related processes. Taken together, the results from the three hotspots (SSC3, SSC2, and SSC6) revealed both distinct and genotype-dependent regulatory mechanisms underlying FA composition. In SSC3, the AA genotype was predominantly associated with metabolic and insulin-related pathways, whereas the AB genotype showed a stronger enrichment for immune-related processes. A similar pattern was observed in SSC2, where the AA genotype was linked to metabolic regulation, while the AB genotype was more strongly associated with immune pathways. In contrast, SSC6 exhibited a broader metabolic profile for the AA genotype, while no significant co-expression modules were identified for the AB genotype, highlighting a marked genotype-specific difference in network organization. These results indicate that the regulatory effects of these hotspots are not only region-specific but also strongly influenced by genotype, leading to distinct biological outcomes. Moreover, the recurrent involvement of metabolic and immune pathways across hotspots suggests a potential interaction between these processes in modulating FA composition. Overall, this integrative analysis demonstrates that the combined effects of multiple eQTL hotspots and genotypes contribute to the complexity of gene regulation, reinforcing the importance of considering both genomic regions and genetic backgrounds when studying complex traits.

Functional analysis of genotypes AA and AB

GO enrichment analyses using REVIGO provided insight into the functional roles of AA and AB genotypes and allowed us to visualize how these genotypes may be involved in important mechanisms related to FA deposition. Several GO terms, including “immune response”, “lymphocyte chemotaxis”, “inflammatory response”, and “chemokine-mediated signaling pathway” were enriched in both genotypes. Fatty acids can influence the immune system, as they are structural components of membrane phospholipids and influence membrane properties and immune cell function [129, 130]. Therefore, changes in FA composition can directly affect immune responses.

For the genotype AA, we identified significant superclusters, including “TCA cycle”, “immune response”, and “adipose tissue development”. In the supercluster “TCA cycle”, we identified the GO term “glycolytic process”. Metabolomic research has indicated that elements of the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathways, and intermediates from the TCA cycle are associated with insulin resistance, prediabetes, and diabetes [131–133]. Metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and β-oxidation oxidize glucose and FA, which are the main substrates for muscular energy production, generating nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADH) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2) [134]. These findings suggest that the AA genotype may have a more prominent role in metabolic processes and immune regulation, as it is associated with the expression of genes correlated with palmitoleic acid, total SFAs, alpha-linolenic acid, and oleic acid. To deepen this understanding, further gene expression studies are needed to clarify how genes behave within these co-expression networks and to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms involved in the observed biological responses.

The pathways associated with this genotype are important because of the complex interaction reported when we identify “adipose tissue development”. Muscles contribute to glucose homeostasis throughout the body and, together with adipose tissue, play important structural and functional roles [135]. The identification of superclusters linked to the TCA cycle and carbohydrate processes indicates that genotype AA may significantly contribute to metabolic health. Together, these findings may indicate that the AA genotype is associated with coordinated metabolic and immune regulatory mechanisms. In contrast, the AB genotype was mainly associated with immune-related processes, including “regulation of T cell activation” and “neutrophil aggregation”. Lipid metabolism is essential for the regulation of key signaling molecules in T cell activation, and metabolic pathways such as glycolysis, FA oxidation, and FA biosynthesis play significant roles during T cell differentiation [136–138]. T-cells help maintain immune system homeostasis and are essential for the repair and regeneration of skeletal muscle [139]. This underscores the importance of FA metabolism in T cell development, indicating that alterations in lipid metabolism may affect immune function. This suggests a strong focus on immune-related functions, indicating that individuals with the genotype AB may exhibit enhanced immune responses.

The results demonstrate how different genotypes can influence gene expression and biological processes associated with different FA compositions, highlighting the complex interaction between genotypes, FAs, and molecular pathways in pigs. Our findings show that different genotypes induce specific changes in gene expression profiles in the LL muscle of pigs, thus modifying the complex co-expression network. Importantly, this study provides novel insights into genotype-dependent regulation of gene co-expression associated with FA composition, addressing a gap in current literature. By integrating genotype-specific analyses with co-expression network modeling and phenotypic data, this study offers a more comprehensive framework for understanding the molecular regulation of complex metabolic traits. By highlighting those different genotypes in pigs, as an animal model, can influence multiple aspects of health, including immune and metabolic processes, our findings support the need for further studies to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and to explore their broader implications in livestock biology and disease research.

Conclusions

By constructing modules of co-expressed genes across different genotypes, this study demonstrated that genotypic variation can be modulated by gene co-expression. We identified modules and hub genes, such as MAPK8, TFG, CCL17, TBX21, ELP2, and GYS1, involved in gene networks related to immunity, lipid metabolism, and fat deposition. These results enhance our understanding of how genetic variations in specific genomic regions impact the co-expression of genes related to metabolic regulation and help identify candidate biomarkers that could be used as important targets in animal breeding programs.
