“This is my place of reference”: transition from adolescent to adult care in a qualitative study of patients with anorexia nervosa
Authors: Caroline Rezakhany, Mathilde Lambert, Corinne Blanchet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnorexia nervosa (AN) is a polyfactorial, long-term condition with an important risk of chronicity, that mainly emerges in adolescence. The transition to adulthood care is a delicate step at high risk of reaggravation and drop-out of treatment. This qualitative study aimed to explore the subjective experiences of adolescents and young adults with AN confronted with the transition process.MethodsSemi-structured video conference interviews were conducted with AN patients recruited from a specialized multidisciplinary department for adolescents. Interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) was applied to analyze the data.ResultsThirteen female patients were interviewed with an average age of 22 years and a standard deviation of 4.2 years Four themes emerged from the IPA: (I) Intense emotional experience, (II) Transition as a reflection of symptom severity, (III) Adapted and personalized transition, and (IV) the need of preparation of the transition.ConclusionsThe transition process from adolescent to adult care appeared challenging for most AN patients. Our study identified several internal barriers to transition, some of which are common to chronic diseases, whereas others are more specific to anorexia nervosa, such as individual psychic structure, psychopathology or associated psychiatric comorbidities. Hence, it seems essential to build personalized transition programs that consider individual developmental trajectories, the specificities of AN psychiatric comorbidities and the unknown long-term prognosis of this complex condition.Keywords: Anorexia nervosa, Transition to adult care, Adolescence, Qualitative methods, Continuity of patient care

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is long-term condition with an important risk of chronicity that most often occurs during adolescence, with an average duration of two to five years [1, 2]. A definition of chronic AN has been developed, Severe and Enduring Anorexia Nervosa (SE-AN), defined as AN which persists for more than 3–7 years after appropriate treatments [3]. The lifetime prevalence in adolescents and young adults is 0.3 to 0.9% in women and 0.3% in men [4–8]. The outcome is non-predictable, with a relatively poor prognosis and a high risk of chronicity [2, 9]. One-third of patients have an intermediate evolution, with elevated mortality rates [9]. However, the progression of this disease is characterized by a 50–60% symptomatic recovery rate (with normalization of weight and eating habits). In addition, there is an intermediate progression in 20–30% of cases, with relapses but a general decrease in symptoms. Finally, there is a risk of chronicity in 10% of cases. Even if the AN has a very good potential of recovery, a not insignificant part will become chronical and will need transition. Given the high risk of disruption and relapse during the transition period, ensuring strong links between medical disciplines and continuity of care from pediatric to adult services is crucial.

Transition was defined by Blum in 1993 as “a planned movement of adolescents and young adults with chronic physical and medical conditions from child-centered to adult-oriented health-care systems” [10]. The TRACK study (Transitions of Care from Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services to Adult Mental Health Services) was conducted in England on the transition from child to adult psychiatric services [11]. This study shows that only 5% of young people reaching the threshold age for transition to adult services would experience an effective and optimal transition. In addition, this study estimates that 50% of young people are not transferred to the adult psychiatry unit. The MILESTONE project (Managing the Link and Strengthening Transition from Child to Adult Mental Health Care) is a study funded by the European Union and running from 2014 to 2019 [12]. It shows the modalities of transition in psychiatric care services in different participating European countries and aims to improve this transition across Europe. The results confirmed a lack of standardization in the transition process. This process of transition from pediatric to adult medical teams is a very delicate step with a very high risk of breaking in the continuity of care, which is why this period is a major public health challenge [13]. This challenge has been a main focus in the French and international medical literature since the 1980s and has increasingly since the 2000s [14]. In AN, the transition process between adolescent and adult care is also a critical moment with a high risk of instability or relapse [15]. Approximately ten years ago, the authors noted the necessity for specific and progressive support based on dedicated programs to sustain the transition process [16]. Indeed, continuity of care during this period seems to be essential and predicts a lower rate of readmission to the hospital [17]. Regarding the transition in AN, the medical literature has focused on the needs and difficulties experienced by specialized medical teams [18], young adults who have undergone the transition [19], or, more recently, families [20]. UK guidelines have been published in 2017 to support AN patient in their transition to prevent disruption of care and relapse [21]. These guidelines are based on 12 principles designed to facilitate this period of instability and changes. Among them we can find an early information on the transition at least 6 months before the planned transition. They insist on the involvement of family and carers to facilitate the process. A flexible timing of transition that consider the needs of the patient and should not be determined simply by age. They also highlight the importance of good working relationships between services and an understanding of each other’s way of working are best developed well before transitions occur. An updated review on transitions in young people with eating disorders has been done, aiming to identify optimal strategies for existing binary care models [22]. They propose an actionable, evidence-based framework (TRIPS) that comprises five transition strategies, designed to provide necessary guidance and support for all stakeholders affected by this service change—patients, caregivers, and providers: Timely talks, Readiness, Inclusion, Preparation, and Synergy. To expand current knowledge, a qualitative study explored the experience of transitioning back to the community from intensive care specialized eating disorder units across the UK, and the difficulties, opportunities and needs that arise during this period for people with anorexia nervosa and their carers [23]. All patients and carers described this period as challenging. When consulted on areas of improvement in transition support, patients and carers believed that greater focus on the whole person in treatment (rather than only targeting anorexia nervosa) was critical for long-term recovery. Another article suggests that both people affected by eating disorders and those supporting them commonly have an adverse experience of the transition process [24]. In particular, both communication between services and communication between the individual and the service is often lacking. In France, there is no structured transition program or recommendations regarding the transition period for patients with eating disorders. We thought it would be interesting to take an upstream position in the transition process by investigating the point of view of adolescents and young adults confronted with the necessity of changing medical services but who have not (yet) done so. We have conducted a qualitative study investigating the subjective experience of AN patients in a “Maison des Adolescents” (MDAs) to explore the obstacles and facilitating factors of the transition. MDAs offer the following essential services: a “Health and Prevention Space” open daily, multidisciplinary consultations, a mobile team visiting youth hospitalized, refresher and remedial courses for school work, network meetings and parent support groups [25]. We aimed to develop suggestions and perspectives on transition modalities for adolescent patients with AN.

Methods

Study design

This study was observational and exploratory and was based on an inductive, phenomenological, and qualitative approach [26]. A qualitative methodology was chosen since it helps to understand subjective perceptions and the significance and meaning of a phenomenon based on detailed narrative reports. It is particularly suited to the complexity of the study subject [27]. A phenomenological approach was thought to be the best adapted approach for studying participants’ experiences and representations. It enables the study of the relationships between several factors and the integration of the impact of social context and is thus particularly appropriate for the topic of this study. It also takes subjective and constructivist aspects into account, where knowledge is the fruit of a shared construction between the researcher and the participant. Written consent was obtained from the participants who were outpatients. The research protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Paris (No. 21-829).

Participants and sampling

The sampling was purposive sampling, i.e. we intentionally selected the participants who would supposedly be able to provide the most information possible about the phenomenon under study. The aim was to find atypical cases, within the homogeneous framework of the inclusion criteria. The subjects were recruited from patients who were receiving medical consultations in the “Maison des Adolescents - Maison de Solenn” (Cochin Hospital, Paris, France) meeting the inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria were (i) AN adolescents aged 18–20 years, for whom the transition process to adult care has been discussed and/or is in progress, and (ii) young adults aged 20–30 years who have not been able to perform the transition (unsuccessful attempts) and who are still followed up at the Maison de Solenn for AN. There were no exclusion criteria and the patients’ comorbidities were not exclusion criteria. Patients received an information document explaining the objectives and methodology of the study, and those who agreed were then contacted by email to find a date for an interview and signed a consent form. The interviews were done by the interviewer (CR), who was not their personal doctor. In qualitative research, the sample size is not calculated before the study begins. In fact, we stop the process of including new participants when we notice redundancy in the few last patients’ experiences [27].

Data collection

We conducted a semi-structured recorded interview with a minimum and maximum range of 40–60 min with each participant. The use of semi-structured interviews was justified by the phenomenological methodology, as these interviews enabled perceptions and subjective experiences to be explored in depth, while allowing new data to emerge thanks to the possible digressions of the interview guide according to the participants [28]. The aim is to co-construct meaning with the participants, starting from the individual story to build a clinical narrative, the semi-structured interview ensuring a certain degree of comparability [29]. Open questions were asked about their thoughts concerning the transition to the adult medical unit. An interview guide was designed specifically to explore this subjective experience, based on both the literature and clinical experience. The questions were not definitive, and the guide was adapted during the study. Patients were also asked to rate the severity of their disease at the time of the interview via a scale created by the study investigators (Fig. 1). One researcher (C.R) conducted all the interviews. The researcher kept in mind a list of topics that had to be covered, but the discussion nonetheless remained very open to be able to enter the subject’s internal world. The subjects were considered experts who were able to identify one or several topics that the researcher had not considered, depending on their concerns and interests. All the interviews, including the questions, were transcribed literally and in full by the researcher. During the interviews, verbatim transcripts formed. We continued inclusions until data saturation, that is, until the analysis of new data provided no new results [30], and the interviews were deleted after transcription. The first part of the analysis consisted of reading the interviews one by one and annotating the main ideas that emerged to familiarize with the information before creating the codes. Each interview was first coded in chronological order, revealing that data saturation had been reached as the interviews progressed. No further interviews were therefore envisaged. Initial codes were identified and next, gathered to identify subthemes. The codes and subthemes were then used to formulate themes and metathemes.

Analysis

The analysis applied the method of interpretative phenomenological analysis, which offers support for studying the meaning that individuals construct from their experiences [31]. Researchers read the interview transcripts several times. They then annotated the text of each interview with their initial comments. These comments were then grouped into themes that succinctly summarized the essential characteristics emerging from these accounts. The themes were not chosen solely on the basis of their prevalence in the data. Other factors, including the richness of the passages showing the themes and the ways they illuminated other aspects of the narrative, were also considered. At each stage, the researchers verified the consistency of these groupings in the transcripts. They then established connections to determine a set of superordinate themes that describe the entire set of narratives. Each superordinate theme was linked to its corresponding themes. This thematic set was then analyzed in a report that itemized all of the themes and was accompanied by their illustrations in the transcripts. This required continuous back-and-forth throughout the analysis between the analytic data and the source material. The researchers also sought to identify the similarities and differences between the narratives of the participants. The interviews were analyzed independently by different researchers to enhance the rigor of the results. The methodological criteria were retrospectively verified according to the COREQ (COnsolidated criteria for Reporting Qualitative research) checklist.

Results

The study participants included 13 girls with an average age of 22 years and a standard deviation of 4.2 years. The average age of AN onset was 13.5 years, and the mean duration of care in the adolescent unit was 7 years. All patients were hospitalised during their course of treatment, with hospital stays ranging from 4 months to 2 years. The disease scale was predominantly III or IV, with only one patient at II.

Four meta-themes emerged from the analysis (Table 2): Intense emotional experience, transition as a reflection of symptom severity, the need of an adapted and personalized transition, and the importance of preparation of the transition.

Meta-themes and themes

Intense emotional experience

The first superordinate theme concerns apprehension about leaving the adolescent care unit. It covers two themes: the trust-based relationship established with the adolescent medical team and the refusal to leave them, and the fear of feeling abandoned by them.

Trust-based relationship and difficulty in creating a new therapeutic alliance

The patients talked about the difficulty of leaving the pediatric care unit. They said that this is their reference place, where they have been since the onset of their disease. This highlights the trust-based relationship with the adolescent medical team; for example, P10 said, “I trust this team”. They also expressed the feeling of being listened to and understood by them, P2 mentioned: “The caregivers are very competent for most of them. Very attentive to our needs”. The patients talked about the fear of leaving this adolescent care unit and their refusal to do so. For example, P2 said, “This is my place of reference, and I would not leave it for anything”. In fact, all of them believe that their relationships with the team are trust-based and strong. They believe that creating this type of relationship is difficult and takes time and effort. They expressed a fear of not being able to feel that same way with another team. According to P1, “I was afraid, and I knew that I would not find someone who listened to me and understood me as well”. Binding new bonds is difficult for our participants. In fact, many of them confessed that they had difficulties creating new relationships. P3 told us, “I am not easy, I do not trust people, I cannot create new relationships easily”.

Feelings of being abandoned

This theme emphasized patients’ fear of being abandoned by the adolescent medical team. They said that they had for the first time the feeling of being alone with their disease. For example, P9 said, “I felt like I am on my own, alone”. And many patients reported that changing medical units make them feel that they have to begin their follow-up from the beginning. P1 told us that “Starting from the beginning is hard, re-explaining everything is very difficult”.

Transition as a reflection of symptom severity

The second superordinate theme concerning the negative representation of the pediatric-to-adult transition, as a reflection of symptom severity, is subdivided into two themes: transition as a fantasy of worsening, and the magical thinking about the temporary nature of symptoms.

Transition as a fantasy of worsening

Transition is often a real source of fear and anxiety for patients. For example, P8 said, “There was a lot of anxiety about it [the transition], I was very nervous.”. This anxiety is mostly caused by the negative view that they have of the adult unit, as an aggravation confirmed by this transfer to the adult unit. P2 argued, “I do not want it. I see it as something very negative”. This negative view can be partly explained by a “psychiatric vision” of this adult unit, necessarily more serious, as noted in P1: “Because AN is considered a psychological, psychiatric disorder, well… I’m classified in it, but I am not like them… I don’t feel like I belong there at all, they are crazy”. Some of them said that they did not initially understand the necessity of changing teams and structure. Some patients thought that the adolescent medical team wanted to orient them to the adult unit because they felt that they could no longer help them. That maybe their disease was too severe for the pediatric unit and there is no more hope for them there; as P3 told us: “I said to myself, oh maybe they think they can’t help me anymore, maybe there is no more hope for me”.

The magical thinking about the temporary nature of symptoms

The transition was seen as a reminder of the reality that anorexic symptoms are not temporary, and that continuity of care is needed. This contradicted a kind of magical thinking about the brevity of this period, which is limited to adolescence. Few patients said they thought that AN was temporary in their life, something that it would not exist in their adult life. P6 confessed, “When the follow-up is dedicated to adulthood, well adulthood is longer than adolescence, so that would mean a disease that lasts in the time and that truly scares me”. In addition, many patients said that they feared being with other patients who had more severe disease. P3 noted, “It is very difficult for me to see women who have been ill for years, that it’s truly scary”. Another interesting point is that some patients said that they feel ambivalent in recovering from their AN. P3 told us that “it is hard to see women who have severe forms of AN in the waiting room, but in my mind, I thought, would not I want to be like her? That’s weird isn’t?”.

The need of an adapted and personalized transition

The third superordinate theme is about the patient’s need to have an adapted and personalized transition. It covers three themes: the patient’s ability to have a voice in decision making, the importance of a progressive transition, and the fact that there is a discrepancy between developmental and chronological age.

Having a voice in decision making

In this theme, the participants underlined the importance of being participative in their own care, taking a real place in the discussions. They do not want to feel passive and just accept the decisions. P6 affirmed, “What is important for me is to be an actor in my care. A good transition is one that happens when the patient wants and decides to go to an adult service”. The worst scenario for our participants would be a forced transition imposed by doctors regardless of the patient’s needs. According to P7, “I think we should wait until the patient is ready to go to the adult care unit. [The worst thing would be] That things are imposed on me, that they do not listen to me at all”.

Progressive transition process

Our participants highlighted the importance of a transition that took time and that was prepared with the patients months prior. They said that they need to process this information and work on it with their therapist. As P5 admitted, “I know that [the transition] will not happen in a blink of an eye and that it will take time, which makes me feel more comfortable”. Our patients said that if the transition happened too soon, without preparation, it would be too brutal for them and would probably worsen their symptoms and disease. As P1 said, “I knew that if it was sudden [the transition], I would fall back into my symptoms”.

Discrepancy between developmental and chronological age

One interesting point that our participants reported was that many of them needed a pediatric approach. The participants said that they were adults but did not feel like adults did in their bodies and minds. They said that they need and want the medical team to abord them as children. As P1 confessed, “And I wasn’t an adult. In addition, I am still not an adult. I may be almost thirty, but I’m still not an adult. I was anticipating the fact that we do not talk to adults the way we approach children, and well, I need to be approached as a child I think”. The patients also talked about their fear of becoming real adults. As P8 said, “It is the whole problem of my life. Telling myself “I’m becoming an adult”, it is too hard, it involves many changes, I can’t accept it right now, it is not easy”.

Preparation of the transition material

The final superordinate theme addresses the preparation of the transition. It involves good communication with the patient on the one hand and between the adult and the pediatric unit on the other hand and takes into consideration the medical history of each patient.

Consideration of the individual medical history

Patients insisted on their need for follow-up in the adult medical unit, where they stopped it with the adolescent team. They said that it is essential to them that the new medical team considers what they have already experienced, lived, and practiced with their previous medical team. For example, P5 said, “I need that they [the new team] take into consideration the time of care I had before”. This way, they don’t feel that they have to start from the beginning. Thus, communication between the two services is essential.

Communication between professionals

This theme highlights the importance for participants of effective communication between medical teams and different actors. In this way, patients would not feel that they have to bear the “burden” of the transition alone. P3 mentioned, “It would make me feel better if the two teams communicated. That they [the adult unit] will be informed about my case”. Furthermore, patients said that, in addition to good communication between the two medical units, good communication with patients is also essential. As much as possible, it is better to prepare transfers in advance with the pediatric unit. In this way, the medical team can answer all the teenagers’ questions, prepare them for the adult unit and minimize the risk of a disruption of care. P6 said, “I want them to tell me what my objectives are, what they expect from me. First, I would like to visit the adult unit and have an interview with the professionals as well”.

Discussion

Our study explored the subjective experiences of thirteen adolescents and young adults in Maison des Adolescents, who were confronted with the necessity of transition to adult care [25]. To date, the qualitative studies available on transition in AN have focused initially on the subjective experience of professionals [32] and young adults who have gone through the transition period and, more recently, on the experience of families [18–20, 33]. Others recent studies focused on this subject and showed that transition is a period of vulnerability and fragility which has to be regulate and anticipate [21].

Our findings suggest an intense emotional experience, transition as a reflection of symptom severity, the need of an adapted and personalized transition, and the importance of preparation of the transition.

We propose three lines of discussion. The first two parts concern obstacles and resistance to the transition, and their underlying mechanisms, specifically related to anorexic cognitions and symptoms. Finally, we address the therapeutic challenges that patients and medical teams face together to develop specific and innovative strategies to support AN patients during the transition process.

Transition process: Obstacles and concerns described by AN patients

As mentioned earlier, the transition process is a real challenge in all chronic diseases with a high risk of disruption of care and relapses [34]. The AN patients described the transition as an obstacle-filled and painful pathway. They reported specific concerns related to their psychological functioning and their own vulnerabilities. As most of the patients mentioned, accepting changes and new experiences can be challenging for them, who are struggling with the prospect of change [35]. For this reason, the transition, which implies major changes and reorganization of therapeutic relationships, may seem impossible, and even violent [36]. Moreover, patients expressed the fear of feeling abandoned by the adolescent medical team and the fear of losing the special bond that they had built up with their referring physician. Another element reported by patients who may represent an obstacle to the transition process is their difficulty in accepting their symptoms and their disease. In AN, undernutrition and nutrient deficiencies can reinforce cognitive distortions, and denial of the reality of the disease contributes to resistance to care [37]. It is therefore understandable that the transition may seem difficult to consider for patients who, at the theoretical age of transition, have denial and lack knowledge of their disease. Moreover, addressing the issue of the transition process would suggest a potential risk of chronicity and a pejorative outcome into adulthood. However, some patients in our study reported the idea of ‘magical thinking’, that their AN is just a small interlude in their adolescence and that they will become healthier when they are adults. Most of them reported themselves as in categories III or IV of the self-reporting scale described in Fig. 1, which correspond to mild or close to recovery perceived disease stage. Talking about transition makes them face the truth that not only are they not yet healed, since they must go to the adult medical unit but also that their disease might be more critical than expected. This painful confrontation with reality and the acceptance that AN will continue throughout adulthood changes patients’ and their families’ representations, can weaken their psychological homeostasis, and can be a barrier to the transition [38]. Furthermore, one of the major concerns of our patients, representing a powerful obstacle to the transition, was the fear of being confronted with more severe cases of the disease in the adult medical unit. Indeed, as in other chronic diseases, going to the adult medical unit also means facing the reality of a potentially more serious disease or seeing patients with other psychiatric comorbidities [20]. Another obstacle noted by our patients is their fear of becoming adults [39]. They expressed their need to be treated like children, as if there was a gap between their chronological age and their developmental condition. The transition process can induce anxiety in these patients, who believe that they have missed some of the steps of adolescence. They are concerned about not having completed their maturation process and are frightened of the gap between them and other adults, authors emphasizing adaptation to “developmental readiness” rather than chronological age [40]. Furthermore, AN is a complex and polyfactorial somatic and psychiatric condition that, in theory, needs a pluridisciplinary transition [41]. However, most of the time, the transition takes place in adult psychiatric structures where there are usually no somatic physicians in charge of these patients. Therefore, patients switch from pluridisciplinary care to quasiexclusive psychiatric care. The awareness of the psychiatric dimension of the disease is strengthened when the question of transition occurs. Indeed, this not only implies moving from the adolescent medical unit to adult care but also from a disease whose psychiatric dimension is uncertain, underestimated or denied to an identified and affirmed psychiatric disease who must be treated in an adult psychiatric institution. As some of them told us, the announcement of the transition confirmed the psychiatric reality of the disease, and this awareness appeared to be just as difficult as the transition process itself. Furthermore, the affirmation of the psychiatric dimension of AN leads to a very psychiatric representation of the adult medical unit that can also represent an obstacle to transition. The patients reported the fear of meeting other psychiatric patients and named the adult medical unit the “madhouse”. Considering the multitude of obstacles expressed by our patients who contribute to their resistance to transition, we thought it would be interesting to understand and describe the underlying mechanisms of these barriers.

Transition process: meeting the AN specificities

We suggest that in AN, the obstacles to transition could be subtended by the psychological characteristics of the patients (dependence, bonding and attachment disorders, fear of abandonment), by the characteristics of the disease (anorexic cognitions, denial, ambivalence toward recovery, unpredictable evolution, unknown prognosis), and finally by psychiatric comorbidities that may be associated (depression, anxiety, personality disorders).

A potential reluctance to transition in AN patients could be their own cognitive and emotional functioning. These patients have a need for control, “omnipotence”, and a very obsessive way of thinking, inflexibility, cognitive rigidity and fear of change [42, 43]. The transition runs into this rigidity, creating a split between their innate need for control and the fact that the transition process requires flexibility. This means talking about changes and flexibility to a population that is completely closed to any element that could disturb mental homeostasis. Moreover, these patients can also have difficulties in social interactions and in creating affective relationships that can interfere with the transition process [44]. In fact, on the one hand, AN patients may present with attachment disorders, with difficulties in losing a relationship and creating others [45]. On the other hand, they often move from separation anxiety to the fear of intrusion [46]. AN medical follow-up is often complex, long, and requires a significant individual investment [47]. The patients have to relate their complex personal and familial history. Therefore, the transition process, involving a reorganization of therapeutic relationships and a reinvestment in trusting another team, may seem impossible for these patients. One of the specific elements encountered in AN that may also represent an obstacle to the transition is the attachment disorder frequently associated with this condition. Many AN patients have an insecure type of attachment, more specifically, an insecure-preoccupied attachment style [48]. These persons have a negative self-model and a positive model of others. They believe that they do not deserve to be loved and have a constant fear of being abandoned [49]. Consequently, these individuals lack autonomy and suffer from emotional dysregulation. These elements are emphasized during the transition process, which involves good self-confidence. Unlike other chronic diseases, AN has an unpredictable evolution and an unknown long-term prognosis [50]. In other chronic diseases, when the evolution is known and predictable, physicians must prepare for this transition by talking about it with the patient in the early stage of the disease. Patients with AN are as likely to recover as they are to develop a chronic form of AN or disability in adulthood. This duality, between recovery and chronicity, represents a real barrier to the transition for AN patients and for health professionals working with them because of this uncertain outcome. Theoretically, during adolescence, the position of teenagers in their families changes as the process of independence and individualization expands. However, the interaction of AN with the adolescent process is complex, blocking its development and standing in the way of becoming an adult [51]. Furthermore, with respect to physical maturation, early onset AN in the peripubertal period may induce growth and pubertal delay [52, 53]. Thus, in AN patients, there is a gap between theoretical and suitable adolescent empowerment and actual sociofamilial dependance. Moreover, in the adolescent medical unit, parents still play a crucial role as legal guardians and in decision-making as cotherapists. However, during the transition process and in the adult medical unit, the legal aspect of the parental role lapses, resulting in the questioning of new roles and responsibilities. These changes require maturation, autonomy, and independence that the patient has not necessarily acquired yet and for which parents are not usually prepared for. Finally, AN patients have multiple and frequent psychiatric comorbidities. Half of these patients have anxiety disorders (obsessive‒compulsive disorder, social phobia, separation anxiety) [54]. In addition, depressive symptoms (low self-esteem, self-criticism, guilt, irritability, shame) are more common in AN patients than in the general population [55]. These comorbidities represent additional barriers to the transition. After discussing the barriers to the transition expressed by AN patients, their underlying mechanisms specifically related to the anorexic cognitions, and the frequently associated comorbidities, it seemed appropriate, at this point, to address the therapeutic challenges that patients and medical teams have to address to enable the transition process.

Transition process: therapeutic challenges for patients and professionals

If our study allowed us to explore and describe the obstacles to the transition and their link to the specific characteristics of anorexic patients, it would be important to better clarify the challenges faced by AN patients and professionals to consider therapeutic strategies for transition in the future. To minimize the risk of disruption of care that could occur during the transition, general recommendations have been developed for somatic diseases to standardize this transition process, as proposed by the British recommendations of the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) [56]. These recommendations include a transition program cocreated with the patient. Furthermore, an international group of experts agreed on the elements that must be included in a transition program [57]. This program emphasizes the importance of effective communication between pediatric and adult institutions and early discussion about the transition program with the patient, at best during childhood [58]. In order to estimate the level of preparation of patients for the transition, evaluation scales have been created. These scales also allow professionals to identify the topics in which the patient still needs to progress before the transition [59]. In Europe, few studies have explored the question of transition in AN. Some of them focused on the subjective experience of health professionals during the transition process [18, 36]. An other one focused on the experiences of transition of AN patients who have gone through it [19]. Finally, some studies explored the experiences of relatives during the transition [20, 60], revealing an overall distressing and disjointed experience with a lack of support throughout the transition process. In accordance with our study, providing the best support for transition involves innovative solutions and strategies to make this difficult period easier. A protocol for co-designing and implementing Canadian practice guidelines for transitions emphasized on the identification of the contextual and cultural factors relevant to guideline implementation [61]. A study informed the development of five transition strategies designed to facilitate effective transfers across ED care: Timely talks, Readiness, Inclusion, Preparation, and Synergy (TRIPS) [38]. We actually consider that individual timing and temporality are essential points to be considered when elaborating, discussing and developing specific transition programs for AN patients. Indeed, one of the major issues for these patients can be the developmental impasse, in which they are frozen with a profound gap between their civil and their developmental age. Thus, the timing of the transfer must remain flexible; depending on the degree of autonomy of the adolescent, the arbitrary age of 18 years is not always relevant. Furthermore, it seems essential to consider that there is not one transition but many possible transition settings. This process must consider the difficulties related to the disease, the individual history, the sociofamilial and cultural context. A recent article focused on developing an intervention model in collaboration with individuals who have lived ED experience fosters hope and informed decision-making. This approach aims to create interventions that are meaningful and relatable to the clinical and firsthand experiences [62]. The level of preparation of each patient must be carefully evaluated by the adolescent medical team before the transition. Moreover, although our patients rarely addressed the place of the family in the transition process, it seems essential to discuss this point. Indeed, this transition is even more threatening for AN patients, as it exacerbates separation anxieties and individualization problems [63]. Furthermore, although parents are aware that the transition is a logical and necessary stage, it remains a major source of anxiety and concern. They can express their fear of being less informed about their child’s health, of playing a less important role in the follow-up and of feeling excluded [20]. It seems crucial to find solutions to accompany parents in an adult environment to invent a new place with them [64]. For AN patient, we can propose a first appointment in the adult medical unit with the referent healthcare professional of the adolescent unit to make the patient feel safe and limit the fear of abandonment. We can also suggest a transition space in which both pediatric and adult professionals could participate in discussion groups and share therapeutic education tools. The other important point to facilitate the transition to AN would be to develop specific transition healthcare coordinators as already available for chronic somatic diseases [65]. The role of these coordinators would be to supervise the transition, answer questions from the patient and family and assist them during the adult unit visit. As we noted previously, during the transition, AN patients usually move from a multidisciplinary adolescent environment to a strictly psychiatric adult institution. Thus, the psychiatric reality of the disease is imposed on patients and their families during the transition with a fear of stigmatization [66]. Therefore, patients and their families must be prepared for it by the adolescent medical unit. They must explain that moving to an adult psychiatric institution does not mean that their disease is more severe or more psychiatric but that the transition process is part of the recovery pathway. Furthermore, the patients in our study reported that their very “psychiatric representation” of the adult medical unit could represent a real obstacle to the transition. This issue raises a broader question for health professionals concerning the nature of the setting in which the transition should take place. We suggest that the answer to this question is not unique and depends on the severity of the AN symptoms and psychiatric comorbidities, the degree of patient autonomy, and the potential risk of chronicity. Thus, the transition should be specific and individualized to each patient, considering their needs and wishes, the local healthcare services available, and the reality of the AN condition. Considering all these elements, the transition process should remain flexible, not systematically hospital-based, and built on a continuum between private and exclusive institutional care but also between outpatient and inpatient treatment. Thus, explaining to patients who the transition has to be done but would not necessarily take place in an adult psychiatric institution and must be codeveloped could improve self-adherence.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The strengths of this study are its originality, the number of patients recruited, the strength of the qualitative methodology, and the quality of the therapeutic alliance with the adolescent medical team. This last one allowed us to collect rich material and to access a very intimate subjective experience. Some limitations must nevertheless be mentioned. The average age of the patients interviewed was 22 years. This means that most of the patients interviewed were not able to complete the transition process, as they were still in the adolescent medical unit despite their age. We have therefore given a certain perspective on the subject that would probably have been different if we had interviewed patients who had gone through the transition process. Including only girls was not an a priori choice, but reflects the epidemiological reality of our patients, which may represent a limitation in the sense that we did not have access to the experience of male patients with AN. Another limitation of this study is that it did not allow us to explore resistance to transition related to the systemic dimension of AN, which would require exploring the subjective experiences of the parents.

Conclusion

Our innovative study explored the subjective experience of AN patients confronted with the transition to adult care. This allowed us to better describe and understand the concerns of these adolescents and young adults during this difficult and complex stage of care. As in any other chronic disease, the continuity of care and the quality of the link between the adolescent and the adult environment must be guaranteed. In addition to the usual obstacles found in other chronic diseases, the major barriers to the transition to AN seem to be related to and reinforced by the specific characteristics of this population. Finally, the psychiatric comorbidities that can be associated with this pathology also represent obstacles to the transition. It appears necessary to develop tools to evaluate these aspects of the disease and target the specific needs of this population to create personalized transition programs. These programs must consider the role of the somatic care relay and family participation in transition and must integrate close collaboration between disciplines and specialised institutions.
