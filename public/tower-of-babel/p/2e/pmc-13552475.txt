The Squalene Oil-in-Water Nanoemulsion Vaccine Carrier AddaVax Potentiates TLR-Induced B-Cell Responses
Authors: Egest J. Pone, Jiin Felgner, Jenny Hernandez-Davies, D. Huw Davies
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
For vaccination, the mechanisms of action of antigens and of immunostimulatory pathogen-associated molecular pattern (PAMP) adjuvants are better understood than the roles of the depot, carrier, and other formulation-related influences. Here, we carried out cytometric studies of vaccine formulations and the effect of adjuvant formulations on B-cell responses. In particular, the AddaVax squalene oil-in-water nanoemulsion formulated with MPLA and CpG, termed IVAX-1, which is particularly effective in eliciting influenza hemagglutinin (HA)-specific mono-reactive or dual-reactive antibodies, was used as the model adjuvant to explore the requirements for generation of these B-cell antibody responses in vitro and in vivo. Using an in vitro inducible germinal center (GC) reaction, we found that AddaVax-mediated pre-assembly of B-cell stimuli for antigen, TLR agonists, CD40 ligand, and cytokines enhanced antibody class switching compared to aqueous buffer solutions. Covalent HA labeling was used to provide high-fidelity detection of mono- and dual-reactive B cells. In immunized mice, MPLA and CpG together enhanced the numbers of total and HA-specific plasma cells relative to each alone. Interestingly, titration of green fluorescent protein (GFP) and fluorescent HAs delivered in AddaVax nanoemulsion revealed that > 50% of input antigens are adsorbed onto the surface of these nanoparticles at the level of ~50–200 protein antigens per nanoparticle. Likewise, several protein antigens, including HAs, a bacterial outer membrane protein, CBU1910 from Coxiella burnetii, and GFP, were all found to associate with AddaVax emulsion nanoparticles in flow cytometry assays. Finally, the AddaVax carrier was more potent in inducing total and dual-reactive antigen-reactive B cells compared to a liposomal carrier, the Immunosome-NTA(Ni). These data cumulatively indicate that concentrated oil-in-water nanoemulsions are effective carriers for molecular adjuvants and antigen in extemporaneous formulations of research-grade protein subunit vaccines.

Introduction

Adjuvants are molecular entities that enhance vaccine efficacy, and are thought to be particularly important for increasing both the magnitude and breadth of antibody responses. This is accomplished by diverse substances that include carrier/depot biomaterials and certain biomolecules acting as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) that activate their cognate immune pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). The main categories by which adjuvants enhance immune responses include the following [1, 2]: (1) the direct stimulation of PRRs by their corresponding PAMPs and other immunostimulants [3]; (2) the carrier (or delivery) effect, in which antigens and other molecules are immobilized to the carrier for enhanced lymphatic, systemic transport, or prolonged half-life [4, 5]; and (3) the depot effect, in which antigens and immune stimulants are released gradually from the deposited formulation to provide a more constant and prolonged mobilization of immune cells [2, 6, 7].

The immunogen in the vaccine, usually a proteinaceous antigen, engages the B-cell receptor (BCR) to license the activation of B cells that bind antigen above an affinity threshold. In addition, sustained B-cell activation and differentiation for production of class-switched, hypermutated antibodies requires activation of multiple, distinct types of PRRs, cytokine receptors, and tumor necrosis family receptors (TNFRs) such as the CD40 receptor [8–10]. These receptors provide nonredundant co-stimulatory signals for optimal B-cell entry into the cell cycle, growth, and selection in GCs [11, 12] and other ectopic lymphoid structures [13]. While TLR agonists engage their receptors directly in DCs as well as B cells, initial activation of such cells would then activate T cells for cognate B: T interactions via CD40, cytokines, and accessory receptors in GCs, culminating in mutually robust adaptive humoral and cellular responses [11, 12].

The mechanism of action of antigen, as well as immunostimulatory PAMP adjuvants, is better understood for vaccine immunogenicity than the depot, carrier, and other influences. To address this knowledge gap, we have used an in vitro inducible GC reaction and in vivo studies in mice to assess the role of vaccine carriers and PAMPs on the induction of HA-specific antibodies. In many viruses, there usually is one main, or dominant [14, 15], protein antigen which typically serves to attach to host cells and, in turn, is the main target of the neutralizing antibody response. The BCR licenses B cells for activation in an antigen-specific manner when antigens crosslink or disperse multiple BCR units or signalosomes [16, 17], with antigen in particulate format typically being more immunogenic than in monomeric, soluble format [5, 16, 18] Synthetic nanoparticles that present highly clustered and repetitive antigen(s) together with appropriate co-stimulatory agonists that enter lymphatics and collect in draining lymph nodes are an efficient means to stimulate robust B and T lymphocyte interactions in the nascent GCs [5].

Microbes contain repetitively displayed surface antigens for B cell/antibody recognition, as well as multiple copies of several types of immunostimulatory PAMPs. In particular, monophosphoryl lipid A (MPLA, a TLR4 agonist) and certain unmethylated CpG DNA motifs (TLR9 agonists) are two prominent molecular PAMP adjuvants used in recently approved human vaccines [3]: MPLA for shingles vaccine (Shingrix) and CpG for hepatitis B vaccine (HEPLISAV-B). Thus, their combination holds particular promise for enhancing vaccine breadth and efficacy, as well as reducing dose and decreasing side effects [19, 20]. Multiplex presentation of antigen variants (i.e., combining multiple variants into one vaccine) also achieves some breadth [19], but may not anticipate reactivity toward novel variants unless antibodies to shared epitopes are generated. Therefore, understanding the requirements for vaccine-induced ‘inherent’ cross-reactivity [21], particularly measured at a single B cell or clonal level rather than by polyclonal serology assays [22], would be paramount and should lead to new insights.

Among depot and carrier reagents, squalene water-in-oil emulsions have a depot effect leading to prolonged release of antigen at the injection site. On the other hand, squalene oil-in-water nanoparticle emulsions of low viscosity, such as MF59 used in licensed human influenza vaccines and AddaVax used for immunology research, do not exhibit this depot effect, and their mechanism of adjuvant activity is less well understood [1]. Several studies have demonstrated that delivery of both antigen and adjuvant to the same anatomical site [23] (e.g., ipsilateral lymph nodes) and within a coincident (or at least overlapping) timeframe is required for optimal vaccine performance [24–27]. Likewise, using smaller repeated injections, osmotic pumps, or biomaterial approaches to mimic the depot effect all have marked influences on the quality of the immune response [26]. In summary, concomitant or controlled delivery of antigen, adjuvant, and other accessory stimuli remains a key influence behind vaccine efficacy.

A complementary study [28] characterized epitope-level specificity and cross-reactivity. Here, we focus on the cytometric characterization of vaccine formulations and the effect of adjuvant formulations on B-cell responses. The AddaVax/MPLA/CpG formulation, termed IVAX-1, which is particularly effective in eliciting HA-specific mono-reactive or dual-reactive antibodies [19, 20], was used as the model adjuvant to explore the requirements for B-cell antibody responses in vitro and in vivo. Several protein antigens, including influenza hemagglutinins (HAs), a bacterial outer membrane protein, CBU1910, from Coxiella burnetii, and green fluorescence protein (GFP), were all found to associate with AddaVax emulsion nanoparticles in flow cytometry assays. Using an in vitro inducible GC reaction [10], AddaVax pre-assembly of B-cell stimuli for antigen, TLR agonists, CD40, and cytokines, enhanced antibody class switching. In immunized mice, MPLA and CpG together enhanced the numbers of total and HA-specific plasma cells. The emulsion AddaVax is a carrier with multiple positive characteristics and is likely to be an effective and compatible vaccine carrier for other combinations of PRR agonists in the development of combination adjuvants tailored for particular functions, such as improving breadth. In summary, concentrated oil-in-water nanoemulsions proved to be effective carriers for molecular adjuvants and antigen in extemporaneous formulations of research-grade protein subunit vaccines.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

Antibodies and cytokines were from BioLegend. MPLA derived from hydrolysis of endotoxin lipopolysaccharide (LPS) isolated from Salmonella minnesota (serotype R595) was from Avanti Polar Lipids. Phosphorothioate backbone CpG-1826 and CpG-1018 were purchased from IDT. AddaVax, a squalene oil-in-water nanoparticle emulsion with an average particle size of 160 nm, was purchased from Invivogen. Goat anti-mouse IgM F(ab′)2 and anti-CD40 (clone 3/23) were from BioLegend. Escherichia coli LPS serotype O111:B4 was from Sigma. hBAFF60, a 60-mer recombinant protein preparation that is reported to activate human and mouse receptors, was from Adipogen. General pipets, polypropylene plates, tissue culture plates, and accessory reagents were from Fisher or USA Scientific.

Adjuvant Preparation

MPLA, a TLR4 agonist, has limited solubility in aqueous solution and was therefore formulated as a liposome with 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoglycerol (DOPG) (an inert co-lipid). In brief, the MPLA liposome was manufactured by combining MPLA and DOPG at a molar ratio of 1/5 in chloroform organic solvent. The organic solvent was then evaporated under nitrogen, and the residual solvent was removed overnight under vacuum. The lipid film was hydrated with sterile 10 mM NaCl to achieve 5 mg/mL (i.e., 2.835 mM) of MPLA and sonicated in a sonicating water bath (Branson M1800) at room temperature (RT, 20°C–25°C) for approximately 15 min or until the formulation was translucent with no large visible particles. Particle size distribution of the formed MPLA liposomes was found to be approximately 100 nm in diameter by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Malvern Zetasizer. The formed MPLA liposome was stored at 2°C–8°C and maintained the adjuvant activity for at least 6 months. CpG-1018, a TLR9 agonist with a modified phosphorothioate backbone, was used in most experiments, except where CpG-1826 was used as indicated. CpG was dissolved in sterile water for injection at a 1 mM concentration and used as a stock. The CpG stock maintained the adjuvant activity for at least 12 months when stored at −20°C. A typical combination adjuvant was used, comprising 1:1 (vol:vol) squalene oil-in-water emulsion AddaVax. Three nanomoles of liposomal MPLA were typically used and one nmole CpG1018 per mouse in a 50 μL injection volume. For binding of His-tagged antigens (H1, H7 or streptavidin) on the surface of Immunosome-NTA(Ni), consisting of 100 nm unilamellar liposomes displaying NTA(Ni) designed to bind His-tagged proteins, the HA-His6 antigens were mixed with 25 μL Immunosome solution, and as indicated were further mixed with 1 nmole CpG1018 and/or 3 nmole MPLA, and the overall volume was brought to 50 μL with PBS, without subsequent dialysis. This system is used as an orthogonal methodological control to evaluate non-covalent multimeric antigen display and to contrast it with the covalent HA labeling approach.

Mouse Immunizations

All animal procedures were approved by the UC Irvine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. AUP-18–096) and carried out in the vivarium in accordance with University Laboratory Animal Resources (ULAR) guidelines and standard protocols. C57Bl/6 mice 8–12 weeks old (Charles River) were injected subcutaneously (sc) on Day 0, boosted on day 14, and typically euthanized on day 19 for analysis. Single-cell suspensions from spleen were prepared for staining and analysis by flow cytometry by gentle disruption through a 40-μm cell strainer (ThermoFisher) with 10 mL Dulbecco’s phospate buffered saline (DPBS; Stemcell Technologies), centrifuging at 500 × g for 5 min to collect the cells, lysing red blood cells with 2.5 mL ACK lysing buffer (Gibco), followed by quenching with 12 mL DPBS, centrifuging as above, and resuspending in RPMI 1640 containing l-glutamine (Gibco) cell culture medium for stimulation assays or DPBS for flow cytometric staining.

Fluorescent Labeling of Antigens

For covalent labeling of H1 (influenza A H1N1: A/California/04/2009), H5 (influenza A H5N1: A/Vietnam/1194/2004), and H7 (influenza A H7N9: A/Anhui/1/2013), full-length His-tagged proteins produced in mammalian cells were lyophilized in amine-free PBS buffer (Sino Biological). Each HA vial was reconstituted in molecular biology-grade water at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL, with each vial typically containing 100 μL volume. The DyLight Microscale Antibody Labeling Kit (ThermoScientific) was used following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, H1 was labeled with DyLight 405 (abbreviated DL-405), H5 with DL-488, and H7 with DL-650, as follows. After pipetting 100 μg of each HA antigen solution to the lyophilized dye at the bottom of each DyLight vial, mixing well, and incubating for 1 h at RT, the excess unreacted dye was removed by the supplied gel filtration columns and resin following exactly the manufacturer’s protocol. The degree of protein recovery was estimated by running sample aliquots on an SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie brilliant blue staining. Note that in our related papers [19, 28], the covalent method of labeling antigens (e.g., via NHS ester fluorescent dyes) was shown to be more suitable for detecting antigen-specific B cells for up to three antigen variants in immunized mice compared to the indirect method of using fluorescent streptavidins conjugated to biotinylated antigens.

Flow Cytometry

Single splenocyte suspensions (~2 × 106 cells/well in 96-well round-bottom plates) were first surface labeled at RT. for 30 min with a cocktail of fluorescent H1 or/and H7 antigens (each at 2 μg/mL, equal to 100 ng per 50 μL staining volume in a well), suitably titrated fluorescent Abs and reagents, typically comprising BV510-anti-CD138, BV655-anti-CD3, BV785-anti-CD19, PE-anti-IgG2a, PE/Cy7-anti-GL7, and APC/Cy7-anti-IgM (BioLegend Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) and 7-Aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; Thermo Fisher). Cells were then fixed, permeabilized, washed, and stained with fluorescent antigens (HAs, streptavidins, or their associated controls) as indicated, washed again using the Cyto-Fast Fix-Perm intracellular staining kit following the manufacturer’s protocol (BioLegend). 7-AAD was validated under our fixation/permeabilization protocol and reliably distinguished intact lymphocytes: 7-AAD (used at 0.25 μg/μwell) together with surface staining mAbs were washed with four volumes of PBS buffer, and thus the intracellular 7-AAD from dead cells did not leak appreciably to stain live permeabilized cells in subsequent steps. Finally, 100 μL samples were acquired from 96-well plates on a Novocyte 3000 flow cytometer (ACEA/Agilent) for 50 s, with FSC-H threshold set at 100,000, and voltage settings such that unstained cells have autofluorescence values between approximately 102 and 103 units. Unstained and single-color stained controls were used to set up the compensation matrix. Flow data were analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC, Ashland, OR, USA). Live lymphocytes were gated by appropriate forward versus side scattering, followed by singlet gating by area vs. height of forward scattering pulse (i.e., the diagonal was gated in FSC-A vs. FSC-H plots), and finally by 7-AADlo gating of live (non-apoptotic and non-necrotic cells). CD19+ (including dim or lo and bright or high) cells were gated in a two-dimensional CD19 versus CD3 plot, excluding CD3+ cells, followed by gating of antigen-specific (plasma or B) cells in a 2D CD138 versus CD19 plot (plasma cells are defined as CD138+ CD19lo).

B-Cell Cultures

LPS, anti-IgM, anti-CD40, BAFF, and IL-4, as indicated, were first mixed individually or in combinations indicated in the main text and figures with indicated volumes of AddaVax in each well of a V-bottom 96-well plate at RT for 2 h. These immunoconjugates were then used to stimulate single cell suspensions (1 × 105–3 × 105 cells/mL in each well) from mouse spleens in RPMI 1640 medium containing l-glutamine (Gibco), 10% FBS, 1× penicillin/streptomycin mix (Gibco), supplemented with 50 μM beta-mercaptoethanol (BME) in flat-bottom 96-well plates in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C for 4 days.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in Excel (Microsoft) to determine p values using the Student’s t-test (unpaired, two-tailed).

Results

Comparison of AddaVax and Immunosome-NTA as Adjuvant Carriers

AddaVax was compared for its adjuvant activity with another commercial vaccine carrier, Immunosome-NTA(Ni), consisting of 100 nm unilamellar liposomes displaying NTA(Ni) designed to bind His-tagged proteins (such as H1 and H7 used here), and PBS as a control group, in the absence or presence of MPLA and/or CpG. AddaVax, together with MPLA and CpG (IVAX-1), yielded the highest number of total plasma cells (CD19lo CD138+) (Figure 1A,B).

AddaVax Induced Higher Frequencies of HA-Specific Plasma Cells/Plasmablasts

When analyzing antigen-specific plasma cells, AddaVax induced higher frequencies of H1+ specific cells (Figure 2A,B) and H7+ plasma cells (Figure 2C,D). Furthermore, the AddaVax carrier induced minor but readily visible and consistent dual-reactive H1+ H7+ cells (Figure 3A–C). Visual inspection confirmed that one particular difference between AddaVax and Immunosome-NTA is the former’s higher total concentration of lipids (5.5% compared to 1%), implying greater adsorption capacity (Figure 3D) for antigens and other immunomodulators, although the NTA is expected to coordinate His-tagged antigens more efficiently and in a regularly arrayed orientation than stochastic adsorption. A calculation based on flow cytometry of fluorescent antigens estimated that several dozen proteins are adsorbed per AddaVax particle as compared to one dozen for Immunosome-NTA (Figure 3D). AddaVax combined with CpG also induced greater total numbers of H1+ or H7+ mono-reactive cells (Figure 3C), though somewhat paradoxically, the relative number of dual-reactive H1+ H7+ cells was not higher in the AddaVax group compared to the PBS (no carrier) group (Figure 3B). Note that the ~2%–4% frequency of H1+ H7+ cells is consistent with primary sequence divergence and with our previous report on multivalent HA immunization [19].

Blocking With Excess Unlabeled HAs Confirmed the Specificity of Detecting Antigen-Specific B Cells/Plasma Cells

Control experiments in which H1 or H7 were blocked with the unlabeled versions of the antigens are also consistent with specific antigen detection (Figure 4A,B) and not any staining or off-target artifacts. While the relative frequency of dual-reactive cells may appear higher without carrier or TLR adjuvants, the absolute number of these cells is rather low due to the lower immune response induction and much lower total plasma cells compared to the adjuvanted groups. Thus, these results suggest caution in combining highly effective carriers and PAMPs when the intended application is the amplification of a particular dual-reactive minor population. Note that B-cell membranes, like respiratory tract cells, have sialic-acid glycoproteins, and this shows up as background signal [29] when HAs are used to stain B cells from immunized animals. However, as our accompanying report detailed [28], at our optimal HA staining concentration, this background HA signal was essentially eliminated; the background HA signal, likely due to HA binding to sialic-acid moieties in membrane glycoproteins, was only readily apparent at the highest HA concentrations used in preliminary staining titrations.

Streptavidin Is a Convenient and Versatile Reagent to Induce and Detect Streptavidin-Specific B Cells

Because of the wide availability of a spectrum of fluorescent streptavidins, they would represent a convenient system to screen immunization and sample processing conditions before larger-scale studies with other immunogens of interest. Indeed, specific detection of H7 (Figure 5A,B) or streptavidin (Figure 5C,D) was readily observed, similar to our earlier studies. As both H7 and streptavidin were His-tagged, no major background staining of anti-His B cells was observed in this system, presumably since the tag epitope is both less immunogenic and occurs at low frequency compared to the number of total epitopes. Previously, we have detected anti-His antibodies using our more sensitive microarrays [30, 31]. Interestingly, we therefore propose that streptavidin molecules themselves can be used as convenient and versatile model protein antigens and as the corresponding detection reagents for streptavidin-specific B cells in scouting and optimization experiments. We emphasize the use of streptavidin as a model antigen and concomitantly as a fluorescent antigen within the context of a methodological takeaway rather than for immunological interpretation (i.e., not necessarily related to any vaccine research or immune studies on the bacterium Streptomyces avidinii).

AddaVax Nanoemulsion May Adsorb Fluorescent Antigens

In order to gain more insights into any mechanisms by which AddaVax enhances immune responses, we took advantage of our NHS fluorescent labeling protocol to see if such antigens can be used to track their interactions with the vaccine formulation below, and their biodistribution in vivo in future studies. Therefore, HA proteins, and two unrelated antigens (the bacterial outer membrane protein CBU1910 from C. burnetii labeled with DL-488, and the naturally fluorescent protein GFP) were used to study their association with the vaccine carrier AddaVax (Figure 6). These proteins were mixed with AddaVax using the same composition as in typical IVAX adjuvant formulations (but minus MPLA and CpG). After overnight incubation at 4°C, the AddaVax mixtures were diluted 10-fold with PBS, and the samples were acquired on a Novocyte flow cytometer using settings that detect particles smaller than cells (FSC-H threshold = 1000) but not spurious noise on buffer alone. There is a fluorescent signal in each expected channel (colored arrows), but not in the controls (first column, all upper PBS rows of each of the four panels, as well as the two channels that do not detect the particular fluorophore) (Figure 6A). When the percent of association with AddaVax nanoemulsion is quantified (Figure 6B), about 50%–70% of the nanoemulsion particles have fluorescent antigen, whereas no appreciable fluorescent signal is present when the same fluorescent proteins were mixed with PBS buffer and collected with the same flow cytometer settings. A representation of the hypothetical bulk equilibrium between free and AddaVax-adsorbed proteins is shown schematically (Figure 6C). Thus, hypothetically, if protein antigens are adsorbed in AddaVax, the particles may thereby engage cognate BCRs with higher avidity, and they may be more enriched in the lymph nodes than soluble monomeric antigens injected in PBS.

AddaVax Pre-Assembly of Stimuli Enhances Antibody Class Switch to IgG1 in Cell Cultures

We hypothesized that if protein antigens are adsorbed in AddaVax, the particles may thereby engage cognate BCRs with higher avidity than monomeric antigens injected in PBS. Since microorganisms like viruses and bacteria are nanometer to micron-sized, AddaVax (squalene nanoemulsion ~160 nm in diameter) was used to adsorb the indicated stimuli. AddaVax was found to particularly enhance CSR to IgG1 at non-saturating concentrations of LPS plus BAFF (Figure 7A) or anti-CD40 stimuli (Figure 7B), possibly via multimerizing these stimuli on the nanoemulsion surface for more effective receptor ligation. This result may enable the use of AddaVax as a carrier for using labeled antigens and adjuvants to study their transport and binding to lymph node or spleen cells, which is a key parameter underpinning vaccine efficacy. Together, these observations suggest that the physical organization of antigens and co-stimulatory ligands by the carrier itself is a key determinant of B-cell activation.

Discussion

Proper antigen folding and, where possible, arrayed and multimeric presentation render them more effective immunogens compared to monomeric, soluble forms [5, 16, 18]. Once BCR senses antigen, its signaling primes B-cell transient activation, and additional co-stimulatory signals from PRR, TNFR, and cytokine receptors, typically provided by T cells and dendritic cells (DCs), are essential for full and sustained B-cell mobilization and differentiation [16, 32]. Similarly, the initial DC activation of naïve T cells and the subsequent licensing of cognate T and B lymphocyte interactions critically depend on near-concomitant and sustained activation of multiple families of receptors [33]. Thus, B-cell responses are quintessential for vaccine efficacy since class-switched high-affinity antibodies neutralize present pathogens, memory B cells are held in reserve for future encounters, and activated B cells also serve as semi-professional APCs for T cells.

Vaccine preparations that maintain the native antigen conformation, density, and topology are expected to be superior to those that use soluble monomeric antigen and separate adjuvants. Nevertheless, investigating the optimal combinations and quantities of stimuli needed to elicit protective B-cell and T-cell responses has been an understudied aspect of vaccine design. In the context of breadth, it is unclear whether strong adjuvants that are effective in inducing B-cell responses to an original antigen can also drive affinity maturation of antibodies against shared epitopes in antigen mutants or variants.

Covalent HA labeling is the most reliable method when comparing multiple antigen variants, as illustrated in the present study, and as analyzed in greater depth in our related publication [28]. This method is entirely analogous to the tried-and-tested backbone protocol of flow cytometry, whereby fluorescent mAbs (typically labeled covalently at their lysine residue amines using succinimide or maleimide reactive esters of the fluorescent dye [34, 35], but also other methods, for example, based on click chemistry [36]) are used to detect biomarkers on and inside cells. The switch is that here we used an antigen covalently labeled with a particular fluorescent dye to detect B cells/plasma cells whose mAbs bind it alone (mono-reactive) or also its antigen variant (dual reactive) labeled with a different fluorescent dye. The method can be extended to measure as many variants as the cytometry setup can accommodate, and therefore, it is likely that dozens of antigen variants can be screened for reactivity in immunized animals using the more capable method of spectral flow cytometry.

MPLA and CpG added up to produce a greater number of total and H7-specific plasma cells than either one alone, and this additive effect was further increased when the carrier was the rather concentrated, but nonviscous, oil-in-water nanoemulsion AddaVax. This triple combination adjuvant, IVAX-1, also induced greater numbers of H1+ or H7+ mono-reactive cells compared to the expected outwardly uniformly displayed HA-His-tag on Immunosome-NTA(Ni) lipid vesicles. The efficiency of a formulation may depend on the degree of immunogen adsorption or multimerization on carriers or scaffolds. Thus, His-tagged antigens bound to lipid nanoparticles derivatized with lipid-NTA(Ni) elicited higher serum antibody responses compared to protein adjuvanted with Alum or adsorbed on the surface of control lipid-NTA particles without nickel [37]. Similarly, liposomes derivatized with NTA loaded with cobalt to capture His-tagged antigens induced stronger B-cell and T-cell responses than the soluble antigen and related controls [38]. Conversely, another study has found that covalently attaching antigens to liposomes elicited significantly stronger antibody responses than the same NTA-anchored His-tagged antigens [39].

Overall, a common explanation for discordant studies is that the degree of antigen adsorption as well as the manner of epitope presentation (uniformly or nonuniformly displayed) are fulfilled differentially by different carriers [40]. Specifically, AddaVax is a more concentrated formulation as a carrier compared to most other lipid carriers routinely used for vaccine delivery, so the degree of antigen immobilization and presentation may be mediated more efficiently [40]. While the dominant antigen mono-reactive plasma cells population in our study was always higher in IVAX-1 compared to other formulations, paradoxically, the relative number of dual-reactive H1+ H7+ cells was not higher in the AddaVax group. Nevertheless, since MPLA and CpG carried in PBS or Immunosome-NTA induced overall lower numbers of plasma cells, the absolute numbers of H1+ H7+ cells are comparable in the three carriers. We can only speculate that the particular formulation that is most potent in the sense of producing the greatest total amount of activated antigen-specific lymphocytes may do so by amplifying particular clones (i.e., stimulating centroblast B cells during the GC reaction), hence not particularly stimulating a more diverse repertoire of responding clones as judged by the dual-reactive H1+ H7+ population. These results suggest caution in combining highly effective carriers and PAMPs when the intended application is the amplification of a particular dual-reactive minor population or the most diverse possible repertoire. In other words, relative composition and absolute numbers are two distinct variables to consider in a particular vaccination intent or phase component, that is, future studies could compare prime in a strong formulation with boost in a less adjuvanted formulation or just unadjuvanted antigen as in classical immunization schemes to yield antibody-secreting cells for subsequent mAb production or to evaluate memory responses [41, 42].

Several protein antigens were deduced to adsorb on the AddaVax squalene oil-in-water nanoemulsion when analyzed by flow. The similar mobilization of diverse proteins in this data set suggests that AddaVax may be a particularly effective carrier and multimerizer of protein antigens in general, and thus a key component of adjuvant protein subunit vaccines. While the AddaVax particles (~160 nm diameter) clearly showed the fluorescent signal from the proteins they were mixed with (compared to a few cytometric events from PBS and fluorescent proteins), the so-called “swarming effect” or “swarm detection” could also be contributing to this difference. In brief, the high density of the AddaVax nanoemulsion increases the probability that multiple small particles (a “swarm”) will enter the interrogation volume simultaneously or in very close succession [43, 44]. That is, the detectors cannot resolve the individual particles in the swarm, and instead, they register the combined signal as a single composite event with amplified intensity (e.g., brighter scatter or fluorescence). In summary, from a heuristic viewpoint, since the fluorescent proteins did not show appreciable signal when collected in PBS solution, but did so when dispersed in AddaVax, it is likely that at least a substantial fraction of the fluorescent signal reflects adsorption on the AddaVax nanoemulsion particles, suggesting additional experiments from complementary techniques to further probe this phenomenon. Separately, AddaVax may also mediate “protein transfection” into the cytosol for antigen presentation and/or cross-presentation.

Pre-assembly of key B-cell stimuli by AddaVax enhanced B-cell activation as measured by a cell culture assay for proliferation and antibody class switching. In this context, AddaVax may be particularly suitable since, on the one hand, it is small enough (~160 nm in diameter) to drain to the lymph nodes via lymphatics, and on the other hand, it may adsorb antigens and molecular ligands for multimeric co-delivery in space and time. In future studies, fluorescent CpG and fluorescent liposomal MPLA may be used to ascertain whether they are also embedded or adsorbed onto AddaVax, and whether this has functional significance for vaccine improvement as suggested in the present studies.

The IVAX-1 adjuvant is particularly potent as evidenced by its induction of mono- and multi-specific B-cell responses. One of our previous related publications showed that administration of several influenza HAs in multiplex manner, together with the strong combination-adjuvant IVAX-1, elicits broad reactivity to HAs beyond each individual HA component [19]. This was followed by our recent study in which covalent labeling with fluorescent dyes of HAs enabled clear detection and validation of influenza HA-specific, cross-reactive, and heteroclitic B cells [28] induced by any one HA dispersed in the IVAX-1 adjuvant. Finally, here we provided additional details on a plausible adsorption mechanism by which the carrier AddaVax potentiates TLR-induced antigen-specific B-cell responses. Cumulatively, these reports indicate that presenting antigen together with co-stimulatory TLR agonists admixed in an oil-in-water nanoemulsion as a coincident delivery carrier has potential for extemporaneous formulation of vaccines for research.
