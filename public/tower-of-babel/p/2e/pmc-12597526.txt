Probiotic Formulation for Patients With Bipolar or Schizophrenia Spectrum Disorder: A Double-Blind, Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trial
Authors: Jenny Borkent, Magdalini Ioannou, Dorien Neijzen, Bartholomeus C M Haarman, Iris E C Sommer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisProbiotic augmentation offers a promising treatment for bipolar disorder (BD) and schizophrenia spectrum disorder (SSD). By targeting microbiome deviations, they may improve both gut and brain health.Study DesignIn this double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial with the multi-strain probiotic formulation Ecologic BARRIER, we aimed to improve psychiatric and cognitive symptoms, intestinal permeability, and gastrointestinal symptoms in patients with BD or SSD. A total of 131 patients were randomized 1:1 to receive either the probiotic supplement (n = 67) or a placebo (n = 64) for 3 months, in addition to treatment-as-usual. The primary outcomes were symptom severity assessed by the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale and cognitive functioning by the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia.Study ResultsNo significant effect of probiotics was observed on psychiatric symptoms, but borderline significant improvement was observed in the cognition category of verbal memory (Linear Mixed Model (LMM) 0.33; adjusted P = .059). Probiotics beneficially affected markers of intestinal permeability and inflammation, including zonulin (LMMserum = −18.40; adjusted P = .002; LMMfecal = −10.47; adjusted P = .014) and alpha-1 antitrypsin (LMM 9.26; adjusted P = .025). Indigestion complaints significantly decreased in male participants in the probiotics group (LMM = −0.70; adjusted P = .010). Adverse events were similar between groups.ConclusionsOur study observed significant advantages of probiotics for gut health in BD and SSD, with excellent safety and tolerability. A borderline effect on verbal memory was also indicated. These results underscore the need for further research into microbiome-targeted interventions for patients with complex brain disorders.Keywords: probiotics, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, gut microbiome, intestinal permeability, gut–brain axis

Introduction

Bipolar disorder (BD) and schizophrenia spectrum disorders (SSDs) share a complex array of symptomatic, genetic, and biological factors.1 In both disorders, patients experience a variety of psychotic, depressive, and manic symptoms,2 along with an increased risk for metabolic syndrome and neurocognitive decline.3–5 Another link between the 2 disorders is the common co-occurrence of alterations in the gut microbiota.6,7 First-line treatments, including antipsychotics and mood stabilizers, often yield suboptimal results, with up to 50% of BD patients and 30% of SSD patients not responding adequately. Additionally, these drugs frequently cause significant side effects, such as fatigue and gastrointestinal issues, underscoring the need for alternative treatment strategies.8–10 One promising approach is targeting the gut microbiota, particularly through probiotics. Emerging evidence from preclinical studies,11,12 as well as clinical populations,13,14 suggests that probiotics may improve psychiatric symptoms and cognitive functioning, offering a novel avenue for intervention in BD and SSD.

The term “gut microbiota” refers to the community of microorganisms, including bacteria, residing in the gastrointestinal tract.15 These microorganisms affect not only the gut but also other organs, including the brain.16,17 The gut and brain are connected through pathways such as the vagus nerve and indirectly through the immune system, with microbiota also producing neurotransmitters and other metabolites.16 Several case–control studies have associated gut microbiota composition with BD and SSD,6,18,19 but findings are inconsistent, and a consensus on the specific taxa involved is lacking. Gut microbiome dysbiosis has also been associated with decreased gut motility, which can lead to symptoms such as constipation.20,21 Notably, microbiota interactions with sex hormones, like estrogens and testosterone, influence its composition and activity in a sex-specific manner.22 These sex-specific differences in microbiota composition have been linked to distinct physiological and pathological states.23 By improving gut microbiome composition through probiotics, we expect gut motility to improve, potentially alleviating these symptoms.

A leading hypothesis states that microbial dysbiosis increases intestinal permeability and inflammation, negatively impacting brain function.16 Studies indicate that patients with BD or SSD have elevated levels of bacterial translocation markers like Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-binding protein (LBP) and soluble CD14 (sCD14), as well as increased zonulin, which regulates gut epithelial tight junctions.24,25 In the gut, gliadin and bacterial components activate the CXCR3 receptor, leading to a MyD88-dependent release of zonulin.26–29 Zonulin then disassembles tight junctions by activating the epidermal growth factor receptor, causing actin microfilament rearrangement and displacement of zonula occludens-1, key components of cell–cell adhesion.30 Intestinal permeability has been linked to gut inflammation, with higher levels of markers like calprotectin and alpha-1 antitrypsin indicating more inflammation, though less explored in BD/SSD.31 Increased intestinal permeability may also cause systemic inflammation and weaken the blood–brain barrier (BBB),17 with cross-sectional studies showing positive associations between BBB permeability and negative symptoms and cognitive impairments in schizophrenia.32–34 Interestingly, some studies suggest sex-specific effects on intestinal permeability, with women exhibiting higher levels.24,35 Based on these findings, reducing intestinal permeability through improved gut microbiota could be a strategy for improving psychiatric symptoms and cognitive functioning.

Probiotics, live microorganisms believed to offer health benefits, may improve gut health and, by extension, mental health36 and cognition.37 However, research on probiotic supplementation in BD or SSD is sparse.38 Probiotic supplementation has been linked to reduced rehospitalization rates in BD39 and improvements in gastrointestinal symptoms in schizophrenia, though no significant effects on psychiatric symptoms were observed.40 Additionally, improvements in attention and psychomotor speed were reported in patients with BD.38 Larger studies are needed to further assess the potential of probiotic formulations for BD or SSD.

This study investigates as primary outcomes the efficacy of a probiotic formulation designed to improve intestinal permeability on psychiatric symptoms and cognition in patients with BD or SSD after 12 weeks of treatment. Secondary outcomes examined the probiotics’ impact on intestinal permeability, inflammation, gastrointestinal complaints, and fatigue. Given the sex-specific differences in microbiome composition and the observed disparities in intestinal permeability, we conducted a sex-specific analysis of all outcomes as a tertiary objective.

Methods

This study was a double-blind randomized placebo-controlled mono-center trial. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (CCMO NL67848.042.19) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered in the international clinical trials registry platform (NL-OMON53363).

Participants

We recruited patients from in and outpatient clinics and through patient organizations. All patients had to be willing and capable to provide informed consent prior to participation. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years with a Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders fifth edition (DSM-V) diagnosis of BD or schizophrenia, schizoaffective, schizophreniform disorder (295.x), or psychotic disorder not otherwise specified (298.9). The Mini International Neuropsychiatric Interview (version 5.0.0) was conducted at the screening to confirm the diagnosis.41 Exclusion criteria were pregnancy or breastfeeding; mental retardation (IQ score <60); active liver, kidney, or pancreas disease; any clinically significant or unstable medical disorder, as determined by the investigators, including inflammatory bowel disease, short-bowel syndrome.

Investigational Product and Randomization

Patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either the probiotics or placebo. The multispecies probiotic formulation Ecologic BARRIER was manufactured by Winclove Probiotics (Amsterdam, the Netherlands). It contains 9 bacterial strains (Bifidobacterium bifidum W23, Bifidobacterium lactis W51, Bifidobacterium lactis W52, Lactobacillus acidophilus W37, Lactobacillus brevis W63, Lactobacillus casei W56, Lactobacillus salivarius W24, Lactococcus lactis W19, and Lactococcus lactis W58). This probiotic formulation was especially selected for 2 reasons. First, because it has been specifically designed to improve gastrointestinal epithelial barrier function.42 Second, this formulation was chosen due to its previously documented beneficial effects on cognition and depressive symptoms.13,43,44

The placebo, also provided by Winclove Probiotics, contained all components of the probiotic formulation except the bacteria, including maize starch and maltodextrin. The placebo and probiotic had indistinguishable appearance, shape, smell, mass, and taste. They were delivered as a powder in sachets containing a dose of 2 g and had to be taken twice daily, adding up to a daily dose of 4 g, equaling 1 × 1010 cfu/day. The participants were instructed to add the powder to a glass of water and to stir well before drinking it in the morning and the evening. Randomization was provided by Winclove, and the codes were not available to the study staff. The study was unblinded after the last participant had finished the intervention, and the database was checked and locked.

Study Design

Participants visited the research center at baseline (week 0) and at the end of treatment (week 12). At the halfway visit (week 6) and the follow-up visit (week 24), the participants were visited at home by the research team. The primary purpose of the home visits was to collect stool samples. By retrieving the samples ourselves, we ensured that they remained frozen by transporting them in a portable freezer in our vehicle. Unfortunately, part of the study was performed during the COVID pandemic, leading to periods of time when the visits had to be done through phone or video calls. Clinical Measures

Psychiatric symptom severity was measured using the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)45 by trained and certified raters. In addition to the BPRS total score, we assessed 4 symptom subcategories: affective, positive, negative, and activation symptoms. Neurocognitive functioning was assessed with the Brief Assessment of Cognition in Schizophrenia (BACS)46 and the Stroop task.47 For BACS, we have assessed 4 subdomains of cognition namely processing speed, reasoning and problem-solving, verbal memory, and working memory. Moreover, gastrointestinal symptoms and fatigue were assessed using the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GSRS) and the Fatigue Severity Scale.48,49 The GSRS includes 15 items, each using a 7-point Likert scale from no discomfort to very severe discomfort (1–7 points). The items are grouped into symptom clusters: reflux (heartburn and acid regurgitation), abdominal pain (abdominal pain, hunger pains, and nausea), indigestion (borborygmus, abdominal distension, eructation, and increased flatus), diarrhea (diarrhea, loose stools, and urgent need for defecation), and constipation (constipation, hard stools, and feeling of incomplete evacuation).48

Assessment of Diet Quality

The diet quality of patients was assessed at baseline and at the end of treatment with the use of the Dutch Healthy Diet (DHD) questionnaire.50,51 The DHD is derived from the Food Frequency Questionnaire and measures adherence to the 2015 Dutch dietary guidelines.51 The food domains assessed include vegetables, fruit, whole grain products, legumes, nuts, dairy, fish, thee, fats and oils, coffee, red meat, processed meat, sweetened beverages and fruit juices, alcohol, salt, and unhealthy choices. Each food domain is scored between 0 and 10, with 0 indicating no adherence to the Dutch dietary guidelines and 10 indicating maximum adherence to the Dutch dietary guidelines. The total DHD score is the sum of the individual subscores.

Blood Sample Collection and Analysis

Blood was collected at baseline and at the end of treatment. To assess intestinal permeability in serum LBP, sCD14 and zonulin were measured. LBP and sCD14 levels were measured according to the manufacturer’s protocol of kits from R&D systems (Human LBP DuoSet Enzyme-Linked Immuno Sorbent Essay (ELISA), R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA; Human sCD14 DuoSet ELISA, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA). Serum dilutions were 1:4000 for sCD14 and 1:500 for LBP. Each assay was performed in duplicate, and the mean values of the 2 assessments were used. Intra-assay variation coefficients were 5.23% for sCD14 and 5.25% for LBP. LBP and sCD14 concentrations were calculated based on standard curves constructed with assay kit standards run for each plate. Furthermore, the obtained data were corrected for batch effect by normalizing the data with respect to an internal control that was present on all 96-well plates.

Measurement of serum zonulin was performed using a competitive IDK Zonulin ELISA kit for serum (Immunodiagnostik AG, Bensheim, Germany). Serum samples were measured at a 20-fold dilution and had an average Coefficient of variation (CV) of 5.40%. The data were corrected for batch effects by normalizing the data with respect to the reference concentrations of 2 controls provided by the manufacturer.

C-reactive protein (CRP) levels were measured via Immunoturbidimetric assays at the hospital laboratory of the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG). The minimal detectable dose of CRP was 0.3 mg/L.

Fecal Sample Collection and Analysis

Participants were provided with home stool collection kits and given detailed instructions on how to collect stool samples. Stool samples were immediately frozen after collection at −20°C at the participants’ homes until transportation to the laboratory site in a mobile freezer and thereafter stored at −80°C. Fecal zonulin (CV of 5.63%), calprotectin (CV of 5.54%), and alpha-1 antitrypsin (CV of 10.31%) were measured by biovis Diagnostik MVZ (Limburg-Offenheim, Germany). The determination was performed with commercially available ELISA kits (IDK Zonulin (stool) ELISA; IDK Calprotectin ELISA; IDK α1-Antitrypsin ELISA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Safety

During the visits, patients were queried about any occurrences of side effects or health issues since their previous visit, particularly emphasizing gastrointestinal symptoms. Our analysis involved comparing the occurrences (both in number and percentage) of serious adverse events (SAEs) and adverse events (AEs) between the 2 groups, including instances such as hospitalizations for any reason.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis and visualization were performed in R version 4.2.2,52 and figures were produced using the package ggplot2.53

The distribution of demographic and clinical variables was determined with the use of the Shapiro–Wilk test, histograms, and Q–Q plots and are presented in the demographics table as mean ± SD for normally distributed continuous variables, as median (IQR) for continuous variables with a skewed/non-normal distribution, or as frequencies (n) and corresponding percentages for discrete variables.

The BACS assessments were divided into 4 domains: processing speed (token motor task, symbol coding, and verbal fluency), reasoning and problem-solving (tower of London test), verbal memory (list learning), and working memory (digit sequencing). Scores were converted into age- and sex-corrected Z-scores. Reference values used for the calculation of the Z-scores were retrieved from table 4 in the paper of Keefe et al.54

Two linear mixed-effect models were used to assess the effects of the 2 experimental conditions, with a random effect for the patient to account for individual variability. Both models used the baseline and end-of-treatment questionnaire scores and biomarker values as dependent variables.

The first model included fixed effects for a treatment × time interaction, baseline scores, age, and sex. The second model included fixed effects for a treatment × time × sex interaction, baseline scores, and age. In both models, time was treated as a categorical variable, and treatment (probiotic or placebo) was considered as a fixed factor, along with sex and their interactions. To evaluate whether the treatment effect differed by sex, the treatment × time × sex interaction was included. However, if the interaction was significant, the dataset was split by sex, and the first model (with the treatment × time interaction) was run separately for men and women. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method controlling for false discovery rate. Cohen’s d effect size was computed to estimate the standardized effect size. Moreover, we performed a sensitivity analysis by using a linear regression model with the change between the baseline measurement and the end-of-treatment measurement as the outcome variable. As independent variables, we used treatment, sex, age, and baseline measurements.

Additionally, to ensure the diagnosis (BD or SSD) did not affect the results, we split the data by diagnosis and reanalyzed it using the first linear mixed-effect model. For exploratory purposes, we also investigated whether the effect of probiotics was greater in patients with more severe psychiatric symptoms (≥median). To do this, we divided the data at the median for each dependent variable and applied the first linear mixed-effect model.

Results

From May 31, 2019 to March 31, 2022, 131 participants were randomized (Supplementary Figure S1). The last follow-up visit was on October 13, 2022. Six participants dropped out before the halfway visit, 8 participants dropped out between the halfway and the end of treatment, and 2 participants dropped out in the follow-up phase.

Demographic Characteristics and Diet Quality

There were no statistically significant differences in the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between treatment groups (Table 1). The only significant finding was a decreased severity of abdominal pain in the probiotics group (med = 2.00) compared with the placebo group (med = 2.67, P = .016). No statistically significant differences in diet quality were observed between the treatment groups with a mean total score of 101.85 (21.46) in the probiotics group and 99.61 (20.68) in the placebo group (Supplementary Table S1). Interestingly, both groups ate relatively healthy when compared with a healthy control group, as described by Ioannou et al.55 Additionally, participants in both the probiotics and placebo groups did not significantly change their diets between baseline and the end of treatment (Supplementary Table S2). Although we did not impose strict exclusion criteria for treatment modifications during the study period, the randomization of participants should ensure that any changes are balanced between both groups. A table detailing medication changes, typically involving dosage adjustments, can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

Primary Outcomes

Total symptom severity did not show a time-by-treatment interaction.

None of the psychiatric symptom severity categories (total, affective, positive, negative, and activation symptoms) showed a significant treatment × time interaction, nor a significant treatment × time × sex interaction (Supplementary Table S4). For exploratory purposes, we examined whether the impact of probiotics on psychiatric symptoms was larger in patients with more severe psychiatric symptoms (≥median). However, no significant treatment × time interactions were found for the BPRS total score or any of the subscores (Supplementary Table S4). A significant interaction between treatment and time was observed for verbal memory (LMM 0.33, 95% CI, 0.04 to 0.61, P = .027, Supplementary Figure S2), indicating that verbal memory improved significantly more over time in the probiotics group compared with the placebo group. Significance became borderline after adjusting for multiple testing (P = .059). We found a significant treatment × time × sex interaction for processing speed (LMM 0.49, 95% CI, −0.82 to −0.16, P = .005) and problem-solving (LMM 0.76, 95% CI, 0.13 to 1.40, P = .021), but sex-specific estimated effects did not survive multiple testing adjustments (Supplementary Table S5). Problem-solving improved significantly in women in the probiotics group compared with women in the placebo group (LMM 0.45, 95% CI, −0.29 to 0.05, adjusted P = .059). Additionally, processing speed improved significantly in men in the probiotics group compared with men in the placebo group (LMM 0.37, 95% CI, 0.06 to 0.69, adjusted P = .059). However, in both cases, the sex-specific effects did not survive multiple testing adjustments. No effects were found for working memory or the STROOP task (Figure 2). The results of sensitivity analysis were similar and can be found in Supplementary Table S6. Finally, to take into account the impact of diagnosis, we conducted linear mixed model analyses separately for the groups stratified by diagnosis (BD or SSD). The results of these analyses are presented in Supplementary Table S7.

Intestinal Permeability and Inflammation Markers

The effects of the probiotic on serum and fecal markers of intestinal permeability and inflammation in patients with BD or SSD can be found in Figure 3. The probiotic formulation led to a significant decrease in serum zonulin levels overtime in the probiotics group when compared with the placebo group (Figure 3C, LMM −18.40; 95% CI, −27.85 to −8.95; adjusted P = .004) and had no significant effect on the levels of LBP (Figure 3A, LMM −0.18; 95% CI, −1.23 to 0.87; adjusted P = .892) and sCD14 (Figure 3B, LMM 0.08; 95% CI, −1.14 to 1.30; adjusted P = .983). With regard to the fecal markers, the interventional product led to a significant treatment × time interaction in the levels of zonulin (Figure 3D, LMM −10.47; 95% CI, −17.65 to −3.33; adjusted P = .018), alpha-1 antitrypsin (Figure 3F, LMM −9.26; 95% CI, −16.30 to −2.23, adjusted P = .033), and had no effect on the levels of calprotectin (Figure 3E, LMM −12.47; 95% CI, −33.82 to 8.89, adjusted P = .404). We also assessed the sex-specific effects of the probiotic on the levels of serum and fecal markers (Supplementary Figure S3). A significant treatment × time × sex interaction was found for LBP (LMM −3.96; 95% CI, −6.05 to −1.86; P = .004). At the end of treatment, LBP levels significantly decreased in women who were treated with the probiotic (LMM −1.65; 95% CI, −3.00 to −0.29; adjusted P = .048). Conversely, in men, LBP levels significantly increased in the probiotics group compared with the placebo group (LMM 2.34; 95% CI, 0.87 to 3.81; adjusted P = .015).

For calprotectin, a significant treatment × time × sex interaction was found (LMM 62.88; 95% CI, 19.91 to 105.84; P = .018), indicating that treatment effects differed between men and women. The mean change in calprotectin levels was more pronounced in men treated with probiotics than those administered placebo (LMM −49.08; 95% CI, −87.42 to −10.73; adjusted P = .048) (Supplementary Figure S4). No significant effects were found in women. Sex-specific treatment effects were not observed for the levels of sCD14, zonulin in serum and stool, and alpha-1 antitrypsin.

Additionally, we measured the metabolic biomarkers high-density lipoprotein (HDL) -cholesterol, triglycerides, glucose, and albumin. Our analysis revealed no significant treatment × time interactions in any of these parameters. Finally, we measured several inflammatory biomarkers, including CRP, S100B, interferron gamma (IFNγ), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNFα). Similarly, we observed no significant treatment-by-time interactions for any of these markers (Supplementary Table S8 and Supplementary Figure S5).

Gastrointestinal Symptoms

Regarding gastrointestinal symptoms, we found no treatment × time interactions. A treatment × time × sex interaction was found for the mean change in GSRS total score (LMM 0.64; 95% CI, 0.11 to 1.17; P = .020) and indigestion symptoms (LMM 0.708; 95% CI, 0.03 to 1.39, P = .047). At the end of treatment, improvements were found in GSRS total scores (LMM −0.42; 95% CI, −0.75 to −0.08, P = .020) and indigestion scores (LMM −0.70; 95% CI, −1.09 to −0.31; P < .001) in men with probiotics compared with those with placebo (Supplementary Figure S5). In women, there were no differences in GSRS total scores or indigestion scores between the treatment groups. However, the significance of the treatment × time × sex interaction for GSRS total score and indigestion did not survive correction for multiple testing, P = .081 and P = .141, respectively. The same applies to the significant treatment × time interaction for GSRS total scores in men (P = .081). We found no significant treatment effects of other categories of the GSRS or fatigue symptoms (Table 2).

Safety Assessments

During the treatment phase, 2 individuals in the probiotics group experienced an SAE vs 0 in the placebo group, both participants were hospitalized for a psychotic episode. Similar incidences occurred during the follow-up phase, with 1 SAE in each group: 1 participant in the placebo group was hospitalized for a depressive episode and 1 in the probiotics group for a manic episode. From the AEs, skin problems were the most prevalent, they were reported by 3 participants in the placebo group and 1 in the probiotics group. This was followed by fatigue problems, which were reported by 2 participants in the placebo group and 1 in the probiotics group. Only 2 participants reported gastrointestinal problems, 1 in the placebo and 1 in the probiotics group. In total, the number of individuals reporting AE(s) was higher in the placebo group compared with the probiotics group (22 [17.5%] vs 13 [10.3%]).

Discussion

Our randomized controlled trial, aimed at exploring the efficacy of a probiotic formulation designed to improve intestinal permeability as an adjunctive treatment for BD and SSD, yielded nuanced insights. Although we did not find efficacy on symptom severity, we showed improvement on some cognitive domains, which remained borderline significant after correction for multiple testing only for verbal memory. Moreover, we found beneficial effects of the probiotic on gut permeability and on indigestion complaints (only in men).

To our knowledge, only 3 probiotic studies have been performed in patients with BD. A notable study by Dickerson et al. followed 66 patients hospitalized for mania, who were randomized into either receiving probiotics or a placebo for 24 weeks after discharge.39 The results indicated a reduction in rehospitalization rates and duration for the probiotic group. However, no difference was found in the mean levels of psychiatric symptom measures, as observed in this study. Reininghaus et al. conducted a pilot study on 20 euthymic BD individuals using a multi-strain probiotic for 3 months, finding significant improvements in attention, psychomotor speed, and executive function.38 Our study also noted cognitive benefits, specifically in verbal memory for the whole group, problem-solving in women, and processing speed in men, though these results were borderline significant after multiple testing corrections. Beyond BD and SSD, studies in other populations, for example, major depressive disorder56 and Alzheimer’s disease,57 have also reported positive effects of probiotics on cognition. However, other studies do not find significant cognitive benefits.58 Therefore, it is premature to conclude a broad cognitive-enhancing effect, but the promising results warrant further research. Finally, Sabouri et al. reported no psychiatric symptom severity improvement in their 7-week, double-blind placebo-controlled study in 38 patients with BD type 1.59 In patients with schizophrenia, 3 probiotic studies have been conducted. Dickerson et al. found no significant changes in psychiatric symptoms from a 14-week probiotic supplementation in outpatients with schizophrenia, consistent with our findings.40 Two other studies that combined probiotics with either vitamin D (n = 60) or selenium (n = 60) in patients with schizophrenia60,61 showed a decrease in Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) general scores and CRP levels, with the probiotic/vitamin D combination also reducing the total PANSS score. These studies were not registered in an international trial registry, and the complex interplay of the 2 interventions makes it challenging to pinpoint the exact source of the therapeutic benefits.

We noticed significant improvements in biomarkers associated with intestinal permeability and inflammation. Specifically, serum and fecal zonulin levels, indicative of gut barrier function, were significantly reduced in the probiotic group, hinting at the formulation’s potential to enhance intestinal integrity. This effect was distinct and did not extend to other markers like LBP or sCD14, underscoring the complexity of the microbiota–gut–brain axis and the selective impact of probiotics on gut health. Others have also demonstrated that patients with BD or SSD have dysbiotic microbiota and intestinal barrier dysfunction.6,7 A recent meta-analysis corroborated our findings, showing a significant reduction in serum zonulin levels across various studies and disorders following probiotic treatment, a trend not observed for LBP (in 2 studies) and fecal zonulin (in 5 studies).62 However, we want to address the discrepancies in zonulin measurements obtained from commercial ELISAs. Recent studies, as well described in the review by Veres-Székely et al., have highlighted the technical issues related to the specificity and accuracy of these assays.30,63,64 For instance, the ELISA from Immunoglobulin, which we use, was found not to measure zonulin itself but rather to detect structurally related proteins.65 Consequently, the outcomes from these ELISAs should be interpreted with caution.

None of the previous studies investigated probiotic effects in a sex-specific analysis. Yet, given the interactions with sex steroids and the differences in microbiota between men and women,22 effects may well differ between male and female participants. Our trial also unveiled sex-specific differences in response to probiotics on biomarkers, particularly in the context of LBP and calprotectin levels, suggesting that male and female participants may experience differential benefits from such interventions. This finding aligns with emerging literature pointing to the influence of sex on dietary habits, gut microbiota composition and function, and by extension, on the efficacy of gut-targeted treatments. In a study conducted in 2013, Severance et al. observed that women with schizophrenia exhibited higher levels of LBP and sCD14 than their male counterparts.24 Similarly, research by Maget et al. revealed that women with affective disorders had elevated serum zonulin levels when compared with men.35 Interestingly, a sex-specific analysis revealed that probiotic treatment resulted in a significant decrease in LBP levels in women, whereas an increase was noted in men. It is important to note that baseline LBP levels were higher in women receiving probiotics and men receiving placebo, which may contribute to the observed changes.

Intestinal barrier dysfunction is often associated with intestinal inflammation.31 We found alpha-1 antitrypsin, but not calprotectin, to be decreased by the probiotic intervention. However, when separated by sex, we found calprotectin to be decreased by the probiotic intervention in men. To our knowledge, no other probiotic intervention studies in psychiatric disorders have investigated these intestinal inflammation markers. In ulcerative colitis, 1 study also found calprotectin to be decreased after 8 weeks of probiotic supplementation.66 Another study, performed in athletes (triathletes, runners, cyclists), found no significant improvement in alpha-1 antitrypsin levels after 14 weeks of probiotics supplementation.67

This study is the first to examine gastrointestinal complaints in patients with BD or SSD, finding that probiotic supplementation reduced indigestion symptoms only in male participants. Interestingly, male participants exhibited significantly lower baseline levels of indigestion symptoms compared with the female participants prior to the intervention. As gastrointestinal issues are common in BD and SSD, this can be an important indication for probiotic use. Probiotic studies performed in unipolar depression that examined gastrointestinal symptoms showed mixed results. Schaub et al. found no time × treatment interaction for the GSRS total score after 4 weeks of probiotic supplementation.68 Tian et al. did find a significant improvement between baseline and the end of treatment in GSRS total score and bowel dysfunction after 4 weeks of probiotics treatment.69

An important advantage of the probiotic intervention is that it was not associated with an increased risk of AEs. As expected, we found similar rates of AEs in both arms. This high tolerability adds to the potential of probiotic augmentation therapy. We also found high acceptability among patients. Recruitment was easier than for pharmacological augmentation treatments, as invited patients mostly agreed to participate. Many of them expressed an interest in interventions, targeting gut microbiota to improve brain health. This high tolerability and acceptability in combination with borderline significant results in some cognitive domains and clear effects on gut permeability suggest that interventions targeting the gut–brain axis can be an interesting option to improve overall health and brain health in people with BD or SSD.

When comparing probiotic intervention studies, it is important to note the variability in intervention length and the use of either single- or multispecies probiotics, which complicates result comparison and general conclusions about efficacy. Moreover, current probiotic intervention studies use conventional probiotics, where the strain selection is confined to a few genera, mainly Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium. Next-generation probiotics, defined as “live microorganisms identified on the basis of comparative microbiota analyses that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host,” might be a more promising strategy.70 However, several key candidate next-generation probiotics, such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, are strictly anaerobic and therefore difficult to use as probiotic strains.71

Our study has several limitations. COVID-19 restrictions during the study period prevented face-to-face meetings, resulting in missing blood and stool samples and potentially affecting questionnaire outcomes related to social functioning. Furthermore, the COVID-19 regulations significantly impacted the daily lives of the participants, which may have affected the outcome measures of some of the questionnaires for example when tapping into social functioning. Second, our study tended to attract participants with an interest in lifestyle and some of them already maintained a relatively healthy eating habit. Some of our participants often adhered to a healthier diet compared with a matched healthy control group.55 For future studies, including individuals with suboptimal healthy lifestyles would be better as they may benefit more from a probiotic intervention. Third, our study did not systematically track participant compliance until later stages. Future research should implement robust methods for monitoring and reporting participant compliance throughout the entire study to gain a clearer understanding of its impact on the results. A frequent problem with trial inclusion, which also affected our study, is that patients with high symptom severity are rarely invited for randomized controlled trials, as informed consent may be problematic during active psychotic episodes, and treatment teams prefer not to bother their patients in a very challenging period. When most severe symptoms have subsided, staff and patients do consider participation, but their low symptom severity at baseline precludes much improvement in either arm.

In conclusion, our study did not show efficacy on symptom severity, but showed some benefit of probiotic addition in the cognitive domain, while improving reflections of gut barrier function. Given the high tolerability and acceptability of probiotic formulations, these findings underscore the potential for further research into the gut–brain axis and probiotic therapy as a promising augmentation strategy for SSD and BD patients.
