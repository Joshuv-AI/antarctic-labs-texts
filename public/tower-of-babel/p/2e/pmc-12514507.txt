Beyond hemostasis, the immunomodulatory nature of platelets
Authors: Wanting Wang, Chaofan Wang, Jun Deng, Bahgat Fayed, Desheng Hu, Heng Mei, Yu Hu, Shanshan Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn addition to playing a key role in hemostasis, platelets are involved in immunomodulatory processes against pathogenic infection. Platelets secrete substances and express membrane proteins that form part of the immune defense environment to effectively remove pathogens. Direct or indirect contact of pathogens with platelets can activate platelets, which subsequently elicit a range of immune responses in vivo, leading to pathogen clearance, evasion, or tissue damage. This review summarizes the immune functions of platelets beyond hemostasis, their interactions with other immune components such as the complement system, neutrophils, and monocytes, and specifically highlights the immune effects exerted by platelets against several microbial infections.Keywords: blood platelets, hemostasis, immunity, infections, monocytes, neutrophils

Introduction

In conventional perception, the primary function of platelets is to maintain vessel wall integrity and promote hemostasis. After endothelial cell injury exposes subendothelial components, the platelet glycoprotein (GP) Ib/V/IX complex binds to exposed collagen via von Willebrand factor (VWF), thereby enabling platelet adhesion to the injury site and initiating platelet activation [1,2]. Aggregating agents released after platelet activation, such as adenosine diphosphate (ADP), act on platelet P2Y1 and P2Y12 receptors to trigger further platelet activation and agent release, forming a positive feedback loop [3]. Finally, activation of platelet GPIIb/IIIa receptors mediates cross-linking of fibrinogen or VWF between the receptors, leading to platelet aggregation and early thrombosis [4,5]. However, it is increasingly being recognized that in addition to playing a key role in hemostasis, platelets are also involved in the immune-inflammatory responses induced by pathogens. Platelets have been found to secrete a variety of inflammatory mediators and express a variety of immune-associated receptors that interact with other components of the immune system to regulate host immune functions [6].

During systemic infections, pathogens come into close contact with blood components such as platelets, which can affect the body’s immune and coagulation functions. If this systemic infection is left uncontrolled, it often leads to overactivation of the coagulation and inflammatory responses, which in turn causes microvascular thrombosis, as well as subsequent life-threatening hemorrhage due to consumption of platelets and coagulation factors. This clinical event is known as disseminated intravascular coagulation [7,8]. Abnormal coagulation is a major cause of death in patients with infections, and thrombocytopenia is thought to be a poor prognosis during sepsis [9]. A better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of the platelet-mediated immune response and coagulation in the host may help develop or improve anti-infection therapies and improve patient survival. This review summarizes the immune functions mediated by platelets beyond hemostasis and their interactions with other immune components, specifically highlighting the immune effects exerted by platelets against several microbial infections.

The Immune Function of Platelets

Granular contents of platelets

Platelets contain 3 types of granules: α-granules, dense granules, and lysosomes. Dense granules contain small nonprotein molecules such as serotonin, ADP, and adenosine triphosphate, which play an important role in the amplification of platelet reactions. Lysosomes contain a variety of acidic hydrolases. α-granules are the most abundant and contain a large number of proteins that play important roles in hemostasis, immunity, and inflammation [5,10,11]. α-granules contain various membrane surface molecules, such as integrins, P-selectin, immunoglobulin family receptors, and leucine-rich family receptors, that regulate platelet interactions with other cells, such as neutrophils. α-granules also contain various cytokines and chemokines that recruit immune cells and further regulate immune functions [6]. Platelet factor 4 (PF4), also known as the chemokine CXCL4, is one of the most abundant proteins in α-granules and is widely involved in innate immune activities. It can influence the immune functions of leukocytes. PF4 has been found to bind to CCR1 on the surface of monocytes, driving monocyte migration to sites of inflammation [12]. PF4 also inhibits monocyte apoptosis and promotes their differentiation to macrophages [13] and differentially regulates monocyte respiratory burst, survival, and cytokine expression through different signaling pathways [14]. Neutrophils can also bind to and be activated by PF4 [15]. In addition to its immunomodulatory function, PF4 can bind to polyanions (P) on the surface of bacteria to undergo conformational changes and expose new antigenic epitopes, which leads to the production of anti-PF4/P IgG. Anti-PF4/P IgG can opsonize PF4-coated bacteria, and this opsonization promotes phagocytosis of bacteria by granulocytes/macrophages and bactericidal activities by platelets [16,17]. Moreover, platelets directly release RANTES, which is the chemokine CCL5, or form RANTES-containing particles that can be immobilized onto activated endothelial cells and further promote monocyte recruitment [18].

Interleukin (IL)-1 plays a major role in the vascular inflammatory response. Activated platelets release IL-1β, which can further induce the release of cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 from vascular smooth muscle cells [19] and also promote neutrophil adhesion to endothelial cells [20].

CD40 and CD40L are important mediators between lymphocytes and antigen-presenting cells that play an important role in the body’s immune response. CD40L can also be detected on the surface of activated platelets, and it has been reported that the majority of soluble CD40L in the circulation is derived from platelets triggered upon infection [21]. Meanwhile, soluble CD40L can activate platelets and help stabilize arterial thrombi [22]. In addition, platelet-derived CD40L is reported to recruit leukocytes and enhance T-lymphocyte immune function, in turn modulating host adaptive immunity [23,24].

Thrombin-induced platelet microbicidal peptides (tPMPs) are small cationic peptides released from human and rabbit platelet α-granules. Human microbicidal peptides include chemokine PF4, connective tissue-activating peptide III (CTAP-Ⅲ), and neutrophil-activating peptide 2 (NAP-2). These peptides can kill pathogens such as Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Cryptococcus neoformans and mediate the chemotaxis of phagocytes. Rabbit PMP-1 is considered to share the greatest homology with human PF4. As the most extensively studied antimicrobial peptides, rabbit tPMPs have demonstrated potent killing effects against pathogens such as S aureus and Candida albicans [[25], [26], [27]]. It is understood that these peptides exert a microbicidal effect by disrupting cytoplasmic membranes, whereas subsequent studies have found that they also inhibit the synthesis of S aureus intracellular macromolecules [28,29]. In vitro, infective endocarditis experiments have reported that platelets can alleviate intravascular S aureus infections by secreting tPMPs [30].

Immune-related receptors on platelets

P-selectin, a key component of platelet α-granules, plays a central role in mediating platelet–leukocyte aggregate formation [10]. Platelet activation leads to the exposure of P-selectin and subsequently activates neutrophils through the interaction of P-selectin with P-selectin glycoprotein ligand-1 (PSGL-1) on the neutrophil membrane. Neutrophil activation results in the upregulation of the integrin CD11b/CD18 on its surface, thereby promoting binding of CD11b/CD18 to fibrinogen, GPIb, as well as intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM-1) [6,31,32]. Neutrophil activation is further enhanced upon the release of cytokines and other contents from platelets, such as CXCL1, PF4, and HMGB1, ultimately leading to the formation of platelet–neutrophil aggregates and the secretion of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [6,[33], [34], [35]].

Platelets also express FcγRIIA, a low-affinity receptor for the constant fragment (Fc) of IgG, which forms immune complexes and triggers intracellular signaling events, thereby leading to platelet activation and aggregation [36]. Thus, when infection occurs, pathogen-specific IgG first opsonizes pathogens but can also bind to FcγRIIA and further activate platelets. In addition, FcγRIIA is involved in an IgG-independent amplification loop of the platelet activation response. Studies have shown that FcγRIIA is functionally linked to platelet αIIbβ3, which amplifies the activation signal of αIIbβ3 and thus promotes thrombosis [37,38] (Table 2).

Protease-activated receptors (PARs), members of the G protein-coupled receptor family, are expressed on platelets, leukocytes, and endothelial cells. Four PARs have been identified that can be activated by a wide range of proteases. PAR1, PAR3, and PAR4 are activated by thrombin and histone G, whereas PAR2 is activated by trypsin, factor VIIa (FVIIa), and FXa [39]. Cleavage of PAR1 and PAR4 on platelets by thrombin promotes platelet aggregation and release of granular contents, thereby promoting thrombosis [40]. Thrombin can also activate a variety of cell types, such as endothelial cells, neutrophils, and macrophages, by binding to PARs [39].

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns and are type I transmembrane proteins that play an important role in the innate immune response against pathogens. Platelets express a range of TLRs, among which TLR4 recognizes bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which can initiate platelet aggregation and promote platelet release of cytokines and chemokines, synthesis of IL-β, platelet–neutrophil aggregate formation, and NET release in response to infection [41,42].

NOD-like receptors (NLRs) are pattern recognition receptors that mediate innate immunity by binding pathogen-associated molecular patterns. NLRs include the intracellular protein, NACHT-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3). Upon stimulation, NLRP3 joins apoptosis speck-like protein containing a caspase recruitment domain and caspase-1 to form the NLRP3 inflammasome, a complex that produces cytokines of the IL-1 family, including IL-1β and IL-18, which initiates the inflammatory response [43]. The NLRP3 inflammasome of platelets has been reported to be involved in inflammatory amplification and increased vascular permeability during infectious events [44]. Moreover, it has been found that NLPR3 inflammasome regulates platelet integrin αIIbβ3 outside-in signaling and affects thrombosis [45]. It has also been reported that NLPR3 can affect platelet GPIb-IX function and thrombosis in an inflammasome-independent manner [46]. Therefore, targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome or IL-1β may be beneficial in the treatment of inflammation-associated thrombosis. NOD2 (nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain 2) is another NLR expressed on platelets that also promotes platelet activation and immune thrombosis when activated during infection [47].

C-type lectin-like receptor 2 (CLEC-2), a type II transmembrane receptor, functions as a dimer on platelets, megakaryocytes, and macrophages. CLEC-2 has an extracellular lectin-like structural domain. Its ligand, podoplanin, is present on lymphocytes and macrophages. The interaction of podoplanin with CLEC-2 initiates platelet activation and aggregation through Src, PLCγ2, and DAG signaling. It has been reported that CLEC-2 drives postinfectious thrombosis in a mouse model with Salmonella infection. CLEC-2 also contributes to inflammatory responses, which can further trigger the migration of dendritic cells (DCs) to lymph nodes and T-cell areas to initiate the immune response [[48], [49], [50]].

Dendritic cell-specific ICAM-grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN) is a C-type lectin expressed mainly on DCs. It binds to ICAM-3 on T lymphocytes and plays an important role in T-lymphocyte activation [51]. However, it was found that DC-SIGN is also synthesized and expressed in platelets and megakaryocytes. Platelets can capture and transfer human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV-1) via DC-SIGN, which may facilitate the spread of the virus in patients [52]. Hottz et al. [53] reported that dengue virus mediates platelet activation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis in a DC-SIGN-dependent manner, which can lead to thrombocytopenia.

Platelet microparticles

Platelet microparticles (PMPs) are membrane vesicles shed by activated platelets, which carry many of the characteristic platelet proteins, such as P-selectin, GPIIIa, and GPIIb, and chemokines such as CXCL4, CXCL7, and CCL5 [54]. The secretion of microparticles by platelets is accompanied by the migration of phosphatidylserine to the membrane surface, thus providing a catalytic surface for prothrombinase assembly, which in turn promotes thrombin generation [55]. Proteomic and functional analyses of PMPs show that they can be categorized into different types according to their size. Individual PMPs differ in their protein components, protein/lipid ratio, and functional effects on platelets and endothelial cells [56]. Although the difference in surface area between PMPs and platelets is ≥2 orders of magnitude, the procoagulant activity of a PMP is almost identical to that of an activated platelet, so at elevated concentrations, they may contribute to the development of thrombotic complications [57]. PMPs have similar immune functions to platelets and can support neutrophil activation and aggregation by binding to neutrophil Mac-1 through GPⅠb [58]. Furthermore, PMPs carry platelet cytoplasmic components and can transmit platelet-derived bioactive lipids, cytokines, mRNAs, and microRNAs to distant sites to affect recipient cells, which plays an important role in intercellular signaling and gene expression programming throughout the circulatory system [59].

Cross-Talk of Platelets with Immune Components Upon Infection

Cross-talk with neutrophils

As the most abundant leukocyte population in the body, neutrophils play an important role in infection-induced immunity in vivo. Upon infection, neutrophils can interact with platelets and further induce the formation of infection-associated platelet–neutrophil aggregates, which are an important part of immune responses. It has been reported that extracellular vesicles generated by platelet-activated neutrophils can transport arachidonic acid to platelets, where it is processed by cyclooxygenase 1 to generate thromboxane A2, further promoting neutrophil activation and thrombosis [60]. In addition, the platelet–neutrophil complex triggers neutrophil-derived extracellular DNA release and protein complex formation, which also plays an important role against infection (Figure 1).

In septic mice, TLR4 on the platelet membrane binds to LPS, allowing platelets to bind neutrophils, which leads to rapid activation of neutrophils and the formation of NETs that capture and kill pathogens [61]. These NETs, or network-like DNA structures, are very effective at trapping pathogens under both static and flow conditions. NETs contain histones H1, H2A, H2B, H3, and H4, as well as neutrophil elastase and myeloperoxidase, which are arranged on the DNA backbone. Once bound to these NETs, pathogens are killed by these histones and antimicrobial proteins [62]. In addition, NETs can provide a scaffold for thrombosis [63], which in turn activates platelets and promotes platelet aggregation as well as continuous thrombosis [64,65]. Because thrombosis involves fibrin, monocytes, neutrophils, and platelets, which can work together to facilitate pathogen recognition, this process helps to inhibit pathogen invasion and spread [66]. The interaction of NETs with platelets to induce thrombosis can be effective in the presence of pathogenic microorganisms; however, overactivation can cause tissue damage and even disseminated intravascular coagulation, which damages the microcirculation. Histones, one of the components of NETs, are cytotoxic, which can cause pathologic coagulation and tissue damage. Neutralization of histones may be an effective therapeutic strategy [67,68]. Furthermore, it has been reported that PF4, in addition to enhancing the antimicrobial function of NETs, may also limit thrombosis and endothelial injury by preventing the cleavage of NETs into short fragments that can activate the coagulation contact pathway [69].

Interaction with complement

Activated platelet surfaces can serve as a platform for initiating complement cascade reactions, and this initiation may occur through different mechanisms (Figure 2). First, the P-selectin (CD62P) expressed on the surface of activated platelets may act as a receptor for C3b, initiating the alternative pathway of complement activation by forming the C3bBb complex, which ultimately leads to the formation of C5b-9 complexes on the platelet surface [70]. In addition, the receptor protein gC1qR/P33 on the platelet may initiate the complement classical pathway activation by binding C1q [71]. Moreover, gC1qR/p33 and P-selectin are found in microparticles released from activated platelets, and the deposition of C3b and C5b-9 has been identified on the microparticles exposed to plasma. Therefore, it has been inferred that the platelet microparticles may also be involved in complement activation [72]. Last, platelet α-granules contain chondroitin sulfate, which binds C1q and thus initiates complement activation via the classical pathway [73]. Mass spectrometry analysis has shown that platelet α-granules contain C3 and C4, which can interact with other complement components after release [74]. Moreover, Arbesu et al. [75] found that bacteria-triggered platelet activation resulted in the translocation of C3 to the platelet surface, which may modulate immune defenses against bacteria by enhancing complement activation. They also found that the complement protein C3 in platelets differed slightly from C3 in plasma in electrophoretic mobility.

When inflammation, infection, and tissue damage occur, the complement system activates platelets and enhances their ability to respond to these events. The coagulation reaction occurs on negatively charged phospholipid surfaces, where phosphatidylserine plays a key role. This property of phosphatidylserine ensures that the process is limited to sites of injury. Therefore, modification of the cell membrane is an important event in the process of platelet activation, which is necessary for the formation of a primary clot [76]. The complement membrane attack complex or terminal complement complex can alter the direction of platelet membrane potential through depolarization, thereby forming a procoagulant lipid surface that provides binding sites for the prothrombin activator, in turn promoting thrombin generation and facilitating platelet secretion of storage granules and release of FVa-rich particles [77]. In addition, the allergenic toxin C3a and its derivative C3a-des Arg induce platelet activation and aggregation in vitro [78]. Platelets express the C3a receptor (C3aR) on their surface. Reinhard et al. [79] reported a prolonged tail hemorrhage and reduced likelihood of disease in stroke and myocardial infarction models in mice with C3aR deficiency, emphasizing the pathophysiologic correlation of C3a/C3aR with platelet activation.

Cross-talk with monocytes

Platelets can form platelet–monocyte aggregates (PMAs) with monocytes by direct contact. P-selectin on the surface of activated platelets binds to PSGL-1 on monocytes, which is the first step in platelet–monocyte aggregation. This binding subsequently upregulates the expression of CD11b/CD18 on the surface of monocytes, which binds to the platelet receptor GPⅠb and further enhances the formation of PMAs [6]. In pathologic conditions such as cardiovascular disease and infections, elevated levels of PMAs can be observed in patients. Platelets can affect inflammatory cytokine secretion by monocytes. It has been reported that platelets exposed to dengue virus induce lipid droplet biogenesis and secretion of cytokines such as CXCL8/IL-8, IL-10, and IL-6 in monocytes [80]. Wang et al. [81] reported elevated levels of PMAs in the peripheral blood of patients with active tuberculosis, and platelets induce IL-1β and IL-6 production by monocytes in a triggering receptors expressed on myeloid cells (TREMs)-like transcript-1 (TLT-1)-dependent manner. When infection occurs, monocytes secrete large amounts of proinflammatory cytokines that contribute to host defense, and Hawwari et al. [82] found that if platelet counts are lowered, they lead to reduced immunoreactivity and proinflammatory gene expression in monocytes. This is because platelets can act as a reservoir for key cytokine transcriptional regulators (eg, NF-κB and MAPK p38) and deliver these inflammatory transcriptional regulators to monocytes in a vesicle-mediated manner.

In addition to immunometabolic reprogramming of monocytes, platelets can promote phenotypic changes in monocytes. Carestia et al. [83] reported that in the presence of LPS, platelets polarize monocytes to proinflammatory macrophage type 1 (M1) via the glycoprotein GPIb-CD11b axis. Monocytes can act as precursors for DCs during infection or inflammation, and Singh et al. [84] observed that DCs derived from PMAs in patients with HIV exhibited reduced levels of molecules critical for DC function (eg, CD206, DC-SIGN, CD80, CD86, CCR7) and reduced antigen uptake in in vitro culture.

Interaction with T lymphocytes

Platelets express major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I, which processes and presents pathogen antigens to directly activate naïve T cells [85]. Guo et al. [86] found that platelet MHC-I expression is upregulated during sepsis, which increases antigen cross-presentation and interaction with CD8+ T cells. However, this interaction negatively regulates CD8+ T-cell number and function, as evidenced by inhibition of CD8+ T-cell proliferation and interferon (IFN)-γ secretion. In addition to participating in the immune response, platelets cooperate with T cells to promote inflammatory remission. In a model of bacterial pneumonia, platelets were recruited into the lungs along with neutrophils to participate in the inflammatory response. However, during the remission phase of inflammation, platelets in the vasculature are activated by sCD40L secreted by Treg cells, and subsequently express P-selectin, which binds to PSGL-1 on Treg cells to form aggregates that are recruited to the lungs to promote clearance of inflammatory neutrophils. Direct contact between platelets and Treg cells also promotes the release of anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β from Treg cells [87].

Interaction of Platelets with Pathogens: Good or Bad

Bacterial infection

Staphylococcus aureus

S aureus is a Gram-positive bacterium that can colonize the human body as a commensal; it can cause a variety of serious clinical infections such as skin infections, pneumonia, endocarditis, toxic shock, and sepsis [88]. Arman et al. [89] reported that S aureus can activate platelets via the immune receptor FcRIIA as well as αIIbβ3. Subsequent secretion of PF4, ADP, and TXA2 by activated platelets is in turn involved in the positive amplification loop of platelet activation responses. Although antiplatelet therapy may be a strategy to prevent S aureus-induced thrombosis, it may increase host susceptibility to pathogenic infections because it has been shown that platelets can exert an immune function to help clear S aureus [90]. In a mouse model of S aureus-induced periprosthetic arthritis, thrombocytopenia was induced with an anti-CD41 antibody, and a significantly higher infection burden was observed upon anti-CD41 treatment, compared with phosphate-buffered saline controls [91]. Wuescher et al. [92] established a new platelet deficiency mouse model to study the functional contribution of platelets to the prevention of S aureus bacteremia. The data showed that platelet-depleted mice died from infections with a shorter survival rate and a significantly higher bacterial load in vivo compared with wild-type mice. Studies have shown that platelet-rich plasma can inhibit bacterial growth [93]. One in vitro experiment identified that platelets damaged DNA and prevented the cell division of S aureus, and platelet-derived TGF-β may be involved in such a process [94]. Liu et al. [95] reported that platelets inhibited the survival of methicillin-resistant S aureus by promoting apoptosis.

In addition to their direct killing effect, platelets can enhance phagocytosis by macrophages to resist S aureus infection [96]. The phagocytic receptor Mac-1 on macrophages has a low affinity for negatively charged ligand sequences; thus, bacteria may escape from scavenging, whereas platelet-derived PF4 is a cationic peptide that localizes on the bacterial surface, thereby facilitating phagocytosis of S aureus by macrophages. In a mouse model of peritonitis, treatment with recombinant PF4 significantly improved the mouse survival rate [97]. In addition, platelets express HMGB1, a danger-associated molecular pattern protein that is released during infection. It has been shown that HMGB1 is associated with the release of platelet-derived chemokines (PF4 and RANTES) as well as platelet–neutrophil complex formation, which contributes to the clearance of S aureus infection [98].

Escherichia coli

E coli is a Gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the human gastrointestinal tract as a commensal, and some virulent strains can cause clinical conditions such as diarrhea, urinary tract infections, meningitis, and sepsis. However, in the elderly population and hospitalized patients, it is a common pathogen causing systemic infections, and its resistance to antibiotics is increasing, which creates a considerable infection burden in the clinic [99,100]. When infection occurs, it is often accompanied by severe sepsis and hemolytic uremic syndrome. In this process, the protective roles of platelets against such pathogenic E coli have been reported, so it is valuable to review the interaction between platelets and E coli.

LPS is an important structure located in the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria that can induce systemic inflammation and sepsis. LPS can bind to TLR4 on the surface of host cells and cause a series of immune responses [101,102]. Therefore, many studies have used this as a focal point for exploring the interactions between E coli and platelets. In addition to TLR4, platelets also express FcγRIIa, which is activated by anti-PF4/P IgG-coated bacteria, and then platelets are activated and secrete a variety of molecules in response to bacterial infection [36]. Through this pathway, platelets can kill E coli and protect the host from infection [17].

The activation and aggregation of platelets by E coli are dependent on the type of strain and the receptor involved. Experiments performed by Moriarty et al. [103] revealed that the O157:H7 strain induced platelet aggregation in a FcγRIIA- and αIIbβ3-dependent manner, whereas its cell wall component LPS was unable to activate platelets via the TLR4 receptor on the platelet surface. However, using strain O111, Matus et al. [104] found that this strain could interact with TLR4 to activate platelets and induce their procoagulant activity. Watson et al. [105] found that E coli strains CFT073 and RS218 activate platelets via IgG in an FcγRIIA-dependent manner. In addition, it was found that the antimicrobial effect of platelets depends on the type of strains involved. Ezzeroug Ezzraimi et al. [106] evaluated 10 clinical and laboratory E coli strains for their interaction with platelets and found that the platelet-mediated antimicrobial effects varied markedly among strains, which was hypothesized to be related to the structural differences of the O antigens of LPSs.

Regarding the interaction between platelets and E coli, the involvement of other immune components has also been characterized. Landsem et al. [107] reported that C3b, the cleavage component of complement C3, promotes E coli-induced platelet aggregation and that the glycoproteins GPIIb/IIIa and the complement receptor CR1 are involved in this process. It has been reported that in the presence of platelets, TLR4 and CD62P-mediated platelet–neutrophil interactions result in the rapid release of NETs, but without neutrophil death, whereas in the absence of platelets, neutrophils respond to E coli-derived LPS by “suicidally” releasing NETs [108].

Fungal infection

Candida albicans

C albicans is a component of the normal human flora and colonizes mucosal areas of the human body such as the oral cavity, intestines, and vagina. However, patients are susceptible to endogenous Candida infections when the body’s immune function declines or the application of antibiotics leads to flora dysbiosis. C albicans infections account for about half of cases of invasive candidiasis. However, in recent years, the prevalence of nonalbicans infections is on the rise globally, and new multidrug-resistant strains, such as C auris, have emerged as a threat to human health due to the widespread use of antifungal drugs [109].

Platelets display an ability to kill C albicans. Previous in vitro experiments found that platelets secreted microbicidal proteins (PMPs) that could kill Candida [110]. After pretreatment of platelets with cytochalasin D, which impairs the release of granules and deformation of platelets, a decrease in the killing ability mediated by thrombin-activated platelets was observed, suggesting that the platelet-mediated killing of C albicans may be dependent on the release of granules or also on mutual contact [111]. In addition to their direct killing effects, platelets may also protect the host by cooperating with other immune system components to limit C albicans invasion and spread. Wu et al. [112] reported that Candida-secreted candidysin activates platelets via the VWF receptor GP1bα, resulting in the release of the Wnt signaling pathway antagonist Dickkopf-1, which in turn drives Th2 and Th17 cell responses. These events are closely associated with a reduction of fungal burden in the lungs.

Previous studies showed that C albicans bound to platelets, causing morphological changes in platelets, as proven in in vivo experiments [113,114]. Subsequent studies reported that the interaction between C albicans and platelets inhibited certain functions of platelets, which likely favored the survival and dissemination of the fungus in the blood [114]. For example, Bertling et al. [115] reported that C albicans and its secreted gliotoxins inhibited platelet aggregation, binding to fibrinogen and GPIIb/IIIa receptor activity without affecting the expression of P-selectin (CD62P). Vancraeyneste et al. [116] reported that the short-chain product of the C albicans cell wall component β-1,3 glucan inhibits platelet aggregation, P-selectin expression, and interactions with neutrophils. Leroy et al. [117] found that chitin purified from C albicans reduced platelet adhesion, aggregation, and activation by decreasing P-selectin expression and intracellular Ca2+ influx, as well as increased expression of TLR8 on platelets. However, different conclusions about the effects of Candida on platelet functions have emerged. An in vitro analysis performed by Zheng et al. [118] found that Candida can induce platelet activation through the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in a GPIIb/IIIa-dependent manner but independent of the pattern recognition receptors TLR2 and TLR4, and that activated platelets enhance monocyte phagocytosis and increase their cytotoxicity against Candida. Eberl et al. [119] investigated the interaction of platelets with Candida mycobacteria, hyphae, biofilms, and secretory products using platelet-rich plasma and whole-blood models and found that platelets did not respond efficiently to prevent Candida infection.

Aspergillus fumigatus

Aspergillus is a common conditionally pathogenic fungus, widely distributed in nature. It produces a large number of spores suspended in the air, which can cause Aspergillus infections after entering the patient’s body via the respiratory tract. A fumigatus is a common pathogenic species and often causes pulmonary aspergillosis.

Analysis of clinical cases suggests that platelet counts may serve as an independent predictor of prognosis in invasive aspergillosis, possibly because higher platelet counts reflect good bone marrow reserve and enhanced immune function, which might be due to platelet-mediated protective roles against fungal infections [120,121]. A genomic analysis revealed specific transcriptional changes in A fumigatus after exposure to platelets, many of which were associated with metabolic and stress responses. This phenomenon reflected the antifungal activity of platelets [122]. Great evidence shows that platelets can adhere to Aspergillus and damage it by releasing particles [123]. In addition to their own immuno-antimicrobial functions, platelets were reported to enhance the antifungal effects of drugs by blocking or delaying fungal germination and hyphal elongation during the early stages of Aspergillus growth [124]. Meanwhile, platelets can also enhance leukocyte antimicrobial capacity [125]. Moreover, in vivo experiments in mice showed that platelets are effective in resisting Aspergillus invasion because of their crucial functions of hemostasis and maintenance of vascular integrity in the lungs [126].

Upon infection, A fumigatus conidia and hyphae can activate platelets and promote inflammatory responses and thrombosis, with hyphae showing a much higher activation efficiency [127]. Rambach et al. [128] studied the components of conidia and hyphae involved in platelet activation and showed that melanin and hydrophobic proteins of conidia and galactosaminogalactan (GAG) of hyphae were important virulence factors in regulating platelet activation. GAG is present in the cell wall of A fumigatus and can also be secreted around the hyphae [129]. Platelets can be activated by secreted GAG at some distance [130]. Subsequent studies have found that activation of platelets by GAG initiates platelet–complement interactions, which may further lead to hyperinflammation, thrombocytopenia, and thrombosis, all of which are important hallmarks of fatal invasive mycoses [131]. In addition to direct exposure, soluble factors secreted by A fumigatus activate platelets, and the resulting diffuse platelet activation may increase the risk of excessive inflammatory response and tissue damage[132].

Viral infection: severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2)

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was a worldwide pandemic that emerged in early 2020 with high morbidity and mortality. SARS-CoV-2 is the COVID-19 pathogen. It predominantly infects the lungs, causing respiratory symptoms; however, in patients with moderate and severe disease, it causes a disturbed systemic immune-inflammatory response and coagulopathy, which is associated with poor prognosis and even death. A hypercoagulable state and thrombosis are common and dangerous events in COVID-19, so the coagulation and immune functions of platelets during infection are of concern [133]. It was found that the procoagulant and proinflammatory activities of platelets were enhanced during infection, and large numbers of platelet–monocyte aggregates and platelet–neutrophil aggregates were detected in patients. Moreover, platelets from patients secreted more cytokines (such as IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-4, IL-10, IFN-α, and IFN-γ) and chemokines (MCP-1/CCL2) than healthy individuals. Platelets also cause an increase in circulating fibrinogen and VWF and contribute to the FXII-mediated coagulation pathway [134].

Cross-talk between platelets and other immune cells during infection may lead to excessive immune-inflammatory responses and cytokine storms in patients. Li et al. [135] reported that the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 binds to CD42b on platelets to activate platelets, which subsequently promotes platelet–monocyte communication through the binding of P-selectin/PGSL-1 and CD40L/CD40. After the formation of PMAs, monocytes are activated and induced to express tissue factor and secrete inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β. In addition, monocytes also exhibit increased binding to platelets, an interaction that amplifies the inflammatory response in vivo [136]. Elevated levels of platelet–neutrophil aggregates, which result in increased formation of NETs, also correlate positively with disease severity, with NETs contributing to pulmonary microthrombosis and lung injury in patients [137]. Activated platelets secrete microparticles containing PS, and Rausch et al. [138] found that, in the weeks after diagnosis of infection, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) bind PS+ PMPs in large numbers; high levels of PS+ PMP+ PBMCs correlate strongly with disease severity. Moreover, PS+ PMPs preferentially bind to CD8+ T cells, and since PMPs carry platelet-derived proteins, it is hypothesized that T lymphocytes can be introduced into platelet-mediated immune-inflammatory responses in this manner.

Conclusions and Perspectives

In summary, platelets express a variety of immune-related receptors that play important roles in pathogen recognition and immune activation. Platelet-secreted granular contents are involved in pathogen clearance and host immune responses. For example, by expressing P-selectin and releasing cytokines, platelets modulate host immune responses. Platelet–leukocyte aggregate formation can upregulate the proinflammatory functions of leukocytes. In addition, platelets also activate the complement system to amplify immune effects. Furthermore, platelets promote the release of NETs from neutrophils, which can promote immune thrombosis and exert antimicrobial effects. In conclusion, platelets are an important component of the body’s immune system, linking innate and adaptive immune processes. The cascade of immune effects triggered by platelet activation upon pathogenic infection can help to clear the pathogen and inhibit its spread. After analyzing the interactions among a variety of pathogens and platelets, it was found that platelet-mediated effects on pathogens are truly species-specific and closely correlated with the unique components of pathogens, and that this effect may vary among different strains of the same pathogen.

As double-edged swords, however, overactivation of platelets can also cause tissue damage, which is involved in the clinical pathogenesis caused by systemic infections.

Patients with severe COVID-19 often exhibit an abnormally hypercoagulable state and excessive inflammatory response, which can lead to microcirculatory thrombosis and multiorgan dysfunction. Platelets have increased proinflammatory and procoagulant activity during the infection and interact with immune cells to further amplify the immune-inflammatory response, and thus, platelet hyperreactivity is thought to correlate with poor clinical outcomes in COVID-19 patients. The platelet NLRP3 inflammasome has also been reported to be involved in amplifying inflammation, increasing vascular permeability, and driving pathologic thrombosis during infectious events. Therefore, searching for key targets to reduce the destructive inflammatory responses caused by platelet activation or directly targeting pathogenic molecules that trigger abnormal clotting responses will be a key task to reduce the organ damage and improve patients’ survival.
