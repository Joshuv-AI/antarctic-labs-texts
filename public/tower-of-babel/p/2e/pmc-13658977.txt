Anatomical risk factors for descending necrotizing mediastinitis in deep neck infections
Authors: Ozan Kuduban, Ali Bilal Ulas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDescending necrotizing mediastinitis (DNM) is among the most severe complications of deep neck infections (DNI), and the anatomical pathways that predispose to mediastinal extension remain incompletely characterized in large cohorts. We aimed to identify the anatomical risk factors associated with DNM in patients with DNI.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 496 consecutive patients hospitalized with DNI at a tertiary referral center between January 2015 and December 2025. Demographic data, the anatomical site of infection, the radiological dissemination pattern, surgical management, and clinical outcomes were analyzed, and patients who developed DNM were compared with those without mediastinal extension. DNM was diagnosed using the modified Estrera criteria.ResultsDNM developed in 9 of 496 patients (1.81%). Retropharyngeal space involvement was identified in 6 DNM cases (66.7%): An isolated retropharyngeal abscess in 4 patients (44.4%) and a combined retropharyngeal-parapharyngeal abscess in 2 patients (22.2%); the remaining cases arose from a parapharyngeal abscess, Ludwig’s angina, and a peritonsillar abscess (1 each, 11.1%). Although retropharyngeal abscess represented only 14 of 496 DNI cases (2.8%), it was the source of 44.4% of all DNM cases. Posterior mediastinal extension through the danger space was demonstrated in every retropharyngeal case. Diabetes mellitus was present in 2 DNM patients (22.2%). All 9 patients underwent aggressive surgical drainage and survived.ConclusionRetropharyngeal space involvement is the principal anatomical pathway for mediastinal extension in DNI. Early contrast-enhanced computed tomography and prompt multidisciplinary surgical drainage are essential to improve outcomes.

Deep neck infections (DNI) remain a clinical challenge despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and cross-sectional imaging.[1, 2] The deep cervical fascia divides the neck into communicating potential spaces that allow infection to spread rapidly between compartments, producing substantial morbidity and occasionally life-threatening complications.[3]

Among adults, odontogenic infections are the most frequent source of DNI.[4] Infection arising from the mandibular molars may extend through the submandibular and parapharyngeal spaces and, from there, through the retropharyngeal space toward the mediastinum. The close anatomical continuity between the oral cavity, the cervical fascial planes, and the mediastinum permits rapid caudal spread, particularly in patients with delayed presentation, poor oral hygiene, diabetes mellitus, or immunosuppression.[4]

Descending necrotizing mediastinitis (DNM) is the most serious complication of DNI. It develops when infection extends caudally through the cervical fascial planes into the mediastinum, producing diffuse mediastinal suppuration, necrosis, and sepsis.[5] Despite advances in critical care, thoracic surgery, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy, DNM is still associated with high mortality and prolonged hospitalization.[3, 5] Its pathogenesis is closely related to the architecture of the deep cervical fascia. The retropharyngeal space and the adjacent danger space, situated between the alar and prevertebral fascial layers, form a low-resistance corridor that extends from the skull base to the diaphragm.[6] Negative intrathoracic pressure generated during respiration and the effect of gravity further accelerate caudal progression, whereas parapharyngeal or carotid sheath involvement may allow direct extension into the superior mediastinum.[7]

Previous studies have identified diabetes mellitus, diagnostic delay, raised inflammatory markers, and multifocal cervical involvement as predictors of DNM.[2, 4] However, the specific anatomical routes of mediastinal extension and their clinical implications remain incompletely characterized, particularly in large cohorts. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is central to the management of DNI because it defines the extent of abscess formation, identifies the cervical spaces involved, and detects early mediastinal extension; it is regarded as the standard imaging modality for the diagnosis of DNM.[8] In the present study, we reviewed a retrospective cohort of 496 patients with DNI to determine the anatomical origin and radiological dissemination pattern of DNM and to identify the anatomical features associated with mediastinal extension.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary referral center and included consecutive patients hospitalized with DNI between January 2015 and December 2025. The study was reported in accordance with the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology statement for observational research. Patients were identified from the institutional electronic medical record database, and their medical records, radiological images, operative notes, and hospitalization data were reviewed.

A total of 496 consecutive patients with a radiologically confirmed diagnosis of DNI were included. Patients with superficial cervical infection without deep fascial space involvement, isolated odontogenic infection without radiological evidence of deep neck extension, postoperative or iatrogenic cervical infection, and patients with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Atatürk University Faculty of Medicine Institutional Ethics Committee (approval number: 2026/53, date: 02.04.2026). Because of the retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Patient anonymity was preserved throughout the study.

Diagnostic Evaluation

The diagnosis of DNI was based on clinical examination, laboratory findings, and contrast-enhanced cervical CT. Radiological assessment recorded the cervical fascial spaces involved, the presence of abscess and gas formation, airway compromise, and any mediastinal extension. Thoracic CT was performed when mediastinal extension was suspected based on clinical deterioration, thoracic symptoms, persistent sepsis, or findings on cervical imaging. DNM was diagnosed using the modified Estrera criteria, namely clinical evidence of severe cervical infection, radiological evidence of a mediastinal collection, pneumomediastinum, or fat stranding, and intraoperative confirmation of mediastinal suppuration or necrosis.[1]

Anatomical Classification

DNI were classified according to the primary anatomical focus as retropharyngeal abscess, parapharyngeal abscess, peritonsillar abscess, submandibular abscess, Ludwig’s angina, parotid abscess, submental abscess, or multi-space infection. For the analysis of mediastinal extension, patients were divided into two groups: Those with DNI without mediastinal extension and those who developed DNM.

Treatment

All patients received empirical broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotic therapy with anaerobic coverage immediately after diagnosis; regimens were subsequently adjusted according to culture and susceptibility results when available. Intraoperative specimens were routinely sent for aerobic and anaerobic culture. Surgical drainage was performed in patients with abscess formation, airway compromise, septic deterioration, or an inadequate response to medical therapy, using an external cervical approach appropriate to the compartment involved. Patients with DNM were managed jointly by otolaryngology, thoracic surgery, anesthesiology, and intensive care teams; mediastinal drainage was performed through a transcervical and/or transthoracic approach according to the extent of mediastinal involvement.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 31.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum-maximum) according to their distribution, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because only nine patients developed DNM, formal multivariable analysis was not performed, and intergroup comparisons were interpreted as exploratory.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

A total of 496 patients with DNI were included. The mean age was 29±18 years (median 27, range 0-75); 261 patients (52.6%) were male, and 235 (47.4%) were female. The distribution of the primary anatomical focus is shown in Table 1. The most common foci were peritonsillar abscess (181 patients, 36.5%) and submandibular abscess (180 patients, 36.3%), followed by parapharyngeal abscess (53 patients, 10.7%) and Ludwig’s angina (28 patients, 5.6%). Retropharyngeal abscess was uncommon, accounting for only 14 patients (2.8%). Infection was confined to a single deep neck space in 483 patients (97.4%), whereas 13 patients (2.6%) had multi-space involvement at presentation. The distribution of disease extent did not differ significantly between men and women (chi-square test, χ2=2.595, p=0.107), and age did not differ significantly between the sexes (Mann-Whitney U test, Z =-1.086, p=0.277).

DNM

DNM developed in 9 of the 496 patients, an overall incidence of 1.81%. Seven of the 9 patients (77.8%) were female, and 2 (22.2%) were male. The anatomical origin of DNM is summarized in Table 2. Mediastinitis arose from an isolated retropharyngeal abscess in 4 patients (44.4%), a combined retropharyngeal-parapharyngeal abscess in 2 patients (22.2%), an isolated parapharyngeal abscess in 1 patient (11.1%), Ludwig’s angina in 1 patient (11.1%), and a peritonsillar abscess in 1 patient (11.1%). Retropharyngeal space involvement, whether isolated or combined, was therefore present in 6 of the 9 DNM cases (66.7%).

Although retropharyngeal abscess accounted for only 14 of all 496 DNI cases (2.8%), 4 of these 14 patients (28.6%) developed DNM, compared with 3.6% or less for every other anatomical focus (Table 2). In all patients with retropharyngeal involvement, CT demonstrated posterior mediastinal extension through the danger space, confirming this fascial corridor as the dominant route of caudal dissemination (Figure 1). Diabetes mellitus was present in 2 of the 9 DNM patients (22.2%).

All 9 patients underwent immediate aggressive surgical drainage and debridement after radiological confirmation, combining cervical drainage with mediastinal drainage through a transcervical and/or transthoracic approach according to the extent of involvement. All 9 patients survived and were discharged after recovery, giving an in-hospital mortality of 0%.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 496 patients with DNI, DNM developed in 9 patients (1.81%), an incidence consistent with the 1-5% range reported in contemporary series.[2, 3, 9] The most important finding was that retropharyngeal space involvement was present in two-thirds of DNM cases, even though retropharyngeal abscess represented fewer than 3% of all DNI in the cohort. This disproportionate contribution identifies the retropharyngeal space as the principal anatomical gateway to the mediastinum.[1, 4]

The anatomical basis of this finding is well established. The retropharyngeal space is continuous with the danger space, which lies between the alar and prevertebral fascial layers and extends without an effective barrier from the skull base to the diaphragm. Infection within this corridor can descend rapidly into the posterior mediastinum under the influence of gravity and the negative intrathoracic pressure generated during respiration.[2, 3, 5] Consistent with this mechanism, CT demonstrated posterior mediastinal extension in every retropharyngeal case in our series (Figure 1). Retropharyngeal abscess has repeatedly been identified as a major risk factor for DNM in clinical series and systematic reviews.[3, 6]

The clinicopathological continuity between cervical infection and mediastinal necrosis was first defined by Estrera et al.[1] and subsequent studies have confirmed that delayed diagnosis and inadequate drainage are the strongest determinants of mortality.[3, 5, 7] Despite advances in imaging and critical care, DNM still carries a mortality of 10-40% in contemporary series.[2, 4, 6, 8] Against this background, the absence of mortality among our 9 patients is notable. It most likely reflects early use of contrast-enhanced CT, prompt broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, and aggressive surgical drainage performed jointly by otolaryngology and thoracic surgery teams. This result should nonetheless be interpreted with caution, given the small number of DNM cases.

Our data also suggest that multi-space cervical infection represents a more aggressive phenotype. Although only 2.6% of the cohort had multi-space involvement at presentation, 2 of the 9 DNM cases (22.2%) arose from a combined retropharyngeal-parapharyngeal infection. Extensive fascial involvement has been associated with a higher bacterial load, more extensive tissue necrosis, and a greater risk of thoracic extension.[5, 7, 9]

Diabetes mellitus was present in 2 of the 9 DNM patients. Diabetes is a well-recognized risk factor for severe DNI and its complications;[10, 11] impaired neutrophil chemotaxis, reduced tissue perfusion, and immune dysfunction may facilitate rapid spread through the cervical fascial planes.[11, 12]

An unexpected observation was the female predominance among DNM patients (77.8%), which contrasts both with the slight male predominance in the overall cohort and with the male predominance usually reported for DNM. Given the small number of events, this most likely represents a chance finding rather than a true sex difference and should not be over-interpreted; larger multicenter studies would be required to determine whether sex genuinely influences the risk of mediastinal extension.

Isolated peritonsillar abscess accounted for only one DNM case. Peritonsillar infections are usually diagnosed early because of prominent symptoms, which limits deep fascial spread;[13] however, in advanced cases, infection may extend to the parapharyngeal space and descend toward the mediastinum.[8, 9] One patient with Ludwig’s angina developed mediastinitis; although mediastinal spread from Ludwig’s angina is uncommon, delayed intervention can lead to airway compromise and descending infection.[14, 15, 16]

Aggressive surgical drainage remains the cornerstone of DNM treatment.[6, 7] Cervical drainage alone may be sufficient for disease confined to the superior mediastinum, whereas combined cervical and thoracic drainage is required for extensive or posterior mediastinal involvement; both video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery and open thoracotomy have been used successfully for thoracic drainage, with the choice guided by the extent and location of disease.[17] Selective tracheostomy may be required for airway compromise; because a tracheostomy stoma may theoretically promote downward contamination of the mediastinum, this decision should be individualized. Multidisciplinary management involving otolaryngology, thoracic surgery, radiology, anesthesiology, and intensive care is essential to reduce mortality and morbidity.[4, 7, 18, 19]

Microbiologically, DNI and DNM are typically polymicrobial infections involving aerobic and anaerobic oral flora; empirical broad-spectrum regimens with anaerobic coverage are therefore appropriate until culture results are available.[10, 12, 20]

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective single-center design may introduce selection bias and limits generalizability. The number of DNM cases was small, which precluded formal multivariable analysis and limited the statistical power of subgroup comparisons; the apparent female predominance and the absence of mortality should be interpreted in this light. Comorbidity, laboratory, and microbiological data were not uniformly available for the whole cohort, and the anatomical Estrera subtype (I, IIA, or IIB) could not be assigned systematically. Finally, the 11-year study period encompasses possible changes in imaging, antimicrobial, and surgical practice. Nevertheless, the large cohort and the detailed anatomical characterization of mediastinal extension are strengths of the study.

DNM is a rare but life-threatening complication of DNI. Retropharyngeal space involvement is the dominant anatomical route of mediastinal extension, and multi-space cervical infection further increases the risk. Early contrast-enhanced cervical and thoracic CT, prompt broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, and timely multidisciplinary surgical drainage are essential to improve outcomes.
