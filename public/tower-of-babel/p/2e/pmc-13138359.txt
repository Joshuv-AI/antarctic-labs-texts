Development and Validation of a Deep Learning‐Based Facial Weakness Score for Objective Assessment in Facioscapulohumeral Muscular Dystrophy
Authors: T G J Loonen, T C ten Harkel, S C C Vincenten, C G C Horlings, C H G Beurskens, S Knuijt, G W A M Padberg, B G M van Engelen, T J J Maal, K Mul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsFacioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) is a muscle disease that leads, among other manifestations, to facial weakness. This weakness can severely impact communication and quality of life, yet it remains under‐researched with limited objective clinical measures. Current manual scoring methods are subjective and exhibit suboptimal inter‐observer agreement. This study aimed to develop and validate a deep learning‐based facial weakness score (DLFWS) as an objective clinical outcome measure for facial weakness in FSHD.MethodsOne hundred and twenty‐two genetically confirmed FSHD patients and 56 controls were recruited. Sixty‐four patients had a 5‐year follow‐up visit. Video recordings of participants performing seven facial exercises, each repeated three times, were analyzed using a convolutional neural network. The deep learning‐based facial weakness score (DLFWS) was trained by comparing start and end frames from these exercises with the manual facial weakness scores (MFWS) assigned by three experienced observers. Pearson correlation coefficients and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were used to evaluate the DLFWS's performance and reliability.ResultsThe DLFWS showed a strong correlation with the MFWS across exercises, with a mean Pearson correlation of 0.79 and a maximum of 0.85 for individual exercises. The DLFWS demonstrated excellent test–retest reliability, with an ICC of 0.90. Over a 5‐year follow‐up period, no significant progression of facial weakness was detected.DiscussionThe DLFWS provides a reliable and objective assessment of facial weakness in FSHD patients. This automated tool holds potential for widespread clinical and research applications, enabling standardized assessment of facial weakness in FSHD.Keywords: facial weakness, facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy, outcome measures

Introduction

Facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy (FSHD) is an inherited muscle disorder that causes weakness and wasting of skeletal muscles. Facial weakness is a highly characteristic sign of FSHD. The severity of facial weakness varies between patients from subtle asymmetry to myopathic facies [1, 2]. Traditionally, the circular muscles around the eyes and mouth (orbicularis oculi and oris, respectively) and the muscle that raises the corner of the mouth (zygomaticus major) are described as most commonly affected [3]. More recent work suggests that other facial muscles such as the buccinator muscle may be involved in FSHD as well [1, 2]. Depending on the severity, facial weakness can lead to difficulties with speaking and swallowing [2, 4, 5]. Moreover, it impairs the ability to produce facial expressions, often hindering non‐verbal communication [6].

FSHD patients have made us aware that facial weakness is a frequent symptom with important psychosocial consequences, but it is an understudied subject in research studies and a neglected symptom in the consulting room [6, 7, 8, 9]. As we lack tools to accurately measure facial weakness, little is known about the progression of facial weakness and no interventional studies on FSHD have included it as an outcome.

To include facial weakness as an outcome measure in future pharmaceutical and intervention‐based trials, outcome measures regarding facial weakness are required. There are a number of patient‐reported instruments for measuring facial weakness, including generic instruments such as the Facial Disability Index [10] and the FSHD‐specific Rasch‐built Facial Function Scale [11]. However, these instruments measure the experienced symptom burden, which does not necessarily correlate with the degree of facial weakness [6, 11, 12].

Several alternative approaches have been explored to assess facial muscle involvement in FSHD without relying on patient self‐report. One such approach is the Iowa Oral Performance Instrument (IOPI); however, its use is limited to a small subset of facial muscles, and patients often find the assessment burdensome [2]. Ultrasound‐based measurements of facial muscles have also been investigated, but this technique is still under development, is time‐consuming, and requires substantial operator training to achieve reliable and reproducible results [13].

An attempt to develop a physician‐reported facial weakness score specifically for FSHD resulted in suboptimal agreement between observers, making it poorly suited as a clinical outcome measure in clinical trials or other longitudinal studies [14]. The complexity of the face and its movements apparently renders it difficult for physicians to grade. An alternative approach is to apply artificial intelligence to develop algorithms that can serve as biomarkers for the degree of facial weakness in patients. Such automated scores will help overcome challenges in reliability and inter‐rater variability in scoring facial weakness.

In this study, we aim to develop a novel clinical outcome measure for facial weakness in patients with FSHD using a deep learning–based analysis of facial movements.

Methods

Population

Patients for this study were recruited from a cohort study at the Neurology department of the Radboud University Medical Center (Nijmegen, the Netherlands), including genetically confirmed FSHD patients aged 18 years and older [15]. The control group consisted of study visit companions aged 18 years or older who were willing to participate. Any medical condition that influences facial function was an exclusion criterion for the control group. Baseline visits were performed in 2014–2015 and 5‐year follow‐up visits in 2019–2020. The regional medical ethics committee (CMO Arnhem‐Nijmegen) approved the study protocol and all participants provided informed consent and signed consent‐to‐disclose forms.

Data Acquisition

Participants were videotaped performing seven different facial expression exercises in random order: closing the eyes gently, closing the eyes firmly, raising the eyebrows, frowning, pursing the lips, showing the teeth, and puffing the cheeks [14]. Each exercise started from a relaxed neutral state of facial muscles until the maximum movement of the exercise and was performed three times consecutively, resulting in 21 records per video.

The videos were recorded with a Canon PowerShot SX280 HS camera (Canon Inc., Tokyo, Japan), placed 70 cm in front of the participant. Recording resolution was set to 1920 × 1680 at 60 frames per second.

Clinical disease severity measures, including the FSHD evaluation score and the Ricci score, were obtained as part of the cohort study protocol and were available for all patients included in the present analysis. The FSHD evaluation score is a fifteen‐point scale which grades muscle weakness in six body regions with higher scores indicating more severe weakness [16]. The Ricci score is a ten‐point scale which grades overall disease severity with higher scores indicating more severe weakness [17].

Manual Assessment of Videos

All facial expression exercises on the videos were semi‐quantitatively assessed by three observers independently using the individual exercise scores of the FSHD Facial Weakness Score (FWS) [14]. This score rates the facial exercises for the left and right side of the face on a 4‐point score from 0 (normal movement) to 3 (inability to initiate or perform a movement). Scores for left and right and for all observers were averaged into one score ranging from 0 to 3 per exercise per video and these scores will be referred to as the manual facial weakness score (MFWS).

Pre‐Processing

For all 21 records, two frames were selected; a neutral frame at the start of the exercise and a maximum frame at the end of the exercise where maximum movement of the pose was seen. Automatic landmark detection was used on both images to find the left and right exocanthions in order to automatically crop the images to a 112 × 112 pixel image [18]. Optical flow image registration was used between the two images to correct for possible rotation movement between the start of the exercise and the end [19]. Next, an absolute difference image was generated between the start frame and the motion corrected maximum frame (Figure S1). The pre‐processing steps resulted in three normalized images per facial exercise per patient with pixel values between 0 and 1 and a size of 112 × 112 × 3 for each input. Pixel values were normalized using min‐max scaling.

Architecture

The convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture consisted of 16 weight layers, with 13 convolution layers and 3 fully connected layers and was based on CNN configuration D as described by Simonyan & Zisserman [20]. Alterations to the architecture were made because of the relatively small amount of data similarly to the work performed by ten Harkel et al. [21]. The input is a concatenation of the three images of the pre‐processing step with Gaussian noise layer applied to the individual images. After concatenation of the images, three data augmentation layers were added: a random horizontal flip, random zoom and random rotation, and only used during training of the model. The modified version of the CNN configuration D from Simonyan & Zisserman with a kernel size reduction by a factor of four followed the data augmentation [20]. Further adjustments were the addition of a kernel and bias constraint with a maximum norm value of three and a batch normalization layer before each maxpool layer, fully connected layers of 1024 nodes with dropout and batch normalization layers after each fully connected layer. For the output layer a linear activation function followed by a Gaussian noise layer was used. The architecture was finalized with a combination of the log‐cosh loss function and an Adam optimizer [21].

Training

All seven facial expression exercises were trained separately in five folds. Stratified k‐fold was used for the division of the dataset using 20% of the data points for validation. In a subset of the participants, both a baseline and a follow‐up recording were available. All data points (baseline and follow‐up) of one subject were always included in either the training or validation group to prevent the training of facial recognition. Stratification was done based on the MFWS and categorized in five bins of equal size, ensuring the same distribution of MFWS scores in the training and validation groups. Across all subsets, all possible manual score values were present. The parameters for data augmentation ranged from 0.8 to 1.2 for the zoom and−20 to 20 degrees for the rotation. A patience of 1000 was used for early stopping and a batch size of 32 [22]. A cyclic triangular learning rate with a base learning rate of 1e‐8, a max learning rate of 1e‐3, and a step size of 4 × (length of training data/batch size) was used [23].

Analysis

The performance of the deep learning algorithm was evaluated by comparing the automatically generated scores with the manual scores given by the three experienced observers. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for each exercise. For test–retest reliability, the intraclass correlation coefficient was calculated between the repeats of each exercise, performed for the multiple repeats. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To assess the practical applicability of the trained model, inference was performed on a standard consumer‐grade desktop computer equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3070 GPU.

Results

Data Acquisition

One hundred and twenty‐two FSHD patients and 56 controls were included in this study. Sixty‐four patients were included in the baseline visit and the 5‐year follow‐up visits and therefore had two videos, resulting in 186 videos of 122 unique FSHD patients. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1. The 242 videos (patients and controls) with seven exercises repeated three times resulted in 5082 records. Ninety‐six records were excluded after manual evaluation of the images due to various reasons: too much rotation of the face between the neutral image and the maximum movement of the exercise (55%); one of the three repeats was performed more poorly than the other two on which the FWS was based on (25%); no completely neutral frame was obtained before the performed exercise could be selected (20%).

Intraclass Correlation of the Deep Learning Based Facial Weakness Score

The intraclass correlation coefficient for the DLFWS among multiple repetitions of the same exercise by the same subjects was 0.90, with a confidence interval ranging from 0.89 to 0.91. This finding underscores the algorithm's exceptional reproducibility, falling within the range of good to excellent consistency in score generation.

Follow‐Up Results

No statistically significant change in the degree of facial weakness was seen in the MFWS or DLFWS between the first visit and the 5‐year follow up visits of 64 patients. The largest potential worsening per individual exercise was found at showing the teeth with a mean difference of 0.21 and a confidence interval between −0.56 and 1.42. The mean facial score from the FSHD evaluation scale increased from 1.2 ± 0.6 at baseline to 1.4 ± 0.6 at 5‐year follow‐up (p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this study, we introduce a novel clinical outcome measure for grading facial weakness in individuals with FSHD. The “deep learning facial weakness score” (DLFWS) was capable of capturing different degrees of facial weakness and showed good to excellent convergent validity and reproducibility.

Our method employed deep learning to train a facial weakness score, which exhibited a robust positive correlation with the manually assessed facial weakness score for all exercises except one. In the case of gently closing the eyes, we observe a moderate positive correlation. This divergence in correlation may be attributed to the use of a four‐point scale for the manual evaluation of exercises, which may have been too finely graded for this specific exercise. Consequently, the quality of input data for training this exercise was somewhat compromised as the raters had difficulty discriminating between response options.

Previously, we reported an intraclass correlation of 0.4 among observers for the MFWS, indicating that this method yielded unreliable results for potential clinical outcome measurements [14]. In contrast, our current study demonstrates a mean intraclass correlation of 0.9 for the DLFWS between repeated assessments of the same exercise by the same subject. This high intraclass correlation shows the great benefit of this automated score to overcome both intra‐ and inter‐rater variability. This finding is further supported by the mean attention maps, which illustrate that the model focuses on the appropriate regions within the images to determine the scores. This is of great importance when using this outcome assessment in longitudinal and multicenter studies.

Throughout a 5‐year period, our examination using the MFWS and DLFWS did not reveal significant progression of facial weakness in patients with FSHD. While the data do not allow for definitive conclusions, trends in the FSHD evaluation scores may indicate some progression of facial weakness in the studied cohort. This observation raises the possibility that facial weakness may progress so gradually that it is difficult to detect within a 5‐year follow‐up period, or that progression only occurs during certain time intervals in life [24]. Alternatively, the results could suggest that the method employed to assess facial weakness lacked the sensitivity required to measure its progression over 5 years, or that the sample size was too limited to draw definitive conclusions. In spite of the absence of observable change at this juncture, it remains imperative to continue pursuing objective measurements of facial weakness in patients. Such measurements are necessary for evaluating participants in clinical trials and assessing a potential benefit of therapeutic interventions on facial weakness.

The introduction of an automated facial weakness system for FSHD patients, as established in this study, lays the groundwork for a more efficient assessment of disease severity in this population. The system's potential for global implementation is noteworthy, as it only requires a camera and a standardized image capture protocol. The widespread availability of high‐resolution cameras in modern cell phones further suggests that associated costs could be kept to a minimum. The low inference time per exercise demonstrates minimal computational overhead and supports the feasibility of real‐time or near–real‐time clinical application. Nevertheless, before this system can be seamlessly integrated into clinical or home settings, its robustness must be thoroughly examined. Factors such as poorly executed exercises, patient movement, camera properties, and background variations need future investigation to ensure the system's reliability and accuracy in diverse scenarios. Additionally, conducting an ablation study could be valuable for identifying the most critical features and components of the model, potentially simplifying the system without compromising performance. To enhance the model's performance and explore the full potential of an automated grading system, a larger sample size is advisable. A more extensive and diverse participant pool would yield valuable insights into the system's performance across a spectrum of conditions, strengthening the study's conclusions.

Looking ahead, future research could explore the incorporation of moving images or 3D imaging to assess their potential for improving the facial weakness score. Additionally, investigating the applicability of a similar system for assessing facial weakness in other neuromuscular disorders holds promise for expanding the scope of this technology and its positive impact on healthcare provision and research.

In conclusion, this research serves as the groundwork for an automated facial weakness grading system tailored for patients with FSHD. The study demonstrated a strong overall correlation with the manual grading system, greatly surpassing the intraclass correlation observed among various professional medical observers. Despite the absence of significant differences in facial weakness over a 5‐year follow‐up, the system remains highly valuable for conducting more extensive follow‐up studies, tracking results of clinical trials or evaluating the impact of therapeutic interventions.
