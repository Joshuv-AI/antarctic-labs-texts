Remnant cholesterol to high density lipoprotein cholesterol ratio is correlated with bone mineral density: Analysis of NHANES 2011–2018
Authors: Junpeng Qiu, Fangjun Xiao, Yaoxin Ao, Jiangfeng Lv, Muyuan Hou, Aoqi Xu, Yifei Liufu, Junxing Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence suggests that lipid metabolism plays a critical role in bone homeostasis. The ratio of remnant cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (RC/HDL-C) has recently been proposed as a novel indicator of metabolic risk; however, its relationship with bone mineral density (BMD) remains unclear. This study aimed to explore the association between RC/HDL-C and total BMD in a nationally representative population. Data were derived from the 2011–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, including 3688 US adults aged 20 to 59 years. The RC/HDL-C ratio was calculated as (total cholesterol – HDL-C – low-density lipoprotein cholesterol)/HDL-C. Total BMD was measured using dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry. Multivariable linear regression models were used to examine the association between RC/HDL-C and BMD, adjusting for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates. Nonlinear associations were explored via smooth curve fitting and threshold effect analyses. Subgroup and interaction analyses were also conducted. A significant inverse association was observed between RC/HDL-C and total BMD. After full adjustment, each 1-unit increase in RC/HDL-C was associated with a 0.019 g/cm² decrease in BMD (β = –0.019; 95% confidence intervals (CI): –0.027 to –0.010; P < .0001). Quartile analysis revealed a dose–response relationship, with the highest quartile showing the strongest negative association (β = –0.029; 95% CI: –0.039 to –0.020; P < .0001). A nonlinear relationship was identified, with an inflection point at 0.677; below this value, the negative association was more pronounced (β = –0.049; 95% CI: –0.068 to –0.030; P < .0001). Subgroup analyses confirmed consistent associations across most groups, and a significant interaction was found with smoking status (P for interaction .05). RC/HDL-C was inversely associated with BMD, with stronger associations observed in smoking status. These findings highlight the RC/HDL-C ratio as a potential predictor for bone health risk stratification and warrant further investigation in longitudinal and mechanistic studies.

1. Introduction

Osteoporosis is a systemic metabolic bone disease characterized by decreased bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, leading to increased fracture risk. It poses a serious threat to the health of middle aged and older adults worldwide.[1] Bone mineral density (BMD), a central diagnostic criterion for osteoporosis, is influenced by multiple factors, including age, genetic predisposition, hormonal status, nutritional intake, and chronic diseases.[2–4] In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to the role of dyslipidemia, particularly metabolic disturbances associated with chronic low-grade inflammation, in bone metabolism. Previous studies have demonstrated that systemic inflammation can influence BMD by regulating the bone remodeling cycle mediated by osteoblasts and osteoclasts. For instance, some investigations have identified associations between inflammatory markers – including the platelet-lymphocyte ratio – and BMD, suggesting their potential as predictive indicators for osteoporosis.[5,6] Abnormal lipid profiles may accelerate skeletal degeneration through enhanced oxidative stress, suppressed osteoblastic activity, and increased bone resorption.[7,8] Although traditional risk factors for BMD have been extensively studied, the mechanisms by which lipid metabolism disorders affect bone health remain incompletely understood.

Remnant cholesterol (RC), a component of triglyceride-rich lipoproteins such as very low-density lipoproteins and intermediate-density lipoproteins, represents the cholesterol content within these atherogenic particles. RC has emerged as a significant risk marker for cardiovascular disease and is considered an independent predictor of atherosclerosis.[9,10] Crucially, elevated RC levels are often intertwined with pro-inflammatory states and metabolic disorders. Elevated RC levels often coexist with metabolic disorders such as fatty liver, insulin resistance, hypertention and hyperglycemia, this association may be mediated through inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and other mechanisms.[11–14] Furthermore, the interplay between RC and systemic inflammation has been highlighted as a potential independent factor for cardiovascular risk in various populations.[15] In contrast, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) exerts atheroprotective effects through reverse cholesterol transport, antioxidant activity, and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. HDL-C plays a pivotal role in clearing excess cholesterol from arterial walls, reducing oxidative damage, and inhibiting LDL oxidation, thereby mitigating atherosclerotic risk.[16,17] Recently, the RC/HDL-C ratio has been proposed as a novel biomarker for evaluating metabolic and cardiovascular risk, as it reflects the imbalance between pro-atherogenic and antiatherogenic lipid fractions. Emerging evidence suggests that a higher RC/HDL-C ratio is closely associated with the development of various metabolic disorders.[18–20] In the context of bone metabolism, this ratio may influence BMD indirectly by modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and osteoblast function. Investigating the association between the RC/HDL-C ratio and BMD may provide new insights into the interplay between lipid metabolism and bone health and potentially offer a novel biomarker for osteoporosis risk assessment.

Therefore, the present study aimed to explore the association between the RC/HDL-C ratio and total BMD in a nationally representative adult population using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). This study seeks to elucidate the potential metabolic-bone link and provide new evidence to support public health initiatives and clinical strategies for osteoporosis prevention.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data source and study population

This study was based on data from the NHANES from 2011 to 2018 and aimed to investigate the association between the RC/HDL-C ratio and total BMD among US adults. NHANES (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.htm) is a nationwide, cross-sectional health survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, which includes health interviews, physical examinations, laboratory measurements, and imaging assessments. The survey employs a complex, multistage probability sampling design to obtain a representative sample of the US population. The NHANES protocol was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study complied with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. For further details regarding the ethical review process, please refer to: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/erb.html.

In this study, we included 3688 participants aged 20 to 59 years from the 2011 to 2018 NHANES cycles (Fig. 1). Participants were excluded if they lacked data on RC/HDL-C (including total cholesterol, HDL-C, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C]), total BMD, or any key covariates. After applying these exclusion criteria, the final analytical sample ensured data completeness and robustness of the findings.

2.2. Study variables

The RC/HDL-C ratio was the primary exposure variable and was calculated as (total cholesterol – HDL-C – LDL-C)/HDL-C,[19] expressed in mmol/L. Serum HDL-C levels were measured using the Roche Cobas 6000 chemistry analyzer. The RC/HDL-C ratio was analyzed both as a continuous variable and in quartiles. The outcome variable was total BMD, measured by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry using standardized protocols and interpreted by certified radiology technicians under strict quality control procedures.

2.3. Covariates

To reduce potential confounding, multiple covariates were included in the analysis. These included demographic factors (age categorized as <40 or ≥40 years, sex, and race); lifestyle behaviors (alcohol use, categorized as ≥4–5 drinks/d or <4–5 drinks/d, and smoking, categorized as ≥100 cigarettes or <100 cigarettes in their entire life); and clinical variables, including body mass index (BMI), total serum calcium, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, and histories of diabetes, hypertension, chronic kidney disease (CKD), thyroid problems, and cancer.

2.4. Statistical analysis

To account for the complex sampling design of NHANES, all statistical analyses incorporated sample weights, clustering, and stratification. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests and reported as percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on distribution. Between-group comparisons were conducted using Student t test for normally distributed variables or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normal distributions. Multivariable linear regression models were constructed to assess the association between RC/HDL-C and total BMD, reporting β coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Four models were used: an unadjusted model; a model adjusted for age, sex, and race/ethnicity; and a fully adjusted model incorporating all covariates. To evaluate potential nonlinear relationships, smooth curve fitting was performed based on the fully adjusted model, and threshold effect analysis was conducted to identify inflection points. Additionally, subgroup and interaction analyses were carried out for variables such as age, sex, race, smoking status, alcohol use, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, CKD, thyroid disease, and cancer. All analyses were performed using EmpowerStats (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions Inc., Boston) and R software (www.R-project.org), with 2-sided P-values <.05 considered statistically significant.

Significant differences were observed across quartiles in serum calcium, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase levels, daily alcohol intake, annual smoking volume, and cancer status (all P < .05). When analyzed by quartiles of RC/HDL-C, a clear dose–response relationship was observed. Compared to the lowest quartile (Q1), participants in Q2, Q3, and Q4 exhibited significantly lower total BMD in the fully adjusted model: Q2 (β = –0.013; 95% CI: –0.022 to –0.004; P < .05), Q3 (β = –0.013; 95% CI: –0.022 to –0.004; P < .05), and Q4 (β = –0.029; 95% CI: –0.039 to –0.020; P < .0001).

The fitted smooth curve further illustrated a nonlinear negative association between RC/HDL-C and total BMD after adjusting for all covariates (Fig. 2). Threshold effect analysis (Table 3) identified an inflection point at an RC/HDL-C value of 0.677. Below this threshold, the negative association was more pronounced (β = –0.049; 95% CI: –0.068 to –0.030; P < .0001).

3.3. Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of the association between RC/HDL-C and total BMD across different populations (Table 4). The negative association remained stable across subgroups defined by sex, age, race/ethnicity, smoking status, alcohol consumption, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, CKD, and cancer, although some associations did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited sample sizes within subgroups. Notably, a significant interaction was observed in the subgroup stratified by smoking status (P for interaction <.05), suggesting a stronger negative association between RC/HDL-C and BMD among smokers.

4. Discussion

This study is the first to systematically evaluate the association between the RC/HDL-C ratio and total BMD in a nationally representative adult population. We identified a significant inverse relationship, with a clear dose–response pattern and a stronger effect observed below a threshold of 0.677. The association remained robust across most subgroups and appeared stronger among smokers, suggesting that smoking status may modify this relationship. These findings provide new epidemiological evidence linking lipid imbalance to bone loss and highlight RC/HDL-C as a potential risk indicator for impaired bone health.

These findings broaden current insights into the role of lipid metabolism in bone health. Historically, lipid abnormalities have been primarily associated with cardiovascular risk. However, emerging evidence indicates they also play a critical role in skeletal health. Population-based studies have reported that LDL-C is inversely associated with BMD,[21] while high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) shows a positive association with bone density.[22] Similarly, other lipid-related markers, such as ApoB/ApoA1 ratios, have been linked to metabolic disorder in epidemiological analyses.[23] Our results are consistent with these prior observations in terms of direction, but they extend the literature by showing that the RC/HDL-C ratio – an integrated measure of atherogenic versus protective lipoproteins – is also inversely associated with BMD. Because RC/HDL-C captures both RC (pro-inflammatory, pro-oxidative)[24] and HDL-C (anti-inflammatory, protective)[25] in a single index, it may provide a more comprehensive assessment of lipid-related skeletal risk compared with individual lipid measures.

Our study is the first to demonstrate a significant inverse association between elevated RC/HDL-C ratios and BMD, suggesting that RC/HDL-C may serve as an indicator of bone metabolism abnormalities. This negative association is biologically plausible, as RC has been shown to promote systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are known to impair osteoblast function and enhance osteoclast activity.[26–28] Conversely, HDL-C is widely recognized for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, which may protect bone metabolism.[29,30] Thus, the RC/HDL-C ratio could reflect an imbalance between bone-resorptive and bone-protective influences, explaining its negative correlation with BMD. Previous studies have shown that elevated RC/HDL-C is closely associated with various metabolic disorders, including hyperuricemia, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.[31,32] The significant association between RC/HDL-C and total BMD observed in this study may reflect a pathological state arising from a disrupted balance between “bone resorption-promoting” and “bone-protective” factors.

Notably, a significant interaction was observed in the subgroup stratified by smoking status, suggesting that the negative association between RC/HDL-C and BMD appeared stronger among smokers. Previous studies have shown that smoking is associated not only with lower BMD but also with adverse lipid metabolism, characterized by elevated RC and reduced HDL-C levels.[33,34] In addition, smoking is known to induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which can impair osteoblast function and enhance osteoclast activity.[35–39] These combined effects on lipid metabolism and bone remodeling may partly explain the stronger inverse association observed among smokers in our analyses. This highlights the need for individualized interventions, particularly for smokers, to mitigate the compounded risk to both bone and cardiovascular health.

From a clinical and public health perspective, the RC/HDL-C ratio – readily available from standard lipid profiles – may serve as a simple, cost-effective indicator associated with the risk of low BMD. If validated in future prospective and mechanistic studies, it could be incorporated into early screening tools for osteoporosis, particularly in populations with metabolic abnormalities or high cardiovascular risk. The observed interaction with smoking suggests that targeted interventions may be especially important in smokers to mitigate combined metabolic and skeletal risks. Additionally, our study population was restricted to adults aged 20 to 59 years, because NHANES provides total BMD data only within this age range. While this design ensured data consistency, it limits the generalizability of our findings to older adults, who are at the highest risk of osteoporosis and fracture. Nonetheless, focusing on younger and middle aged adults remains clinically important, as this period represents a critical window for the attainment and maintenance of peak bone mass and for the early prevention of bone loss. Future research including elderly populations is needed to confirm whether the observed associations extend to older age groups.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference. Second, although we adjusted for a wide range of demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors, some relevant variables were unavailable in the NHANES dataset, including bone turnover markers (e.g., osteocalcin, β-CTX), dietary calcium and vitamin D intake, use of lipid-lowering medications, and menopausal status or hormone therapy in women. Each of these factors may influence both lipid metabolism and bone health. Future studies with more comprehensive covariate information are warranted to validate and extend our results. Third, the study population included only US adults aged 20 to 59 years, which may restrict generalizability to older individuals or other populations. Future research in older age groups and diverse populations is warranted to validate our conclusions.

In conclusion, this study is the first to confirm a significant inverse association between the RC/HDL-C ratio and BMD, thereby enriching our scientific understanding of the interplay between lipid metabolic disturbances and skeletal health. However, given the cross-sectional design, the findings represent associations rather than causal relationships, and further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are warranted.

5. Conclusions

RC/HDL-C was inversely associated with BMD, with stronger associations observed in smoking status. These findings highlight the RC/HDL-C ratio as a potential predictor for bone health risk stratification and warrant further investigation in longitudinal and mechanistic studies.
