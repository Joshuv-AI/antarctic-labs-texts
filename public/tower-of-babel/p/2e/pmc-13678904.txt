Assembly and comparative analysis of the complete mitochondrial genome of a orchid cultivar ‘Jingzi’ reveals multichromosomal architecture and plastid-derived DNA transfers
Authors: Boyang Zhang, Jiuyang Tang, Hailong Wang, Bo Wang, Zhang Zhang, Guiming Liu, Yunfu Li, Jianhua Wei, Jiewei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrchidaceae is one of the most diverse flowering plant families in angiosperms and holds significant cultural, economic, and ecological value in China. Despite economic and horticultural significance, the mitochondrial genomes of Cymbidium kanran remain poorly characterized.ResultsWe assembled and annotated the complete mitochondrial genome of a new orchid variety, ‘Jingzi’, using PacBio HiFi sequencing technology. The mitochondrial genome is 704, 624 bp in length with a GC content of 43.91%, comprising 24 circular contigs. It contains 38 protein-coding genes (PCGs), including 24 unique core mitochondrial genes, 27 tRNA genes, and 3 rRNA genes. Notably, atp6 exhibits an unprecedented duplication event. Intracellular gene transfer analysis revealed 91 fragments homologous between mitochondria and chloroplasts, totaling 92, 762 bp (13.16% of the mitochondrial genome). Based on 24 conserved PCGs from 20 angiosperm species, our phylogenetic analysis indicated a potential affinity between ‘Jingzi’ and Paphiopedilum from Shenzhen. Given the poor sampling of closely related genera within Cymbidium, this observation is preliminary and awaits validation from future studies with expanded taxon coverage.ConclusionsThis study elucidates the main features of the ‘Jingzi’ mitochondrial genome, highlights extensive mitochondrial-chloroplast homology, and reports the presence of two copies of atp6 in the mitochondrial genome. These findings provide valuable insights for genetic and evolutionary studies of orchids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09481-y.Keywords: Cymbidium kanran, Mitochondrial genome, Genetic evolution, atp6

Introduction

The Orchidaceae family is the largest among angiosperms, encompassing over 750 genera and 28,000 species [1]. Orchids have a widespread global distribution, found in nearly all terrestrial regions except Antarctica [2, 3]. Referred to as the “gentlemen of flowers,” orchids have held cultural significance in China for over 2,400 years, dating back to the Spring and Autumn Period, and are associated with virtues like kindness and elegance. The orchid possesses high ornamental, medicinal, ecological, and cultural value [4] and is considered a popular ornamental plant worldwide and one of the ten traditional Chinese flowers. The Cymbidium genus, commonly known as the Chinese or Oriental orchid, is characterized by flowers with a corolla comprising three sepals, three petals, and a central column, with the lower petal specialized into a lip structure. Typically, these orchids bloom once annually, but their leaves remain evergreen throughout the year, displaying appealing leaf forms and patterns. There are seven primary species in this group: Cymbidium goeringii, C. faberi, C. ensifolium, C. sinense, C. kanran, C. tortisepalum, and C. goeringii var. longibracteatum. The natural propagation of orchids is challenging, as their seeds are extremely small and lack endosperm. For germination, they require a symbiotic relationship with specific fungi, a process that generally takes 5–7 years [5]. Furthermore, the increasing exploitation of wild orchids in recent years has led to a drastic decline in their populations, underscoring the urgency for orchid conservation and the development of new varieties [4, 6]. Cymbidium kanran ‘Jing Zi’, was developed at Beijing Vocational College of Agriculture. It displays a rare and vibrant purplish-red color, typically producing 1–2 flowers with a strong and fresh fragrance. Symbolizing festivity and auspiciousness, its flowering period coincides with the Spring Festival, enhancing the festive atmosphere of traditional Chinese celebrations and offering promising market prospects.

Mitochondria are essential organelles within cells, playing crucial roles in various metabolic pathways, including energy production, synthesis, and degradation [7]. Beyond serving as the cellular “powerhouse” and facilitating aerobic respiration, mitochondria are involved in numerous key cellular processes such as division, growth, apoptosis, and differentiation [8, 9]. In angiosperms, mitochondrial genomes exhibit notable variability, characterized by extensive changes in genome size, highly dynamic structural organization, extensive non-coding regions, RNA editing, and horizontal gene transfer [10–12]. Sequenced mitochondrial genomes of Orchidaceae species vary greatly, with some as large as 1,339 kb and others as small as 576 kb. Their structures are equally diverse, ranging from a complete circular form to multiple smaller circles [13, 14]. Mitochondrial evolution is highly non-conservative, with rapid structural changes yet a low mutation rate, likely associated with DNA repair mechanisms, including base excision repair (BER) and homologous recombination (HR) [15, 16]. During recombination, alterations in mitochondrial DNA configuration can occur, frequently leading to gene deletions or the presence of multiple gene copies, which contribute to the genetic diversity observed in plant mitochondrial DNA [12, 15]. Mitochondria are maternally inherited, which aids in the study of the systematic evolution of Orchidaceae species. Similarly, chloroplasts are also maternally inherited organelles, and as a result, plant and chloroplast genomes have been relatively well studied.

Mitochondrial genomes are widely utilized in studies of phylogeny and interspecific relationships due to their genetic system, which is relatively independent of the nucleus and exhibits a degree of conservation [17, 18]. However, research on plant mitochondrial genomes has lagged behind that of chloroplast genomes. This is primarily due to the complex structure of plant mitochondrial genomes, which involves multi-chromosomal arrangements, frequent gene recombination, long repetitive sequences in introns, and recurrent horizontal gene transfer events [19, 20].

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) is the exchange of genetic material between species by specific mechanisms other than fertilization, playing a significant role in the evolutionary development of organisms. Initially identified in prokaryotes, HGT has increasingly been recognized in eukaryotes due to advances in genome sequencing, revealing that it occurs at a notably high frequency in flowering plants [21]. Mitochondria trace back to an Alpha proteo bacterial endosymbiont. That origin left their genomes unusually receptive to horizontal gene transfer, more active, and quicker to fuse than chloroplast or nuclear DNA [22]. Consequently, mitochondrial genomes have integrated a range of foreign genetic material through HGT. The sources of mitochondrial HGT fragments are diverse. For instance, studies on Staphylinidae have shown the acquisition of entire mitochondrial genomes from three green algae and even a moss [23]. In plants, it is not uncommon for mitochondrial genes to be derived from foreign species. Orchids, in particular, have had a long-standing coexistence with fungi, which provide crucial nutrients during seed germination [24, 25]. The orchid ancestor incorporated approximately 270 bp of fungal mitochondrial genome sequences, with subsequent HGT events replacing parts of the initial transfer [25]. Most foreign DNA in mitochondria comes straight from the chloroplast. Across angiosperms, anywhere from 0.1% to 10.3% of the mitochondrial genome is chloroplast-derived, and although the vast majority of these inserts are non-coding, they can still tweak mitochondrial performance once they settle in [26]. Despite the Orchidaceae family’s large size, there is a notable lack of published data on orchid mitochondrial genomes. So far, complete mitochondrial genomes have been reported for just eight orchids: Gastrodia elata, G. angusta, Paphiopedilum micranthum, Cremastra lancifolium, C. macrorhizon, Apostasia shenzhenica, Epigeneium amplum, and Phalaenopsis aphrodite.

To further investigate the genomic characteristics of mitochondria in the Orchidaceae family, we assembled and annotated the complete mitochondrial genome of C. kanran for the first time, utilizing PacBio HiFi data. A comprehensive analysis of its mitochondrial genome characteristics was performed, including assessments of codon usage, repeat sequences, sequence migration, phylogenetic relationships, RNA editing events, and collinearity. We present an integrated analysis of mitochondrial genome architecture, relative synonymous codon usage, and pervasive horizontal gene transfer in the hybrid orchid C. kanran. These data illuminate the evolutionary dynamics and functional consequences of mitochondrial genome reorganization, clarify C. kanran’s phylogenetic position within Orchidaceae, and establish a robust benchmark for future investigations of mitochondrial evolution across the family.

Results

Characters and structure analysis of the C. kanran mitochondrial genome

The primary structure of the C. kanran mitochondrial genome is multi-branched. After filtering out duplicate regions from PacBio HiFi data, we identified 24 circular chromosomes, with a combined length of 704, 624 bp and a GC content of 43.91% (Fig. 1). Bandage software (v0.8.1) [27] was used to visualize the assembled mitochondrial genome, revealing 24 circular genomic DNA sequences (Fig. 2). The mitochondrial genome annotation of C. kanran revealed a total of 38 unique protein-coding genes (PCGs), including 24 core mitochondrial genes and 15 non-core genes, along with 27 tRNA genes (12 of which are present in multiple copies) and 3 rRNA genes. The core genes comprise 5 ATP synthase genes (atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, and atp9); 9 NADH dehydrogenase genes (nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, and nad9); 4 cytochrome c biogenesis genes (ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC, and ccmFN); 3 cytochrome c oxidase genes (cox1, cox2, and cox3); 1 protein transport subunit gene (mttB); 1 maturase gene (matR); and 1 cytochrome b gene (cob). The non-core genes include 3 ribosomal protein large subunit genes (rpl2, rpl5, rpl16), 11 small ribosomal subunit genes (rps1, rps2, rps3, rps4, rps7, rps10, rps11, rps12, rps13, rps14, and rps19), and 1 succinate dehydrogenase gene (sdh4) (Table 1).

Relative synonymous codon usage

The codon usage of the 38 unique protein-coding genes (PCGs) in the C. kanran mitochondrial genome was analyzed, and the codon usage for each amino acid is presented in Table S1. Codons with a relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) greater than 1 are considered preferred for the corresponding amino acid. As shown in Fig. 3, aside from the start codon AUG and tryptophan (UGG), both of which have RSCU values of 1, the mitochondrial PCGs exhibit a general codon usage preference. For example, alanine (Ala) shows a strong preference for the codon GCU, with the highest RSCU value among mitochondrial PCGs at 1.62. Similarly, histidine (His) prefers the codon CAU, with an RSCU value of 1.48.

Long-repeat and simple sequence repeat

DNA sequences in biological cells contain numerous repetitive elements, which can be broadly categorized as tandem repeats or dispersed repeats. The distinction between these two lies in the adjacency of the repeated segments: tandem repeats consist of adjacent sequences, while dispersed repeats are non-adjacent. A specific type of tandem repeat, the Simple Sequence Repeat (SSR), is generally less than 6 bp in length and is frequently used for developing molecular markers due to its dominant inheritance. In this study, we analyzed the repeat sequences within the mitochondrial genome of C. kanran (Fig. 4). Microsatellite (SSR) analysis identified a total of 191 SSRs across the mitochondrial genome. Among these, Chromosome 2 contained the highest number of SSRs, while Chromosome 21 had the lowest. Monomeric SSRs accounted for 32.46% of all SSRs, and only one hexameric SSR was detected in the entire mitochondrial genome. Tandem repeat analysis (satellite DNA), defined as core repeat units of approximately 7–200 bp arranged in tandem, revealed 61 tandem repeats with matching identities > 70% and lengths ranging from 10 bp to 252 bp. Chromosome 1 harbored the most tandem repeats, whereas no tandem repeats were found in Chromosomes 16 or 23. For dispersed repeats, a total of 4, 744 repeat pairs with a length ≥ 30 bp were detected, including 1, 991 palindromic repeats and 2, 753 forward repeats. The longest dispersed repeat unit, designated R1, had a length of 743 bp.

Sequence migration analysis between the two organelle genomes

In the course of plant evolution, intracellular transfer of genetic material frequently occurs within mitochondrial genomes, with relatively few conserved sequence fragments originating from chloroplasts. Sequence similarity analysis of C. kanran revealed 102 fragments homologous to both mitochondrial and chloroplast genomes, totaling 92, 762 bp and accounting for 13.16% of the mitochondrial genome (Fig. 5). Among these, MTPT22 is the longest fragment, measuring 11, 539 bp (approximately 1.62% of the total mitochondrial genome length) and containing the complete genes rpoB, rpoC1, and rpoC2. Most plastome-derived sequences exhibited high similarity to their conspecific plastome counterparts, with sequence identity ranging from 78.85% to 100%. Annotation of these homologous fragments identified 91 complete genes across the 55 homologous sequences, including 33 protein-coding genes and 22 tRNA genes (detailed in Table S3). These results indicate the presence of protein-coding and tRNA gene sequences derived from the chloroplast genome within the mitochondrial genome of C. kanran.

Phylogenetic analysis and sequence collinearity analysis

To examine the phylogenetic placement of C. kanran based on its mitochondrial genome, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the DNA sequences of 24 conserved mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) shared across 20 species from 5 orders of angiosperms (Table S4) (Fig. 6). The PCGs included in the analysis were atp1, atp4, atp6, atp8, atp9, ccmB, ccmC, ccmFC, ccmFN, cob, cox1, cox2, cox3, matR, mttB, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad7, and nad9. Two mitochondrial genomes from the order Ranunculales were used as outgroups. The resulting phylogenetic topology was consistent with the most recent Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) classification, placing C. kanran within the Orchidaceae family of the order Asparagales and showing a close relationship with Apostasia shenzhenica. It should be noted that this analysis is based on a limited set of conserved genes and a moderate number of taxa; a more comprehensive analysis incorporating additional markers and broader taxon sampling would provide a more robust framework for inferring deeper evolutionary relationships within Orchidaceae.

RNA editing events

RNA editing is widespread in the mitochondria of higher plants and constitutes a crucial step in gene expression within plant mitochondrial genomes. To investigate RNA editing in C. kanran, we utilized the sequences of all mitochondrial protein-coding genes (PCGs) and applied Deepred-mt to predict C-to-U RNA editing sites. Only results with probability values greater than 0.9 were retained for further analysis. Under this criterion, a total of 584 potential RNA editing sites were identified in the mitochondrial PCGs, all of which were C-to-U edits (Fig. 8). Among these genes, nad4 contained the highest number of editing sites, followed by ccmC and nad7, each with 35 sites (Table S6).

Discussion

The mitochondrial genome of the C. kanran exhibits a distinctive multi-branched structure comprising a linear contig and 24 circular contigs, with a total length of 704, 624 bp. This mosaic configuration epitomizes the ceaseless recombination, dynamic rearrangement, and incremental variation that have sculpted orchid mitochondrial genome over millions of years of evolution. Furthermore, the mitochondrial genome size displays considerable variation among orchids, which serves to underscore the remarkable diversity of Orchidaceae. For perspective, the mitochondrial genome spans 576 kb in Phalaenopsis aphrodite subsp. formosana [13], but expands significantly to 1, 340 kb in the mycoheterotrophic orchid Gastrodia elata [28]. These findings not only underscore the singular architecture of orchid mitochondrial genomes but also illuminate the adaptive strategies they have forged for survival in fiercely competitive ecosystems. Across the field, researchers are systematically dissecting orchid mitochondrial diversity and evolution to decode the genetic and evolutionary engines that drive this extraordinary plant lineage. Together, these distinctive traits define a singular mitochondrial landscape within Orchidaceae. Continued investigation of this landscape is pivotal for enhancing photosynthetic efficiency, safeguarding biodiversity, unlocking elite germplasm, and refining hybrid breeding strategies.

The published mitochondrial structures of Orchidaceae demonstrate significant diversity and variation, potentially related to the organelle’s DNA repair mechanisms. During continuous mitochondrial DNA repair and recombination, coding genes may undergo changes, deletions, or duplications. In this study, two copies of atp6 were annotated in the mitochondrial genome of C. kanran. The mitochondrial atp6 gene encodes subunit a of ATP synthase, which is critical for the enzyme’s function and cellular energy production [29]. However, we did not assess whether both copies are intact, transcribed, or functionally differentiated. Previous studies have reported that atp6 gene mutations or defects can lead to male sterility in various plant species, including carrot [30], rice [31], sorghum [32], pepper [33], radish [34], and celery. Whether the atp6 duplication observed in C. kanran has any association with male sterility or other phenotypic traits remains unknown and would require experimental validation (e.g., expression analysis and phenotypic characterization).

In plant organelles, RNA editing is a post-transcriptional process that operates independently of the transcriptional mechanism [35, 36]. In angiosperms, most RNA editing events involve C-to-U conversions, with editing sites distributed unevenly across species, genes, and codon positions. Retroprocessing can reverse-transcribe edited transcripts into cDNA, which is subsequently integrated into the genome via homologous recombination, potentially leading to the loss of editing sites [37, 38]. The number of RNA editing sites in Orchidaceae varies greatly. In this study, 584 putative RNA editing sites were identified in C. kanran. For comparison, 941 editing sites were reported in Phalaenopsis aphrodite subsp. Formosana [13] (note: these were also computationally predicted), and 416 putative sites across 28 PCGs were found in Apostasia shenzhenica [39]. It should be noted that all these predicted sites require experimental validation (e.g., via RNA-seq or cDNA sequencing) to confirm their actual occurrence. Computational predictions suggest that RNA editing may affect the start or stop codons of PCGs. In the mitochondrial genes of C. kanran, nad1, nad7, rps1, and cox2 are predicted to acquire start codons through putative RNA editing events. In the mitochondrial genome of sweet potato, it is hypothesized that the rps10 gene, which contains an initiation codon ACG, arises due to a C-to-U editing event at the initiation site [40]. Other studies suggest that in plant chloroplasts, ACG can directly function as a start codon, allowing transcription initiation without requiring organelle genome editing [41]. However, whether the editing events in nad1, nad7, rps1, and cox2 result from activation at the initiation sites remains to be verified. Additionally, atp8 and ccmFC were found to terminate prematurely, yet their gene sizes did not show substantial changes compared to other angiosperms. This preservation of gene size may be due to cellular self-editing mechanisms that maintain normal function or reduce redundancy. However, further research is needed to elucidate these underlying mechanisms.

Plant cells contain three relatively independent genomes: the nuclear genome, the chloroplast genome, and the mitochondrial genome. Gene transfer events frequently occur among these genomes, with varying recipient-donor relationships and exchange frequencies. For example, MTPT has been documented in the mitochondrial genomes of Cymbidium lancifolium and Cymbidium macrorhizon (5–6%), Cymbidium ensifolium (6.81%), and Paphiopedilum micranthum (10.34%) [12, 42, 43]. In this study, we analyzed the homologous fragments between the chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes of the orchid C. kanran. The total length of these homologous fragments in the mitochondrial genome was 92, 762 bp, accounting for 13.16% of its total length. This includes 55 complete genes, of which 33 are protein-coding genes and 22 are tRNA genes, representing the highest proportion reported among orchids to date. Previous research indicates that most chloroplast-derived sequences in mitochondria are non-functional pseudogenes [26]. In C. kanran, we observed a higher number of intact chloroplast-derived gene sequences; however, whether these sequences are transcribed or functional remains to be determined. It should be noted that MTPT is not limited to transfers within the cell; it has also been reported to occur between different species via mechanisms such as parasitic transfer, grafting, and vector-mediated transfer. These various forms of MTPT have been suggested to play roles in agriculture, including weed control and the development of new crop varieties [44]. The relatively high abundance of MTPT in C. kanran may warrant further investigation into its potential applications.

Materials and methods

Plant sampling and DNA sequencing

Fresh leaf samples of C. kanran were collected from the Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences (39° 56′ 32′′ N, 116° 16′ 46′′ E). The collection of plant materials was conducted with the requisite permissions from the host institution. The species was formally identified by Prof. Jiuyang Tang. A voucher specimen (BVCA202401) has been deposited in Orchid Germplasm Repository, Beijing Vocational College of Agriculture. The leaves were washed with DEPC-treated water, dried, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored in an ultra-low temperature freezer at -80 °C. Total DNA was extracted using the FastPure® Plant DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vanzyme Biotech, Nanjing, China), while total RNA was extracted using the CTAB method. The quality of the DNA and RNA was assessed with a NanoDrop One Microvolume UV-Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). DNA samples with OD260/OD280 ratios between 1.7 and 1.9, and OD260/OD230 ratios between 2.3 and 2.5, as well as RNA samples with OD260/OD280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.1 and OD260/OD230 ratios between 2.3 and 2.5, were considered to be of high quality.

Assembly and annotation of mitogenomes

In this project, the mitochondrial genome assembly was conducted using PacBio HiFi data. Initially, the HiFi long-read sequencing data were assembled directly using OATK software [45] with key parameters (syncasm -k 1001 -c 30), resulting in graphical assembly files in GFA format. To identify contigs containing mitochondrial genome sequences, we constructed a database of all assembled contigs in FASTA format using the makeblastdb tool. We then performed BLASTn analysis with the parameters “-evalue 1e-5 -outfmt 6 -max_hsps 10 -word_size 7 -task blastn-short,” using plant mitochondrial genes from Arabidopsis thaliana as query sequences. The GFA files were visualized using Bandage software (v0.8.1) [27], and mitochondrial contigs were screened based on BLASTn results to obtain a preliminary sketch of the C. kanran mitochondrial genome. The mitochondrial genomes of Arabidopsis thaliana (NC_037304), Liriodendron tulipifera (NC_021152.1), and Gastrodia elata (MF070084-MF070102) were used as reference genomes. Annotation of the mitochondrial genome was performed with PMGA (http://www.1kmpg.cn/pmga/) [46]. The tRNA genes were annotated using tRNAscan-SE software (v2.0.11) [47], and the rRNA genes were annotated using BLASTn (v2.16.0) [48]. Annotation errors in each mitochondrial genome were manually corrected with Apollo software (v1.11.8) [49].

Codon usage bias analysis and structural analysis

The protein-coding sequences of the mitochondrial genome were extracted using PhyloSuite software (v1.1.16) [50]. Codon usage of these protein-coding genes was analyzed with MEGA software (v7.0) [51], and the Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) values were calculated.

To resolve repeated regions in the graphical genome assembly, long reads were aligned to the repetitive sequences to determine whether these regions, particularly the repeats, were spanned by long reads. This approach allowed us to infer the most probable structure of the mitochondrial genome based on long-read data.

Repetitive sequences analysis

Repetitive sequences, including microsatellites, tandem repeats, and dispersed repeats, were identified using the following tools: MISA (v2.1) (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) for microsatellites [52], TRF (v4.09) (https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.unix.help.html) for tandem repeats [53], and the REPuter Web Server (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/) for dispersed repeats [54] The results were visualized using Excel (2021) and the Circos package (v0.69.9) [55].

Analysis of Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT)

The chloroplast genome was assembled using GetOrganelle [56], annotated with CPGAVAS2 [57], and then corrected with CPGView [58]. Homologous fragments were identified through BLASTn analysis (v2.13.0) [49], and the results were visualized using Circos package (v0.69. 9) [55].

Phylogenetic analysis and sequence collinearity analysis

Based on genetic relationships, related species were selected, and their mitochondrial genomes were downloaded (as per the project species selection). Common genes were extracted using PhyloSuite software (v1.1.16) [51], followed by multiple sequence alignment with MAFFT software (v7.505) [59]. A phylogenetic tree was then constructed using IQ-TREE software (v1.6.12) [60] based on the maximum likelihood (ML) method with parameters set to “--alrt 1000 -B 1000.” The phylogenetic analysis results were visualized with ITOL software (v6) [61].

Conserved homologous sequences, referred to as collinear blocks, were identified using BLASTn with the parameters “-evalue 1e-5 -word_size 9 -gapopen 5 -gapextend 2 -reward 2 -penalty − 3.” Only collinear blocks longer than 500 bp were selected for subsequent analysis. Using MCScanX [55], pairwise comparisons were performed based on the BLASTn results to generate a Multiple Synteny Plot, highlighting conserved collinearity blocks.

RNA edits determination

The sequences of all protein-coding genes (PCGs) encoded by the mitochondrial genome of this species were used as input to predict putative C-to-U RNA editing sites. Predictions were performed using Deepred-mt [56, 62], a tool based on a convolutional neural network (CNN) model that provides higher accuracy compared to previous prediction tools. Results with probability values greater than 0.9 were retained as candidate editing sites. It should be noted that these are computational predictions and require experimental validation (e.g., via RNA-seq or cDNA sequencing) for confirmation.
