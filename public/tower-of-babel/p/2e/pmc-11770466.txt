Faecal microbiota transplantation for eradicating Helicobacter pylori infection: clinical practice and theoretical postulation
Authors: Zhi-Ning Ye, Guy D Eslick, Shao-Gang Huang, Xing-Xiang He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe sustained increase in antibiotic resistance leads to a declining trend in the eradication rate of Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection with antibiotic-based eradication regimens. Administration of a single probiotic shows limited efficacy in eradicating H. pylori infection. This review indicates that faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), a novel therapeutic approach, either as a monotherapy or adjunctive therapy, exhibits beneficial effects in terms of the eradication of H. pylori infection and the prevention of adverse events. The role of FMT in H. pylori eradication may be associated directly or indirectly with some therapeutic constituents within the faecal suspension, including bacteria, viruses, antimicrobial peptides and metabolites. In addition, variations in donor selection, faecal suspension preparation and delivery methods are believed to be the main factors determining the effectiveness of FMT for the treatment of H. pylori infection. Future research should refine the operational procedures of FMT to achieve optimal efficacy for H. pylori infection and explore the mechanisms by which FMT acts against H. pylori.Keywords: Helicobacter pylori, Infections, Gastrointestinal Microbiome, Digestive System Diseases

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), a gram-negative bacterium readily adaptable to the acidic environment of the human stomach, was classified as a class I carcinogen for gastric cancer by the World Health Organization as early as 1994.1 Previous studies have shown that H. pylori infection induces or is associated with a variety of gastrointestinal (GI) diseases such as acute and chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, gastric mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma, gastric cancer, functional dyspepsia and other GI disorders.2 3 Expert consensuses worldwide endorse the recommendation that H. pylori eradication for the treatment or even prevention of the abovementioned diseases.4,8 In Japan, H. pylori eradication is even considered an essential approach for the prevention of gastric cancer.7

Currently, the prevailing treatment strategies for H. pylori eradication, including high-dose double, triple, quadruple and sequential regimens, are fundamentally based on antibiotics in combination with a proton pump inhibitor with or without a bismuth salt, and with a treatment duration of 10 or 14 days.9 10 Although antibiotic-based regimens generally achieve an eradication rate of over 80% for H. pylori infection, the efficacy has gradually diminished due to the global rise in secondary or acquired antibiotic resistance in H. pylori.11 12 In addition, treatment with multiple drugs for up to 14 days has increased the incidence of potential adverse events, such as taste disturbances, diarrhoea and nausea. More importantly, it has also led to the emergence of secondary or acquired antibiotic resistance in H. pylori.13 Therefore, exploring non-antibiotic approaches that increase or maintain efficacy with less usage of antibiotic drugs and fewer incidences of potential adverse events is required. Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is an emerging gut microbiota-based therapy that transfers the intestinal microbiota from a healthy donor into the GI tract of the patient, thus achieving therapeutic goals for various diseases, including GI and extraintestinal conditions.14 15 The aim of this review is to present the current preliminary clinical data on the efficacy of gut microbiota modifying modalities, probiotics and especially FMT, in the treatment of H. pylori infection, and provide a theoretical postulation on the potential therapeutic effect of FMT for eradicating H. pylori infection.

Efficacy of probiotics in the treatment of H. pylori infection

H. pylori infection may regulate the gut microbiota by expressing various virulence factors and modulating the pH value of the gut.16 Currently, several studies have indicated that the diversity of the gut microbiota is significantly lower in H. pylori-infected patients than in uninfected subjects and recovers after eradication therapy.17,19 However, Bik et al reported that after the removal of H. pylori sequences, the diversity of the gut microbiota in H. pylori-infected patients was higher than that in uninfected subjects,20 suggesting that detection means may influence the assessment of the relationship between H. pylori infection and the diversity of the gut microbiota. However, all these studies support the notion that H. pylori infection impacts gut microbial diversity. Among the gut microbiota, the abundance of Actinomyces, Gemella, Streptococcus and Haemophilus are dominant genera in H. pylori-infected patients, and the class Bacilli, order Lactobacillales, family Streptococcaceae and genus Streptococcus appear to be key taxa in patients with H. pylori-induced atrophic gastritis.21 Moreover, in H. pylori-infected patients, the composition of the gut microbiota changes over time after H. pylori eradication and is eventually restored to a pattern similar to that of the uninfected subjects.22 23

Probiotics are defined as live microorganisms that have beneficial effects on the health of the host when consumed in sufficient quantities.24 It has been demonstrated that H. pylori infection is associated with a reduction in partial probiotic bacteria such as Blautia and Lachnoclostridium and elevation in harmful bacteria such as Alistipes and that H. pylori eradication treatment is capable of restoring the disrupted features of the gut microbiota mentioned above.22 Clinically, an addition of a probiotic to antibiotic-based treatment significantly increases the eradication rate of H. pylori infection and reduces the incidence of adverse events and reinfection rates.25,27 Furthermore, the utilisation of probiotics for H. pylori eradication mitigates fluctuations in the gut microbiota and contributes to the reduced risk of H. pylori-associated carcinogenesis.28 29

A variety of probiotics are currently used for the treatment of H. pylori infection, with Lactobacillus acidophilus, Bacillus licheniformis, Clostridium butyricum, Saccharomyces boulardii and Bifidobacterium believed to have definitive efficacy against H. pylori infection (online supplemental table 1)25 27 Among them, Bifidobacterium is a widely used probiotic in H. pylori eradication. It has been shown that supplementation with Bifidobacterium alongside quadruple therapy increases the eradication rate of H. pylori and alleviates clinical symptoms such as abdominal pain and gastro-oesophageal reflux.30 However, while Bifidobacterium administered alone exhibits antimicrobial activity in vitro and in animal models,31 clinical studies have only demonstrated its ability to alleviate clinical symptoms associated with H. pylori infection, rather than the eradication efficacy.32 Besides Bifidobacterium, previous studies have demonstrated that as an adjunctive therapy to the standard triple regimen, Lactobacillus reuteri significantly reduces the incidence of abdominal bloating, diarrhoea, scores on GI symptom assessment scales and the frequencies of adverse events during treatment.33 34 Furthermore, L. reuteri has been shown to be a promising alternative to bismuth in quadruple therapy, potentially serving as a viable option within the standard quadruple regimen for patients with bismuth intolerance.35 In addition to being used as a supplement in antibiotic-based triple or quadruple therapy regimens, probiotics used alone also play a role in eradicating H. pylori infection. In addition, recent studies have demonstrated that L. reuteri supplementation alone36 37 or combination therapy with L. acidophilus and Lactobacillus rhamnosus38 effectively reduces the H. pylori load and alleviates H. pylori-induced GI symptoms. It has been shown that L. reuteri inhibits the colonisation and pathogenicity of H. pylori by interfering with the binding of gangliotetraosylceramide and sulfatide, regulating the expression of tight junction molecules and mucins and suppressing the inflammatory factors (such as IL-1β, IL-6 and IFN-γ).39,41

In general, the efficacy of probiotics as a standalone eradication treatment for H. pylori infection remains unsatisfactory despite their ability to counteract H. pylori infection. As reported in a meta-analysis incorporating 11 studies, probiotics alone achieve a modest eradication rate of 14.0% for H. pylori infection with a reduction of 8.61‰ in the δ-value on the urease breath test.42 Additionally, the efficacy of coadministration of antibiotics and probiotics for H. pylori eradication appears to be inconsistent among studies. Feng et al indicated that, with the exception of fungal probiotics such as Brettanomyces, the efficacy of most probiotics is compromised by antibiotics.43 To mitigate the impact of antibiotics on probiotics, clinicians often employ strategies such as staggering doses or extending treatment cycles.44 However, whether these strategies sufficiently enhance the effectiveness of probiotics against H. pylori remains unclear. Some studies have indicated that both inactivated and non-inactivated probiotics show therapeutic effects on H. pylori infection,45 46 and that supplementation with probiotic metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids and lactic acid, is more therapeutically beneficial than that with direct probiotic supplementation.47 However, there is no difference in the eradication rate between supplementation with a single probiotic and that with multiple probiotics in an antibiotic-based treatment for H. pylori infection.25 These findings suggest that single or multiple probiotics may not fully realise their therapeutic potential within antibiotic-based treatment regimens.

The human gut harbours approximately 3.8×1013 bacteria, including a vast number of beneficial bacteria.48 It has been reported that although the total number of cultivable microbes reported in human gastric samples (mucosa or gastric fluid) ranges from only 102 to 104 CFU/g or mL,49 some common probiotics, such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, are able to tolerate the high acidity of the stomach and play crucial roles in maintaining the balance of the gastric microbiota.50 Previous studies have demonstrated that H. pylori infection alters the interactions and balance among the gastric flora and causes dysbiosis of the gastric microbiota, which is involved in the development of gastric precancerous lesions and gastric cancer,51 and that eradication of H. pylori infection leads to a gradual restoration or normalisation of the gastric microbiota.52 53 Therefore, it is believed that H. pylori infection directly disrupts the overall gut microbiota rather than some specific probiotics, and it is conceivable that restoring or normalising the gut microbiota may attenuate H. pylori infection or facilitate the efficacy of currently recommended eradication regimens.

Clinical practice of FMT for eradicating H. pylori infection

Theoretically, FMT should exert a more pronounced therapeutic effect in increasing the abundance of probiotics in the GI tract compared with the administration of individual probiotics.54 55 Our recent pilot observational study reported an overall eradication rate of 40.6% in patients who were treated with washed microbiota transplantation (WMT), a modified form of FMT, for various indications and who were positive for H. pylori infection prior to WMT but without previous H. pylori eradication therapy.56 Noticeably, this eradication rate achieved with WMT alone is higher than that (14%) as reported by Losurdo et al for probiotics alone.42 Furthermore, our study also revealed that female patients and patients with higher pretreatment pepsinogen ratio exhibited enhanced efficacy against H. pylori infection following FMT treatment.56 In a randomised controlled clinical study, Zhang used FMT as an adjuvant therapy in combination with high-dose dual therapy for the treatment of H. pylori infection and found that FMT did not increase the eradication rate of high-dose dual therapy. However, adjunctive therapy with FMT effectively reduced the GI symptom scores associated with H. pylori infection and the incidence of adverse events.57 Given the lack of effective alternative therapies to antibiotics for H. pylori eradication, and the suboptimal eradication rate of a single probiotic, FMT may be a promising alternative to antibiotics, either as a monotherapy or as an adjunctive treatment. Theoretical postulation on the potential therapeutic effect of FMT for eradicating H. pylori infection

Despite the absence of studies elucidating the precise mechanisms by which FMT eradicates H. pylori, it is hypothesised that any therapeutic constituents (including bacteria, viruses, fungi and their metabolic products) within the faecal suspension may act as the principal therapeutic substances during the treatment.58 Therefore, the role that FMT plays in H. pylori eradication may be related to the direct and/or indirect effects of these components on H. pylori.

Bacteria or fungi, especially probiotics, have been extensively studied for their role in H. pylori colonisation.59 Sakarya et al reported that the neuraminidase activity of S. boulardii selectively removes α-(2-3)-linked sialic acid, which serves as a ligand for the sialic acid-binding adhesin of H. pylori, from the cell surface, thereby diminishing the adhesion of H. pylori to the duodenal epithelial cells.60 Kim et al reported that lactic acid bacteria (LAB) such as Bifidobacterium longum, Lactobacillus casei and Pediococcus pentosaceus have been found to decrease ammonia production by inhibiting the urease activity, thus lowering the pH and forming an unfavourable environment for H. pylori colonisation.61 In addition, antimicrobial substances produced by probiotics, such as reuterin from L. reuteri, bacteriocin-like inhibitory substances from Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. ulgaricus, usnic acid from Nephromopsis pallescens and bacteriocins from Lactobacillus brevis BK11 and Enterococcus faecalis BK61, may directly inhibit H. pylori activity.62,65 Notably, probiotics may also enhance host immunity against H. pylori infection. Yakovenko et al demonstrated that the supplementation of Bifiform, a probiotic containing strains of Enterococcus faecium and B. longum, in the triple therapy increased the local plasma cell count and maintained a stable secretory immunoglobulin A level, exerting a positive influence on the host’s immune response to H. pylori.66 Moreover, probiotics such as L. acidophilus, Lactobacillus plantarum, Lactobacillus paracasei and L. rhamnosus inhibit the release of H. pylori-induced inflammatory mediators (eg, IL-8, IFN-γ and TNF-α) that disrupt the gastric barrier, thereby enhancing resistance to H. pylori colonisation.67 68 Furthermore, probiotics alter H. pylori adhesion to the gastric epithelium by secreting antiadhesion factors or competitive adhesion factors that disrupt or compete for adhesion sites between H. pylori and the gastric epithelium, thereby reducing H. pylori colonisation.60 69 Although there has been no report on the direct association between the efficacy of FMT in eradicating H. pylori and probiotics, it has been shown that FMT treatment significantly enhances the abundance of intestinal probiotics.55 Moreover, the abundance of probiotics in the faecal suspension directly influences the therapeutic effectiveness of FMT.54 Therefore, we hypothesise that the efficacy of FMT in eradicating H. pylori may, at least in part, be attributed to changes in probiotic abundance.

Beyond bacteria, the stomach also harbours other microorganisms such as viruses, which may potentially influence H. pylori colonisation.70 A previous study indicated that phages, a major component of gut viruses, could impact gut bacteria activity.71 Notably, phage genes have been identified within the genetic sequence of H. pylori and are considered a significant driving force in its evolution.72 In addition, a few studies have reported that phages targeting H. pylori exert bactericidal capabilities, resulting in the direct eradication of H. pylori.73 74 Indeed, phages are regarded as the primary agents responsible for the therapeutic effects of FMT.75 Brunse et al employed precise filtration techniques to isolate a faecal suspension primarily composed of phages, and experimental results showed comparable efficacy of faecal phage transplantation to conventional FMT with comparable efficacy and higher safety levels.76 Thus, phages may play important roles in the treatment of H. pylori infection by FMT, but more in-depth studies are needed to reveal the underlying mechanisms.

In addition to microorganisms, numerous bioactive substances exist within the GI tract, especially metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and secondary bile acids that are exclusively produced by intestinal microorganisms, which are of significant importance in maintaining normal physiological functions in the body.77 Faecal suspension may potentially influence the progression of diseases by supplementing these elements.78 Apart from the aforementioned lactic acid, previous studies have shown that metabolites from the gut microbiota, such as short-chain fatty acids, effectively inhibit the activity of H. pylori.79 80 In addition, the H2O2 produced by probiotics, when catalysed by the catalase of H. pylori, generates a large number of oxygen radicals that inhibit the enzyme activity of H. pylori, thus exerting an antimicrobial effect (figure 1).81

Optimisation of FMT for the treatment of H. pylori infection

Noticeably, the absence of a globally unified consensus on FMT has given rise to considerable variations in the implementation methods of FMT.82 These variations have resulted in significant heterogeneity in the effectiveness of using FMT for treating the same disease across different geographic areas or within different units.83 While there is no specific consensus on the operational procedures for FMT in H. pylori infection, the clinical study mentioned above suggests that essential optimisations in the implementation methods of FMT should be considered when used for treating H. pylori infection.56 The implementation of FMT encompasses several critical steps, including donor screening, faecal suspension preparation and delivery methods, all of which have the potential to impact the efficacy of FMT for specific indications.84

To date, the faecal donors have primarily consisted of healthy individuals and patients themselves or their family members, with healthy individuals being the frequent choice for researchers.85 86 It has been indicated that the efficacy of FMT for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) varies depending on the donor, with the faecal matter from universal donors resulting in a higher remission rate of clinical symptoms compared with that from relatives or acquaintances.87 Although there is no universal consensus on donor screening, published studies concur that known diseases should be assessed through a combination of questionnaires (or interviews) and medical examinations, with specific attention given to infections like H. pylori infection.88,91 Although clinical tests for H. pylori infection, including breath tests, antigen tests, tissue urease assays and pathology tests, are unable to definitively exclude the presence of H. pylori infection, it has been demonstrated that faecal suspensions derived from healthy individuals do not pose a risk of healthcare-acquired H. pylori infection for patients.92 Therefore, when choosing faecal donors for patients with H. pylori infection, it is advisable to consider using faecal samples from healthy individuals and to maintain regular monitoring of the donors for H. pylori infection. Furthermore, Vermeire et al demonstrated a positive correlation between the richness of the faecal microbiota and the effectiveness of FMT in treating IBD, suggesting the necessity to assess the microbiota characteristics of the donor’s faeces prior to performing FMT.93

Various methods have been employed in previous studies for the preparation of faecal suspension. For clinical applications, a purified faecal suspension could be obtained through repeated centrifugation and filtration.94 According to Zhang et al, a faecal bacterial suspension obtained through multiple centrifugation steps, known as ‘washed microbiota’, has been shown to significantly enhance safety compared with faecal bacterial suspensions obtained through simple centrifugation.95 The only documented study using FMT as a standalone treatment for H. pylori infection employed the washed microbiota preparation method, indicating that this preparation method holds promise as a potentially effective approach.56

Moreover, the delivery method of faecal suspension also plays a crucial role in the efficacy of FMT. Previous studies have demonstrated that faecal suspension could be administered to the GI tract through capsules, tubes or enemas.85 Specifically, tube transplantation is further classified into upper, middle and lower GI tube transplantation.85 Jamshidi et al indicated that, compared with the administration of faecal suspension via the upper GI tract, administration via the lower GI tract significantly improved the clinical symptoms of patients with irritable bowel syndrome.96 As described above, middle GI tube transplantation appears to be more effective for the treatment of H. pylori infection with FMT, likely due to the moderate pH and distance from the stomach.79 Nevertheless, more comprehensive studies are warranted to compare the effects of various transplantation methods on the efficacy of FMT for eradicating H. pylori infection and to investigate the potential underlying mechanisms. If feasible, it may be possible to prioritise the use of middle GI tube transplantation for FMT in the treatment of H. pylori infection.

The dosage and infusion frequency of faecal suspension are also crucial factors to consider when evaluating the efficacy of FMT.94 Cold et al suggested that the remission rate of clinical symptoms in patients with recurrent Clostridium difficile infection (CDI) through FMT was positively correlated with the number of capsules and the delivered amount of faeces used in capsule production, indicating that the dosage of the faecal suspension administered is significantly associated with the therapeutic efficacy of FMT in treating diseases.97 In the aforementioned clinical study on FMT for H. pylori infection, all patients were treated with a standardised dosage of faecal suspension, and no significant differences in efficacy among patients receiving different treatment frequencies.56 However, a previous study indicated that repeated and high-dose faecal suspension supplementation could enhance the efficacy of FMT for diseases such as IBD.98 Therefore, further investigation and discussion are needed to assess the efficacy of FMT in patients receiving different dosages or treatment frequencies, particularly in the context of H. pylori infection treatment.

In addition to the implementation method of FMT, the selection of FMT recipients is also a potential influential factor for the efficacy of FMT. It has been shown that the gut microbiota of patients prior to FMT treatment may influence the effectiveness of FMT.99 Sims et al showed that patients under 65 years old, as well as those treated with FMT based on PCR-positive results, had a significantly lower recurrence rate of CDI after FMT compared with the controls.100 In the clinical study mentioned above, we found that female patients or patients with higher pretreatment PGR values had a higher eradication rate of H. pylori infection after FMT treatment, suggesting that gender and PGR may influence the efficacy of FMT for H. pylori infection.56 Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of FMT in treating refractory or recurrent diseases such as CDI and immunodeficiency disorders.101,103 Moreover, the treatment for H. pylori infection faces challenges such as the increasing antibiotic resistance and the risk of reinfection.11 104 Given that probiotics effectively enhance the eradication rate of H. pylori infection when used in combination with currently recommended regimens containing antibiotics25,27 and that FMT has been shown specific efficacy in treating antibiotic-resistant infections, such as CDI,97 exploring the efficacy of FMT in patients with refractory or recurrent H. pylori infection, especially in the context of antibiotic resistance, is a highly promising research direction. Thus, further comprehensive research is needed to define the most suitable criteria for H. pylori-infected patients to receive FMT treatment.

Conclusions

Considering the declining efficacy of currently recommended antibiotic-based treatment for H. pylori eradication and their contribution to an increasing rate of antibiotic resistance, the development of a novel therapeutic approach is of paramount importance. FMT represents a potential treatment option that may eradicate H. pylori by replenishing the deficient microbiota (including bacteria, fungi and phages) and their metabolites in H. pylori-infected patients. However, due to the limited clinical evidence regarding FMT for H. pylori eradication, further well-designed studies are still needed to confirm the specific efficacy of FMT for H. pylori infection and to optimise FMT treatment by addressing implementation methods (including donor screening, faecal suspension preparation, transplantation methods, dosage and duration) and defining target recipients.
