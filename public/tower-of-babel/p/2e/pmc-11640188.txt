Building the private practice workforce in the Northern Territory: Barriers and facilitators for implementation of the allied health assistant role
Authors: Lori Roberts, Bea Staley, John Callanan, Kristy Logan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo identify barriers and facilitators contributing to the successful implementation of the allied health assistant role in private disability practice to better meet population needs.DesignA qualitative case study.SettingThis study was completed with staff working in private disability practices in a regional context in the Northern Territory.ParticipantsEight participants were interviewed, including three allied health assistants, three allied health professionals, and two managers with allied health backgrounds.ResultsMore barriers were reported than facilitators, with four key themes identified. Financial risk was a barrier when employing allied health assistants. This risk was mediated by providing part‐time employment or having allied health assistants in dual roles. Reduced confidence from allied health professionals and assistants to complete delegation work was the second barrier. A facilitator was increasing allied health assistants' task variation, which participants reported increased retention. Finally, a positive working relationship between allied health professionals and assistants facilitates delegation.ConclusionThis research offers private disability providers insight into the realities of employing an allied health assistant. It also suggests that formal training programs for both allied health assistants and professionals require increased focus on delegation in the private disability sector. On a government level, a review of the NDIS price guide for allied health assistant rates is needed if private providers are to better meet the requirements for NDIS participants in regional areas.Keywords: allied health, allied health assistant, allied health professional, delegation, disability, National Disability Insurance Scheme, private practice

INTRODUCTION

The disability sector has undergone rapid transformation since the rollout of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS). 1 The landscape for allied health service delivery has presented unintended complications, one of these being workforce shortages that have resulted in significant unmet population needs. 2 , 3 Extensive waitlists for allied health therapy is a consequence for such workforce shortages. Issues associated with long waitlists include increased psychological distress 4 , 5 and reduced therapeutic opportunities to implement early intervention. 6 , 7 , 8 In addition, NDIS allied health professionals (AHPs) are often catalysts for other services and processes, for example, inpatient discharge from hospital to supported living, 9 correctional centre release plans 10 and access to assistive technology. 11 Waitlists result in delays for people to move forward to achieve their NDIS goals.

NDIS workforce shortages are a national problem that is amplified In the Northern Territory (NT). The number of AHPs in the NT is lower per capita than the rest of Australia. 12 Private disability providers in the NT have extensive waitlist times for allied health services. 13 Furthermore, the NT has a diverse demographic with a comparatively higher ratio of Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander Peoples living in remote areas where health and disability statistics are often more complex than what is reflected in population data. 14 The complexities associated with population needs across remote and regional contexts require AHPs to have well‐established therapy skills. 15 This study was completed in Darwin—a regional classification scale, 16 that is expected to support the greater needs of the Territory. The remote characteristics of Darwin, coupled with complexities in the NDIS contribute to staff turnover and burnout for the allied health NDIS workforce, 17 , 18 which is exacerbated by longstanding recruitment challenges and workforce shortages. 12

One of the approaches for improving allied health workforce efficacy is to employ allied health assistants (AHA). An AHA works with AHPs to complete tasks that do not require a specialised skillset. 19 Examples of tasks completed by an AHA can be direct client work, such as facilitating prescribed therapy programs, as well as non‐clinical tasks, such as administrative, clerical and housekeeping tasks. 20 There are many proposed benefits of using an AHA model, with most evidence coming from acute and/or government‐funded community health sectors. 21 A benefit can be increased workforce output, whilst not compromising clinical outcomes for clients. The clinical outcomes from acute and/or community health sectors indicate neutral to positive outcomes. In some studies, the client outcomes remained the same as if an AHP were to implement therapy. 22 , 23 In terms of workforce outputs, a hospital‐based study found that 24% of AHPs time was spent completing tasks that could be safely delegated to an ‘appropriately qualified and supervised AHA’. 24 Theoretically, AHA models of service delivery should increase AHP service delivery outputs; however, barriers remain that impact AHA utilisation.

The primary barriers identified in the literature include uncertainty about the AHA scope of practice, 25 , 26 , 27 inconsistencies in AHA prerequisite training and demonstrated competencies, 21 AHP confidence in supervision and delegation, 28 , 29 and mixed consumer perspectives of the role. 30 , 31 AHAs are often expected to work with a variety of allied health disciplines and the scope of practice can be broad, impacting on role clarity. 19 Current AHA vocational training is often not prioritised by disability employers' because the learning content is often not transferrable into the disability context. 21 To establish foundational skills for disability delegation, increased ‘on‐the‐job’ training is required for AHAs and AHPs, which has a time and cost expense for practice owners. Finally, patients in acute hospital settings report high satisfaction with the AHA role 22 ; however, findings from remote community‐based studies report consumer ambivalence and in some instances, consumers declined the AHA service. 30 , 31 How, and if, these barriers are also present in the disability sector is unknown.

A key difference between the private disability and government‐funded sectors is the explicit focus on financial revenue. Private disability providers are reliant on revenue generated from billing NDIS participants' funding packages. There is a significant price difference between an AHA and an AHP hourly rate as per the NDIS price guide. The maximum hourly rate for an AHA compared with an occupational therapist or speech pathologist there is a 76.36% price difference, and an 88.52% price difference for the services of a physiotherapist. 32 For NDIS participants, they can maximise their NDIS budget by seeing an AHA at a lower cost; however, for employers, the financial contribution of an AHA is much lower than that of the AHPs.

Another key revenue consideration in private disability practice is ‘billable time’. 33 This refers to the therapist's time spent directly working on the NDIS participant's goals which is then invoiced to the client's. 32 A report by the National Disability Insurance Agency 34 stated that ‘most providers aim to bill between 4.5 and 6 hours a day for contracted therapists to financially break even’. 34 , p. 33 By comparison, ‘non‐billable time’ refers to time spent on tasks that are not related to the client's NDIS goals, such as team meetings, clinical supervision and staff training. 32 The required ‘on‐the‐job’ training for AHA is non‐billed time for practice owners. How financial requirements are being managed with AHAs is unknown. The research question guiding this study was: What are the barriers and facilitators contributing to the successful implementation of the AHA role for private disability practice in the NT?

METHODOLOGY

Using a qualitative case study design, this study was designed to garner the perspectives of individuals working in the private disability sector and implementing AHA service delivery. Topics within the semi‐structured interview covered questions about the tasks involved in delegation, billable hour expectations, what currently worked well with delegation, what did not, and participants proposed solutions. See Appendix A for details. All participants either signed a written consent form at the meeting or provided prior verbal consent on the audio recording. On average, interviews lasted 45 minutes. All audio recordings were stored in password access software on a cloud database. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee.

Research context

The study was completed in Darwin, the regional centre of the NT characterised by lower socio‐economic status, ongoing issues of workforce recruitment and retention, as well as a large population of Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander People relative to the national average. 12 Research participants were all working in a private disability service with NDIS clients. All practices serviced a broad range of client ages and diagnostic groups. Two of the four practices were comprised of multidisciplinary teams. Practices ranged in size from the smallest employing two AHPs and one AHA, to the largest comprising of approximately 20 AHPs and four AHAs. Two of the four practices had alternative revenue streams that contributed to the business in conjunction with therapy service delivery. Such revenue streams will not be detailed to prioritise participant anonymity.

Participants

At the time of this study, there were six private practices within the regional area who had AHAs in the team. All six practices were invited to participate in the study by phone call and email. The participant information sheet and consent form were emailed for further clarification. Of the six practices invited, four returned correspondences and agreed to participate in the study. Participation rate was not a key focus in this preliminary study, rather, a priority was maintaining professional relationships in the small allied health community. When staff did not return emails or phone calls, this was not followed up by the researcher out of respect for the already established professional relationship. Within two of these practices, a manager, at least one AHP and an AHA were interviewed to gain insights from multiple roles involved in AHAs service delivery. For the other two practices, only one staff member was involved. In two instances, managers were also the delegating AHP. Information about their management and clinical experiences was included in data analysis. The sample size was determined by availability within the community. This consisted of eight participants: three AHAs, three AHPs and two managers. This study was not funded, and participants did not receive payment for their time.

Data analysis

Data was analysed based on the research question about barriers and facilitators. Transcripts were coded using an iterative method of reading to identify key themes and frequently cited words, then categorised as a barrier or facilitator. 35 Audio recordings were first transcribed verbatim and uploaded to NVivo12. 36 Themes with the highest number of codes were used as the key findings. The data was coded by the primary author under the supervision of the second author.

Situating the authorship team

This authorship team has a vested position in this research and community as clinicians, researchers and lecturers at the regional university. The research team came to this work as a part of the context, not as neutral or passive observers. We approach it from a transitional worldview, with the intention of engaging in research that translates to practice and provokes change for individuals experiencing disability in our community.

RESULTS

The content analysis uncovered four key themes, which were then categorised according to whether participants stated themes as barriers or facilitators for AHA implementation. There were a higher number of barriers reported by participants than facilitators. AHAs reported more facilitators, whilst managers reported mostly barriers. The frequency counts of codes are presented in (Table 1). Two barriers and two facilitators were identified. The theme with the highest number of codes was Theme 1—managing financial viability (n = 25). The managers were highly represented in this theme (n = 13, 52%). Theme 2—reduced confidence, contained the next highest number of codes when participants spoke about reduced confidence sharing therapy plans (n = 23). This was primarily coded from AHAs and AHPs (n = 19, 83%) as a barrier. Theme 3—AHA retention (n = 21) AHAs and AHPs' codes were most highly represented in this theme (n = 18, 86%), with a focus on facilitators for retention. Finally, Theme 4—AHP and AHA dynamic was coded (n = 19). Codes in this theme were largely represented by AHAs (47%) and were also identified as a facilitator for delegation. The following subthemes arose in the data. They are presented in order of frequency (see also Table 1).

Theme 1: Managing financial viability

Financial viability was a concern and focus primarily for managers; however, it was a topic highlighted by all participants. The following subthemes illustrate commonalities across coding.

Challenges with full‐time AHA employment

Of the four private practices, three had employed AHAs on a part‐time basis or in dual roles. Dual roles meant that the staff members were employed full‐time; however, their time was divided between completing AHA tasks and another assigned role (e.g. administration). The practice that had employed full‐time AHAs was a relatively large practice and had additional revenue streams beyond therapy provision. The larger teams could employ full‐time AHAs, while managers from the smaller private practices stated that with fewer AHPs, meant less delegative work and did not fund a full‐time AHA position. A manager stated:

It's about volume of hours. I think that's where there will always be a mismatch. We do not have any full‐time AHAs and it wouldn't be sustainable…. and that's the thing, there's no provision for AHAs to go above and beyond therapists [AHPs]. They're reliant on therapist's [AHP's] delegation. (Manager 2)

Whilst employing AHAs part‐time reportedly reduced financial risk, the compromise created scheduling challenges. This included AHAs not being available when the NDIS client had scheduled therapy sessions. An AHP stated:

It's tricky, and also not knowing if the times you're free, and the family is free, will overlap with the time the AHA is in the office to do that work. (AHP 1)

Two AHPs also stated that the fast‐paced nature of private practice can make forward planning delegation tasks challenging. This meant that work opportunities for an AHA were sometimes missed.

I definitely think we could be delegating more if we were more organised. (AHP 3)

Fluctuating billable work for allied health assistants

Fluctuating volumes of billable work for an AHA was explicitly noted across three of the practices; reported by managers and AHPs as a financial barrier. AHAs billable work is dependent on AHP delegation and participants acknowledged that not all AHP work is appropriate to delegate. A manager reported about his own allied health caseload:

If I haven't got any work on, I don't have any work on. So….it could be 10 h [of delegative work] in 1 week, but then I may not have the work for that particular job for another 3 month. (Manager 1)

Meeting salary expectations

A challenge identified by AHAs and managers was meeting salary expectations for high‐performing AHAs. An AHA stated, ‘I certainly had to advocate for my salary expectations to be met’ (AHA 1). A manager spoke of the challenges about the current AHA billable rate in the NDIS price guide and how this influenced recruitment of skilled AHAs:

It's [NDIS] not a model that has been well developed in terms of price guides to recognise what should be expected. When you set the bar low and you have limited margin contribution, you are getting what you pay for. (Manager 2)

Theme 2: Reduced confidence

Reduced confidence was coded in three main contexts. Firstly, AHAs and AHPs reported limited formal training which impacted on confidence to delegate. Secondly, it takes time to build AHAs' confidence in disability service delivery. Finally, AHPs were also lacking in confidence to delegate within the context of complex disability.

Limited formal disability delegation training

AHPs reflected that a barrier for delegation was their lack of formal training to work with AHAs. Formal training in this context included both university training, and postgraduate professional development opportunities. Reconceptualising therapy plans to include an AHA was an identified challenge and required explicit effort. An AHP reflected:

I think I can definitely say that for me, not utilising AHAs is my own old habits. (AHP 1)

Similarly, AHAs reported their learning about disability had largely occurred within the workplace, with very little content covered in the certificate three AHA course.

The course has been good, but a lot of it [learning content] I don't use here [in NDIS disability practice]. (AHA 3)

Building confidence takes time

It was acknowledged by AHPs and managers that building AHAs' confidence and competencies in disability practice takes time. A supervising AHP stated:

I'd say it takes around 6–12 months of more focus on the therapy programs before the AHAs would think ‘I feel confident that I can implement this program’. (AHP 2)

Another supervising AHP reported from their observations, it can take up to 1–2 years before the AHA starts to demonstrate increased and consistent confidence. AHAs recalled their learning journey as an ongoing learning experience, which takes time:

‘There is so much to know in disability….’ (AHA 1) and another ‘….because it was a rapid time of information uptake [the first 6 months in the role]….the time that I wanted to spend on learning things that I thought I needed to know to catch myself up, I found that the other [therapists] were not as quick to value [the learning timeframe]’. (AHA 2)

A manager stated:

It did require a lot of training to bring the AHA ‘up to speed’ [for clinical practice]. Which was OK, but we have to put the time in. (Manager 1)

This was the consensus across all roles – that time was required to bolster AHA confidence and performance.

The skills and experience required for NDIS referrals

Three AHPs cautioned delegation to an assistant because of the described nuanced disability knowledge required to work with NDIS clients and that this was a barrier for delegation. An AHP reflected:

It's important for me to feel confident in knowing that the AHA will be able to do a good job of the work I have delegated. (AHP 3)

Another identified the challenge of creating a delegation plan that encompassed a variety of possible situational outcomes:

…doing things like planning up and down contingencies, which you do at university, but I wouldn't normally plan this explicitly in sessions….it's something that is sort of adjusted in‐the‐moment. (AHP 1)

A manager who is also an AHP articulated a similar reflection:

Being able to implement the strategies at the right time, or whatever strategies they are – this requires a bit of skill, and timing, and experience to get it right. (Manager 1)

Delegation plans take explicit effort and reconceptualisation for AHPs to safely delegate to an AHA.

Theme 3: Variation in tasks for AHA retention

Participants in this study reported a facilitator for AHA implementation was having increased variety in the AHA's tasks.

Multidisciplinary teams

Two of the private practices were comprised of multidisciplinary teams. Participants in this study reported that increased variety in the AHA tasks was a facilitator for the implementation. Managers reported that multidisciplinary teams offered increased variety of work tasks, resulting in the AHAs remaining in the organisation for longer (longer than 2 years). In contrast, single‐discipline practices reported higher AHA turnover (less than a year). It is acknowledged that retention is not singularly explained by multidisciplinary work; however, participants stated it can assist. An AHA working in a multidisciplinary practice stated:

I like working across the disciplines….and now I'm working with Speechies [Speech Pathologists], which I also find really interesting. (AHA 3)

The two AHPs reported that it was helpful to have AHAs trained to work with multiple disciplines, because this increased the AHA's overall competencies. An AHP articulated:

I think allowing more opportunities for AHAs to have those, not just singular discipline experience, but to give them as many opportunities as we can, then we can get them higher caseloads because they've already got some really lovely foundations that you've got confidence in their knowledge. (AHP 3)

In multidisciplinary teams, the training requirements were shared across AHPs, which eased the requirements for a single supervising therapist to hold all AHA training responsibilities.

Variation in tasks

Including a variety of tasks for AHAs counterbalanced the acknowledged ‘dry’ aspects of an AHA's role (Manager 1). The type of work completed by an AHA can be perceived to have reduced stimulation.

‘We're expecting the AHA to do maintenance programs, which therapists can find a bit dry’ (Manager 1). A manager acknowledged that repetitive, non‐specialised tasks, may impact the AHA's workplace satisfaction and where possible implemented strategies to increase task variety. AHPs reported delegating non‐specialised tasks to be a facilitator for them to practice at the top of their scope. An AHP identified value in billing the NDIS client a lower rate for tasks that could be completed by an AHA because it does not require specialist knowledge or skills to complete.

To have the option for these tasks to be billed at a more appropriate rate [the AHA rate compared to AHP rate] is a good option for participants [NDIS clients]. (AHP 1)

Theme 4: AHP and AHA dynamic

This theme represented the importance of professional relationships as a facilitator for AHA implementation. Factors highlighted within this theme's broader scope are listed below.

Relationships are important

A well‐connected relational dynamic between an AHP and AHA was identified by all participants to be a facilitator for increasing the volume of delegation. AHPs spoke of this dynamic, if going well, assisting their experiences of time pressures, staff shortages and burnout. When things were working well between the AHP and AHA, it was described as having added value to the quality of therapy service delivery.

Collaboration

Value was seen in the process of collaboration. When the AHA could constructively contribute to service delivery, this made for reduced pressure on the AHP. Supervising AHPs reported that frequent meetings for debriefing and feedback about the therapy service delivery between AHAs and AHPs were beneficial for deepening the professional relationship and increasing AHPs delegation of tasks to AHAs.

Managing workload demands and burnout

AHPs found it comforting to have an AHA on the team to assist with competing demands and burnout:

‘We have so much pressure on AHPs that I think knowing that we've got AHAs who can manage some of those pressure around you is helpful’. (AHP 3). Another AHP reflected on the long‐term nature of disability service delivery and the impact this can have on therapist burnout:

There are some instances where you never really get a break from the same clients unless you leave an organisation. Unless you work in blocks, there is the potential that you've got this longstanding relationship with a complex case for years. So, I see potential there if you're doing alternating blocks with an AHA, that they could then continue on and give the clinicians a bit of a break, knowing that there is still someone there [with the NDIS client]. (AHP 1)

Access to senior AHPs

A manager highlighted a key facilitator for the success of an AHA role in the team was having strong leadership from senior AHPs:

It's been demonstrated that where there is fierce advocacy for the role, it can work. (Manager 2)

The other manager noted that senior AHPs bring with them a foundation of knowledge to better identify safe delegation tasks and assist the team with this decision‐making.

DISCUSSION

The content analysis brought to light several relevant dilemmas that exist in the private disability sector in regional NT. Four main points are derived from this study, which will be discussed in the following paragraphs.

Financial considerations for the existence of the practice

Private disability providers are reliant on revenue. How AHAs contribute to a billable model of practice remains relatively unexplored. This study attempted to fill some gaps in knowledge. The AHA employment model from this study reflect the findings from Somerville et al., 24 that 24% of AHP work could be delegated to an AHA. Currently, employing AHAs in a part‐time or casual basis reflects the billable work being delegated from AHPs. Administration support and AHA was a common dual employment model, which is consistent with findings from research in other sectors. 20 , 37 This employment model is a facilitator due to the nature of the high administrative tasks required for NDIS disability providers. Managers identified that a dual role was a helpful solution to fill gaps in administrative support and AHA work when it arose. This employment model may change with more research, to gain a better understanding of how to incorporate full‐time AHAs into a billable model of practice.

Confidence is built through knowledge

Employers in this study confirmed findings from Huglin et al. 21 as employers also did not prioritise certificate three or four AHA training in the recruitment process. Managers and some AHPs in this study noted that AHAs were more likely to succeed in the role if they had previous exposure to disability. This exposure was identified by AHAs as missing in their formal training. AHPs also identified gaps in their formal training, that impact their ability to confidently delegate work to an AHA. There are existing professional development opportunities available for AHP to upskill in delegation; however, this was not something that had been sought after by participants in this study. AHPs reported they were often reluctant to delegate tasks—with perceived deficiencies in their skillsets being a barrier. In other sectors, there has also been an unwillingness to delegate clinical tasks due to the perceived complexity of the patient population. 37 These findings were also supported in this study. Further research is required to deeply understand the perceived barriers for AHPs to delegate to AHA.

AHPs identified that disability therapy in Darwin is dynamic and complex. One of the eligibility criteria for NDIS funding is having a ‘permanent impairment’ that ‘substantially reduces functional capacities’. 32 This definition alludes to the complexity of some clinical presentations in NDIS practice. In the NT, there is an added layer of complexity due to population. Here, trauma‐informed care is a key skill in therapy service delivery. 33 Allied health professional NDIS work in the NT is often complex, and how the role of AHAs fits within this is yet to be clear.

Time requirements

The professional relationship between AHPs and AHAs was identified as a facilitator when working well. Whilst this finding is also supported in existing literature, 27 , 28 , 38 how time is allocated within a billable practice model to foster this dynamic is unknown. The introduction of the NDIS has created a strong culture focused on billable hours. Whilst participants spoke about the importance of building strong relationships, aside from the strategy of scheduling regular supervision time, how effective professional relationships were established in private disability practices, when there is such little time allocated to do so is unclear.

In relation to time requirements, increased ‘on the job’ training for AHAs is highlighted in existing literature. 21 , 37 This finding was also well supported in this study. Private disability practices were required to spend more time upskilling and building AHAs' confidence within the workplace than they had expected. Two participants estimated that it takes 6–24 months of ‘on the job’ training before AHAs confidently deliver services. This timeframe is not sustainable for most private disability practices when training time equals revenue loss.

Creating a sustainable allied health workforce

Findings from this study indicate that when AHAs demonstrate competencies in NDIS disability practice, this relieves work pressures for AHPs. Participants identified that AHAs can provide a necessary client break and increase the variety of service outputs. This adds diversity to the AHP's workload and can facilitate AHP retention. At a time when AHPs' career longevity is reducing, 39 this is an important finding. However, burnout and longevity for AHAs are also important to consider. This study also highlighted that employers are struggling to meet AHA salary expectations, which is consistent with other existing literature 21 ; however, this study shows that this can be somewhat counterbalanced by providing diverse experiences for the AHA, such as working across AHP disciplines.

Increased numbers of senior AHPs were identified as key facilitators for successful AHA retention. However, the definition of ‘senior’ must be approached with caution. The workforce in the NT has a high number of early career workers relative to other states. In 2022, 66% of the allied health workforce in the NT were in their first 5 years of career experience. 40 What this may mean for the NT allied health workforce is that AHP teams may have reduced capacity to lead AHA service delivery, therefore impacting on best implementation.

Limitations and research directions

This study was a preliminary investigation into this research topic. There were limitations, including a small sample size, single author coding and a single regional context. Furthermore, this study was completed with attention to participant anonymity which limited the detail and specificity that could be provided in our work. Future research will include the perspective of NDIS clients receiving service from AHAs.

CONCLUSION

The NT is known to have high levels of underserviced health needs and social disadvantage. 12 Furthermore, the location struggles to attract and retain a skilled allied health workforce. 40 Employing and appropriately training AHAs is a potential way to meet the staffing shortfall. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Moving forward, research is required to inform the extent to which AHAs can be utilised in the private disability sector to assist the allied health workforce to meet population needs.

The findings from this paper lead us to call for change on an institutional, government and private provider level. For institutions, there was insistent messaging that improved training requirements are needed for the success of an AHA role in NDIS disability practice. University and vocational training sectors must acknowledge that a shift in content is needed to prepare AHA students and AHPs to implement safe delegation. For the government, short‐term funding is recommended to support NT private providers to cover training costs for AHAs so they can be utilised in a constructive way to better meet population needs. The allied health NDIS workforce is often called upon to work within complex public health contexts without government support. This is a large responsibility for private disability practices to carry. Finally, private disability practices need to prioritise fostering relationships between AHAs and AHPs for the role to be best utilised.

The NDIS has placed increased strain on the private disability sector and this study indicates that AHAs have added value in reducing this strain on AHPs. However, many variables impact successful AHA service delivery, which warrants further research, focus, and advocacy. The private disability sector needs support to increase service delivery outputs to meet the needs of the NT community. This is a unique opportunity to innovate in this space by providing support to the workforce and filling a longstanding service provision gap.
