Re-centering education on the learner: insights from a Pediatric Residency Coaching Program
Authors: Keegan D’Mello, Herman Singh Dayal, Andrea Ens, Marta Wilejto, Tamara Van Hooren, Robin Mackin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCoaching has emerged as a key educational strategy to promote learner success in competency-based medical education (CBME). Effectively operationalizing coaching requires ongoing exploration, with particular attention to its perceived benefits within the context of existing supports in residency training.MethodsA descriptive qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured interviews with pediatric residents and faculty coaches enrolled in a longitudinal coaching program. Eleven interviews explored participants' experiences, perceived benefits, and challenges of coaching. A thematic analysis included open coding followed by more focused coding, which informed the generation of themes.ResultsFour themes were identified. Overall, participants emphasized the value of coaching as an opportunity to refocus the education journey on the learner and foster individualized goal setting. Faculty shared the importance of a community of practice to provide space for reflection and to help mitigate doubt about their coaching effectiveness. Participants distinguished coaching and mentorship based on their structure and process rather than content covered, reinforcing the need for both supports. While benefits of coaching programs were recognized, words of caution were also shared.ConclusionLongitudinal coaching offers a means of re-centering the education journey on the learner. For programs seeking to adopt or refine coaching models in CBME, success will depend on fostering flexibility, supporting faculty through communities of practice, and maintaining learner-centered relationships grounded in reflection and growth.

Introduction

Coaching has emerged as an important model to support learner development and professional growth within competency-based medical education (CBME) (1–3). Endorsed by the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (2), coaching is a partnership that is founded in reflection and co-construction (3,4). When offered longitudinally, the coaching partnership can foster a growth-oriented culture that supports pediatric residents as they navigate their progression through CBME training (3,5,6).

Despite its theoretical appeal, the operationalization of coaching in medical education has been challenging (4). Much of the profession's understanding of coaching draws from its success in other domains such as sport, business, and the performing arts, yet medical training presents unique contextual barriers (7). The literature describes obstacles such as role ambiguity, administrative burden, competing clinical and educational demands, and variable faculty development, all of which can compromise the intended benefits of coaching (2,8–13). As a result, while longitudinal coaching has been promoted as a promising model for learner support, uncertainty remains about how it is experienced in practice and which implementation strategies create added value or added burden for residents and faculty.

This study explored the perceptions of Pediatric residents and faculty coaches participating in a longitudinal coaching program. Based on our program design, understanding how both groups perceive the purpose and value of coaching, especially with respect to existing mentorship opportunities, can inform best practices and provide important insights to refine longitudinal coaching models to ensure their intended benefit.

Methodology

A descriptive qualitative methodology was used to explore the perceptions of Pediatric residents and faculty participating in the coaching program at Western University.

Setting

The Western Pediatric residency program is a medium sized program in Canada (10 residents/training year) that implemented a longitudinal coaching program in 2021 in line with the official implementation of CBME in Pediatric programs across Canada.

Coaching program process and structure

In 2021 to 2022, all incoming PGY1 residents were assigned a faculty coach for the duration of their training (4 years). All faculty coaches and resident coachees were provided education and materials before program enrollment. This outlined their role, expectations and required skills. Coaching guides and a coaching contract were designed for both the coach and the coachee to serve as a resource to support the partnership. Coaching pairs are expected to meet every 2 to 3 months with documentation of a meeting summary. Coaches have access to their residents' assessment data and are invited as a nonvoting member to attend the competence committee review of their coachee. A community of practice for coaches was created through optional “Check-in” meetings held three to four times per year where faculty coaches can come together and participate in a facilitated discussion to share their successes, challenges, and needs. Education materials and professional development opportunities are shared with the coaches and resident representatives on an ongoing basis and collated on a website for reference.

Participants

With the launch of CBME, our residency program transitioned from a reactive academic advisor program (reserved for residents in difficulty) to proactively assigning all CBME residents (N = 18) with a longitudinal coach. Non-CBME residents who wanted to voluntarily enroll in the program were supported. We conveniently sampled faculty and residents enrolled in our residency coaching program from June 2022 to October 2023. Recruitment was conducted through an e-mail invitation that was sent to all active faculty coaches (N = 18) and residents who had been in the coaching program for at least 9 months. In total, we interviewed 11 participants (7 faculty and 4 residents). Faculty and residents in the coaching program who were involved in the program design or study author team were not eligible to participate.

Data collection and analysis

After providing informed consent, participants were invited to participate in a 45-min virtual semi-structured interview conducted by a research coordinator skilled in interviewing who had no involvement in the Department of Pediatrics. The interview guide contained probes that elicited participants' perceptions on the benefits, challenges and/or burden of the program and their conceptualization of coaching and mentorship based on their experience as a coach or coachee in the program. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim with identifying information removed.

A thematic analysis was conducted by the authors (KD, HS, and RM) using N-Vivo qualitative analysis software. Using an inductive approach, we independently open coded three transcripts before meeting to generate a codebook. Consistent with this approach, we did not employ a sensitizing framework but the research team brought familiarity with relevant literature on coaching, mentorship and community of practice to the analysis. During the open coding process, the authors went line by line through the first three transcripts, making annotations and assigning codes to each line of the transcript. This informed the generation of the codebook. Coding took place iteratively to allow for exploration of new or emerging ideas and determining when recruitment could cease. Using the codebook, KD and HS coded the remaining eight transcripts. On an ongoing basis, KD, HS, and RM reviewed the coded data and consolidated it into themes. Recruitment ceased when no new or emerging ideas were identified. Following the thematic analysis, two authors (MW and AE) triangulated the findings to ensure the themes were representative of the data.

Reflexivity

The research team brought perspectives shaped by experiences with coaching both within and outside of medical education. RM, MW, AE, and TV are clinician educators and education leaders with shared interests in operationalizing coaching within medical education to support learners while balancing the demands placed on faculty. RM, MW, and AE were coaches in this program. HS contributed a learner perspective as a medical student, while KD, as a research assistant, brought experience with coaching both within and outside of medical education. These collective perspectives informed our interpretation.

The study was approved by Western University's Research Ethics Board (Project #118537).

Results

Participants shared several benefits of a longitudinal coaching program, particularly its role in shaping reflective pediatricians who are self-directed, while also offering words of caution. With respect to its longitudinal nature and individualized goal orientation, coaching emerged as an important mechanism to support ongoing development of pediatric trainees. Most notably, coaching was recognized as an avenue to refocus the educational journey on the learner and foster growth minded behaviors and attitudes. However, these benefits can be undermined if there is a lack of flexibility and misaligned expectations from either party. These results were captured in four themes that we will further elaborate in the paragraphs below.

Theme #1: Refocusing the education journey on the individual learner

Participants described longitudinal coaching as a platform to re-center the education journey on the individual learner. Faculty valued the continuity of the relationship, noting that it provided a “bird's-eye view” of a resident's growth across clinical, academic, and professional domains (Faculty P6). This longitudinal perspective afforded learners' space to reflect on the “big picture” in an era where CBME and entrustable professional activities (EPAs) has arguably “pulled the training experience apart to pieces” (Faculty, P3).

as a coach, while I want to help them meet those competencies and finish the program, I think it's important that we're able to knit things back together for them, like how does this speak to who you are as a whole physician? (Faculty, P3)

Faculty described how they encouraged residents to consider broader questions such as, “What kind of physician do you want to be?” (Faculty P8). Residents echoed this emphasis on learner-led goal setting and appreciated how coaching empowered them to identify and pursue their own priorities: “The coach is there to help formulate and define those goals and hold the residents accountable” (Resident P10). Several residents credited the process with the ability to realize their own growth over time and a shift toward a growth mindset that fostered motivation and well-being,

..I think having someone there to always create a learning mindset, a growth mindset instead of me having a fixed mindset, I think has been the biggest change. So now I catch myself, I’m having more of a growth mindset talk within myself which has helped overall in terms of day-to-day happiness. (Resident, P11)

By promoting self-reflection and goal setting, coaching fosters a holistic integration of learning experiences with the opportunity to support professional identity formation.

Theme #2: Distinguishing mentorship from coaching

Participants were able to differentiate coaching from mentorship, emphasizing that both are valuable but serve distinct purposes in a resident's learning journey. Coaching was described as a goal-oriented, learner-driven partnership focused on promoting growth, whereas mentorship offered guidance based on shared experiences and mentor expertise. As one faculty member explained, “As a coach, it has nothing to do with who I am as a coach…whereas someone I’m mentoring, I assume wants to be like me in a certain aspect. The primary goals are a bit different” (Faculty P2).

Residents echoed this distinction, noting that mentors tend to offer advice, while coaches help them identify and develop areas for improvement. One resident summarized, “A mentor is more that advice providing, whereas a coach is a little bit more focused on your areas for growth… I’m going to help you through that until you get to where you need to be” (Resident P10).

Despite these distinctions, participants recognized the fluidity between the two roles. Both faculty and residents acknowledged that coaches may at times adopt a mentoring stance and that this dynamic exchange can enhance the relationship rather than threaten its intent. As one faculty participant reflected,

I think there's overlap and I think there's often stuff that is more questions towards the mentor role as opposed to the coaching role. And I don't think that's a problem necessarily (Faculty, P5).

This quote highlights the importance of embracing the opportunity for coaches to don a “mentor hat” at times throughout the relationship recognizing when and how to shift between coaching and mentorship may strengthen the relationship and optimize support for resident growth.

Theme #3: Fostering confidence through a community of practice

Faculty emphasized that coaching is an evolving skill that benefits from collective reflection and shared learning. Many expressed uncertainties about their effectiveness as coaches, particularly given limited feedback mechanisms. As one participant noted,

There's no anonymous way for coachees to provide their coaches with feedback… I could be going through thinking, ‘Oh I’m doing a great job coaching,’ and my coachees feel like they don’t have the support they need (Faculty P6).

Engaging in a community of practice helped mitigate these concerns and normalize the challenges of coaching. Regular meetings among faculty coaches created opportunities to exchange experiences, troubleshoot difficulties, and validate each other's efforts. “It's always validating when you hear that other people are having the same struggles…and being able to troubleshoot some of those things” (Faculty P6). This collegial environment was described as both reassuring and motivating, enabling faculty to “learn from each other” (Faculty P2) and continue refining their coaching approach.

By fostering connection and shared reflection, the community of practice served as an avenue to strengthen faculty confidence and reinforced coaching as a developmental opportunity.

Theme #4: Words of caution

While faculty and residents valued the benefits of longitudinal coaching, they also identified challenges that could undermine its success if left unaddressed. Faculty highlighted the difficulty of balancing coaching responsibilities with competing clinical and educational demands warning that if you “add on more and more commitments” it can compromise the investment because “there's too many competing priorities” (Faculty P6). Sustaining meaningful engagement required adequate time and institutional support.

Residents emphasized the importance of flexibility within the coaching relationship and collaborative goal setting. When meetings were overly structured, they felt agency was reduced. Conversely, adaptability enhanced the experience by making it more learner focused. One resident reflected,

My first coaching meeting…was a bit more formalized… But what I found was my coach was very eager to get feedback on the style… and he's been extremely helpful with trying to figure out where I need to be (Resident P9).

Both groups agreed that some flexibility within the expectations and meeting styles over time was key to maintaining a meaningful partnership. Unrealistic assumptions about a resident's ability to construct goals could threaten the experience reinforcing the importance of co-construction.

Together, these reflections highlight the practical considerations necessary for implementing meaningful and effective longitudinal coaching programs.

Discussion

Our findings highlight how longitudinal coaching can meaningfully refocus postgraduate education on the learner through self-reflection, goal setting and conversations about professional identity. Faculty and residents described coaching as a structured yet flexible partnership that promotes conversations that connects progressive attainment of competence with a broad overview of who they are becoming as pediatricians. By encouraging learners to develop ongoing goals and reflect on their evolving professional identities, coaching acts as a counterbalance to the checklist mentality often described in CBME (14). These findings position coaching as a means of restoring meaning and learner agency within CBME.

While CBME has advanced postgraduate medical education, its granular focus on competencies and frequent EPA completion can fragment the learning experience (15). Participants in this study highlighted how longitudinal coaching re-focuses the education journey on the learner by maintaining a holistic view of progress that includes assessment, wellness, professional values, and long-term aspirations. Coaches described the partnership as a means of “knitting things back together,” allowing learners to step back from individual EPAs and integrating them within bigger conversations about who they are striving to be. In doing so, coaching fosters reflection and promotes a growth mindset—attributes critical to sustaining engagement and motivation within CBME training (5,6,16).

A novel contribution of this study is the identification of a faculty community of practice (CoP) as a key mechanism for building coaching competence and sustaining engagement. Many coaches expressed uncertainty about their effectiveness, often compounded by limited opportunities for feedback from coachees. Elster et al (2025) explored challenges faced by coaches and have described coaches feeling high personal responsibility for their learners' success potentially leading to emotional burden (17). By having regular coaching “check-in” meetings, it provided a forum to share experiences, troubleshoot challenges, and validate mutual struggles to counteract the potential of emotional burden. This sense of shared purpose enhanced confidence and commitment, echoing broader faculty development literature that underscores the value of peer learning and collective reflection (4). Embedding a CoP into program design can therefore strengthen coaching quality and continuity, particularly in the early stages of implementation.

Participants also offered pragmatic lessons for sustaining longitudinal coaching reinforcing that successful coaching program require attention to role clarity and flexibility. Faculty and residents distinguished coaching from mentorship but emphasized that the boundaries between these roles are fluid. While the literature offers definitions and distinctions of coaching and mentorship (1,5,6,18,19), the findings of this study highlight how the coaching relationship is strengthened when coaches are able to move flexibly between them. Coaching was viewed as a partnership that situates the learner as the expert and is a goal-directed process that supports growth through reflection and goal setting. On the other hand, mentorship requires mentor expertise and lived experiences to advise the mentee. Our results suggest that rigid distinctions between coaching and mentorship may feel artificial. Rather, faculty development efforts should focus on equipping and empowering both residents and faculty to intentionally navigate the fluidity between these overlapping roles. Faculty development initiatives can emphasize that effective coaching focuses less on what is discussed and more on how by fostering co-construction and reflection.

This study has several limitations. Conducted within a single pediatric residency program during the early stages of implementation, it will be important for future work to examine the sustainability of longitudinal coaching models and their long-term impact on learner professional identity formation. While formal program evaluation was beyond the scope of this study, the coaching program undergoes ongoing annual evaluation looking at outcomes such as faculty participation in “check-ins,” faculty retention and participant feedback. Integration of data from program evaluation in future work may provide an additional layer of triangulation to further support these findings. In this study, we aimed to explore both resident and faculty perceptions but acknowledge that less trainees were interviewed. Recruitment took place post-COVID during a time of unseasonably high Pediatric volumes leading to burnout among residents and faculty. Iterative analysis demonstrated similar themes within trainee interviews with important messages that warrant inclusion in the study.

Conclusion

Longitudinal coaching offers a means of re-centering the education journey on the learner. For programs seeking to adopt or refine coaching models in the CBME era, success will depend on fostering flexibility, supporting faculty through communities of practice, and maintaining learner-centered relationships grounded in reflection and growth.
