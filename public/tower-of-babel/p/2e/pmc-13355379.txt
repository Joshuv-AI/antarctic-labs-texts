Feasibility of opportunistic screening for preserved ratio impaired spirometry using chest radiography-based deep learning models
Authors: Akifumi Yoshida, Chiharu Kai, Ikumi Sato, Hitoshi Futamura, Kunihiko Oochi, Satoshi Kondo, Satoshi Kasai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough deep learning models using chest radiographs can estimate spirometry measurements, further investigation is needed to evaluate their ability to screen for preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm), an important pre-COPD subtype. This study assessed whether a chest radiograph-based deep learning model can accurately detect PRISm and perform efficient opportunistic screening.MethodsThis retrospective study included 54654 paired chest radiography and spirometry datasets from 38470 health checkup participants at a Japanese institution who underwent chest radiography and spirometry on the same day in 2018 and 2019. The dataset of 80% participants was used for model development, and the remaining 20% was used for performance evaluation. We developed a deep learning model that used frontal chest radiographs and demographic scalar inputs to detect PRISm and other pulmonary dysfunction subtypes, including FEV₁ decline, FVC decline, FVC and FEV₁ decline, and airflow limitation. Model performance was evaluated on the internal testing dataset. Subgroup analyses were performed across five independent factors: age, height, ppFVC, ppFEV₁, and FEV₁/FVC.ResultsThe model achieved an AUROC of 0.892 (95% CI, 0.875–0.906), a sensitivity of 71.6%, and a specificity of 87.9% for PRISm detection on the testing dataset (N = 10917). No significant differences in AUROC were observed across subgroups defined by age, height, FEV₁/FVC, or ppFEV₁. The AUROC values for detecting all pulmonary dysfunction subtypes exceeded 0.8.ConclusionsThe chest radiography-based model can effectively detect PRISm and may be useful for opportunistic screening of PRISm for supporting early risk management and intervention for pre-COPD conditions and obstructive pulmonary diseases.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) accounts for approximately 85%–90% of obstructive airway diseases and is the third leading cause of death worldwide [1], [2.]. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in 2024 revealed that the prevalence of COPD in individuals aged 40 years was 12.64% using the fixed ratio criteria (95% confidence interval [CI] 10.75%–14.65%) and 7.38% (95% CI 5.47%–9.55%) using the lower limit of normal criteria [3]. Diagnosing COPD is difficult in asymptomatic patients, and the COPD diagnostic rate worldwide is reportedly 10% [4], [5]. However, undiagnosed COPD is associated with increased risk of mortality [2]. Spirometry is employed to definitively diagnose COPD [6], [7], [8]. A ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) to forced vital capacity (FVC) of less than 0.7 (FEV1/FVC < 0.7) indicates that the patient has airflow limitation [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]. The criterion for the definitive diagnosis of COPD is the presence of airflow limitation post-bronchodilation [8], [11], [12]. COPD is an irreversible pathology in which airflow limitation does not sufficiently improve, preventing pulmonary function from recovering to its healthy state with COPD progression [6], [7], [8]. Hence, the timely detection of the precursor state of COPD is crucial for the early diagnosis of COPD and for improving patient prognosis [6], [13], [14]. However, the utilization of spirometry for COPD screening is limited among the elderly and smokers, for whom spirometry is recommended [15], [16]. Delayed COPD detection prevents the provision of early intervention to patients [6], [14]. Using convenient methods other than spirometry to opportunistically identify the precursor conditions of COPD could help improve patient prognosis by enabling follow-up and management of high-risk cases [17].

According to the GOLD 2023 report, the term “pre-COPD” is used for individuals with structural lung lesions (e.g., emphysema) and/or physiological abnormalities, (including low FEV1, gas trapping, hyperinflation, reduced lung diffusing capacity, and/or rapid FEV1 decline) without airflow obstruction (FEV1/FVC ≥ 0.7 post-bronchodilation) [14]. Pre-COPD is a precursor to persistent airflow obstruction; pre-COPD patients are at a high risk of developing COPD [14], [18]. Among the pre-COPD subtypes, preserved ratio impaired spirometry (PRISm) has been proposed to identify individuals with normal ratio but abnormal spirometry [14]. PRISm is perceived as a transitional state to COPD and high-risk condition for its development [14], [19], [20]. Although the definition of PRISm varies across studies, it is defined as having a percent predicted FEV1 (ppFEV1) and percent predicted FVC (ppFVC) of less than 80% (ppFEV1 < 80% and ppFVC < 80%) and no airflow limitation (FEV1/FVC ≥ 0.7) [19]. PRISm, characterized by ppFEV1 and ppFVC decline, has an estimated 5-year progression rate to COPD of approximately 25% and is considered a precursor phenotype of COPD [19]. Notably, the COPDGene study, a major cohort study, reported that FVC decline is an essential factor in stratifying the prognosis of PRISm [19]. Meanwhile, PRISm has the potential to improve pulmonary function with an appropriate intervention, and early detection and intervention of PRISm is expected to prevent progression to COPD [11], [19], [20].

One of the most promising conventional methods for PRISm detection is the parametric response mapping (PRM) analysis of chest computed tomography (CT) [21]. Lu et al. reported that a nomogram model, which takes radiomic features from inspiration and expiration chest CT imaging with PRM, is capable of detecting PRISm with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.786 [22]. Although the method of extracting PRISm-related information from imaging test data is hopeful, it is not applicable to typical chest CT imaging as PRM analysis of chest CT employs expiratory imaging [21], [22]. To effectively promote opportunistic screening, it is more suitable to employ a method that can accurately and opportunistically detect PRISm from commonly collected medical data in primary care settings [15]. In this context, recent studies have evaluated pulmonary function from chest radiographs using machine learning models [23], [24], [25]. These studies have suggested that chest radiographs contain information related to FVC, FEV1, ppFEV1, and FEV1/FVC in spirometry and that pulmonary function on ventilation can be accurately estimated using deep learning models [23], [24], [25]. However, those previous studies using chest radiograph-based deep learning models have not evaluated detection performance for PRISm.

This study focused on the opportunistic screening of PRISm during chest radiography, particularly in health checkups. Chest radiography is the most commonly performed imaging modality in primary care and various clinical settings. It has been widely used for many years in both individual medical care and public health programs. The World Health Organization strongly recommends systematic screening and active case identification with chest radiography among populations with high risks for tuberculosis, including migrants, detainees, and homeless persons [26]. Thus, chest radiography is widely used in tuberculosis screening of immigrants and occupational lung disease surveillance for dust-exposed workers [27], [28]. In Japan, chest radiography is systematically incorporated into the pre-employment and periodic employee health examinations mandated by national occupational health legislation. It is also commonly utilized in population-based lung cancer and tuberculosis screening programs for residents aged ≥ 40 [29]. Chest radiography is similarly incorporated into statutory health checkups and medical surveillance schemes for workers in hazardous occupations in other Asian countries, including China, South Korea, Taiwan, Indonesia, and the Philippines, and several Western countries [30], [31]. If PRISm can be detected timely in chest radiography, it may contribute to improved patient outcomes. In this study, we developed a chest radiograph-based model for PRISm detection. According to current guidelines [8], [12], spirometry is the gold standard for diagnosing COPD and airflow limitation. The intended use for the developed model is to detect PRISm during the interpretation of chest radiographs and to promote spirometry for detailed examination, enabling diagnosis and evaluation based on clinical guidelines.

This study aimed to evaluate the performance of a deep learning model in detecting PRISm using chest radiographs. Furthermore, this paper contributes to the performance estimation of the model for PRISm screening among the public undergoing health checkups and discusses the feasibility of opportunistic PRISm screening. To the best of our knowledge, previous research has not established a standard recall rate for PRISm or COPD screening. Meanwhile, the expected recall rate in lung cancer screening with low-dose CT is approximately 15% based on the Lung CT Screening Reporting & Data System v2022, with category 3 or higher as a definition of recall [32]. This paper reports the performance of the PRISm detection by the chest-radiography-based model, using this recall rate as a reference.

Methods

Study design and data

This retrospective study was designed to develop a chest radiography-based model for detecting PRISm, and to demonstrate the feasibility of PRISm screening in health checkups. This manuscript adheres to the Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) guidelines [33].

We retrospectively obtained 55331 sequential paired datasets of chest radiography and spirometry from 38854 patients who underwent chest radiography and spirometry examinations on the same day in 2018 and 2019 at health checkup facilities in Japan. Each participant underwent chest radiography, either as part of a mandatory employee health checkup required by Japanese law or at their own discretion. All spirometry was voluntary and performed at the discretion of the participant at the health checkup. Fig. 1 presents the inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with the corresponding number of patients and data. This study included sequential data consisting of chest radiography and spirometry obtained on the same day from eligible patients at eligible institutions within the study period. A total of 677 data points from 417 patients were excluded due to missing FVC and FEV1 measurements. The number of data points per patient ranged from 1 to 4. The number of patients by data count was 22781, 15202, 479, and 8, respectively. The interval between data acquisition dates averaged 363.4 days, with a median of 364 days, an interquartile range of 349–378 days, a minimum of 65 days, and a maximum of 717 days. These repeated data were not excluded and were included in the model development and testing. This study utilized 54654 paired datasets of chest radiography and spirometry from 38470 patients. Patients were randomly assigned to the training, tuning, or internal testing datasets in a 7:1:2 ratio. The numbers of data points following the distribution were 38249 (26940 patients) for training, 5488 (3844 patients) for tuning, and 10917 (7686 patients) for internal testing.Fig. 1Inclusion/exclusion criteria and number of relevant data used in the study. We used data from frontal chest radiographies and spirometry obtained during forced expiratory testing. The data acquisition parameters and preprocessing methods are provided in the Supplementary Material (section A). Predicted FEV1 and FVC were calculated using the reference equation based on the spirometry guidelines established by the Clinical Pulmonary Functions Committee of the Japanese Respiratory Society [34]; ppFEV1 and ppFVC were obtained as the percentages of each patient’s measured FEV1 and FVC relative to their predicted values based on sex, age, and height. PRISm was defined as ppFEV1 < 80% and ppFVC < 80% and FEV1/FVC ≥ 0.7 [19]. To evaluate the model’s efficacy in PRISm detection compared with other common pulmonary dysfunction subtypes, the following four subtypes were assigned: airflow limitation (FEV1/FVC < 0.7), FEV1 decline (ppFEV1 < 80%), FVC decline (ppFVC < 80%), and FEV1 and FVC decline (ppFEV1 < 80% and ppFVC < 80%).

Model development

Fig. 2 presents a diagram of the developed model. The model was designed to simultaneously identify the presence of five pulmonary dysfunction subtypes, including PRISm. The model comprises two types of input layers: the image-input layer for chest radiographs and the scalar-input layer for patients’ sex, age, and height. We used the image recognition model as an image encoder by removing its final fully connected layer. Baseline of the image encoder was ConvNeXt-XL with an input resolution of 384 × 384 [35], [36]. Furthermore, a fully connected layer with a rectified linear unit was used as a scalar encoder to increase features from the patients’ scalar features of sex, age, and height. The classification layer was configured as a multilayer perceptron (MLP) comprising two fully connected layers and a rectified linear unit. The model’s inputs consisted of a chest radiograph as an image in a 384 × 384 matrix and scalar values for sex, age, and height. Age and height were standardized to obtain a mean and variance of 0 and 1, respectively, and sex was coded as 0 for male and 1 for female at the scalar input layer. The input images and scalar features were converted into feature vectors using image and scalar encoders, respectively, and then integrated before being fed into the MLP. The MLP with five nodes in the final fully connected layer estimated five probabilities for each subtype, enabling binary classification by applying thresholds. The training dataset was used to train a binary classification model for the five distinct subtypes of pulmonary dysfunction. The target pulmonary dysfunction classes of the model were PRISm, airflow limitation, FEV1 decline, FVC decline, and FEV1 and FVC decline. The model’s outputs were estimated probabilities for each of the five subtypes, which were utilized to perform binary classification tasks with variable thresholds. To reduce the effects of the imbalanced numbers of positive and negative cases in each dysfunction class on training, we used a class-weighted binary cross-entropy loss as the loss function. This weights the loss values for positive examples. The other training conditions are detailed in the Supplemental Material (section C).

Evaluation

Model performance was assessed based on outputs on the internal testing dataset. To evaluate the performance of AI-enhanced chest radiography for the timely detection of PRISm, all results for patients’ repeated measurements data in the testing dataset were included in the evaluation. Additionally, to account for potential bias in results from repeated data, the PRISm detection performance was also assessed using only the earliest data points within the period. The study endpoint was the spirometry results obtained on the same day as the chest radiography; follow-up was not included. AUROC, sensitivity, and specificity were used to assess detection performance for PRISm and each pulmonary dysfunction subtype. Sensitivity and specificity were evaluated using the threshold for a 15% recall rate of 15% for PRISm, based on the recall rates for lung cancer screening by low-dose CT with Lung-RADS v2022 [32]. The Bootstrap method was employed to obtain the CI of the AUROC. To determine the factors affecting the model’s performance in PRISm detection, intra-factor subgroups were assigned on five independent factors: age, height, ppFEV1, ppFVC, and FEV1/FVC; details for each subgroup are provided in the Supplemental Material (section D). For each of the five factors, ROCs were obtained based on the model outputs for the evaluation data attributed to each intra-factor subgroup. The AUROCs stratified by intra-factor subgroups were compared and evaluated for each factor. The DeLong-U test was employed to estimate the probability of differences between AUROCs in each intra-factor subgroup, whereas Bonferroni’s multiple comparison method was employed for statistical tests with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

Table 1 presents the attributes of the case data utilized in the study. In the overall data, the prevalence of PRISm was 4.85%. The prevalence rates of other pulmonary dysfunction subtypes were 6.59% for FVC decline, 13.12% for FEV1 decline, 5.40% for FEV1 and FVC decline, and 4.24% for airflow limitation. In the internal testing dataset, the prevalence rates were 4.77% for PRISm, 6.42% for FVC decline, 12.99% for FEV1 decline, 5.29% for FEV1 and FVC decline, and 4.30% for airflow limitation. Table 2 summarizes the number and proportion of PRISm cases in each subgroup within the evaluation dataset. Fig. 3 presents the AUROC for PRISm detection on the internal testing dataset using the developed model, achieving an AUROC of 0.892 (95% CI 0.875–0.906). The ROC for PRISm detection using unique patient data after removing repeated measurements is in the Supplemental Material (Fig. A.1); the AUROC was 0.890 (95% CI 0.872–0.903). Table 3 summarizes the model’s performance for detecting PRISm on the internal testing dataset. For this dataset, adjusting the recall rate to 15% yielded a sensitivity of 71.6% and specificity of 87.9%. Fig. 4 presents the AUROC for the PRISm detection for each subgroup within the internal testing dataset, whereas Fig. 5 shows the AUROC and DeLong test results for each subgroup. For the age, height, and FEV1/FVC subgroups, the AUROCs were greater than 0.85 and did not show significant differences across the subgroups. In the ppFEV1 subgroup, the AUROCs were 0.777 (95% CI 0.727–0.820) for the < 70% subgroup and 0.796 (95% CI 0.763–0.817) for the 70%–80% subgroup and did not exhibit a significant difference across the subgroups (P = 0.507). The AUROCs for the ppFVC subgroup were 0.825 (95% CI 0.669–0.891) for the < 70% subgroup and 0.619 (95% CI 0.565–0.666) for the 70%–80% subgroup, with significant differences observed between the AUROCs for each subgroup (P < 0.001). Fig. 6 shows the AUROC for the detectability of pulmonary dysfunction subtypes other than PRISm, with AUROCs greater than 0.8 for all four subtypes: FEV1 decline, FVC decline, FVC and FEV1 decline, and airflow limitation. Discussion

The developed model achieved high performance, demonstrating an AUROC of 0.892 (95% CI 0.875–0.906) for PRISm detection using chest radiography data. To the best of our knowledge, this represents a substantial improvement in accuracy over previously published methods for detecting PRISm. The results of this study suggest that the developed model can accurately and opportunistically detect PRISm from chest radiography. Furthermore, the developed model was trained concurrently for the prevalence of pulmonary dysfunction subtypes. It demonstrated excellent detection performance, with an AUROC > 0.8 for each dysfunction subtype other than PRISm. Ueda et al. reported that the AUROCs for detecting FVC decline, FEV1 decline, and FEV1/FVC decline for multiple external validation datasets were 0.85–0.88, 0.87–0.87, and 0.83–0.87, respectively [25]. The results of this study are numerically comparable with these performances, demonstrating good detection performance not only for PRISm but also for various kinetic pulmonary dysfunction subtypes such as vital capacity and expiratory flow velocity from static imaging.

The method employed in this study relied solely on chest radiographs and associated patient information (sex, age, and height) as model inputs. The methods used in this study can be applied in diverse circumstances among people undergoing chest radiography and would be a beneficial means of increasing the opportunities to screen for PRISm. The highly accurate and opportunistic method for PRISm detection could be effectively used to recommend spirometry screening for asymptomatic patients and to expand the opportunities for promoting management and intervention in patients with PRISm.

The epidemiology of PRISm has been investigated in numerous countries and regions worldwide. A systematic review by Robertson et al. estimated the pooled prevalence of PRISm to be approximately 12% (95% CI 10%–15%) worldwide [37]. In an epidemiological study with a Japanese health checkup cohort, Kanetate et al. reported a PRISm prevalence of approximately 10% [38]. Based on this data, the present rate of PRISm in Japan is estimated to be approximately 7.4 million among the 74 million members of the Japanese population aged 15–64 years. When the model’s detection threshold was adjusted to achieve 90% specificity (resulting in approximately 6.66 million false positives), the sensitivity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and recall rate were 67.4%, 42.8%, 96.1%, and 15.7%, respectively. This enables the timely detection of PRISm in approximately 4.98 million people in Japan, with a recall rate of 15%–16%, comparable to that of lung cancer screening using LDCT. Adjusting the detection threshold to achieve 95% specificity (resulting in 3.33 million false positives), the sensitivity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and recall rate were 51.8%, 53.5%, 94.7%, and 9.68%, respectively. This would allow early detection of PRISm in 3.83 million people. Thus, assuming generalizability in Japan, it is estimated that approximately half of the PRISm patients could be detected with a false-positive rate of 5%.

This study targeted a population undergoing spirometry and chest radiography during health checkups at a single institution in Japan. In the previous study analyzing spirometry-tested populations, the prevalence of PRISm was 5.5% [39], comparable to the 4.77% observed in this study. In this population, most spirometry tests were voluntary. Consequently, biases driven by individuals’ health awareness and socioeconomic status may have influenced the results, yielding a lower PRISm prevalence in this study than in the general population. To decrease the influence of prevalence on model training, the model was trained using a loss function weighted by the proportion of cases with pulmonary dysfunction in the training dataset. However, if the model’s target population consists of a diverse group of citizens undergoing chest radiography, the prevalence estimated by PRISm may be higher than that found in this study; under such cases, the positive predictive value may be higher, while the negative predictive value is lower. Furthermore, this study developed and evaluated the model based on measurement data from a single institution. It would be valuable to further validate the model using data from multiple institutions to account for institutional factors, such as imaging conditions. Although these considerations exist, the novel findings of this study demonstrate that chest radiography can detect PRISm in a timely manner. However, for clinical application, future studies are required to conduct external validation using data from the target population to strengthen the generalizability of these findings.

In the stratified results of PRISm detection by subgroups showed no significant effects of age, height, FEV1/FVC, or ppFEV1 on the model’s robustness. However, a significant difference in AUROCs was found between the subgroups within ppFVC < 70% and 70–80%. As the range of ppFVC 70%–80% is close to the criterion threshold of 80% ppFVC for restrictive ventilatory defects [40], the imaging features associated with restrictive ventilatory defects may be relatively less prominent in the 70%–80% subgroup than in the ppFVC < 70% subgroup. Meanwhile, the ppFVC 70%–80% subgroup accounted for 5.07% of the testing dataset, and the PRISm cases represented 3.58% of the dataset. The PRISm detection sensitivity and specificity for this subgroup were 69.1% and 46.0%, respectively. Although the false-positive rate is relatively high at 54% in these borderline cases of ppFVC decline, re-evaluation with spirometry would allow diagnosing restrictive ventilatory dysfunction. Furthermore, given that PRISm may serve as a precursor to irreversible obstructive ventilatory defects, the model’s performance with prioritized sensitivity could be potentially reasonable.

This study had some limitations. First, its generalizability was limited by the use of internal testing only. We included no data from other institutions in our model development. This was because pulmonary function tests are not standard in either Japanese legally mandated periodic health checkups or the nationwide Specific Health Checkups program [41]. Guidelines explicitly recommends targeted spirometry as a second-stage test rather than for use with all test attendees [41]. Consequently, multi-center datasets that include both chest radiography and spirometry are scarce. This limited the opportunities for external testing available to us at this stage. Following a recent recommendation [42], we prioritized robust random hold-out testing with patient-level discrimination over underpowered external testing, as the latter can yield imprecise or misleading estimates. We intend to conduct pre-specified external testing of an adequately-sized sample of data from another institution once we have secured access to an eligible site. The second limitation of this study was potential bias due to the non-random nature of our sample. We developed and evaluated the model using data from a study population who underwent voluntary spirometry testing during Japanese health checkups. Therefore, the testing dataset was not representative of the overall patient population who undergo chest radiography in health checkups. Future randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate our model using real-world data, and validate its clinical utility and effectiveness. The third limitation is that the model’s detection performance of PRISm for borderline cases of FVC decline was lower than that for other subgroups with FVC decline. In future studies aimed at improving the robustness of the model’s PRISm detection performance, an approach that compensates for PRISm detection through the identification of cases with relatively high ppFVC might be beneficial.

Conclusions

The chest radiography-based deep learning model effectively detected PRISm. This study demonstrates the model’s feasibility in PRISm detection in health checkups, primary care, and other settings by determining suspected cases of PRISm via chest radiography.
