Effects of tea polyphenols on intestinal barrier, antioxidant capacity, and cecal microbiota in lion-head geese
Authors: Zhiqi Fu, Jing Fu, Yuchuan Wang, Ke Zhan, Yuwen Liang, Na Ao, Qidong Shen, Chunpeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTea polyphenols are natural bioactive compounds associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity and improved gut health in poultry. This study evaluated the effects of dietary supplementation with tea polyphenols on intestinal morphology, barrier integrity, antioxidant status, and cecal microbiota in lion-head geese. A total of 240 one-day-old male lion-head geese were randomly allocated to 2 treatments: a basal diet (control) or the same diet supplemented with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols (catechin purity, 50.4%) for 18 wk (6 replicates/treatment; 20 birds/replicate). Compared with the control, dietary supplementation with tea polyphenols significantly increased villus height and villus-to-crypt ratio (V/C) in the jejunum and ileum (P < 0.05) and reduced serum lipopolysaccharide (LPS) concentration (P < 0.05), whereas serum diamine oxidase (DAO) activity did not differ (P > 0.05). In the jejunum, mRNA expression of ZO-1, Claudin-5, and Occludin was significantly upregulated (P < 0.05); in the ileum, mRNA expression of ZO-1, Claudin-5, Occludin, and E-cadherin was significantly upregulated (P < 0.05). Tea polyphenols increased jejunal total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) and upregulated GPX1, GPX2, HO-1, and Nrf2 mRNA expression (P < 0.05). In the ileum, tea polyphenols significantly increased glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD) activities, decreased malondialdehyde (MDA) content, and upregulated SOD1, GPX1, GPX2, HO-1, and Nrf2 mRNA expression (P < 0.05). Metagenomic sequencing showed lower relative abundances of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobia at the phylum level (P < 0.05). At the genus level, tea polyphenols increased Prevotella and Subdoligranulum and decreased Oscillibacter and Desulfovibrio (P < 0.05). Functional annotation (KEGG, eggNOG, and CAZy) indicated enrichment of carbohydrate transport and metabolism, glycosyltransferases (GT), and polysaccharide lyases (PL) in the tea polyphenol group. Spearman correlation analysis indicated positive associations of Prevotella with KEGG thermogenesis and the two-component system, and of Desulfovibrio with biotin metabolism (P < 0.05). Overall, tea polyphenols promoted intestinal development, enhanced barrier- and antioxidant-related responses, and altered the composition and functional potential of the cecal microbiota, supporting improved gut health in lion-head geese.Keywords: Lion-head geese, Tea polyphenols, Intestinal barrier, Intestinal antioxidant capacity, Cecal microbiota

Introduction

The intestine is the primary site of digestion and nutrient absorption in poultry, and intestinal health is largely determined by the integrity of the mucosal barrier. Located at the interface between luminal contents and the internal milieu, this barrier provides the first line of defense against environmental stressors and pathogens by limiting transepithelial translocation of harmful molecules and microbes and thereby maintaining intestinal homeostasis (Duangnumsawang et al., 2021). When barrier function is compromised, the restrictive capacity of intercellular junctions is weakened and intestinal permeability increases, allowing luminal lipopolysaccharide (LPS) to enter the circulation and increase the inflammatory burden. LPS can regulate tight-junction permeability via TLR4-dependent processes and contribute to the initiation and progression of inflammation (Guo et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2025). In particular, LPS–TLR4 activation can engage NF-κB signaling, promoting inflammatory responses and changes in tight-junction organization, thereby exacerbating barrier dysfunction (Guo et al., 2015). Oxidative stress is a major contributor to barrier dysfunction because an imbalance between reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and clearance can damage cellular structure and function and destabilize tight junctions, further impairing mucosal barrier integrity (Rao, 2008).

The lion-head goose originated in Raoping County, Guangdong Province, and is mainly distributed in the Chaoshan region. It is the only large goose breed in China and is considered a key conserved genetic resource in China’s poultry germplasm. The breed exhibits favorable production traits, including tolerance to roughage, rapid growth, and large body size, highlighting its industrial value and development potential (Fu et al., 2025; Hong et al., 2024). However, with the restriction or ban of antibiotics for growth promotion in multiple countries and regions (Gong et al., 2021), intestinal health challenges under intensive production have become increasingly prominent in lion-head geese. These challenges include oxidative stress–related injury and intestinal dysbiosis, which can reduce nutrient utilization and productive performance and ultimately constrain industry development (Cui et al., 2024). Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop green, safe, and sustainable functional feed additives to support gut health in lion-head geese. Under antibiotic-restricted production, oxidative stress and barrier injury often coincide with microbial disturbances; therefore, additives that can improve redox status and help stabilize the intestinal microecology are of practical interest in lion-head geese.

Against this background, tea polyphenols have received increasing attention as a practical dietary option to support gut health in poultry (Luo et al., 2024). Tea polyphenols comprise diverse phenolic constituents, including catechins, anthocyanins, phenolic acids, and flavonoids. Catechins are the dominant fraction and typically account for approximately 60%–80% of total tea polyphenols (Fu et al., 2025). Previous work showed that dietary tea polyphenols at 200 and 400 mg/kg improved ileal morphological development in squabs, regulated the expression of tight-junction–related genes, and reduced intestinal permeability (Chen et al., 2023). As natural antioxidants, tea polyphenols may alleviate oxidative stress by increasing the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) and reducing the generation of lipid peroxidation products such as malondialdehyde (MDA) (Chen et al., 2023). In addition, tea polyphenols have the potential to modulate the gut microbiota by promoting or inhibiting the growth of specific microbial taxa, thereby influencing intestinal microecology and metabolic functions (Pérez-Burillo et al., 2021). In our previous studies, supplementation with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols (catechin content, 50.4%) improved muscle antioxidant capacity, reduced abdominal fat percentage, decreased lipid peroxidation, and improved liver function and lipid metabolism in lion-head geese (Fu et al., 2025, 2024). Moreover, the same inclusion level has been evaluated in Magang geese and was reported to improve jejunal morphology and antioxidant-related indices (Wang et al., 2022b). Therefore, we chose 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols for this study to assess its effects on the intestinal barrier and cecal microbiota in lion-head geese.

The application of high-throughput sequencing has markedly advanced gastrointestinal microbiome research in poultry, enabling not only compositional profiling but also functional inference of microbial metabolic potential. With advances in 16S rRNA–based technologies, metagenomics can clarify the phylogenetic composition of gastrointestinal microorganisms and support functional analyses (Qi et al., 2019). Cao et al. (2020) used metagenomic sequencing to investigate the effects of grape seed proanthocyanidin extract on the cecal microbiota of broilers and found that dietary supplementation significantly increased the relative abundances of Lactobacillales, Lewinalla, Lactobacillus, and Candidatus-Stoquefichus, along with enhanced amino acid, lipid, and carbohydrate metabolism. Wen et al. (2022) used metagenomic sequencing to assess microbiota dynamics during fatty liver formation induced by overfeeding in Landes geese, showing that the relative abundance of Firmicutes in the jejunum and ileum decreased initially and then increased over time, and that carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and energy metabolism pathways differed significantly in the cecum.

At present, reports on the application of tea polyphenols in lion-head goose production remain limited, particularly regarding systematic evaluation of their effects on intestinal barrier function, redox homeostasis, and the structure and functional potential of the cecal microbiota. Therefore, we supplemented a basal diet with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols and evaluated intestinal morphology, barrier-related indices, antioxidant status, and the structure and function of the cecal microbiota using metagenomic sequencing. These data provide a scientific basis for the rational application of tea polyphenols in lion-head goose production.

Materials and methods

All procedures were conducted in accordance with guidelines approved by the Animal Management Committee of Zhongkai University of Agriculture and Engineering (Approval No. 20220801) and complied with relevant local laws, regulations, and institutional requirements.

Preparation of tea polyphenols

The tea polyphenol preparation used in this study was produced in the laboratory of the College of Animal Science and Technology, Zhongkai University of Agriculture and Engineering, and contained 50.4% catechins.

Animals and experimental design

The feeding trial was conducted from August to December 2022 at the Lion-head Goose Science and Technology Backyard in Raoping County, Guangdong Province, China. A completely randomized design with a single factor was used. A total of 240 healthy one-day-old male lion-head geese with similar initial body weight were randomly allocated to 2 treatments (6 replicates/treatment; 20 birds/replicate). The control group received a basal diet, and the tea polyphenol group received the basal diet supplemented with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. Ambient temperature and humidity, and health status (including diarrhea) were recorded daily, and the house was disinfected periodically. The experimental period lasted 18 wk. The basal diet was formulated according to NRC (1994) nutrient requirements for geese and local production practices for lion-head geese in the Chaoshan region. Detailed diet composition and nutrient levels were reported previously (Fu et al., 2025).

Sample collection

At the end of the trial, birds were fasted for 12 h. Two geese with similar body weight were randomly selected from each replicate. Wing vein blood (10 mL) was collected, allowed to clot, and centrifuged at 3,000 r/min for 15 min to obtain serum. Serum was aliquoted and stored at −80°C for measurement of serum LPS and diamine oxidase (DAO). After slaughter, jejunal and ileal tissues were collected. A portion was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis, and the remaining tissue was stored at −80°C for determination of antioxidant indices and mRNA expression of antioxidant- and tight-junction–related genes. Cecal contents were collected and stored at −80°C for metagenomic sequencing.

Intestinal morphology measurement

Paraffin sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Villus height and crypt depth in the jejunum and ileum were measured using Image Scope software, and V/C (villus height/crypt depth) was calculated.

Serum LPS and DAO assays

Serum LPS and DAO were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Jiangsu Enzyme Immunoassay Co., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Determination of intestinal antioxidant indices

Antioxidant indices in jejunal and ileal tissues were determined using commercial kits (Jiangsu Enzyme Immunoassay Co., China). Total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) was measured by a biochemical method, whereas GSH-Px activity, T-SOD activity, and MDA content were determined using ELISA-based assays.

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from jejunal and ileal mucosa using TRIzol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed, and high-quality RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA (TaKaRa) and stored at −20°C. Primers for tight-junction– and antioxidant-related genes were designed based on NCBI sequences and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) (Table 1). Quantitative PCR was performed using PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix with 3 technical replicates per sample. β-actin was used as the reference gene, and relative expression was calculated using the 2 ^- ΔΔCt method.

DNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Approximately 200–300 mg of cecal content was used for total DNA extraction according to the kit instructions. DNA purity and integrity were evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. Qualified DNA was fragmented to ∼350 bp using Covaris ultrasonication and then subjected to end repair, A-tailing, adaptor ligation, purification, and PCR amplification for library construction. Libraries were quantified using Qubit 2.0, assessed for insert size using an Agilent 2100 system, and quantified by qPCR (effective concentration > 3 nM) before sequencing. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Quality control, annotation, and statistical analysis of metagenomic data

Raw reads generated by Illumina sequencing were quality-filtered to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads, yielding high-quality clean data for downstream analyses. Protein sequences were aligned to the NR database using DIAMOND v2.1.6 (E-value < 1e-5), and taxonomic annotation was performed in MEGAN v6.21.12 using the LCA algorithm. Functional annotations were obtained by DIAMOND v2.1.6 against SwissProt, COG, CAZy, CARD, eggNOG, and KEGG databases. Bowtie2 v2.3.5 was used to map sequencing reads to the gene catalog to generate taxonomic and functional abundance profiles. Based on LCA taxonomic annotations and gene abundance tables, relative abundances were summarized at multiple taxonomic ranks (kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species), and KronaTools-2.7 was used for Krona visualization. Community structure differences were evaluated using the R package VEGAN v2.6.2 by ANOSIM, and PCA, NMDS, and clustering analyses were also conducted to assess between-group differences and sample clustering patterns. Differences in taxonomic and functional abundances between groups were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, and significance was declared at P < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Independent-samples t tests were performed in SPSS 26.0. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Prior to analysis, mRNA expression data and antioxidant indices were assessed for normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variance (Levene’s test). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2.

To evaluate mucosal morphological development, histological measurements were performed for the jejunum and ileum. As shown in Fig. 1, compared with the control, the tea polyphenol group exhibited significantly increased villus height and V/C in both the jejunum and ileum (P < 0.05), whereas crypt depth did not differ between groups in either intestinal segment (P > 0.05).

Summary of metagenomic sequencing data

Twelve cecal content samples from lion-head geese were subjected to Illumina sequencing (Table S1). In total, 5,281,957,672 raw reads were generated across all samples. After quality filtering and removal of host-derived sequences, each sample retained 17,558,956–22,183,808 high-quality reads. The assembly statistics showed N50 values ranging from 1,264 to 1,631 bp and N90 values ranging from 591 to 609 bp.

Cecal microbial composition and diversity

Subsequently, to further evaluate the effects of tea polyphenols on cecal microbial composition and function, metagenomic sequencing was performed on cecal contents. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) at the phylum level (Fig. 2A) indicated that the microbial communities of the 2 groups were largely separated. Based on NR-based taxonomic annotation (Fig. 2B), 12 phyla, 27 classes, 50 orders, 82 families, 143 genera, and 239 species were identified in the cecal bacterial community. Alpha-diversity indices (Chao1, observed_species, and Shannon) did not differ between groups (P > 0.05) (Figs. 2C–2E). ANOSIM based on Bray–Curtis distance (Figs. 2F–2G) further confirmed that between-group differences were greater than within-group differences at both the phylum level (R = 0.478, P = 0.004) and genus level (R = 0.826, P = 0.002); moreover, inter-individual variation in the tea polyphenol group appeared relatively larger.

Microbial community structure at the phylum and genus levels

At the phylum level, Fig. 3A presents the top 10 phyla by relative abundance. Compared with the control, the tea polyphenol group showed significantly higher relative abundance of Actinobacteria (P < 0.05) and significantly lower relative abundances of Firmicutes and Verrucomicrobia (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3B). At the genus level (Fig. 3C), the enrichment pattern of the top 20 genera is shown. Compared with the control, the tea polyphenol group exhibited significantly higher relative abundances of Prevotella, Faecalibacterium, Subdoligranulum, and Olsenella (P < 0.05), whereas Bacteroides, Oscillibacter, and Flavonifractor were significantly decreased (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3D).

LEfSe analysis of intestinal microbial communities

LEfSe analysis was performed to identify the bacterial taxa significantly affected by tea polyphenol supplementation. The cladogram (Fig. 4A) showed that differential taxa in the tea polyphenol group were mainly enriched in Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria, whereas those in the control group were mainly enriched in Firmicutes. The LDA histogram (LDA > 2.0, P < 0.05) (Fig. 4B) indicated that the tea polyphenol group was enriched in taxa including Collinsella, Coriobacteriaceae, Chlamydiaceae, Chlamydiales, Chlamydia, Chlamydiia, Atopobiaceae, Coriobacteriales, Coriobacteriia, Proteobacteria, and Prevotellaceae, whereas the control group was enriched in taxa including Oscillospiraceae, Verrucomicrobiae, Verrucomicrobiales, Akkermansiaceae, Deltaproteobacteria, Desulfovibrionales, and Desulfovibrionaceae.

Functional annotation and differential functional analysis

To further characterize microbial functions, functional annotation was conducted using KEGG, eggNOG, and CAZy databases. At KEGG level 1 (Fig. 5A), “Metabolism” showed the highest gene abundance, followed by “Genetic Information Processing”. At KEGG level 2 (Fig. 5B), the top 3 functional categories in both groups were carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and replication and repair. eggNOG functional annotation counts (Fig. 5C) showed that the top 3 categories by gene number were amino acid transport and metabolism (56,561), carbohydrate transport and metabolism (50,357), and replication, recombination and repair (49,007). eggNOG level 3 analysis (Fig. 5D) indicated higher relative abundance of replication/recombination/repair and carbohydrate transport and metabolism in the tea polyphenol group than in the control. CAZy annotation (Fig. 5E) identified 6 genes assigned to auxiliary activities (AA), 3,267 genes to carbohydrate-binding modules (CBM), 17,183 genes to glycoside hydrolases (GH), 7,583 genes to glycosyltransferases (GT), and 392 genes to polysaccharide lyases (PL). At CAZy level 1 (Fig. 5F), the tea polyphenol group exhibited higher relative abundances of GT and PL compared with the control.

Based on differential analysis of KEGG functional annotation, at level 3 (Fig. 6A; Table S2), the phosphotransferase system was significantly higher in the tea polyphenol group (P < 0.05). To link differential genera with functional shifts, Spearman correlation analyses were performed between representative genera and significantly different KEGG level-3 pathways, CAZy level-2 enzyme families, and eggNOG categories (Fig. 6D–F; P < 0.05). In KEGG (Fig. 6D; Table S5), Prevotella was positively correlated with Thermogenesis and the two - component system but negatively correlated with biotin metabolism and lipoic acid metabolism, whereas Desulfovibrio showed the opposite pattern (positive correlations with biotin metabolism/peroxisome and a negative correlation with Thermogenesis). In CAZy (Fig. 6E; Table S6), Faecalibacterium was positively correlated with GH3 and PL1. In eggNOG (Fig. 6F; Table S7), Prevotella was positively correlated with cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis.

Discussion

Villus height, crypt depth, and V/C are important indices for evaluating intestinal mucosal function and nutrient absorption efficiency. Greater villus height reflects larger absorptive surface area, shallower crypt depth indicates more mature epithelial cells and stronger secretory capacity, and a higher V/C suggests improved absorptive capacity (Fu et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024). Previous research showed that dietary supplementation with 300 and 400 mg/kg tea polyphenols significantly increased villus height and V/C in the duodenum and jejunum of 42-day-old Ross broilers, while significantly decreasing crypt depth (Jelveh et al., 2022). In our study, dietary supplementation with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols significantly increased villus height and V/C in the jejunum and ileum, consistent with these findings, indicating that tea polyphenols may enhance digestion and absorption and improve the integrity and uniformity of the intestinal mucosa in lion-head geese (Ding et al., 2020). In contrast, another study reported that dietary supplementation with 10 mg/kg vanadium and 600 mg/kg tea polyphenols did not significantly affect duodenal mucosal morphology in laying hens (Yuan et al., 2016), which may be attributable to differences in supplementation dose and animal species or strain. LPS can stimulate intestinal cells, upregulate TLR4 expression, and induce the release of inflammatory mediators. Tight-junction (TJ) proteins are closely associated with intestinal permeability; when LPS-driven inflammation increases permeability, TJ protein expression may be disrupted, leading to barrier dysfunction (Lan et al., 2021). A previous study showed that dietary supplementation with 6 mg/kg tea polyphenols, 6 mg/kg propyl gallate, and 30 mg/kg ethoxyquin significantly decreased serum LPS in broilers (Xiao et al., 2024). In the present study, supplementation with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols significantly decreased serum LPS, consistent with prior findings, suggesting that tea polyphenols may influence intestinal epithelial cells and modulate intestinal permeability. Tea polyphenols may alleviate mucosal inflammation by downregulating LPS recognition and inflammatory pathways (e.g., TLR4/CD14/MyD88–NF-κB), thereby reducing barrier injury and luminal LPS translocation (Xiao et al., 2024). Tight junctions are key structures determining barrier function and are widely distributed across epithelial cell types; in the intestine, disruption of tight-junction structure can weaken normal intestinal physiological function and compromise systemic homeostasis, thereby contributing to a range of health problems (Moonwiriyakit et al., 2023). Dietary supplementation with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols reportedly upregulated jejunal mRNA expression of ZO-1, Claudin-5, Occludin, and E-cadherin in 70-day-old Magang geese (Wang et al., 2022b). Similarly, 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols significantly upregulated these tight-junction–related genes in the jejunum and ileum of lion-head geese, which may strengthen intercellular junctions and help maintain mucosal integrity. Tea polyphenols, as the major bioactive constituents of tea, possess strong antioxidant capacity (Luo et al., 2024). T-AOC reflects the overall level of antioxidant substances and the capacity to scavenge free radicals and resist oxidative injury (Fu et al., 2023). GSH-Px is a key enzyme in the antioxidant defense system that protects organelles from oxidative damage, whereas MDA is a product of free radical–mediated lipid peroxidation and is a reliable indicator of oxidative stress severity (Fu et al., 2024). Previous studies have shown that dietary supplementation with 600 mg/kg tea polyphenols significantly increased serum T-AOC and hepatic GSH-Px activity in laying hens (Zhou et al., 2021). In addition, supplementation with 200 and 400 mg/kg tea polyphenols significantly increased serum T-SOD and GSH-Px activities and decreased MDA in squabs (Chen et al., 2023). In the present study, supplementation with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols increased ileal T-SOD and GSH-Px activities and decreased MDA, consistent with these reports. The underlying mechanism may involve the catechol moieties of tea polyphenols, which enable metal chelation and thereby attenuate metal-catalyzed oxidative reactions. In addition, tea polyphenols can directly scavenge reactive oxygen species (e.g., superoxide anion and hydroxyl radical), limiting oxidative injury to biomolecules such as DNA. Tea polyphenols may further support cellular redox homeostasis by suppressing pro-oxidant enzymes and enhancing endogenous antioxidant defenses (e.g., GSH-Px) (Zhao et al., 2018). At the molecular level, SOD1, GPX1, GPX2, HO-1, and Nrf2 are key components of the antioxidant system. Dietary supplementation with 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols reportedly upregulated jejunal mRNA expression of SOD1, GPX1, GPX2, and HO-1 in 70-day-old Magang geese (Wang et al., 2022b). Consistently, our results suggest that tea polyphenols may modify cysteine residues on Keap1, induce conformational changes, inhibit Keap1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of Nrf2, and thereby facilitate nuclear translocation of Nrf2 to activate transcription of downstream antioxidant genes (e.g., SOD1, GPX1, GPX2, and HO-1), ultimately enhancing antioxidant capacity (Zhang et al., 2024).

Excessive lipid deposition in poultry is often accompanied by alterations in lipid metabolism and inflammatory status, which may compromise growth efficiency and animal welfare. The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as an important modulator of energy harvest and metabolic homeostasis. Tea polyphenols are absorbed to a limited extent in the small intestine; unabsorbed fractions can reach the hindgut and interact with the gut microbiota (Pérez-Burillo et al., 2021; Rothenberg et al., 2018). Such interactions may reduce pro-inflammatory stimuli in the intestinal lumen (e.g., LPS and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S)) and help explain the lower serum LPS and strengthened tight-junction/antioxidant responses observed in the host. A bidirectional relationship exists between tea polyphenols and intestinal microbes: microbes can convert polyphenols into small-molecule phenolic metabolites with higher bioavailability, whereas polyphenols and their metabolites can, in turn, influence microbial composition and function (Rothenberg et al., 2018). Firmicutes are closely linked to energy metabolism associated with obesity. In geese, dietary supplementation with 200 mg/kg epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) reportedly reduced the relative abundance of Firmicutes (Liu et al., 2025). In line with this, tea polyphenols significantly decreased the relative abundance of Firmicutes in lion-head geese in the present study, suggesting that tea polyphenols may influence microbial composition and potentially alter pathways of oligosaccharide degradation and host energy metabolism, thereby exerting beneficial regulation in obesity-related metabolic contexts (Xue et al., 2016). At the genus level, the relative abundance of Prevotella increased significantly in the tea polyphenol group. Prevotella has been reported to be negatively associated with blood lipid profiles (Tong et al., 2020), and its abundance has been linked to improved glycemic control and weight-loss prediction in both animal models and humans, suggesting potential relevance to the prevention or treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes (Verbrugghe et al., 2021). In this study, dietary tea polyphenols at 1,000 mg/kg increased the relative abundance of Prevotella in the cecum of lion-head geese. This may be attributable to the entry of unabsorbed tea polyphenols into the cecum and subsequent metabolic interactions with the microbiota, as well as potential effects on carbohydrate digestion and absorption that increase the delivery of fermentable carbohydrate residues to the cecum (Xu et al., 2013), thereby providing a more favorable substrate niche for Prevotella with strong capacity for plant polysaccharide degradation and utilization. In addition, selective inhibitory effects of tea polyphenols on certain taxa may alter competitive relationships and indirectly promote enrichment of Prevotella. Subdoligranulum is a strictly anaerobic, non–spore-forming gram-negative microorganism capable of producing short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, which may support intestinal health (Gradisteanu Pircalabioru et al., 2022). In the present study, tea polyphenols increased the relative abundance of Subdoligranulum, suggesting that butyrate production may contribute to host benefits, for example, butyrate has been reported to inhibit lipogenesis and promote lipid catabolism via the GPR41-mediated ERK–AMPK pathway, thereby improving lipid metabolism (Zhang et al., 2022). Oscillibacter has been considered closely associated with obesity induced by diets high in saturated fat (Lam et al., 2012). Feeding young tea powder reportedly decreased the relative abundance of Oscillibacter in the cecum of mice (Wang et al., 2022a). Consistently, in the present study, tea polyphenols reduced Oscillibacter, suggesting a potential role in improving cecal microbial structure and maintaining intestinal microecological stability and gut health. Such effects may involve selective regulation of the microbiota and altered carbohydrate digestion and fermentable substrate supply, thereby weakening the relative abundance of Oscillibacter (Rothenberg et al., 2018). LEfSe analysis further showed that Desulfovibrio was significantly more abundant in the control group than in the tea polyphenol group. Desulfovibrio is a widely distributed sulfate-reducing anaerobic gram-negative bacterium in the environment. Feeding a novel compound dark tea reportedly decreased the relative abundance of Desulfovibrio in the gut of high-fat diet–fed mice and revealed a positive association between Desulfovibrio and obesity (Qu et al., 2023). Mechanistically, Desulfovibrio performs energy conversion via dissimilatory sulfate reduction (DSR), reducing sulfate or oxidized sulfur compounds using hydrogen (H₂) or organic compounds and producing H₂S; excessive H₂S may disrupt intestinal mucosal homeostasis and adversely affect host health (Singh et al., 2023). Therefore, the reduced relative abundance of Desulfovibrio in the tea polyphenol group suggests that tea polyphenols may help maintain intestinal barrier function and gut health by limiting the enrichment of potentially unfavorable sulfate-reducing bacteria and improving the cecal microecological environment.

KEGG annotation indicated that, at level 3, the phosphotransferase system (PTS) was significantly higher in the tea polyphenol group. PTS typically comprises enzyme I (EI) and HPr and is widely present in bacteria and fungi. This system promotes the transport and phosphorylation of various sugars and derivatives and regulates multiple physiological functions, including carbon, nitrogen and phosphate metabolism, microbial chemotaxis, potassium transport, and the virulence of some pathogens (Deutscher et al., 2014). In Listeria monocytogenes, PTS can utilize efficient carbon sources such as cellobiose, glucose, fructose, and mannose to repress virulence gene expression, thereby reducing pathogenicity (Park and Kroll, 1993). Thus, enrichment of PTS may indicate that tea polyphenols influence carbon source uptake and metabolic regulatory networks of the cecal microbiota, although the specific drivers require further verification by integrating evidence on substrate supply and metabolite profiles. Carbohydrates are among the most abundant biological macromolecules in nature and are widely present in animal extracellular matrix and in bacterial and fungal cell walls; they also represent a major energy source for life processes. As an energy source, carbohydrates regulate blood glucose and insulin metabolism and participate in cholesterol and triglyceride (TG) metabolism (Holesh et al., 2023). Excessive carbohydrate intake can elevate blood glucose and increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases and obesity. Studies have shown that catechin-soluble polysaccharides can delay intestinal glucose absorption, potentially through effects of the gut microbiota or probiotics (e.g., lactobacilli and Streptococcus thermophilus) on carbohydrate metabolism, thereby contributing to glycemic control (Jones et al., 2019; Lee et al., 2020). In the present study, eggNOG functional annotation indicated higher carbohydrate transport and metabolism in the tea polyphenol group, suggesting enhanced microbial potential for carbohydrate substrate uptake and utilization, which may relate to TG metabolism. In our previous study, 1,000 mg/kg tea polyphenols significantly reduced serum TG in lion-head geese (Fu et al., 2025), therefore, the present functional shifts may be relevant to this phenotype. CAZy functional annotation further showed that, at level 2, GT and PL were significantly higher in the tea polyphenol group. As CAZy is gene-abundance–based, this enrichment reflects functional potential rather than measured activity. GT are natural biocatalysts that transfer glycosyl moieties from activated sugar donors to specific acceptors to form diverse glycosides; enzymes from the GT1 family exhibit strong glycosylation capacity for various natural products such as glycolipids, flavonoids, and macrolides (Zhang et al., 2020). GH1 family enzymes constitute an important component of β-glucosidases and are widely present in bacteria, insects, and vertebrates, participating in carbohydrate metabolic pathways. PL are enzymes that depolymerize certain acidic polysaccharides via an eliminative mechanism, cleaving activated glycosidic bonds in acidic polysaccharides and generating unsaturated oligosaccharides (Loiodice et al., 2025). Taken together, these results suggest that tea polyphenols may modulate the structure of the cecal microbiota and its functional potential for carbohydrate transport and metabolism, including increased gene abundances annotated to glycosyltransferases and polysaccharide lyases, which may be relevant to intestinal health in lion-head geese. Further Spearman correlation analysis indicated that shifts in dominant genera were accompanied by functional changes in the microbial community. For example, higher abundances of Prevotella and Faecalibacterium were associated with thermogenesis and two-component system, whereas Desulfovibrio was positively associated with biotin metabolism and negatively associated with thermogenesis-related signals, suggesting a coordinated shift in microbial composition and metabolic potential. In addition, only a single dose was evaluated in this study, future work incorporating multiple doses and measuring microbial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) will help further define the dose–response relationship and verify metabolic outputs.

Conclusions

Dietary tea polyphenols improved intestinal morphology, barrier-related indices (lower serum LPS and upregulated tight-junction–related gene expression), and antioxidant capacity in lion-head geese. In this study, metagenomic sequencing was employed, revealing changes in the structure and functional potential of the cecal microbiota (e.g., increased Prevotella and Subdoligranulum and decreased Desulfovibrio). Correlation analysis further suggested associations between these differential genera and selected functional metabolic pathways. Overall, these findings support the rational use of tea polyphenols in gut-health management strategies for lion-head goose production.
