Association of Heavy Smoking With High Body Mass Index and Cardiovascular Risk Score in Japan
Authors: Katsushi Takeda, Daisuke Aotani, Hiroya Hashimoto, Keiko Hamada, Chie Hasegawa, Yuki Shimizu, Yusuke Kuga, Takemasa Ando, Takashi Yagi, Hiroyuki Koyama, Hiroshi Takagi, Shigehiro Shiraki, Takashi Yokochi, Takeshi Kamiya, Hiromi Kataoka, Tomohiro Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveAlthough smoking, an established cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk, is a modifier of body mass index (BMI), studies are still lacking addressing a potential association between smoking and BMI or BMI‐related risk factors in Asia. The aim of this study was to investigate the association between smoking and BMI or CVD risk factors in a working‐age population in Japan.MethodsEight thousand eight hundred thirty‐six male and seven thousand three hundred seventy female health checkup examinees in a single center without self‐reported heart disease or stroke were analyzed by multiple regression analysis for an association between the smoking status (never, current, or past) or the number of cigarettes consumed (never (0/day), mild (< 10/day), moderate (10–19/day) or heavy (≧ 20/day)) and age‐adjusted BMI. In subgroups of 3430 males and 2407 females, the predictive risk score of CVD from the Hisayama study except for the smoking item was calculated and compared among groups with different smoking habits.ResultsIn the analysis of the association between smoking and BMI, age‐adjusted BMI was significantly higher in heavy smokers than never, mild, or moderate smokers in males (p < 0.01 vs. never, p < 0.05 vs. mild, p < 0.01 vs. moderate). Past smokers exhibited a higher CVD risk score than never or current smokers (p < 0.01 vs. never, p < 0.01 vs. current). Even though the score of the smoking item was excluded from the calculation, heavy smokers showed a significantly higher CVD risk score than the other three groups in males (p < 0.01 vs. never, p < 0.05 vs. mild, p < 0.01 vs. moderate). This was accompanied by the deterioration of CVD risk factors, including diabetes, dyslipidemia, proteinuria, and lack of exercise habit.ConclusionsSmoking status or the number of cigarettes was associated with age‐adjusted BMI or CVD risk factors in Japanese males.

Introduction

Since tobacco smoking and obesity are among the leading causes of cardiovascular death in Japan [1], a potential interaction between smoking habits and body weight dysregulation has been a topic of interest. Prior studies reported that current smokers showed lower body mass index (BMI) or lower incidence of obesity than never smokers [2, 3, 4, 5]. When smokers were subdivided by the number of cigarettes consumed per day (cigarette number), the prevalence of self‐reported obesity in Japan was higher among those who consumed higher cigarette number [2]. However, direct large‐scale analyses of the potential association between clinic‐measured BMI and the smoking status are still lacking in Asia.

Cardiovascular risk is often evaluated by Framingham risk score (FRS), which was developed in a largely white population living in the United States [6]. However, the incidence of coronary heart disease (CHD) is remarkably lower in Japan than in Western countries [7]. The risk score from the Hisayama study, that is, the Hisayama score (HS), was developed in Japan [8], which is one of the well‐established cardiovascular risk scores in Japan. Indeed, HS was adopted as a risk prediction tool for cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the latest guideline for prevention of CVD issued by the Japan Atherosclerosis Society [9].

BMI of Asians is generally known to be lower than that of Westerners [10, 11]. Furthermore, Asians are more likely to develop metabolic disorders at lower BMI than other ethnicities [10]. Therefore, HS was developed to evaluate the cardiovascular risk specifically in the Japanese population [8].

There have been a large number of studies demonstrating the association between smoking and increase in CVD [12, 13, 14]. However, there are few studies analyzing the association in terms of metabolic disorders. By using HS, calculated from various metabolic parameters, it would be possible to examine how metabolic parameters associated with CVD are affected by smoking in the Japanese.

The hypothesis of the present study was that heavy smokers exhibited higher BMI and worsened obesity‐related metabolic parameters. If BMI was substantially higher in heavy smokers, it would be possible to speculate that higher cardiovascular risk in heavy smokers might be the result of obesity in conjunction with the health hazards of cigarette smoke itself.

Based on this hypothesis, the association between smoking habits and BMI at a single health checkup center was cross‐sectionally investigated. Furthermore, the potential association between the smoking habits and the predictive risk of CVD was explored using HS.

Methods

Study Design

This was a cross‐sectional, retrospective study based on data collected from medical examination records of Midtown Clinic Meieki in Japan. From 17786 consecutive (M:9532, F:8254) participants, those with missing information or a history of heart disease or stroke were omitted.

Among 16887 participants, those with factors influencing body weight, e.g. use of anti‐diabetes drug, kidney failure, or malignant tumor were excluded from the analysis of the association between smoking habits and BMI, resulting in 16206 (M:8836, F:7370) participants. In the analysis of the association between smoking habits and CVD risk, those with age less than 40 or more than 84 or lack of laboratory data were excluded, resulting in 5837 (M:3430, F:2407) participants (Figure 1).

Flowchart depicting the selections of the present research participants. F: Female, M: Male.

Age, sex, self‐reported medical history, exercise and smoking habits, and other parameters obtained in the clinic were retrieved from the health checkup records. Smoking status and cigarette number were based on open‐ended self‐reported records. Smoking included combustible cigarettes, heated tobacco products, and e‐cigarettes. Height, body weight, and blood pressure were measured and recorded at the clinic.

Firstly, the association between smoking habits and BMI was examined. Among male participants, BMI was compared among never, current, and past smokers. Moreover, BMI was compared among never smokers, mild (< 10 cigarettes/day), moderate (10–19 cigarettes/day), and heavy (20 ≦ cigarettes/day) smokers. As for female participants, BMI was compared across smoking status and across cigarette number as in males (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Since age was a critical determinant of BMI [15, 16, 17], the BMI was adjusted by age.

Secondly, the association between smoking habits and risk of developing CVD was investigated. For the evaluation of risk for developing CVD, HS was calculated, which was a predictive score for 10‐year risk of CHD or atherothrombotic brain infarction [8]. HS was calculated by summing the scores of items of sex, age, systolic blood pressure, diabetes, serum HDL cholesterol, serum LDL cholesterol, proteinuria, exercise habit, and smoking habit.

The score was either measured or calculated according to the definition given in the original paper [8], which was established to estimate absolute cardiovascular risk in the Japanese population, unless otherwise mentioned. Diabetes was defined as either fasting blood glucose ≧ 126 mg/dL or taking antidiabetic medication. Regular exercise habit was defined as performing light exercise at least twice a week.

Hisayama score excluding smoking habit item (HSes) was also calculated. This was because the presence of the smoking habit item automatically increased the score of current smokers, which made it difficult to fairly compare CVD risk across smoking habits.

Those with age less than 40, age more than 84 in accordance with the original article [8], or lack of clinical information were excluded (Figure 1). In the analysis of the association between smoking status and CVD risk among male participants, the data were analyzed among never, current, and past smokers. Furthermore, the data were compared among never, mild, moderate, and heavy smokers. As for female participants, the data were analyzed across smoking status and cigarette number as in males (Supporting Information S1: Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

In the analysis of the association between smoking habit and BMI, multiple regression analysis was used to investigate the potential association between smoking habit and age‐adjusted BMI. In addition to the model with age and smoking habit as explanatory variables, a model that added age squared to that model, a model that added an interaction between age and smoking habit, and a model that added both age squared and an interaction between age and smoking habit were examined.

Regression models for BMI were determined for each sex by the smallest value of Akaike information criterion (AIC) [18]. Tukey's method was used to adjust multiple comparisons.

Data were collected and analyzed by BellCurve for Excel (Social Survey Research Information Co. Ltd., Japan) version 3.20 for the background and Hisayama score analyses, or by SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) for the age‐adjusted BMI analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (n) and relative frequencies (%). Statistical significance was determined by p value < 0.05.

Ethics Approval

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shinkokai Medical Corporation (No. SK‐2019‐7).

BMI was higher for past smokers, current smokers, and never smokers, in this order both in males and females (Table 1). However, given that the age group composition varied greatly among smoking status groups, age should have been taken into account to assess the association between smoking status and BMI. Prior studies reported that BMI peaked in the 40 and 50s for Japanese males, while it increased with age for females [15, 16].

BMI Comparison by Smoking Status

In the analysis of the association between smoking status and BMI in males, a multivariate regression model with smoking status, age, and age squared as explanatory variables was selected according to AIC. Based on the model, least square means (LS Means) of BMI for each smoking status group were estimated. Pairwise comparisons of these LS Means were conducted to evaluate the difference in age‐adjusted BMI among the groups (Supporting Information S1: Table S2A).

The comparison of age‐adjusted BMI among different smoking status groups yielded the following results: the difference between current smokers and never smokers was 0.06 (95% CI: −0.16, 0.27; p = 0.82), between past smokers and never smokers was 0.01 (95% CI: −0.27, 0.30; p = 0.99), and between current smokers and past smokers was 0.04 (95% CI: −0.25, 0.34; p = 0.94).

In the analysis in females, a linear regression model with smoking status and age as explanatory variables was selected according to AIC. Pairwise comparisons of these LS Means were conducted as in males (Supporting Information S1: Table S2B).

The comparison of age‐adjusted BMI among different smoking status groups yielded the following results: the difference between current smokers and never smokers was 0.28 (95% CI: −0.05, 0.62; p = 0.12), between past smokers and never smokers was 0.64 (95% CI: 0.28, 0.99; p < 0.01), and between current smokers and past smokers was −0.35 (95% CI: −0.82, 0.11; p = 0.17).

Hisayama Score by Smoking Status

To assess the risk of developing CVD by smoking status, HS was calculated for each smoking status group. HSes was also calculated to evaluate the risk of CVD independent of smoking (Supporting Information S1: Table S3A and S3B).

The distributions of scores for age, systolic blood pressure, diabetes, HDL cholesterol, proteinuria, and exercise habit were significantly different among never, current, and past smokers in males, as indicated by the p value (Supporting Information S1: Table S3A). The percentages of those with diabetes, lower HDL cholesterol, proteinuria, and lack of exercise habit tended to be higher in current smokers than in the other two groups. The percentages of those with higher age and higher systolic blood pressure tended to increase in past smokers compared with the other two groups.

HSes was significantly higher in past smokers than in never smokers and current smokers. HSes was significantly higher in current smokers than in never smokers (Supporting Information ;S1: Table S3A).

HS and HSes were calculated in the three groups classified by smoking status in females as well as in males. There was no significant difference in HSes between the three groups (Supporting Information S1: Table S3B).

Discussion

This study showed that heavy smoking was associated with higher BMI in Japanese males. Furthermore, heavy smoking was associated with an increased comprehensive cardiovascular risk score in males. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first epidemiological study conducted in Asia to reveal the association between heavy smoking and increased risk for developing CVD with worsening metabolic parameters.

BMI was higher in heavy smokers. Nicotine dependence was positively correlated with the frequency of food cravings for high fat, sweets, and carbohydrates [19]. Blendy et al. demonstrated that nicotine reward obtained from smoking was reduced in individuals with obesity, suggesting that those were prone to be nicotine dependent [20]. That is, neurobiological dysfunction in inhibitory control‐related regions would be disturbed by effects from regular nicotine and highly palatable food consumption, which is known as a kind of “cross‐addiction.” [21, 22].

In males, HSes was higher in past smokers compared with never smokers and current smokers, indicating that past smokers had a higher risk of developing CVD compared with never smokers and current smokers. This was an unexpected result since smoking cessation is known to reduce the onset of CVD [23]. Tendency for a high score in the item of systolic blood pressure might contribute to higher HSes in past smokers. Further studies are needed to investigate the effects of smoking cessation on the risk of developing CVD adjusted for blood pressure.

Current heavy smokers showed higher HSes than never smokers, mild smokers, and moderate smokers, suggesting that heavy smokers were more prone to developing CVD. HSes was composed of eight items: sex, age, systolic blood pressure, diabetes, serum HDL cholesterol, serum LDL cholesterol, proteinuria, and exercise habit. Higher HSes in male heavy smokers thus suggested a potential association between heavy smoking and some or all of these items besides sex. Tendency for a higher score was observed in items of diabetes, serum HDL cholesterol, proteinuria, and exercise habit in heavy smokers and might contribute to the higher total score.

The association between heavy smoking and higher HS in males was found even if the item of smoking in the original HS system was excluded, suggesting that the adverse influence of heavy smoking on CVD might exceed the level covered by the item of smoking in the HS and might possibly be mediated through the effect of higher BMI. The increase in BMI in male heavy smokers might affect the deterioration of metabolic parameters, leading to an elevated risk of developing CVD. This suggested that the risk for onset of CVD should have been evaluated not only by the presence or absence of smoking status but also by cigarette number considering the change in BMI.

In the present study, sex differences were found. Past smokers had a higher age‐adjusted BMI than never smokers in females but not in males. This finding corroborated previous studies showing that weight gain after smoking cessation was more likely to be observed in females [24, 25]. There was no significant difference in HSes by cigarette number in females, unlike males. The sex difference might arise from the fact that cigarette number had no significant effect on BMI in females. This supported again that the increased BMI in heavy smokers observed in males might contribute to the elevated risk of developing CVD.

In the present study, age‐adjusted BMI was higher in heavy smokers than in never smokers, mild smokers, and moderate smokers in males. This result was in line with a previous study conducted in Japan [2]. However, a previous study reported that age‐adjusted BMI in male current smokers was lower than that in never smokers and past smokers [2], which was inconsistent with the present study. The reason for this discrepancy might be that the percentage of heavy smokers among current smokers was higher in this study than in the prior study [2], which might have weakened the difference in BMI among smoking status.

There were some limitations to the present study. It was not possible to discuss causal relationships because the protocol was cross‐sectional. Since all types of smoking styles such as combustible cigarettes, heated tobacco products, or e‐cigarettes were included, the effects of differences in smoking styles were not able to be discussed. Although cigarette number was self‐reported, the reliability of the data was ensured in the prior study [26]. Since there was no information on the number of years after quitting smoking, the analysis by duration of smoking cessation was not available for past smokers. Because each item of HS was probably interrelated, differences in results by smoking habits might not simply indicate the effect of smoking habits. HS was originally created including smoking habit items, and therefore, the validity of HSes was unclear.

Other limitations of this study include the following. Because some lifestyle factors including smoking period, nutrition, alcohol intake, and socioeconomic level were not considered, the results might be subject to confounding. For example, a previous study suggested that heavy smokers were more likely to consume alcohol, which would be associated with higher BMI [27]. In the analysis of CVD risk score, age was not adjusted. However, adjusting for age did not make sense because HS included the age item. A baseline parameter‐matched prospective study may, in the future, provide conclusive results on the association between smoking and BMI or CVD risks. Risk score systems such as FRS and HS do not include the item of BMI, a risk factor of developing CVD. Establishments of cardiovascular risk scores that include BMI are warranted.

The present study demonstrated the association between smoking habit and BMI or the surrogate risk score of CVD among the Japanese health checkup examinees. This study further showed that the cigarette number as well as the smoking status was important in assessing the risk of BMI increase and the risk of CVD. The results of the present study provided the basis on which public health program be built to prevent obesity or CVD.
