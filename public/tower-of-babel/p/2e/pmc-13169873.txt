Latent profiles of psychopathological and criminological characteristics in prisoners: associations with suicide risk
Authors: Jue Deng, Jianping Ma, Yuanhua Ou, Liqin Lu, Jianhan Cai, Xiaoli Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrison suicide continues to be a global public health concern. Based on the stress-diathesis model, there may be interactions between psychopathological vulnerabilities and criminological stressors that contribute to suicide risk. However, no prior studies have empirically elucidated how these factors jointly configure distinct risk profiles among prisoners.MethodsWe conducted a latent profile analysis of 1,031 male prisoners from two correctional facilities in mainland China. This involved integrating criminological stressors (sentence length and remaining sentence) and psychopathological vulnerabilities (intolerance of uncertainty, sense of control, and interpersonal security). Suicide risk was assessed by the Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-revised.ResultsFour latent profiles emerged: Class 1, high intolerance of uncertainty (IU), low sense of security, and a short sentence (39.2%, OR = 5.16, 95%CI = 3.06–8.70); Class 2, longer sentence, moderate IU, and moderate sense of security (14.0%, OR = 2.60, 95%CI = 1.30–5.19); Class 3, longest sentence, moderate IU, and moderate sense of security (9.9%, OR = 2.28, 95%CI = 1.04–5.00); and Class 4, low risk (37.0%, reference). Class 1, 2 and 3 had a significant increase in suicide risk compared to that of Class 4.ConclusionsThis is the first study to use person-centered integration of criminological and psychological variables to identify prisoner subtypes with a differential suicide risk. The high-risk profile demands urgent clinical prioritization that advocates for routine screening of IU and sense of security during prison intake and also policy reforms to reduce systemic uncertainties (e.g., transparent sentence adjustments).Keywords: Prisoner suicide, Latent profile analysis, Intolerance of uncertainty, Sense of security, Stress-diathesis model

Introduction

Suicide behavior in prisons is a highly concerning public health issue. Previous studies have shown consistently that individuals in correctional facilities have higher rates of suicide compared to the general population of similar ages (Favril et al., 2020, 2022; Fazel et al., 2011, 2016; Zhong et al., 2021). According to a systematic review in 2024 (Mundt et al., 2024), the incidence rate ratios for suicide in male, incarcerated individuals and age-standardised general populations in the same jurisdictions were typically in the range of 1.9–6.0. A study performed in 24 high income countries in 2013–17 also reported that suicide rates in male prisoners were 3–8 times higher than in the general population, while the rate in female prisoners was typically more than 10 times higher (Fazel et al., 2017). However, death by suicide is more common among male than female prisoners (Daigle & Côté, 2006), which might be attributable to the fact that male prisoners tend to employ more lethal suicide methods. Another study on suicide risk in prisons in low- and middle-income countries also reported a considerably higher rate of suicidal ideation morbidity than that in the general population (Hill et al., 2022). Approximately 1 in 10 people make at least one suicide attempt during their prison term (Favril et al., 2022). Suicide is therefore one of the leading causes of death in prisons (Fazel et al., 2011; Stijelja & Mishara, 2022), and presents significant challenges not only for prison management but also for psychological intervention and treatment. Suicide prevention in prison was highlighted as an international priority by the World Health Organization (WHO, 2007) and in 2014, the organization recommended that prison suicide prevention should be included in national suicide prevention strategies (WHO, 2014).

According to the stress-diathesis model (Mann & Arango, 1992; Rubinstein, 1986), the development of suicidal behavior is the result of an interaction between diathesis and stressors. The diathesis is an endophenotype manifested in both cognitive styles and traits (Allan et al., 2023; van Heeringen, 2018), that reflect stable individual differences. Stressors refer to various negative psychological and social events. The relationship between stressful events and susceptibility is dynamic and mutually shaping. On one hand, stressful events may exacerbate susceptibility, an effect which has been confirmed in many epigenetic studies (van Heeringen, 2018). On the other hand, the interaction between traits and stressors may follow an ipsative model, which indicates an inverse relationship (Ingram & Luxton, 2005; Monroe & Hadjiyannakis, 2002). Specifically, individuals with high susceptibility may exhibit suicidal behavior in response to relatively minor stressful events, whereas those with low susceptibility may require exposure to more severe stressors to manifest such behavior. Fjeldsted et al. (2017) compared various types of stressful events and showed that compared with other stressful events (i.e., divorce, death of a close relative, and being physically assaulted), imprisonment had the strongest correlation with suicide. The aim of the current study was therefore to examine how psychopathological vulnerability and criminological stressors jointly predicted suicide risk in prisoners.

Among the criminological variables, the length of sentence is a factor that warrants particular attention and is considered as an inherent stressor in prisons. Existing research on the association between prison sentences and suicidal behavior has yielded several interesting findings (Zhong et al., 2021). For example, Favril and colleagues (Favril et al., 2022) show that serving a sentence of five years or longer compared with less than five years, or serving a life sentence compared with a determinate sentence, both increase suicide risk. Oyeleke1 et al. (2018) also demonstrated that duration of imprisonment was a significant independent predictor of the suicide capability among prisoners. Bogue and Power (1995) demonstrated that a substantially higher proportion of adults who died by suicide were serving sentences longer than 18 months, in comparison to the general adult sentenced prison population. In addition, Campaniello et al. (2017) found that prisoners’ subjective expectations of their sentence length were associated with suicidal behavior; specifically, perceived prospective reductions in sentence length may buffer suicide risk among incarcerated individuals. Collectively, these findings indicate that both total sentence length and remaining sentence length may serve as potential predictors of suicidal behavior in prison populations.

Intolerance of uncertainty (IU) is a psychopathological variable of diathesis and is considered in an increasing number of studies as a higher-order, transdiagnostic etiology of suicide behavior (Allan et al., 2023; Carleton, 2016). IU is defined as “an individual’s dispositional incapacity to endure the aversive response triggered by the perceived absence of salient, key, or sufficient information, and sustained by the associated perception of uncertainty” (Carleton, 2016). Difficulty in tolerating uncertainty is associated with various mental illnesses including anxiety disorders, bipolar and related disorders, depressive disorders, and obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (Allan et al., 2023; Boswell et al., 2013; Sadeh & Bredemeier, 2021). Previous research found a significant association between prisoners’ IU and the risk of suicide (Wang et al., 2025). Prisoners face considerable uncertainties due to social isolation and may lack available and reliable information of the changes happening in the world outside of the custodial setting, with current events unfolding beyond their confined environment. Moreover, they harbor substantial uncertainty regarding the situations following their release, such as the employment situation, technological innovations, or changes in policy for education and healthcare (Turney et al., 2024). The longer the duration of imprisonment, the greater the uncertainties that prisoners have to endure. Therefore, IU was regarded as a critical predictor in this study, the level of which may be combined with the characteristics of imprisonment length to form various patterns, which can then be used to predict suicide risk.

Another psychopathological vulnerability is the sense of security, which is considered as an individual’s basic perception of the possibility of threat and menace in real life (Bar-Tal & Jacobson, 1998), and represents a feeling of confidence and freedom from fear and anxiety (Reber et al., 2009). The sense of security is a relatively stable psychological diathesis associated with the processing of information related to control over one’s environment and interpersonal relationships, and contains two dimensions, sense of control and interpersonal security (Cong & An, 2004). Maslow and colleagues (Maslow et al., 1945) emphasized that the sense of security constitutes a fundamental human need and serves as the foundation for psychological well-being. According to the life history theory, coached within an evolutionary framework (Belsky et al., 2012; Del Giudice et al., 2011; Ellis et al., 2009), organisms strategically allocate resources (e.g., time and energy) to various activities throughout their lifespan. For individuals lacking a sufficient sense of security, the world and its inhabitants appear untrustworthy and uncontrollable (Maslow, 1942). As a consequence, when faced with uncertainties, individuals with a low sense of security are more likely to perceive these situations as threatening and intolerable. This perception often leads them to adopt a rapid life history strategy that prioritizes immediate goals and engagement in more impulsive behaviors such as suicide (Zhong et al., 2022). This approach offers an ostensibly easier means of achieving the short-term objective of escaping frustrating circumstances, rather than pursuing the long-term goal of confronting uncertainties. In this regard, previous studies showed significant correlations between the sense of security and suicide behavior in prisoners (Deng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). The sense of security was therefore also regarded as a predictor of susceptibility dimension in our study.

In summary, psychopathological vulnerabilities (IU and sense of security in this study) and criminological stressors (length of sentence in this study) are intricately interconnected and have a dynamic interactive effect on the prediction of suicidal behavior. However, to the best of our knowledge no study has yet identified subgroups of prisoners using both stressors and diathesis factors, and its relationship with suicide risk. Notably, while numerous factors are linked to prison suicide (Cooper & Berwick, 2001; Sarchiapone et al., 2009; Gooding et al., 2015; Fazel et al., 2017; Zhong et al., 2021; Favril et al., 2022), this study, grounded in the stress-diathesis model, prioritizes etiological, dispositional constructs reflecting stable individual differences, rather than symptom states (e.g., depression, anxiety) or affective experiences (e.g., loneliness, guilt), to identify foundational, pre-symptomatic risk mechanisms interacting with criminological stressors. Additionally, though IU and sense of security are established suicide risk factors in general psychiatric research (Carleton, 2016), they remain understudied among incarcerated individuals: only two recently exploratory correlational studies have examined their associations with suicide risk of prisoners (Deng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025). As theoretically grounded, pre-symptomatic vulnerabilities, they represent promising yet insufficiently validated factors for prison suicide risk prediction.

Building on the stress-diathesis model and addressing the identified gaps in the literature, this study pursues two interrelated objectives: First, we apply latent profile analysis (LPA), a person-centered statistical approach, to identify distinct subtypes of incarcerated individuals characterized by heterogeneous configurations of psychopathological factors (IU and sense of security) and criminological factors (sentence length). Unlike variable-centered methods that estimate linear associations among factors, LPA is suited to modeling complex, nonlinear characteristic configurations, aligning with the theoretical emphasis on interactive vulnerability-stress dynamics. Second, we examine the associations between these latent profiles and suicide risk. While prior research has confirmed that individual psychopathological and criminological factors correlate with elevated suicide risk in prisoner populations, no studies have explored how combinations of these diathesis and stressor factors cluster or differentially predict suicide risk—a critical gap that LPA addresses to inform targeted correctional mental health interventions.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedures

Data were collected from offenders in two male-only prisons located in a southeastern province in China. One prison was medium-security, and the other was minimum-security. There were no prisoners serving life sentences in the two prisons. We requested the prison officers to circulate information about the study and ask for volunteers. Prior to collecting data, we informed the participants that neither participation nor non-participation in the study would affect their routine care and management and that they could withdraw from the study at any stage without consequences. Eligibility for inclusion required all the following criteria: (1) Willingness to participate voluntarily and to give written consent; (2) Sufficient cognitive abilities to fill out the questionnaires; (3) Age 18–70 years.

A total of 1127 male prisoners were invited to complete demographic items and questionnaires that assessed IU, sense of security, and suicidal risk. The penal status of all the participants was checked. The data of 96 participants were discarded due to deceptive answers or were 10% of missing data (Bennett, 2001; Schafer, 1999). The data of the remaining 1031 participants were included in the subsequent analysis, resulting in an effective response rate of 91.5%.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Police College for research conducted with human subjects (EC-20230824-1022) and was performed in full compliance with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later addenda. The participants were informed of the purposes and procedures and provided written, informed consent before the study commenced. Assessments of intolerance of uncertainty (IU), sense of security, and suicide risk relied exclusively on self-report measures. All self-reported responses and psychometric data collected from participants were held in strict confidentiality, and prison staff could not access any of the participants’ personal-level information.

Measurements

Demographic variables

The study collected a set of demographic and criminological characteristics including age, educational level (coded as: primary school or below, junior high school, senior high school, vocational college, bachelor degree, postgraduate or above), marital status (coded as: unmarried, married, remarried, divorced, widowed), sentence length (years), remaining sentence length (years), substance abuse history (yes/no), recidivism (defined as a criminal record preceding the current incarceration), and offense type (violent/non-violent). For participants convicted of both violent and non-violent offenses, the violent offense category was prioritized for coding. Age, educational level, and marital status were self-reported by participants, while objective criminological variables (sentence length, remaining sentence length, substance abuse history, recidivism, offense type) were provided by prison officers and confirmed by participants to minimize concealment of their past illegal conduct.

Intolerance of uncertainty

The Intolerance of Uncertainty Scale (IUS-12) was used to assess IU (Carleton et al., 2007). It was a 12-item questionnaire; responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree). The questionnaire contains items such as “Uncertainty keeps me from living a full life” and “I always want to know what the future has in store for me”. The higher the IUS-12 score, the worse the intolerance of uncertainty. The IUS-12 has been demonstrated to have excellent internal consistency and good test-retest reliability (Dekkers et al., 2017). We used the Chinese version of the IUS-12 (Wu et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2017). In the sample for this study, Cronbach’s α for the Chinese IUS-12 was adequate at 0.802.

Sense of security

The Security Questionnaire (SQ; Cong & An, 2004) was used to assess participants’ sense of security. This self-report scale comprises 16 items loading on two distinct dimensions: interpersonal security (e.g., “I am afraid to build and maintain close relationships with other people”) and sense of control (e.g., “I have always felt quite unlucky”). Responses were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with anchors ranging from 1 (very strongly agree) to 5 (do not agree at all); higher total and subscale scores on the SQ indicate a stronger sense of security. The SQ has demonstrated adequate psychometric properties, including good reliability and construct validity, in prior research (Cong & An, 2004; Liao et al., 2015). In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients for the total SQ scale, interpersonal security subscale, and sense of control subscale were 0.900, 0.809, and 0.847, respectively. Rather than using a single total score, factor scores for the two dimensions were utilized in subsequent analyses to enable a more nuanced examination of their unique effects. This analytical approach was according to the theoretical distinction between the two dimensions, which reflect vulnerability along divergent conceptual directions: sense of control pertains to internal psychological perceptions, while interpersonal security reflects external relational perceptions (Cong & An, 2004).

Suicide risk

The suicide risk of prisoners was assessed via the Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised (SBQ-R). The SBQ-R is a self-reported measure comprising four items that evaluate four aspects related to suicide (e.g., “How often have you thought about killing yourself in the past year?”). The questionnaire has been shown to have adequate psychometric properties (Osman et al., 2001), with the validity and reliability of the Chinese version of SBQ-R having been confirmed by Shi et al. (2021). The total scores range from 3 to 18, with higher scores reflecting a greater risk of suicidal behavior. According to the manual, a cut-off score ≥ 7 is defined as an indicator of suicide risk; this threshold was derived from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses and identified as the most useful threshold for nonclinical samples (Osman et al., 2001; Shi et al., 2021). In our study, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the SBQ-R was 0.731.

Statistical analysis

Latent profile analysis (LPA) is a person-centered, latent variable approach used to classify cases into optimal clustered categories and recover hidden groups from observable data. The technique allows for the identification of discrete latent variables based on scores from multiple continuous variables and uses these latent classes to characterize participants according to patterns of complex identity attributes (Davis et al., 2021; Green et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024; Oberski, 2016). In this study, sentence length, remaining sentence of imprisonment, IU, interpersonal security, and sense of control were integrated into the LPA to characterize prisoners based on the pattern of their complex identity attributes, thereby creating distinct latent classes.

LPAs were conducted using Mplus version 8.0. In this study, we evaluated latent class models with two to six classes to ensure the most suitable number of classes. To determine the optimal number of classes, a combination of fit indices was analyzed including the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), adjusted BIC (aBIC), and Akaike information criterion (AIC). Lower values of BIC, aBIC, and AIC indicate a better model fit (Davis et al., 2021; Green et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024). Entropy, which ranges from 0 to 1, measures classification accuracy, with a value closer to 1 indicating higher accuracy and values > 0.8 reflecting excellent classification accuracy (Li et al., 2024). The Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-aLRT) and Bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were also used in this study. These two tests are useful tools for class enumeration, and there is evidence that a k-class model is significantly better than a (k–1)-class model (p < 0.05) (Guo et al., 2022). In addition, a minimum threshold of 5% was set for the sample size ratio of each class according to that used in previous studies (Davis et al., 2021; Li et al., 2024).

IBM SPSS version 26.0 was used for the analysis of variance (ANOVA), Chi-squared analysis, covariance analysis (ANCOVA) and logistic regression analysis. The ANOVA and the Chi-squared test were used to compare continuous and categorical demographic variables across the classes, while ANCOVA was used to compare SBQ-R scores across classes, with demographic variables that showed significant inter-class differences used as the covariates. Bonferroni correction was used for multiple comparisons. A binary logistic regression was then conducted to investigate the odds of suicide risk (dichotomized) according to class membership, using the lowest-risk group as the reference. Demographic variables with significant inter-class differences were added as control variables in the logistic regression to give a more nuanced picture of the association between those variables and suicide. This method simultaneously controlled for variance above and beyond class membership.

Results

Descriptive characteristics of the samples

The valid sample comprised 1031 male offenders, with a mean age of 36.10 years (SD = 10.45). The mean sentence length for the prisoners in the study was 4.29 years (SD = 4.02), with a mean remaining sentence of 2.57 years (SD = 2.72). Of the 1031 individuals, 729 (70.7%) had a junior middle school or lower educational level, 242 (23.5%) had completed senior high school or technical vocational college education, 35 (3.4%) had bachelor’s degrees or above, and 25 (2.4%) did not provide educational level information. Regarding marital status, 406 (39.4%) were unmarried, 395 (38.3%) were married, 202 (19.6%) had another marital status (remarried/divorced/widowed), and 28 (2.7%) did not report their marital status. Class 1 included 404 prisoners (39.2%), with high SBQ-R scores (M = 4.85, SD = 2.82). According to the manual of SBQ-R (Osman et al., 2001; Shi et al., 2021), 88 prisoners (21.8%) in this class currently had a high suicide risk. As shown in Fig. 1, the prisoners in Class 1 had the poorest IU, the lowest sense of security, and shorter sentences. Therefore, Class 1 was identified as a “high IU, low sense of security, and short sentence group”.

Class 2 comprised 144 prisoners (14.0%), with high SBQ-R scores (M = 4.35, SD = 2.64). Based on the SBQ-R manual (Osman et al., 2001; Shi et al., 2021), 19 prisoners (13.2%) in this class met the threshold of currently elevated suicide risk. The prisoners in class 2 had longer sentences, relatively poorer IU, and a moderate sense of security (Fig. 1). Therefore, class 2 was classified as “longer sentence, moderate IU and sense of security group”.

Class 3 included 102 prisoners (9.9%), with a mean SBQ-R score of 4.23 (SD = 2.13) and 12 prisoners (11.8%) having a high suicide risk. As shown in Fig. 1, the class 3 prisoners had the longest sentences of imprisonment, moderate IU, moderate sense of control, and mildly better interpersonal security. Based on these results class 3 was designated as the “longest sentence, moderate IU and sense of security group”.

Class 4 contained 381 prisoners (37.0%), with lower SBQ-R scores (M = 3.55, SD = 1.34) compared to Classes 1, 2, and 3, and was therefore described as the “low suicide risk group”. Only 9 prisoners (2.4%) in this class met the threshold of a high suicide risk. As shown in Fig. 1, the Class 4 prisoners had shorter sentences, the lowest IU, the highest sense of control, and the best interpersonal security.

Chi-squared analysis and ANOVA

The chi-square values and the significance level are presented in Table 5. No significant inter-class differences were observed for educational level (χ2 = 3.480, p = 0.323) or marital status (χ2 = 6.272, p = 0.099), whereas there was a significant inter-class difference in the history of substance use (χ2 = 45.201, p<0.001). Specifically, individuals in Class 1 reported significantly fewer substance use histories compared with those in Class 2 (p<0.001) and Class 3 (p<0.001); individuals in Class 4 also reported significantly lower rates than those in Class 2 (p<0.001) and Class 3 (p<0.001). The proportion of “no recidivism” in Class 4 is significantly higher than that in Class 2 (p<0.001) and Class 3 (p = 0.002), and the proportion of “once prior conviction” in Class 2 is significantly higher than that in Class 4 (p<0.001).

ANCOVA and logistic regression analysis

The ANCOVA showed a significant inter-class effect for the SBQ-R scores (F = 19.625, p<0.001, ηp2 = 0.057). Multiple comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that the SBQ-R scores of Class 4 prisoners were significantly lower than those in Class 1 (p<0.001), Class 2 (p = 0.002) and Class 3 (p = 0.047) prisoners. There was no significant difference in SBQ-R scores between Classes 1, 2, and 3 (p ≥ 0.082) which suggests that prisoners in these three classes had significantly higher suicidal behavior tendencies than that of Class 4 prisoners. Therefore, the binary logistic model that focused on the suicide risk according to the cut-off score (≥ 7) used the threshold of the SBQ-R manual (Osman et al., 2001; Shi et al., 2021), with Class 4 as the reference group.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the first attempt to integrate criminological stressors (sentence length and remaining sentence length) with psychopathological vulnerabilities (sense of control, interpersonal security, and IU) in the context of predicting the suicide risk in 1031 male prisoners. Drawing on the theoretical framework of the stress-diathesis model (Mann & Arango, 1992; Rubinstein, 1986), LPA was conducted on the sample of 1,031 male prisoners, resulting in the identification of four distinct prisoner classes specific to this study. The study provided valuable insights into how different profiles of prisoners, shaped by both psychological traits and the stressors of imprisonment, contribute to suicide risk (Zhong et al., 2021; Favril et al., 2022).

Class 1 consisted of individuals characterized by high IU, low sense of security, and short sentences; Class 2 included those with longer sentences and moderate IU and sense of security; Class 3 comprised prisoners with the longest sentences and moderate IU and sense of security; and Class 4 included the group with the lowest suicide risk, characterized by low IU, high sense of security, and short sentences. Compared to Class 4, the other three subgroups had higher suicide risks.

Extension of the suicide stress-diathesis model in prison

Our findings support the Stress-Diathesis Model’s core tenet that suicide risk arises from interactions between susceptibilities and stressors, as observed in this prisoner sample. Based on the characteristics of the prisoners in our study sample, suicide risk can be explained by the following three aspects.

Firstly, in accordance with the ipsative model (Ingram & Luxton, 2005; Monroe & Hadjiyannakis, 2002), the interaction between psychological traits and stressful events may follow an inverse relationship. For prisoners in Class 1, characterized by high IU and a low sense of security, even a very short prison term or the mere fact of imprisonment can have a significant negative impact on their mental health (Wang et al., 2025; Deng et al., 2024). High IU reflects a difficulty in coping with uncertainty, a dispositional trait linked to various psychological disorders (McEvoy et al., 2019) and recognized as a transdiagnostic etiology of suicidal behavior (Allan et al., 2023; Carleton, 2016). Our findings suggest that IU, as a relatively stable predictive variable of the psychopathological dimension, may be well-suited for categorizing potential subtypes in similar prisoner samples and assisting in the identification of incarcerated individuals with persistently elevated suicide risk in such contexts.

Sense of security is also a dispositional trait of the psychopathological dimension (Maslow, 1942). As a fundamental pathological mechanism, its role in suicidal behavior is also transdiagnostic (Bar-Tal & Jacobson, 1998; Reber et al., 2009). That is to say, in Class 1 prisoners, their characteristic pattern of high IU and low sense of security may lead to an extremely low threshold for the onset of suicidal ideation. The psychological distress resulting from even a brief loss of freedom and social isolation may be associated with elevated suicidal ideation among prisoners in Class 1. For these individuals, suicide may represent a maladaptively potential coping response to such distress.

Secondly, stressful events may not only exacerbate the potential effects of susceptibility but also shape susceptibility itself (van Heeringen, 2018). On the one hand, stressful events influence the structure and function of the brain to varying extents (van Heeringen, 2018; Rubinstein, 1986). The experience of imprisonment may continuously affect the psychological characteristics of prisoners, including IU and sense of security. During the process of enduring isolation and uncertainty, prisoners may develop adaptive cognitive strategies and behavioral patterns related to the prison environment, which gradually solidify into trait-like psychological susceptibility. On the other hand, the dose-response relationship between the number of stressful events and the risk of suicidal behavior, may result in sub-additive effects (van Heeringen, 2018). As a consequence, prisoners in Classes 2 and 3, who had medium levels of IU and a sense of security along with longer prison sentences (8.63 years and 12.73 years, respectively), also had a relatively high risk of suicide compared to Class 4, supporting previous research on the correlation between sentence length and suicide risk (Favril et al., 2022; Oyeleke1 et al., 2018).

Implication for suicide prevention in prison

According to the interpretation of the stress-diathesis model of McGirr and Turecki (McGirr & Turecki, 2007), a strategy to improve the clinical detection of suicide risk involves identifying stable risk factors that precede the onset of mental disorders such as major depression and anxiety disorder. These mental disorders appear to be a necessary yet insufficient condition for suicide in the majority of cases (van Heeringen, 2018). Transdiagnostic psychological traits act as stable risk factors that represent an individual’s pre-onset endophenotype and interactions with stressors linked to the development of mental disorders, thereby increasing the likelihood of suicidal behavior (McGirr & Turecki, 2007; van Heeringen, 2018). This study constitutes a pioneering effort to explore the stress-diathesis model in the context of forensic psychiatry practice and demonstrates that high IU and low sense of security are risk factors for suicidal behavior in prisoners (Deng et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2021). Therefore, the assessment and prediction of suicide risk among criminals should not only focus on the proximal symptoms of mental disorders but also integrate susceptibility variables such as IU and sense of security into the risk assessment system.

Given the scarcity of mental health resources in correctional settings (Daigle & Côté, 2006; Fazel et al., 2016, 2017; Hill et al., 2022; Mundt et al., 2024), this study provides empirically grounded, feasible screening strategies and actionable evidence to inform the identification of high-risk subgroups warranting prioritized, targeted interventions. Logistic regression revealed significantly elevated odds of high suicide risk in Class 1 and Class 2 relative to Class 4, and marginally elevated odds in Class 3. Notably, while mean SBQ-R scores did not differ significantly across Classes 1–3, all three classes exhibited significantly higher SBQ-R scores compared with Class 4, indicating they may represent a population requiring prioritized early detection and tailored psychological support. Prisoners in these high-risk classes could benefit from interventions aligned with their distinct profiles: Class 1 may gain most from targeted strategies to improve tolerance of uncertainty and strengthen sense of security, supplemented by social support to mitigate the psychological impact of incarceration (van der Heiden et al., 2012; Stijelja & Mishara, 2022); Classes 2 and 3 may also benefit from IU-focused interventions, alongside supports addressing the stressors associated with prolonged incarceration (e.g., clarity on sentence progression, post-release planning) (Campaniello et al., 2017; Turney et al., 2024). Notably, treatment targeting IU has already been developed and proven effective as a clinical tool (van der Heiden et al., 2012), making it a viable foundation for cross-group interventions while accounting for class-specific characteristics. In addition, the incorporation of coping skills that promote a sense of security may be considered for integration into existing intervention systems, such as trauma-focused cognitive-behavioral therapies (CBT) and dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), which are currently prevalent in prison settings.

Furthermore, as a potential amplifier of uncertainty and insecurity, sentence length—including remaining sentence length—may be a risk factor that warrants consideration in similar prisoner populations. However, as a variable within the criminal justice dimension, the term of imprisonment cannot be shaped through training and intervention in the same way for variables within the psychology dimension. Therefore, it is essential to pay close attention to the chain reactions triggered by the adjustment of relevant policies. For instance, maintaining consistency in rules governing prison sentence length adjustments (especially decisions related to sentence extension, commutation, or parole) and reducing unannounced visit cancellations may help mitigate uncertainty among prisoners. In addition, providing more vocational skills training and information on employment policies to prisoners could alleviate their concerns about post-release reintegration into society. All these strategies can, to a certain extent, mitigate the adverse interaction between high IU and a low sense of security with the length of imprisonment, thereby reducing the risk of suicide.

Limitations and future directions

While this study contributes to our understanding of the complex relationships between criminological stressors and psychopathological vulnerabilities to predict the suicide risk in prisoners, there were several limitations that should also be considered.

First, the study’s cross-sectional design limited our ability to make causal inferences about the relationships between the variables and suicide risk. A longitudinal design would allow for a better understanding of how changes in psychological vulnerabilities and criminological stressors over time may influence suicide risk in prisoners. Tracking prisoners over a longer period would help to determine whether the identified latent classes remained stable or shifted, particularly as psychological and environmental factors evolved.

Second, the present study has limitations regarding sample representativeness. On the one hand, the sample was restricted to male prisoners from two correctional facilities in China, excluding both individuals serving life imprisonment and female prisoners. On the other hand, detention status was not incorporated as a research variable, due to the institutional separation of detained populations under mainland China’s judicial system. Specifically, prisons in mainland China exclusively house convicted offenders who have confirmed sentences, while individuals awaiting trial are detained in detention centers. Given that the study sample was recruited solely from two prisons and consisted entirely of convicted offenders with confirmed sentences, detention status (i.e., imprisoned convicts vs. pretrial detainees) was not applicable to the research context. Future research should aim to include a more diverse sample of prisoners, including individuals from different types of prisons (e.g., high-security), different detention status (i.e., imprisoned vs. detained awaiting trial) and females. This would enhance the generalizability of the findings and allow for a deeper understanding of the factors contributing to suicide risk across different prison settings.

Third, while this study focused on IU and sense of security as key psychological vulnerabilities, there are other relevant psychopathological factors that may also have a significant role in suicide risk, such as impulsivity and sensation seeking. Future research could explore these additional factors and their interactions with criminological stressors to construct a more comprehensive model of suicide risk in prisoners (Zhong et al., 2021; Gooding et al., 2015).

Conclusions

This study makes a novel contribution to the understanding of suicide risk among prisoners by integrating both criminological stressors (sentence length and remaining sentence length) and psychological vulnerabilities (IU and two dimensions of sense of security). Through the application of LPA, we identified four distinct subgroups of prisoners, each characterized by unique patterns of psychological vulnerability and criminological stressors. These subgroups also exhibited different levels of suicide risk. These findings not only advance the existing body of knowledge on suicide risk in prisons but also underscores the complex interplay between environmental and psychological factors. By emphasizing the necessity to consider both the criminological context and psychological vulnerabilities in suicide risk assessment, this study provides a foundation for more refined and personalized approaches to suicide prevention in prisons. Moreover, it highlights the importance of early identification and intervention, particularly for prisoners with a high IU and low sense of security, who are at an elevated risk of suicidal behaviors. This study therefore offers valuable insights into developing targeted interventions and prevention strategies to address both the psychological and environmental needs of high-risk prisoners. Looking ahead, longitudinal studies incorporating additional psychological factors and a broader, more diverse sample are essential to further refine our understanding of suicide risk predictors in prisoners and to enhance the effectiveness of intervention and prevention programs.
