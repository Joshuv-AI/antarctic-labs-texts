Inadvertent detection and management of intraoperative pneumothorax during spinal navigation imaging using Loop-X®
Authors: Balaji Vaithialingam, Brenda Paulson, Ramachandran Govindasamy, Swaroop Gopal
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Mobile 3D imaging devices in the operating room (OR) include Airo TruCT® (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA), O-arm® (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), and Loop-X® (Brainlab AG, Munich, Germany).[1] The Loop-X® is an advanced mobile robotic system that provides high-definition 2D and 3D cone beam computed tomographic (CBCT) imaging while allowing for intraoperative navigation. It improves surgical precision during navigation-guided pedicle screw placement.[2] This report highlights the extra-surgical use of Loop-X® in intraoperative pneumothorax detection and management.

A 24-year-old paraplegic male without any medical comorbidity (American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status class II) was diagnosed with left thoracic vertebral osteoblastoma (D3-D7) extending to the medial part of the left ribs. The patient was scheduled for tumour curettage, hemicorpectomy, and cage implantation with posterior stabilisation (D1-D9) in the prone position. To assist with the surgical procedure, intraoperative one-lung ventilation (OLV) was planned. Following induction of general anaesthesia, direct laryngoscopy was performed, and the trachea was intubated with a 39-Fr left double-lumen tube (DLT) using a blind technique. The successful placement of the DLT was confirmed using the ‘three-step’ method.[3] Following successful tumour decompression with left lung isolation, the Loop-X® spin was performed for 3D spinal navigation. Two-lung ventilation was resumed, as OLV was not required for pedicle screw placement. During spinal navigation imaging for pedicle screw placement, a hypodense space was noted on the left side, adjacent to a collapsed lung border, measuring 2 cm in the left upper field and 1 cm in the left lower field [Figures 1a and b]. The patient was haemodynamically stable (heart rate 88 bpm, blood pressure 130/80 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 100%), with normal airway pressures. However, OLV was immediately resumed to deflate the left lung as a precautionary measure. The surgical team was informed, and a left intercostal drain (ICD) was placed immediately in the prone position (fifth intercostal space, midaxillary line). The correct position of the ICD was confirmed using the Loop-X®, and the spinal instrumentation was resumed. After surgery, the trachea was extubated, and the ICD was successfully removed after 2 days.

(a and b) The spinal navigation imaging showing left pneumothorax (white arrows) during pedicle screw placement. (c)Loop-X® mobile robotic system installed in the operating room

Life-threatening pneumothorax can occur during thoracic spinal surgeries.[4] Intraoperative ultrasonography (USG) can enhance the accuracy of pneumothorax detection.[5] However, doing USG on prone patients under surgical drapes can be difficult. Furthermore, mild-to-moderate pneumothorax may go undetected by USG and necessitate clinical competence. A CT scan is the most effective modality for identifying pneumothorax; however, moving the patient from the OR to the CT room presents ergonomic issues. The Loop-X® mobile robotic system is permanently installed in the OR [Figure 1c], ensuring immediate availability during spinal instrumentation. The Loop-X® offers 3D CBCT volumetric images that inherently encompass the lung fields during thoracic spinal navigation and can detect small pneumothorax with higher accuracy. In our case, Loop-X® enabled us to detect an inadvertent pneumothorax in the absence of clinical signs such as oxygen desaturation or elevated airway pressures. Moreover, it enabled us to promptly intervene to avert a tension pneumothorax with positive pressure ventilation and the immediate placement of an ICD.

In conclusion, intraoperative identification of pneumothorax with Loop-X® imaging can be regarded as an extra-surgical use. Spinal navigation utilising Loop-X® imaging during pedicle screw placement inherently includes the lung fields, allowing the anaesthesiologist to detect any mild pneumothorax that may not present clinically.
