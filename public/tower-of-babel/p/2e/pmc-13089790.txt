Validation of the Double Awareness Scale: A Novel Measure of Psychological Adaptation to Advanced Cancer
Authors: Melissa Miljanovski, Kenneth Mah, Athena Li, Anne Rydall, Mairead H McConnell, Gilla K Shapiro, Breffni Hannon, Camilla Zimmermann, Gary Rodin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIndividuals with advanced cancer face the challenge of remaining engaged in life, while also contemplating death. However, there has been no validated measure of the adaptive capacity to balance these tasks, which has been termed “double awareness.”AimsThis study aimed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the novel Double Awareness Scale (DAS).MethodsPatients with advanced cancer attending an outpatient palliative care clinic (N = 197) completed the DAS and other self‐report measures assessing quality of life near end of life, life completion, satisfaction with life, preparation for end of life, death anxiety, and depression. Factor structure, construct validity, and internal consistency of the DAS were evaluated, as were group differences across high, intermediate, and low levels of double awareness.ResultsFactor analysis supported two factors, Life Engagement and Death Contemplation, with high internal consistency (α = 0.92 and 0.87, respectively). Life Engagement met the convergent validity criterion with quality of life near the end of life (r = 0.62) and life completion (r = 0.60), but not with life satisfaction (r = 0.46). Death Contemplation did not meet the convergent validity criterion with preparation for end of life (r = 0.23) but demonstrated discriminant validity with death anxiety (r = −0.03). Those with high levels of double awareness (high Life Engagement and high Death Contemplation) exhibited the best quality of life and lowest distress.ConclusionsPreliminary evidence supports the validity of the DAS. Further research is needed to demonstrate its validity in diverse settings, its utility in clinical care, and its responsiveness to therapeutic interventions in the context of advanced disease.Keywords: adaptation, advanced cancer, attitudes to death, cancer, oncology, palliative care, psychological, validation study

Background

A growing number of individuals worldwide are developing cancer, living with this disease, and dying from it. Globally, over 19 million new cancer cases were reported in 2022, with approximately 10 million cancer‐related deaths reported that same year [1]. By the year 2050, the global incidence of cancer is predicted to surpass 35 million per year, with over 18 million cancer‐related deaths [1]. Treatment advances, such as targeted therapy and immunotherapy, have prolonged survival in individuals with advanced cancer, such that increasing numbers are actively living with cancer for extended periods of time [2, 3].

The term “double awareness” has been used to refer to the simultaneous capacity of individuals with advanced cancer to remain engaged in life, while also contemplating and preparing themselves for the end of life [4]. The capacity to sustain this duality may indicate optimal psychological adaptation to the circumstances of advanced disease. This view differs from earlier models of death and dying, in which adaptation to an advanced disease was viewed as a linear process that progressed from earlier stages, such as denial and anger, through to a final stage of acceptance [5]. However, despite the growing recognition of the importance of double awareness in adaptation to life‐threatening and advanced disease [6, 7, 8, 9], modern cancer care tends to be more focused on the treatment of the disease and management of physical symptoms than on the psychological and social consequences for the affected individual and their family [10].

Remaining involved in valued life activities and sustaining social connections has been shown to help individuals with advanced cancer maintain their sense of self, normalcy, agency, and independence [11]. At the same time, the ability to reflect on and prepare for the end of life can lead to end‐of‐life care that is more consistent with personal goals and values, which may mitigate distress related to the dying process [12]. Previous research has assessed the constructs of life engagement [11, 13] and death contemplation [14, 15, 16] in individuals with advanced cancer, but there has been no validated measure of the ability to balance the paradoxical phenomenon of engaging in both tasks. To address this gap, we developed a novel measure, the Double Awareness Scale (DAS) [17], using best practices for scale development and validation [18].

The initial steps of the DAS development were previously reported [17]. An initial pool of 52 items was subsequently reduced to 41 items representing Life Engagement (22 items), Death Contemplation (18 items), and one item assessing the oscillation between the two domains [17]. The present study was conducted to validate the DAS in patients with advanced cancer receiving outpatient palliative care. We evaluated the factor structure of this measure, the internal consistency and construct validity of derived factors, and group differences on outcomes.

Methods

Participants

Patients with an advanced cancer (Stage III or IV, or as otherwise determined by their palliative care physician) were recruited from the outpatient palliative care clinic at the Princess Margaret Cancer Centre (PM), University Health Network, a large, comprehensive cancer center in Toronto, Ontario, Canada. This clinic at PM specializes in pain/symptom control, as well as supporting psychosocial and practical needs of patients with advanced cancer and their families. Care is delivered by specialized palliative care physicians, nurses, occupational therapists, social workers, music therapists, and spiritual care professionals, and tailored to individual patient's needs. Inclusion criteria for this study included age ≥ 18 years, English fluency, and actively receiving ongoing outpatient palliative care at PM. Exclusion criteria included cognitive impairment (documented in the patient's electronic health record or indicated by their palliative care physician) that would interfere with the patient's ability to provide informed consent or complete the study measures.

Measures

Sociodemographic and Medical Data

Participants provided self‐reported sociodemographic information, including age, sex, gender identity, country of birth, primary language, ethnicity, religion, relationship status, highest level of education, employment status, combined household income, and living situation. Clinical data were extracted from electronic health records with patients' prior permission; data included cancer type, date of advanced cancer diagnosis, initial date of palliative care receipt, and date of death (if applicable).

Double Awareness

The construct of double awareness was measured using the DAS [17]. The DAS is a novel 41‐item scale that is intended to assess Life Engagement (22 items) and Death Contemplation (18 items), and to capture the duality of these dimensions with one oscillation item. Participants rated each statement on a Likert scale from 1 (“Rarely true for me”) to 4 (“Nearly always true for me”). Higher scores on the DAS subscales reflect higher levels of life engagement and death contemplation, respectively. Participants were categorized into groups of low, intermediate, or high levels of double awareness by calculating tertile groupings for each DAS subscale (i.e., Life Engagement, Death Contemplation). Tertile groups were used to allow for increased sensitivity and power.

Life Satisfaction

Life satisfaction was measured using three of the five items on the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS) [20]: (1) “If I could live my life over, I would change almost nothing,” (2) “I am satisfied with my life,” and (3) “So far I have gotten the important things I want in life.” Items are rated from 1 (“Strongly disagree”) to 7 (“Strongly agree”). The SWLS has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.87), 2‐month test‐retest stability (r = 0.82), and moderate correlations with measures of subjective well‐being [20]. It has further demonstrated good psychometric properties in a sample of patients with cancer [21]; higher scores on these items reflect better life satisfaction.

Quality of Life at the End of Life

The 17‐item Quality of Life at the End of Life‐Cancer (QUAL‐EC) scale has been validated in patients with advanced cancer [22]. Items make up four subscales and are rated from 1 (“Not at all true for me”) to 5 (“Completely true for me”). Two of the subscales, Life Completion (5 items) and the Preparation for End‐of‐Life (EOL) (4 items), were used to assess construct validity of the Life Engagement and Death Contemplation factors of the DAS. Examples of Life Completion items are “Despite my illness, I have a sense of meaning in my life” and “I make a positive difference in the lives of others.” Examples of Preparation for EOL items are “I worry that my family is not prepared to cope with the future” and “Thoughts of dying frighten me.” Both subscales demonstrated adequate internal consistency (Life Completion: Cronbach's α = 0.83; Preparation for EOL: Cronbach's α = 0.73) and construct validity with patients with advanced cancer [22]. The EOL items are reverse‐scored; higher scores on each subscale reflect more positive aspects of quality of life.

Death Anxiety

The Death and Dying Distress Scale (DADDS) is a validated, 15‐item measure of death‐related distress in patients with advanced cancer [23]. Items are rated from 0 (“I was not distressed about this thought or concern”) to 5 (“I experienced extreme distress”), with higher summed total scores indicating greater distress related to death and dying. The DADDS has demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.93–0.95) and validity based on its associations with depression, physical, functional, emotional, and spiritual well‐being in expected directions in patients with advanced cancer [23].

Depressive Symptoms

The Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9) [24] was used to measure depressive symptoms. The PHQ‐9 is a nine‐item, self‐report measure, with items rated on frequency of occurrence from 0 (“Not at all”) to 3 (“Nearly every day”). The PHQ‐9 has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.89) and validity in primary care and obstetrics‐gynecologic patients [24]. It has also demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.84) and significant item‐level relationships with emotional and physical functioning, fatigue, anxiety, fear of progression, and quality of life in patients with cancer [25].

Procedures

This study was approved by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board on February 5, 2020 (CAPCR ID: 19‐5952). Data were collected between March 2020 and October 2023. Eligible patients with Stage III or IV cancers were identified through weekly screening of the palliative clinic appointment lists and were introduced to the research by a member of their clinical team (a “circle of care” introduction for research recruitment was a requirement at our center). Interested individuals were subsequently contacted by a member of the research team. Due to the COVID‐19 pandemic and institutional restrictions, recruitment was halted completely between mid‐March 2020 and October 2020. When research operations resumed at our center, the recruitment process continued entirely by telephone, due to ongoing limitations on in‐clinic recruitment. A research team member called and explained the nature and purpose of the study to eligible patients and answered all questions. Informed consent was provided verbally over the telephone and/or, when possible, written consent was provided via email or standard post. Questionnaires and consent forms were sent by mail and completed either by mail (in a stamped, addressed envelope that was provided), or online (via an emailed link to a secure REDCap database), or over the telephone.

Statistical Analyses

All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 software for macOS. Participants with > 10% missing data on the DAS were excluded from further analyses. Multiple imputation with five imputations, automatic imputation method, and no defined constraints were used to estimate missing values on the DAS in the analytic sample. Descriptive statistics (means [M], standard deviations [SD], frequencies) were calculated for sociodemographic and disease‐specific characteristics, as well as for the derived DAS subscales. The variables “months since advanced cancer diagnosis” and “time to death, in months,” were calculated as the difference between the date of consent and the dates of diagnosis and death, respectively. For individuals with only partial dates in their medical charts (i.e., month and year, or year alone), date values were imputed as the midpoint of that particular month or of that year (e.g., June 15, 2023).

DAS Factor Structure

An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to examine the factor structure of the DAS. This was considered to be appropriate, as the Kaiser‐Meyer‐Olkin test of sampling adequacy produced a coefficient greater than 0.60 (KMO = 0.87) and the Bartlett's test of sphericity was significant (χ2 [820] = 4100.60, p < 0.001) [26]. The EFA employed principal axis factoring with direct oblimin oblique rotation. We extracted two factors, in line with our theorized two‐dimensional double awareness construct. Scale items were removed if their loadings did not follow the “0.40, 0.30, 0.20” rule, indicating that an item is satisfactory if it loads onto a primary factor above 0.40, if its cross‐loadings are below 0.30, and if the difference between the primary and other loadings is at least 0.20 [27].

Internal Consistency and Construct Validity ß

Cronbach's α was calculated for each DAS subscale identified from the EFA to assess the internal consistency of the DAS, with α ≥ 0.70 indicating acceptable internal consistency [19]. Construct validity of the DAS subscales was assessed by evaluating convergent and discriminant validity, using Pearson's correlations. The criterion used for convergent validity was r ≥ 0.50, and the criterion for discriminant validity was r < 0.30, in line with Cohen's standards for small (r = 0.10), moderate (r = 0.30), and large (r = 0.50) correlation coefficients [28]. The Life Engagement subscale was assessed for convergent validity with measures of similar constructs: satisfaction with life (SWLS), quality of life at the end of life (QUAL‐EC total score), and Life Completion (QUAL‐EC subscale). The Death Contemplation subscale was assessed for convergent validity with the measure of Preparation for EOL (QUAL‐EC subscale) and for discriminant validity with the measure of death anxiety (DADDS).

Differences in Outcomes Among Double Awareness Groups

We conducted one‐way ANOVAs to evaluate group differences, with Life Engagement or Death Contemplation tertile groupings as the independent Group variable and quality of life at the end of life (QUAL‐EC total), depressive symptoms (PHQ‐9), and death anxiety (DADDS total) as the outcome variables. With a significant omnibus test, post‐hoc pairwise comparisons with Tukey's test were conducted to identify specific group differences. Significance was set to p < 0.05. In addition, we crossed the two tertile groupings to create nine Life Engagement x Death Contemplation groupings and conducted the ANOVAs with the combined grouping as the independent Group variable and with the same quality of life and distress outcomes.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Of the 370 potentially eligible patients approached, 239 (64.6%) consented to partake in the present study. Overall, 12 patients subsequently withdrew consent, five were lost to follow‐up, two no longer met eligibility criteria, and 17 died before completion or return of their measures. Two hundred and three participants completed the questionnaire package; however, data from six participants were excluded due to > 10% missing data on the DAS, leaving 197 participants included in the following analyses.

Table 1 summarizes the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the 197 participants who formed the analytic sample. Their mean age was 61.5 years (range 27–91), and the majority were female (55.6%), White (70.7%), married or in a common‐law relationship (62.0%), not working (79.3%), had an undergraduate or higher degree (61.9%), and had a household income of < $100,000 CAD (67.8%). The most common cancer type was lung (18.3%), followed by gastrointestinal (17.8%). On average, the duration of time since participants were diagnosed with an advanced cancer was approximately 47.5 months (range 2.9–249.1), and the average duration of time that participants had been receiving palliative care was approximately 13.5 months (range 0–88.5; n = 195). For the subsample of participants who were confirmed to have died by the time of analyses (n = 96), the time from study consent to date of death ranged from 1.0 to 43.5 months, with an average of approximately 12.5 months.

These items loaded onto two extracted factors labeled Life Engagement (21 items) and Death Contemplation (13 items). The content of all items appeared to accurately reflect the meaning of their respective factor, except for two reverse‐scored items (“Thinking about dying makes me feel hopeless” and “Thinking about my death makes me feel hopeless”) that were designed to represent Death Contemplation but loaded onto Life Engagement. The oscillation item, “I accept that I have good days and bad days,” had a factor loading of only 0.27 on both factors and therefore was not retained.

Internal Consistency and Construct Validity

Both the Life Engagement (Cronbach's α = 0.92) and Death Contemplation (Cronbach's α = 0.87) subscales, as well as the full DAS (Cronbach's α = 0.91), demonstrated high internal consistency. The range of possible subscale scores and observed subscale scores in our sample were as follows: Life Engagement, 21–84 and 35–84, respectively; Death Contemplation, 13–52 and 14–52, respectively. The average Life Engagement score for the sample was in the upper range (M [SD] = 70.89 [10.39]), whereas the average Death Contemplation score was in the mid‐range (M [SD] = 34.41 [8.65]). The two subscales showed a small positive inter‐correlation (r = 0.30, p < 0.001, N = 197).

Correlations for assessing construct validity (all p's < 0.001) are presented in Table 3. Across validity measures, the correlations were consistently lower for the Death Contemplation subscale. Convergent validity was supported by the relationship between Life Engagement and the QUAL‐EC total score (r = 0.62) and QUAL‐EC Life Completion subscale (r = 0.60). Discriminant validity was supported by the relationship between Death Contemplation and death anxiety (DADDS total score; r = −0.03). However, convergent validity was not supported by the relationship between Life Engagement and Satisfaction with Life (SWLS score; r = 0.46), nor by Death Contemplation and QUAL‐EC Preparation for EOL subscales (r = 0.23).

Differences in Outcomes Among Double Awareness Groups

Tertile groupings were made based on the Life Engagement and Death Contemplation total scores. For Life Engagement, total scores of 35–68 represented the low group, 69 to 76 represented the intermediate group, and scores of 77–84 represented the high group. For Death Contemplation, total scores of 14–30 represented the low group, 31 to 37 represented the intermediate group, and scores of 38–52 represented the high group. The number of participants within the individual Life Engagement and Death Contemplation tertile groupings, as well as within the combined Life Engagement x Death Contemplation interaction groupings, are reported in Table 4. For both Life Engagement and Death Contemplation, 65 (33.0%) showed low levels, 62 (31.5%) showed intermediate levels, and 70 (35.5%) showed high levels. For the combined Life Engagement x Death Contemplation groupings, similar numbers of participants were considered to have low double awareness (low Life Engagement and low Death Contemplation) and high double awareness (high Life Engagement and high Death Contemplation; n = 32–34 [16.2%–17.3%]), while fewer were considered to have intermediate double awareness (intermediate Life Engagement and intermediate Death Contemplation; n = 20 [10.2%]). We limited comparisons to these three groups for simplicity.

The Death Contemplation groups demonstrated a significant group effect only on the QUAL‐EC (F[2, 189] = 12.26, p < 0.001). The low Death Contemplation group had worse quality of life than both the intermediate (p = 0.027) and high (p < 0.001) groups, while the latter two groups did not differ significantly (p = 0.081).

Finally, the combined Life Engagement x Death Contemplation groups showed a significant Group effect on all three outcomes (QUAL‐EC: F[2, 82] = 28.95, p < 0.001; PHQ‐9: F[2, 83] = 9.26, p < 0.001; DADDS: F[2, 82] = 7.66, p < 0.001). On the QUAL‐EC, all groups differed significantly (ranging from p ≤ 0.001 to p ≤ 0.007), with the low double awareness group showing the worst quality of life and the high double awareness group showing the best quality of life. On the PHQ‐9, the high combined group had significantly less severe depressive symptoms than both the low (p < 0.001) and intermediate (p = 0.04) groups, while the latter two groups did not significantly differ (p = 0.44). Similarly, the high combined group had significantly less death anxiety, based on scores on the DADDS, than either the low (p ≤ 0.001) and intermediate (p = 0.04) groups, while the latter two groups did not differ significantly (p = 0.70).

Discussion

This study is the first to evaluate the psychometric properties of the DAS, a novel measure created to assess ‘double awareness’ in individuals with advanced disease. We found that the items on the two identified factors, Life Engagement and Death Contemplation, mostly loaded onto their expected factors, and that both factors displayed good internal consistency.

The final DAS included 34 total items, with 21 measuring Life Engagement and 13 measuring Death Contemplation. Two of these included items did not load onto Death Contemplation as expected and instead loaded and are included in Life Engagement. These items both enquire about hopelessness when thinking about death or the dying process and were reverse scored. It appears that the ability to think about dying and death without feeling hopeless is a characteristic that may facilitate or be associated with life engagement. We also reviewed the seven items excluded due to their low factor loading on either Life Engagement or Death Contemplation, which revealed that these statements include themes (e.g., social connection/impact, resiliency, life preservation) that may have less specificity for either of the two DAS factors. Other features that may have contributed to these items not loading onto either factor include their compound phrasing, or their definitive or negative phrasing.

Convergent validity was supported by the relationship of Life Engagement with quality of life near the end of life and life completion, while discriminant validity of Death Contemplation was supported by its lack of association with death anxiety. While discriminant validity between Death Contemplation and death anxiety was met based on our pre‐set cut‐off, the final correlation was close to but below the cut‐off. Thus, future research should continue to examine the relationship between death contemplation and death anxiety and the potential overlap of these constructs. The criteria for convergent validity were not met for Life Engagement and satisfaction with life, nor between Death Contemplation and preparation for the end of life. There are a few plausible explanations for this lack of convergent findings. For example, the Satisfaction with Life subscale measures satisfaction with the life already lived, whereas the DAS Life Engagement subscale assesses present life engagement. The lack of convergence between the Death Contemplation subscale and Preparation for EOL subscale of the QUAL‐EC could be because the Death Contemplation items of the DAS reflect thinking about and discussing one's dying and death openly, whereas the four Preparation for EOL items on the QUAL‐EC mostly reflect the perceived burden of one's death on others. Life Engagement and Life Satisfaction may also have correlated less strongly because cultural norms can shape how individuals interpret and report these constructs. In addition, differences in item wording on these measures may introduce method effects that reduce correlations by altering how participants cognitively process and respond to those items.

Differences in the patient‐reported outcomes of quality of life at the end of life and other distress outcomes across double awareness groups were in the hypothesized directions for the Life Engagement subscale and the combined Life Engagement x Death Contemplation groups; for the Death Contemplation subscale, the expected differences were observed with quality of life at the end of life. Taken together, these findings lend preliminary support for the validity of the revised, 34‐item DAS as a measure of double awareness in individuals living with advanced cancer [17].

High double awareness in patients in the present study was associated with better quality of life and with lower levels of depression and death anxiety, compared to either the low or intermediate double awareness groups. These findings support the theorized adaptive benefit of double awareness [29, 30]. High life engagement alone appeared to be more beneficial than intermediate or low life engagement across all outcomes and, in individuals with advanced cancer, may reflect a flexible capacity to adapt to the changing circumstances of disease progression [11]. Those with high and intermediate death contemplation reported better quality of life near the end of life, compared to those with low death contemplation. This finding is consistent with observations that the capacity to reflect on one's death may increase the likelihood of engagement in advance care planning and end‐of‐life conversations [31].

Implications

A variety of individual and contextual factors may affect the achievement of double awareness in individuals with advanced disease. These include the nature and progression of the disease, the support provided by their environment, and their ability to hold multiple or alternative perspectives, a psychological process known as mentalization [32]. While the capacity for mentalization has often been regarded as an individual characteristic, research suggests that it may be enhanced in the context of a therapeutic relationship [33]. Mentalization‐based psychotherapeutic interventions, such as Managing Cancer and Living Meaningfully (CALM) [30], may be of particular value in that regard, by providing a safe space in which patients can contemplate death, while also considering their active engagement in life [30]. The DAS also has potential value as a research outcome measure to assess the effectiveness of psychotherapeutic and palliative care interventions in the promotion of double awareness. Such research could shed light on the utility of the DAS to assess the quality of psychosocial or palliative care for patients with advanced disease.

Limitations

The relative homogeneity of the sample, which was predominantly White, highly educated, and English speaking, may limit the generalizability of the study findings. Further, as participants were recruited from a single tertiary cancer center and were receiving longitudinal outpatient palliative care, the findings may not generalize to patients with more diverse sociodemographic characteristics, at different disease stages, or who lack access to palliative care. Study recruitment partly took place during the COVID‐19 pandemic, and the widespread restrictions in social engagement may have limited patients' access to clinical care and social support, as well as their ability to engage in meaningful life activities. Given these limitations, further research is also needed to determine the validity and relevance of the DAS in other advanced diseases and in other clinical and geographic settings. Finally, although Tukey's post‐hoc test already controls the family‐wise error rate within each ANOVA, we recognize that multiple ANOVAs were conducted across several outcomes. This broader multiplicity may increase the chance of Type I error.

Conclusions

The overall findings of the present study provide preliminary support for the validity of the DAS as a measure of double awareness in patients with advanced cancer. Further validation of the DAS is needed in diverse clinical and geographic settings to confirm the generalizability of the scale and its two factors. Research is also needed to evaluate the utility of the DAS as a clinical outcome measure, as well as an outcome measure in clinical trials of psychotherapeutic and palliative care interventions in the context of advanced cancer [34, 35].
