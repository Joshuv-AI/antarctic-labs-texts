Improving emergency care capacity with the WHO-ICRC Basic Emergency Care (BEC) course
Authors: Boris Arnaud Nteungue Kouomogne, Bissouma-Ledjou Tania, Chanceline Bilounga Ndongo, Alvine Choula Noulala, Hyppolite Kalambay Ntembwa, Pierre Claver Kariyo, Baonga Ba Pouth, Douba Epée, Bitang Louis Joss, Bertolt Brecht Kouam Nteungue, Dieudonnée Reine Ndougou, Mochache Trufosa, Simon Tumusiime, Ambomo Sylvie Myriam, Lauren Lai King, Annet Ngabirano Alenyo, Antoinette Naidoo, Linda Endale Esso, Yap Boum, Habimana Phanuel, Georges Alain Etoundi Mballa, Lee Wallis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlobally, the demand for acute care is surging, disproportionately burdening low- and middle-income countries. Training for healthcare providers can play a pivotal role in reducing related mortality and improving overall health and well-being, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Despite the potential for impact, the World Health Organization- International Committee of the Red Cross (WHO-ICRC) Basic Emergency Care (BEC) course is yet to be fully rolled out in Francophone African nations such as Cameroon, Djibouti, Haiti, Morocco, and Tunisia. In this paper, we describe the introduction of this training in these countries.MethodsThis study utilized pre- and post-course surveys to gauge change in knowledge and confidence in delivering acute care. A comprehensive 4-day French BEC course and a concise 1-day Training of Trainer (ToT) course were delivered in Yaounde, Cameroon, in early 2023. Participants undertook surveys pre- and post-training, offering insights into their acute care knowledge, skills, and self-assurance.ResultsThe courses were attended by 50 participants. The post-course evaluation highlighted a marked improvement in scores, showcasing a median score elevation from 72% pre-course to 88% post-course (p < 0.001). Participants’ self-confidence in handling acute care scenarios doubled. Universal acclaim was received for the quality of training materials, courses, and facilitation methodology, with nearly half feeling no amendment was necessary in the course design. However, 30% felt a longer duration should be dedicated to the practical/skills components of the course.ConclusionsThe WHO-ICRC BEC course significantly enhanced the acute care knowledge, skills, and self-confidence of participants from these five countries, laying a solid foundation for future training sessions. Provisional trainers are now proficient in expanding such training within their regions. Evaluating patient outcomes post-training presents significant challenges, highlighting a crucial area for future research and scrutiny.Key words: Basic Emergency Care, Knowledge, Emergency care, Training, Cameroon

BACKGROUND

Acute care spans emergency, critical, and operative care and describes health system and service delivery innovations that facilitate early recognition and timely intervention for conditions where a delay of hours may result in avoidable death or disability or make treatments less effective [1]. Conditions addressed by acute care include injury, infections including sepsis, acute complications of pregnancy, and non-communicable diseases such as myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiac arrest. These conditions account for over 50% of the deaths in Low-and-Middle-Income Countries (LMICs) and comprise the ten leading causes of death globally and the top killers in the under-18 age group [2]. Models suggest that in LMICs, over half of deaths and up to 2.5 billion disability-adjusted life-years annually could be addressed through the implementation of effective acute care, and we are unlikely to meet the Sustainable Development Goals targets without major near-term efforts to strengthen health systems to deliver effective acute care [3]. The coronavirus disease pandemic (COVID-19) highlighted the extraordinary cost of failed acute care readiness, both for early surveillance and maintenance of access to health care, and gaps in acute care delivery have resulted in millions of avoidable deaths in countries across all income levels [4].

One of the major gaps in acute care delivery in these settings is in the capacity of first contact healthcare workers, who often lack formal training in recognition and management of emergency conditions, leading to critical gaps in preparedness and response [[5], [6], [7]]. Emergency care courses delivered in LMICs often rely on partnerships with high-income countries and have limitations in scope, targeting specific areas such as trauma or resuscitation [8].

In response to the pressing need for context-specific and accessible emergency care education, the WHO, in collaboration with the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), developed the Basic Emergency Care (BEC) course. It is an open-source learning program designed specifically for first-contact healthcare workers operating in low-resource settings [9]. BEC is delivered as a 5-day face-to-face course or, from late 2023, as a hybrid model through the World Health Organization Academy, and, to date, well over 20,000 providers have been trained.

Assessments of BEC conducted in Uganda, Tanzania, Nigeria, Zambia, Malawi, and Ukraine have reported positive outcomes, including improved participant knowledge-based test performance and increased healthcare worker confidence in executing essential emergency care skills [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. There are no published studies on BEC in French-speaking countries.

In response to the rising need, the World Health Organization (WHO) collaborated with the African Federation for Emergency Medicine (AFEM) and the Cameroon Ministry of Health to offer BEC training for participants from several French-speaking African nations in early 2023. Together, they organized two BEC and Training of Trainer (ToT) courses in Cameroon. This initiative aimed to augment the availability of competent BEC trainers in Cameroon, Djibouti, Haiti, Morocco, and Tunisia. This paper reports the change in emergency care knowledge, skills, and attitude for this cohort of participants.

METHODS

Study design

A prospective study was performed to assess pre- and post- changes in a range of participant measures.

Participants and setting

French-speaking WHO BEC facilitators were identified through AFEM. Under the coordination of the WHO Regional Office for Africa, selected countries (Cameroon, Djibouti, Haiti, Morocco, and Tunisia) designated participants. The number of participants per country was determined by the WHO at its three levels, and the complete list was obtained from the Ministries of Health of each country based on the profile of being either a nurse or physician with a teaching background (session 1) and working at first contact health facilities (session 2). Two BEC trainings with a ToT session (four day training plus one day ToT) and one BEC course were executed in the city of Yaoundé in Cameroon from January 9th to 20th, 2023. Courses were conducted in French and endorsed by WHO, AFEM and the MoH.

Data collection

There were three main evaluation tools used throughout the courses: (a) a 25-item multiple-choice questionnaire that aimed at evaluating participants’ knowledge, filled out by all the 50 participants, (b) a 12-item questionnaire that helped assessing participant’s confidence filled by the 21 participants who attended the second BEC session; and (c) a 12-item semi-structured feedback survey filled out by all 50 participants.

The success of the 4-day BEC course was defined as the attendance of all sessions, the completion of all sections in the course workbook, and a score > 75% in their post-course examination. Moreover, participants needed to succeed to the practical workshops led by facilitators that involved simulations with real patients with the aim of evaluating the participants’ responses to each emergency care skill. To become a provisional BEC trainer, each participant who succeeded in the 4-day course attended the 1-day ToT course.

The 12-item questionnaire designed to assess participants’ confidence in providing emergency care had a list of 12 emergency care actions and was filled out before and after the course using a Likert scale with the range 1 (least confident), 2 (not confident), 3 (confident), and 4 (very confident). A paired sample t-test was used to assess differences between mean confidence ratings before and after training.

The 12-item semi-structured feedback survey was completed only after the course. While the first 8 questions were based on assessing the content of the course using a Likert Scale (very appropriate to very inappropriate, excellent to unacceptable, very clear to not clear, strongly agree to strongly disagree), the last questions were open-ended questions designed to assess what participants appreciated and what they would like to be changed as far as the BEC course is concerned.

Data Analysis

All data were coded in Microsoft Excel and analyzed in SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.). All data were reported as frequencies and percentages. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess differences in the median score with 95% Confidence Interval (CI) of each participant before and after training, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used to assess differences between categories of participants (nurses, doctors, and specialists). Change in proportions in self-reported confidence was analysed using McNemar’s chi-square test for paired proportions with the exact McNemar significance probability. All significance levels were set at a p-value lower than 0.05.

Costs analysis

We also reported the expenses related to the implementation of the BEC and ToT courses with a focus on the identifying costs spent per participant and per facilitator.

Ethical approval

The training and assessments used here were conducted under an administrative approval provided by the Cameroon Ministry of Health; the study received clearance to be published by the World Health Organization under the number ePub-IP-00349826-PC on July 1st, 2024.

Emergency Care Knowledge

The median pre-test score was 70% (95%CI: 64% - 80%), which is below the end-of-course passing mark (75%). This rating improved significantly to 88% (95%CI: 88% - 92%) at the post-test (Table 2), increasing by 16% (95%CI: 8% - 24%). Five (10%) participants failed: four nurses and a specialist (Table 3). The score of nurses improved by approximately 20%, although the median remained below the doctors. Significant differences were found in the median post-test scores between nurses and doctors (p=0.003) (Table 4).

Confidence

All the 21 participants attending the second BEC session were surveyed before and after the training on their confidence in providing a set of 12 core life-saving emergency care skills. Before the course, less than 34% of participants were confident in performing emergency care skills, increasing to 85% on conclusion of the course (Table 5). Only confidence in the management of the acutely ill adult did not reach significance. We further computed the correlation analysis of confidence levels between and after the training, that showed how the strengths of the correlation varied from being moderate to weak, with all p-values being greater than 0.05, thus the correlations were not significant.

Evaluation of the course by participants

All participants rated their opinion on multiple aspects of the course. In terms of appreciation for the courses: 40% (20/50) reported nothing should be changed, 30% (15/50) reported there should be more time for practices, 72% (36/50) appreciated the methodological approaches used, 2% (1/50) reported there should be more time for reading BEC book, and 4% (2/50) said some protocols should be contextualized to the context; 24% (12/50) enjoyed being exposed to new approaches (ABCDE and SAMPLE).

Cost analysis

The WHO Geneva funded this activity to a total cost of US$137,683 from January 9 to 21, 2023; the share in the budget was 38% for transportation and flights tickets, 35% for daily subsistence allowance (DSA), and 27% for logistics. The median cost of participation for trainees was US$762 and US$3071 for trainers, mostly due to flights and hotel costs.

DISCUSSION

This multi-country French language BEC training was successfully conducted in Cameroon, involving participants from five countries and generating 50 BEC-trained providers, 26 WHO BEC provisional trainers, and one BEC master trainer. Overall emergency care knowledge was significantly improved, with 90% of participants successfully passing the course. The course content was reported to be context appropriate and useful in the participants’ clinical practice and would improve their ability to manage a range of emergency presentations. There was a request for more hands-on practical skills training through the course. This is consistent with reports by Kivlehan et al. [12] , who found an increase of five points with an overall appreciation of training materials after the 5-day BEC course in Tanzania and Uganda; Olufadeji et al. [11] found an increase of 14% in knowledge with an increase in participants’ self-confidence to perform the 12 emergency skills after a BEC course in Lagos, Nigeria ; Broccoli et al. [13] found an increase of 19 points in EC knowledge with an improvement in self-confidence in managing emergency conditions in Zambia; in Ukraine Kivlehan et al. found an increase of 3 points with improvements in self-confidence in managing emergencies and positive course feedback after a 5-day BEC training [10], and an improvement in more than 80% of participants was reported by Khongo et al. [16] after a BEC course in Malawi.

Participants’ self-confidence in performing emergency skills increased significantly, reflecting well on both the quality of the training and the course contents. The lowest confidence was reported for the use of emergency drugs, suggesting that while the BEC course only teaches a small number of medications, more time should be devoted to their usage. Mastery of skills requires consistent practice, and a mentorship approach could be useful in achieving this. Other similar studies reported an improvement in confidence after BEC courses in Malawi, a three-time increase in Zambia, an increase of 0.74 points in Tanzania and Uganda, and a significant increase in skills in Nigeria and Ukraine [[11], [12], [13],16].

Since there were few French-speaking BEC master trainers, the multi-country approach with participants from multiple WHO regions (Africa, the Middle East, and the Americas) was helpful in such an international training approach. However, it did dramatically increase the overall costs. Local course costs (excluding per diem and transportation) only involved venue and equipment hire, where necessary, printing, and catering, making the course highly affordable even in low-resource settings. In similar 5-day BEC TOT courses conducted in Tanzania and Uganda, the average cost of training provisional trainers was US$335 per participant, and that of training providers was US$237 per participant, significantly lower than in this course. Although these were in-country courses with local participants, the costs were still impacted by the need for international trainers, given that they were pilot courses. One of the achievements from this multi-country training is to increase the number of locally available in-country French-speaking trainers, which will in turn make the course more accessible to health care workers in these countries by driving down the training cost [12].

Investogators were unable to assess the impact of the BEC course on clinical practice or patient outcomes. It was reported that a 1-year follow-up study in Neno District, Malawi, following a BEC course with mentorship of participants, showed sustained knowledge gains in emergency care and an increase in skills confidence 1 year post the course [16]. It is therefore reasonable to expect sustainment in knowledge after this course; however, a follow-up assessment will be planned.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. The sample size was determined by the funding partners in collaboration with the Ministry of Health, and the selection of participants was not random; each country was required to appoint nurses and physicians according to the specified profile; this could have led to some biases in the evaluation of knowledge and confidence. Additionally, it did not monitor how participants managed patients in the emergency department, making it challenging to associate knowledge gains with practical emergency actions. The training format included participants from five different nations with significant costs, making it potentially challenging to replicate. Participants indicated a preference for an extra day focused on practical skills, though this was not possible. A hybrid training format (combining online and in-person sessions) might help to minimize theoretical content and enhance practical application time.

CONCLUSION

Participants' knowledge and confidence in handling emergencies were positively enhanced by the WHO BEC course. A hybrid training program is suggested to provide additional time for practical exercises. All participants were eager to expand the course's reach in their home countries, and the course's overall components were viewed as contextually appropriate.
