PMOS in black girls and women: from high to low risk
Authors: Francis de Zegher, Lourdes Ibáñez
Published: 2026

The group of Anuja Dokras in Philadelphia is to be lauded for uncovering that socio-economic deprivation in black women with polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) is a major contributor to the black-white disparity in the subsequent development of metabolic syndrome [1]. The aims of this comment are to draw attention on mechanisms whereby socio-economic deprivation may participate in the sequential development of PMOS and metabolic syndrome, and to point to a hopeful perspective for black girls and women.

Let’s start with a reminder of a current hypothesis: PMOS may be essentially driven by ectopic fat (in liver and viscera) resulting from a mismatch between early adipogenesis and later lipogenesis, in other words, from an imbalance between a limited capacity and a generous demand to store lipids in subcutaneous depots [2, 3]. Socio-economic deprivation may contribute -- prenatally and postnatally -- to such imbalance.

Indeed, more socio-economic deprivation associates with lower birth weight [4, 5] which, in turn, associates with less subcutaneous adipogenesis, by raising the circulating levels of DLK-1 (formerly called Pref-1, an inhibitor of preadipocyte differentiation into adipocytes) [6, 7] and/or by reducing the placental supply (via the umbilical cord) of exosomal peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPAR-gamma) to fetal preadipocytes [8]. In the United States, black infants have a lower mean birth weight (for gestational age) than white infants, and this difference appears to have socio-economic rather than genetic origins [9]. Higher levels of socio-economic deprivation are also consistently linked to higher averages of postnatal weight gain and adult body mass index [10–12].

For a given genetic background, it is the mismatch between less prenatal and more postnatal weight gain that co-determines the risk of ultimately developing a metabolic disorder such as PMOS or type 2 diabetes [2, 3, 13, 14]. In the current United States environment, black girls and women are at relatively high risk for developing PMOS (Fig. 1, left panel) [1].

In the future, however, black girls and women in the United States may become individuals at relatively low PMOS risk (Fig. 1, right panel). Epigenetic settings should become more advantageous, once socio-economic deprivation finally fades away. Genetic variation favors an abundance of gluteofemoral adipose tissue in black girls and women of African descent (Fig. 2) [15, 16], and this subcutaneous depot allows for a healthy fat storage that protects against the adverse effects of ectopic lipid accumulation in insulin-sensitive organs, and thus also against PMOS [17]. In the absence of early growth restraint and/or later obesity, black girls and women are among the least susceptible to develop PMOS [18–20].

In conclusion, the hopeful perspective is that black girls and women will evolve from a high-risk to a low-risk scenario for the development of PMOS, and potentially for other disorders driven by ectopic lipid accumulation.
