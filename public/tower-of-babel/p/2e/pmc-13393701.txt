Evaluation of an emotion regulation and healthy lifestyle program (HealthTRAC) for adolescents with obesity: a randomized controlled trial protocol
Authors: Wendy Hadley, David Barker, Christopher Houck, Alex Foster, Kimberly Reynolds, Katherine Darling, Jennifer Warnick, Elissa Jelalian
Published: 2026

Introduction

An alarming number of youth are impacted by obesity [1], including approximately 22% of 12-19 year-olds in the U.S [2]. Adolescents with obesity are at increased risk for continued weight gain and negative health consequences, including increased risk for type 2 diabetes [3,4], polycystic ovary syndrome [5], obstructive sleep apnea [6], and nonalcoholic liver disease [7]. Adolescent obesity is also a strong predictor of obesity in adulthood [8,9], which in turn is related to increased rates of morbidity and mortality [10,11]. In addition to experiencing significant health consequences, these youth are at increased risk for a number of negative psychosocial outcomes, including significant reductions in health related quality of life, decreased self-esteem, and behavioral concerns [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].

Despite the importance of addressing obesity during adolescence, it remains an understudied area compared with the focus on adults and school age children [17].Both the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) have identified intensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment (IHBLT) as the cornerstone of intervention for youth with obesity [18,19]. While the frequency of pharmacotherapy for treatment of obesity in adolescents has increased during the last five years [20], IHBLT remains a critical standalone or adjunctive intervention. Effective IHBLTs are multicomponent interventions that include >26 contact hours, attention to nutrition and physical activity, and strategies to support behavior change combined with family involvement. Interventions that meet these criteria and specifically target adolescents are less common [21] and tend to produce small effects [22]. Furthermore, there is considerable variability within studies in adolescent response to the same intervention [23,24]. Thus, there is a pressing need for research to increase the types and potency of evidence-based lifestyle interventions for adolescents [25].

One domain that may be particularly relevant to the development and maintenance of obesity in teens is emotion regulation. Emotion regulation is thought of as the set of processes used to manage feelings and their expression in order to achieve goals [26]. It includes the use of both effortful and automatic processes [27] that serve to reduce the intensity or frequency of emotional states (e.g., lability) and improves across adolescent development. Notably, self-report of emotion regulation has been related to eating in response to negative affect in youth across the weight continuum [28], and maladaptive emotion regulation strategies have been found to relate to emotional eating [29]and mediate the relationship between negative interpersonal events and emotional eating in adolescents with obesity [30]. Further, adolescents with poorer general emotion regulation have been found to consume more energy dense (snack/junk) foods and report greater amounts of sedentary behavior [31]. Poor emotion regulation among adolescents has also been associated both cross-sectionally [32] and longitudinally with more rapid weight gain and higher body mass index (BMI) [33,34]. Taken together, this work shows that regulation of emotions play a pivotal role in eating and activity behaviors and weight regulation.

While lifestyle interventions commonly focus on teaching strategies to manage global negative mood (e.g., coping), they do not address the distinct process of emotion regulation. As outlined by Gross [26], coping and emotion regulation, while related, are distinct processes. Specifically, emotion regulation is thought of as a more momentary response to either positive or negative feelings, whereas coping targets reduction in negative mood over a longer period and is not linked to momentary decision making. Emotion regulation skills can be taught via teaching and modeling, making them an excellent target for intervention [36]. Two pilot studies have utilized emotion regulation strategy training to address adolescent weight-related behaviors. Key constructs from dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), including distress tolerance, mindfulness, and emotion regulation, were integrated with cognitive behavioral therapy in a small randomized controlled trial focused on adolescent girls with loss of control eating resulting in reductions eating disorder cognitions, but no improvements in BMI reduction relative to IHBLT [37,38]. A second open trial explicitly examined the effectiveness of an intervention combining DBT skills with IHBLT in adolescents with obesity. This intervention was delivered to both adolescents and caregivers across separate and combined treatment sessions. It led to significant decreases in emotional eating due to negative affect and a reduction in depressive symptoms, with non-significant decreases in BMI and zBMI [39].

We previously performed a pilot study where adolescents with overweight and obesity were randomized to an intervention integrating emotion regulation training (TRAC) with IHLBT (HealthTRAC) compared to those randomized to IHLBT only. This prior pilot trial demonstrated significant reductions in BMI in adolescents (d = .58) [40]. Furthermore, adolescents (N = 38; Mage = 14.67; 60% female; 60% minoritized backgrounds) randomized to HealthTRAC reported increased awareness of emotions and greater use of emotion regulation strategies at the 4-month follow-up. Building from this preliminary work, the current study is being conducted to examine the efficacy of the HealthTRAC intervention in a larger trial with rigorous assessment of proposed treatment mechanisms over a longer follow-up period. The study is ongoing and in the process of completing final follow-up assessments.

Study objectives

In this randomized controlled trial (NCT04861636), adolescents ages 13 to 17 years are randomly assigned to one of two group-based treatment conditions: IHBLT combined with training in emotion regulation (hereafter referenced as HealthTRAC) or IHBLT only. Both conditions include four months of weekly or bi-weekly treatment followed by eight monthly sessions over a 12-month period. Adolescents and their caregivers (hereafter referenced as dyads) were randomized to a treatment condition across seven different recruitment periods over 3 years in Rhode Island (RI) or Oregon (OR) within the United States of America. The primary aim of the study is to determine the impact of the HealthTRAC intervention on BMI and percent over median BMI. Secondary aims include examining treatment effects on emotion regulation, minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity, sedentary time, and diet quality.

Study design

The current study utilizes a two-site parallel group design in which adolescents and their caregivers are consecutively enrolled and assigned to one of two group treatment conditions, HealthTRAC or IHBLT, using a block randomization procedure stratified by adolescent gender. Dyads are randomized within the site (Rhode Island or Oregon) after completing the first assessment and confirming attendance in the two days prior to the first intervention session.

Written caregiver consent and verbal adolescent assent are obtained by trained study staff prior to enrollment. Study assessments are conducted at baseline, prior to randomization, and at 4-, and 12-months after the start of intervention sessions. Participant data is stored in secure physical or password protected and encrypted electronic files and only trained study staff have access to these files. The study is approved and administered by a single IRB by the Rhode Island site through a reliance agreement.

Enrolled participants

include 141 adolescents (BMI = 35.39; 14.95 years; 57% female; 31% Hispanic or Latino, 42% Non-Hispanic White, 27%Non-Hispanic Non-White) and a primary caregiver (44.48 years; 87% female). Seventy-three dyads are participating at the Oregon site and 68 at the Rhode Island site.

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

Adolescents are deemed eligible for study participations if: 1) they are 13-17 years of age, 2) have a BMI ≥95th percentile and an absolute BMI of ≤50, 3) both adolescent and caregiver speak English, and 4) agree to participation and randomization. Adolescents are excluded if: 1) they are enrolled in another weight management program or taking a weight loss medication, 2) have a medical condition that precluded participation in physical activity or adherence to dietary recommendations, 3) are developmentally delayed such that the intervention would not be appropriate, 4) endorse unhealthy weight control behaviors of purging, laxative, or diuretic use or report a history of eating disorder, or 5) are experiencing psychiatric impairment that would preclude participation (e.g. actively suicidal or psychotic). One adolescent and caregiver per household are allowed to participate, to eliminate the potential impact of having multiple family members participate in the same group and to eliminate potential for crosstalk.

Recruitment

Participants are recruited through direct and indirect referrals from pediatric clinical services at Hasbro Children's Hospital in Providence, RI, Doernbecher Children's Hospital in Portland and Salem, OR and Salem Pediatrics in Salem, OR. Hasbro Children's Hospital is centrally located within RI and provides care to a diverse urban population of children and adolescents (∼1635 adolescents annually; 68% from RI's urban core). Doernbecher Children's Hospital, affiliated with Oregon Health & Science University, serves the greater Portland and Salem areas and provides care to over 20,000 unique patients annually through its ambulatory care clinics. Mailings are sent to caregivers of adolescents seen within pediatric primary care and subspecialty clinics at each of the respective hospitals following a procedure in which potential participants are identified through review of electronic medical records. At both the Oregon and Rhode Island site, caregivers of potential participants are contacted by phone one week after mailers had been sent to provide more information to families. Interested participants are then screened for eligibility prior to scheduling the consent/assent and baseline assessment appointment. The Rhode Island site also uses BuildClinical, a digital service supporting participant recruitment through targeted social media advertising Interested participants are able to click on the relevant advertisement, which directs them to a study-specific landing page to complete a screening questionnaire to determine study eligibility, after which they are contacted by a research assistant.

Measures

Assessments are conducted by trained research assistants masked to intervention conditions. Each assessment includes two components. The first phase includes collecting anthropometric data and administering questionnaires, while the second phase involves completing a 7-day monitoring period during which adolescents complete periodic dietary recalls and a daily survey to assess eating in response to affect and wear an accelerometer. Baseline assessments are conducted in person with the adolescent and their caregiver and last approximately 90 min. At baseline, adolescent BMI is obtained to confirm study eligibility prior to consent. After consent, adolescents complete additional screeners to confirm the absence of an eating disorder or a serious mental health condition that would preclude participation in the study. Follow-up assessments are conducted through both face to face and virtual assessment procedures with caregivers and adolescents and last approximately 60 min. Adolescents and parents are compensated for their time completing all assessments.

Pre-Enrollment Screening: a.) The Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (EDE-Q) [41,42] is used to evaluate disordered eating attitudes and behaviors. Adolescents who endorse current or recent use of diet pills or laxatives, engaged in purging, or severely restrictive eating are excluded from participation and provided with an appropriate referral.

Descriptors: a.) Sample demographics including age, ethnicity, race, sex assigned at birth, gender, parent education, and household income are collected from caregivers and teens at baseline. b.) Mental health treatment utilization is assessed at baseline and 12 months to obtain information regarding the adolescent's mental health diagnoses, medication, participation in inpatient or day treatment programs, and number and type of therapy sessions (individual, group, family).

Primary and secondary outcomes and mechanisms are assessed at baseline, 4-, and 12-months.

BMI (primary outcome): Weight is obtained on a digital scale in light weight clothing without shoes and height is obtained using a wall-mounted or portable stadiometer. Weight and height are used to calculate BMI (kg/m2) as well as percent over median BMI [43]. Measurement of anthropometrics are manualized and parallel procedures for collection are implemented across the two sites. Anthropometric data that cannot be collected from participants at four-and twelve-month assessments are obtained from health care providers.

Adolescent Emotion Regulation (ER; mechanism): A multi-informant approach provides comprehensive assessment of a complex construct, which is rarely done and has been called for in research in the area of emotion regulation [44]. ER is assessed across two important domains (Emotional Competency and Emotion Regulation) and is obtained across multiple measures using multiple informants (caregivers and teens) and multiple methods (self-report and objective). We will use latent variable measurement models to generate factor scores for the Emotional Competency and Emotion Regulation domains for examination in subsequent analyses.

Emotional Competency The Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) assesses six domains of ER on a five-point Likert Scale via adolescent report. Alphas range from .76 to .89 with good construct validity [45]. The Awareness, Clarity, and Acceptance subscales are used to assess the domain of Emotional Competency. Emotion Regulation is measured using three DERS subscales (Strategies, Impulse, and Goals) completed by adolescents. The Emotion Regulation Behaviors Scale [46] assesses use of the specific ER strategies taught in the intervention across nine items. Sample items include “In the last week, when you were having a strong feeling (i.e., mad, sad) how often did you … get away from whatever was causing your feeling? … think about the situation differently?” Higher scores indicate more use of the ER strategies per adolescent report. Affect Dysregulation Scale [47] (both adolescent- and caregiver-report) is used to assess the frequency of adolescents' difficulties with affect regulation. Sample items include “In the past 3 months … small problems got me very upset; I had trouble controlling my feelings; people have suggested that I ‘calm down’.” Participants respond on a 4-point scale, and higher scores indicate more difficulty managing feelings (α = .72). Toronto Alexithymia Scale (adolescent report) [48] is a 20-item instrument which measures alexithymia or the awareness of emotional experiences that is central to the enaction of emotion regulation strategies. This measure generates a total scale score (α = .81). The Adolescent Self-Regulatory Inventory (ASRI) [49] was developed and validated with a sample of 6th-10th graders and demonstrates strong validity and reliability. It measures an individual's use of both functional and dysfunctional emotion regulation strategies from adolescent and caregiver perspectives. Finally, the Behavioral Indicator of Resiliency to Distress [50] is a 5-min computerized distress tolerance task for adolescents. This measure generates a score of total time that adolescents persist on a frustrating computerized task, which has been linked to emotion regulation abilities.

Emotional Eating (secondary outcome): The Emotional Eating Scale [51] is a 25-item self-report measure used to assess the propensity to cope with emotions by eating. Subjects rate their desire to eat in response to each emotion on a 5-point scale from "I have no desire to eat” to "I have a very strong desire to eat.” Subscales include: eating in response to anxiety, anger, and frustration (AAF), depressive symptoms (DEP), and feeling unsettled (UNS). One additional item assesses eating in response to positive affect (“happy”). (α = .83 to.95).

Dietary Intake (secondary outcome): Dietary Intake is measured via three, non-consecutive 24-h diet recalls completed during a 14-day window. Each recall is collected by a trained research assistant using Nutrition Data Systems for Research (Nutrition Coordinating Center, University of Minnesota, MN), a software program that employs a variation of the USDA's validated Multiple Pass Method to collect detailed information on each food and beverage consumed at each eating occasion over the previous 24-h period [52]. Outcomes of interest include consumption of energy dense foods [53,54] and overall diet quality using the Healthy Eating Index [55].The HEI measures adherence to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020-2025 and is made up of 13 component scores [56]. For this analysis, we will examine both overall diet quality (total HEI-score), as well as consumption of energy dense foods and fruits and vegetables.

Physical Activity and Sedentary Behavior (secondary outcome): Physical Activity and sedentary behavior are assessed with wrist-worn accelerometer (ActiGraph, LLC, Pensacola FL) for a 1-week period at each assessment point. The ActiGraph (wGT3X-BT) is a device the size of a pager that is worn on the wrist to measure 3-dimensional movement. It is calibrated to store data in 1-min epochs and scored using the GGIR R package [57]. We will examine daily minutes of moderate to vigorous physical activity and total sedentary time. To minimize selection bias, we will use imputation to account for missing wear times.

Interventions

Interventions: Intervention sessions are delivered in person during evening times and occur consecutively. Intervention order is randomly assigned, and dyads are unmasked to intervention condition at the start of the first treatment session. All adolescents receive a 22-session intervention comprised of 14 primary sessions (12 consecutive weekly sessions, followed by two biweekly sessions) and eight monthly maintenance sessions over a 12-month period across both interventions. Monthly maintenance sessions allow for ongoing review of content and continued support for implementation of changes made during the primary treatment phase. After cohort 1 in Oregon and cohort 2 in Rhode Island, the monthly maintenance sessions are delivered via a HIPAA-compliant video platform as a strategy for increasing attendance.

Caregiver involvement includes a total of seven sessions conducted over the course of the 22-session intervention. Three sessions are delivered during the 14 primary sessions, and an additional 4 maintenance sessions are delivered at 6-, 10-,8- and 12-months. Caregiver session content includes orientation to the specifics of the nutrition and activity intervention and principles of weight management emphasizing stimulus control, effective strategies for supporting teen nutrition and physical activity goals, focus on eating out and stimulus control in the home, sleep hygiene, communication and reinforcement of general content. This content is the same across both treatment conditions.

All group sessions last for 1 h and 15 min. The first 15 min are devoted to adolescent check-ins, including weigh-in and review of self-monitoring. The remaining hour is focused on the group content outlined below. Both conditions included standard lifestyle intervention content regarding diet, physical activity, behavior modification, and caregiver involvement (7 sessions). Total potential intervention time is 27.5 h, which is consistent with the USPSTF guidelines for recommended contact hours for intensive lifestyle interventions [58].

The Intensive Health Behavior and Lifestyle Treatment (IHBLT) 1) The Nutrition component includes a reduced deficit diet derived from calculation of estimated energy requirements needs based on the Harris and Benedict Equation [59] combined with a focus on improving diet quality, which has been implemented successfully in previous adolescent weight management trials. Sample meals and menus are provided as guidance regarding distribution of calorie choices throughout the day. Adolescents are trained in measuring foods and are asked to track consumption. Nutrition topics include orientation to the dietary plan, reading nutrition labels, portion sizes, making healthy snack choices, and dining out. Review of each topic includes presentation of written material, visual demonstration, and a “hands on” activity to encourage application of didactic material. 2) The Activity component focuses on encouraging adolescents to incrementally increase physical activity to an optimal level of 60 min per day on most days of the week, while decreasing screen time to ≤2 h/day. Adolescents are provided with an orientation to types of physical activity, the benefits of exercise, strategies for increasing physical activity, reducing sedentary time and information on how to monitor these behaviors. 3) The Behavioral component includes focus on a) self-monitoring; b) goal setting; c) stimulus control strategies; d) planning ahead; e) cues for eating and stress management; f) motivation; g) managing social situations; and h) self-esteem/body image.

The HealthTRAC intervention (see Table 1) combines the elements of IHBLT and a previously tested emotion regulation intervention targeting risk reduction (TRAC) [60,61] to create a single integrated intervention. It mirrors IHBLT in nutrition and physical activity content (see above), session length, and inclusion of caregivers. The goal of HealthTRAC is to help adolescents identify the connection between emotions and behaviors with the aim of using emotion regulation (ER) skills to increase adherence to the dietary and activity prescriptions included in IHBLT, as has been demonstrated in previous health behavior trials integrating emotion regulation training [46,60,62]. The ER content of the intervention emphasizes four skills: (1) recognizing bodily changes that signal emotional arousal, (2) labeling emotions appropriately, (3) choosing and implementing effective strategies to respond to emotions (e.g., getting away from triggers [GET OUT], physical release of emotions [LET IT OUT], and cognitive reappraisal [THINK IT OUT]), and (4) utilizing these skills in highly arousing situations. After information and skills concerning ER are taught (sessions 1-6), they are incorporated into the remaining sessions in a series of skill building activities which target a variety of emotion inducing contexts in which emotion regulation strategies are practiced. Within all sessions, special attention is given to interpersonal situations that involve intense or dysregulated affect, such as family criticism or teasing from peers. Topics focus on situations and emotions commonly experienced by adolescents, with specific emphasis on sensitivities for adolescents with obesity. Sample situations include concern about peer evaluation, frequency of snack choices, making healthy choices when home alone, exercising in front of others, and parental criticism. Attention is paid to the range of affect generated by such circumstances – e.g. sadness, embarrassment, boredom, pride – as well as how such affect could be effectively managed to support healthy behaviors. Initially, emotion regulation strategies are applied to more general situations and then to specific weight-related behaviors. ER strategies are also integrated with the key behavior of weekly weighing. Adolescents are asked to identify bodily sensations and feelings experienced at the time of weigh-in and coached to manage these responses to support the use of weight changes to better inform behavioral choices as well as practice skills learned in the intervention.

A sample lesson is illustrative of the integration of weight management behaviors with emotion regulation. In session 8, adolescents watch a brief film clip that commonly elicits feelings of sadness. Following this mood induction activity, adolescents are offered a variety of snack options that include both healthier and less healthy options. After selecting their snack, adolescents are asked to identify the bodily sensations they experience while watching the clip (e.g., tightness in chest, tears in eyes) and label their emotions (e.g., sadness). The influence of feelings on snack choices is discussed and information on healthy snack options reviewed. The discussion then turns to a review of emotion regulation strategies – including those that were used or could have been used to effectively manage the feelings experienced to allow for a healthier choice. This activity frequently leads to discussion of other behaviors (e.g., excessive screen time) that are impacted by feelings of sadness, effectively extending the activity to other behaviors that can interfere with weight management goals.

In addition to the in-vivo exercises targeting emotions and weight related behaviors, the group-based intervention allows adolescents a safe environment to disclose challenges with emotional arousal in the context of making healthier food and activity choices during the week. The group-based approach normalizes these experiences and highlights the challenges of changing and establishing new behaviors while also increasing group cohesion and support for managing these situations. The group-based approach of HealthTRAC allows for normalization of emotional arousal leading to overeating or unhealthy behaviors and broadens the discussion of the range of possible emotions that can interfere with healthy choices. Maintenance Sessions: Emotion regulation content is woven into session content as is done in the primary intervention sessions.

Facilitator Training, Supervision, and Fidelity: The manualized interventions are co-facilitated by a master's level (or beyond) clinician with training in behavioral psychology and a nutritionist. Facilitator training consisted of 17 remotely delivered cross-site sessions (1-2 h each) delivered over the first three years of intervention delivery during which information related to nutrition and physical activity content, emotion regulation skills, group dynamics, and behavior management were reviewed. General overview of topics was followed by information on session content, which facilitators are required to review in advance of meetings. This process is followed by site specific training in which facilitators complete a “walk-through” of the entire curriculum with members of the investigative team demonstrating intervention delivery. After these mock trainings, facilitators led intervention sessions during the “run-through” phase of the project, during which they were observed by the MPIs to further refine competence in intervention delivery.

Weekly supervision meetings are conducted throughout the trial and led by the site PIs. Meetings are used to review delivery of intervention content and problem-solve any challenges related to managing behavior within the group. Fidelity of intervention delivery is assessed through observer rating of videotaped sessions. Review of videotaped sessions was selected in order to capture both verbal and nonverbal aspects of participant and facilitator interactions, especially relevant for the emotion regulation skill building components of the HealthTRAC intervention. A random sample of 25% of videotaped intervention sessions stratified by condition and facilitator will be coded. Ratings of fidelity are specific to each session and include intervention content, time allocation, competence in delivering the intervention, and process elements (e.g., rapport building and validation).

Data analysis

Primary Aim: The primary outcome of this efficacy trial is change in BMI from baseline to12 months post-intervention. We will test the efficacy of HealthTRAC relative to IHBLT using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with baseline BMI being included as a covariate. Nesting of participants within small groups will be accounted for by using robust standard errors adjusted for non-independence. Analyses will be intent-to-treat and include all randomized participants.

Secondary Aims: ANCOVAs will also be used to evaluate the secondary aims of examining the impact of HealthTRAC relative to IHBLT on improvement in emotion regulation abilities, diet quality, physical activity, and sedentary activity, with the baseline assessment used as a covariate for each outcome. Standardized difference scores (i.e., Cohen's d) will be used as effect sizes.

Exploratory aims: We will examine treatment effect modifiers using causal random forests [63,64]. The approach systematically examines all measured baseline characteristics and their interactions to identify prominent treatment modifiers or combinations of modifiers and avoids many of the weaknesses of conventional single moderator approaches to subgroup analysis.

Missing data: Multiple imputations will be used in all analyses to address bias introduced by missing data. The imputation model will be fully conditional (e.g., chained equations) which can address both missing baseline data of various distributions and missing outcome data. The model will include all measured baseline data and all primary and secondary endpoints.

Covariate balance: It is expected that randomization will result in between-condition balance on baseline characteristics. However, even small to moderate between condition differences can adversely affect the test of treatment efficacy [65]. Differences in baseline characteristics among treatment conditions will be evaluated using semi-parametric tests (Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic). Given that participants are individually randomized, we will not adjust for baseline differences in the primary analyses but will provide supplementary analyses that use causal modeling approaches to adjust for between-group differences in baseline characteristics [66].

Power: Sample size was determined using G∗power 3.1.9.2. Assuming 20% attrition and a sample size of 80 participants per group, our study will be powered at .80 to detect an effect of −1.19 in BMI change between HealthTRAC and IHBLT (Cohen's d = −.50). This effect is within the range of results from our pilot work (between group difference in BMI change = −1.38 (SD = .47). It is also in line with results from a pooled analysis of two adolescent weight loss trials (combined sample size = 194) that showed a moderate effect size 10-12 months after intervention start (d = −.57) [67].

Discussion

The limited and variable impact of previous IHBLTs and the severe burden of obesity in adolescence requires testing novel approaches that can support sustainable changes in healthy dietary and physical activity practices for youth. While the primary aim of the current study is change in BMI, we recognize that lasting change is accomplished through behavioral changes to dietary choices and physical activity as identified in our guiding model (see Fig. 1). The current study will address the need for these behavioral changes through a series of innovative practices. First, a targeted focus on teaching emotion regulation strategies to adolescents with obesity may help them learn to manage the variety of emotional states that can undermine their attempts to reduce sedentary behavior, increase physical activity, and improve dietary intake. Our intervention addresses several aspects of emotion regulation, including recognizing emotion in oneself and linking dysregulated emotions to behaviors (e.g., overeating and consumption of energy dense foods in response to positive or negative emotions; increased sedentary behavior in response to negative emotions), which can impede behavior change efforts.

Additionally, mood induction tasks are practiced in vivo in the group setting and provide adolescents with the opportunity to: a) Identify and label emotions as they experience them rather than relying on retrospective reports, b) Practice using emotion regulation skills that are taught within the intervention in real-time moments of dysregulated affect (in session) that are connected to food and activity behaviors, and c) Evaluate the use of these strategies on reducing emotional intensity and making healthier choices. Through these in-vivo activities adolescents are provided with the opportunity to engage with the emotion regulation skill components and connect these to real world behaviors that can potentially interfere with weight loss efforts.

Finally, the inclusion of adolescents with psychiatric comorbidities allows for greater generalizability of study findings. Adolescent weight control trials commonly exclude participants with psychiatric diagnoses and/or are engaged in psychological treatments [68,69]. Our pilot study demonstrated that HealthTRAC was robust in the context of adolescents with a range of psychological symptoms, including Generalized Anxiety Disorder, Bipolar Disorder, and Major Depressive Disorder; with 52% of the sample reporting clinically significant mental health problems, which, while high may be more representative of the population of adolescents presenting to providers with clinical obesity [70]. Developing an intervention that is effective in supporting weight management in adolescents with psychiatric comorbidity and captures a broader range of adolescents with obesity is a novel and much needed contribution. The combination of these innovative approaches to both intervention and recruitment has the potential to provide meaningful information on what practices are necessary to impact adolescent healthy lifestyle behaviors.
