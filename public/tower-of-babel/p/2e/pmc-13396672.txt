Bioinformatics and experimental validation of druggable targets in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
Authors: Xiangqian Zhang, Hanyang Su, Quan Zhou, Yuling Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a common metabolic disease with high heterogeneity and currently lacks approved targeted therapies. Identifying druggable genes with diagnostic relevance and potential translational value may facilitate further research into precision medicine approaches for NAFLD. This study integrated transcriptome data from three independent GEO datasets (GSE33814, GSE63067, and GSE89632) and used sva and limma to correct for batch effects. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs), druggable genes obtained from DGIdb, and genes from WGCNA modules significantly associated with NAFLD were intersected to identify candidate genes. Candidate genes were systematically evaluated using functional enrichment analysis (GO, KEGG, GSEA), immune infiltration analysis (CIBERSORT), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, and co-expression networks. Finally, the expression of key genes was verified by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF). A total of nine key candidate drug targets were identified: FABP4, ADAMTS1, FOS, GPR88, IL1RL1, CD52, JUN, SERPINE1, and THBS1. These genes are primarily involved in metabolism, inflammatory response, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune regulation. ROC analysis showed that FOS and JUN had high diagnostic accuracy. Furthermore, IHC and IF results demonstrated that CD52 was significantly upregulated in NAFLD tissues, suggesting its potential relevance as a candidate target for further investigation. This study systematically identified key druggable genes for NAFLD through bioinformatics analysis and partially validated selected candidates experimentally. In particular, CD52 was upregulated in NAFLD tissues, suggesting a potential association with NAFLD-related pathological alterations. Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now renamed metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MASLD), is one of the most common chronic liver diseases worldwide, affecting over a quarter of the adult population1–4. The pathological manifestations of NAFLD lie along a continuum, ranging from simple steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)5. Severe cases can progress to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)6–9. The onset of NAFLD is closely associated with metabolic abnormalities such as obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and chronic inflammation, and is generally considered the hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome10–12. Despite the increasing prevalence and disability of NAFLD, there is currently no specific treatment for the disease, and there is an urgent need to establish prevention and treatment strategies.

The development of NAFLD involves a multifactorial process, including impaired lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and immune cell-mediated inflammatory responses13,14. The traditional “two-hit hypothesis” provides a basic framework for disease progression, but with increasing research, a growing number of scholars have proposed the “multi-hit model” to more fully elucidate its complex mechanisms15. This model emphasizes the synergistic effects of multiple molecular pathways, exacerbating the progression of liver damage and fibrosis16,17. Therefore, systematically exploring the core factors regulating these key processes is expected to provide new targets for precision diagnosis and treatment strategies.

In recent years, with the advancement of transcriptomics and systems biology, big data-based bioinformatics methods have been widely applied to explore the underlying mechanisms of chronic diseases18,19. Among them, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) has demonstrated unique advantages in identifying gene modules significantly associated with disease phenotypes, thereby enabling the screening of key genes that may play a core role in disease regulation20,21. However, for these research findings to truly translate into clinical practice, their therapeutic feasibility, particularly their druggability, must be considered.

“Druggable genes” are genes that encode proteins that can be targeted and regulated by small molecule drugs or biologics22. The targeting of research targets directly affects the potential for converting basic research results into drug development. In recent years, the Drug-Gene Interaction Database (DGIdb) has systematically compiled drug-gene interactions, covering thousands of potentially druggable genes23. However, the specific roles of these genes in NAFLD remain largely unexplained. Integrating transcriptome data with well-annotated drug target databases (including structural and functional information) can provide new insights and approaches for identifying novel molecular targets involved in the pathogenesis of NAFLD and potentially promising drug development.

Based on this background, this study integrated transcriptome data from NAFLD liver tissues from three GEO datasets (GSE33814, GSE63067, and GSE89632). Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we constructed a co-expression network and identified key modules and core genes closely associated with NAFLD status. In addition, we combined bioinformatics technology with the list of druggable genes to systematically identify several key genes with high druggability. Subsequently, the potential biological functions of these genes in NAFLD were explored through functional enrichment analysis and immune infiltration analysis. Specifically, we validated the expression and distribution of CD52 in human NAFLD tissues by immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence, further supporting its potential for future functional investigation.

The integrative analysis strategy proposed in this study effectively bridges disease mechanism research with therapeutic target discovery, providing a theoretical basis for further mechanistic studies and target validation for NAFLD. The identification of these key druggable genes not only deepens our understanding of the pathobiological mechanisms of NAFLD but also provides new directions for future translational and mechanistic research.

Methods

Transcriptome integration and identification of druggable genes

Three microarray datasets (GSE33814, GSE63067, and GSE89632) related to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) were downloaded from the GEO database, including platform and sample information: GSE33814 (GPL6884; normal group n = 13, NAFLD group n = 31), GSE63067 (GPL570; normal group n = 7, NAFLD group n = 11), and GSE89632 (GPL14951; normal group n = 24, NAFLD group n = 38). Probe-gene symbol annotation was performed using custom Perl scripts based on the corresponding GPL files; probes mapped to the same gene were averaged, and non-specific probes mapped to multiple genes were removed.

Based on the data distribution, the expression matrix was preprocessed by quantile normalization and, where necessary, log₂ transformation. Batch effects between datasets were corrected using ComBat from the sva package, and the effectiveness of normalization/batch correction was examined using box plots and density distribution plots. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between NAFLD and normal samples were identified using the limma function, with |log₂FC| > 0.6 and corrected P < 0.05 as criteria.

Drug-targeting gene information was obtained from DGIdb. Candidate genes were retained as druggable genes for subsequent analysis by performing intersection analysis between the DGIdb results and a literature-supported list of drug-targeting genes24.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

To identify gene modules associated with NAFLD, we performed weighted gene network analysis (WGCNA) using the WGCNA package (v1.73) on GSE33814 and GSE63067. An optimal soft threshold was selected to ensure scale-free network topology. Co-expression networks were then constructed and associated with disease features. The core module genes were intersected with differentially expressed genes and druggable genes for downstream analysis. Intersection genes were visualized using Venn diagrams.

Validation of druggable genes

After batch correction, the expression of candidate drug target genes was verified in the combined datasets (GSE33814, GSE63067, and GSE89632). Violin plots generated with ggpubr (v0.6.0) were used to display their differential expression.

Diagnostic evaluation using ROC analysis

The diagnostic value of candidate genes was assessed by generating receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves using the pROC package (v1.18.5). Area under the curve (AUC) values and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated to quantify diagnostic accuracy.

Expression clustering and subgroup stratification

Patients were divided into high-expression and low-expression groups based on the median expression level of each druggable gene. A heatmap was used for visualization to show the clustering pattern of the merged dataset.

Transcriptome-wide correlation analysis of candidate druggable genes

Based on the merged dataset, Pearson correlation analysis was performed between each candidate druggable gene and all genes in the expression matrix to assess co-expression patterns. The correlation matrix was visualized using corrplot (v0.95), and hierarchical clustering was used to identify co-expression patterns.

GO, KEGG, and GSEA functional enrichment analysis

To elucidate the biological functions and pathways associated with the identified candidate genes, a comprehensive functional enrichment analysis was performed. First, differential expression analysis of the nine candidate genes was conducted within the integrated dataset. Genes meeting the thresholds of |log₂FC| > 1 and an adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. This set of significantly differentially expressed candidate genes was then used as the input gene set. Subsequently, Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses for this gene set were carried out using the clusterProfiler package (v4.12.6) via the standard hypergeometric test25. The significance of enriched terms and pathways was determined with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Additionally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using 1,000 permutations. Gene sets with a normalized enrichment score (NES) > 1 and an FDR < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration was estimated using CIBERSORT with the LM22 signature matrix and 1,000 permutations. The input data were quantile-normalized microarray expression matrices obtained by integrating GSE33814, GSE63067, and GSE89632 (probe values mapped to gene symbols, log2-transformed when necessary, and normalized using normalizeBetweenArrays in limma). For quality control, only samples with CIBERSORT deconvolution P value < 0.05 were retained for downstream analyses.

Experimental methods

To elucidate the expression of CD52 in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), liver tissue samples were collected from NAFLD patients and healthy controls. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Yueyang Hospital Affiliated to Hunan Normal University (ethics approval number: 2025016). All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Liver tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, P0099), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining. For antigen retrieval, sections were subjected to acidic antigen retrieval according to standard protocols. The primary antibody against CD52 (Biodragon, BD-PT6029) was used at a dilution of 1:300 for IHC and 1:150 for IF. Sections were incubated with the primary antibody under standard humidified conditions, followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies. For IHC staining, an HRP-labeled secondary antibody and DAB chromogenic system (Seville Biosciences, G1212) were applied, and CD52-positive signals were visualized as brownish-yellow staining. For IF staining, sections were incubated with a fluorescent secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594; Wuhan Proteintech, SA00013-4), followed by DAPI nuclear counterstaining (Beyotime, C1006). Cell permeabilization was performed using Triton X-100 (Beyotime, P0096), and fluorescence quenching was reduced using an anti-fade mounting medium (Beyotime, P0126). In addition, immunohistochemical staining was also performed for GPR88 and IL1RL1. The primary antibodies used were GPR88 (Affinity Biosciences, Cat# DF2812, RRID: AB_2840018) and IL1RL1 (Anti-IL1RL1 antibody, bs-23758R, Bioss, Shanghai, China), both applied at a dilution of 1:200 for IHC analysis. Furthermore, multiplex immunofluorescence staining was performed using a tyramide signal amplification (TSA) method according to the manufacturer’s protocol to enable simultaneous detection of multiple targets. Images were acquired using a Leica fluorescence microscope under identical imaging settings across all samples. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software. All measurements were conducted using consistent thresholds and analysis parameters across different groups to ensure comparability.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.1). Differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma package (v3.60.6) with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, and genes with adjusted P < 0.05 were considered significant. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Diagnostic performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis using the pROC package (v1.18.5), and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals was calculated. In weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) and gene co-expression analyses, Pearson correlation analysis was applied to assess associations between module eigengenes, clinical traits, and candidate genes using the WGCNA package (v1.73). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using hypergeometric tests implemented in the clusterProfiler package (v4.12.6) with FDR correction, while Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) significance was determined based on permutation testing. Immune cell proportions estimated by CIBERSORT (R script implementation) were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant unless otherwise specified.

Validation of candidate genes by ROC curve analysis

To evaluate the diagnostic utility of candidate druggable genes in NAFLD, ROC curve analysis was performed using the integrated expression profiles from three GEO datasets (GSE33814, GSE63067, GSE89632). Among the nine selected genes, FOS showed the highest diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.804), followed by JUN (AUC = 0.773), FABP4 (AUC = 0.747), and ADAMTS1 (AUC = 0.743). Other genes such as CD52, GPR88, and THBS1 also exhibited moderate diagnostic value (AUC > 0.68). These findings suggest that these genes may have potential diagnostic relevance for distinguishing NAFLD from controls (see Fig. 4).

Experimental validation of key protein expression in NAFLD clinical samples

In clinical samples, CD52 expression was significantly increased in liver tissue from NAFLD patients. Immunofluorescence staining revealed significantly increased fluorescence intensity in liver tissue from NAFLD patients compared with controls (Fig. 11A). Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed this finding, showing increased brown-positive staining for CD52 in NAFLD liver tissues, while expression levels were lower in controls (Fig. 11B). These findings suggest a potential association between increased CD52 expression and NAFLD.

Additionally, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed to evaluate the expression patterns of IL1RL1 and GPR88 in NAFLD clinical samples (Figure S7B–C). As shown in Figure S7B, IL1RL1 exhibited positive brownish-yellow staining in both control and NAFLD liver tissues, with hematoxylin counterstaining of nuclei. Compared with the control group, the overall staining intensity of IL1RL1 in the NAFLD group showed a decreasing trend. In Figure S7C, IHC staining of GPR88 demonstrated a similar pattern, with reduced expression observed in NAFLD liver tissues relative to controls. These results indicate that both IL1RL1 and GPR88 display a downward trend in expression in NAFLD tissues.

Discussion

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), a hepatic manifestation of metabolic syndrome, is characterized by complex pathogenesis and marked heterogeneity26,27. Currently, there are no approved targeted therapies for this condition28,29. Therefore, the identification of biologically relevant and potentially druggable targets may facilitate future precision-oriented research in NAFLD. In this study, we integrated transcriptomic data from three independent NAFLD cohorts and employed WGCNA to identify disease-associated gene modules. By cross-referencing these modules with known drug target databases, we identified a panel of genes with potential therapeutic relevance. Through functional enrichment analysis and immune infiltration profiling of nine candidate genes, together with expression validation of CD52 by IHC and IF, we further characterized the potential biological relevance of these candidates.

A total of nine key druggable genes were identified in this study, including FABP4, ADAMTS1, FOS, GPR88, IL1RL1, CD52, JUN, SERPINE1 and THBS1. These genes are associated with biological processes previously reported in NAFLD, such as lipid metabolism, inflammation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune regulation. Among them, CD52 encodes a small glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored glycoprotein that is highly expressed on mature lymphocytes and monocytes30. It modulates immune cell adhesion and migration through its interaction with the inhibitory receptor SIGLEC1031. Notably, CD52 is the molecular target of alemtuzumab, a monoclonal antibody approved for immunosuppressive therapy in chronic lymphocytic leukemia and organ transplantation32. Emerging evidence indicates that CD52 plays a role in maintaining innate immune function in cirrhosis, with its expression levels varying across natural killer (NK) cells and monocyte subsets depending on disease severity33. Together, these findings suggest that CD52 may represent a potential immunomodulatory candidate.

FABP4, a fatty acid-binding protein primarily expressed in adipocytes and macrophages, plays a key role in lipid transport, inflammatory signaling, and endoplasmic reticulum stress34–37. Its elevated expression has been closely linked to hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and progression to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)38–41. These associations suggest that FABP4 may serve as a potential link between metabolic dysregulation and liver injury in NAFLD.

ADAMTS1 is a matrix metalloproteinase involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and tumor immunity42,43. Its expression is modulated by estrogen and microRNAs44,45, suggesting a context-dependent role in liver regeneration and hepatocarcinogenesis45. This highlights ADAMTS1’s potential relevance to extracellular matrix remodeling in NAFLD.

FOS and JUN, as core components of the activator protein-1 (AP-1) transcription factor complex, serve as key regulators of hepatic inflammation and fibrosis46,47. Their activation has been consistently associated with NAFLD-related fibrogenesis, hepatocyte proliferation, and immune dysregulation, supporting their potential relevance as candidate targets48–50. Together, FOS and JUN represent key nodes in the inflammatory and fibrotic pathways underlying NAFLD.

GPR88 is an orphan G protein-coupled receptor predominantly expressed in the central nervous system51. Although hepatic expression of GPR88 is relatively low, recent studies suggest its involvement in systemic energy homeostasis and lipid metabolism via central-peripheral regulatory pathways52, potentially influencing hepatic lipid accumulation and glucose tolerance53. These findings suggest a possible indirect association with metabolic regulation relevant to NAFLD. IL1RL1 is the receptor for interleukin-33 (IL-33) and mediates several proinflammatory signaling cascades54. IL1RL1 has been associated with HBV-related cirrhosis and childhood obesity55,56, suggesting a role in inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. Thus, IL1RL1 may contribute to the inflammatory milieu and metabolic disturbances observed in NAFLD.

SERPINE1, also known as plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), is the principal inhibitor of fibrinolysis and a pivotal mediator of liver fibrosis57,58. Elevated levels of PAI-1 correlate with NAFLD progression59, insulin resistance60, and increased cardiovascular risk61. Experimental studies have shown that PAI-1 inhibition can attenuate liver fibrosis62, indicating its potential biological relevance. These findings suggest that SERPINE1 may be associated with fibrotic processes observed in NAFLD.

THBS1 encodes thrombospondin-1, a multifunctional glycoprotein involved in the regulation of angiogenesis, immune response, and tissue fibrosis63–66. Its re-expression in pathological conditions such as obesity67, liver failure68, and liver cancer underscore its biological significance in liver disease progression and its potential utility as a non-invasive biomarker58,69. THBS1 may be associated with pathological features observed in NAFLD and warrants further investigation.

Among all candidate genes, CD52 stood out due to its dual functions in metabolism and immune regulation. Based on its specific expression pattern in immune cell subsets and emerging evidence linking it to innate immune function in the liver, we selected CD52 for preliminary experimental validation. Using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) analysis, we determined the spatial localization of CD52 expression in liver tissue and found that its expression was significantly upregulated in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). This result suggests that CD52 may be associated with immune microenvironment alterations in NAFLD liver tissue and supports further investigation of its functional role.

In addition, we evaluated the diagnostic potential of the nine candidate genes using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The results demonstrated good discriminatory power in distinguishing NAFLD patients from healthy controls, suggesting their potential as a diagnostic biomarker. Among them, FOS (AUC = 0.804) and JUN (AUC = 0.773) showed the highest diagnostic performance, consistent with their central role in inflammation-driven mechanisms in NAFLD. Although THBS1 and SERPINE1 exhibited relatively lower AUC values, both genes are deeply involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and fibrogenesis, suggesting their potential relevance to the underlying pathology of NAFLD. The remaining genes, including ADAMTS1 (AUC = 0.743), CD52 (AUC = 0.724), FABP4 (AUC = 0.747), GPR88 (AUC = 0.719), and IL1RL1 (AUC = 0.684), also demonstrated favorable diagnostic accuracy and are implicated in diverse biological processes such as lipid transport, immune regulation, and intracellular signaling. However, it should be noted that a single gene is unlikely to serve as an independent diagnostic biomarker; its value is more likely to be reflected in multifactorial models or combined indicators. Further validation in larger samples and prospective cohorts is still needed in the future.

The key candidate genes identified in this study (such as FABP4, CD52, and SERPINE1) provide new clues for drug repurposing in NAFLD. Some of these genes already have corresponding targeted drug candidates or marketed drugs. For example, the FABP4/5 dual-target inhibitor Y18 has shown potential for treating inflammatory diseases in preclinical studies70; the marketed antibody alemtuzumab, targeting CD52, has clear safety data71; and the SERPINE1 inhibitor TM5441 has shown activity in tumor studies72. These agents may offer a conceptual starting point for future drug repurposing exploration in NAFLD. The feasibility of repurposing lies in the potential overlap between the known mechanisms of action of these drugs and the pathological process of NAFLD. FABP4 is involved in lipid metabolism and inflammation, CD52 regulates the immune response, and SERPINE1 is related to the immune microenvironment; theoretically, all of these could potentially intervene in the progression of NAFLD. However, successful retargeting remains a significant challenge. First, direct evidence for the specific efficacy, mechanism, and appropriate dosage of these drugs in NAFLD is lacking, requiring systematic preclinical validation. Second, NAFLD is highly heterogeneous, involving multiple processes such as metabolism, inflammation, and fibrosis; single-target drugs may only be effective for some patients or at specific stages, necessitating the combination of biomarkers for precision treatment. Furthermore, NAFLD patients often have other metabolic diseases, and the long-term safety of drugs in this population needs rigorous reassessment in new clinical trials. In summary, this study provides potential targets for drug retargeting in NAFLD, but translating these findings into clinical practice still requires overcoming the aforementioned challenges and depends on future, more in-depth mechanistic studies and clinical validation.

This study has several limitations. First, although we performed cross-validation of the candidate genes using multiple public datasets and bioinformatics approaches, further functional validation through in vitro cellular assays and in vivo animal models is still lacking. Moreover, the ROC analyses based on the merged dataset were conducted primarily for exploratory evaluation of diagnostic performance rather than as definitive validation evidence, and single-gene AUC values within the 0.7–0.8 range are not uncommon in transcriptomic screening studies and therefore should be interpreted with caution. Further systematic validation with larger sample sizes, prospective cohorts, and multifactorial combined models is needed in the future to further reduce the risk of overfitting and improve its clinical translational value. Second, as the datasets were derived from different cohorts, inter-individual heterogeneity may have introduced potential biases. In addition, as this study was based on cross-sectional data and a binary classification design, future studies incorporating well-defined disease staging or longitudinally followed clinical cohorts are needed to further characterize molecular dynamics associated with disease progression. Future research should integrate single-cell transcriptomics, multi-omics analyses, and validation in clinical specimens to more precisely elucidate the roles and mechanisms of these candidate genes across various NAFLD subtypes and stages of disease progression.

In conclusion, this study identified a panel of key genes with diagnostic potential, biological relevance, and therapeutic tractability in NAFLD. These findings offer molecular insights into NAFLD and provide a basis for further mechanistic studies and future target validation.
