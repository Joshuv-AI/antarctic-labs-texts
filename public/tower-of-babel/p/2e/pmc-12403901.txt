Moral distress and musculoskeletal disorders in Iranian prehospital EMS workers
Authors: Hossein Sheikhi, Monir Nobahar, Elahe Saleh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrehospital emergency personnel are exposed to a high prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs), and moral distress. This study aimed to assess the impact of moral distress on MSDs among prehospital emergency workers.MethodsThis cross-sectional study, conducted in 2024, by using a census sampling approach, involved 125 employees from the prehospital emergency centers of Semnan University of Medical Sciences. Data collection tools included a demographic questionnaire, a moral distress scale, and the Nordic Musculoskeletal Questionnaire (NMQ) for assessing ergonomic complications and discomforts. Data analysis methods were structural equation modeling (SEM).ResultsThe results indicated that the most common pain complaints were in the lower back and buttocks (52.8%) and the back (42.4%). Over the past week, neck pain was the most frequently reported (44%), followed closely by lower back and buttocks pain (42.4%). At the time of the study, the highest pain prevalence was in the neck (73.6%) and the lower back and buttocks (36.8%). The mean moral distress score was 54.5 ± 41.9, with 86.4% of participants reporting low levels of moral distress. The moral distress was not significantly affect MSDs (P = 0.061). However, age and work experience were significantly associated with MSDs (P = 0.001) and moral distress (P = 0.005).ConclusionThe findings demonstrate that nearly half of the prehospital emergency workers experienced MSDs, particularly in the neck and back. Most participants reported low levels of moral distress, which did not significantly influence MSDs. This P-value may indicate the presence of a true effect that could become more apparent with a larger sample size or a different study design. It is recommended to include intervention studies and qualitative research. In order to reduce moral distress and MSDs in prehospital emergency workers, in addition to regular screenings, it is recommended that the amount of work-related pressure be reduced, especially for older employees and those with more extensive work experience.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Moral distress, Musculoskeletal disorders, Prehospital emergency workers

Background

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are defined as any damage to the musculoskeletal system or associated nerves that impairs function [1]. They can affect a wide range of body structures, including muscles, tendons, ligaments, joints, peripheral nerves, and blood vessels [2]. MSDs are one of the leading causes of occupational injuries and disability in both advanced and developing countries [3]. The International Labor Organization estimates that approximately 160 million work-related illnesses occur worldwide each year, where the highest prevalent illnesses are MSDs. In Iran, there is a relatively high prevalence of MSDs affecting the lower extremity (particularly the lower back) and upper extremity (including the neck and shoulders) of workers which is more acute compared to other countries [4]. MSDs are among the most common complaints reported by prehospital emergency medical services (EMS) workers [5].

Prehospital care refers to medical care that begins at the patient’s bedside and continues until they are admitted to the hospital emergency department [6]. It is a crucial component of the emergency healthcare continuum, providing life-saving medical services to critically ill or injured patients [7]. Prehospital emergency workers are responsible for providing and coordinating prehospital emergency medical care [8]. As first responders to critically ill patients [9], they play a crucial role in improving patient survival outcomes [10].

EMS personnel work in high-stress environments and encounter a diverse range of patients with both physical and mental health issues [11, 12]. The demanding nature of prehospital emergency work exposes these professionals to significant physical harm [13]. Additionally, the unpredictable and emotionally demanding work environment exposes them to a combination of physical, psychosocial, and psychological risks, contributing to a high prevalence of MSDs [14, 15].

The diverse range of work tasks, including repetitive activities such as lifting, moving, or changing positions, and standing for extended periods, along with improper postures, significantly contribute to the development of MSDs [16]. Emergency workers frequently engage in strenuous tasks such as lifting and moving patients, often in awkward or cramped positions [9]. Tasks requiring maximum speed such as moving, lifting, pushing, pulling, carrying, and transferring patients along with their attached equipment are key contributors to the development of MSDs [17].

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis (2024) revealed an overall prevalence of MSDs among emergency medical service workers at 52.56%. These findings highlight the urgent need to address and prevent MSDs to ensure the health and well-being of this essential workforce [8].

MSDs manifest with a range of signs and symptoms, including pain, physical discomfort, heaviness, fatigue, sensory disturbances, and joint limitations [13]. MSDs in emergency workers impose significant physical, psychological, and financial burdens [18]. Discomfort or psychological imbalance resulting from these conditions has been described as a state of moral distress. Moral distress arises when individuals are unable to take morally appropriate actions due to barriers such as time constraints, resistance from superiors, medical limitations, organizational policies, and ethical challenges [19]. Moral distress arise in contexts contexts involving patient care, managing organisational demands, and collaborating. Ethical challenges are addressed informally among personnel in emergency vehicles, at ambulance stations, outside working hours, and ocationally involve family or friends with a background in healthcare. Moral distress is recognised as a serious problem among nurses, physicians, and other healthcare professionals as it negatively affects their well-being [20].

Research also indicates a correlation between moral distress and various dimensions of burnout, emphasizing the need to address these challenges to support the well-being of emergency workers [21]. Moral distress presents a significant challenge that impacts the physical and mental health of prehospital emergency workers [22]. Prehospital emergency workers frequently encounter a range of ethical challenges in their roles [23], and these ethical dilemmas contribute to moral distress [24]. The results of a systematic review and meta-analysis by Tahernejad et al. (2024) indicate that both physical and psychosocial risk factors contribute to the development of MSDs within this occupational group. Maintaining a healthy musculoskeletal system is essential for optimal performance and preventing declines in productivity among emergency medical workers [8]. Additionally, another meta-analysis revealed that, despite the significant burden of MSDs in Iran, particularly low back and neck pain, there is currently no comprehensive health policy in place to address these issues. This gap highlights the urgent need for targeted interventions to mitigate the impact of MSDs [4].

Ergonomic risk factors encompass physical such as improper posture, lifting and carrying heavy loads, and performing repetitive movements, as well as psychological, organizational, and individual factors [25]. Psychological factors in the workplaces can negatively impact ergonomic disorders. Khandan et al. reported that psychological and psychosocial issues are often neglected in Iran [26]. While physical demands and requirements are recognized as work-related risk factors for the musculoskeletal system, the bio-psycho-social model is of higher priority [27]. Workplace factors, including physical labor, repetitive tasks, and poor postures, contribute to the development of MSDs. Psychosocial factors are equally important, and in some cases, even more significant than physical agents [28].

Also, moral distress among prehospital emergency personnel has not been adequately addressed, and previous studies have called for further research on this issue within emergency medical service personnel [29, 30]. Support and competence in managing ethical challenges benefit not only prehospital emergency personnel but also patients, their families, external colleagues, and the overall prehospital service organization [24]. Consequently, it is essential to explore the impact of moral distress on MSDs among prehospital emergency personnel.

In conclusion, several sources have consistently highlighted the role of demographic factors in moral harms among health workers [31]. Additionally, multiple studies have confirmed the impact of demographic factors and inappropriate working conditions on musculoskeletal disorders [32, 33]. Research indicates that psychological and emotional stress is a significant factor contributing to the occurrence of musculoskeletal disorders [33, 34]. Based on these findings, the researchers of this study have designed and examined the conceptual model. The researchers aimed to confirm the following hypotheses (1) Moral distress affects MSDs. (2) Demographic information impacts MSDs. (3) Demographic information influences moral distress. Since no study has been conducted on the prehospital emergency system of Semnan to date, this research was designed to evaluate the impact of moral distress on musculoskeletal disorders among prehospital emergency personnel at Semnan University of Medical Sciences in 2024.

Study method

Study type

This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024.

Participants

The research population included all prehospital emergency staff of Semnan University of Medical Sciences (n total = 125) from the cities of Semnan (n = 35), Damghan (n = 34), Garmsar (n = 14), Mehdishahr (n = 18), Sorkheh (n = 14), and Aradan (n = 10). Prehospital services at Semnan University of Medical Sciences are provided through 35 active emergency bases, covering a population of 485,081 people in the region. The average response time to reach a patient’s bedside in the city is seven minutes, while it is 12 min on the roads.

Sampling

The required sample size for this study was determined to be 120 individuals using GPOWER software, based on a medium effect size of 0.3, a type I error rate of 0.05, and a power of 80%. In total, data from 125 individuals were collected and analyzed. Sampling was conducted using a census approach, with 125 prehospital emergency personnel from various cities in Semnan participating in the study.

Data collection tools

The data collection tools comprised demographic, moral distress, and ergonomic questionnaires related to Nordic MSDs.

Demographic/Occupational questionnaire

The demographic questionnaire included the following variables age, weight, height, education level, marital status, number of children, smoking status, alcohol consumption, duration of physical activity, type of base, employment history, number of missions per shift, hours worked per month, second job, city of employment, place of residence, job satisfaction, medications used and other illnesses.

Moral distress scale-revised (MDS-R)

The original Moral Distress Scale (MDS) was developed by Corley, Elswick, Gorman, and Klor (2001) [35] and was subsequently reduced to 18 items through psychometric testing in Iran [36]. This questionnaire utilizes a five-point Likert scale to assess moral distress across two dimensions: severity and frequency. Each item’s scores for the severity and frequency dimensions range from 0 to 4. The frequency dimension is scored from “never” (0) to “very much” (4), while the intensity dimension is scored from “not at all” (0) to “very much” (4), yielding a combined range of 0 to 16. The overall moral distress score for each statement is calculated by multiplying the severity score by the frequency score for that statement. To obtain the total moral distress score, the item scores are summed, resulting in a range from 0 to 288, with higher scores indicating higher levels of moral distress. The combined scores of the two subscales (frequency and intensity) classify moral distress as low (0–96), moderate (97–192), or high (193–288) [36, 37]. The face and content validity of the questionnaire were confirmed [36], According to Otaghi, Arab, and Barzegari (2018), the face and content validity of the questionnaire were confirmed by expert professors. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, yielding an overall reliability of 0.75, indicating appropriate reliability for this questionnaire [36]. The tool has been translated into Persian by Soleimani et al. (2019), who reported a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.71 for the entire tool and coefficients of 0.853 to 0.685 for its subscales [38]. In this study, the reliability of the questionnaires was 0.88.

Method of implementation

Following ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Semnan University of Medical Sciences, permission was obtained for the prehospital emergency centers in the regions served by the university. The officials of these centers were first informed about the study’s purpose and general procedures. After this explanation, prehospital emergency staff were invited to participate in the study voluntarily. Those who expressed their willingness were provided with an informed consent form through the Porsline website. After reading and signing the written informed consent, the participants completed the data collection tools, including the demographic, moral distress, and MSDs questionnaires, on the Porsline website. The collected data were subsequently entered into SPSS version 22 for analysis.

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Semnan University of Medical Sciences, Semnan, Iran (IR.SEMUMS.REC.1402.277) on 21/12/1402. It was conducted in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki on Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects and adhered to the research regulations of Iran. Oral and written informed consent was collected from all prehospital emergency staff participants, and adhering to the principle of confidentiality regarding participant information with number code.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine the effect of moral distress on MSDs in prehospital emergency staff of Semnan University of Medical Sciences. The results showed that moral distress had no significant effect on MSDs. In the study by Sahraei et al. (2019) conducted in Hamedan, although there was an increase in MSDs with rising stress scores, no statistically significant relationship was found between these two variables (P = 0.061) [17]. The observed p-value for the effect of moral distress on musculoskeletal disorders is very close to the significance level of 0.05. Considering a higher significance level (for example, 0.1), it may be possible to accept the effect of moral distress on musculoskeletal disorders. Therefore, further research is needed to explore the relationship between these two variables, particularly through longitudinal studies or with larger sample sizes.

Our results revealed that 52.8% of prehospital emergency staff experienced pain in the buttocks and lower back most common pain complaints. Complaints of pain in the past years included 44% for neck pain and 42.4% for buttocks and lower back pain. At the time of the study, the highest reported pain sensation was in the neck (73.6%), followed by the buttocks and lower back (36%) (Table 3). Sahebi et al. (2022) found a prevalence of back pain in emergency workers of 50.3%. Furthermore [48], Moskel et al. (2021) noted a significant prevalence of pain among German prehospital emergency workers, 59% reporting various types of pain [49]. Additionally, a study by Shirzad et al. (2023) in Guilan corroborated the high prevalence of low back pain among prehospital emergency workers [5]. Work-related MSDs symptoms vary depending on the ward, hospital, and even the country in which healthcare professionals work [50, 51].

The results of this study revealed that only age and work experience had a significant effect on MSDs among prehospital emergency staff (Table 2). Results from a systematic review indicated the most evident musculoskeletal risk factors reported in the literature are age and working as first responder [52]. According to the literature, from the age of 30 onwards, human functional capacity, particularly at the physical level, begins to decline, with a significantly accelerated rate of deterioration after the age of 45. Between the ages of 40 and 60, frequent patient-handling tasks involving heavy lifting further increase the risk of MSDs in this profession [53]. This finding is supported by the study conducted by Sahraei et al. (2019) [17]. Similarly, by Davis and Kotowski observed that the incidence of MSDs increased with years of work experience. This suggests that as work experience accumulates, the likelihood of developing MSD may rise, potentially due to a decline in physical strength over time [54].

Interestingly, the majority of prehospital emergency workers in this study reported low levels of moral distress. Results from Sedláček et al. (2025) showed that resource diversion, interpersonal toxicity, systemic handover failures, and role inconsistency lead to moral distress [55]. The intensity of moral distress varies across different healthcare settings [56]. Boulton and Farquharson (2024) reported that emergency department nurses typically spend less time with each patient, which may contribute to lower levels of moral distress. Low to moderate levels of moral distress have been reported among emergency nurses. In Semnan, due to gender restrictions, the presence of only male staff, and the small number of prehospital emergency personnel, professional relationships among them are strong, and they benefit from a supportive management atmosphere (psychological and psychosocial support). In fact, supportive culture is a part of the organizational culture. Supportive climate represents the level of a manager’s interest in safety issues as well as the degree of participation of employees in their workplace risk control. Therefore, despite the challenges they face, the close professional relationships and supportive environment may contribute to the low incidence of moral distress among prehospital emergency staff. A study suggests that integrating confidential ethics consultations, structured debriefings, and leadership-driven ethics training within EMS protocols can reduce barriers to ethical practice, alleviate distress, and enhance clinician resilience [57]. Therefore, despite the challenges they face, the close professional relationships and supportive environment may contribute to the low incidence of moral distress among prehospital emergency staff. A study suggests that integrating confidential ethics consultations, structured debriefings, and leadership-driven ethics training within EMS protocols can reduce barriers to ethical practice, alleviate distress, and enhance clinician resilience [55].

Conversely, research by Jafari et al. (2023) in multiple cities in Iran found that prehospital emergency workers experienced moderate levels of moral distress [58]. Additionally, Soleimani et al. (2022) reported significant moral distress among prehospital emergency staff in East Azerbaijan [59]. A culture of trust, respect, and open communication has been shown to reduce stress and moral distress, facilitating better management and reporting of pain and injury. Addressing moral distress is crucial for employee well-being, as it is an often-invisible challenge within the workplace [18]. Ensuring manageable workloads, access to a supportive work environment, and providing healthcare workers with conditions that enable them to follow best practices are essential for their well-being and performance [57].

In this study, age and work experience were identified as directly related to moral distress. However, some studies have suggested an inverse relationship between age and moral distress. The study by Babamohammadi et al. (2021) in Semnan found that female nurses under 30 years old, with less than 10 years of work experience and higher education levels, exhibited significantly higher levels of moral distress [60]. The study by Kızıltepe and Koç (2025) observed that nurses with 10–20 years of experience tend to have higher moral distress scores [61]. However, the relationship between ages, years of nursing experience, and moral distress remains inconsistent in the literature. Some studies suggest that more experienced nurses have higher clinical awareness, adapt more easily to different situations, and develop effective solutions, leading to lower moral distress scores [60, 62]. Other studies indicate that less experienced nurses experience higher levels of moral distress due to lower competence and skill. Additionally, long-term exposure to workplace challenges, burnout, and job dissatisfaction may exacerbate moral distress over time [63].

Increasing age and work experience, on the one hand, can increase the sensitivity of prehospital emergency personnel to moral distress, and on the other hand, it may increase the tolerance for distress in some individuals. Given the negative effects of moral distress on the physical and mental health of emergency medical service personnel and the quality of their care services, strategies to prevent or reduce it through the management of contributing factors are needed [64].

In the present study, all prehospital emergency staff in Iran were male, so moral distress was assessed only in males. The systematic review by Boulton and Farquharson (2024) reported an unclear effect of gender on moral distress levels [57]. Additionally, two studies reached conflicting conclusions on whether gender influences moral distress [65, 66]. A 2022 systematic review on moral distress in critical care nurses, whose sample was predominantly female, found no significant difference in moral distress between male and female respondents [67]. However, further research is needed to better understand how different genders experience moral distress.

Overall, these findings underscore the complexity of factors influencing both MSDs and moral distress among emergency personnel, highlighting the need for tailored interventions and support systems to promote the well-being of these critical workers.

In this study, important confounding variables such as psychological stress, physical activity, ergonomic factors, and organizational support were not thoroughly controlled. Since psychological and psychosocial issues are often neglected in developing countries such as Iran, the researchers anticipate that the findings can serve as a guideline for policymakers, as well as in the supportive and preventive domains for managing safety and health issues. It is recommended to improve working conditions and prevent these disorders by controlling and eliminating the risk factors associated with these areas through effective measures [26]. Any prevention programs should specifically focus on addressing the risk factors related to psychological and psychosocial aspects in the workplace [68].

This is the first study to examine the relationship between MSDs and moral distress in prehospital EMS workers, so caution should be exercised when generalizing its results. Given the gap in this field, it is hoped that future research will explore the relationship between physical aspects and psychological and psychosocial dimensions, as well as personal characteristics and organizational conditions.

Limitations

The exclusive focus on male prehospital emergency workers in Semnan limits the applicability of the findings to broader, gender-diverse populations and different geographical regions.

Additionally, the cross-sectional design of the study restricts the ability to draw causal inferences regarding the relationship between moral distress and musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs). To establish stronger conclusions, longitudinal or interventional studies are needed.

Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce biases, such as recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly in the context of cultural norms surrounding the expression of distress. This reliance on self-reported data can affect the accuracy of prevalence estimates. It is also important to acknowledge potential measurement biases, the restricted sample, and unmeasured confounders. Future research should include a larger number of prehospital emergency staff from various universities and incorporate qualitative approaches for a more comprehensive understanding of the issues at hand.

Conclusion

The findings indicate that nearly half of the prehospital emergency staff reported experiencing MSDs, particularly in the neck and back regions. To enhance the well-being of emergency medical service personnel, it is essential to prioritize periodic screening and implement strategies to alleviate work pressures, reducing both physical and psychological demands, as well as improving ergonomic standards in the workplace, should be addressed to decrease MSDs, especially among older individuals and those with extensive work experience. It is recommended that workplaces use educational interventions against the risk of MSDs. Such measures may help reduce the incidence of MSDs among these critical workers. This health promotion approach is crucial for human resource care.

The results also showed that participants exhibited low levels of moral distress, and this variable did not significantly influence the prevalence of MSDs. However, the use of adaptive coping behaviors and positive reinterpretation, along with support from colleagues, family, and friends, may contribute to lower moral distress among prehospital emergency staff. Additionally, effective practical and structural strategies, combined with managerial efforts to create a healthy and supportive work environment, play a crucial role in reducing moral distress. The use of qualitative and interventional research methods is recommended to identify the relationship between MSDs and factors associated with the occurrence of moral distress. In addition, it is recommended to investigate the internal, external, and environmental factors that contribute to MSDs in prehospital emergency personnel. It also seems that improving physical, chemical, biological, ergonomic, and psychological conditions is an essential step in improving the management and productivity of prehospital emergency personnel.
