Economic and Health Care Resource Use Burden of Systemic Sclerosis
Authors: Dinesh Khanna, Daniel E Furst, Justin W Li, Qian Meng, Yuan Yuan, Tamara Lesperance, Kenyatta Peoples, Farah Ali, Brian LaMoreaux, Stephanie D Taylor
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe the health care resource use (HCRU) and costs of patients with systemic sclerosis (SSc) prior to and after diagnosis.MethodsThis retrospective study used a claims data set (Merative MarketScan; 2015‐2019). Eligible patients with SSc were identified by diagnosis codes and required at least 24 months of enrollment without an SSc diagnosis before their first SSc claim and at least 12 months of enrollment thereafter. Total HCRU and costs were reported for three intervals: 2 years and 1 year before and 1 year after index diagnosis. A general population cohort without SSc was matched 1:1 to the SSC cohort on age and sex for comparison.ResultsEligibility criteria identified 902 patients with SSc (mean age: 54 years old; 85% female). Mean per‐member per year costs increased each year from $22,383 to $29,708 to $47,095, 2 years before, 1 year before, and 1 year after index diagnosis versus $10,232 to $9656 to $9714 in the general population cohort. Outpatient settings represented the largest proportion of cost 1 year after SSc diagnosis ($16,392), followed by prescription drugs ($10,692), physician office ($10,523), and inpatient ($9448) settings.ConclusionPatients with SSC accrued greater costs and required more services than a general population cohort. These elevated expenditures and HCRU were observed at least 2 years before an SSc diagnosis and increased over time, reflecting both the progressive, multisystem nature of SSc and potential challenges in diagnosis. These findings suggest that SSc poses a substantial burden on the US health care system and highlights the need for early diagnosis and effective therapies.

INTRODUCTION

Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a chronic, rare autoimmune disease affecting approximately 80,000 patients in the US (1, 2). SSc is characterized by progressive autoimmunity, inflammation, microvascular damage, collagen deposition, and subsequent fibrosis of the skin and internal organs, which contributes to substantial morbidity and premature death (3, 4, 5, 6, 7). Clinical presentations vary markedly, and this degree of heterogeneity in presentation complicates the recognition, diagnosis, and treatment of the disease (3, 8, 9). The presence of musculoskeletal, pulmonary, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular complications is common, and cutaneous manifestations are present in nearly all cases (3, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17). Many of the initial symptoms of SSc, including Raynaud's phenomenon, cough, and gastrointestinal symptoms (ie, gastroesophageal reflux disease, diarrhea, constipation, etc.), are present in the general population, which may delay the suspicion of SSc, and initial treatment may be directed toward addressing these specific symptoms (3, 8, 9). However, even after diagnosis of SSc, there are currently no disease‐modifying therapies available, leaving clinicians to carefully monitor and treat SSc‐associated symptoms and complications, especially organ involvement, including pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), interstitial lung disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and cardiac manifestations. Lung involvement is the most common cause of SSc‐related death (3, 6, 10, 12, 13, 15, 16, 18, 19).

Because of the substantial comorbidity burden associated with SSc and the lack of disease‐modifying therapies, treatment, and management of patients with SSc are associated with high direct and indirect health care costs that have been demonstrated in studies across multiple health care systems, including three retrospective data studies in the US, and in those with or without interstitial lung disease (20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27). These studies show that SSc is associated with substantial health care resource use (HCRU) and negatively impacts patient quality of life, including patients’ ability to work.

Notably, although the diagnostic journey to an SSc diagnosis is long and patients may exhibit symptoms requiring treatment prior to their formal diagnosis, prior studies of the burden of SSc have focused on after diagnosis time periods and have not quantified the prediagnosis burden associated with SSc. The objective of this analysis was to describe the HCRU and costs of patients with SSc prior to and immediately after diagnosis to provide a more complete assessment of the economic burden associated with SSc early in the patient journey and to examine the progressive cost of SSc over time.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data source

This study was a retrospective analysis of a large commercial medical and pharmacy claims dataset (Merative Marketscan Commercial Database). These longitudinal data provide a fully adjudicated view of inpatient, outpatient, and prescription drug use for approximately 25 million annual patients covered by 350 employer‐sponsored private insurance plans in the US. Available data include demographics, enrollment history, procedures and diagnoses rendered, and all associated expenditures, including allowed and paid costs. Analyzed claims data originated from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2019.

Study design

A case‐matched control design was used to quantify the HCRU of the SSc population relative to the general population without SSc. Patients with SSc were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD‐9 and ICD‐10) codes for SSc (710.1, 517.2, and/ or M34.x). Eligible patients with SSc were required to have received at least one inpatient claim with an SSc diagnosis code of interest or at least two outpatient or physician office (PO) SSc claims separated by a minimum of 30 days. Additionally, to assess incident cases, eligible patients with SSc were required to have greater than or equal to 24 months of continuous health plan enrollment without a SSc diagnosis before their first SSc claim (“index date”) and greater than or equal to 12 months of enrollment thereafter. Conversely, patients in the general population cohort without SSc were required to have no claims with a SSc diagnosis code during their continuous enrollment period. All included patients were required to be continuously enrolled in their health plan for at least 36 months. The general population cohort of patients were matched from the database on age, sex, and year of diagnosis at a 1:1 ratio to the patients in the SSc group, with no exclusion criteria beyond the absence of an SSc diagnosis. In the absence of a diagnosis for indexing, the index year for the general population cohort was determined based on the age at which the matched patient was diagnosed with SSc.

Outcomes

Baseline patient demographics and characteristics, including mean Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) values, were assessed for the SSc cohort for patients at the year of diagnosis (28). The CCI is a commonly used assessment tool adapted for use with administrative data (ie, ICD diagnosis codes and current procedural terminology codes) that uses 19 conditions to broadly assess patient health and predicts long‐term mortality (28). For this study, a modified form of the CCI that excludes SSc diagnoses and SSc‐related comorbidities was used (24). All services rendered to the SSc and general population groups were segmented into the mutually exclusive setting of care categories of inpatient admissions, outpatient services (subcategorized within the data base into procedure claims, surgeries, emergency department visits, and PO visits), home health agency and durable medical equipment use, and prescription drugs (subcategorized via National Drug Code drug class). The most frequent comorbidities were assessed using classifications from the Chronic Conditions Warehouse (29). The top five prescription drug categories by spend were assessed for both the SSc and general population groups. Although there are no medications approved for the treatment of SSc, medications for sequelae associated with SSc, such as immunomodulating therapies, and medications for PAH, were examined based on literature and the authors’ clinical experience. Rendered services were segmented by listed specialty of the rendering physician whenever possible.

The number of annual visits to a setting of care or physician specialty was evaluated as the number of distinct admissions for the inpatient setting and distinct dates of service with a claim for all outpatient settings. The category of PO included visits to rheumatology, dermatology, pulmonary disease, radiology, cardiology, nephrology, and primary care.

Costs for all health care services rendered to the SSc and general population groups were calculated in 2019 US dollars on a per‐member per year (PMPY) basis using all allowed costs and segmented into mutually exclusive segments by care setting for inpatient, outpatient, PO, and prescription drugs. Total costs and costs by care setting were reported as means for 3‐time intervals: 2 years and 1 year before and 1 year after index diagnosis.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarized by their mean ± SD and compared between the SSc and general population groups by Student's t‐test. Use, when defined categorically as the proportion with at least one stay/visit in the year, was compared by chi‐square test. For all analyses, results were considered statistically significant with a two‐tailed P value less than 0.05. All analyses were performed in SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc.).

RESULTS

Eligibility criteria resulted in identification of 902 patients for the SSc cohort (Figure 1). The mean age at SSc diagnosis was 54 years, and 85% of patients were female. Compared with the general population cohort, mean CCI scores were higher in the SSc cohort across time intervals: 2 years and 1 year before (0.84 vs. 0.39 and 1.13 vs. 0.43) and 1 year after (1.3 vs. 0.45) index (Table 1). Within the SSc cohort, the mean CCI score increased year over year (0.84 vs. 1.13 and 1.13 vs. 1.30; P < 0.01 for both) (Table 2). No statistically significant changes in the mean CCI were observed in the general population cohort. At baseline (ie, the first year after SSc diagnosis), based on definitions from the Chronic Condition Warehouse, nine out of the ten most frequent comorbid conditions observed in the SSc cohort were higher than in the general population cohort, including rheumatic conditions (43.7 vs. 15.0%) and their associated symptoms (fibromyalgia, chronic pain, and fatigue 26.4 vs. 9.5%); hypertension (43.0 vs. 31.9%); mental illnesses (anxiety disorders 20.8 vs. 12.0% and depressive disorders 19.4 vs. 10.4%); and chronic respiratory diseases (asthma 18.3 vs. 6.9% and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/bronchiectasis 13.6 vs. 6.0%) (all <0.01) (Table 1).

Baseline HCRU and cost

At baseline (ie, the first year following an SSc diagnosis), the mean number of all annualized health care services (including inpatient, outpatient, PO, and prescription drug) was higher among patients with SSc versus the general population cohort (50.7 vs. 23.1, P < 0.01), which was driven primarily by use of PO services (29.1 vs. 12.0; P < 0.01) (Table 3). Total mean cost was also substantially higher among patients with SSc ($47,095 vs. $9714; P < 0.01) (Table 3), with outpatient services comprising the largest proportion of total expenditures, followed by prescription drugs, PO visits, and inpatient costs. Across care settings, mean use and costs were higher among patients with SSC versus the general population (all <0.01) during all time periods assessed (Table 3, Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Trends in HCRU and costs of patients with SSc

Within the population of patients with SSc, total mean PMPY HCRU increased each year (from 32.77 to 38.65 to 50.67 services; P < 0.01 for first interval; P < 0.01 for second interval) (Table 2), as did mean PMPY cost (from $22,384 to $29,707 to $47,095; P < 0.01 for first interval; P < 0.01 for second interval) (Table 2) 2 years before, 1 year before, and 1 year after index SSc diagnosis, respectively. To investigate these increases, we specifically examined the SSc cohort. In the SSc cohort, the largest relative increase in HCRU was observed in the inpatient site of care, which was associated with a 2.5‐fold increase in cost (Table 2). Mean PMPY prescription drug cost also increased 2.3‐fold (Table 2). At their index diagnosis, more than half (62%) of newly diagnosed patients with SSc received a steroid prescription and nearly half received a prescription for an immunosuppressant (49%) or a proton pump inhibitor (46%); 31% of patients received a calcium channel blocker prescription within 1 year after index diagnosis (Table 4). Use of these medications also increased substantially in the preceding year before peaking 1 year after diagnosis.

DISCUSSION

The central findings of the present study are threefold. First, patients with SSc exhibited greater HCRU and incurred substantially greater cost across settings and categories of care as compared with an adjusted general population. Second, elevated HCRU and expenditures are observed from at least 2 years before to 1 year after a confirmed SSc diagnosis with both increasing over time. Finally, patients with SSc accrued substantial drug costs for therapies used to quell or manage emerging sequalae of the underlying disease, such as skin fibrosis, PAH, gastrointestinal complications, and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis. Together, these findings suggest that patients with SSc pose a substantial burden on the US health care system and highlight the need for early diagnosis and effective therapies.

The health care resource and economic burdens of SSc observed in the current study are consistent with the available literature, which also shows substantially higher costs in the first post‐SSc diagnosis year versus adjusted general population controls (24, 26, 30). Notably, compared with the Zhou et al 2019 study in SSc with and without interstitial lung disease, the present study reports a higher mean cost in the first year post‐index for both the SSc and the control cohort (SSc, $47,095 vs. $18,069; Control, $9714 vs. $5250) (24). These differences could stem from multiple factors, including different time periods and differences in the identified populations captured in the data sets. The present study also used allowed costs, but it is unclear if prior studies used allowed or paid costs. Differences in the costs may also reflect differences in baseline severity, as suggested by the mean CCI values (1.13 vs. 0.7 before diagnosis), despite having the same mean CCI values in the first post‐index year (1.3 vs. 1.3). Notably, the higher costs reported here are similar to the annual costs reported by Fischer et al for a 5‐year period for SSc with PAH (range from $44,454 to $63,320) (22). Although differential SSc drug costs for medications, such as nintedanib (US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved in September 2019) or tocilizumab (FDA‐approved in March 2021), might have been considered, the data accrual dates for this study are only through December 2019, suggesting the costs of these two expensive drugs would not likely affect the costs in our study (12).

Beyond the absolute or relative costs of SSc, the existing retrospective studies of SSc, including the present study, all demonstrate increased HCRU across patients with SSc, in both inpatient and outpatient settings. The highest costs in the present study were observed in the ambulatory setting and support prior studies indicating greater costs for patients with SSc versus patients without SSc in multiple studies (24, 26, 30). Further research is required to understand the diagnoses driving these ambulatory care episodes. Notably, these studies of the economic burden of SSc all report comparisons to general population controls, and future studies could also provide comparisons of patients with SSc to patients with similar autoimmune diseases to further define the economic burden of illness of SSc.

In contrast to prior studies of the economic burden of SSc, the current study demonstrates that these marked differences are present and increase in the years preceding diagnosis, a dynamic that likely reflects both the progressive, multisystem nature of SSc and the potential challenges obtaining a timely diagnosis. In the 2 years prior to their SSc diagnosis, patients with SSc showed increased morbidity, HCRU, and costs versus the matched general population. Given that SSc is typically a diagnosis of exclusion, this increased burden may actually be related to undiagnosed SSc. Accordingly, patients with SSc show high levels of steroid and immunosuppressant use in this time period, which may reflect suspicion of an autoimmune etiology for symptoms or misdiagnosis with a non‐SSc disorder. Future claims‐based studies may attempt to detect and define these non‐SSc autoimmune diagnoses; alternatively, a medical chart audit could reveal addition information regarding diagnoses by physicians.

The present study highlights a substantial increase in cost in the first post‐index year, but because the analysis focused on costs before formal diagnosis with SSc, it does not capture the cost trajectory beyond that year. Two prior studies have reported little to no increase in total direct costs associated with SSc populations, excluding incident SSc with interstitial lung disease or PAH, but the evolution of treatment costs over time after diagnosis remain an area of interest, particularly in light of the nondisease modifying therapies recently approved for the treatment of SSc with interstitial lung disease (22, 25). The investigation of a SSc over a longer time period—both before and after diagnosis—would be of great interest to capture a broader picture of the burden of disease, but such analyses will likely require data sets with greater longitudinal capture of patients than MarketScan, which is adversely affected by churn in the commercial health insurance market in the US. A greater longitudinal view would also contribute to further analysis of clinical features of SSc and potentially provide insight into the differences observed in prediagnosis year CCI scores between patients with SSc and the matched general population.

As is the case for all retrospective observational studies leveraging administrative claims, this study has some limitations. Misclassification of patients’ diagnosis status is possible due to incorrect or undocumented coding, and the nature of claims data bases does not allow for corroboration via secondary methodologies (such as a chart audit). However, the other recent studies have also used ICD‐10 coding and similar algorithms for the identification of patients with SSc (23, 24, 31). Additionally, the index SSc diagnosis was designated based on two prior years of continuous enrollment without an SSc diagnosis, and although it is considered unlikely that a diagnosed case of SSc would not receive coding in the two pre‐index years, it remains possible. This analysis also focused on direct health care costs and does not account for any health care expenditures or services paid by the beneficiary out‐of‐pocket or any indirect costs, such as time off work or loss of income from family, etc. Relatedly, allowed costs were assessed instead of paid costs in part to maintain consistent costs in the absence of complete out‐of‐pocket cost capture. This analysis also does not include the population of patients with Medicaid or Medicare as their primary payer, which may represent a significant portion of underinsured and older SSc populations. As such, the results from this study of a primarily commercially insured patients may not be generalizable to all payers. Patients with SSc were also matched to the general population based on age and sex for the analyses, which may contribute to differences between the matched cohorts. Additional matching parameters, such as CCI score, could be introduced, but could result in sample size reductions and/or obscuring of the pleiotropic effects of SSc that could be captured in a broad measure of comorbidity such as the CCI.

Overall, patients with SSc were associated with greater costs and HCRU compared with a general population. The observed increased costs versus the general population were observed 2 years prior to the SSc diagnosis, and the cost of care increased over time, including a sharp increase upon formal diagnosis. The high cost and prediagnosis increase in cost likely reflect both the progressive, multisystem nature of SSc and potential challenges in diagnosis. These findings suggest that SSc poses a substantial burden on the US health care system and highlights the need for early diagnosis and effective therapies.
