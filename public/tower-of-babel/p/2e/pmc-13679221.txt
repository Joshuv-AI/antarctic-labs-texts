Efficacy of injectable platelet-rich fibrin and microneedling for thin gingival phenotype: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Aaron Nedungadi, Sharath Shetty, Anita Kulloli, Santosh Martande, Magesh Kumar, Saumya Singh, Poornima Ramamurthy, Dileep Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThin gingival phenotypes, characterized by a reduced gingival thickness (GT), are particularly susceptible to recession and inflammation. Injectable platelet-rich fibrin (i-PRF) and microneedling (MN) are emerging minimally invasive approaches for enhancing gingival thickness and improving periodontal health. To evaluate the efficacy of i-PRF and MN combination compared to i-PRF or MN monotherapy in the treatment of thin gingival phenotype.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across five databases with no language restrictions. Studies were selected based on predefined selection criteria that assessed primary outcomes (Gingival Thickness - GT, Width of Keratinised Tissue - KTW). Four split-mouth, prospective randomized trials involving 116 participants were included. Data from these studies were extracted individually by two reviewers, with a risk of bias assessment performed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. A meta-analysis was executed using RevMan Version 5.1.ResultsOf the 164 studies initially identified, four studies were eligible for inclusion and three for the meta-analysis. The combined use of i-PRF and MN showed a significant increase in GT, particularly within the first three months, compared to i-PRF alone. However, no significant differences were found for KTW across groups. The results for secondary outcomes, including clinical attachment level (CAL), probing depth (PD), and plaque index (PI) showed significant improvements, with i-PRF + MN leading to enhanced patient comfort and aesthetic satisfaction. The certainty evidence was estimated as low.Conclusioni-PRF combined with MN may provide an adjunctive benefit for increasing GT in thin periodontal phenotypes, particularly within the first three months. These results should be interpreted with considerable caution given the small number of studies included and the high risk of bias. While a modest improvement in GT was observed with the combination approach, the impact on KTW remains inconclusive.

Introduction

In modern dentistry, achieving a harmonious and aesthetically pleasing smile goes beyond the alignment and appearance of teeth; it also depends on the surrounding gingival tissue, often termed as gingival or pink aesthetics [1]. One of the critical but often overlooked factors influencing the stability of periodontal and restorative treatment outcomes is the gingival biotype (GB). Different biotypes respond uniquely to inflammation, trauma, and surgical or restorative interventions, making their identification essential for treatment planning [2]. The periodontal phenotype is determined by the gingival biotype and the thickness of the buccal bone. Ochsenbein and Ross first identified two main gingival morphologies: flat and thick gingiva, or scalloped and thin gingiva [3]. This was later refined into periodontal biotype, categorizing it as thick flat biotype with gingival thickness (GT) of ≥ 2 mm and thin scalloped biotype with a GT of ≤ 1.5 mm. Egreja et al. recently defined a GT over 1 mm as thick and under 1 mm as thin [4]. Thick biotypes are characterized by a wider keratinized zone, dense fibrotic tissue, and greater resistance to recession and trauma, though they are more prone to pocket formation. In contrast, thin biotypes exhibit a scalloped contour, narrower keratinized tissue, and higher susceptibility to recession and inflammation. As research advances, it is now recognized that different biotypes behave distinctively under similar clinical conditions, which can influence treatment considerations [2, 5].

Gingival or marginal tissue recession presents clinically as functional and aesthetic concern and increasing GT is a key preventive and therapeutic goal. Various surgical and non-surgical procedures have been suggested for gingival augmentation, including root coverage procedures and tissue grafting [6]. However, recent advances in regenerative dentistry favor autologous approaches, such as the usage of platelet concentrates [7].

Injectable platelet-rich fibrin (i-PRF), a second-generation autologous blood derivative, has gained attention due to its ease of preparation, cost-efficiency, and its potential in gradually release growth factors like insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), transforming growth factor- beta (TGF-β), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) [8]. Unlike traditional PRF, i-PRF retains its fluid state for 15–20 min before clotting, allowing for its injection into the tissue [9]. It contains high concentrations of leukocytes, platelets, and regenerative growth factors, promoting wound healing, collagen production, and neovascularization [2, 10, 11].

Another minimally invasive technique, microneedling (MN), also known as percutaneous collagen induction (PCI) is known to stimulate collagen formation by inducing controlled micro-injuries in the tissue [12, 13]. This process triggers a cascade of healing responses, with the release of various growth factors, leading to neocollagenesis, fibroblast activation, and neo angiogenesis [14]. Based on these properties, the combination of i-PRF and MN may potentially provide a synergistic effect in enhancing gingival thickness.

This systematic review aims to assess the effectiveness of i-PRF combined with MN versus either i-PRF or MN alone in the treatment of thin gingival phenotype. If proven effective, this approach could serve as an alternative to traditional surgical techniques, offering a less invasive solution for improving periodontal health.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) and in accordance with a protocol registered earlier in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) database (CRD42023491214) [15].

The focused PICO question was: In individuals with thin gingival phenotypes, does the use of injectable platelet-rich fibrin with microneedling improve gingival thickness more effectively compared to i-PRF or microneedling alone?

The population of interest comprised individuals presenting with thin gingival phenotypes. The intervention evaluated was the combined application of injectable platelet-rich fibrin (i-PRF) with microneedling. This was compared against either i-PRF or microneedling alone. Primary outcomes assessed included gingival thickness (GT) and keratinized tissue width (KTW) while secondary outcomes evaluated were plaque index (PI), probing depth (PD), recession depth (RD), bleeding on probing (BOP), and clinical attachment level (CAL).

Eligibility criteria were defined as follows: Inclusion criteria - patients aged 18 years or older with a thin gingival phenotype, parallel-group and split-mouth randomized controlled trials (RCTs), studies published in English, and full-text articles. Exclusion criteria included non-randomized controlled trials, retrospective and prospective studies, case reports, reviews, case series, editorials, abstracts, animal studies, and in vitro studies. Studies were also excluded if they did not measure or report any of the specified clinical outcomes, compared treatments other than i-PRF or microneedling, involved peri-implant tissues, lacked a control group, or were published in languages other than English.

A literature search was conducted across five databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library (Trials), LILACS, and Google Scholar, with no language restriction. The last search was completed in November 2025. The search focused on studies related to i-PRF and microneedling for individuals with thin gingival phenotypes. Forward (citations) and backward (references) searches of pertinent articles were also performed to capture supplementary studies not identified in the initial search. All relevant peer-reviewed journals, clinical trials, and grey literature were included in the literature search. Detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Table 1.

The methodological quality of the studies was independently assessed by two reviewers (SM and MK) using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for RCTs, with analysis in RevMan 5.1 and visualization via robvis [16, 17] Disagreements were resolved by a third reviewer (SS) to reach consensus.

Meta-analysis was performed using Review Manager (RevMan) Version 5.1. A random effects model was used due to expected heterogeneity across studies. Effect sizes were calculated for all continuous outcomes. Although all four included trials used a split-mouth design, the two sites analyzed per patient were not always strictly contralateral (e.g. multiple mandibular anterior, canine, or premolar tooth-sites per patient per arm in three of the four studies), each representing an independent site with its own local anatomy, socket morphology, and healing dynamics. Accordingly, the tooth/site rather than the patient was treated as the unit of analysis, and the two sites per patient were analyzed as independent (parallel group) samples rather than as paired, correlated measurements, consistent with the design and reporting of the primary studies. Forest plots were generated to visually present the effect measures. For primary outcomes (GT and KTW), the overall effect size and heterogeneity were determined. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The I² statistic was used to quantify the degree of heterogeneity, with I² values interpreted as follows: 0–25% as low, 26–50% as moderate, and 51–100% as high heterogeneity.

Results

A total of 164 records were identified from five databases as shown in Fig. 1. After removing 58 duplicate records, 106 articles were screened based on their titles and abstracts. Following the screening, 97 records were excluded for the following reasons: review articles (n = 55), case reports and case series (n = 10), irrelevance to the study (n = 18), animal studies (n = 8) and titles related to other skin conditions (n = 6). Full text was retrieved for 9 records and assessed for eligibility. Among them, 5 articles were excluded due to the following reasons: the comparator group being different (n = 4) or the study focusing on i-PRF alone (n = 1). Ultimately, four studies were included in the review, with three of these studies being included in the meta-analysis.

The included studies defined thin gingival phenotype by reduced gingival thickness of ≤ 0.8 mm in the mandibular anterior region [18, 19, 21], while Chetana et al. [20] used a broader threshold of < 1.5 mm in canine and premolar sites.

Qualitative analysis

Four split-mouth, prospective randomized, studies were included in this review, with one conducted in Turkiye [18] and the rest in India. The sample sizes ranged from 15 to 36 patients, with an aggregate of 116 patients included. As all four studies used a split-mouth design, the number of treatment sites analyzed per patient varied across studies (e.g. two sites per patient in Adhikary et al., versus multiple mandibular anterior, canine, or premolar tooth-sites per patient per arm in the other three studies), so the sample sizes reported in Table 2 refer specifically to the number of patients enrolled in each study, and should not equated with the number of treatment sites analyzed. The mean age and gender distribution varied across studies, with one study failing to report age [18] and gender distribution [19]. All included studies used injectable platelet-rich fibrin (i-PRF) combined with microneedling (MN) as intervention, with the control group receiving i-PRF or microneedling alone. The primary outcomes assessed were GT and KTW, with additional parameters like PI, PD, RD, BOP, and CAL being evaluated in some studies. All the encompassed studies had follow-up periods of up to 6 months, except for one study, which had a follow-up period of 3 months [21]. (Tables 2 and 3).

Quantitative analysis

Gingival thickness (GT)

At the 1-month follow-up, the pooled analysis of 2 studies [18, 21] indicated no significant difference between i-PRF + MN and i-PRF alone with a mean difference of 0.02 mm (p = 0.48). The heterogeneity was low (I² = 0%), suggesting that the results were consistent across the studies. i-PRF + MN showed a significant increase in GT of 0.5 mm at the 3-month follow-up, as revealed by the pooled analysis of three studies [18, 19, 21] compared to i-PRF alone (p = 0.01). i-PRF + MN demonstrated no significant increase in GT (0.10 mm) at the 6-month follow-up, as indicated by the pooled results from two studies [18, 19] when compared to i-PRF alone (p = 0.14). The heterogeneity was high (I² = 82%), indicating variability in the studies, and the evidence strength was weak. This high heterogeneity at 6 months was largely driven by Ozsagir et al. [18], whose i-PRF + MN group showed a comparatively smaller incremental change in GT between 3 and 6 months than the corresponding change reported by Adhikary et al. [19]; furthermore, differences in the microneedling device and needle depth used, variation in patient age and baseline gingival thickness, and differences in the precision of the GT measurement technique (digital caliper versus transgingival probing with a stopper) between the studies.

i-PRF + MN exhibited a significant difference in the total pooled analysis across all subgroups, demonstrating a marked increase in gingival thickness compared to i-PRF alone. (MD = 0.05; 95% CI: 0.02, 0.08; p = 0.003). The heterogeneity was low (I² = 10%), suggesting a high degree of consistency across the studies. The Test for Subgroup Differences indicated significant variation in the treatment effects across the subgroups, with a Z-value of 2.22 (p = 0.03), suggesting that the treatment’s effectiveness differed at various time points (1, 3, and 6 months). (Fig. 4).

Keratinized tissue width (KTW)

At 3 months, the pooled analysis of 2 studies [18, 19] showed a small increase in KTW by 0.04 mm for i-PRF + MN in comparison to i-PRF alone, and the result was not statistically significant (95% CI: -0.29, 0.37; p = 0.82). The heterogeneity was low (I² = 0%), indicating consistency across the studies. However, the wide confidence intervals suggest weak evidence. Similarly, at 6 months, no statistically significant difference in KTW was observed between i- PRF + MN and i-PRF alone with a 0.04 mm increase in KTW for i-PRF + MN. (95% CI: -0.30, 0.37; p = 0.82). The heterogeneity was low (I² = 0%), reinforcing the consistency across the studies, but the evidence strength remained weak.

The overall pooled analysis shows no significant changes in keratinized tissue width among the two treatments (MD = 0.04; p = 0.75), with no heterogeneity (I² = 0%) and a strong consistency across the studies. The test for subgroup differences (p = 1.00) suggests no significant variation between the treatments at various time points (3, and 6 months). (Fig. 6).

Keratinized tissue width (KTW) pre-post intervention

For i-PRF + MN, the pooled MD of 0.43 mm (95% CI: -0.24, 1.10) showed a modest increase in KTW from baseline to 6 months post-intervention, but the result was insignificant (p = 0.21). The heterogeneity was high (I²=79%), indicating inconsistency amongst studies. For i-PRF alone, the pooled MD of 0.39 mm showed insignificant difference (p = 0.26), with high heterogeneity (I²=79%). The evidence strength was weak, as the lack of statistical significance and high variability reduced the overall certainty.

The total pooled analysis reveals a combined mean difference of 0.43 mm (p = 0.03), with heterogeneity high (I² = 65%), suggesting a significant improvement in keratinized tissue width for both treatments from baseline to 6 months post-intervention. The test for subgroup differences reveals no significant variation between the groups (p = 0.94). (Fig. 7).

GRADE assessment

The evidence quality was rated by means of “Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE)” approach. The overall certainty evidence was graded as Low for GT and KTW. (Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

For patients with thin periodontal phenotypes conventional surgical approaches such as free gingival grafts (FGG) and subepithelial connective tissue grafts (SCTG) remain the gold standard for increasing keratinized tissue width and gingival thickness, respectively. However, donor site morbidity, patient discomfort, and limited tissue availability have prompted interest in minimally invasive options. Hyaluronic acid (HA) injection has been proposed as a non-surgical method to augment soft tissue volume, with studies reporting modest gains in gingival thickness without autologous grafting [22]. Platelet concentrates, including PRF membranes and leukocyte-PRF (L-PRF), have also been used as adjuncts to surgical procedures for phenotype modification [23]. The choice among these alternatives depends on patient-specific factors, the extent of phenotype deficiency, clinical expertise, and the desired outcome.

This systematic review and meta-analysis compared the effectiveness of i-PRF combined with micro-needling against i-PRF or micro-needling alone on gingival thickness and keratinized tissue width in individuals with thin gingival phenotypes. The findings suggest that the combination approach provides a substantial improvement in gingival thickness compared to i-PRF or micro-needling alone. However, no significant differences were found in keratinized tissue width across the groups.

Microneedling, also known as percutaneous collagen induction therapy, is a minimally invasive procedure performed with devices such as dermarollers or dermapens for a range of skin conditions [24, 25] These instruments contain arrays of fine needles (typically 0.5–2.5 mm in length, 0.1 mm in diameter) that are rolled or stamped across the treatment area in multiple directions—horizontal, vertical, and oblique—until uniform pinpoint bleeding is achieved, which serves as the clinical endpoint [26]. Devices may be manual (drum-shaped dermaroller), motorized (dermapen with disposable cartridges), or combined with radiofrequency or LED therapy to enhance neocollagenesis and improve patient outcomes [27–29]. The controlled micro-injuries stimulate the wound-healing cascade, beginning with platelet and neutrophil activation and the release of growth factors such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), transforming growth factor (TGF β), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [30]. This cascade promotes fibroblast migration, collagen deposition, elastin synthesis, angiogenesis, and epidermal thickening [30]. Post MN, a fibronectin matrix can form within days, guiding collagen alignment and remodeling [30]. Additonally, MN can trigger potassium release locally that stimulates formation of collagenases due to altered resting potential of fibroblasts within the connective tissue [31].

Beyond MN, i-PRF has been explored in combination with various other approaches to enhance its clinical outcomes. Combining i-PRF with PRF membranes has been described for socket preservation and guided bone regeneration, leveraging the sustained growth factor release of solid PRF alongside the injectability of i-PRF. i-PRF has also been evaluated alongside hyaluronic acid for soft tissue augmentation, with the rationale that HA provides an immediate volumizing effect while i-PRF promotes longer-term regenerative changes [32]. Furthermore, combinations of i-PRF with collagen matrices and allografts have been explored in the context of recession coverage procedures [33]. Each of these strategies highlights the versatility of i-PRF as a regenerative adjunct although evidence specific to thin periodontal phenotype management remains limited.

In periodontal therapy, microneedling has been adapted as a minimally invasive adjunct to various soft tissue procedures for gingival phenotype modification. The procedure is performed on keratinized gingiva after topical anesthesia application, using a dermapen inserted vertically until bone contact, with uniform pinpoint bleeding as the expected endpoint. MN approach has been used recently as a modality for gingival depigmentation in combination with Vitamin C [34]. Another clinical study involving a combination therapy MN and Vitamin C injections into the deficient interdental papilla (IDP) reported a statistically significant improvement in IDP growth within the esthetic zone [35]. In a recent clinical trial comparing MN with acellular dermal matrix when used in conjunction with coronally advanced flap (CAF) for recession coverage reported that MN following CAF can be considered a potential graft-less approach for improving gingival thickness [36].

Based on our findings, the primary outcome, gingival thickness, demonstrated a significant improvement in the combination group at the 3-month follow-up, as evidenced by the pooled analysis of 3 studies that showed a moderate effect size [18, 19, 21]. While both combination approach and i-PRF alone led to significant improvements in gingival thickness post-intervention, the combination showed an added effect, amplifying the gingival tissue healing process. This suggests that MN contributes synergistically with i-PRF, resulting in more pronounced tissue healing and improved gingival volume, particularly in the short term. However, the results also highlighted variability at the 6-month follow-up, where no significant difference was found between combination and i-PRF alone. This inconsistency may be due to the high heterogeneity across the studies, potentially driven by differences in the intervention protocols, patient characteristics, or measurement methods. This suggests that while combination approach shows promising short-term effects, its long-term efficacy may need further investigation.

A plausible biological explanation for this early benefit followed by convergence at 6 months relates to the transient nature of i-PRF’s growth factor release: i-PRF and MN act largely by accelerating early wound healing and neocollagenesis, so their added effect is most apparent while active tissue remodeling is still underway (within the first 1–3 months). By 6 months, tissue maturation and remodeling in both the combination and i-PRF-alone groups may have largely plateaued, so that the biological “head start” conferred by MN is no longer reflected as a measurable difference in gingival thickness between groups.

Historically, the significance of keratinized attached gingival width around teeth and implants have been widely researched. In this review, no significant differences were observed between combination approach and i-PRF alone on keratinized gingiva width at both 3-month and 6-month follow-ups. The lack of statistically significant difference suggests that while both treatments may contribute to a modest increase in KTW, neither approach is significantly superior in enhancing keratinized tissue. The variability in results across studies, as indicated by the moderate to high further emphasizes the need for more standardized protocols in future research.

The secondary outcomes in one study, including Gingival Index (GI) and Plaque Index (PI), were evaluated in comparison to MN alone [20]. The results showed statistically significant reductions in PI and GI scores over time in both groups, indicating effective plaque control and improved gingival health. In another study comparing clinical periodontal indices significant improvements across multiple parameters over the 6- month study period were noted [18]. Specifically, the PI scores showed a significant reduction from 0.17 ± 0.05 at baseline to 0.12 ± 0.04 at the 6th month, indicating a significant decrease in plaque accumulation [18]. Probing Depth (PD) also declined from 2.04 ± 0.22 at baseline to 2.01 ± 0.21 at the 6th month (p<0.001), suggesting improved periodontal health and reduced inflammation. Interestingly, no significant changes were observed in Recession Depth (RD), which remained consistently low (0.04 ± 0.06) throughout the study. This may indicate that the intervention did not directly affect recession, a finding that could be attributed to the non-invasive nature of the treatment. Furthermore, BOP and CAL both showed statistically significant improvements (p< 0.001), with BOP decreasing from 7.9 ± 3.3 at baseline to 6.5 ± 2.5 in the 6th month, and CAL improving slightly from 2.07 ± 0.22 to 2.05 ± 0.21. Only one of the recent studies, evaluated patient outcomes showing a notable increase in GT and KTW after free gingival grafting, with no significant changes amongst the groups [2]. The combination group showed reduced discomfort and enhanced aesthetic satisfaction at the six-month follow-up. Both treatments improved GT and KTW, but combination approach offered additional benefits in terms of comfort and satisfaction.

These findings are broadly consistent with recent umbrella reviews of autologous platelet concentrates in dentistry. Acerra et al. [37], reported that PRF used adjunctively with open flap debridement or coronally advanced flap consistently improves periodontal soft-tissue parameters such as CAL and root coverage but does not consistently improve keratinized tissue/mucosa width - a dissociation that parallels our own finding of a significant gingival thickness benefit without a corresponding benefit in KTW. D’Ambrosio et al. [38], specifically addressed palatal soft-tissue donor-site healing, identified PRF as one of the more effective adjuncts for accelerating wound epithelialization and reducing postoperative pain, but found that its advantage over control was concentrated in the first one to two postoperative weeks and had largely converged with control by the third week. Although this earlier study examined a donor-site healing outcome rather than gingival thickness gain at a recipient site, the same general time-course pattern is evident in our own results, where the benefit of i-PRF + MN over i-PRF alone was significant at 3 months but no longer significant at 6 months.

The strengths of this review lie in its focus on minimally invasive techniques for enhancing gingival thickness in patients with thin phenotypes, a growing area of interest in periodontics. i-PRF and MN represent viable alternatives to conventional surgical techniques, providing a less invasive option with potential long-term benefits. The significant increase in gingival thickness observed with i-PRF + MN, particularly in the first three months, supports its clinical applicability in improving periodontal outcomes.

However, a notable limitation is the inclusion of only four studies, each with a relatively small sample size, with a maximum of 36 patients per study. This restricts the external validity and statistical power of the findings. Furthermore, the follow-up periods were limited to 3 to 6 months, thereby constraining the ability to evaluate the long-term efficacy and sustainability of the intervention. Regarding i-PRF injection protocols, a critical consideration is its limited working time, as i-PRF transitions from liquid to gel within approximately 15–20 min after centrifugation, necessitating prompt preparation and administration. Injection sites in the included studies predominantly targeted the buccal gingiva of the mandibular anterior region, with submucosal placement at multiple points to achieve uniform distribution. The injected volume per site varied across studies, contributing to heterogeneity in outcomes. Standardization of injection timing, volume, depth, and site distribution will be essential for comparability across future trials. Moreover, the number of i-PRF sessions was inconsistently reported and not analyzed in this review, representing a notable gap for future research.

All four included trials employed split-mouth RCT designs, yet this review conducted the meta-analysis using parallel-group methods. This conservative statistical handling may have widened standard errors by ignoring intra-patient correlation, potentially underestimating precision and masking significant results. Moreover, the meta-analysis was based on only three studies, with high heterogeneity at the six-month which limits the robustness of the pooled estimates. Risk of bias across the primary literature was also concerning.

A further source of clinical heterogeneity that could not be fully accounted for in this review is variability in i-PRF preparation protocols across the trials, including variations in centrifugation speed and duration, tube type, and time from blood draw to injection; because these parameters directly influence the concentration of leukocytes, platelets and growth factors within the final i-PRF product, unstandardized preparation protocols may partly explain the inconsistent effect sizes observed across studies and limit the clinical interpretation of the pooled findings [37, 38]. These limitations underscore the necessity for future studies with larger sample sizes, extended follow-up periods, standardized i-PRF preparation protocols, and more rigorous methodological designs to yield more comprehensive and reliable conclusions.

From a clinical translation perspective, the findings of this review suggest that in patients presenting with a thin gingival phenotype who are unwilling or medically unsuited to undergo autologous grafting procedures such as FGG or SCTG, a course of i-PRF combined with microneedling may be considered as a minimally invasive adjunct to increase gingival thickness, particularly where the treatment goal is short-term (up to 3-month) volumetric gain, or as a supportive measure alongside non-surgical periodontal therapy. Because the procedure avoids a second surgical site, it may be particularly suitable for medically compromised patients such as those on anti-thrombotic therapy wherein graft harvesting carries added risk. However, given the low certainty of the evidence, the absence of a demonstrated benefit for keratinized tissue width, and the uncertain effect beyond 6 months, i-PRF + MN should currently be regarded as an adjunctive or interim option rather than a replacement for conventional grafting where an increase in keratinized tissue width is the primary treatment objective. Clinicians considering this approach in practice should counsel patients that repeated sessions may be needed to sustain any gain in gingival thickness, and that the current evidence base does not yet permit firm conclusions on the optimal number, timing, or interval of i-PRF + MN sessions.

Conclusion

Based on low-certainty evidence derived from only four split-mouth trials, most of which raised at least some concerns on risk-of-bias assessment, i-PRF combined with microneedling was associated with a statistically significant, short-term (up to 3-month) increase in gingival thickness compared with i-PRF alone; however, this between-group difference was no longer significant at 6 months, and no benefit was observed for keratinized tissue width at either time point. Given the small number of pooled studies, the high heterogeneity at 6 months, and the elevated risk of bias, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than as a basis for firm clinical recommendations; a demonstrable 3-month effect alone is not sufficient evidence to conclude that i-PRF + MN reliably improves patient outcomes, since the durability of this early gain, and its ultimate clinical significance, could not be established beyond 6 months in the available data. Adequately powered randomized trials with standardized i-PRF preparation and microneedling protocols, accounting explicitly for the paired nature of split-mouth data, and with follow-up extending well beyond 6 months, are needed before i-PRF + MN can be recommended as a reliable adjunct for gingival phenotype modification in clinical practice.
