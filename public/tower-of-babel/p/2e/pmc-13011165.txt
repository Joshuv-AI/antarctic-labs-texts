Expansion of preexisting cancer driver mutant clones is induced by the genotoxic carcinogen benzo[b]fluoranthene in MutaMouse lung
Authors: Jennifer B Faske, Binsheng Gong, Danielle P LeBlanc, Juergen Funk, Gu Zhou, Sabrina Kehm, Paul A White, Timothy Robison, Andreas Zeller, Carole L Yauk, Francesco Marchetti, Barbara L Parsons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClonal expansion (CE) of cells carrying cancer driver mutations (CDMs) is being developed as a biomarker of cancer risk. CE in lung of MutaMouse males treated with 0, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/kg/d benzo[b]fluoranthene (B[b]F) by gavage for 90 and 180 d was assessed by CarcSeq. DNA regions encompassing mouse correlates of human hotspot CDMs were PCR amplified, attaching 18-base unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) during the PCR. Following library preparation and sequencing, UMI-defined read families were assembled to produce single-strand consensus sequences (SSCSs). Recovered mutants with mutant fractions (MFs) ≥10−4 were stratified based on their occurrence in lung-specific or nonlung driver sequences and CE was assessed on a per mouse basis as median absolute deviation in mutant fraction (MAD). A significant, dose-dependent increase in MAD was observed for lung-specific MFs after 180 d of B[b]F treatment, a duration that did not cause a significant increase in lung lesions. Dose- and treatment duration-related increases in MF were observed for Egfr, the mouse correlate of a known human lung tumor driver gene. MF and mutation counts were significantly decreased in response to longer treatment duration for some nonlung drivers, suggesting negative selection. Importantly, the normalized trinucleotide mutation spectrum derived from CDMs reflects amplification of preexisting spontaneous mutations, distinct from those induced by B[b]F mutagenesis. These results show CarcSeq detects CE of preexisting cancer driver gene mutants induced by the genotoxic carcinogen B[b]F and suggest a CE endpoint may be useful for evaluating cancer risk associated with tumor promoters or complete carcinogens.

Biomarkers that provide an early prediction of the carcinogenic impact of chronic treatment could speed and increase the throughput of carcinogenicity assessments, potentially improving the efficiency of developing safe products. Error-corrected sequencing (ECS) technologies (Marchetti et al. 2023a, 2023b), have significant potential for developing new biomarkers, and a regulatory path exists for incorporating validated biomarkers into carcinogenicity testing (ICH 2022). A critical feature of any biomarker is establishing its biological relevance, which in the case of carcinogenicity testing means showing that the biomarker is predictive of future cancer development (Corton et al. 2022). Clonal expansion (CE) of cancer driver mutant cells is an obligatory early step in the development of neoplasia (Grody et al. 2023; Parsons 2024). Thus, we hypothesize that molecular identification of CE, achieved using treatment durations too short to produce histopathologically detectable preneoplastic lesions, could be the basis of a shorter-term test for carcinogenicity.

CarcSeq is a single-strand consensus sequence (SSCS)-based ECS method to quantify cancer driver mutations (CDMs). In CarcSeq, 9 degenerate bases are incorporated at the 5′-ends of primers used to PCR amplify target sequences, thereby creating 18-base unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) for error correction by consensus. CarcSeq can quantify mutant fractions (MFs) ≥10−4. Mutant fraction (MF) is the number of mutant SSCSs at a position divided by the total number of SSCSs at that position. MF (equivalent to mutant allele fraction) is distinct from mutation frequency (the average mutation occurrence across many nucleotides without reference to a particular position) (Menon and Brash 2023). CarcSeq sensitivity appears to be appropriate for assessing CE because a sensitivity of 10−4 is likely insufficient to detect direct mutagenesis (a potential background signal) and large clonal expansions are expected to be more predictive of carcinogenicity than minor ones. CarcSeq MF measurements are reproducible and concordant with those obtained using alternative methods (Faske et al. 2023). Through examination of samples with known cancer risk factors (age, rodent strain, and sex) or known tumor response, high variation in MF (quantified as median absolute deviation in MF, MAD) was identified as a promising measure of CE and predictor of future cancer risk (Harris et al. 2020, 2021; McKim et al. 2021). A high MAD indicates greater variability in MFs within a sample, suggesting some mutant cells replicated more than others. In addition, CE of spontaneous mammary Pik3ca mutants correlated with rat mammary tumor response induced by the nongenotoxic carcinogen lorcaserin (Faske et al. 2023). These studies showed CarcSeq is a valuable approach for assessing endogenous and exogenous factors that promote carcinogenesis.

Herein, we report the first study applying CarcSeq to a genotoxic carcinogen, benzo[b]fluoranthene (B[b]F). B[b]F is a United States Environmental Protection Agency priority polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) that is a somatic mutagen (LaVole et al. 1982; Long et al. 2016; Schuster et al. 2024). B[b]F is considered a significant human health threat based on its prevalence in the environment (Zelinkova and Wenzl 2015). It is classified as a possible human carcinogen, i.e. IARC Group 2B, and is a known rat and mouse lung carcinogen (IARC 2010) (File S1, Table S1).

Our study is part of an international collaboration designed to investigate the impact of B[b]F dose and treatment duration on multiple genetic toxicology and genomic endpoints (e.g. micronucleus, Pig-a mutations, lacZ transgene mutants, and mutations). Using TwinStrand’s Duplex Sequencing (DS) mouse mutagenesis panel, work from this collaboration showed the mutational spectrum induced by B[b]F is dominated by C: G > A: T mutations, followed by C: G > T: A and C: G > G: C mutations (Schuster et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024). B[b]F is a relatively potent aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) agonist in human and mouse (Machala et al. 2001; Vondráček et al. 2017; Goedtke et al. 2021). Thus, B[b]F is well-suited mouse lung carcinogen to use in addressing our study’s primary objective: Generating additional data to validate CarcSeq-based assessment of the MAD biomarker for future cancer risk.

CarcSeq was performed on lung DNA from mice treated with 0, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/kg B[b]F for 90 and 180 d. Histopathological analysis was performed on lung tissue from mice treated with 0, 1.56, 3.125, 6.25,12.5, and 25 mg/kg B[b]F for 180 d. The results show that CarcSeq detected significant B[b]F-induced CE after 180 d but not after 90 d of treatment. Three important conclusions drawn from this study are: (i) CE was the superior endpoint relative to histopathology, the gold standard for standard toxicology studies, (ii) the mutation spectrum of recovered CDMs is consistent with B[b]F having induced CE of preexisting CDMs, and (iii) CarcSeq measurement of CDM MF provides a useful biomarker of CE.

Materials and methods

Lung DNA isolation and first-round PCR

MutaMouse males, 13 to 14 wk old at the beginning of the study, were treated via oral gavage with olive oil (vehicle) or 6.25, 12.5, or 25 mg B[b]F/kg/d (CAS No. 205-99-2) in olive oil for 90 and 180 d at Health Canada with approval from the Health Canada Ottawa Animal Care Committee. Dose selection was based on a separate, 2-fold dose escalation range finding study that identified and excluded doses that caused high levels of morbidity or toxicity (e.g. body weight loss greater than 20%) (Stewart et al. 2025). The control (0 mg/kg) and 3 top doses (6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/kg) were selected for CarcSeq analysis based on the expectation that CE of CDMs would be detected most easily at high doses. Lung tissues of mice (n = 6 per dose) were shipped on dry ice to the National Center for Toxicological Research (NCTR), where DNA was isolated from ∼10 mg of tissue/mouse using a DNA Extraction Kit (No. 200600, Agilent Technologies, La Jolla, CA).

Fifteen amplicons were synthesized individually by PCR, using 0.5 μg of mouse lung DNA (∼1.6×105 genome copies) per amplicon PCR. The primer sequences and concentrations used to amplify each target sequence are provided in File S1, Table S2. PCR primers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, Coralville, IA) and synthesized with 9 bases of degenerate 5′ sequence. In this way, products from single DNA molecules were all labeled with an 18-base UMI. First-round PCRs (100 μl) were performed in 96-well plates using Eppendorf MasterCycler Pro Thermal Cyclers (Eppendorf North America, Enfield, CT). PCR conditions were: 1× PfuUltra Hotstart DNA Polymerase buffer, 200 μM of each deoxynucleoside triphosphate, and 5 units of PfuUltra High-fidelity DNA Polymerase (Agilent Technologies). Cycling conditions included heating for 2 min at 94°C, then 27 cycles of 1 min at 94°C, 2 min at 57°C, 1 min at 72°C, then 7 min at 72°C, followed by 4°C soak. PCR products were quantified using a Quant-iT dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA). Based on the Quant-iT measurement, PCR products were adjusted to a concentration of 100 pg/µl and analyzed using Agilent High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTapes and a TapeStation System, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, thereby determining the concentrations of the correct-sized products.

Library preparation and sequencing

Individual PCR products were pooled using the masses identified in File S1, Table S2. Pools of first-round PCR products were quantified using a Quant-iT dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (ThermoFisher); 10 ng of DNA from each amplicon pool was used for library preparation. Library preparation was performed using Illumina® TruSeq® ChIP Sample Preparation Kits (Illumina, San Diego, CA) as described in the manufacturer’s instructions. The gel-purified products were quantified using a Bio-Rad QX200 Droplet Digital PCR (ddPCR) System (Hercules, CA) and ddPCR Library Quantification Kits for Illumina TruSeq. Based on the ddPCR quantification, a fraction of each library containing 2.25×107 copies (approximating 1.5×106 copies of each amplicon) was incorporated into the final library PCR amplification step, which employed the following cycling conditions: 30 sec at 98°C, then 23 cycles of 10 sec at 98°C, 30 sec at 60°C, 30 sec at 72°C, then 5 min at 72°C, followed by 4°C soak.

The lung DNA libraries were quantified by Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA). Generally, four libraries with different indices were combined, PhiX (Illumina) DNA was added, and the sample was diluted and denatured as previously described (Faske et al. 2023). Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 500 using NSQ 500/550 Mid Output KT (300 CYS) flow cells, 151-cycle paired-end sequencing, and a 6-nucleotide index.

Error-correction, data filtering, and processing

Reads were deconvoluted by sample based on their index. Error-correction was performed using the Duplex-Seq-Pipeline version 2.1.4, based on Kennedy et al. (2014) (see https://github.com/Kennedy-Lab-UW/Duplex-Seq-Pipeline), but modified to allow for amplicon sequencing (i.e. includes steps that sort reads by length/amplicon, see File S1, Table S3). Reads were sorted into read families based on their 18 base UMIs. At least 2 family members and 90% identity at a given position were required to constitute an SSCS.

As part of running the error correction pipeline, the number of FASTQ files analyzed and the maximum number of read families analyzed (cap) were varied to standardize the SSCS recovery. The pipeline output (mutation/position, mutpos data created by a Python script, mut-position.py, from https://github.com/Kennedy-Lab-UW/Duplex-Sequencing/tree/master/Nat_Protocols_Version) for each amplicon was transferred to an Excel workbook, using separate workbooks for 90- and 180-d samples, and one worksheet per sample. The mutant SSCS numbers for two polymorphisms (chr2 7567902 G > A and chr6 145246710 A > G), with MFs of ∼0.5 or 1, were removed from the worksheets before further processing. Excel workbooks were used as input for an R script that filtered out:

Mutations not represented by at least 3 mutant SSCSs, to eliminate variation due to sampling errors (McKinzie et al. 2001; Blomquist et al. 2015; Menon and Brash 2023);

Mutations not represented by at least 3 mutant SSCSs, to eliminate variation due to sampling errors (McKinzie et al. 2001; Blomquist et al. 2015; Menon and Brash 2023);

mutations with MFs that did not demonstrate a coefficient of variation (COV) of at least 60% across the 24 samples in each dataset, to exclude invariant background mutations and retain those with biologically meaningful variability indicative of CE; and,

mutations with MFs that did not demonstrate a coefficient of variation (COV) of at least 60% across the 24 samples in each dataset, to exclude invariant background mutations and retain those with biologically meaningful variability indicative of CE; and,

mutations that had MFs <1×10−4, to eliminate mutations below the threshold of reliable detection (i.e. PCR errors) or that potentially represent mutagenesis rather than CE.

mutations that had MFs <1×10−4, to eliminate mutations below the threshold of reliable detection (i.e. PCR errors) or that potentially represent mutagenesis rather than CE.

The R script also normalized mutant recovery relative to the sample with the lowest SSCS recovery, to account for any remaining technical variation in number of SSCSs across samples and dose groups (Faske et al. 2023). The custom R script (CS-ecNGS-spreadsheet-processing-08-08-2023v3.Rmd, written by Page B. McKinzie, NCTR, see File S2.txt) reported the output of filtering in several useful forms (Excel worksheets reporting SSCS numbers, COV calculations, MF and mutant counts for each sample before and after normalization, searchable by amplicon, etc.). A graphic representation of the error correction pipeline inputs and outputs constituting the data processing and evaluation is provided in File S1, Fig. S1. In the analysis of CarcSeq reproducibility, sample 334 was analyzed in triplicate. Each 334 replicate was run through the error correction pipeline, then each was separately added to the Excel workbook containing all other 180-d samples and processed separately using the R script.

Measurement of MF and calculation of MAD

MF was calculated as the number of mutant SSCSs at a given position divided by the total number of SSCSs at that position. MAD was calculated on an individual mouse basis. Specifically, for each mouse, the median MFs for recovered lung-specific (LS) and nonlung-specific (NLS) mutations were calculated. Then, the absolute deviation between each measured MF and the median MF was determined for the LS and NLS mutations, separately, for each mouse. Finally, the median of the absolute deviations was determined, thereby deriving an LS and NLS MAD value for each mouse.

Simple mutation spectra and normalized trinucleotide mutation spectra determination

Mean MF and mean MF standard deviation were determined across dose groups for single base substitutions, insertions, and deletions. For all treatment groups, the fractions of all recovered mutations representing these types of mutations were calculated. To investigate the trinucleotide mutation spectra, the trinucleotide representation across both strands of the CarcSeq target sequences was determined for all target sequences in LS and NLS amplicons, separately, using an R script as previously described (Faske et al. 2023; Miranda and Revollo 2024). Six mutations occurred at a 3′ or 5′ amplicon terminus. In these cases, an extra base was added to the target sequence considered, so the correct trinucleotide context could be evaluated. A tab-delimited text file containing the chromosome, mutation position, reference base, alternate base, and sample ID was prepared for each sample. Groups of files (e.g. 90 d treatment, 0 mg/kg B[b]F dose) were used as input for an R script that incorporated the trinucleotide representation data and reported the frequency of recovered mutants with respect to mutation type and trinucleotide context, normalized with respect to the number of trinucleotide occurrences (Miranda and Revollo 2024). Mutation spectra were determined after samples were stratified by dose, treatment duration, and/or amplicon type (LS or NLS).

Histopathological examination

Lung tissues from mice treated at Health Canada with B[b]F at 0, 1.56, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/kg/d for 180 d (n = 7 or 8 per dose) and mice treated with a single dose of N-ethyl-N-nitrosourea (ENU, positive control) at 50 mg/kg (n = 3) then held for the same duration were sampled as previously described for oral studies (Kittel et al. 2004). After instillation and fixation for 24 to 48 h with 10% neutral buffered formalin, tissues were embedded in paraffin, and blocks were sent to F. Hoffmann-La Roche Ltd, Basel, Switzerland, where blocks were sectioned using a nominal thickness of 3 µm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Histopathological evaluation was performed by ordinary brightfield microscopy.

Statistical analysis

MAD and individual amplicon log10 MFs (analyzing each target gene separately) were evaluated for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The effects of dose on MAD and individual amplicon 180-d log10 MFs were tested for statistical significance by ordinary analysis of variance (ANOVA) when data were normally distributed and by Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA when not normally distributed. When a significant effect was observed, Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test was used to evaluate the significance of each treatment relative to the 0 mg/kg dose group, reporting an adjusted P-value corrected for multiple comparisons. In the gene-specific log10 MF analyses, all recovered mutations in each dose/treatment duration group were combined.

Dose- and treatment duration-associated effects on log10 MF and mutation count data were analyzed by ordinary two-way ANOVA, analyzing each target gene separately. For the log10 MF analysis, all recovered mutations in each dose/treatment duration group were combined. Two-way ANOVA on count data considered the number of mutations observed in the animals of each treatment group separately.

Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the significance of dose on the presence of histopathological lesions. Mouse weights were analyzed by ordinary one-way ANOVA. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA), with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

CarcSeq was used to analyze somatic mutations in 15 regions of 10 cancer driver genes (Apc, Braf, Egfr, Hras, Kras, Nfe2l2, Pik3ca, Setbp1, Stk11, and Trp53). The FASTQ files generated were archived in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive (accession PRJNA1254623) [Reviewer link: https://dataview.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/object/PRJNA1254623?reviewer=7fovo8t37ctbl4ve69g2dql3eu]. FASTQ files were input into an adapted Duplex-Seq-Pipeline v2.1.4 pipeline for error correction (see https://github.com/Kennedy-Lab-UW/Duplex-Seq-Pipeline/releases and File S1, Table S3) (Kennedy et al. 2014). MF can be biased by the number of SSCS analyzed, specifically with greater SSCS numbers yielding lower MFs (unpublished observations) (Myers and McKinzie 2023). To avoid this bias, the pipeline was run using different numbers of FASTQ files and SSCS number cap values (the default/no cap value is 1 million), with the goal of analyzing between ∼300,000 and ∼350,000 SSCSs at every sequenced position. The specific files analyzed, and the cap values used, are provided in File S3. The SSCS output from the analysis of DNA from mice treated for 90 or 180 d is depicted in Fig. 1. Each plotted value is the median SSCS number for an amplicon, considering all positions within the amplicon (see Files S4 and S5). Ultimately, the mutpos data generated for each of the 15 amplicons from the 48 samples (720 amplicons interrogated) is based on between 298,992 and 316,563 SSCSs. Linear regression between SSCS and MF values for each mutation confirmed this approach eliminated bias related to the numbers of SSCSs interrogated (File S1, Fig. S2).

Single-strand consensus sequence (SSCS) recovery. Numbers of SSCSs recovered from mice treated for 90 and 180 d are plotted in A and B, respectively.

The mutpos data were collected in Excel workbooks (provided in Files S4 and S5) and an R script (provided in File S2) was used to filter the mutation data to enrich for biologically meaningful CE signals. The R script applied 3 criteria to eliminate MFs: (i) likely impacted by sampling noise (McKinzie et al. 2001; Blomquist et al. 2015; Menon and Brash 2023), (ii) that could be technical artifacts, or (iii) might represent hotspots for mutagenesis, without CE (see Materials and methods, error-correction, data filtering, and processing). The script also randomly down-sampled mutants to normalize mutant recovery relative to the sample with the lowest SSCS recovery on a per amplicon basis. However, because the capping process had already standardized SSCS recovery, only a small fraction (0% to 0.1%) of mutants were randomly down-sampled (see Table 1). Postfiltering, 953 and 1026 mutations representing putative CE events were recovered in the 90- and 180-d samples, respectively. These counts are based on distinct mutation position and type but include multiple occurrences of the same mutation when observed in >1 mouse. One 180-d sample (333) had high MFs in 14 positions of several amplicons that all corresponded to rat reference bases. Based on this, the sample was judged to be contaminated with rat DNA and removed from all downstream analyses. File S1, Text Box S1 describes the steps taken to examine a subset of the remaining samples for potential rat and/or human cross-contamination. After removal of sample 333, the mutpos data from the remaining 23 samples were re-analyzed using the R script and 908 mutations were recovered (Table 1).

Mutation recovery and classification of LS and NLS drivers

CarcSeq panel amplicons include drivers for different tumor types, so not all CarcSeq amplicons are expected to be drivers for any single tissue/tumor type. Past analyses of CarcSeq mutations have included partitioning of tissue-specific and nontissue-specific driver mutations, as an approach to validate CE results (Harris et al. 2021; McKim et al. 2021). Classification of drivers has been based on the number of times a gene mutation has been reported in neoplasia for a given tumor/tissue type, as captured in COSMIC lists of top 20 drivers (Tate et al. 2019). According to COSMIC, larger numbers of mutation observations are more meaningful than mutation frequency, based on the idea that a small number of mutational observations could result in an artifactually high mutation frequency (COSMIC | Catalogue of Somatic Mutations in Cancer, Cancer Browser, help pages, Top 20 Genes). In the current study, we compared two approaches for stratification of CarcSeq amplicons/mutations as lung specific, namely COSMIC ranking and COSMIC mutation frequency. Using the COSMIC cancer browser, mutation ranking and frequency data for abnormal lung tissue, benign lung tumors, lung tumors that could be benign or malignant, and lung carcinomas were collected. The numbers of mutations reported for the different tissue/tumor types, using each classification approach, are provided in File S1, Table S4. Based on COSMIC ranking, mouse Egfr, Braf, Kras, Pik3ca, Stk11, and Trp53 were classified as LS, whereas Apc, Hras, Nfe2l2, and Setbp1 were classified as NLS. As an alternative approach, mouse Egfr, Kras, Pik3ca, Setbp1, Stk11, and Trp53 were classified as LS based on a mutation frequency of ≥5%, with Apc, Braf, Hras, and Nfe2l2 classified as NLS.

MF measurement and MAD analyses of CE

The reproducibility of CarcSeq MF measurement was evaluated by performing 3 independent analyses of sample 334 (treated for 180 d with 12.5 mg/kg B[b]F, arbitrarily selected), including all PCR, library preparation, sequencing, and bioinformatic steps. Across the 3 replicates, 111 mutations were recovered of which 51 (45.9%) were unique to a single replicate. Twenty-five mutations were recovered in 2 or 3 replicates (15 and 10 mutations, respectively) that in aggregate accounted for 60/111 (54.1%) of the total mutations. The average COV of the replicate measurements was 9.93% of the average MF (COV range 0.05% to 33.68%, see File S1, Fig. S3), indicating reasonably good precision was achieved in the measurement of MF.

MAD, the primary endpoint used to assess CE, was calculated separately for LS and NLS mutations on an individual mouse basis. To do this, LS and NLS median MFs were first determined for each mouse. Then, for all measured MFs, the absolute deviation from the appropriate median MF was determined. The median of the absolute deviation values for LS and NLS mutations in each mouse constitutes LS and NLS MAD. MAD was first evaluated in mice treated for 180 d with 0, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/kg B[b]F (Fig. 2). Importantly, a significant dose response was observed only for LS mutations and was observed using both approaches for driver classification (one-way ANOVA, P = 0.0246 or P = 0.0257 for LS driver classification based on COSMIC ranking or COSMIC mutation frequency, respectively). Further, in both instances a significant difference between the 0 and 12.5 mg/kg treatment groups was observed in pairwise comparisons (P = 0.0125 and P = 0.0135 for LS drivers based on COSMIC ranking and COSMIC mutation frequency, respectively). No significant dose-related change in MAD was observed for NLS mutations (Fig. 2). The only subtle difference in the results obtained using the two different strategies for lung driver classification was a slightly lower P-value for LS MAD based on COSMIC ranking relative to COSMIC mutation frequency. Consequently, all secondary analyses were performed using lung driver classification based on COSMIC ranking.

However, no significant effect of B[b]F dose on either LS or NLS MAD was observed in the mice treated for 90 d (see File S1, Fig. S4).

A plot of MF relative to amplicon/panel position (Fig. 3) shows some of the mutations with the greatest MFs after the 90-d exposure were in NLS drivers (Nfe2l2, Hras, and Setbp1). MFs of NLS drivers were not as great after 180 d of treatment, consistent with negative selection of some mutants.

Characterization of recovered mutations

A total of 1861 mutations were recovered from the 47 mouse DNA samples analyzed, encompassing 209 unique mutations (categorized as unique based on sequence location and variant type) found in mice treated for 90 and 180 d (File S1, Table S5). Of these, 65 were observed in only one mouse. Thus, most mutations identified were recurrent (144/209, 68.9% were observed in lungs of at least 2 mice), accounting for 1796 of the mutations recovered across the mouse DNA samples. Of the 209 unique mutations, 199 (95.2%) were single-base substitutions, 8 (3.8%) were deletions, and 2 (1.0%) were insertions. Of the mutations at coding bases, 154 were nonsynonymous and 48 were synonymous; 4 of the synonymous mutations were identical to human mutations in the Synonymous Mutations in Cancer database (SynMICdb) that have SynMICdb scores indicating a high probability of functional impact (Sharma et al. 2019). In addition, 1 of 3 intronic mutations occurred at a conserved intronic splice acceptor site. Thus, it is likely that 159/209 (76.1%) of recovered mutations are phenotype-altering (154 nonsynonymous, 4 SynMiCdb mutations, and 1 splice acceptor site). Eighteen mouse mutations were recovered in the mouse homologs of the human hotspot codons the amplicons were designed to interrogate (File S1, Table S6).

The 209 unique mutations occurred at 183 positions within the 993-base target, with more than one mutation type observed at 26 positions. The localization of recurrent mutations to a limited number of sites, the variation in the MFs of the recovered mutations at those sites, and the impact of dose on MF are apparent in Fig. 3.

Impact of B[b]F dose and treatment duration on individual cancer driver genes

Given the dose-related increase in MAD observed after 180 d of treatment, log10 MFs were analyzed for each gene across treatment groups. Only Egfr MFs increased significantly relative to dose (Fig. 4A). Ten of the 24 different mouse Egfr mutations contributing to the dose response are reported as COSMIC human tumor mutations, including mutations observed in lung tumors and lung tumors treated with EGFR inhibitors (File S1, Table S7).

Along with an increase in MF, CE may be discerned as an increase in the absolute numbers of mutations recovered, based on the idea that as CE increases MF a larger number of mutations will reach the CarcSeq limit of detection (≥10−4). Consequently, both log10 MF and numbers of mutations recovered in each cancer driver gene were investigated using two-way ANOVA to determine how they were impacted by B[b]F dose and treatment duration. Log10 Egfr MF (Fig. 4B) showed a significant increase with duration of treatment (P = 0.0217), a dose response of borderline significance (P = 0.0666), and an increase in mutation counts with longer duration treatment of borderline significance (P = 0.0688, not shown). Longer treatment duration caused a significant decrease in log10 Apc MFs (P = 0.0116, Fig. 4C), as well as a significant decrease in number of Hras mutations recovered (P = 0.0002, Fig. 4D), suggestive of negative selection.

Mutation spectrum does not change with B[b]F dose

The etiology of observed mutations can be informed by analysis of mutation spectra (Committee on Mutagenicity of Chemicals in Food Consumer Products and the Environment 2015). The B[b]F mutation spectra obtained using the TwinStrand Mouse Mutagenesis Panel was reported for several mouse tissues and timepoints, including a majority of lung samples from the same mice analyzed in the current study (Zhang et al. 2024). These studies report that the B[b]F-induced mutation spectrum is dominated primarily by C: G > A: T transversions (Schuster et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024). The simple mutation spectrum of CarcSeq mutations from mice treated with B[b]F was analyzed, both in terms of mean MF and proportion of mutation types (Fig. 5). Figure 5A and B show large variation in mean MF across mutation types, without evidence of B[b]F dose response. A slight increase in the percentage of C: G > A: T transversions with B[b]F dose was observed in mice treated for 180 d (Fig. 5C). To evaluate further whether the small increase in fraction of C: G > A: T transversions with dose was meaningful, normalized trinucleotide mutation spectra were generated from the CarcSeq 90- and 180-d data. Figure 6 presents the mutation spectra in all amplicons, stratified by dose and treatment duration. All spectra were similar to the control mutation spectra, meaning neither B[b]F dose nor treatment duration had a significant impact on the observed trinucleotide mutation spectra. The predominant mutations were C: G > T: A in the trinucleotide contexts of CCG, TCG, GCC, GCG, ACG, and GCA (in order of decreasing proportion). Some minor differences in mutation spectra were observed when mutations from mice treated for 90 or 180 d were stratified as LS or NLS (File S1, Fig. S5). Considering all dose groups together, a slight increase in C: G > A: T transversions was observed for the NLS targets of mice treated for 180 d. Overall, the mutation spectra analysis indicates that B[b]F-induced CE occurred primarily via amplification of preexisting or spontaneous lung mutations rather than induction of the mutational specificities associated with direct B[b]F mutagenesis. Mutation spectrum analysis of CarcSeq data, therefore, characterized CDM involvement in the promotional impact of B[b]F exposure.

Lung histopathology and concordance with MAD

An attribute that defines the value of an “early” biomarker of CE is whether it can be assessed before the appearance of histopathological lesions. To investigate this and derive a phenotypic anchoring point of reference, histopathology was performed on lungs from mice treated for 180 d with 0, 1.56, 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 mg/kg B[b]F, along with 3 positive control mice that received a single 50 mg/kg dose of ENU. Histopathology was performed on 46 B[b]F-treated mouse samples, which included all CarcSeq samples. The histopathology results are provided in Table 2. No adenomas were observed in the lungs of B[b]F-treated mice; a lung adenoma was present in 1/3 (33%) of ENU-treated mice. Very subtle bronchioloalveolar hyperplasia was observed in a few mice that would most likely not have been considered test item-related if the mode of action of the compound was not known. No significant effect of B[b]F treatment on the appearance of bronchioloalveolar hyperplasia was observed (Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.6871). Interestingly, the highest percentages of bronchioloalveolar hyperplasia occurred in the dose groups that also had the highest MAD values, specifically the 12.5 and 25 mg/kg dose groups (see Fig. 7A). Integrating the results from the histopathological and CarcSeq results, we conclude that significant molecular indicators of CE (MAD for LS drivers and an increase in Egfr log10 MF) were observed before a significant B[b]F treatment effect was discernable based on histopathology.

The MAD, Egfr Log10 MF, and histopathology data show non-monotonic B[b]F dose responses (Figs 2, 4A, and 7A). These non-monotonic responses may reflect B[b]F toxicity at the top dose. Indeed, a significant dose-related decrease in terminal mouse weight was observed (Fig. 7B), with a significant difference in the pairwise comparison between the 0 and 25 mg/kg dose groups. Mice in the 12.5 and 25 mg/kg dose groups exhibited mean 11.7% and 16.2% reductions in terminal body weight after 180 d of treatment. A terminal body weight reduction of 10% to 12% is a criterion for having exceeded the maximum tolerated dose (MTD) and guidance indicates acceptable carcinogenicity studies should not exceed the MTD because this level of toxicity may delay tumor development (van Berlo et al. 2022). Observing greater evidence of CE at 12.5 than 25 mg/kg B[b]F, therefore, indicates that B[b]F toxicity may have delayed CE in mice treated with 25 mg/kg for 180 d.

Discussion

CE of cells carrying CDMs is an obligatory feature of tumor development. Cells carrying CDMs are prevalent in normal tissues and may confer a tissue-specific selective advantage enabling them to accumulate over time (Parsons et al. 2017; Martincorena et al. 2018; Suda et al. 2018; Salk et al. 2019; Moore et al. 2020; Acha-Sagredo et al. 2022). Chemical exposure can generate a selective pressure that alters the fate of cancer driver mutant clones; for example, cytotoxic therapy causes expansion of pre-existing mutants resistant to the therapy (Wong et al. 2018). CDMs can enable positive or negative selection and selective pressures may change over the course of carcinogenesis (Parsons 2024). In experimental rodent models, CDMs that arise spontaneously as DNA replication errors or via other endogenous mutagenic mechanisms can serve as substrates and reporters of chemical carcinogenesis (obligatory for carcinogenesis induced by nongenotoxic carcinogens). Changes in CDM levels in response to chemical treatment are being investigated as measures of CE and future cancer risk (Parsons et al. 2010, 2017; Parsons 2018, 2024; Harris et al. 2021, 2025; McKim et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2024). In the current work, we used CarcSeq to quantify changes in levels of CDMs in mouse lung tissues in response to treatment with B[b]F, an established mouse lung mutagen and carcinogen.

Our study yielded evidence of B[b]F-induced CE, most notably as a dose-related increase in LS MAD, a biomarker that captures total variability in CDM levels. The dose-related increase in LS MAD was accompanied by nonsignificant decreases in NLS MAD, consistent with the a priori expectation that CE will be accompanied by concomitant decreases in nonexpanding clones. Further evidence of this was the detection of gene-specific positive effects on a lung-specific driver (Egfr) and negative effects on nonlung-specific drivers (Apc and Hras). Although the numbers of histopathological lesions detected in the mouse lung samples were small, the highest percentage of mice with lesions was observed in the 12.5 mg/kg dose group followed by the 25 mg/kg dose group, which mirrored the MAD dose response. Importantly, after 180 d of treatment, when the MAD biomarker of CE showed a significant effect, there was no significant increase in histopathological lesions, indicating CE is being detected at an early stage of tumor development. Unfortunately, significant differences in study design preclude correlating MAD in MF measurements from the current study with late-stage tumor response. The lack of change in mutation spectrum across doses supports the conclusion that B[b]F induced CE of preexisting/spontaneous, lung-specific driver mutations.

The main driver events that occur in the earliest histologically identifiable stage of human lung tumor development are EGFR, KRAS, and BRAF, but only EGFR occurs with equal frequency across all stages of human lung cancer (Inamura 2018). KRAS and BRAF mutation frequencies decrease during progression, supporting the view that additional events may be necessary to overcome mutant cell susceptibility to oncogene-induced senescence. The observation that EGFR mutant lung tumors occur with equal frequency in smokers and nonsmokers is suggestive of an endogenous origin of EGFR mutants in humans (Inamura 2018), and consistent with the early mutational spectra observed in the B[b]F-treated mice. Thus, the promotional effect of B[b]F observed as CE in mouse lung by CarcSeq is likely relevant, qualitatively, to human cancer risk. Importantly, an analysis of lung tumors in A/J mice reported enrichment of Kras codon 12 G > T mutations in B[b]F induced lung tumors relative to spontaneous lung tumors (Mass et al. 1996), suggesting B[b]F genotoxicity drives progression at a later stage of carcinogenesis.

There are several possible mechanisms by which B[b]F treatment may have induced CE of preexisting CDMs. One possibility is that direct mutagenesis acts as a tumor promoter by inactivating tumor suppressor genes and abrogating the ability of wild-type cells to restrain proliferation of nearby mutant clones (Parsons 2024). In this regard, it is interesting to note that a tumor-promoting B[b]F activity has been reported, specifically an ability to release cells from contact inhibition (Andrysík et al. 2013; Misaki et al. 2016). Alternatively, CE could result from regenerative proliferation subsequent to local cytotoxicity, a nongenotoxic effect involving B[b]F activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, or a combination of these mechanisms.

Through activation of AHR, B[b]F can induce CYP1A1, an extrahepatic enzyme predominantly found in rodent and human lung, intestine, placenta, and kidney (Shivanna et al. 2022), where it is believed to impact normal development, xenobiotic metabolism, oxygen stress response, cell cycle control, and apoptosis (Nebert et al. 2000; Goedtke et al. 2021). B[b]F was shown to alter mouse lung gene expression appreciably (Labib et al. 2016). B[b]F upregulation of xenobiotic-metabolizing enzymes (Misaki et al. 2016; Goedtke et al. 2021), enables it to induce its own metabolism to DNA-reactive forms, thereby potentiating mutagenesis. Mutations arising after the early CE of preexisting mutations documented in the current study would have the potential to augment mutation burden and mutational heterogeneity, thereby advancing tumor progression and development of metastatic phenotypes. Initiation of apoptosis as a consequence of B[b]F-induced CYP1A1 expression, resulting in reactive oxygen metabolite-mediated oxidative stress, could confer a proliferative advantage to mutant cells with abrogated apoptosis (e.g. Trp53 mutants) by a mechanism known as apoptosis-induced proliferation (Bethune et al. 2010; Castillo Ferrer et al. 2021; Sumbly and Landry 2022; Cokpinar et al. 2024). In mouse lung, activation of AHR by 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) promoted lung tumorigenesis (Chen et al. 2014). In a mouse cell line, TCDD increased CYP1A1 expression, with subsequent upregulation of inflammatory cytokines and increased Egfr expression (Wong et al. 2018). Given that Egfr log10 MF increased significantly with B[b]F dose and duration of treatment, it is interesting that ligand-activated AHR has been shown to activate EGFR downstream signaling (Sondermann et al. 2023), and AHR activation has been linked to nonsmall cell lung cancer resistance to EGFR thymidine kinase inhibitors (Ye et al. 2018).

Although B[b]F can activate the human constitutive androstane receptor (CAR) (Goedtke et al. 2021), the low level of CAR gene (Nr1i3) expression in mouse lung (∼150 fold lower than in mouse liver, http://www.ncbi.nlm.gov/gene/12355) suggests CAR activation is probably not a mechanism by which B[b]F impacts CE.

Development of chemical products and drugs for which carcinogenicity testing is required would benefit from the availability of an early biomarker approach, like the assessment of CE by ECS. A long-term goal in the analysis of CE as an endpoint for chemical risk assessment is to analyze 4 or 5 noncontrol dose levels and perform benchmark dose-response modeling (White et al. 2020). That said, ECS-based assessment of CE is at an early stage of development. Regarding the CarcSeq approach, the current study yielded technical insights and observations that should be considered in future validation studies. The study established an SSCS number capping approach that eliminated bias in MF measurement and compared two approaches for stratifying lung driver genes, finding only subtle differences between the two. The precision and reproducibility of CarcSeq MF measurement were investigated by performing triplicate analyses of a single sample. The results indicated CarcSeq MF measurement is reproducible (COV 9.93%); however, only ∼54% of the identified mutants were recovered in 2 or 3 of the replicate analyses. This likely reflects the mutational complexity of the sample, sampling error associated with rare event detection (McKinzie et al. 2001), and the efficiency of deriving SSCSs. Capture of only a subset of sample mutations was apparently sufficient to derive evidence of CE. Nevertheless, more robust capture of sample mutations could be an area for future technical improvement.

Three observations from the current study should guide future efforts. This is the second treatment study utilizing CarcSeq in which the MTD was exceeded (based on terminal body weights) and a decrease in MAD was observed relative to the next lower dose. This result suggests that exceeding the MTD may delay CE of driver mutants as it also delays tumor development (van Berlo et al. 2022; Faske et al. 2023). Therefore, care should be taken to not exceed the MTD in future assessments of CE.

This is the second carcinogen treatment study where a significant effect was observed using CarcSeq after 180 d of treatment but not after 90 d of treatment (Faske et al. 2023). Based on this, it may be most productive to focus future validation efforts on the analysis of 6-mo repeat-dose studies, which are required to support long-term, late-stage clinical trials as described under ICH M3(R2) and ICH S6(R1) for pharmaceuticals and biotechnology-derived pharmaceuticals. Such results may be informative for carcinogenicity assessment as described under ICH S1B(R1), while potentially reducing the numbers of rodents needed for carcinogenicity assessment.

Importantly, this study provides an example of how mutation spectra analysis can be used to probe a chemical’s impact on direct mutagenesis versus CE. If direct mutagenesis or direct mutagenesis followed by CE of B[b]F-induced mutations were the source of the recovered CDMs, then the mutation spectrum of the recovered mutants should be consistent with the known spectrum induced by B[b]F (primarily C: G > A: T) (Schuster et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2024). In the current work, the absence of a significant B[b]F-induced shift in mutation spectra is the basis for concluding B[b]F treatment induced CE of preexisting CDMs. Further, the lack of a B[b]F mutagenic signature in the CarcSeq data supports the interpretation that its ≥10−4 limit of detection aids in the preferential capture of CE events. Future ECS analyses of mutation spectra from neutral gene and cancer driver gene sequences may be informative for understanding chemical-specific mechanisms of carcinogenesis. Indeed, this experimental strategy may create new opportunities to conduct relatively short-duration analyses that characterize the extent to which carcinogens act as initiators, promoters, or complete carcinogens, information that could be extremely valuable in carcinogenicity testing and public health protection.
