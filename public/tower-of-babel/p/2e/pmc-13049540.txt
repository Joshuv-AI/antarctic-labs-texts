Red but not the same: Biochemical, metabolomic and flavoromic analyses reveal signature variations in red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars
Authors: Edgar Manuel Bovio-Zenteno, Mian Faisal Nazir, Tianjiao Jia, Chen Feng, Yi Zhang, Jinpeng Lou, Yixiao Zhou, Shuaiyu Zou, Hongwen Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study employed UPLC-MS/MS and GC–MS metabolomics to analyze quality traits in three red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars: ‘RubyRed’, ‘Donghong’, and ‘Hongyang’. ‘RubyRed’ showed enhanced biosynthesis of flavonoids, particularly anthocyanins, and a volatile profile rich in esters, aldehydes, and ketones contributing to fruity and floral aromas. ‘Donghong’ and ‘Hongyang’ were characterized by pyrazines and monoterpenes, respectively. Pathway analysis revealed significant activation of flavonoid and volatile metabolism in ‘RubyRed’. Additionally, ‘Hongyang’ contained higher levels of essential amino acids, highlighting its nutritional value. This study provides critical biochemical insights into the quality traits of red-fleshed kiwifruit. By targeting specific pathways such as flavonoid biosynthesis and volatile compound metabolism, future breeding efforts can optimize traits like pigmentation, aroma, and nutritional value to meet consumer demands and enhance the functional benefits of kiwifruit.Keywords: Actinidia chinensis, Red-flesh, Antioxidants, Flavor, Amino acids

Introduction

The kiwifruit (Actinidia chinensis) is a globally significant fruit crop, prized for its unique flavor and high nutritional content. While green- and yellow-fleshed varieties have historically dominated the market, red-fleshed cultivars are rapidly gaining consumer interest due to their appealing visual characteristics, enhanced sweetness, and perceived health benefits (Asadi et al., 2024; Piani et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Consumers are increasingly drawn to fruits with high functional value, and the vibrant red pigmentation in these kiwifruits suggests a high concentration of bioactive compounds, particularly anthocyanins (Harker et al., 2007; Mian et al., 2022). In general, variations in cultivar quality attributes are recommended as criteria for selecting cultivars based on their potential, e.g. A higher sugar content and lower acid ratio are recommended for ready-to-eat fruits, while nutritional value and antioxidant activity are prioritized for consumption and health products. Poor color or taste, but higher levels of volatiles, are more suitable for juices and wines (Yang et al., 2025).

Red kiwifruits are primarily developed as visually attractive varieties. In addition, preliminary evidence indicates that red-fleshed kiwifruit possesses superior antioxidant activity compared to other color types, which is attributed to higher levels of total phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins (Lister et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2023). Many of these compounds are associated with significant health-promoting effects, including neuroprotective properties and a reduced risk of chronic diseases (Abdollahi et al., 2025; Andrade et al., 2023; Oluwole et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2020). In this context, changes in the flavonoid pathway which is directly linked with color, flavor and beneficial compounds, lead to pigmented kiwifruits showing abundant flavonoid content in comparison to unpigmented ones (Yu et al., 2020).

The term “red-fleshed” refers to a variety of cultivars with different genetic origins and characteristics, the flesh is comprehended as the mixture of fruit components excluding the peel, where the red coloration can have different patterns. Most cultivars in the red-fleshed classification, belonging to the A. chinensis complex, only show a small portion of red color around the core area (inner pericarp) of the fruit. Among them, ʻHongyang,’ the world's first red-fleshed cultivar, is the most extensively investigated (Liu et al., 2025). Studies have revealed that the red flesh portion of the fruit contains higher concentrations of metabolites and offers superior flavor and nutritional value compared to the yellow flesh portion, whereas phenolic acids, terpenoids, and flavonoids are major contributors to this difference (Mao et al., 2024).

While some studies have documented differences in polyphenols content in red-fleshed cultivars (Du et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2017; Yu et al., 2020), a comprehensive understanding of the metabolic foundation that defines their unique quality profiles remains elusive, especially referring to ‘RubyRed’, the first cultivar with extended red-flesh phenotype in the A. chinensis complex (McGlone et al., 2025). Critically, the specific metabolites and pathways that orchestrate the variations in color intensity, antioxidant capacity, nutritional value, and complex flavor profiles have not been systematically evaluated and integrated.

Previous studies have demonstrated that in many fruit species, cultivar identity plays a pivotal role in determining metabolite composition, often outweighing the effects of environmental factors. For example, in a study on apple cultivars grown under different management practices, Chen et al. (2025) found that cultivar identity had a more substantial influence on metabolic profiles than the growing region. Similarly, research on Jujube cultivars (Yao et al., 2023) confirmed that metabolic profiles were primarily cultivar-specific, with environmental variability having a secondary impact. A similar trend was observed in kiwifruit, where metabolic signatures from six different cultivars were reliably differentiated despite variations in growing conditions (Shen et al., 2022). These findings underscore the importance of genotype in shaping metabolomic diversity, which provides a strong rationale for our focus on genetic differences in this study.

Therefore, this study was designed to dissect the biochemical and metabolic signatures of three commercially important red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars: ‘Donghong’ (DH), ‘Hongyang’ (HY), and ‘RubyRed’ (XHG). The primary objectives were to: (1) quantify key biochemical attributes, including bioactive compound content (total phenolics, flavonoids, anthocyanins, vitamin C), corresponding antioxidant capacities, and color characterization; (2) perform untargeted metabolomic (LC-MS) and flavoromic (GC–MS) profiling to identify the key differential non-volatile and volatile compounds that distinguish the cultivars; (3) perform targeted metabolomics to identify key amino acids as drivers of nutritional value; and (4) integrate these datasets to elucidate the metabolic pathways responsible for the distinct color, flavor, and nutritional characteristics of each cultivar.

We hypothesize that the distinct quality profiles of red-fleshed kiwifruits, specifically in terms of color, aroma, and antioxidant properties, are primarily determined by cultivar-specific metabolic pathways. Specifically, we propose that the fully red-fleshed ‘RubyRed’ exhibits superior pigmentation, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and a distinctive aroma due to a broad activation of the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, leading to increased accumulation of anthocyanins and related secondary metabolites. In contrast, the partially red ‘Hongyang’ and ‘Donghong’ are more reliant on metabolic pathways, which influence their flavor complexity and nutritional value, rather than red color. This differential metabolic allocation offers insights into how specific biochemical pathways contribute to the unique characteristics of these cultivars and may provide valuable targets for future breeding programs aiming to enhance both sensory and functional qualities in red-fleshed kiwifruits.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

We selected three red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars for pulp obtention: ‘Donghong’ (DH) and ‘Hongyang’ (HY), which were developed and are widely grown in China (the two cultivars have the largest cultivation areas in China), and ‘RubyRed’ (XHG), which was developed and imported from New Zealand (Fig. 1A). XHG, although from a different geographical zone, was selected because of its increasing global commercial relevance, as well as its distinct phenotype, which set it apart from the local Chinese cultivars(DH and HY), allowing to better understand the diversity and potential applications of red-fleshed kiwifruits. The inclusion of XHG also provides an initial overview of how different environmental conditions, crop management (New Zealand vs China), and genetic backgrounds influence key properties such as nutritional composition, flavor, and color characteristics, which are critical for global trade and consumer acceptance.

Fruit samples of DH and HY were collected (when the soluble solid content of fruits reached 7.0) in the kiwifruit germplasm repository of Lushan Botanical Garden (28° 54′ N, 115°50′ E), Nanchang, China, grown under similar environmental conditions. While the fruit samples of XHG were bought from fruit markets in 2024 (because it has not yet been authorized for cultivation in China) and had reached commercial maturity with SSC ≥ 7. Three kilograms (kg) of each kiwifruit cultivar was peeled, cut into pieces, homogenized, and then stored at −80 °C. Biological samples were placed in a lyophilizer (Scientz-100F) for vacuum freeze-drying for 48 h and grounded (30 Hz, 1.5 min) with a grinder (MM400-Retsch) until powdered for the following analyses comprehending three replicates per cultivar.

Quantification of chemical composition and color assessment

Total phenolic content (TPC)

The determination of TPC was executed in accordance with the Folin-Ciocalteu method (Jia et al., 2025) by employing a plant total phenol content assay kit (BC1340, Solarbio, Beijing, China), in strict adherence to the kit's manufacturer's instructions. The TPC was expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE)/g dried flesh (mg GAE/g).

Total flavonoid content (TFC)

The determination of TFC was executed in accordance with the AlCl₃ colorimetric method (Jia et al., 2025) by employing a plant flavonoid content assay kit (BC1330, Solarbio, Beijing, China). This procedure was meticulously adhered to the stipulated manufacturer's instructions. The result was expressed as mg rutin equivalents (RE)/g of dried flesh (mg RE/g).

Total anthocyanin content (TAC)

TAC was estimated through pH-differential methods using a plant anthocyanin test kit (G0126F, Suzhou Grace Biotechnology Co.,Ltd., Suzhou, China). The result was expressed as mg cyanoside-3-glycoside equivalents/100 g dried flesh.

Vitamin C content (VC)

The measurement of VC content in dried fruits was conducted by employing the spectrophotometer method, with minor adjustments to the established protocol (Desai, 2019). In summary, 5 g of dried flesh was meticulously placed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube containing 10 mL of a 2% (mass fraction) acetic acid solution. The contents of the tube were then thoroughly agitated to ensure homogeneity. A dark treatment was administered for 30 min. Following a centrifugation process at 4000 r/min for 10 min, the supernatant was transferred into a 200-mL volumetric flask, and its volume was standardized. After the filtration process, the absorbance at 243 nm was read with 3 repetitions. Concurrently, standard curves were prepared with varying concentrations of VC standard solutions. The VC content of the samples was then calculated based on the standard curves. VC content was expressed as mg/100 g dried flesh (mg/100 g).

Antioxidant capacity

Extracts

In summary, 25 mL of 75% methanol (v/v) was combined with 1 g of fruit powder and extracted ultrasonically for 30 min at 300 W and 50 kHz (KQ5200E, Shumei, Kunshan, China). After centrifugation (5424R, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) at 10,000 g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected and utilized for subsequent analysis.

DPPH scavenging capacity

The extracts were appropriately diluted with 75% (v/v) methanol and then used for DPPH assay. The DPPH scavenging capacity was determined using the published method (Jia et al., 2025). A DPPH solution (0.2 mmol/L) in anhydrous ethanol (1 mL) was prepared and subsequently mixed with the diluted extracts (0.1 mL). The mixture was then allowed to react at room temperature in the dark for a period of 30 min. Absorbance values of the reaction solution at 517 nm were measured using a spectrophotometer (UV-2700i, Shimadzu, Japan). The DPPH scavenging capacity was expressed as the DPPH scavenging rate using the following equation:

DPPH scavenging rate (%) = [1-(A1-A2)/A0)] × 100

where A1 was the absorbance of reaction solution containing diluted extracts and DPPH solution in anhydrous ethanol, A2 was the absorbance of mixed solution containing diluted extracts and anhydrous ethanol, and A0 was the absorbance of mixed solution containing 75% methanol and DPPH solution in anhydrous ethanol.

ABTS scavenging capacity

The extracts were appropriately diluted with 75% (v/v) methanol and then used to for ABTS assay. The ABTS scavenging capacity was analyzed according to an earlier report (Jia et al., 2025). The ABTS working solution was prepared in the following steps: First, 25 mL of a 7 mmol/L ABTS radical cation solution was mixed with 0.44 mL of a 140 mmol/L potassium persulfate solution to create the stock solution. This mixture was then incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 20 h. Next, the working solution was obtained by diluting the stock solution with distilled water until the absorbance at 734 nm reached 0.70 ± 0.02. ABTS working solution (5 mL) was mixed with 0.1 mL of the diluted extracts, and then the mixture was incubated for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark. The absorbance of the mixture was read at 734 nm using a UV-2700i spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The ABTS scavenging capacity was expressed as the ABTS scavenging rate using the following equation:

ABTS scavenging rate (%) = [1-(A1-A2)/A0)] × 100

where A1 was the absorbance of the mixture containing diluted extracts and ABTS working solution, A2 was the absorbance of the mixture containing diluted extracts and distilled water, and A0 was the absorbance of the mixture containing 75% methanol and ABTS working solution.

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

The detection of FRAP was based on a previously described method (Jia et al., 2025). The FRAP working solution was prepared according to the following procedure: The mixture comprised 300 mmol/L acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 20 mmol/L FeCl3 solution, and 7 mmol/L TPTZ in HCl (40 mmol/L), with a volumetric ratio of 10:1:1. The mixture was preheated at 37 °C for 10 min. To conduct the experiment, 3.9 mL of the working solution was combined with 0.1 mL of the extracts or Trolox standard solution. The mixture was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Following the reading of the absorbance of the reaction solution at 593 nm, a calibration curve was generated (y = 0.01021× + 1.042, R2 = 0.9993). The absorbance of the samples was then compared to the standard Trolox curve. The results were expressed as μmol Trolox equivalents/g dried flesh (μmol TE/g).

Colorimetry

Colorimetry test was performed on kiwifruit pulp (freeze-dried powder with four replicates per cultivar) using CIELAB1976 color space, and the instructions of a digital portable chroma meter (Konica Minolta-CR 400, Tokio, Japan). The following experimental conditions were set Illuminant: D65 and Observer: 10°. The data was analyzed with ColorLab web app (https://colorlab.dnaphone.it/ct).

LC-MS sample preparation and extraction

Sample was prepared modifying a previously reported method (Wang et al., 2023). 50 mg of flesh powder were weighed using an electronic balance (MS105DM) and 1200 μL of −20 °C precooled 70% methanolic aqueous internal standard extract was added. The internal standard for the previous mixture consisted of 2-octanone, 10 μg/mL in isopropanol. The solution was vortexed every 30 min, each lasting 30 s for a total of 6 vortex rotations. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm for 3 min), the supernatant was aspired and filtered with a microporous filter membrane (0.22 μm pore size) and stored in an injection bottle for UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

UPLC-MS/MS analysis

The sample extracts were analyzed using an UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC™). The analytical conditions followed (Wang et al., 2023)method with some modifications. UPLC: column, Agilent SB-C18 (1.8 μm, 2.1 mm * 100 mm); The mobile phase was consisted of solvent A, pure water with 0.1% formic acid, and solvent B, acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid. Sample measurements were performed with a gradient program that employed the starting conditions of 95% A, 5% B. Within 9 min, a linear gradient to 5% A, 95% B was programmed, and a composition of 5% A, 95% B was kept for 1 min. Subsequently, a composition of 95% A, 5.0% B was adjusted within 1.1 min and kept for 2.9 min. The flow velocity was set as 0.35 mL per minute; The column oven was set to 40 °C; The injection volume was 2 μL. The effluent was alternatively connected to an ESI-triple quadrupole-linear ion trap (QTRAP)-MS.

Metabolite identification was performed by matching the MS/MS fragmentation patterns, retention times (RT), and accurate mass-to-charge ratios (m/z) against a proprietary, in-house database (MWDB, Metware) and publicly available databases (e.g., MassBank, KNApSAcK, HMDB, MoToDB, and METLIN). The identification process adhered to the following strict criteria: for MS/MS data, a spectral similarity scores greater than 0.8 was required. For compounds lacking MS/MS data, identification was based on matching the precursor ion m/z within a 0.1 Da tolerance. Isotope patterns were also used to aid in annotation. The confidence of metabolite annotation was categorized according to the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) levels. Identifications confirmed with authentic reference standards were assigned Level 1; identifications based on spectral library matching without a chemical standard were assigned Level 2; identification of compound class was assigned Level 3 (Dossou et al., 2021).

ESI-QTRAP-MS/MS

The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: source temperature 500 °C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V (positive ion mode)/−4500 V (negative ion mode); ion source gas I (GSI), gas II(GSII), and curtain gas (CUR) were set at 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; the collision-activated dissociation (CAD) was high. QQQ scans were acquired as MRM experiments with collision gas (nitrogen) set to medium. DP (de-clustering potential) and CE (collision energy) for individual MRM transitions were done with further DP and CE optimization. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the metabolites eluted within this period (Dossou et al., 2021).

GS-MS sample preparation and treatment

500 mg (1 mL) of flesh powder was transferred to a 20 mL head-space vial (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA), containing NaCl saturated solution, to inhibit any enzyme reaction. The vials were sealed using crimp-top caps with TFE‑silicone headspace septa (Agilent). At the time of SPME analysis, each vial was placed in 60 °C for 5 min, then a 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS fiber (Agilent) was exposed to the headspace of the sample for 15 min at 60 °C (Mamat et al., 2020).

GC–MS analysis

The identification and quantification of VOCs was conducted using (Mamat et al., 2020) modified method. An Agilent Model 8890 GC and a 7000D mass spectrometer (Agilent), equipped with a 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm DB-5MS (5% phenyl-polymethylsiloxane) capillary column. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a linear velocity of 1.2 mL/min. The injector temperature was kept at 250 °C. The oven temperature was programmed from 40 °C (3.5 min), increasing at 10 °C/min to 100 °C, at 7 °C/min to 180 °C, at 25 °C/min to 280 °C, held for 5 min. Mass spectra was recorded in electron impact (EI) ionization mode at 70 eV. The quadrupole mass detector, ion source and transfer line temperatures were set, respectively, at 150, 230 and 280 °C. The selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode was selected for MS and was used for the identification and quantification of analytes.

VOCs were identified by comparing their mass spectra and retention times with entries in the NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) and Wiley mass spectral libraries. A spectral match factor of >80% was required for tentative identification. Furthermore, the linear retention index (LRI) of each compound was calculated based on the retention times of a C7-C30 n-alkane series run under the same chromatographic conditions. The calculated LRI was compared to values reported in literature and public databases to increase identification confidence. Where possible, tentative identifications were confirmed by co-elution with authentic chemical standards (Farag et al., 2022).

Qualitative and quantitative analysis of metabolites

For the LC-MS platform, the software Analyst 1.6.3 was utilized to process mass spectrometry data and for GC–MS platform, the raw data after mass spectrometry analysis was processed through MassHunter Software for qualitative and quantitative analysis. To compare the difference in the content of each metabolite in different samples among all detected metabolites, the chromatographic peaks detected in different samples were calibrated for each metabolite according to the information of metabolite retention time and peak shape to ensure qualitative and quantitative accuracy. Total Ion Flow Diagram Quality control samples (QCs) were prepared from a mixture of sample extracts and used to analyze the repeatability of samples under the same processing method. By overlapping the total ion flow maps (TIC plots) of different QC samples, the repeatability of metabolite extraction and detection, i.e., technical duplication, can be judged. The high stability of the instrument provides an important guarantee for the repeatability and reliability of the data (Mamat et al., 2020).

Targeted analysis of amino acid metabolism

Samples were weighed (50 mg ± 2.5 mg), put into 2 mL centrifuge. Immediately 500 μL of −20 °C pre-cooled 70% methanol water extract and 400 μL chloroform were added and vortexed for 3 min. The mixture was centrifugated at 12000 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was aspirated (250 μL) and put into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, and it was let rest in the refrigerator at −20 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged at 4 °C with 12,000 r/min for 10 min. A total of 180 μL of supernatant was poured into the injection vial and store at −20 °C (Chen et al., 2013).

UHPLC-MS Conditions

The analytical conditions followed (Lim et al., 2020; X. Wang et al., 2023) methods with some modifications. The sample extracts were analyzed using an LC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC, ExionLC AD， https://sciex.com.cn /; MS, QTRAP® 6500+ System, https://sciex.com /). The analytical conditions were as follows, HPLC: column, ACQUITY BEH Amide (i.d.2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm); solvent system, water with 2 mM ammonium acetate and 0.04% formic acid (A), acetonitrile with 2 mM ammonium acetate and 0.04% formic acid (B); The gradient was started at 90% B (0–1.2 min), decreased to 60% B (9 min), 40% B (10–11 min), finally ramped back to 90% B (11.01–15 min); flow rate, 0.4 mL/min; temperature, 40 °C; injection volume: 2 μL. For mass spectrometry the AB 6500+ QTRAP® LC-MS/MS System, equipped with an ESI Turbo Ion-Spray interface, was operated in both positive and negative ion modes and controlled by Analyst 1.6 software (AB Sciex). The ESI source operation parameters were as follows: ion source, turbo spray; source temperature 550 °C; ion spray voltage (IS) 5500 V(Positive, −4500 V(Negative); curtain gas (CUR) were set at 35.0 psi; DP and CE for individual MRM transitions was done with further DP and CE optimization. A specific set of MRM transitions were monitored for each period according to the amino acid eluted within this period.

Preparation of standard curves and sample content

Standard curves for amino acids and derivatives were prepared, concentrations as follows: 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000, 2000, 2000, etc. Mass spectrometry peak intensity data corresponding to each concentration of standard was obtained. The linear equations and correlation coefficients of the standard curves of the substances tested in this project are shown (Table S1). The integral peak-area ratio of all detected samples is calculated by substituting the standard curve linear equation and then substituting into the calculation formula to finally obtain the content data of the substance in the actual sample. Content of each substance in the sample (ng/g) = c*V/1000/m. Where, c: Concentration value obtained by substituting the integral peak area ratio in the sample into the standard curve (ng/mL); V: Volume of solution used at the time of extraction (μL); m: Mass of the sample weighed (g) (Chen et al., 2013; Lim et al., 2020).

Data processing

The chemical and bioactivity analysis results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of each sample's triplicate measurements. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey's test (P < 0.05) were performed using GraphPad prism 8 software. The correlation values (Pearson's correlation coefficients) between content components (TPC, TFC, TAC, and VC) and biological activity (antioxidant inhibitory properties) were determined using R software v. 3.6.1. Principal component analysis (PCA) of samples (including quality control samples) was performed to obtain a preliminary understanding of the overall metabolite differences between groups and the size of variability between samples within groups. The metabolite content data was processed by UV (unit variance scaling), and a heat map was drawn by the R software ComplexHeatmap package, and the accumulation patterns of metabolites among different samples were analyzed by hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA). Replicate correlation assessments in biological replicates within a group were observed through Pearson correlation analysis between samples. The Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated using the built-in cor function of the R software (Dossou et al., 2021).

Differential metabolites detected

To ensure that the untargeted data detected by the two platforms are used at the same time for the analysis of biological problems, analyses were performed by integrating the data of the two platforms. For two-group analysis, differential metabolites were determined by VIP (VIP > 1) and absolute Log2FC (|Log2FC| ≥ 1.0). VIP values were extracted from Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) result, which also contain score plots and permutation plots, was generated using R package MetaboAnalystR. The data was log2 transform and mean centered before OPLS-DA. To avoid overfitting, a 200-permutation test was performed. Identified metabolites were annotated using KEGG Compound database and annotated metabolites were then mapped to KEGG Pathway database using (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) and (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) respectively. Targeted amino acids data followed the same procedures, but the differential criteria applied was fold change ≥2 and fold change ≤0.5. A difference of more than 2 times or less than 0.5 between the control and experimental groups is considered significant (Dossou et al., 2021).

Results

Color quality assessment

As a starting point, a colorimetric analysis was conducted on fruit from the three cultivars, setting the stage for subsequent chemical, metabolomic, and flavoromic investigations. To have a clear color classification and understand the changes in colorimetric variables, a colorimetric analysis using CIELAB space was performed. The results revealed different coordinates for each group of samples, as expected in a axis, XHG was located in red area with moderated brightness between the other cultivars, DH had the brightest color located in yellow-green area, and HY had the darkest color in yellow-green area, the b axis indicated highest blue tendency in HY and highest yellow tendency in XHG (Fig. S1 A).

The LCH projection indicated a clear separation in color between cultivars using h (hue grades) locating XHG between 0° and 45° the red to orange zone, while DH and HY were placed in the orange to green zone, again indicating changes in brightness but also in chroma, meaning that the perceptions of color in the tree varieties can be perceived different by color, brightness and saturation (Fig. S1B). Furthermore, the delta analysis of comparison between cultivars indicated clear differences in each comparison with a general ΔE > 25 in all of them, indicating that changes in color are perceptible to the human eye (Fig. S1C). These comparisons also supported the previous stated observations in color tendencies, chroma, brightness and hue.

Phytochemical characterization

Given the differences in flesh color, we investigated the content of key bioactive compounds and their contribution to antioxidant activity (Fig. 1). Consistent with its intense red color, XHG contained the highest TAC, TPC, and VC (Fig. 1A-C, E). Consequently, XHG demonstrated significantly greater antioxidant capacity in all three assays: DPPH, FRAP, and ABTS (Fig. 1F-H).

To identify the key drivers of this activity, a Pearson correlation analysis was performed (Fig. 1I). The results revealed that TAC and VC were the most dominant contributors, showing exceptionally strong positive correlations with FRAP (r = 0.999 and r = 0.992, respectively) and ABTS (r = 0.991 and r = 0.989, respectively). TPC also showed a very strong correlation, particularly with DPPH scavenging (r = 0.984). In stark contrast, TFC exhibited only weak and inconsistent correlations across the assays (r = −0.09 to 0.30), indicating it is not a primary driver of antioxidant capacity in these cultivars. These findings establish a clear biochemical basis for the superior functional quality of the XHG, directly linking its red phenotype to a higher concentration of potent antioxidant compounds, especially anthocyanins and vitamin C.

Variation in Hue is particularly similar to the concentration of anthocyanidins, indicating the contribution of this substance to the red color, characteristic appearing in the same order starting with XHG. However, the contribution of other substances must be responsible for other characteristics of color, e.g., carotenoids and chlorophyl, which were not quantified here.

Untargeted Metabolomics Analysis

To investigate the metabolic differences underlying the phenotypic traits of the three red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars, untargeted metabolomic analysis was performed using LC-MS and GC–MS platforms. The total ion chromatograms, in both positive ion mode (ESI+) and negative ion mode (ESI−), were assessed through QC overlay (Fig. S2 A-C), providing a comprehensive snapshot of metabolite profiles. A total of 3096 metabolites were identified upon platform data merging, demonstrating high-quality support (Fig. S2D, E, Table S2).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation of the cultivars into distinct clusters, confirming that each cultivar has a unique metabolic signature (Fig. S3A). Further analysis of the LC-MS (non-volatile) and GC–MS (volatile) data independently also reinforced this separation (Fig. S3B, C). To enhance the resolution of metabolic differences, Orthogonal Partial Least Squares-Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed, showing robust discrimination between groups (Fig. S3D—F). Validation of the models using 200-repetition permutation tests yielded high predictive ability (Q2 > 0.9) and goodness of fit (R2Y > 0.9), confirming the reliability of the data (Fig. S4, Table S2). Together, these multivariate analyses reveal systematic and significant metabolic differences between the cultivars, with XHG exhibiting a distinct metabolic profile compared to HY and DH.

Overview of Metabolite profiles of three Kiwifruit cultivars

A total of 3096 metabolites were detected and categorized into 27 metabolite classes (Fig. 2A, Table S2), including terpenoids, flavonoids, phenolic acids, amino acids and derivatives, alkaloids, esters, and nucleotides. Cultivar-specific profiles showed that XHG exhibited predominant accumulation of amino acids, alkaloids, phenolic acids, and flavonoids. In contrast, HY was enriched in terpenoids, flavonoids, nucleotides, and organic acids, whereas DH displayed higher levels of lipids, esters, and selected terpenoids (Fig. 2B).

(C) Differential accumulated metabolites counted across comparison groups. (D) Venn diagram of differential metabolites between groups. ‘Donghong’ (DH), ʻHongyang’ (HY), and ‘RubyRed’ (XHG). A total of 1005 metabolites were significantly differentially accumulated between DH and HY, including 717 downregulated and 288 upregulated in DH relative to HY (Fig. 2C, Table S3). In the comparison between XHG and DH, 1284 differential metabolites were identified, with 723 showing higher abundance in XHG and 561 downregulated (Table S4). Meanwhile, 1120 metabolites were significantly altered in XHG vs HY, comprising 454 upregulated and 666 downregulated compounds in XHG (Table S5). These differences highlight the extensive metabolic remodeling between cultivars, with XHG displaying a particularly substantial number of upregulated metabolites compared to both DH and HY.

A Venn diagram comparison revealed 367 differential metabolites shared across all cultivar pairs. Notably, the XHG vs DH comparison showed the highest number of unique differential metabolites (167), followed by DH vs HY (106), and XHG vs HY (93) (Fig. 2D). This pattern suggests that XHG and HY are more chemically similar to each other than to DH, reflecting closer metabolic clustering despite their distinct origins.

Most significant differential metabolites and pathway analysis

A comprehensive analysis of UPLC-MS/MS and GC–MS datasets identified distinct metabolomic signatures across the three red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars, revealing differential accumulation of numerous metabolites in pairwise comparisons: XHG vs DH, XHG vs HY, and DH vs HY. A total of 54 significant metabolites (VIP > 1, FDR < 0.05, |Log₂FC| > 1) were identified, comprising 27 non-volatile compounds (UPLC-MS/MS) and 27 volatile compounds (GC–MS) (Tables S6-S8). The top 20 differential metabolites spanned flavonoids, phenolic acids, terpenoids, alkaloids, and aldehydes, underscoring the cultivar-specific metabolic profiles.

The flavonoid biosynthetic pathway emerged as a key factor in distinguishing the cultivars, with a particular emphasis on anthocyanins. In XHG, anthocyanins were notably elevated, with pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside-5-O-arabinoside showing a 27-fold increase compared to DH. This strong accumulation of anthocyanins is intricately linked to the cultivar's deeper red pigmentation (Fig. 3A). Similarly, cyanidin 3,5-O-diglucoside exhibited a remarkable 295-fold increase in XHG compared to HY, further solidifying the importance of anthocyanin accumulation in shaping the red-fleshed phenotype of XHG (Fig. 3C). These anthocyanins are integral to the broader flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, which also includes key compounds such as quercetin, and naringenin. The combined increase in anthocyanins and other flavonoids suggests a coordinated upregulation of the entire flavonoid pathway, contributing not only to enhanced pigmentation but also to improved antioxidant capacity, a hallmark of fruit quality.

The broader flavonoid pathway, characterized by the biosynthesis of anthocyanins, flavones, and flavonols appears to play a central role in determining the overall quality attributes of XHG. Enhanced accumulation of these metabolites provides molecular support for XHG's superior pigmentation, antioxidant potential, and flavor complexity. In contrast, DH and HY exhibited different metabolic profiles, with enriched levels of terpenoids and lipid-derived compounds. For instance, blumenol C 9-O-(4’-O-glucosyl-β-glucoside) and megastigmadiene-diol glucosides, associated with sesquiterpenoid metabolism, were significantly higher in DH, while XHG displayed downregulation of compounds like gingerglycolipid C and urmiensolide. This indicates a cultivar-specific routing of terpenoid biosynthesis (Fig. 3E), which contributes to their distinct aromatic and quality traits.

Volatile compound profiling through GC–MS further differentiated the cultivars based on aroma-active compounds, particularly esters, alcohols, and aldehydes. In XHG vs DH, esters such as butanedioic acid diethyl ester, 1-ethylpropyl acetate, and 1-methoxy-2-propyl acetate were significantly enriched in XHG, contributing to its sweet and fruity aroma profile (fold changes ranging from 5× to over 15×). Additionally, aldehydes like 2,4-dimethylcyclohex-3-ene-1-carbaldehyde and alcohols such as 2-hexanol (R-) were elevated in XHG, reinforcing the complexity of its aroma and highlighting the broader impact of the flavonoid pathway and the volatile compound production. In contrast, purine- and alkaloid-related metabolites such as isopentenyl adenosine and triacanthinapioside were downregulated in XHG and HY compared to DH, suggesting that these nucleotide derivatives may serve as biomarkers for DH, further supporting the metabolic differences between cultivars.

Together, these UPLC-MS/MS and GC–MS datasets underscore that XHG stands out due to a broader and more pronounced activation of the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway. The increased accumulation of anthocyanins, flavonoids, phenylpropanoids, and esters in XHG directly contributes to its superior pigmentation, antioxidant properties, and complex aroma. This coordinated activation of the flavonoid pathway, involving both structural and volatile compounds, enhances the overall fruit quality of XHG, marking it as a premium cultivar. Meanwhile, DH and HY maintain more selective accumulation patterns, reflecting genetic and metabolic divergence linked to their unique fruit quality traits (Fig. 3A-D, E-F). Thus, anthocyanins, key products of the flavonoid pathway, play a crucial role in defining the high quality of XHG. They interact with other flavonoids that influence color and work in synergy with those affecting aroma and taste (including quercetin, apigenin, and naringenin). This interplay creates a balanced composition that enhances not only visual appeal but also the health and nutritional benefits. As a result, differences between cultivars, reflected in their color appearance, can be linked to these variations, supported by the earlier findings in colorimetry and pigment chemistry.

Enhanced Flavonoid Metabolism in ‘RubyRed’ Drives Pigment and Functional Variation

Upstream intermediates such as naringenin, naringenin chalcone, and liquiritigenin were more abundant in XHG than in DH and HY, pointing to an elevated precursor pool driving downstream metabolic activity (Fig. 4). Downstream of naringenin, the accumulation of hesperetin-7-O-glucoside was significantly enhanced in XHG, indicating preferential glycosylation and stabilization of flavanones. In parallel, branching toward both flavanone and flavanol biosynthesis was observed with quercetin upregulated leading to many derivatives.

In the flavone pathway, several key metabolites including apigenin, vitexin, isovitexin, and apigenin-7-O-beta-D-glucoside were significantly elevated in XHG, along with 8-methoxyluteolin-8-glucoside and 6-hydroxyluteolin-7-sambubioside, marking a clear activation of this otherwise less dominant branch. These compounds, known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, further diversify XHG's functional profile.

The flavonol pathway showed a different pattern. While quercetin, quercetagenin, and myricetin-3-(2′,3′-digalloyl)rhamnoside were upregulated in XHG, other kaempferol-derived glycosides such as kaempferol-3-O-(6″-galloyl)glucoside and kaempferol-3-O-(6″-galloyl)galactoside were downregulated compared to DH. This suggests a selective redirection of metabolic flow from kaempferol-based flavonols toward more highly decorated quercetin and myricetin derivatives in XHG.

The anthocyanin branch, central to fruit pigmentation, was notably upregulated in XHG. Key intermediates such as leucocyanidin, cyanidin, and its glycosides—cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, cyanidin-3-O-gentiobioside, cyanidin-5-O-beta-D-glucoside, and cyanidin-3-O-beta-D-sambubioside—were all significantly more abundant in XHG than in DH. Likewise, acylated and rutinoside derivatives such as peonidin-3-O-(6”-O-p-coumaroyl)glucoside, peonidin-3-O-rutinoside, malvidin-3-O-galactoside, malvidin-3-O-(6”-O-malonyl)glucoside, and petunidin-3-O-arabinoside showed increased accumulation, contributing to XHG's characteristic deep red pigmentation. Moreover, the glycosylated anthocyanins add sweetness to the taste experience highly claimed by consumers.

Furthermore, the proanthocyanidin-related flavan-3-ols such as catechin-4′-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside, (−)-epicatechin, (−)-epigallocatechin, and (+)-gallocatechin were also more abundant in XHG compared to DH, while (−)-epicatechin-3-O-beta-D-glucopyranoside was elevated in both XHG and HY. These results suggest that not only monomeric anthocyanins, but also polymeric flavonoid derivatives contribute to the color intensity and antioxidant properties of XHG. In contrast, DH showed enrichment in a narrower range of flavonoids, particularly those derived from kaempferol and luteolin, while HY exhibited intermediate levels across several branches, with partial overlap with XHG in anthocyanin content but lower in flavone diversity.

Altogether, this expanded pathway analysis highlights that XHG possesses a broad and highly active flavonoid biosynthetic network, spanning early intermediates, flavanones, flavones, anthocyanins, and proanthocyanidins. These findings could be suggesting that transcriptional or post-translational upregulation of multiple enzymes—likely including CHI, F3H, FNS, FLS, DFR, ANS, and UFGT—is responsible for the rich chemical diversity observed in this cultivar. The integrated accumulation of colored anthocyanins and bioactive flavones may underline both the distinctive appearance and enhanced functional qualities of XHG compared to DH and HY.

Flavoromic Profiling Reveals Cultivar-Specific Volatile Signatures

Volatile metabolite analysis by GC–MS uncovered clear distinctions in the flavoromic profiles of the three red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars. Among the significantly differential volatiles, several compounds showed striking cultivar-specific accumulation patterns, suggesting strong metabolic divergence in aroma-related pathways (Fig. S5A, B).

In the comparison between XHG and DH, 3-octen-2-one, a ketone associated with green and mushroom-like aromas, was markedly upregulated in XHG (fold change = 8.45), but significantly reduced in HY (fold change = 0.03 vs DH). This suggests that this compound may contribute to the unique earthy freshness of XHG. Similarly, 5,9-undecadien-2-one, 6,10-dimethyl- (E), another ketone with potential fruity and spicy notes, was also substantially higher in XHG (fold change = 4.62), further supporting the distinct aromatic character of this cultivar.

In contrast, DH was enriched in pyrazine volatiles, particularly 2,3-diethylpyrazine, which exhibited more than 43-fold higher levels in DH than HY. Pyrazines are commonly associated with roasted and nutty sensory attributes and their presence in DH may underline its more savory and toasted aroma profile. These same compounds were significantly downregulated in XHG, reinforcing the cultivar-specific divergence in sensory chemistry.

XHG also exhibited selective enrichment of terpenoid volatiles, including β-phellandrene, a compound known for citrus and peppery nuances. This terpenoid was upregulated in XHG vs DH (fold change = 2.04) but downregulated in HY, indicating that monoterpene metabolism may be more active in XHG. Meanwhile, 1-methylnaphthalene, a compound associated with woody and floral notes, was moderately upregulated in XHG vs DH and slightly elevated in XHG vs HY, contributing to the complex aromatic structure of XHG.

Overall, these differential volatiles illustrate the cultivar-specific expression of volatile pathways—such as ketone biosynthesis, pyrazine formation, and terpenoid metabolism—that give rise to the distinctive flavor profiles of each red-fleshed kiwifruit. The data reinforces the sensory richness and biochemical uniqueness of XHG particularly its fruity, sweet, and citrus-forward character, in contrast to the nutty/roasted notes of DH and the citrus-herbal-minty tones of HY given by limonene, levomenthol, and formic acid, heptyl ester.

Amino acids profiling reveals cultivar-specific signatures related to nutritional value

The targeted UPLC-MS/MS dataset yielded high quality data (Fig. S6A—D) and revealed distinct profiles among the three red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars (Fig. S7A—B), with multiple amino acids and derivatives differentially accumulated across the pairwise comparisons: XHG vs DH, XHG vs HY, and DH vs HY. A total of 50 significant metabolites (FDR < 0.05, P-value <0.05, |Log₂FC| > 1) (Table S9).

Two classes emerged, with organic acids and its derivatives as a small portion contributing to cultivar differences. Notably, amino acids and derivatives were highly increased in HY cultivar, including essential amino acids, such as methionine, lysine, isoleucine, threonine, tryptophan, and histidine, indicating a highly nutritional value emphasized by a promotion of amino acid metabolism (Fig. S7C, E). Similarly, DH had second place in concentration of amino acids and derivatives, but only two essential metabolites i.e., methionine and lysine, were highly increased in XHG vs DH, highlighting its non-essential and derivatives amino acid metabolism priorities (Fig. S7A, E).

In contrast, XHG was the least enriched with scarce accumulation of amino acids, especially when compared to HY, having only one amino acid (L-tryptophyl-L-glutamic acid) and two organic acids (3,7-Dimethyluric acid and (S)-.-Amino isobutiric acid) as differential metabolites, that could serve as specific biomarkers for this cultivar (Fig. S7C). Interestingly, compared to DH, L-phenylalanine, was highly expressed, the substrate to begin with the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, this appears to be aligned with the higher content of anthocyanin, the expressed color and the general striking antioxidant capabilities of XHG (Fig. S7A).

Together, the UPLC-MS/MS dataset demonstrate that HY exhibits a broader and more pronounced activation of amino acids and derivatives associated to tryptophan, nicotinate and nicotinamide, and cyanoamino acid metabolism (Fig. S7C—F). The accumulation of essential amino acids in HY provides a molecular basis for its superior nutritional value. In contrast, DH maintains a balanced pattern just under HY, and XHG maintains more selective accumulation, indicating metabolic divergence reflective of genotype and fruit quality traits, e.g., the top two significant enriched pathways were not shared in any comparison (Fig. S7B—F).

Enhanced amino acid metabolism in ‘Hongyang’ drives nutritional enrichment

Up-regulation of amino acid metabolites was evidently higher in HY counting 17 steps in the biosynthesis pathway (Fig. S8). Histidine, tryptophan, and tyrosine branches were significantly up regulated in HY indicating selective direction to the sugar pentose metabolism, tryptophan biosynthesis, and tyrosine biosynthesis, with the latter also having a role in the phenylpropanoid pathway. In this case, XHG had a preferential biosynthesis of phenylalanine, the main precursor of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, matching with the enrichment found in the previous results of polyphenols in ‘Rubyred.’ However, unlike the other cultivars the general amino acid metabolism was clearly not a central path for this cultivar.

Continuing with HY, up regulation of aspartate was key for the accumulation of other amino acids such as asparagine, alanine, lysine, pipecolic acid, and threonine, permitting the synthesis of isoleucine, and up regulation of urea cycle. Accumulation of ornithine, citrulline, and proline was observed. Together several amino acid biosynthesis branches were affected in HY through key steps, resulting in the enrichment of amino acids profile. Similarly, DH had an increased number of amino acids up regulated, overlapping with HY in methionine, lysine, ornithine, among others. However, the branching was slightly different, urea metabolites were shared with HY, excepting citrulline, and a preferential biosynthesis of serine and cysteine was evident, thus increasing the amount of conditionally essential amino acids. Moreover, DH was the only cultivar without up regulation of precursors for the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, which can be seen as a reason for lower content of phenolic acids and diminished antioxidant activity.

Discussion

Red-fleshed kiwifruits are increasingly valued for their vibrant appearance and enhanced health benefits, yet the metabolic underpinnings of their unique traits remain underexplored. In this study, UPLC-MS/MS and GC–MS-based metabolomics were combined to unravel the biochemical diversity among three commercial red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars— ‘Donghong’ (DH), ‘Hongyang’ (HY), and ‘RubyRed’ (XHG)—revealing distinct metabolic signatures that correlate with pigmentation, antioxidant potential, and aroma complexity. With targeted UPLC-MS metabolomics of amino acids, divergences in nutritional value and precursors for color in the three cultivars were depicted.

Red-fleshed kiwifruits exhibit variation in the distribution and intensity of their red color throughout the flesh. To assess their chemical composition and antioxidant capacity, total phenolic content (TPC), total flavonoid content (TFC), total anthocyanin content (TAC), and vitamin C content (VC) were measured. Our results revealed differences in TAC levels across the three cultivars, with the highest levels observed in XHG, the reddest kiwifruit. This finding is consistent with studies on pigmented potatoes (Yin et al., 2016), which demonstrated that the overall quantity of phenolic compounds can be linked to expressed phenotypes. In the case of kiwifruits, this is visible in a pulp that has a uniformly red appearance, not just in the center. This makes sense, as previous studies have indicated that the red inner portion of kiwifruit contains higher amounts of bioactive compounds, including flavonoids (Mao et al., 2024), hence it was expected that the redder the phenotype, the higher the concentrations of these components, as were detected in XHG.

Additionally, discrepancies in TFC and TAC levels between HY and DH were observed, even though previous studies reported no significant differences between these two cultivars (Y. Liu et al., 2019). In our study, XHG exhibited higher TPC and VC, while the TFC of DH did not differ significantly from XHG. These findings align with existing literature, which reports that both HY and DH have lower antioxidant capacity and concentrations of flavonoids and anthocyanins compared to redder phenotypes, such as A. arguta cv. purpurea (Liu et al., 2019). This suggests that the observed differences in these parameters are not solely attributed to interspecies variation, but are more closely linked to the synthesis of metabolites associated with the red coloration, such as anthocyanins. Previous studies have proposed that red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars are distinguishable from their green counterparts by their dominant levels of flavanols and anthocyanins (Liu et al., 2019). This further underscores the biochemical distinction based on flavonoid profiles, where the presence of specific metabolites, particularly those involved in pigmentation, plays a significant role in defining the unique characteristics of kiwifruits including those classified as red-fleshed.

The weak correlation between TFC and antioxidant activity (r = −0.09 to 0.30 across assays) suggests that total flavonoid content alone cannot explain the observed antioxidant capacity. This highlights the complexity of flavonoid contributions to antioxidant activity. While TFC provides a general measure of flavonoid levels, the specific composition—particularly the presence of anthocyanins—likely plays a more critical role. Given that anthocyanins are abundant in red-fleshed kiwifruits (Chen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2024), their potent antioxidant properties may be the primary drivers of the superior antioxidant capacity observed in XHG. The strong positive correlations between TAC and antioxidant assays such as FRAP (r = 0.999) and ABTS (r = 0.991) further support this. In contrast, the broader TFC measure did not exhibit the same correlations, suggesting that the antioxidant activity is more strongly influenced by the composition and proportion of specific flavonoids (e.g., anthocyanins, flavanones, and flavanols) rather than their total quantity.

As suggested by previous studies (Liu et al., 2019), flavonoid profiles, rather than total content, appear to be a more reliable predictor of antioxidant activity, particularly in red-fleshed cultivars where anthocyanins play a major role (Chen et al., 2023). Future studies should focus on a detailed analysis of individual flavonoid compounds to more accurately determine their specific contributions to antioxidant activity. By quantifying specific flavonoid subclasses and correlating them with antioxidant assays, we can better understand the complex biochemical basis for the observed functional quality of red-fleshed kiwifruits.

The colorimetric tests also supported these differences, effectively distinguishing the phenotypes based on their anthocyanin content. Moreover, vitamin C content was higher in the reddest phenotype, a relationship that is consistent with observations in other red fruits like tomatoes and strawberries (Cömert et al., 2020). However, the link between red color and vitamin C content is not entirely clear, as not all red fruits exhibit this characteristic. Further studies are needed to explore a broader spectrum of pigments and non-pigment interactions, as copigmentation and synergies between pigments can modify the final color observed (Gonnet, 1998). In our study, we employed vacuum freeze-drying (lyophilization) to preserve the chemical and organoleptic characteristics of the fruit, including its color, by preventing enzymatic degradation and microbial growth. Lyophilization is widely used in plant-based colorimetric analysis for its ability to retain the integrity of samples (Bhatta et al., 2020). However, it is important to note that while lyophilization is effective in preserving the overall quality of the sample, it may alter tissue structure, potentially disrupting cell walls. This could influence how light interacts with the sample, possibly affecting color measurements.

Despite these potential changes, several studies have demonstrated that vacuum freeze-drying is one of the most effective methods for preserving plant sample properties, especially in retaining color integrity. The technique operates in an environment devoid of liquid water and oxygen, preventing deterioration of key components. While minor shifts in color readings can occur due to lyophilization, these changes are generally consistent across replicates and do not significantly impact the overall results (Bhatta et al., 2020; Taskin, 2025). Thus, the benefits of lyophilization in terms of sample preservation outweigh its drawbacks, including cost and time requirements. The differences in color between cultivars are unlikely to be caused by the preservation method, but rather can be attributed to variations in flavonoid metabolism, chlorophyll metabolism, and carotenoid content in the outer pericarp. (Zhang et al., 2018). While Rubyred accumulates more anthocyanins, the other cultivars may primarily focus on carotenoid accumulation, followed by a later stage of chlorophyll degradation.

Among the most striking findings was the extensive upregulation of flavonoid biosynthetic intermediates and end-products in XHG. This cultivar exhibited higher accumulation of upstream metabolites like naringenin and liquiritigenin, as well as downstream anthocyanins including cyanidin-3-O-glucoside, peonidin-3-O-rutinoside, and malvidin-3-O-galactoside. The high abundance of cyanidin and its derivatives is consistent with earlier reports highlighting these compounds as major contributors to red pigmentation in ʽHongyangʼ and other red-fleshed genotypes (Liu et al., 2019; Montefiori et al., 2005; Yu et al., 2020). Moreover, activation of minor but functionally important branches was observed, including flavone and flavonol pathways, with elevated levels of apigenin, vitexin, and myricetin derivatives—compounds known for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties (Khoo et al., 2017).

These findings align with the broader view that anthocyanin and flavonol biosynthesis is tightly co-regulated by transcription factors such as MYB, bHLH, and WD40 complexes (Jaakola, 2013), and suggest that transcriptional activation of genes such as F3H, FLS, DFR, and UFGT is particularly pronounced in XHG, understanding that these genes are candidate genes for anthocyanin accumulation in ʻHongyangʼ, which belongs to the A. chinensis complex as XHG does (Yu et al., 2020). The compositional shift from kaempferol-based glycosides to more diversified quercetin and myricetin conjugates suggests a metabolic redirection that enhances both coloration and antioxidant potential in ‘RubyRed.’ These results raise the possibility that the current categorization of ‘red-fleshed’ kiwifruits may require reconsideration, particularly since only XHG exhibits a fully red-fleshed phenotype, while DH and HY show more yellowish and greenish flesh with only a red ring in the center. This distinction along with future developments could lead to a reclassification of red kiwifruit into two categories: red-fleshed and red-ringed, each with its own specific criteria. Further studies could help refine the classification of red-fleshed cultivars based on both phenotypic and metabolic characteristics.

Regarding flavor, volatile metabolite analysis by GC–MS revealed distinct aroma chemotypes for each variety. ‘RubyRed’ (XHG) was notably enriched in esters, such as butanedioic acid diethyl ester and 1-ethylpropyl acetate, which are associated with sweet and fruity aromas. Additionally, ketones like 3-octen-2-one and aldehydes like 2,4-dimethylcyclohex-3-ene-1-carbaldehyde contributed to a fresh, green sensory profile, evoking citrus and herbal nuances. In contrast, ‘Donghong’ (DH) exhibited higher concentrations of pyrazines, such as 2,3-diethylpyrazine, which are often linked to nutty and roasted notes. ‘Hongyang’ (HY), on the other hand, showed an abundance of monoterpenoids, including limonene and levomenthol, which are typically associated with citrus and minty fragrances. These profiles mirror previous work on green (ʻHayward’) and yellow (ʻHort16a’) kiwifruit where distinct volatile patterns were linked to cultivar-specific traits (Du et al., 2019; Garcia et al., 2012).

Importantly, these cultivar-specific volatiles are not merely incidental but are the result of differentially regulated biosynthetic pathways, including fatty acid degradation (producing esters and aldehydes), isoprenoid metabolism (terpenes), and amino acid derivatives (such as pyrazines). The observed patterns suggest that selective breeding for flavor must account for the complex interplay among multiple chemical classes—not just the traditional sugar–acid balance.

Moreover, the synergistic effects between these volatiles and anthocyanins, which contribute to coloration, should be considered in future studies. Many anthocyanins are not present in their free form; in fact, the most abundant anthocyanins found in our study are glycosylated, which may enhance flavor perception. Given their higher accumulation in XHG, these compounds may contribute not only to color but also to the heightened sweetness of this cultivar. This role has been previously highlighted in ‘Hongyang’, where key metabolites such as cyanidin-3,5-O-diglucoside, pelargonidin-3-O-rutinoside-5-O-glucoside, quercetin-3-O-glucoside, kaempferol-3-O-glucoside, and pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside were identified as major contributors to differences in flesh color and flavor across tissues (Mao et al., 2024).

A broader comparison of the three cultivars revealed that XHG is not only the most pigmented but also the most chemically diverse in both volatile and non-volatile metabolites. The UPLC-MS/MS analysis showed accumulation of functionally relevant compounds such as dicaffeoylquinic acid, hesperetin-7-O-glucoside, and eriodictyol chalcone—metabolites associated with cardiovascular, anti-cancer, and anti-diabetic benefits (Singh & Silakari, 2018; Wu et al., 2022). This highlights the potential of flavonoid-rich fruits in the development of functional foods and underscores XHG as a promising cultivar for nutraceutical applications, pending further evaluation through physiologically relevant studies, especially considering that metabolic diversity alone does not necessarily equate to metabolic superiority.

Conversely, DH and HY presented more limited metabolic profiles. DH's abundance of lipid and terpenoid derivatives reflects a different allocation of metabolic resources, potentially linked to defense mechanisms that also influence fruit texture, shelf life, and carotenoid accumulation. Meanwhile, HY exhibited a mix of traits, with intermediate anthocyanin levels and distinctive nucleotides and organic acids, suggesting it represents a transitional phenotype between DH and XHG. Moreover, the amino acids profile revealed that HY and DH have extended accumulation compared to XHG. Primary metabolites including sugars, organic acids, and free amino acids, play a pivotal role in shaping the taste and nutritional value of kiwifruit (Hu et al., 2025), and HY was the richest in essential amino acids (Those that the human body is not able to synthetized itself), 6 out of the total 9 were up regulated (isoleucine, threonine, lysine, methionine, tryptophane, and histidine), this highly increases the fruit value as a source of vital nutrients for humans.

Dietary supplementation with this and other amino acids are beneficial for (1) ameliorating health problems in human life cycle (e.g., fetal growth restriction, neonatal mortality, wasting syndrome, obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and infertility); (2) increasing efficiency of metabolic transformations to enhance muscle growth, milk production, egg and meat quality and athletic performance, while preventing extra fat deposition and reducing adiposity (Wu, 2009).

The amino acid profiles observed in this study align with previous reports, which found no significant increase in nutritional value positively correlated with enhanced color (Ma et al., 2017). Specifically, the reddest cultivar exhibited lower accumulation of both essential and non-essential amino acids. Additionally, most of the amino acids identified in the analysis were L-amino acids. These amino acids, well known as building blocks of proteins and recognized for their health benefits, also contribute to the fruit's flavor profile, influencing traits such as sweetness and aroma formation (Aristoy & Toldrá, 2021; He et al., 2024). These findings should be interpreted with caution, as other factors, such as environmental influences, may also contribute to these observations. Further studies in controlled conditions are required to clarify this relationship.

Key amino acids, such as phenylalanine, cysteine, serine, alanine, and methionine, were upregulated in the DH and downregulated in XHG. These amino acids are known to impart complex flavor notes, ranging from bitter and sweet to more sulfurous and meaty tones. In particular, phenylalanine and cysteine, both aromatic amino acids, serve as important precursors for a variety of secondary metabolites, including flavonoids, lignin, and vitamin E (He et al., 2024). Highlighting their importance as nutritional and sensorial drivers. Along with this, the upregulation of alanine, threonine, and proline can contribute to a sweeter taste in HY due to their differential presence (Hu et al., 2025). These findings suggest that the distinct flavor profiles observed in kiwifruits may stem from the interplay of different metabolic pathways, with both amino acids and secondary metabolites contributing to the overall taste experience, indicating that the fruit is not only tasty but beneficial for human health, adding extra value for the consumers.

For instance, the sweetness observed in the XHG cultivar seems to be primarily driven by glycosylated anthocyanins, whereas in the HY and DH cultivars, sweetness appears to result from a combination of sweet amino acids. This suggests that different biochemical components contribute to a similar sensory experience of sweetness across these cultivars. In the case of XHG, the primary contributors to sweetness can be viewed as antioxidants, while in HY and DH, they are more intricately linked to nutritional compounds. These differences further emphasize the distinct biochemical profiles of red-fleshed versus red-ringed kiwifruits.

Between DH and HY up regulation of histidine, isoleucine, tyrosine, tryptophan, and cysteine indicate that HY is likely to be having a high bitterness taste contribution compared to DH. The contribution of amino acids to flavor is complex, and again more variation due to this could be seen in DH and HY, previous reports have determined glutamic acid, arginine, and aspartate are the main amino acids in kiwifruits, also able to shape the taste (Aristoy & Toldrá, 2021; Jeong et al., 2025; Macrae & Redgwell, 1992; Paulauskiene et al., 2014). However, in red kiwifruit the differences mentioned above indicate a broader interplay that could be tested in future studies considering crucial features such as genotype, cultivation characteristics, and maturation stage with tools such as electronic tongue and taste panels.

Our integrated analysis allows us to suggest a metabolic model that distinguishes the biochemical basis of quality between fully red-fleshed and red-ringed kiwifruit. In the ʻRubyRedʼ cultivar, a “premium color-premium quality” profile is driven by the broad upregulation of the entire flavonoid biosynthetic pathway. This not only results in a high accumulation of anthocyanins, which are responsible for its deep red color (Fig. 1E, Fig. 4), but also enhances its antioxidant capacity (Fig. 1F-H). Simultaneously, this metabolic route appears to be linked to the production of desirable esters and ketones, contributing to the characteristic sweet and fruity aroma (Section 3.6).

In contrast, the quality profiles of ʻHongyangʼ and ʻDonghongʼ are less dependent on the flavonoid pathway. Their sensory characteristics, particularly sweetness and flavor complexity, are more significantly influenced by the metabolism of amino acids (Section 3.7). This suggests two distinct metabolic strategies for achieving desirable quality traits: one driven by secondary metabolite pathways (flavonoids in ʻRubyRedʼ) and another more reliant on primary metabolite pathways (amino acids in HY and DH). This model suggests that targeting the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway may enhance color and aroma, while focusing on amino acid biosynthesis could optimize nutritional value and taste. However, these ideas should be further explored in future breeding programs to validate their effectiveness and impact.

Notably, the biosynthesis of anthocyanins in red-fleshed kiwifruits is likely regulated by genetic factors fixed through breeding programs, but it is also modulated by crop management and environmental conditions, such as light exposure and temperature stress, which are known to enhance the synthesis of anthocyanins and ascorbic acid (Jaakola, 2013; González et al., 2019). In XHG, the upregulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis genes, such as DFR and UFGT, may be a direct response to the environmental stressors or direct management strategies in its New Zealand growing region, which might favor flavonoid production over other metabolic pathways. This upregulation could also explain the cultivar's higher antioxidant capacity and its vibrant red color. Moreover, the metabolic shift from kaempferol-based glycosides to more diversified quercetin and myricetin conjugates in XHG suggests a redirection of metabolic resources toward compounds that enhance both coloration and antioxidant potential.

While the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway seems to dominate in XHG, other cultivars like HY and DH may allocate resources differently, resulting in lower anthocyanin levels and a greater reliance on amino acid metabolism for the synthesis of compounds that contribute to their flavor profiles. This differential allocation of metabolic resources could be due to the plants' varying priorities for growth versus defense, or it could reflect genotype-specific adaptations to their respective growing conditions. For example, HY and DH exhibited higher accumulation of essential amino acids like histidine and tryptophan, which are not only crucial for protein synthesis but also contribute to the flavor complexity of the fruit. In contrast, XHG's focus on flavonoid biosynthesis likely limits the accumulation of amino acids, though it results in enhanced pigmentation and antioxidant properties.

The identification of key biomarkers across multiple metabolic branches offers practical value for breeding programs. Anthocyanin derivatives like cyanidin 3,5-O-diglucoside, ester volatiles contributing to fruity aromas, and non-volatile flavones and amino acids could serve as molecular indicators of superior pigmentation, taste, nutritional value, and functionality. This aligns with prior suggestions that metabolomics-guided selection could streamline cultivar improvement in Actinidia and other fruit crops (Jaakola, 2013; Li et al., 2020). Furthermore, the integrated analysis framework used here—combining biochemical quantification, untargeted and targeted metabolomics, and flavoromics—provides a holistic strategy for assessing cultivar performance and consumer appeal. Such approaches are increasingly valuable in precision agriculture and personalized nutrition.

While our study provides valuable insights into the metabolic profiles of red-fleshed kiwifruit cultivars, it must be explicitly acknowledged that the compared genotypes in this study were grown under different environmental conditions. Specifically, ‘Hongyang’ (HY) and ‘Donghong’ (DH) were cultivated in China, whereas ‘RubyRed’ (XHG) was grown in New Zealand. These environments differ in climate, soil type, rootstock, and orchard management practices, all of which are known to influence both primary and secondary metabolite accumulation. Therefore, environmental factors may have partially contributed to the metabolomic variation observed among the cultivars.

While the observed differences in metabolite profiles are likely driven predominantly by genetic factors, as supported by previous studies showing that cultivar identity is a major determinant of metabolomic composition (Chen et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2023), the present results should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of genotype-driven metabolic superiority. Instead, our findings should be regarded as indicative of genotype-associated metabolic trends that require further confirmation. Definitive attribution of the observed metabolic differences to genetic factors will require future experiments in which all genotypes are grown under identical environmental conditions, using the same rootstock and standardized orchard management practices. Such controlled studies will be essential to disentangle genetic effects from environmental influences and to validate the extent to which the observed metabolomic differences are genetically determined.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that red kiwifruit quality is underpinned by distinct metabolic strategies rather than a uniform biochemical profile. Using integrated metabolomics, we show that the fully red-fleshed cultivar ‘RubyRed’ achieves superior pigmentation, antioxidant capacity, and fruity aroma primarily through coordinated activation of the flavonoid biosynthetic pathway, whereas the red-ringed cultivars ‘Hongyang’ and ‘Donghong’ rely more heavily on primary metabolism, particularly amino acid accumulation, to shape nutritional value and flavor. These findings support a metabolic distinction between fully red-fleshed and red-ringed kiwifruits, with implications for cultivar classification and metabolomics-guided breeding. However, as the cultivars examined were grown in different geographic regions, environmental factors such as climate, soil, and orchard management may have contributed to the observed metabolic variation alongside genetic effects. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as indicative of genotype-associated metabolic trends, and future studies under uniform growing conditions will be essential for a deeper understanding of red-fleshed kiwifruit variability.
