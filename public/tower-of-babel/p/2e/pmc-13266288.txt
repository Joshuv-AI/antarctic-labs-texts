Chloroplast‐ and mitochondrion‐specific random C‐to‐T mutagenesis for forward genetics of organelle genomes
Authors: Nanami Kosaka, Yoshiki Harada, Issei Nakazato, Miki Okuno, Takehiko Itoh, Nobuhiro Tsutsumi, Shin‐ichi Arimura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYOrganelle genomes in plastids (including chloroplasts) and mitochondria encode essential genes for photosynthesis, respiration, and agronomic traits, representing promising targets for crop improvement. However, their high copy number and non‐Mendelian inheritance have long hindered efficient modification compared to nuclear genomes. Recent advances in organelle base editing (C‐to‐T and A‐to‐G) have enabled precise nucleotide substitutions, yet information on useful mutations remains limited. Here, to establish a forward genetics platform for C‐to‐T substitutions, we developed a method to introduce random C‐to‐T mutations throughout the entire organelle genomes of Arabidopsis thaliana. We engineered a fusion protein, WHY2‐CD mutator, combining cytidine deaminase (CD), uracil glycosylase inhibitor, and sequence‐nonspecific DNA‐binding protein WHIRLY2 (WHY2), fused to organelle‐targeting peptides. This system introduced dispersed C‐to‐T substitutions specifically within plastid or mitochondrial genomes. In T2 lines, we identified homoplasmic (homozygous) plastid genome mutants, including rpoA knockouts and rbcL variants with an amino acid substitution. Screening T3 populations on spectinomycin revealed plastid genome mutants with resistant traits and their causal mutation. These mutations can be transferred or combined using targeted base editors, such as transcription activator‐like effector cytidine deaminase (TALECD). This comprehensive, C‐to‐T‐focused mutagenesis provides a powerful tool for organelle forward genetics and molecular breeding.

INTRODUCTION

The plastid genome of flowering plants encodes genes involved in photosynthesis. On the other hand, the mitochondrial genome harbors genes for essential functions such as respiratory chain complexes, and often has chimeric genes related to cytoplasmic male sterility (CMS) (Unseld et al., 1997; Sato et al., 1999; Wang et al., 2024). Variation in these organelle genomes and their interactions with the nuclear genome can influence field performance (Flood et al., 2020; Moison et al., 2010; Roux et al., 2016; Theeuwen et al., 2024), and subtle or single nucleotide polymorphisms within organelle genomes have been linked to traits such as CMS (Ducos et al., 2001), herbicide resistance (Hirschberg & McIntosh, 1983; Murphy & Tranel, 2019), and adaptive variation in photosynthetic performance (Liu et al., 2012; Sen et al., 2011). Thus, the ability to modify and precisely control organelle genomes holds great potential for improving crop performance; therefore, such methods could be a promising tool for crop breeding. Nevertheless, the use of organelle genomes has been limited in breeding, except for exploiting CMS in F1 hybrid seed production. Because organelle genomes are typically inherited uniparentally, creating a new combination of genes between the parental alleles and trait mapping is not feasible through crossing. Moreover, their high copy number per cell can make it difficult to achieve homoplasmy of introduced alleles or mutations, further complicating genetic analysis and breeding.

In recent years, plant organelle genome editing has advanced rapidly (Arimura & Nakazato, 2024; Wang et al., 2024), enabling both targeted DNA cleavage‐based editing (Kazama et al., 2019) and targeted base substitution (Kang et al., 2021; Mok, Hong, et al., 2022; Nakazato et al., 2021, 2022; Zhou et al., 2024). These technologies have been applied to identify CMS‐associated genes in the mitochondrial genome of rice and rapeseed (Kazama et al., 2019), tomato (Kuwabara et al., 2022), broccoli (Xu et al., 2024), potato (Tamburino et al., 2025), and eggplant (Tsujimura et al., 2025), to confer resistance to herbicides such as metribuzin and atrazine in Arabidopsis (Mok et al., 2024; Nakazato et al., 2025), and to improve photosynthetic capacity in Arabidopsis (Yamori et al., 2025). Currently, organelle‐targeted base editing is largely limited to C‐to‐T and A‐to‐G substitutions, but due to the minimal alterations and the fact that such edits seem more acceptable to society and are exempt from GMO regulations in some countries (Turnbull et al., 2021), this technology holds great promise for improving crop productivity. While the sequence information of organelle genomes is abundant (the number of complete genome sequences as of September 2023 in the NCBI is nearly 13 000 for plastid genomes and 673 for mitochondrial genomes, respectively (Wang et al., 2024)), data on naturally occurring or targetable base polymorphisms—particularly those that lead to phenotypic changes upon base editing—remain critically scarce (Gabay‐Laughnan & Newton, 2012; Greiner, 2012; Massouh et al., 2016). This lack of information currently represents a major bottleneck for the effective application of genome editing in crop trait improvement.

Forward genetics by irradiation, chemical, and insertion mutagenesis has long served as a powerful tool in the nucleus; numerous nuclear genome mutants have been generated and analyzed not only for identifying gene functions of interest within the laboratory (Alonso & Ecker, 2006) but also for enhancing crop performance via mutational breeding (Yali & Mitiku, 2022). In contrast, reports on organelle genome mutants with point mutations remain extremely limited, with only a few examples, such as rbcL mutants deficient in Rubisco holoenzyme in Nicotiana tabacum induced by ethyl methane sulfonate (EMS) (Shikanai et al., 1996), lincomycin‐resistant mutants in Capsium annuum by EMS (Subhash et al., 1997), and pigment‐deficient or spectinomycin‐resistant mutants by nitroso‐methyl urea (NMU) (Avni et al., 1989; Azhagiri & Maliga, 2007; Sears & Sokalski, 1991). Ethidium bromide increased the appearance of mitochondrial nad9 point mutations under gene‐drive‐like circumstances (Forner et al., 2022). Since chemical mutagens introduce mutations simultaneously in the nucleus, efficient isolation of organelle genome mutants is technically challenging. Some nuclear genome mutants are known to impair organelle DNA repair pathways, leading to the accumulation of mutations in organelle genomes. A representative example is the MutS Homolog 1 (MSH1) mutant, originally isolated as chloroplast mutator (chm) (Abdelnoor et al., 2003; Martínez‐Zapater et al., 1992; Rédei, 1973; Sakamoto et al., 1996; Xu et al., 2011), which exhibits elevated point mutation frequencies in both plastid and mitochondrial genomes (Broz et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2020). However, msh1 mutants induce mutations simultaneously in both organelle genomes, and each mutation often cannot be transferred separately to other plants. Furthermore, msh1 can also trigger extensive genomic rearrangements via illegitimate homologous recombination between repeated sequences in the mitochondrial genome (Gualberto & Newton, 2017). From an engineering perspective, multiple attempts to create organelle genome variations have been reported. In Arabidopsis, plastid‐targeted restriction enzymes cause nonspecific structural changes in the plastid genome (Sugimoto et al., 2020), although not focused on point mutations. Attempts have also been made to generate a plastid genome mutant library using transposon tagging in tobacco, but the insertions tend to occur repeatedly at the same few loci (Kohl & Bock, 2009). TALE nuclease (TALEN) gene‐drive mutagenesis (Forner et al., 2022) introduces mutations, but they are limited to the TALE binding site.

In this study, we propose a method to introduce random C‐to‐T mutagenesis into the entire organelle genomes of A. thaliana for forward genetic analysis, which can be applied to mutational breeding of crop organelle genomes. We employed a construct termed “WHY2‐CD mutator,” which was made by fusing cytidine deaminase (CD), uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI), a sequence‐nonspecific DNA‐binding protein AtWHIRLY2 (WHY2) (Cappadocia et al., 2010; Krause et al., 2005), and a transit peptide for plastids or mitochondria (Arimura & Tsutsumi, 2002; Nakazato et al., 2021), to induce C‐to‐T base substitutions at undefined loci in the targeted organelle genomes. Since double‐stranded DNA deaminase toxin A (DddA) was adopted as CD (Mok et al., 2020), due to its intrinsic preference for inducing TC‐to‐TT substitutions, the “TC” context is a potential editing site. Although the sites of mutagenesis are limited to specific contexts, the resulting variants are expected to be transferable or re‐introducible into wild‐type or other crop species using precise C‐to‐T base editing with ptp/mitoTALECD (Nakazato et al., 2021, 2022). Here, we attempted to introduce transferable semi‐random C‐to‐T mutations in a plastid‐ or mitochondrion‐specific manner via signal peptide targeting, and to isolate mutants from a population of plastid genome‐mutagenized lines.

RESULTS

Construction of organelle‐specific C‐to‐T mutagen vectors

To induce mutations in organelle genomes, binary vectors for Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation were constructed to express artificially designed proteins, termed “WHY2‐CD mutators.” These proteins consist of a mitochondrion‐ or plastid‐targeting peptide, A. thaliana Whirly2, which binds DNA without sequence specificity (Cappadocia et al., 2010), and the double‐stranded DNA deaminase toxin A (DddA) from Burkholderia cenocepacia (Mok et al., 2020) (Figure 1a). Initially, we attempted to clone WHY2‐CD mutators containing the wild‐type, full‐length DddA in Escherichia coli, as illustrated in Figure S1; however, all examined clones contained mutations within the DddA coding region (Figure S1). This is likely due to the high cytotoxicity and excessive substitution activity of DddA (Mok et al., 2020). To attenuate the base substitution activity of DddA in the expression constructs, we employed two strategies: (1) splitting DddA at the 1397th amino acid residue (Mok et al., 2020), resulting in constructs termed CD 1397 N‐WHY2‐CD 1397C (CWC), and (2) using less active DddA variants, E1347D and GSVG (Mok, Lee, et al., 2022) (Figure S2). Ultimately, eight WHY2‐CD mutator constructs with different configurations were successfully designed, cloned, and introduced into plants (Figure 1b). DddA preferentially induces C‐to‐T substitutions at 5′‐TC‐3′ sites. In A. thaliana ecotype Columbia‐0 (Col‐0), 52 381 and 20 219 “TC” sites are present in the mitochondrial and plastid genomes, respectively (Figure 1c), representing potential targets for the mutator. Of these, 5951 and 7587 sites in the mitochondrial and plastid genomes, respectively, are located within coding regions or open reading frames (ORFs) and may result in amino acid substitutions (Figure 1d).

Phenotypes and genotypes of T1 plants

We first examined the phenotypes of transformed T1 seedlings grown on sucrose‐containing medium (Figure 2a,b; Table S1, Figure S3). Among the eight mitochondria‐targeted (mt) WHY2‐CD mutators, some or many of the T1 individuals of all lines exhibited growth retardation phenotypes, except for the line G. mtCWC170_GSVG. In contrast, plastid‐targeted (pt) mutators showed less pronounced growth retardation but displayed visible color phenotypes such as whitening (A. ptCWC188), variegation (B. ptCWC170), and yellowing (E. ptGSVG). Several individuals with severe aberrant phenotypes, particularly those of A. mtCWC188, B. mtCWC170, E. mtGSVG, and A. ptCWC188, died before reproducing and producing seeds, resulting in no T2 progeny (Table S1). For each mutator, a higher or comparable frequency of abnormal phenotypes was observed in mitochondrion‐targeted lines compared to plastid‐targeted ones (Figure 2b). These results suggest that WHY2‐CD mutators targeted to mitochondria or plastids can introduce visibly distinct phenotypes, with varying degrees of severity.

To test whether WHY2‐CD mutators induced mutations in organelle genomes, we PCR‐amplified and Sanger‐sequenced two short regions containing essential (atp1 in mitochondria and 16S rRNA in plastids) or non‐essential (atp6‐2 (Arimura et al., 2020) in mitochondria and ndhH (Ueda et al., 2012) in plastids) genes. To avoid amplifying highly homologous mitochondrial DNA sequences in the nucleus (NUMTs (Fields et al., 2022; Stupar et al., 2001)), primers were designed to specifically target mitochondrial DNA (Table S4). Even in these short regions (several hundred base pairs, Figure 2c), C‐to‐T substitutions—defined as roughly ≥10% signal in Sanger chromatograms—were detected in A. mtCWC188, B. mtCWC170, E. mtGSVG, and A. ptCWC188. In B. mtCWC170 and A. ptCWC188, phenotypic data supported a link between mutations and abnormal growth. Mutation mapping (Figure 2d) showed that (1) the number of mutations varied by construct, (2) not all “TC” sites were mutated, (3) some sites were mutated in multiple individuals and lines, and (4) some T1 plants had probable homoplasmic mutations. C‐to‐T changes in “TCC” contexts were also observed in A. mtCWC188. These results indicate that WHY2‐CD mutators induced C‐to‐T mutations in the targeted organelle genomes with partial sequence preference.

Next‐generation sequencing (NGS) analysis of T1 plants

To assess the genome‐wide mutagenic effects of WHY2‐CD mutators in T1 plants, we performed short‐read Illumina sequencing and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) call analysis. To determine whether C‐to‐T mutations occurred specifically in the targeted organelle genomes, bulk DNA samples from 5 to 11 T1 plants of mitochondrion‐ or plastid‐targeted lines D. E1347D and E. GSVG were sequenced, respectively. These four lines were selected for their distinct phenotypes (Figure 2a,b; Table S1) and sufficient biomass for DNA extraction and NGS. In the E. mtGSVG sample, 6441 C‐to‐T mutations above the threshold were detected in the mitochondrial genome, whereas only two were detected in the plastid genome (Figure 3a; Data Set S2). In the E. ptGSVG sample, 88 C‐to‐T mutations were identified in the plastid genome, and only two in the mitochondrial genome. In the D. mt/ptE1347D sample, no C‐to‐T mutations were detected in either of the organelle genomes. In all four samples, only a few C‐to‐T mutations were detected on nuclear chromosome 1, except near the centromeric region, where the abundance of repetitive sequences makes variant calling unreliable (Figure 3a, Figure S4). These results indicate that C‐to‐T mutations were introduced almost exclusively in the organelle targeted by the corresponding signal peptide, at least when using the E. GSVG construct.

To examine the mutation profiles in individual T1 plants, three plants per line were independently sequenced (Figure 3b–d). Samples were obtained from D. mt/ptE1347D and E. mt/ptGSVG (same constructs as in Figure 3a), as well as from B. ptCWC170 and H. mtSepCD_GSVG, which showed visible abnormal phenotypes but retained fertility (Table S1). In D. mtE1347D, two to five independent mutations were detected in the mitochondrial genome of each plant (Figure 3b,d). The corrected allele frequencies (cAFs: sample AFs subtracted by wild‐type AFs at the same position) ranged from 5% to 15%, indicating heteroplasmy or chimerism involving mutated and wild‐type mitochondrial DNAs. The mutation sites differed among the plants. In H. mtSepCD_GSVG, two to three mutations with cAFs between 5% and 10% were identified. Two of these mutations were common to all three plants (Figure 3b,d). In E. mtGSVG, which was lethal or sterile, 6200 to 7200 mutations were detected across the mitochondrial genome (Figure 3d). Most variants with cAFs greater than 15% were shared among the three plants (Figure 3b). In addition, structural changes involving increased copy number in the specific mitochondrial EE2‐EE1 and X1‐X2 repeat regions (defined in the Col‐0 mitochondrial reference genome (Sloan et al., 2018)) were observed in E. mtGSVG #2 (Figure S5A). This type of structural change in the mitochondrial genome resembles those reported in mitochondrial DNA repair mutants (Wallet et al., 2015). A strong preference for the “TC” sequence context, a known DddA target (Mok et al., 2020), was confirmed (Figure S5B). A weak tendency for G or A at the −2 position was also observed, although this was not reported in Mok et al. (2020). These results indicate that WHY2‐CD mutators can introduce C‐to‐T mutations in the mitochondrial genome with partial sequence context specificity. In E. mtGSVG, excessive mutation levels may have contributed to lethality or sterility. In contrast, D. mtE1347D successfully generated viable T1 plants carrying distinct mitochondrial mutations.

For plastid‐targeted lines, approximately 400 C‐to‐T mutations were detected in the plastid genomes of individuals #1 and #10 of E. ptGSVG, and 26 mutations in individual #7 (Figure 3c,d). In D. ptE1347D, no mutations were found in two of the three individuals, while 13 mutations with cAFs around 1% were detected in individual #9. In B. ptCWC170, two individuals had approximately 50 mutations each, and individual #4 had 3855 mutations (Figure 3c,d). Regarding high cAF values, in E. ptGSVG, mutations with cAFs around 40% were observed only in individual #1. In B. ptCWC170, mutations with cAFs ≥50% were found only in individual #4. In these analyses, no homoplasmic mutations (cAF = 100%) were detected in any plastid‐ (and also mitochondrion‐) targeted samples (Figure 3b,c). In B. ptCWC170 #4, the nucleotide preference surrounding mutated cytosines resembled that observed in E. mtGSVG, the preference for “TC” context (Figure S5B). These results indicate that C‐to‐T mutations were also induced in the plastid genome in T1 plants. The number and cAF distribution of these mutations varied among constructs and among individual plants. Both common and unique mutation sites were detected in plastid‐targeted lines.

Phenotyping of the T2 plants

First, phenotypes of 40 T2 seedlings per T1 parental line were evaluated by visual observation. As a control, a recessive msh1 mutant line (CS3246; msh1‐2) was used. To reset the organelle genomes to their original wild‐type state, an F1 heterozygous msh1 mutant was generated by crossing msh1‐2 with the seed parent Col‐0, five F2 plants homozygous for msh1 were selected, and their F3 progeny were subjected to phenotypic analysis (Wu et al., 2020). In these msh1 F3 populations, approximately 25% of plants exhibited abnormal phenotypes such as variegation or growth retardation. In WHY2‐CD mutator‐transformed lines, abnormal phenotypes also appeared in T2 populations, but their frequency varied depending on the T1 parent, even within populations derived from the same construct (Data Set S3, Data Set S4, Figure S6). Approximately 0–50% of T2 plants from D. mtE1347D, H. mtSepCD_GSVG, B. ptCWC170, and E. ptGSVG showed abnormal phenotypes (Data Set S3). Some individuals lacking seed GFP fluorescence, indicating the loss of the nuclear‐integrated WHY2‐CD construct through Mendelian segregation, also exhibited abnormal phenotypes (Data Set S3, Figure S6). These findings suggest that WHY2‐CD mutators can induce heritable organelle mutations with visible phenotypes, and that phenotypic screening in the T2 generation may be feasible for mutant screening, in a manner comparable to that observed in msh1‐mutant‐derived progeny.

Genotyping of T2 plants

The sequenced T1 plants that produced T2 seeds did not exhibit homoplasmic mutations (Figures 2 and 3). However, previous studies have shown that heteroplasmic states in plant organelle genomes can become fixed as a single allele (homoplasmy) within one or two generations (Broz et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024). To evaluate this phenomenon in our system and assess the utility of each T2 population for homoplasmic mutant screening, genotyping was implemented for T2 plants of B. ptCWC170 and E. ptGSVG, which had C‐to‐T mutations with cAFs ≥10% on the plastid genome in the T1 generation (Figure 3c and Data Set S2). Two plastid loci, G55202 and G102787 (Data Set S2), which showed C‐to‐T variants with cAFs above 30% in T1 plants, were selected for analysis. Twenty T2 individuals with seed GFP fluorescence and 20 without GFP fluorescence were genotyped using PCR‐Sanger sequencing. At the G55202 site, which results in a G82E amino acid substitution in the rbcL gene, six and five individuals homoplasmic for the mutated allele were identified in both GFP‐positive and GFP‐negative groups, respectively (Figure 4a). While all individuals homoplasmic for the mutant allele (A) exhibited yellow‐green leaves and growth retardation, 16 out of the total 18 individuals (three for GFP‐positive and 15 for GFP‐negative) homoplasmic for the wild‐type allele (G) displayed wild‐type like phenotypes (Figure 4b,c). Among GFP‐positive seedlings, more than 10 showed heteroplasmic or chimeric genotypes. In contrast, no heteroplasmic or chimeric individuals were detected among GFP‐negative seedlings (Figure 4a). These results indicate that it is feasible to isolate homoplasmic plastid genome mutants in the T2 generation of B. ptCWC170 and E. ptGSVG, and that the mutation can be fixed in individuals in which the mutator construct was removed from the nucleus.

To investigate the number and positions of inherited C‐to‐T mutations across the plastid genome, whole‐genome sequencing using Illumina short‐reads was conducted on several GFP‐negative T2 individuals derived from B. ptCWC170 #4 and E. ptGSVG #10. The T1 plant B. ptCWC170 #4 carried more than 1000 heteroplasmic or chimeric mutations (Figure 3d), whereas none of its three T2 progeny exhibited C‐to‐T mutations in the plastid genome (Figure S7). In contrast, all three T2 individuals from E. ptGSVG #10 carried mutations with corrected allele frequencies (cAFs) ≥99.5%, suggesting probable homoplasmic status (Figure 5). The mutation sites differed among the three individuals, and the number of mutations appeared to vary by individual (Figure 5b). The individual #10‐24_noGFP, which showed an aberrant pale‐green to white phenotype, carried 13 homoplasmic C‐to‐T mutations, a higher number than those in #10‐21_noGFP (one mutation) and #10‐22_noGFP (one mutation), which exhibited wild‐type‐like phenotypes (Figure 5). Several C‐to‐T mutations were identified at positions not previously detected in the parental T1 plant (Figure 5). Despite this inconsistency in germline transmission, 24 individuals each derived from #10‐21_noGFP and #10‐22_noGFP were proven to be T‐DNA null‐segregants and to stably inherit the homoplasmic mutations from the parental T2 (Figure 5c, Figure S8). These results indicate that (1) T2 individuals derived from E. ptGSVG can carry homoplasmic C‐to‐T mutations, and (2) they carry distinct mutations in different individuals, and so suggest that (3) they are suitable for genome‐wide screening of plastid genome mutants.

Identification of a mutation causing a chimeric, chlorophyll‐deficient phenotype

Among T1 individuals transformed with B. ptCWC170, one individual exhibited a chimeric phenotype with distinct green and white tissues (Figure 6a). To determine whether this phenotype was caused by a plastid genome mutation, DNA was extracted separately from each tissue and subjected to Illumina short‐read sequencing. A specific C‐to‐T substitution (C78279T) with a cAF of 98.47% was identified in the white tissue. This mutation introduced a premature stop codon in the plastid‐encoded RNA polymerase (PEP) subunit A gene (rpoA), resulting in W204* (Figure 6b). Both green and white tissues separately bolted, bloomed, and then produced T2 seeds independently. Thirty T2 individuals derived from the green tissue and 15 from the white tissue were grown on 1/2 MS medium with sucrose (Figure 6c). All progeny from the green tissue grew as well as the wild‐type, while all from the white tissue exhibited a fully white phenotype. Genotyping revealed that the green plants carried the wild‐type W204 allele, whereas the white plants were homoplasmic for the W204* mutation (Figure 6c). Although most white individuals failed to bolt or reproduce, one bolted but did not set seeds and ultimately died (Figure S9). These results suggest that the rpoA W204* mutation causes the chlorophyll‐deficient phenotype but could be fertile and produce seeds at least in the T1 generation, implying that PEP RNA polymerase may be dispensable for bolting, flowering, reproduction, and embryogenesis, as supported by the presence of the chimeric green tissue. These findings also suggest the feasibility of isolating a lethal or severe phenotype‐causing mutation from the chimeric parts.

Selection of the mutant resistant to spectinomycin from the plastid‐targeted T3 population

To assess the feasibility of large‐scale screening for plastid genome mutants, spectinomycin (spm) selection was conducted on T3 populations of B. ptCWC170 and E. ptGSVG. Each T3 population originated from a single T1 line and subsequent 21 randomly selected T2 plants grown in bulk. Several mutations in the plastid genome conferring spm resistance have been previously reported in five angiosperms, including two mutations in Arabidopsis, C1015T and G1141A in the 16S rRNA gene (Table S2). Green plants emerged in two independent T3 populations derived from E. ptGSVG (#1 and #9) (Figure 7a). Approximately 40 green individuals were observed in E. ptGSVG #1, and one in E. ptGSVG #9, out of approximately 7500/plate spm‐treated seedlings per population. To determine whether these resistant phenotypes were associated with base substitutions in the 16S rRNA gene, the corresponding region was amplified and sequenced in 12 individuals from E. ptGSVG #1 and one from E. ptGSVG #9. All resistant plants carried the same homoplasmic mutation, C102026T (C1015T in 16S rRNA gene). This mutation was absent in three spm‐sensitive T3 individuals (Figure 7b). The positive control line had the same mutation (see Materials & Methods), but the possibility of seed contamination was excluded by detecting both the 16S rRNA gene and the mutator transgene by PCR (Figure S10). These findings demonstrate that plastid homoplasmic mutants conferring spm resistance can be isolated de novo from T3 populations generated by WHY2‐CD mutators.

DISCUSSION

Organelle genomes have long been challenging to manipulate or improve through conventional breeding, largely due to their high copy numbers and non‐Mendelian inheritance in most species. The advent of targeted base editing technologies enabling C‐to‐T and A‐to‐G substitutions (Arimura & Nakazato, 2024; Kang et al., 2021; Mok, Hong, et al., 2022; Nakazato et al., 2021, 2022, 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024) now allows precise and minimal modification of organelle genomes, opening opportunities for crop breeding outside GMO regulations. However, the loci that should be modified to achieve desirable traits remain largely unknown, with only a few examples—such as antibiotic or herbicide resistance and correlations between Rubisco large subunit variants and C4/C3 photosynthesis—currently documented (Gabay‐Laughnan & Newton, 2012; Greiner, 2012; Massouh et al., 2016; Whitney et al., 2011). This knowledge gap contrasts with human mitochondrial DNA, where tens of thousands of natural single nucleotide variations and 117 pathogenic point mutations have been cataloged (https://www.mitomap.org, accessed Aug 2, 2025), enabling direct application of base editors for therapeutic and modeling purposes (Cho et al., 2022; Gualberto & Newton, 2017). Similarly, random mutagenesis tools and mitochondrial disease models are well established in animals, including mice (Kauppila et al., 2016; Trifunovic et al., 2004) and Drosophila (Andreazza et al., 2019; Bratic et al., 2015), leaving plant organelle research behind. In plants, forward genetics tools such as chemical mutagens and irradiation have been utilized to explore various gene functions and to generate beneficial alleles for crop breeding. However, their focus of mutagenesis has been the nuclear genome. Here, we aimed to develop plant organelle‐genome‐specific random mutagenesis as a forward genetics approach to efficiently detect organelle genome mutants and to agriculturally improve the organelle genomes. In this study, we successfully achieved random mutagenesis biased toward C‐to‐T point mutations in Arabidopsis organelle genomes using the “WHY2‐CD mutator.”

The C‐to‐T substitutions introduced by the WHY2‐CD mutator appeared specific to the organelle genome targeted by their N‐terminal signal peptides (Figure 3a). This is in contrast with chemicals or irradiation, which induce mutations across both nuclear and organelle genomes. Another point is whether structural alterations occurred in the organelle genomes. Copy number increases in the two specific repeat regions of the mitochondrial genome were observed in one of the E. mtGSVG‐introduced T1 plants (Figure S5A). On the other hand, no obvious structural change in the plastid genome was detected from our short‐read NGS data. This can be compared with msh1 mutants, which accumulate point mutations and sometimes cause structural changes in both plastids and mitochondria (Broz et al., 2025; Martínez‐Zapater et al., 1992; Wu et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2011). Thus, according to the data provided in this study, the WHY2‐CD mutator is a suitable tool for at least plastid‐specific, C‐to‐T‐substitution‐focused forward genetics. When using the mutator for mitochondrial mutagenesis, copy‐number variation and potential structural changes should be carefully assessed. The introduced mutations, however, were not entirely random but showed a certain bias (Figures 2d and 3b,c; Figure S5B). The preference for T at the −1 position of the substituted C is consistent with DddA characteristics (Mok et al., 2020), yet our data suggest that not all “TC” contexts are equally prone to editing (Figure 2d). Whether this reflects additional DddA sequence preferences or WHY2 binding properties remains unresolved. Nevertheless, useful C‐to‐T variants identified here can be validated through reverse genetics or transferred to related crops using ptp/mitoTALECD (Nakazato et al., 2021, 2022). Indeed, the isolated spm‐resistant mutant carried an identical mutation at the position previously introduced by base editing (Figure 7b), highlighting that although discovered in reverse order, such horizontal application is feasible.

To establish heritable and stable mutant lines, it is desirable that introduced mutations reach homoplasmy. We observed cases where heteroplasmic or chimeric mutations in T1 plants of B. ptCWC170 and E. ptGSVG fixed to homoplasmy in T2 (Figure 4a). As msh1 mutants show delayed segregation compared with wild‐type (Broz et al., 2022; Broz et al., 2024), mutations introduced by the WHY2‐CD mutator in a wild‐type background are expected to reach homoplasmy more rapidly than msh1‐derived variants (Wu et al., 2020). In three E. ptGSVG T2 null‐segregants, the number of homoplasmic C‐to‐T mutations per individual ranged from 1 to 13 (Figure 5). With such numbers, promising variants could feasibly be reverse‐genetically validated by generating single mutants via targeted base editing. Rapid segregation of heteroplasmic mutations within a generation agrees with previous reports (Broz et al., 2022; Broz et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). However, it should be noted that the DNA extracted from limited tissue may either overrepresent homoplasmic sectors or miss variants entirely (Figures 5 and 6; Figure S7). Likewise, T1 genotypes represent only somatic mosaics and do not necessarily reflect the germline transmitted to all T2 progeny, as demonstrated by genotype differences among inflorescence branches leading to distinct T2 genotypes (Figure 6). Despite these caveats, we show that, at least for the plastid genome, homoplasmic C‐to‐T mutants can be obtained within T2 or T3 generations, comparable to nuclear mutations (Figures 4, 5, 6, 7). For the mitochondrial genome, so far, we have not yet isolated next‐generation‐reproducible, homoplasmic mutants probably because of the low cAFs in the variants detected in the sequenced T1 plants introduced with D. mt1347D or G. mtSepCD_GSVG (Figure 3b). However, it is likely that if a heteroplasmic mutation reaches a higher cAF, homoplasmic mutants can also be fixed within one to two generations (Broz et al., 2022). Furthermore, the successful isolation of spm‐resistant mutants through population screening (Figure 7), identification of a chimeric rpoA knockout mutant among ~30 ptCWC170 T1 plants (Figure 6), and the correspondence between rbcL amino acid substitution (G82E) and pale‐green, slow‐growth phenotypes (Figure 4b,c) collectively highlight the potential to isolate diverse mutants, including those affecting photosynthesis. For example, in a future study, mutant screening based on agriculturally relevant traits such as D1 protein‐affecting herbicide resistance and photosynthetic parameters (PSII efficiency, non‐photochemical quenching, etc.) could be conducted using plastid‐mutagenized T2 lines.

In summary, we demonstrate that (1) the WHY2‐CD mutator introduces C‐to‐T mutations at multiple undefined sites specifically in the targeted organelle genome, (2) homoplasmic mutants can be isolated from T2 onward, and (3) diverse mutants with phenotypic alterations can be obtained through screening. Systematic screening of such populations will advance understanding of organelle genes and regulatory functions, while uncovering agriculturally valuable mutations in traits such as photosynthesis, herbicide resistance, and CMS. Combined with targeted base editing, this approach promises efficient direct engineering of organelle genomes, accelerating the realization of “organelle genome breeding.”

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials & growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana Columbia‐0 (Col‐0), msh1 mutants [msh1‐2; CS3246, Arabidopsis Biological Resource Center (ABRC)], and other transgenic lines were grown at 22°C under long‐day conditions (16 h light, 8 h dark). Col‐0 seeds were sown on 1/2 Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium (pH 5.7) containing 2.3 g/L of MS Plant Salt Mixture (Wako, Osaka, Japan), 500 mg/L of MES, 10 g/L of sucrose, 1 ml/L of Gamborg's Vitamin Solution (Sigma–Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany), and 6 g/L of agar. Medium was supplemented with 125 mg/L Claforan for the transgenic T1 plants. Two‐ to three‐week‐old seedlings were transplanted to Jiffy‐7 (Jiffy Products International, Zwijndrecht, South Holland, The Netherlands) and subsequently subjected to Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation.

Vector construction

The nucleotide sequences encoding CD and UGI coding regions were the same as the ones used in ptpTALECD (Nakazato et al., 2021). The coding regions of WHY2‐CD (wild‐type)‐UGI and the GSVG variant of CD (Mok, Lee, et al., 2022) were synthesized (EurofinsGenomics, Luxembourg, Luxembourg) with codon optimization for A. thaliana. The E1347D variant of CD is an attenuated variant isolated during cloning of wild‐type CD. Attempts to clone Ti plasmids containing full‐length DddA were unsuccessful (Figure S1). For A. CWC188, B. CWC170, F. CWC188_GSVG, G. CWC170_GSVG, in order to avoid the cleavage of the native WHY2 organelle‐targeting signal, thereby potentially causing separation and inactivation of the mutator, the N‐terminal 50 or 68 amino acids of WHY2 were removed based on cleavage site predictions generated by TargetP 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TargetP‐2.0/) (Figure S11). The entry vectors for C. SepCD were cloned by In‐Fusion reaction using ptpTALECD entry vectors. The entry vectors for D. E1347D and E. GSVG were cloned by In‐Fusion reaction using synthetic WHY2‐CD (wild‐type)‐UGI. The entry vectors for H. SepCD_GSVG were cloned by In‐Fusion reaction using the C. SepCD entry vector. The reading frames in the assembled first and third entry vectors and the second entry vector described below were transferred into the Ti plasmid by multi‐LR reaction with LR Clonase II Plus (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The backbone of destination vector was pK7WG2 (Karimi et al., 2002). RPS5Apro cloned from pKAMA‐ITACHI1_1R (Tsutsui & Higashiyama, 2017) was integrated as a promoter (Arimura et al., 2020; Choi et al., 2021). Either the N‐terminal 36 amino acids of ATPase δ prime subunit (Arimura & Tsutsumi, 2002) as MTP or the N‐terminal 51 amino acids of RecA1 (At1g79050) (Nakazato et al., 2021) as PTP were also integrated. Ole1pro::Ole1‐GFP derived from pFAST02 (Shimada et al., 2010) was inserted for selection of transformed seeds. The second entry vector harboring Arabidopsis heat‐shock protein terminator, RPS5Apro and MTP/PTP was used for Ti plasmid construction of C. SepCD and H. SepCD_GSVG. Single‐LR reaction was performed to construct D. E1347D and E. GSVG. Ti plasmids of A. CWC188, B. CWC170, F. CWC188_GSVG, G. CWC170_GSVG were constructed by In‐Fusion cloning. No nuclear export signal was added to the constructs.

Plant transformation

Plant transformation methods and selection of transgenic T1 seeds were described previously (Nakazato et al., 2021). Each of the vectors was introduced into A. tumefaciens strain C58C1 and subsequently introduced into Col‐0 by floral dipping (Clough & Bent, 1998). Transgenic T1 seeds were selected based on seed GFP fluorescence.

Plant phenotyping

Arabidopsis phenotypes were observed at 9 or 14 DAS. The phenotypes were visually assessed and subsequently captured by digital cameras, OM‐D E‐M5 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) or α6400 (SONY, Tokyo, Japan).

Plant genotyping

Crude DNA extraction was performed using an emerging true leaf or cotyledon of the seedlings at 14–21 DAS, incubated in DNA extraction buffer (100 mm Tris HCl & 5 mm EDTA) at 98°C for 15 min. PCRs were performed using the primers listed in Table S4 with KOD One PCR Master Mix or Quick Taq HS DyeMix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). Sanger sequencing was performed by EurofinsGenomics or FASMAC, and the results were analyzed by Geneious Prime 2025.02 (Biomatters, Auckland, New Zealand).

NGS analysis

Total DNAs were extracted from three to four rosette leaves of 3–4‐week‐old plants (except for the samples marked with * in Data Set S2) using the DNeasy Plant Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Venlo, Limburg, The Netherlands) or the Isospin Plant DNA (NIPPON GENE, Tokyo, Japan) with standard protocols. For analysis shown in Figure 3a, paired‐end libraries using TruSeq DNA PCR‐Free (350) (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and sequencing of 4 Gb/sample using NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) platform were carried out by PAGS (https://www.genome‐sci.jp). For analyses shown in Figure 3b and below, paired‐end libraries using Nextera DNA Flex Library Prep Kit (Illumina) and sequencing of 4 Gb/sample using NovaSeq6000 or NovaSeq X (Illumina) platform were performed by Macrogen Japan.

Paired‐end 150‐bp reads were trimmed by fastp v0.23.4 (Chen et al., 2018) and then mapped to the reference genomes by BWA v2.2.1 (Li & Durbin, 2009). Inadequately mapped reads with mapping identities ≤97% or alignment coverage rates ≤80% or supplementary flags were filtered out by samtools v1.20 (Li & Durbin, 2009; Nakazato et al., 2022). SNPs were called by samtools mpileup (‐uf ‐d 50 000 ‐L 2000 for organelle genomes, ‐uf ‐d 1000 ‐L 2000 for chromosome 1) and bcftools (v1.20) call (‐m ‐A ‐P 0.1) SNPs with allele frequency (AF) meeting the following criteria: (1) AF in the same position of the wild‐type sample was below the threshold (mitochondria: 0.5%, plastids: 0.5%, chromosome 1: 10%), and (2) sample AF correction by the wild‐type sample (cAF) was over the threshold (mitochondria: 5%, plastids: 1%, chromosome 1: 20%) were counted. Furthermore, SNPs on chromosome 1 were counted only when their read depths were ≥15 and ≤50 (Figure S12). The reference genomes used were: BK010421.1 for mitochondrial genome (Sloan et al., 2018), AP000423.1 for plastid genome (Sato et al., 1999), CP002684.1 for chromosome 1 (Theologis et al., 2000), CP002685.1 for chromosome 2 (Lin et al., 1999), CP002686.1 for chromosome 3 (European Union Chromosome 3 Arabidopsis Genome Sequencing Consortium, The Institute for Genomic Research, and Kazusa DNA Research Institute, 2000), CP002687.1 for chromosome 4 (Mayer et al., 1999), and CP002688.1 for chromosome 5 (Kazusa DNA Research Institute, 2000).

Spectinomycin screening

Six independent T3 populations, each originated from 21 randomly selected, regardless of whether they were GFP‐positive or GFP‐negative, T2 plants derived from one T1 individual, were subjected to spm screening. For each population, 21 T2 plants were grown and their seeds were harvested in bulk (Figure S13). Approximately 250 μl of seeds per T3 population (about 7500 seeds) were sown on 1/2 MS medium supplemented with 20 mg/L spm. As a control, a null‐segregant T4 mutant population generated by targeted plastid genome editing, harboring the homoplasmic mutation conferring spm resistance (plastid C102026T; Nakazato et al., 2021), and Col‐0 were used. Three msh1 mutant F4 populations and an EMS mutant M2 population were also tested.

Image processing

Plant images were captured by digital cameras, OM‐D E‐M5 (Olympus) or α6400 (SONY). Gel images were captured by ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). They were processed with Adobe Photoshop 2025 (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA). Figures and tables were generated using Adobe Photoshop 2025, Adobe Illustrator 2025 (Adobe), Microsoft Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, Washington, USA), R 4.4.0, and Python 3.11.
