Treatment of Bile Duct Transection Injury in an Integrated System
Authors: Hyunjee V Kwak, Amber L Chang, Madeline Ryu, George Kazantsev, Austin L Spitzer, Ching-Kuo Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLaparoscopic cholecystectomy is one of the most commonly performed surgical procedures in the United States. Introduction

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is among the most commonly performed general surgical procedures, with more than 750,000 operations conducted annually in the United States to treat symptomatic gallstone disease. 1 Despite its widespread use, iatrogenic bile duct injury (BDI) remains its most serious complication. Although the reported incidence of BDI is relatively low at 0.2% to 0.4%, such injuries can have a profound long-term impact on a patient’s quality of life and impose substantial financial burdens on health care systems. 2–4

Several factors increase the likelihood of BDI, including male sex, morbid obesity, and acute cholecystitis, 5 all of which make the procedure more technically challenging. Adopting specific intraoperative strategies can help reduce this likelihood. Techniques such as achieving the Critical View of Safety (CVS) or performing a subtotal fenestrating (subtotal cholecystectomy) are well-established methods for minimizing injury to the common bile duct (CBD). 6,7

Intraoperative cholangiography (IOC) remains an essential tool for delineating biliary anatomy and identifying anatomical variations. Emerging techniques, such as fluorescence cholangiography using indocyanine green (ICG), offer alternative approaches for visualization (Figure 1). Use of these imaging modalities may help lower the incidence of BDI and facilitate earlier intraoperative recognition of injuries, thereby reducing the time to definitive repair. 8,9

BDI severity is commonly classified using the Strasberg modification 10 of the Bismuth classification, 11 which categorizes injuries based on anatomical location. Although the level of injury is important, concomitant vascular injuries substantially influence the timing and complexity of repair. Vascular injuries occur in 10% to 47% of BDI cases, most often involving the right hepatic artery 12,13 (Figure 2).

Computed tomography scan showing atrophy of the right liver after ligation of the right hepatic artery and transection of the common bile duct with a right liver biloma.

The optimal timing of hepaticojejunostomy after BDI remains controversial. Early repair requires prompt detection, absence of biliary sepsis, and access to hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) surgeons. When performed by experienced HPB surgeons, outcomes of early and delayed repairs appear comparable. 14,15

This retrospective study aims to assess the impact of time to injury recognition and the time to repair within an integrated health care system that provides daily coverage by HPB surgeons.

Methods

The study population consisted of members of Kaiser Permanente Northern California, a large integrated health care system that provides comprehensive inpatient and outpatient care through 21 major medical centers and serves approximately 4.5 million patients. This system utilizes a unified electronic medical record (EMR) that captures all hospitalizations, diagnoses, and procedures, offering a robust and centralized data source for research. The annual volume of hepatopancreatobiliary (HPB) surgery across the 4 Centers of Excellence within Kaiser Permanente Northern California is approximately 500 cases. One center performs roughly 250 HPB operations each year, while the remaining 3 centers each average about 100 cases annually. All complex biliary procedures are centralized at these sites to ensure consistent expertise. Additionally, an HPB surgeon is available daily for telephone or video consultation to support emergency HPB presentations, including BDI, ampullary perforations, and postoperative HPB complications. Outside-network referrals are reserved for highly complex cases and require discussion during a weekly HPB surgeon review that includes representatives from each site; no outside referrals were approved during the study period.

The authors conducted a retrospective cohort study including all patients treated for bile duct transection at Kaiser Permanente Northern California between August 2019 and November 2025. All subsequent bile duct repairs were performed by experienced HPB surgeons at 1 of Kaiser Permanente Northern California’s 4 designated Centers of Excellence. Data were extracted from the EMR and included demographic and clinicopathologic variables related to BDI and repair. Analyses were limited to descriptive statistics.

This study was approved by the Kaiser Permanente Northern California Institutional Review Board. Individual patient consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Between August 2019 and November 2025, 25 patients with Strasberg E1–E5 BDIs requiring surgical repair were identified. Strasberg A–D injuries were excluded, as these are typically managed successfully with endoscopic or percutaneous interventions. During the same period, a total of 60,743 laparoscopic cholecystectomies were performed across the Kaiser Permanente Northern California network, corresponding to an overall BDI incidence of 0.041%. Of these procedures, 54,500 were performed using traditional laparoscopy, 3451 were robotic-assisted cholecystectomies, 2445 included intraoperative cholangiography, and 347 were conducted as subtotal cholecystectomies.

The cohort had a mean age of 61 years (range: 29–83 years), with 46% aged ≥ 70 years. Thirteen patients (52%) were male and 12 (48%) were female. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 27 kg/m² (range: 18–42). Indications for cholecystectomy included cholecystitis in 22 patients (88%), Mirizzi syndrome in 2 patients (8%), and cholangitis in 1 patient (4%). Mean gallbladder wall thickness was 4.5 mm (maximum: 10 mm), mean gallstone diameter was 1.9 cm (maximum: 5.5 cm), and the CBD diameter averaged 5.1 mm (range: 2–13 mm) (Table 1).

BDI was identified intraoperatively in 17 patients (68%). The longest interval from injury to diagnosis was 6 days in a single patient. Three patients underwent immediate repair during the index operation following prompt interhospital communication between the operating surgeon and HPB specialists. 11 patients underwent repair within 6 days of injury; these patients were clinically stable with minimal contamination in the porta hepatis. The delayed-repair group included 10 patients, 2 of whom had concurrent right hepatic artery injuries and one with segmental portal vein thrombosis (Table 2). Remaining delayed cases were characterized by severe inflammation, clinical instability, or uncontrolled biliary drainage, precluding early reconstruction. Once the BDI was recognized, HPB surgeons were consulted immediately, with 68% of consultations occurring intraoperatively. After consultation, HPB surgeons assumed direct care and proceeded with immediate operative repair when feasible and when operating resources were available at the same facility. All patients underwent arterial-phase CT imaging to evaluate for vascular injury. Depending on imaging findings, patient stability, and operating room availability, patients were either transferred postoperatively to a Center of Excellence or, alternatively, an HPB surgeon traveled to the local facility to perform the repair. Repairs performed at a Center of Excellence routinely involved 2 experienced HPB surgeons to assess the level of injury and vascular anatomy prior to hepaticojejunostomy.

E2 and E3 injuries accounted for 80% of cases and represented “high” BDIs requiring complex reconstruction, most commonly hepaticojejunostomy, performed over a biliary stent or following prior percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (PTC). Both patients with right hepatic artery injury had E3 lesions. The single E5 injury was associated with segmental portal vein thrombosis requiring anticoagulation.

Mean clinical follow-up was 39 months (range: 1–73 months). Two patients were readmitted for infected PTC drains; both were managed with drain exchange and a short course of antibiotics. One patient developed an anastomotic stricture successfully treated with endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography dilation and remained stricture-free at 1 year. Two patients required incisional hernia repair. No postoperative bile leaks occurred after reconstruction, and all surgical drains were removed before discharge.

Discussion

The incidence of BDI within the integrated health care system has remained stable at approximately 0.043%, consistent with prior findings by Chuang et al 4 and the current series of 25 patients. This persistence occurred despite extensive initiatives aimed at improving operative safety and technique. Following the 2020 Society of American Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons (SAGES) guidelines emphasizing attainment of the CVS during laparoscopic cholecystectomy, 10,16,17 mandatory educational programs were implemented across Northern California to standardize best practices. Additional strategies included increased use of subtotal or fenestrated cholecystectomy, top-down dissection, and intraoperative cholangiography in cases where CVS was difficult to achieve. 6 Despite these measures, the incidence of BDI has remained unchanged at approximately 1 per 2500 laparoscopic cholecystectomies, underscoring the inherent complexity of preventing this complication even with advanced techniques and structured surgeon education. We continue to advocate for obtaining CVS whenever possible, given its low associated likelihood. 18

In this series, 68% of BDIs were identified intraoperatively, while 16% were diagnosed more than three days postoperatively. Early recognition facilitated timely repair in patients without concomitant vascular injury, as confirmed by preoperative CT angiography. This integrated system ensures the availability of senior surgeons for intraoperative consultation at most medical centers; in all 17 cases of intraoperative injury, a second senior surgeon was called to assist. 3

The optimal timing of bile duct repair remains debated. Repairs are typically classified as early (< 2 weeks), intermediate (2–6 weeks), and late (> 6 weeks). A recent meta-analysis demonstrated substantially increased morbidity associated with intermediate repairs, recommending that reconstruction between 2 and 6 weeks be avoided. 19 Schreuder et al 20 analyzed 1821 patients across 15 studies and found decreased biliary strictures with repairs performed after 9 weeks, further advising against intermediate repairs. Alternatively, Wang et al 21 favored delayed repair but noted that early referral to experienced HPB surgeons was a key determinant of success. Likewise, Giuliante et al 22 concluded that timing alone is not an independent predictor of outcome; rather, surgeon expertise, patient condition (including cholangitis and nutritional status), and vascular injury play critical roles.

In this cohort, 15 patients (60%) underwent early repair, while the remainder underwent late reconstruction, with a median interval of 13 weeks for the late group. Late repairs were primarily necessitated by right hepatic artery ligation, segmental portal vein thrombosis, uncontrolled biliary sepsis, or substantial comorbidities. These findings align with meta-analytic evidence favoring early repair when feasible, as delayed reconstruction is associated with increased technical complexity and higher complication likelihood. 19–22 The authors’ institutional preference is early repair in clinically stable patients without vascular compromise or biliary infection.

During a mean follow-up of 39 months, 1 patient (4%) developed a biliary stricture approximately 1 year after Roux-en-Y hepaticojejunostomy. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) was negative for ductal dilation; the stricture was successfully treated with double-balloon endoscopic dilation, and the patient remained asymptomatic with regular liver function tests and MRCP at 1 year postintervention. The remaining 24 patients have not developed anastomotic strictures during follow-up, confirmed by serial liver function tests and annual MRCPs. One patient died of cardiac causes 2.5 years after repair.

Stilling et al 23 reported a 30% stricture rate with a median onset of 12 months (range, 2–141), with 71% occurring within 2 years. Other studies report stricture rates of 10% to 20%, with median onset between 11 and 30 months. 24 A meta-analysis identified concomitant vascular injury, postoperative bile leaks, and repair by nonspecialist surgeons as factors increasing stricture likelihood after hepaticojejunostomy. 25

Two patients (8%) in this series had concomitant right hepatic artery injury. Both underwent delayed repair at 101 and 491 days post-injury after multiple readmissions and outpatient procedures to control biliary sepsis. Both underwent Hepp-Couinaud hepaticojejunostomy without liver resection. Reported incidence of vascular injury at the time of BDI ranges from 12% to 67%. 26–28 The hepatic artery plays a critical role in bile duct perfusion; its ligation increases ischemic likelihood to the liver and bile duct, predisposing to abscess, bile leaks, strictures, or even liver resection. 29 Concomitant arterial injury has been identified as the only independent factor associated with loss of anastomotic patency. 30

Conclusion

In this large, integrated health care system, the incidence of BDI following laparoscopic cholecystectomy remained stable at approximately 0.04%, despite widespread adoption of safety initiatives and adherence to SAGES guidelines. Early recognition of BDI, facilitated by intraoperative vigilance and access to senior surgical consultation, enabled timely repair in most cases. The authors’ experience supports early reconstruction in clinically stable patients without vascular compromise or biliary sepsis, as delayed repair is often necessitated by complex factors such as concomitant vascular injury and uncontrolled infection.

Although short-term outcomes were favorable, with a low stricture rate and no postoperative bile leaks, the presence of vascular injury remains a critical determinant of repair complexity and long-term success. These findings underscore the importance of structured intraoperative strategies, early diagnosis, and referral to experienced HPB surgeons to optimize outcomes. Future research should focus on refining preventive measures and evaluating long-term functional outcomes beyond the current follow-up period.
