Adopting common data elements for the National Trauma Research Repository: the results of a prehospital Delphi survey
Authors: Nathan James White, Cynthia Lizette Villarreal, Eileen M Bulger, Mirinda Ann Gormley, Jennifer Margaret Gurney, James Hill Homes, Mary J Homer, N Clay Mann, Matthew D Neal, Craig Douglas Newgard, Susan E Rowell, William Arthur Teeter, Olga Volk, Suresh Agarwal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTo support the development of the National Trauma Research Repository (NTRR), a multidisciplinary workgroup focused on prehospital trauma care used a consensus-driven approach to review established data elements and recommend basic common data elements (CDEs) for inclusion in the NTRR data dictionary.MethodsA 13-member workgroup of military and civilian trauma researchers and data scientists located and reviewed databases, codebooks, data collection forms, and published articles for data elements relevant to prehospital trauma care. Identified data elements were reviewed in a three-round Delphi survey and during monthly workgroup meetings. Consensus during the Delphi survey was determined with an 80% agreement threshold.ResultsTwenty-one sources were reviewed for prehospital data elements. Following three rounds and workgroup discussions, 52 elements (84%) reached consensus for inclusion, two elements (3%) were excluded, and eight elements (13%) did not reach consensus.DiscussionThe Delphi process proved effective in achieving expert consensus on basic CDEs for prehospital trauma care research. The resulting standardized basic CDEs will improve data harmonization and support consistent data collection in the NTRR. These CDEs represent an initial framework and serve as a foundational starting point for prehospital data collection within the NTRR. As researchers use the NTRR, the list of CDEs will grow and evolve to meet the needs of the trauma research community.

Introduction

Trauma remains a leading cause of death and disability across all age groups in the USA, presenting a persistent public health challenge with significant societal and economic impacts.1 Trauma research remains hindered by a lack of harmonized data elements that enable meaningful comparison and aggregation of findings across studies and institutions.2 The generalizability and reproducibility of trauma research, particularly within the field of prehospital trauma care, are limited by the variability in how data are defined, collected, and reported. Prehospital data collection is difficult due to the diversity of geography, demographics, resources, and patient populations.3

As a key strategy for promoting data harmonization across research studies, common data elements (CDEs) are standardized, precisely defined variables intended to be consistently collected and used across studies within a given field. CDEs facilitate data sharing, reduce duplication of effort, and enhance the ability to conduct large-scale analyses and meta-analyses.4 The adoption of CDEs within trauma research can enable investigators to build more comprehensive, interoperable datasets that better support scientific discovery, policy development, and improvements in patient care. The Delphi method is a well-established, systematic approach for achieving consensus among a panel of experts, making it particularly suitable for selecting CDEs. This iterative process allows for anonymous input, controlled feedback, and statistical aggregation of group responses, which helps mitigate the influence of dominant individuals and supports balanced decision-making.

The National Trauma Research Repository (NTRR) was established to serve as a centralized platform for storing, sharing, and curating trauma research data. As part of its development, the NTRR convened a series of expert workgroups to identify basic CDEs in specific study environments or phases of care. The workgroups used the Delphi method for selecting CDEs, as this is a well-established, systematic approach for achieving consensus among a panel of experts.

The NTRR Prehospital Workgroup was tasked with identifying and prioritizing basic CDEs relevant to prehospital trauma care research. Collecting prehospital data is essential for trauma studies, as it enables a comprehensive understanding of the patient’s vitals, disposition, injury details, incident details, interventions, and medical history. Such data are crucial for identifying risk factors, informing prevention strategies, optimizing trauma care systems, and guiding resource allocation. By capturing variables such as mechanism of injury, type of destination, and intervention procedure, researchers can track trends, evaluate interventions, and improve clinical protocols. Furthermore, improvements in the prehospital phase of trauma care, where potentially preventable deaths are greatest, will ultimately contribute substantially to reducing injury-related morbidity and mortality.

This manuscript describes the systematic approach undertaken by the NTRR Prehospital Workgroup to identify “basic” prehospital data elements, defined as data elements recommended to be collected by all prehospital trauma studies and suitable for inclusion in the NTRR. Using a structured Delphi consensus process, the workgroup comprising a diverse panel of subject matter experts from the trauma and prehospital environments reviewed, refined, and prioritized candidate basic CDEs. The goal of this process was to develop a consensus-driven set of data elements that would enhance the quality, consistency, and utility of prehospital trauma care research conducted or shared via the NTRR.

Methods

The Executive Steering Committee and CDE Steering Committee recommended subject matter experts for the NTRR Prehospital Workgroup and several other workgroups in different environments of care. The selection and membership of these two steering committees are described in previous manuscripts.5 The Coalition for National Trauma Research (CNTR) gathered information on all those recommended to serve, including work environment, medical subspecialty, research experience, geographic location, and military experience, to comprise workgroups with diverse perspectives from across the USA. Following guidance from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) regarding effective Delphi groups, we sought to form workgroups of roughly a dozen members each.

Thirteen subject matter experts served on the Prehospital Workgroup. Of these, 11 participated in the Delphi survey. The multidisciplinary workgroup included experts in civilian and military trauma research and data scientists. Workgroup members were representatives of university-based trauma centers, the National Institutes of Health, the Department of Health and Human Services, the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke, and the Joint Trauma System. Their specialties and areas of expertise included trauma surgery, emergency medicine, pediatrics, burns, emergency medical services, and traumatic brain injuries.

To identify a set of data elements relevant to prehospital trauma care, workgroup members compiled major prehospital studies for review by the CNTR team (see Box 1). The team located and reviewed data dictionaries, codebooks, data collection forms, and/or published articles resulting from the recommended studies. These sources were selected to reflect current practices and standards in data collection within the field. From these resources, we extracted individual data elements and conducted a frequency analysis to determine which variables were most commonly used across studies. The most frequently collected data elements were selected for inclusion in the initial Delphi survey (online supplemental 1). The process for researching existing definitions and permissible values (PVs) for each of the data elements has been described previously.5 Participant engagement, respondent burden, timeline, and feasibility were all considered when setting the parameters for the number of data elements to include in the survey. To ensure consistency and promote alignment with existing standards, definitions for each data element were gathered from established repositories. Definitions were selected following a pre-defined hierarchy of precedence.5

CNTR developed expertise in configuring, running, and analyzing online Delphi surveys through its development of the National Trauma Research Action Plan.4 That project engaged more than 400 multidisciplinary stakeholders participating in 11 different Delphi panels to identify research gaps and high-priority research questions that should direct extramural research support in the future. Thus, we built on our familiarity with Calibrum’s Surveylet platform (https://calibrum.com) to design a similar approach on the basic CDEs for the NTRR data dictionary.

We created a Delphi survey using Calibrum’s Surveylet application, populating it with the data elements previously identified, along with their definitions and PVs, following the order of precedence. The workgroup proceeded through three rounds of consideration in the online Delphi survey (May–August 2024), where they rated whether an element should be included using the response options of “Include,” “Exclude,” or “No Opinion.” While the Delphi survey was initially designed to incorporate a traditional 5-point Likert scale, the NTRR Executive Steering Committee recommended an abbreviated three-response approach to reduce participant cognitive load and avoid regression to the mean. During round 1, workgroup members could anonymously (1) rate whether an element should be included in the NTRR, (2) rank the existing definitions/PVs of the data element in order of preference for adoption (rank 1 being most preferred), (3) comment on their rationale regarding the inclusion of a data element or preferred definition, and (4) recommend additional data elements and/or PVs for inclusion. For a data element to reach consensus for inclusion in the NTRR, an 80% agreement threshold was set. Definition selection was based on the definition receiving the lowest mean ranking (ie, closest to 1). Data elements that reached consensus were hidden in subsequent rounds so that workgroup members considered only those elements not reaching consensus. Email reminders were also sent periodically throughout the entire process to encourage the timely completion of each round. In rounds 2 and 3, respondents saw the current consensus statistics for each item, read comments made by others, and had the option of changing their rating. Eleven workgroup members were available to participate in the Delphi survey. A total of 11 (100%) workgroup members participated in round 1, nine (82%) in round 2, and nine (82%) in round 3.

Throughout the Delphi process, the workgroup met monthly via teleconference to discuss outstanding items for review, including (1) comments left by reviewers, (2) data elements with tied rankings on their definitions and PVs, (3) creation of definitions/PVs for data elements that did not exist within the repositories in the order of precedence, (4) data elements selected for inclusion by more than one NTRR workgroup, and (5) adoption of form structures to more effectively collect specific data element groupings. All responses from the Delphi were anonymous when shared with the group; however, members were identifiable when providing feedback during the teleconference meetings. Consensus decisions made during these meetings informed revisions to the Delphi survey and the final selection of CDEs for inclusion. On completion, results were shared with the NTRR CDE Steering Committee for final approval.

Results

Twenty sources were reviewed for prehospital data elements (Box 1), from which 48 elements were included in the first round of the Delphi survey.626 After the first round and addition of workgroup recommendations, there were 52 data elements in the Delphi. On completion of the three Delphi rounds and post-Delphi workgroup meetings, and addition of “Other” data elements (Box 2) to capture instances where “Other, specify” was a PV were added, 42 (81%) data elements reached consensus for inclusion, two (4%) reached consensus for exclusion (online supplemental supplemental 2), and eight (15%) did not reach consensus (online supplemental supplemental 3). Additionally, after discussions on determining the best way to collect the most comprehensive data, seven data elements were added, and an additional 10 “Other” elements were also included (figures1 2 and online supplemental supplemental 4).

Discussion

The systematic approach used by the workgroup successfully identified a set of basic CDEs to support trauma prehospital research within the NTRR. Through a rigorous review of existing resources and a structured Delphi consensus process, we reached consensus on most of the candidate data elements. The high rate of inclusion (81%) suggests strong alignment among experts regarding fundamental variables necessary for prehospital trauma care studies. The 15% of elements that did not reach consensus highlight areas where further clarification or development may be needed, either due to differences in current practice, lack of standard definitions, or evolving research priorities. For example, the Automatic Crash Notification incident airbag deployed was a data element that did not meet inclusion. This data element was excluded because it would only apply to motor vehicle crashes and not all prehospital studies.

The following data elements reached consensus; however, as presented in the Delphi survey, they were not main concept CDEs in their simplest forms: Incident State, Incident County, Incident City, and Incident Zip Code. To minimize the number of CDEs and discourage the creation of future county, state, city, and zip code data elements, these elements were converted into the main concepts of Address County Name, Address City Name, Address State Code, Address State Other Text, and ZIP Partial Code and ZIP Code. These are all existing NIH CDEs and will be incorporated into form structures to collect the “incident” context. The main concept CDEs will be incorporated into the Federal Interagency Traumatic Brain Injury Research Informatics System (FITBIR) Injury History Form structure.

The workgroup further clarified that intervention procedure date/time was retained as a general data element intended to capture procedures performed in the prehospital setting that do not have their own dedicated CDEs. Consensus was to retain airway device placement as a distinct data element aside from other procedures. Administering a medication would not align with the definition of “procedure” and therefore remained its own data element. Furthermore, the workgroup included blood given as a nominal data element to enable efficient data mining/cohort screening applications for users. The inclusion of the circulation support type while redundant, the consensus was that both variables are needed for proper database management and utility for end users.

Data element definitions were adopted from FITBIR. As data elements are used, there may be a need to refine definitions and PVs. In order to make adjustments to the definitions and PVs, the NTRR CDE Steering Committee would propose edits and provide them to the NIH CDE curation team who would review a proposed change.

The Delphi process of selecting CDEs is not without its limitations. The process reflects the perspectives of the participants involved; therefore, the studies and projects reviewed for data elements were collected based on workgroup members’ knowledge and previous experience. Thus, some essential studies may have been overlooked. Additionally, the decision to limit the Delphi survey to approximately 65 data elements, while practical, may have excluded less frequently used but essential variables. Also, many of the sources included were projects conducted in the USA. Therefore, the recommendations provided may not apply to researchers and studies conducted outside of the USA. Finally, an intentional effort was made to have each basic CDE workgroup be comprised of roughly a dozen participants; thus, since this group was small (n=11), we are cognizant of the potential for biased opinions.

The success of this effort reinforces the importance of using a Delphi process for developing CDEs. The iterative survey rounds, combined with structured expert discussion, allowed for refinement of both the data elements themselves and their associated definitions and PVs. Specifically, monthly workgroup meetings played a critical role in resolving instances where more than one data element for a specific topic reached consensus.

The selection of prehospital CDEs (figure 3) presented here represents an initial framework, intended to serve as a foundational starting point for data collection within the NTRR. These CDEs were developed as part of a broader NTRR effort to harmonize data across the continuum of trauma care. Parallel workgroups addressing epidemiology, acute hospital, outcomes and rehabilitation, and social determinants of health data elements have similarly focused on standardization to support interoperability across datasets. Aligning these efforts will facilitate the integration of patient data as individuals move from the prehospital setting through subsequent phases of care, thereby enabling cross-level investigations across the spectrum of care. Mode of data collection in real time by EMS agencies and trauma systems is a top priority but must be determined by individual agencies and is outside the scope of this work. As researchers design, conduct studies, and contribute their data to the NTRR, the list of CDEs is expected to expand. New data elements may be proposed based on emerging research needs, novel methodologies, or gaps identified through real-world use. This iterative process ensures that the basic CDEs remain responsive to the needs of the trauma research community, supporting both standardization and innovation over time.
