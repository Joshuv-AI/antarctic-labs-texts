MicroRNAs and SGLT2 inhibitors in cardiovascular disease: Shared pathways and emerging roles
Authors: Anna Bjarvin Bhaskaran, Antoine Sakr, Desislava Doycheva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
MicroRNAs are non-coding RNA sequences capable of transport outside the cell to regulate essential cellular processes. They are able to induce structural changes implicated in prevalent cardiovascular conditions such as heart failure, atherosclerosis and myocardial ischemia. SGLT2 inhibitors are newer anti-diabetic drugs used in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular disease, which modulate metabolic pathways shared with microRNAs. Direct evidence linking a mechanistic relationship between microRNAs and SGLT2 inhibitors is limited. However, they share multiple pathways affecting key cellular processes implicated in cardiovascular disease progression, such as fibrosis and inflammation. This mini-review explores the effects of microRNAs on cardiac disease, with potential implications in cardiovascular disease management. Shared mechanisms between microRNAs and SGLT2 inhibitors and subsequent impact on heart failure development will be emphasized throughout. Certain microRNAs implicated in cardiovascular disease could potentially serve as diagnostic and therapeutic targets. Future use of microRNA-based technologies may include biomarker panels of circulating microRNAs and exosomal delivery of therapeutic microRNAs. However, these technologies are not fully developed, as the majority of relevant studies are preclinical. Possible future applications of biomarker panels in precision medicine include diagnosis and risk stratification of patients for cardiac disease progression, monitoring response to heart failure therapies, and identification of microRNA targets for modulation.

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are non-coding RNA sequences which can affect protein expression through epigenetic modifications. This often occurs intracellularly through suppression of gene expression via mRNA degradation and translational repression, or activation of transcription and translation [1]. Extracellular miRNAs are present in the blood, urine and saliva; affording their utilization as biomarkers of physiologic and pathologic processes [2]. Extracellular miRNAs exist in several forms, including exosome-associated miRNAs, other extracellular vesicle-associated miRNAs, and non-vesicular protein or lipoprotein-bound miRNAs [3]. Because many published studies quantify miRNAs directly from serum or plasma without isolating exosomes, findings from those studies should be interpreted as evidence of circulating miRNAs rather than exosomal miRNAs specifically. Exosomes are a subset of extracellular vesicles (EVs) containing cellular constituents, such as miRNAs, which are released to recipient cells via fusion and endocytosis (Fig. 1). Though the precise impact of extracellular miRNAs on recipient cells is currently unclear, a growing body of evidence suggests they are not mere byproducts. Instead, their roles as potential biologic regulators and intercellular messengers provoke clinical curiosity [1]. Clinical applications of exosomes are currently under investigation for their ability to deliver metabolically active constituents to cellular targets, including miRNAs and medications [4].Fig. 1Biogenesis of exosomal microRNAs and release into circulation.Fig. 1

The effects of individual miRNAs can vary across different cell types and biologic contexts, and certain miRNAs may have opposing effects across cell types and disease states. Thus, the effects of miRNAs are highly context-dependent [5]. Circulating miRNAs may serve as biomarkers for many cardiovascular diseases, as elevated levels of specific miRNAs are associated with heart failure (HF), myocardial ischemia, infarction, and more [2] (Fig. 2). Changes in miRNA expression levels may mediate protective mechanisms in heart failure patients through alterations in angiogenesis, fibrosis and inflammation [6]. Additionally, modulation of miRNA levels in heart failure patients may positively impact cardiac remodeling. Moreover, miRNA expression levels may predict response to advanced heart failure therapies such as angiotensin receptor/neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) medications, cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), or left bundle branch pacing. Higher expression levels of certain miRNAs correlated with improvement in heart failure parameters such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and six-minute walk test [7], [8], [9]. However, expression of some miRNAs has been associated with pathologic changes implicated in heart failure; including worsening fibrosis, hypertrophy and inflammation [7], [10]. Regardless of whether their effects are protective or deleterious, expression levels of circulating miRNAs could be quantified using biomarker panels. This could eventually assist in the diagnosis of cardiac disease and help guide risk stratification, following further validation. Therapeutic alteration of miRNA expression levels could theoretically augment medical and interventional therapies for heart failure, particularly in poor responders. However, there are currently no FDA approved uses of microRNAs in the clinical setting in the United States [11], including biomarker panels. Thus, the use of microRNAs in CVD management is still under investigation.Fig. 2MicroRNAs, SGLT2 inhibitors and their roles in CVD.Fig. 2

Although the use of miRNAs in clinical practice has not been established, research on their potential roles in the management of CVD holds promise. Circulating miRNAs display diagnostic and prognostic potential as biomarkers for metabolic and vascular conditions [12]. In heart failure patients who underwent CRT, expression levels of certain miRNAs negatively correlated with endothelial dysfunction, resulting in better outcomes such as improved LVEF. Additionally, miRNA expression levels may predict response to CRT therapy [8]. However, further research is needed to determine whether miRNAs could constitute viable diagnostic and therapeutic targets. SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) are a gold-standard therapy in the treatment of heart failure with significant improvements in cardiovascular outcomes [13]. SGLT2i target the proximal tubules of the kidneys to lower blood sugar by promoting glucose excretion and inhibiting its reabsorption to effectively treat diabetes. Their hypoglycemic effect is hypothesized to delay vascular aging by ameliorating deleterious effects of glucose on vascular cells, and may improve cardiovascular outcomes through reduction of inflammation and adverse cardiac remodeling [8], [13]. Their beneficial metabolic effects are purportedly mediated through mechanisms shared with cardioprotective miRNAs (detailed further in Section 3.2). While shared mechanisms raise the possibility of a direct relationship between miRNAs and SGLT2i, there is currently little evidence to suggest that one exists. Proposed uses of circulating miRNA biomarker panels include risk stratification of heart failure patients based on their predicted response to medical and interventional therapies, including use of SGLT2i. It is the goal of this review to explore how miRNAs function as metabolic messengers in CVD, while analyzing their potential interplay with metabolic modulators such as SGLT2i.

MiRNAs and cell signaling- markers of disease or vehicles of cellular regeneration?

Dual roles of miRNAs-cardioprotective and pathological effects

Multiple studies have examined the role of both intracellular and extracellular microRNAs in cellular crosstalk across diverse cell types in the cardiovascular system. This communication may facilitate physiologic roles in maintaining homeostasis, or contribute to aberrant pathophysiologic processes [14]. Certain miRNAs were associated with various cardioprotective mechanisms, including anti-apoptotic, pro-angiogenic, and anti-fibrotic effects (Table 1; Fig. 3). Beneficial anti-apoptotic effects have been observed with miR-19a [15], miR-30d [16], miR-146a [17], miR-199a-5p [18], miR-423-3p, miR-494 [19] and miR-1246 [20] in cardiomyocytes in response to myocardial injury. MiR-30e prevented atherosclerosis via promotion of apoptosis in vascular smooth muscle cells [21]. Increased angiogenesis was displayed in mouse models treated with miR-1246 [20] and miR-210 [22]. MiRNAs associated with decreased cardiac fibrosis included miR-22 [23], miR-378 [24], miR-19a-3p and 19b-3p [25], and miR-29b and − 455 [26]. Inhibition of vascular remodeling occurred with miR-146a [27] and miR-155-5p [28]. MiR-21-5p mediated calcium handling in L-type calcium channels in human mesenchymal stem cells; increased activity correlated positively with cardiac contractility [29]. Reduced cellular hypertrophy was observed with miR-146a [17], miR-223 [30] and miR-373 [31]. Anti-inflammatory effects were exhibited by miR-146a [17], miR-223-3p [32], miR-9 [33] and miR-30c-5p [34]. Notably, several studies delivered miRNAs to target cells through exosomes or EVs (including miR-19a, -30d, -423-3p, -1246, -210, -29b, -378, -455, -21-5p, -155-5p and -199a-5p), while others were detected intracellularly. Collectively, these findings may suggest that expression of both intracellular and extracellular miRNAs is capable of mediating protective cardiovascular effects. Limitations surrounding data using different disease models and miRNA types will be discussed further in 5.3, 6. Pro-fibrotic, anti-apoptotic, anti-angiogenic and pro-inflammatory effects of certain miRNAs may promote pathophysiologic changes at the cellular level (Table 2). Serum-derived exosomes may induce endothelial dysfunction and contribute to the progression of coronary artery disease (CAD) by increasing the production of inflammatory factors [35]. Specific miRNAs associated with pro-inflammatory effects include miR-155 in fibroblasts [36] and miR-34a in vascular smooth muscle cells [37], [38]. MiRNAs which promote cardiac hypertrophy include miR-21-3p [39]; while miR-144-3p [40] and miR-208b [41] promoted cardiac fibrosis, and miR-132 impaired cardiac contractility [42]. Anti-angiogenic miRNAs have been associated with endothelial dysfunction; examples include miR-185 [43], miR-199a-3p, 199a-5p [44], miR-200a-3p [45] and miR-320 [46]. Pro-apoptotic effects in vascular smooth muscle cells were observed with miR-199a-5p [47]; anti-apoptotic effects occurred in cardiomyocytes in another study [18]. MiR-21-3p induced pathologic cardiomyocyte hypertrophy [39], however exosomal delivery of miR-21-3p to cardiomyocytes enhanced cardiac contractility through SERCA activation [29]. Contradictory findings across distinct studies support the existence of differential effects of miRNAs, which may be dependent on cell type and disease context. Altogether, deleterious effects of miRNAs may occur through multiple mechanisms across studies using different miRNA types. While some studies utilized exosomal miRNAs (miR-185, -200a-3p, -320, -21-3p and -155), others examined intracellular expression levels (miR-199a-3p and -5p, -132, -144-3p and -34a). Levels of miR-208b were assessed both intracellularly and in the serum. MicroRNAs as biomarkers of disease

MiRNA expression levels can be detected in circulation, and changes in expression levels may correlate with certain disease states. Upregulation of miR-193b-5p, miR-193a-5p and miR-125a-3p was observed in patients with atherosclerosis [48]. Elevated serum levels of miR-133b, -208b, and -499 were found in animal models of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury [49]. Patients who survived MI exhibited significantly increased serum levels of miR-183 [50] and miR-208b and -499, and levels of all three miRNAs correlated with the level of myocardial injury [51]. In patients with heart failure after acute MI, elevated serum levels of miR-192, −194 and -34a were observed [52]. Serum expression levels of miR-125b-5p were increased in heart failure patients, while expression levels of miR-92 and miR-21-5p were decreased [53]. In hypertensive rat models, upregulation of miR-208b-3p, miR-132-3p, miR-31a-5p and miR-222-3p was observed [54], however expression levels of the same miRNAs were reversed in rat models of cardiac hypertrophy after treatment with ipragliflozin [55]. Altered expression levels of circulating miRNAs found in certain pathological states correlated with effects observed in other studies, including studies using exosomal miRNAs. Exosomal miR-21-5p increased cardiac contractility via SERCA activation [29], and circulating levels of miR-21-5p were decreased in a cohort of HF patients [53]. Further characterization of intracellular and extracellular miRNAs identified in molecular studies should be investigated using human patients to determine their value as circulating biomarkers for cardiac disease.

Exosomal applications of microRNAs

Exosomes can be synthetically engineered from a variety of stem cell types, including embryonic stem cells (ESCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), heart-derived stem cells and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs). They have been associated with various protective effects via the delivery of genetic material and proteins, attracting interest in their potential clinical utility as therapeutic messengers. iPSCs are created through transfection of four specific genes conferring pluripotency, yielding reprogrammed cells that can enhance cellular repair and regeneration when transplanted into the cardiomyocytes of mice [56], [57]. Studies using rat models to explore the role of mesenchymal stem cell EVs after acute myocardial infarction (MI) showed enhanced blood flow and preservation of cardiac pump function after ischemia upon injection of EVs [58]. Similarly, treatment with human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell (hucMSC)-derived exosomes was associated with preservation of cardiac systolic function after acute MI in a rat model. This was attributed to reduction of apoptosis and promotion of angiogenesis [59]. Thus, exosomal delivery of miRNAs may confer cardioprotective benefits to their cellular targets. Furthermore, they can be artificially created through use of stem cell vectors. However, research investigating the roles and benefits of exosomes in CVD treatment is minimal, and much of the existing literature surrounding exosomes focuses on cancer.

MiRNAs and SGLT2 inhibitors-relationship and roles in CVD management

SGLT2 inhibitors in the treatment of cardiometabolic disorders

SGLT2i are valuable anti-diabetic drugs used to treat diabetes, heart failure, and CVD. Commonly used medications include empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin and ipragliflozin. Adverse effects of SGLT2i therapy may include genitourinary infection due to glycosuria, hypotension, and euglycemic ketoacidosis [60], [61]. While their full therapeutic potential is likely under-explored, their protective effects include delayed vascular aging [13], reduced body weight and body fat [62], inhibition of cardiac fibrosis [63], and decreased secretion of proinflammatory chemokines in myocardial tissue [64]. SGLT2i therapy is associated with reduced mortality from cardiovascular events. Long-term therapy decreases the progression of renal disease and the number of hospitalizations for heart failure [65]. As of 2022, SGLT2i treatment has remained a cornerstone of guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure. SGLT2i therapy is a class 1 recommendation for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), and constitutes a class 2a recommendation for treatment of HF with mildly reduced or preserved EF [66]. While the cardiovascular and metabolic benefits of SGLT2i have been established, the mechanisms behind their effects are still being uncovered.

SGLT2i treatment is considered first-line therapy for patients with diabetes and chronic kidney disease (CKD) to prevent the progression of renal dysfunction and CVD [67]. However, recent evidence suggests that their cardioprotective benefits may transcend their antidiabetic effects. In both diabetic and non-diabetic patients who underwent heart transplants from non-diabetic donors, SGLT2i therapy mitigated myocardial insulin resistance and toxic lipid accumulation via the JunD/PPAR-γ pathway, mitigating progression of cardiac dysfunction [68]. SGLT2 overexpression has been observed in both diabetic and non-diabetic cardiomyocytes of failing hearts; and dysglycemia further drives SGLT2 expression. Furthermore, inhibition of SGLT2 reduced cardiovascular mortality in heart failure patients regardless of whether they had diabetes [69]. While SGLT2 inhibition may reduce glucose uptake in failing cardiomyocytes, it likely also diverts cellular metabolism to alternative processes such as ketosis. This may prevent deleterious structural changes caused by the uncoupling of glycolysis. Independent of hyperglycemia, SGLT2 overexpression contributes to cardiomyocyte dysfunction through promotion of toxic lipid accumulation, apoptosis, inflammation and oxidative stress [69], [70], [71]. Furthermore, SGLT2 inhibition may help regulate cardiac dysautonomia. In a cohort study of diabetic patients with recurrent vasovagal syncope, patients treated with SGLT2i had fewer syncopal episodes and lower variability in heart rate in comparison to untreated patients. This benefit was likely mediated through reduction in serum levels of catecholamines and inflammatory markers, as measured by serum norepinephrine and CRP [72]. Thus, SGLT2 inhibitors may stabilize cardiac dysautonomia by balancing vagal and sympathetic inputs, expanding their known anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects to the neurohormonal axis. Systemically, SGLT2 inhibition likely acts on inflammatory pathways to influence adipogenesis by decreasing adipocyte size and differentiation. On breast tissue obtained from healthy premenopausal women, higher fat content was associated with increased SGLT2 expression, correlating with higher inflammatory cytokine production and reduced myocardial performance [73]. In epicardial adipocytes, SGLT2 expression was associated with increased differentiation of adipocytes, which was subsequently inhibited by treatment with empagliflozin [74]. Similarly, treatment with canagliflozin reduced adipogenesis and inflammation in diabetic patients, resulting in weight loss [75]. In pre-diabetic patients referred for coronary artery bypass grafting, examination of pericoronary fat revealed higher levels of inflammatory cytokines and overexpression of SGLT2 compared to non-diabetics. These patients experienced higher rates of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [76]. Moreover, diabetic patients treated with SGLT2i after myocardial infarction demonstrated reductions in inflammation and infarct size, independent of glycemic control [77]. SGLT2i therapy also resulted in lower incidence of in-stent restenosis after percutaneous coronary intervention [78]. In diabetic patients with multivessel, non-obstructive coronary stenosis, treatment with SGLT2i was associated with decreased coronary plaque inflammation, leading to increased fibrous cap thickness and reduced incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events. Additionally, patients in the treatment group demonstrated reductions in BMI, glycemia and serum inflammatory markers [79]. Collectively, these findings suggest that SGLT2 inhibition exerts cardioprotective effects through anti-inflammatory, metabolic and neurohormonal mechanisms.

Shared pathways between microRNAs and SGLT2 inhibitors

SGLT2i and miRNAs modulate similar cellular mechanisms with cardiovascular and metabolic consequences (Table 3; Fig. 4). At present, causal evidence linking SGLT2i therapy to cardioprotective modulation of miRNAs is sparse. The NLRP3 inflammasome is a well-characterized pathway that activates an innate inflammatory response through IL-1β and IL-18 [80]. The anti-inflammatory effects of SGLT2i therapy may occur through attenuation of reactive oxygen species via reduction of TNFα-induced endothelial inflammation [81]. SGLT2i therapy may cause inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome, defending cardiomyocytes from myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury [82]. Exosomal miR-223-3p [32] and intracellular miR-9 [33] and miR-30c-5p [34] were associated with suppression of the NLRP3 inflammasome. The PPAR-γ pathway, associated with myocardial lipid accumulation and insulin resistance, may be attenuated by SGLT2 inhibition in cardiomyocytes [68] and by intracellular miR-27a [83] and -130 in adipocytes [84], resulting in decreased adipogenesis. Interestingly, intracellular miR-613 inhibited cholesterol efflux from macrophages, also via the PPAR-γ pathway, contributing to the development of atherosclerosis [85]. SGLT2i treatment can activate the AMPK pathway and increase autophagy in cardiomyocytes; it also reduces inflammation through inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines [86]. However, circulating miR-199a-5p increased apoptosis and decreased autophagy, causing endothelial damage via downregulation of AMPK [47]. The MMP9 pathway is a known player in cardiac remodeling; activation of MMP9 enhances contractile dysfunction via endothelial-myocyte uncoupling [87]. Down-regulation of MMP9 and NHE1 with SGLT2i treatment likely confers cardioprotective benefits through inhibition of apoptosis [88]. Exosomal miR-29b and -455 similarly mediated favorable effects indirectly through downregulation of the MMP9 pathway, resulting in attenuation of deleterious cardiac remodeling and fibrosis [26]. SOD is an antioxidant enzyme which reduces cardiac apoptosis via reduced oxidative stress and inflammation [89]. IL-1β is an inflammatory mediator downstream of the SOD pathway which may be related to the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes [90]. Diabetic hearts display decreased SOD levels and increased IL-1β; SGLT2i treatment has been shown to upregulate SOD with propagation of antioxidant effects [91]. MiR-199a-3p and 199a-5p may also target the SOD pathway via inhibition of SOD, promoting endothelial cell dysfunction intracellularly [44]. Furthermore, SGLT2i therapy attenuates vascular remodeling by impeding the proliferation and migration of vascular smooth muscle cells [92]. EV-associated miR-155-5p similarly inhibited vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation [28]. MicroRNAs target metabolic pathways shared with SGLT2 inhibitors, with cardioprotective or deleterious effects.

Data examining the direct effects of SGLT2i therapy on miRNA levels are scarce. In one study observing the influence of SGLT2i therapy on circulating miRNA levels, treatment with dapagliflozin caused upregulation of miR-30e-5p and downregulation of miR-199a-3p in comparison to levels prior to treatment [93]. Treatment with both empagliflozin and dapagliflozin showed increased intracellular expression of miR-146a, and decreased cardiac remodeling and reduced vascular aging was observed on histopathologic examination of cardiomyocytes [91]. HF patients exhibiting higher expression levels of miR-30 showed improvement in cardiac function and were more responsive to LBB-pacing; similar effects were observed with SGLT2i treatment [9]. Furthermore, alterations of exosomal miRNA expression with positive impacts on cardiovascular remodeling has been observed with other heart failure treatments, including ARNI therapy [10]. Given the existence of shared mechanisms between SGLT2i and miRNAs, it is plausible that SGLT2 inhibition directly alters miRNA levels to exert its effects. However, a causal relationship has not been established. Studies examining expression levels of intracellular miRNAs in SGLT2i treated hearts demonstrate miRNAs that have yet to be characterized as favorable, neutral or pathological [55]. Further research should determine if SGLT2i therapy directly alters expression levels of miRNAs in cardiometabolic pathways, while seeking to identify new miRNA players in CVD.

Clinical implications of microRNAs in heart failure management

Changes in circulating miRNA levels and associations with heart failure outcomes have been assessed in large cohort studies. Elevated levels of miR-1254 and miR-1306-5p correlated with higher rates of death and hospitalization in HF patients, while miR-208a-3p, miR-622 and miR-133b were associated with mortality alone [94]. HF patients treated with cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRTd) were assessed for endothelial dysfunction (ED). These patients had higher rates of hospitalizations and cardiac deaths in comparison to those without ED. Higher serum expression of miR-130a-5p and ARNI therapy were associated with lower inflammation and ED. MiR-130a-5p expression also significantly predicted rate of CRTd response and risk of hospitalization [8]. In another study, HF patients who responded poorly to CRTd were treated with ARNI medications; these patients had increased levels of miR-18 and miR-145, and decreased levels of miR-181 and inflammatory markers in comparison to the non-treatment group. These findings correlated with improvement in cardiac remodeling parameters such as LVEF and the six-minute walk test [7]. Similarly, HF patients treated with left bundle branch (LBB) pacing exhibited significantly lower levels of miR-26, -29, -30, -92 and -145. Lower miR-30 levels were significantly correlated with worsening LVEF and six-minute walk test performance, and predicted response to LBB-pacing. LBB-pacing responders with higher levels of miR-30 expression exhibited cardiac pump improvement; as did treatment with SGLT2i and ARNI medications [9]. If validated in prospective clinical studies, biomarker panels detecting circulating miRNAs may be helpful in predicting response to advanced HF therapies. Furthermore, risk stratification using biomarker data, if supported by future evidence, could allow clinicians to select therapies with greater insight and reduce adverse outcomes from ineffective treatments. Additionally, miRNA inhibition used in several studies [10], [40] may represent a novel strategy in the treatment of CVD through mitigation of deleterious miRNA pathways. Such therapies are currently under study; oligonucleotide CDR132L ameliorated adverse cardiac remodeling in pig MI models through inhibition of miR-132 [95]. It progressed to a phase 1b clinical trial in 28 human patients, which was tolerated without significant adverse effects [96]. Thus, therapeutic repression of miRNAs using inhibitors may help clinicians achieve positive cardiovascular outcomes in patients with aberrant levels of deleterious miRNAs. If developed, precision medicine strategies such as biomarker panels and miRNA modulation could augment existing heart failure therapies. However, it is important to recognize that all miRNA-based products are still experimental, with insufficient data to support their clinical use. The limitations and challenges surrounding their development are discussed further in Section 5.

Treatment for cardiovascular disease using exosomal delivery

Exosomes are capable of delivering therapeutic nanoparticles such as drugs and nucleic acids, with a lower risk of immunogenic reactions when compared to other therapies [97]. An advantage with exosomal therapies lies in their capacity to deliver drugs to often impermeable tissues, including areas of inflammation [98]. Exosomal cargo delivered by iPSCs to injured cardiomyocytes in the peri-infarct region can exert beneficial effects by preventing apoptosis, necrosis, inflammation, fibrosis and deleterious remodeling [99]. Moreover, exosomes provide a cell-free vehicle to deliver target molecules such as miRNAs in a stable fashion that protects them from digestion [100]. Exosomes containing 16 miRNAs associated with tissue repair were delivered to cardiovascular progenitor cells and cardiomyocytes. In vivo, the effects of injected exosomes were studied on mice with decreased left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) after MI; mice showed improvement in LVEF after delivery of miRNAs [101]. In another study, mice injected with iPSC EVs exhibited superior improvement in LVEF function, vascularization, and reduction in apoptosis and hypertrophy when compared to iPSC transplantation alone [102]. Exosomes containing 11 upregulated miRNAs isolated from cardiac progenitor cells in hypoxic conditions were delivered to cardiomyocytes after ischemia-reperfusion injury; treatment improved cardiac function and reduced fibrosis [103]. Proposed therapeutic uses of exosomal miRNAs include delivery of protective miRNAs to cellular targets. However, there is a paucity of research supporting the efficacy of exosomal miRNAs in preventing the development of cardiovascular disease. Although stem-cell based exosomal therapies are experimental with no current role in clinical practice, they could represent a promising advancement in precision medicine if successfully developed.

Concerns regarding microRNA based-technologies

Limitations of exosome-based therapies

The feasibility of using exosomes in common clinical settings is currently limited by the lack of cost-efficient, regulated methods of production necessary for their widespread use, storage and distribution. Practical application of exosomal therapies would thus require a standardized, reproducible system of testing and production to regulate their quality and streamline their distribution. The synthesis of exosomes is expensive, technically complex and resource-intensive. This creates difficulties in manufacturing a stable and consistent product on a large scale, capable of maintaining molecular stability during storage [104]. The lack of standardization in isolation and characterization of exosomal therapies diminishes their reproducibility; and there is currently no FDA regulated process of production in the United States to ensure quality control. However, the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles has released several guidelines for the standardization of EV isolation and characterization, most recently in 2023 [105]. Thus, production techniques are currently under development. Although the widespread use of exosomes remains limited by challenges in production, emerging technologies could help overcome these hurdles. Microfluidic isolation is a lightweight system that provides isolation on a single-cell level, representing a highly efficient and sensitive method of separating exosomes [106]. Additionally, the engineering of artificial exosomes is another area of interest that could alleviate difficulties in culturing cells for mass production and maintaining a stable genetic product suitable for storage [107]. Practical applications of artificially created exosomes used novel methods such as electroporation, the use of electrical pulses to overcome the cell membrane barrier. One study used electroporation to successfully deliver miR-126 to cellular models of ischemia, resulting in improved cardiac function [108]. These early advances in the study of exosomal applications represent a growing interest in their development. However, the lack of evidence supporting their use is currently a major barrier to advancement toward clinical trials.

Potential adverse outcomes of exosomal therapies

Delivery challenges of exosomal cargo include immunogenicity, off-target effects and rapid clearance [109]. Theoretical risks of artificially created stem cells include enhanced immunogenicity with synthetically engineered vectors [110], although exosomes are natural EVs which are less immunogenic than synthetic biocarriers, yielding greater biocompatibility. However, off-target effects are an important concern in the delivery of exosomal miRNAs, as they are often involved in multiple signaling pathways with several protein targets. Increased accumulation in off-target regions can also occur due to their negative charge [111]. One major drawback of exosomes is incidental activation of undesirable cellular processes. For this reason, much of the current literature in the practical application of delivering exosomal miRNAs focuses on modifying exosomes to increase their stability and targeting ability [109]. Despite theoretical risks, one advantage of exosomes lies in their versatility as delivery vehicles. Their lipid bilayer structure yields the possibility of loading both hydrophilic and lipophilic drugs, and surface engineering of the exterior of natural exosomes represents a growing field of interest. Studies on exosomes as delivery vehicles focus on various loading strategies for exosomal cargo using chemical, physical and genetic modifications [112]. At the moment, a major barrier to clinical use of exosomes is the lack of evidence supporting their safety and efficacy, which is discussed further in Section 6.

Comparison of study findings across cardiovascular disease models

The generalizability of the findings from molecular miRNA studies (refer to Section 2) is limited by the use of animal and in-vitro models. Use of murine models was common, although one study utilized a pig model of MI. The remainder of the studies in Section 2 were performed in-vitro using either rodent or human cells. Multiple studies examining the effects of identical miRNAs showed varying levels of consistency across model types. Concerning diabetic rat and mouse models of miR-146a, both demonstrated favorable cardiac effects. However, these occurred via different mechanisms in different cell types. Favorable versus pathologic cardiac outcomes were reliably demonstrated by rodent models in several cases. In-vitro studies of miR-34a using human vascular smooth muscle cells and rat cardiomyocytes both demonstrated deleterious effects. Elevated serum levels of miR-34a were observed as a possible biomarker of heart failure in acute MI patients [52]. MiR-21-5p demonstrated beneficial pro-contractile effects when studied in-vitro using human cells [29], and circulating levels of miR-21-5p were decreased in heart failure patients [53]. Similarly, miR-208b demonstrated pro-fibrotic effects in rat cardiomyocytes in-vitro [41]. As a biomarker, it was elevated in the serum of acute MI patients [51], and in animal models of ischemia [49]. However, other studies yielded conflicting findings between human and animal models. MiR-199a-5p had beneficial anti-apoptotic effects on cardiomyocytes using rat MI models [18], but demonstrated pathologic, pro-apoptotic effects on human vascular smooth muscle cells used as in-vitro models of HTN [47]. In endothelial cells from mouse models of HTN, miR-199a-5p caused deleterious anti-angiogenic effects [44], highlighting variations in molecular mechanisms and outcomes across model types and disease contexts. Additionally, the majority of murine studies utilized exosomal miRNAs, while in-vitro studies did not consistently use exosomes. These discrepancies may reflect differences in species, disease context, miRNA type or cell-specific effects, and warrant further investigation.

Rodent models are widely used in the study of cardiovascular diseases due to their ease and cost efficiency, and the circumvention of ethical challenges surrounding the use of human subjects. Advantages of rodent models include their reproductive capability, simplicity in detecting the disease of interest, and the ability to easily simulate multiple cardiovascular conditions in a short time frame. Additionally, rodent models of hereditary diseases allow uniform phenotyping and favorable controllability. Atherosclerosis and CAD are commonly modeled by ApoE and LDL receptor negative mice, while HTN is reproduced by spontaneously hypertensive rat models. Surgical ligation of the LAD in rats can mimic MI; while HF is modeled through transverse aortic constriction surgery. The major downsides of surgical models are their time-consuming and technically difficult nature [113]. While rodent models can provide cost-effective, reliable and reproducible data on cardiac disease, the differences between gene expression profiles and the anatomy of cardiomyocytes in mice versus humans are substantial [114]. However, both murine and in-vitro models provide an essential starting point for determining molecular players in metabolic pathways of interest. While in-vitro studies may circumvent issues surrounding differential embryonic development and gene expression profiles between rodent and human hearts, significant limitations exist. Firstly, the complex physiologic environment of a cell inside a living organ is not well-replicated by in-vitro studies. However, this can be partially overcome by ensuring the cell model chosen expresses the mechanism in question, and the disturbance of stressors can be quantitatively determined. Additionally, the reproducibility of the results of in-vitro studies can be negatively affected by factors such as cell line contamination, instrument performance and accuracy of pipetting [115]. Despite disadvantages with in-vitro models, they represent a fast and easily reproducible approach to elucidate the molecular mechanisms behind miRNAs involved in cardiovascular function.

Future perspectives and limitations of current evidence

Standardization of miRNA biomarker panels to measure circulating levels of miRNAs implicated in heart failure, ischemia or other cardiac pathologies may represent a viable diagnostic tool in CVD management. If developed, biomarker panels could identify abnormalities in circulating miRNA expression levels. Theoretically, protective miRNAs could be delivered to cardiovascular cells using EVs, and deleterious miRNAs could be targeted for inhibition. Additionally, levels of circulating miRNAs could theoretically be quantified in HF patients and used to predict response to advanced HF therapies. Several barriers stand in the way of developing miRNA biomarker panels, including the lack of direct evidence supporting their use. Much of the research characterizing miRNAs in CVD involve animal or in-vitro models, which limits the generalizability of these studies. Additionally, the effects of distinct types of miRNAs may vary, thus data from studies with different miRNA types may not be interchangeable. For instance, studies of exosomal miRNAs may not correlate well to biomarker studies, which often measure circulating miRNAs. Moreover, most of the mechanistic evidence supporting the role of miRNAs in CVD remains derived from animal and cellular studies, whereas human data are largely associative. Concerning exosomal therapies, the lack of clinical trials in human subjects currently precludes their use. More phase 1 and 2 trials are needed to assess the safety and efficacy of exosomal products in human patients, prior to implementing phase 3 trials. The obstacles concerning the technical development of exosomes were previously discussed in Section 5.

Molecular effects of miRNAs previously characterized with in-vitro or murine models should be correlated with biomarker studies in CVD patients to fully elucidate their clinical effects. Furthermore, research examining miRNA expression levels in patients treated with SGLT2i could determine if a mechanistic relationship between them exists. Currently, most available evidence demonstrates shared downstream pathways rather than direct regulation. Moreover, SGLT2i therapy exerts its effects beyond the cardiovascular system (evidenced by their influence on adipogenesis and the neurohormonal axis, discussed in Section 3.1). It may be worthwhile to explore the relationship between miRNA expression levels and SGLT2i treatment in other disease contexts as well. Given their anti-inflammatory effects and influence on adipocyte size and differentiation, obesity provides a logical example of a disease that might be influenced by both SGLT2i therapy and miRNA expression. The idea of therapeutic repression of deleterious miRNAs via oligonucleotide inhibition was briefly mentioned in Section 3.3. This potential treatment also has limited evidence to support its efficacy, mandating further research. Also, sufficient generalizability of data from studies using circulating miRNA levels to those using exosomal therapies has not been proven, and should be studied further. Successful development of exosomes capable of delivering therapeutic cargo likely relies on the continued advancement of technologies such as surface engineering, production of artificial exosomes, and microfluidic isolation. Lastly, therapeutic delivery of miRNAs using exosomes would require further investigation into the safety and efficacy of exosomal therapies.

Conclusion

Evidence directly supporting the diagnostic and therapeutic use of miRNAs is limited, precluding their use in clinical settings. Circulating microRNAs may serve as potential biomarkers to identify cardiac pathology, provide risk of disease progression, and predict response to heart failure therapies, if validated by future studies. Despite the paucity of evidence exploring a causal relationship between miRNAs and SGLT2 inhibitors, the existence of their shared metabolic outcomes warrants further scrutiny. Although preclinical studies suggest exosomal microRNAs may eventually be used as therapeutic delivery vehicles, there is currently little data surrounding their safety and efficacy. The use of exosomal products in the management of cardiovascular diseases is not well-studied, and relies mainly on data from animal studies. Stem cell-derived EVs are costly and difficult to produce, and their adverse effects are not well characterized. Until barriers to the development and use of miRNA-based technologies can be overcome, their use in clinical settings is impractical. Further research is necessary to support the integration of miRNA products into CVD management, and to explore their potential cardiovascular benefits.
