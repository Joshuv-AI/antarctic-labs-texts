Attitudes, Beliefs and Perceptions of Medication Prescribing for Older First Nations Peoples: A Scoping Review
Authors: Nicola Franks, Karl McDermott, Karen Carlisle, Sarah Russell, Valda Wallace, Edward Strivens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo explore what is known about the attitudes, beliefs and perceptions of appropriate prescribing from the view of older First Nations Peoples (aged 45 years or older, residing in the community or residential care), substitute decision‐makers and health‐care professionals working with First Nations Peoples.MethodsThe scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and PRISMA‐ScR. Databases and grey literature sources were searched with no limitations from inception until 12 May 2025. Titles and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers, with full‐text assessment for inclusion and data extraction undertaken by two independent reviewers. Study characteristics were described descriptively.ResultsFive articles were included for analysis. First Nations Peoples represented included Māori, American Indians and Australian Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander Peoples. Health‐care workers represented included Aboriginal Health Workers, general practitioners and nurses. From the older First Nations People's perspective, themes regarding traditional medicine use, lack of medication information provision and concerns about adverse effects were identified. Dominant themes from health‐care workers included medication information, compliance to medications and continuity of care.ConclusionsOlder First Nations Peoples expressed a desire for more medication information and culturally appropriate care. Included studies indicated most health‐care workers supported interventions to increase knowledge and understanding of medications for First Nations Peoples. Unfortunately, paternalistic attitudes remain, which limit the provision of information and prevent a trusted partnership from forming.Keywords: aged, culturally competent care, drug utilisation, First Nations Peoples

Introduction

Prescribing medications for older people is a complex process involving multiple factors that prescribers must carefully assess [1]. Key aspects include interactions, both drug–drug and drug–disease, altered pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics along with potential medication management issues [1, 2]. In addition to clinical concerns, understanding a patient's attitudes, beliefs and perceptions on medication use are essential factors to consider in the prescribing processes, and one that can be overlooked amidst the broader complexities of prescribing. Health care workers' attitudes, beliefs and perceptions on medication use can also impact prescribing, which may be more profound for patients of culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds such as First Nations Peoples [3].

Appropriate prescribing in the older person using quantitative methods is common. This approach provides analysis on overprescribing, underprescribing, polypharmacy and the use of potentially inappropriate medications. Exploring the concept of appropriate prescribing via qualitative methods is less common. Qualitative studies in this area have mainly explored attitudes of patients, carers and health‐care professionals towards deprescribing, and not necessarily a holistic view of appropriate prescribing for the older person [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. A qualitative approach to appropriate prescribing allows exploration of patient factors that affect prescribing [10]. Appropriate prescribing can be linked to three domains: the patient, prescriber and environment [10]. With regard to the patient, evidence‐based therapies should be used; however, the patient can influence prescribing decisions [10]. The prescriber draws on their own clinical experience and attitudes to make the final prescribing decision; furthermore, poor communication between prescribers can result in inappropriate prescribing [10, 11]. Third, the environment in which the prescriber operates can affect prescribing decisions, for example an acute care ward vs. a residential care home [10]. Additional contextual factors such as staffing and available resources can affect prescribing decisions [10]. As expressed by Spinewine et al., the final prescribing decision is an ‘interaction of these three factors and in some cases, the family’ [10, p. 180].

Qualitative research plays a crucial role in health‐care research, allowing exploration of the social, cultural and emotional dimensions of healthcare that qualitative research cannot capture [12]. Qualitative techniques allow for a deep insight into patients' lived experiences, beliefs and perceptions, providing a way for health‐care professionals to understand patient challenges and support patient‐centred care [12]. Additionally, qualitative research can enhance communication and relationships within health care and provide a framework for addressing ethical and cultural aspects of care by giving a voice to diverse patient populations [13, 14].

Research has shown that attitudes, beliefs and perceptions on medication use significantly impact a person's engagement with the health care system and medication use [15, 16, 17]. Cultural differences can further impact this engagement [3, 18]. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples are the Indigenous peoples of Australia [19]. For Australian Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples, as with many First Nations populations, the concept of ‘health’ is holistic [20]. Health encompasses all aspects of the individual including social, emotional and cultural well‐being of the whole community, not just physical well‐being [20, 21]. Over the last decade, considerable improvements in health have been made to reduce the gap between Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander People's and non‐Indigenous Australians' health. Despite improvements, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples still experience an increased burden of disease and a lower life expectancy than non‐Indigenous Australians [19]. This ‘gap’ in health equity is shared with other First Nations populations [22]. This is due to an interaction of a number of factors including social dislocation, economic disadvantage and social determinants as ongoing ramifications of European settlement [19]. In recognition of this, the definition of ‘older’ or ‘geriatric’ for First Nations Peoples is aged 50 years and over, which is the definition supported by the Australian Government [19]. With the aim of working towards cultural safety and health equity, it is imperative that First Nations People's attitudes, beliefs and perceptions on medication use are understood.

The objective of this scoping review was to identify, explore and map literature relating to attitudes, beliefs and perceptions of medication use for older First Nations Peoples, their carers/substitute decision makers as well as health‐care professionals from Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United States of America (USA).

A scoping review was selected as the appropriate method of enquiry to explore, map and summarise the depth and breadth of current literature [23]. Additionally, the scoping review aimed to identify knowledge gaps within this topic.

Review Question

What is known about the attitudes, beliefs and perceptions of appropriate prescribing from the view of First Nations peoples, carers and health care professionals working with First Nations Peoples?

Purpose of Review

This scoping reviewing was undertaken as part of the lead author's Master of Philosophy research, which focussed on ‘appropriate prescribing’ for older First Nations Peoples residing in a rural aged care home. The impetus for the study originated from the aged care home itself, reflecting a locally identified need. The study design and implementation were developed in close collaboration with the local rural community, including First Nations stakeholders, to ensure cultural relevance and appropriateness. Results from this scoping review were fed back to potential First Nations participants as part of their involvement in the research.

The lead author acknowledges their positionality as a non‐Indigenous researcher and has approached this work drawing on the principles of Indigenous research methodologies and community led support. In addition, co‐author Valda Wallace is an Aboriginal woman and identifies as a Gugu Badhun woman (Valley of Lagoons/Ewan Country). Valda Wallace has provided ongoing input for this review and cultural mentoring on the scope of the review and interpretation of the findings. Other members of the authorship team, KC, SR and ES, all have considerable experience in undertaking research with First Nations Peoples and promoting healthy ageing for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples.

Methods

The scoping review was conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews and PRISMA‐ScR [23, 24].

Search Strategy

A preliminary search of MEDLINE, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL) and JBI Evidence Synthesis was conducted, and no current or underway systematic reviews or scoping reviews were identified on the topic. The search strategy aimed to locate published and unpublished studies. The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, was adapted for each database and/or information source (Appendix A). Databases searched include MEDLINE (OVID), CINAHL, Embase, Emcare, Scopus, PsychInfo Net and grey literature sources. Sources of unpublished studies and grey literature included Google Scholar, HealthInfo Net, Lowitja Institute and government resources from inception up to 12 May 2025. The reference list of all included sources of evidence was screened for additional studies. No language or data filters were placed on the search to allow for retrieval of any studies on the topic. Search terms relating to ‘First Nations’, ‘Medication management’, ‘attitude’, ‘polypharmacy’, ‘deprescribing’, ‘inappropriate prescribing’, ‘cultural’, ‘traditional medicine’ and ‘compliance’ were included. Terms relating to appropriate/inappropriate prescribing, compliance and medication management were included as articles of interest may discuss these as an attitude or perception on medication use (Appendix A—Table A1).

The search strategy and search strings were developed in consultation with a senior liaison librarian and the authorship team. Multiple combinations of the search strings were searched to allow for maximum data retrieval.

Data Screening

Identified citations were collated and uploaded into EndNote (version 20), Clarivate then duplicates removed. Citations were retrieved and imported into the JBI System for the Unified Management, Assessment and Review of Information (JBI SUMARI) (JBI, Adelaide, Australia) [25]. Title and abstracts were screened by two independent reviewers (NF and KMc). Potentially relevant sources were retrieved in full. The full text of selected citations was assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers (NF and KMc). Reasons for exclusion of sources of evidence at full text that did not meet the inclusion criteria were recorded and reported in Figure 1. Any disagreements that arose between the reviewers at each stage of the selection process were resolved through discussion, or with a third reviewer (KC).

PRISMA flow diagram of search and selection process.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was performed by two reviewers (NF and KMc). A data extraction tool (Appendix B) was developed by the reviewers for extraction of relevant variables. Articles were summarised by participant type, that is, older First Nations Peoples, substitute decision‐maker and health‐care workers.

Quality Appraisal

A formal quality appraisal of the included articles was not undertaken in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews [23]. However, the quality of the articles are addressed in the discussion of the review. All articles which met the inclusion were included in the review.

Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion

Population

First Nations Peoples (aged 45 years or older), carers and/or substitute decision makers for older First Nations Peoples and health‐care workers (medical doctors, nurses, pharmacists, personal care assistants and Aboriginal health workers).

The inclusion age of 45 years or older was used for ‘older First Nation Peoples’, as previous studies have utilised this age marker, acknowledging the earlier onset of medical conditions, comorbidities and frailty in First Nations Peoples along with Australian Government programs having a lower inclusion age [26, 27, 28].

Concept

Literature that explored attitudes, beliefs and perceptions of appropriate medication use from or for older First Nations Peoples.

Context

Studies with community dwelling participants or those residing in residential care facilities were included. Studies from Australia, the United States of America, Canada and New Zealand were eligible for inclusion. These regions were chosen because of similarities between the countries' development, colonisation and socioeconomic outcomes of their respective First Nations Peoples.

Study Design

Studies of any methodology were eligible for inclusion. No date or language constraints were applied.

Exclusion

Studies were excluded if data relating to First Nations Peoples aged 45 years or older could not be separated. Studies that focus on specific disease states or medication classes were excluded, as the intention of this scoping review was to explore participants' holistic attitudes, beliefs and perceptions of all their medications. Studies conducted in a mental health setting, end of life or palliative care intent were excluded, as patients, substitute decision‐makers and health‐care professionals would have different perspectives and beliefs regarding medication use in these settings.

The rationale for this exclusion was to maintain a holistic lens on medication use and prescribing, rather than a condition‐specific or pharmacological focus. The aim of the review was to understand broader perspectives on prescribing which may be shaped by multiple factors including the patient, the prescriber and the environment; for example, rurality and setting (community compared with residential care).

Results

Search Results

A total of 3755 articles were identified from database searches. Following removal of duplicates, 2785 articles were screened at the title and abstract phase. From this, 75 articles and four articles identified from reference screening were included for full text review. After full‐text review, 74 articles were excluded with five articles undergoing analysis (Figure 1).

Summary of Studies Included in the Review

A total of five articles were included for analysis. Articles for inclusion were from Australia (n = 3), New Zealand (n = 1) and the USA (n = 1). First Nations Peoples represented were Australian Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander (n = 3), American Indian (n = 1) and Māori (n = 1). Health‐care workers represented included Aboriginal health workers (n = 2), general practitioners (n = 2) and nurses (n = 1). The majority of included studies were journal articles from primary data (n = 4), with one book chapter (n = 1). Most of the studies were conducted using qualitative methods (n = 4), with the book chapter reporting qualitative and quantitative data (n = 1). The majority of studies were conducted in a community setting (n = 4), with one study including a mix of community and RACF participants; however, results were not disaggregated based on dwelling type. Older First Nations Peoples: Attitudes, Beliefs and Perceptions on Medication Use

Two studies were included for data extraction that explored patient perceptions from First Nations populations (Table 2): [29, 31] One study from Māori perspectives and the other from American Indian perspectives [29, 31]. Common themes identified across both studies included the use of traditional medicine, lack of information provision for their medications and concerns about adverse effects and cost.

Use of Traditional Medicines

Both studies provided insights into the use of traditional medicines. Participants expressed that they felt they had no choice but to use Western medicines due to the loss of knowledge about traditional medicines. They also highlighted the challenges involved in preserving and practicing their traditional methods [29, 31]. Some participants described using both traditional and Western medicine, along with acknowledging that there is little evidence about potential interactions [29, 31]. One study identified that American Indian Elders are twice as likely as Caucasian Americans to rely on home remedies with the aim of trying to preserve traditions [31].

Medication Information

The study conducted in New Zealand highlighted that whilst Māori participants wanted to know more about their medications, cultural beliefs influenced their ability to seek information, clarification or justification for medication use [29]. Furthermore, if Māori participants did source further information they were less likely to question or challenge the health‐care professional [29]. With respect to provision of information, Māori participants expressed the need for more culturally appropriate information and preferred lengthy verbal discussion compared to the provision of written information [29].

Factors Impacting Medication Use

Medication management and adverse effects were discussed in both studies [29, 31]. Māori participants often raised concerns about adverse effects of medications and the ‘toxic build‐up’ of medications within their bodies [29]. Key challenges for managing medications included lack of understanding regarding the purpose of the medication and how the medication helped with disease management as well as the cost of medicines [29]. These factors result in a perceived lack of need for the medication [29]. Cost was cited as a confounding factor for medication misuse across both studies [29, 31]. Some Māori participants explained they would miss their medications for up to 3 months secondary to cost [29]. Access to medications was an additional factor for medication non‐adherence for American Indian participants [31].

Health Workers: Attitudes, Beliefs and Perceptions on Medication Use

Three studies examining health workers' attitudes, perceptions and beliefs towards appropriate prescribing for First Nations Peoples were included for analysis, all of which were conducted in Australia (Table 2) [18, 30, 32]. Two studies were based in rural Australia and the remaining study involved 11 Aboriginal Health Services in urban, rural and remote locations [18, 30, 32]. Health‐care workers represented included doctors, nurses and Aboriginal health workers. Key themes that emerged were the provision of medication information, compliance to medications and continuity of care.

Medication Information

The literature revealed various perspectives on providing medication education to Aboriginal Peoples. Most nurses and all Aboriginal Health Workers described the benefits of interventions to increase medicines knowledge and empowering Aboriginal Peoples to make medication choices [32]. This article describes the use of Home Medicine Reviews (HMRs) as a tool to increase understanding of medications and their importance and a way of reducing the ‘fear and worries about the unknown’ [32, p. 2]. Paternalistic views were expressed by some non‐Aboriginal health care professionals [32], with comments such as, ‘there's no point filling them with huge amounts of information if they are not going to take the medicines anyway’ [32, p. 2], and ‘In terms of education, which I know is one of the really important parts, I'm not sure. I'm not convinced that those people think it is a high priority and that we have any way of educating these people about their medications’ [32, p. 2]. One healthcare professional claimed Aboriginal patients were ‘uninterested or incapable of learning more information about their medicines’ [32, p. 6]. These paternalistic remarks are in direct contrast to the views expressed by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and Aboriginal Health Workers within the same health service [32]. The majority of patients welcomed interventions to improve their understanding of their medicines.

Factors Impacting Medication Use

From the perspective of health‐care workers, non‐compliance with medications was frequently attributed to a lack of understanding regarding medication, limited family support and sharing of medications [18, 29]. One Aboriginal Health Worker noted ‘[they] don't understand how the medicines work, when they work and they don't take them at the right times or the right way’ [32, p. 2]. Complexity of the language within medication information contributed to a lack of understanding and Aboriginal People felt ‘embarrassed or even ashamed of asking a pharmacist for clarification or advice’ [18, p. 3]. Lack of family support was another reason cited for non‐compliance [18]. This included a belief that the medication taking was not cultural, ‘It's [medication] not cultural to start with; it's not how they would have treated it, had they done back 200 years ago. They wouldn't have popped a pill for feeling sad’ [18, p. 4]. Other identified factors that impacted on medication taking were forgetting, polypharmacy, complex medication regimens and cost [18]. Aboriginal Health Workers observed that sharing of medications was common for Aboriginal people [18]. This practice can result in the prescribed individual receiving less medications and the person given the shared medication not seeking medical care [18].

Continuity of Care

Continuity of care and the application of chronic disease guidelines is another challenge for appropriate prescribing [30]. Within this study, continuity of care referred to the ‘smooth and coordinated flow of health care between different health care settings and health care providers’ [30]. Three key themes were identified: knowledge of the patient, provision of follow‐up care and health literacy of patients [30]. The included GPs expressed that in order for care to be effective, they need to tailor the guidelines to the patient; however, without knowledge of the patient, this is not possible [30]. Frustration was expressed by GPs, as patients often would not return for follow‐up appointments, making chronic disease management fragmented [30]. Low health literacy was attributed to the lack of follow‐up for non‐acute problems [30]. Without continuity of care, doctors felt unable to appropriately implement chronic disease guidelines [30]. Additionally, the high turnover of staff, especially in remote areas, further compromises continuity of care [30].

Discussion

This scoping review synthesised evidence with respect to attitudes, beliefs and perceptions regarding appropriate prescribing and medication use for older First Nations Peoples and health‐care professionals defined in this review as doctors, nurses, pharmacists, Aboriginal health workers and personal care assistants. The five included studies revealed similar overarching themes, such as the need for culturally appropriate care, a lack of information provision, medication management issues and continuity of care.

Although available evidence was limited, the included studies provided valuable insights into patients' and health‐care workers' perceptions on appropriate medication use and prescribing for older First Nations Peoples. As Mort et al. [31] highlights, ‘there is limited information available in this area so it is difficult to draw sweeping conclusions and at best, the clinician should be aware of these potential perceptions’ [31, p. 118].

This scoping review emphasised participants' desire for more information about their medications, and for them to be delivered in a culturally appropriate way. Providing such information has the potential to allay concerns and fears regarding adverse effects, provide an appropriate explanation for the need for the medication and assist with medication management. Non‐adherence has been linked to not understanding the role of medication for disease management. Cultural beliefs also limit First Nations Peoples from asking questions, clarifying their medication regimen and reason for continued medication use, as highlighted in this review [9, 29, 32].

Discontinuity of care coupled with a paternalistic attitude of some non‐Aboriginal health workers has the potential to significantly impact on appropriate prescribing for First Nations Peoples. These condescending views are in direct contrast to the statements given by the patients themselves and trusted health‐care workers, such as the Aboriginal Health Workers. The desire for a better understanding of medications is identified in a study by Swain et al. [9] This study provides in depth data regarding medication use in Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander Peoples [9]. This study was not included for data extraction as there was no specified age of participants; however, it reinforces many of the key elements highlighted by Māori participants from the included studies [29]. With regard to medication use, the study by Swain et al. [9] revealed 70% of participants expressed ‘I don't know why I take them’ [9, p. 218]. The concept of medication information improving adherence was reinforced by a participant who stated, ‘If I know more, I feel more confident and try harder to take my medicines’ [9, p. 219]. Similar to Māori participants, Aboriginal participants did not question their medications as they didn't know what to ask, were too shy or found it ‘shameful to take so many tablets’ [9, p. 218].

Discontinuity of care and its impact on prescribing and health management was explored. Prescribers reported adjusting chronic disease management recommendations due to potential lack of follow up and monitoring. This may result in potentially inappropriate medications and potential prescribing omissions. As outlined by the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare's Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Framework, having a regular primary health‐care provider improves communication, trust and satisfaction levels between patient and provider, resulting in better patient outcomes [21]. However, access to health services varies based on geographical location, with availability of doctors decreasing with remoteness [21]. This has considerable impact on Australian First Nations Peoples, as approximately 34% live in outer regional, remote or very remote locations compared with 9% of non‐Indigenous Australians [36]. This issue is consistent across all included populations, with approximately 20% of Māori peoples living rurally, and Mort et al. also comment on the shortage of doctors for Indian Health Services, especially those in remote areas [31, 37].

Included studies indicated most health‐care workers supported interventions and approaches to increase knowledge and understanding of medications for First Nations Peoples. Unfortunately, paternalistic attitudes are still present, which limits the provision of information and prevents a trusted partnership from forming. Additionally, the rural and remote location of many First Nations Peoples can further compromise appropriate prescribing and medication use due to discontinuity of care.

While this review identified some negative attitudes among non‐Indigenous health workers, it also highlights important opportunities for critical reflection, change and to enhance appropriate prescribing practices. Actively engaging with and genuinely listening to the voices of First Nations Peoples can strengthen prescribers' and health‐care workers' capacity to provide care that is culturally safe and patient centred. Such engagement supports respectful understanding, partnerships and contributes to more appropriate prescribing practices.

Strengths

Currently, this is the only scoping review to map the literature regarding the attitudes, beliefs and perceptions of medication use for First Nations Peoples. Disparity between health‐care workers' views on medication use and those of First Nations Peoples was highlighted in this scoping review.

Limitations

Included studies had small sample sizes, due to the included population and/or geographical study location. The majority of studies were qualitative in nature, limiting application of results to a broader demographic. Health‐care workers' views were drawn from three studies with small participant numbers [18, 30, 32]. These studies were set in rural Australian locations, and therefore, views and opinions cannot necessarily be extrapolated to a broader Australian population or overseas populations. Included studies for First Nations People's perceptions were drawn from Māori and American Indian participants and, therefore, may not be representative of Australian First Nations Peoples [29, 31]. The study by Jones et al. utilised convenience sampling, which could potentially bias results; additionally, the author was known to the participants which may further bias results [30]. The included book by Mort et al. is an older reference and therefore information may be dated [31]. However, themes raised throughout this older reference are consistent with themes from current references.

Conclusions

There is limited research available regarding patient, carer and health‐care workers' attitudes, beliefs and perceptions towards appropriate medication use for First Nations Peoples. Culturally, First Nations Peoples are less likely to seek clarification regarding their medications or question their use. These aspects coupled with paternalistic views and barriers for continuity of care for those living in rural and remote settings can culminate in polypharmacy, inappropriate prescribing, prescribing omissions and/or complex medication regimens resulting in intentional as well as non‐intentional non‐adherence for First Nations Peoples.
