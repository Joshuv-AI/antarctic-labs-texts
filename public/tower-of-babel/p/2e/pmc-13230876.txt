An operational target trial emulation framework for causal inference using electronic health record data
Authors: Yanfei Wang, Yongqiu Li, Tuo Lin, Yi Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTarget trial emulation (TTE) enables causal inference from electronic health record (EHR) data when randomized clinical trials are infeasible, but its validity depends on whether EHR data can support the required trial design components. This review presents an operational framework that distinguishes trial specification from its realization in EHR data and clarifies how healthcare-driven data generation constrains identifiability and when EHR-based TTE can or cannot yield interpretable causal estimates.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are widely regarded as the gold standard for estimating treatment effects in clinical research, but their high cost and operational complexity often limit feasibility for many clinically important questions. As a result, investigators increasingly rely on real-world clinical data to inform treatment decisions in settings where RCTs are impractical or unlikely to reflect routine clinical care. Target trial emulation (TTE) provides a principled framework for emulating RCTs using observational data by requiring investigators to define and adhere to the protocol of a hypothetical RCT (i.e., target trial) before any analysis is conducted. This protocol imposes strong design discipline, such as the explicit definition of eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, follow-up, and outcomes, which reduces common bias in observational studies and supports the causal interpretation of estimated treatment effects1–3. Regulatory agencies, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), increasingly recognize TTE-based analyses as a structured approach for generating real-world evidence when RCTs are not feasible4.

With the emergence of large-scale electronic health record (EHR) networks, EHR data have become a dominant data source for TTE because they capture large, heterogeneous populations, including groups underrepresented in RCTs, as well as rich longitudinal clinical information. These features make EHR data attractive for evaluating treatment effects in routine clinical settings at scale5. However, while TTE is explicitly modeled on RCT designs, EHR data are not generated under a randomized assignment mechanism but instead arise from routine clinical care, billing, and documentation workflows. Observations in EHRs are conditional on healthcare encounters rather than disease onset or prespecified follow-up schedules and are therefore irregular, selectively missing, and closely tied to health status and care-seeking behavior. As a result, many assumptions implicit in the target trial design are violated before any statistical analysis begins. Investigators may carefully specify a target trial and apply advanced causal analytical methods, yet still obtain estimates that are difficult to interpret or fail to align with evidence from RCTs. In such cases, the limitation arises not from the choice of analytical methods, but from how the target trial design is operationalized using EHR data.

Despite broad recognition of these challenges posed by EHR data, existing TTE studies using EHRs have largely emphasized analytic strategies for effect estimation (e.g., propensity score-based weighting approaches). However, a central limitation of EHR-based TTE often arises not from the choice of analytic methods, but from whether core target trial design components can be credibly implemented under EHR data-generating constraints. In this review, we address this gap by examining how data-generating processes shape which target trials can, and cannot, be emulated using EHR data, building on the TTE framework described by Hernán and colleagues1. We introduce an operational framework that distinguishes conceptual trial specification from its realization in EHR data, and describe seven systemic barriers along with commonly used mitigating strategies and their limitations. By reframing TTE as a data-constrained design problem rather than solely an analytic exercise, this framework provides guidance for aligning causal questions with data fitness and for interpreting EHR-based TTE responsibly. This fit-for-purpose emphasis is consistent with recently updated real-world evidence frameworks that foreground data quality, design feasibility, and transparent methodological choices6.

An operational target trial emulation framework for EHR data

The TTE framework described by Hernán and colleagues requires explicit specification of a target trial by defining seven key design components: eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, treatment assignment, the start and end of follow-up, outcomes, causal contrasts, and a data analysis plan1. TTE studies apply this framework by approximating these components using observational data, with the goal of preserving the causal meaning of the target trial without recreating randomization. In EHR-based research, direct implementation of the target trial is rarely possible. Instead, each design component must be operationalized under constraints imposed by the EHR data-generating process, which determines the study population, how follow-up is defined, and the treatment effect that can be identified.

To make these constraints explicit, we distinguish the conceptual specification of the target trial from its operational realization in EHR data. We therefore discuss design components in an operational order reflecting design dependencies, from eligibility and baseline (time zero) definition to treatment strategies and assignment, follow-up, outcome measurement, causal contrast, and analysis. We examine how each component is constrained or distorted in EHR-based studies, along with commonly used strategies for mitigating these limitations (Table 1). This roadmap emphasizes that TTE in EHR data is fundamentally a data-constrained design problem, when implementation requires information not observable in the EHR, the intended estimand is not identifiable and inference necessarily targets a data-defined estimand.

Operational constraints and mitigation strategies across target trial design components in EHR-based studies

Eligibility criteria and cohort definition

Understanding the barrier

TTE requires defining eligibility at a baseline time point that represents the population at risk prior to treatment assignment. In EHR data, this requirement is structurally constrained because eligibility assessment and cohort entry are driven by healthcare utilization rather than by the onset of biological risk. Individuals enter observation only when they seek care, and the timing of this engagement depends on many factors (e.g., disease severity, access to care)7. As a result, eligibility is determined by post-onset processes related to disease progression and clinical risk, rather than capturing the underlying at-risk population8,9. Disease onset therefore frequently precedes the first recorded encounter, and individuals who experience early events or rapid progression before seeking care are systematically unobserved (i.e., left truncation), which induces selection bias. Empirical studies have shown that failure to account for care-driven eligibility can lead to estimates that substantially diverge from RCT results10.

Eligibility-related selection bias is further compounded by explicit or implicit conditioning on observed healthcare engagement. Engagement itself is influenced by underlying disease severity and other determinants of risk11. While left truncation determines who enters observation, conditioning eligibility on utilization induces collider bias at cohort entry, opening non-causal pathways between treatment and outcome12. Importantly, this bias arises before treatment assignment and cannot be resolved through downstream adjustment alone12.

Furthermore, TTE is constrained when trial eligibility criteria cannot be fully operationalized using structured EHR data. RCTs often restrict eligibility based on clinical characteristics such as symptom severity13,14, functional status15, contraindications16, or clinician judgment, many of which are not systematically recorded in structured EHR fields. Consequently, EHR-based TTE studies often rely on simplified operational definitions of eligibility using diagnosis codes, laboratory results, or medication histories as proxies. These approximations may not correspond to the eligibility criteria specified in the target trial protocol, and lead to systematic differences between the emulated cohort and the intended target population and a mismatch between the data identified and the target estimand17.

Mitigating strategies and limitations

Several strategies can partially mitigate eligibility-related barriers in EHR-based TTE. Eligibility should be defined prior to treatment assignment, with time zero anchored at a clinically meaningful and reliably observed decision point, and analyses restricted to treatment initiators within a prespecified window8,9,18. When eligibility-defining variables are incompletely observed, missingness should be treated as part of the selection process rather than as a purely technical issue12,19. Inverse probability of selection weighting (IPSW), constructed using selection models that estimate the probability of being observed, provides a principled approach to addressing care-driven selection bias under explicit assumptions19,20, and linkage to external data sources21, such as claims or clinical registries, can expand the evidence available on prior medical events relevant to eligibility.

These approaches have important limitations. Selection models may be weakly supported by the available data, and sensitivity analyses are therefore essential22. Although IPSW cannot recover disease processes or events that occur prior to the initial healthcare encounter, it can improve the interpretability of the observed baseline risk set. When eligibility depends on information not recorded in structured EHR data, validated proxy measures may partially approximate non-computable eligibility criteria23,24, whereas natural language processing, including large language models, can be leveraged to extract relevant clinical information from unstructured EHR data such as clinical notes25–27. More recent machine learning-based phenotyping28,29 using multimodal EHR data may further reduce eligibility misclassification. However, some aspects of trial eligibility, particularly those based on nuanced clinical judgment, cannot be reconstructed, and full emulation of the target trial is therefore not possible. Transparent reporting of how eligibility criteria are operationalized, together with explicit description of the population to which the estimated effects apply, is therefore essential30.

Baseline definition and time zero alignment

Understanding the barrier

TTE requires alignment of eligibility assessment, treatment assignment, and the start of follow-up at a clearly specified baseline time, or time zero. In EHR-based studies, this alignment is often difficult because baseline is typically anchored to the first recorded diagnosis, prescription, or encounter, which reflects clinical detection and documentation rather than disease onset or treatment decision-making31. As a result, baseline is frequently defined late relative to the underlying biological process, introducing immortal time bias that distorts the baseline risk set30,32. Because individuals must survive event-free until cohort entry, some survival time is guaranteed before follow-up begins, and treatment groups may therefore differ in unobserved prior events. Exposure timing may also be misclassified when treatment initiation is inferred from delayed or incomplete records, further undermining comparability between treatment strategies33.

Mitigating strategies and limitations

Several design-based strategies can partially address misalignment at baseline. Explicit specification of the target trial baseline clarifies the intended time zero32, landmark designs restrict analyses to individuals observable at a fixed time point34, and delayed entry models account for late cohort entry when the entry process can be characterized31. For sustained treatment strategies (i.e., longitudinal treatment regimens), the cloning-censoring-weighting framework can be used35, in which individuals are cloned and assigned at baseline to all compatible treatment strategies, and deviations from the assigned strategy are handled through artificial censoring and weighting. This approach enforces treatment assignment at time zero and eliminates immortal time bias. However, these approaches do not resolve uncertainty in disease onset, delays in clinical detection, or imprecision in treatment timing, and therefore cannot fully restore alignment between biological risk and EHR-observed follow-up. Time zero misalignment, therefore, remains a fundamental limitation of EHR-based TTE.

Treatment strategies and assignment

Understanding the barrier

TTE requires well-specified treatment strategies that define how treatment is initiated and continued over time. However, in routine clinical care, treatments are inherently dynamic. Patients may switch therapies, change doses, interrupt treatment, or discontinue altogether in response to evolving clinical status, side effects, or access constraints17,36,37. Such patterns complicate the definition of treatment strategies corresponding to fixed or sustained interventions in a target trial.

Defining treatment strategies is further challenged by fragmented medication data38. Prescriptions, dispensing records, administration data, and patient-reported use are often fragmented across systems and incompletely captured within a single EHR39. Therefore, observed records may reflect prescriptions rather than actual exposure. Adherence, persistence, and timing of use are rarely directly observed, making it difficult to determine whether individuals truly followed a given strategy. When treatment strategies are ill-defined or poorly measured, the causal contrast of interest becomes ambiguous32.

These challenges in defining treatment strategies directly undermine the identification of treatment assignment at baseline. Treatment initiation may be delayed, incompletely recorded, or inferred retrospectively, making assignment timing ambiguous. Consequently, the treatment groups identified in the data may not correspond to the strategies specified in the target trial. Intention-to-treat (ITT)-like analyses may be feasible when treatment assignment is reliably captured, through a policy or system-level decision (e.g., mandated clinical pathways), even if subsequent adherence is imperfect. In contrast, estimation of per-protocol or sustained treatment effects may introduce bias if longitudinal treatment behavior is not accurately characterized. Specifically, bias can arise when baseline treatment assignment is not clearly separated from post-baseline changes, such as treatment switching or discontinuation related to prognosis. Together, dynamic treatment patterns and incomplete exposure measurement complicate both treatment strategy definition and treatment assignment in EHR-based TTE.

Mitigating strategies and limitations

Several approaches can partially address challenges in defining treatment strategies and improve the alignment between observed data and the longitudinal treatment strategies specified in the target trial. Treatment episode reconstruction uses available prescribing, dispensing, and encounter data to approximate treatment initiation, periods of exposure, and gaps in treatment40. Treatment strategy harmonization further simplifies heterogeneous real-world treatment patterns into a limited set of clinically interpretable strategies that can be consistently assigned across data sources3.

In turn, improved treatment strategy definition can facilitate clearer identification of treatment assignment at baseline. When treatment decisions and subsequent changes unfold over time, g-methods, including marginal structural models and the g-formula, allow estimation of effects of dynamic treatment strategies while accounting for time-varying confounding affected by prior treatment38,41,42. In this framework, treatment assignment is conceptualized at baseline, while post-baseline treatment changes are handled analytically rather than through retrospective reclassification of exposure.

Nonetheless, adherence and treatment intent are often only partially observable in EHR data, and deviations from assigned strategies may be driven by unmeasured clinical judgment or patient behavior. When baseline treatment assignment cannot be clearly separated from post-baseline treatment implementation, no analytic method can fully recover the per-protocol effect defined by the target trial. Complexity in treatment strategies thus imposes fundamental constraints on the causal effects estimable using EHR-based TTE.

Follow-up definition, censoring, and competing events

Understanding the barrier

In EHR data, follow-up is governed by continued engagement with a healthcare system rather than by study design. Individuals are observed only while they receive care within a given system, and follow-up may end when patients transfer care or otherwise disengage. Because these transitions are often closely linked to patients’ evolving health status and care needs, loss to follow-up in EHR data is rarely independent of the outcome process43, leading to informative censoring. In some cases, follow-up terminates due to competing events, such as death from other causes, that preclude further observation of primary outcome of interest. Accordingly, censoring in EHR-based studies often reflects a joint process of disease progression and healthcare utilization rather than an independent truncation of observation. Failure to appropriately account for informative censoring can bias survival and incidence estimates, and consequently treatment effect estimates44.

Mitigating strategies and limitations

Several methodological approaches can partially address challenges related to follow-up and censoring in EHR-based TTE. Inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) aims to reconstruct the target trial risk set under assumptions about the censoring mechanism43,45, and Fine-Gray method can be applied when competing events are observed and well defined46. Linkage to external data sources47,48, such as claims data or death registries, can improve capture of specific outcomes occurring after disengagement from the primary EHR system, thereby reducing some forms of unobserved system exit. Nonetheless, these approaches rely on strong and often unverifiable assumptions about observation and censoring processes. Both informative censoring, which arises from auxiliary variables that are difficult or impossible to identify, and unobserved competing events impose fundamental constraints on the validity of survival and incidence estimates, and, by extension, on treatment effect estimates derived from EHR-based TTE.

Outcome definition and measurement

Understanding the barrier

Outcome ascertainment in EHR is often incomplete, heterogeneous, and temporally uncertain49,50. Clinical outcomes are recorded through diagnosis, procedure, and laboratory codes, and other structured EHR representations, each with different sensitivity and clinical meaning. Many outcomes are observed only when patients interact with the healthcare system, such that recorded event times reflect documentation and coding practices rather than true clinical onset49. Consequently, outcome timing is frequently subject to delayed detection and temporal uncertainty arising from intermittent observation (i.e., interval censoring). Furthermore, outcome misclassification in EHR data is often differential. Patients with greater disease severity, closer clinical monitoring, or higher healthcare utilization are more likely to have outcomes detected, whereas milder or subclinical events may go unrecorded. Documentation and coding practices also vary across clinicians and institutions, further contributing to heterogeneity in outcome capture51. When outcome ascertainment depends on treatment status, disease severity, or access to care, estimated treatment effects may reflect utilization-driven differences rather than true differences in underlying event risk.

Mitigating strategies and limitations

Several approaches can partially mitigate these challenges. Multi-source phenotyping integrates diagnoses, procedures, laboratory results, and clinical narratives, and other EHR data elements to improve outcome validity52. Probabilistic or uncertainty-aware outcome models treat event occurrence and timing as latent processes, allowing analyses to explicitly account for measurement error and temporal uncertainty53. Despite these efforts, residual misclassification and temporal uncertainty often persist, which constrains the interpretation and generalizability of causal conclusions.

Causal contrasts and estimand specification

Understanding the barrier

TTE requires that causal contrasts between treatment strategies be identifiable under the treatment assignment mechanism. In EHR data, this requirement is often violated because treatment assignment in routine care is rarely exchangeable. Clinical decisions are shaped by symptom severity, disease stage, functional status, frailty, social determinants of health, clinician preference, and access constraints, many of which are missing or poorly measured in structured EHR data36,37. As a result, treated and untreated groups may differ systematically in prognostically important ways that are not accounted for by measured covariates, leading to residual confounding. Even with extensive adjustment, estimated effects may therefore reflect differences in who receives treatment rather than the causal effect of treatment itself.

Violations of the positivity assumption further constrain causal contrasts in EHR-based TTE54,55. In routine care, treatment assignment may be nearly deterministic for certain patient profiles due to contraindications, institutional protocols, or access limitations. This creates structural non-overlap between strategies, where the probability of receiving one treatment is (near) zero. In such regions, the target trial contrast is not identifiable, and causal effects cannot be defined for the full target population without redefining the estimand. Weak overlap, by contrast, reflects near-positivity, where treatment probabilities approach 0 or 1 but remain non-zero. Although contrasts may remain theoretically identifiable, estimation becomes weakly supported by the data, often yielding high-variance and unstable estimates. Limited overlap also increases model dependence, as inference relies on extrapolation beyond the data-rich region. Restricting analyses to areas of common support (e.g., trimming or overlap weighting) can improve stability, but these approaches modify the target population and therefore the estimand, yielding inference for a restricted population rather than the original target trial estimand.

Competing events introduce an additional challenge for estimand specification. Events such as death or hospitalization outside the observed system may preclude the occurrence or observation of the outcome of interest. When such events are unobserved or implicitly treated as censoring, the resulting risk sets no longer correspond to the causal contrast defined by the target trial. Apparent treatment effects may then arise from differential survival or retention rather than from differences in the underlying outcome process, effectively altering the causal estimand being identified.

Mitigating strategies and limitations

Several approaches are commonly used to assess the robustness of causal contrasts when exchangeability and positivity assumptions are violated. Proxy adjustment may reduce bias from unmeasured confounding by incorporating variables correlated with latent constructs such as disease severity or access to care56,57. Negative control analyses provide diagnostics for residual confounding by evaluating associations that should be null under the target trial22,58. Quantitative bias analyses further characterize the sensitivity of effect estimates to explicit assumptions about the magnitude of unmeasured confounding59,60.

Importantly, these approaches do not restore the causal contrasts defined by the target trial. They do not recover exchangeability, resolve structural non-overlap, or undo changes to the risk set induced by competing events. Instead, they make the dependence of causal conclusions on unverifiable assumptions explicit and help restrict interpretation to contrasts that are weakly, but observably, supported by the data. When diagnostics reveal strong violations or extreme sensitivity, this should be interpreted as evidence that the target causal contrast is not identifiable in the available EHR data.

Analysis plan and estimation

Understanding the barrier

In EHR-based TTE, the analysis plan is constrained by what the data can support rather than chosen freely. A central challenge is aligning the target trial estimand with analytic methods given the structure, completeness, and provenance of EHR data17. Different estimands impose distinct data requirements45. ITT estimands require a well-defined treatment assignment rule at baseline that precedes individual treatment behavior. In many EHR-based studies, such assignment mechanisms are absent or not directly observable, limiting the feasibility of ITT-style emulation. In these settings, treatment initiation reflects post-baseline clinical decision-making rather than assignment, and ITT estimands are not well-defined.

Temporal structure further constrains estimation. Treatment decisions and confounders evolve over time and are measured irregularly at clinical encounters rather than at prespecified study visits. When treatment decisions depend on evolving clinical status affected by prior treatment, standard regression approaches no longer correspond to the target trial estimand. In multi-site EHR-based TTE, pooled EHR cohorts may represent mixtures of distinct subpopulations governed by different data-generating processes, violating assumptions of exchangeability and positivity and rendering a single pooled estimand ill-defined or uninterpretable61.

Mitigating strategies and limitations

Analytic strategies can partially address these challenges only when they are explicitly aligned with the target trial estimand and supported by the available data. Explicit specification of the estimand should precede method selection, as it constrains decisions regarding censoring, handling of treatment changes, and interpretation of effect estimates. When dynamic or sustained treatment strategies are of interest and longitudinal treatment decisions and time-varying confounders can be adequately characterized, g-methods42,45,62,63, including marginal structural models, the g-formula, and the cloning-censoring-weighting framework64, can be used to explicitly model the temporal structure of the target trial. When these data requirements are not satisfied, attempts to estimate such estimands should be avoided in favor of simpler analyses targeting more limited, but defensible, causal contrasts.

In multi-site settings, analytic choices should be guided by assumptions about cross-site comparability rather than by the validity of single-site estimates alone. When sites are believed to share sufficiently similar data-generating processes and treatment assignment mechanisms, pooled analyses with appropriate adjustment may be reasonable. When substantial heterogeneity exists, site-stratified analyses provide transparent, site-specific causal contrasts without imposing unsupported assumptions of transportability. Hierarchical models65 offer partial pooling when site-specific effects66 are expected to vary but are assumed to arise from a common distribution. However, this assumption is substantive and should be explicitly justified. Federated analytic approaches67,68 (e.g., FedAvg69, FedDistill70) enable coordinated estimation while preserving local data structures and privacy constraints, but they do not, by themselves, guarantee the existence of a single, transportable causal effect. In many settings, federated analyses are most appropriate for estimating and comparing site-specific estimands rather than for enforcing a pooled causal interpretation.

Across all settings, transparency should be prioritized over analytic complexity. Diagnostics for covariate balance, overlap, and weight stability help assess whether the data meaningfully support the intended causal contrast, while sensitivity analyses characterize dependence on unverifiable assumptions. The primary role of the analysis plan in EHR-based TTE is therefore not merely to produce precise estimates, but to delineate which causal questions can be answered, and which cannot, given the structure and limitations of the available data.

Illustrative example of EHR-based TTE: GLP-1RAs and cancer risk

Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) are increasingly used for obesity and type 2 diabetes because of their established effects on weight and glycemic control71. Obesity is strongly associated with elevated risk of multiple malignancies, motivating questions about whether GLP-1RA initiation alters subsequent cancer risk72. Outside RCTs, however, observational evidence remains limited and challenging to interpret due to treatment selection, imperfect exposure capture, and long outcome latency.

In this illustrative example (Table 2), we apply a TTE framework using EHR data to estimate the effect of initiating a GLP-1RA at a clinically plausible decision point compared with an active-comparator initiation strategy defined at the same time zero73. The primary contrast follows an ITT analog, comparing GLP-1RA initiators versus active-comparator initiators using a prespecified 7-day grace period for strategy classification. This example highlights common design challenges in EHR-based TTE, including confounding by indication mitigated through adjustment for prespecified baseline confounders (BMI and weight trajectory, diabetes status and management, smoking-related proxies, comorbidities, and healthcare utilization), exposure ascertainment that prioritizes the most reliable source when multiple sources exist (dispensing records, prescription orders, then administration records when available), and sensitivity analyses evaluating alternative follow-up horizons (6, 9, and 12 months).

Conclusion

TTE using EHR data yields interpretable causal estimates only when the available data can support the design elements of the target trial being emulated. In EHR-based studies, cohort entry, measurement, and follow-up are governed by healthcare utilization rather than by biological risk or experimental design. These data-generating constraints shape how target trial components are implemented and, in many settings, alter the causal estimand that is identified. EHR-based TTE should therefore be understood as defining a data-constrained target trial. When target trial components cannot be implemented without conditioning on post-baseline information, selective observation, or unobserved processes, the intended causal estimand is not identifiable from the EHR data alone. Even when an estimand is identifiable in principle, limited overlap can yield high-variance, model-sensitive estimates, and restrictions to regions of common support should be framed as targeting a restricted estimand supported by the observed data. In these settings, analyses should be interpreted as targeting an operationally defined estimand, rather than as a biased approximation to the original target trial estimand. Statistical adjustment cannot recover a causal contrast that is not supported by the data.

EHR-based TTE is most appropriate when eligibility and treatment assignment can be defined at a defensible baseline, outcomes are routinely captured, and the data include comparable patients receiving different treatment strategies. It is not appropriate for causal questions that depend on unobserved disease onset, non-computable eligibility criteria, poorly observed adherence, or unsupported assumption of transportability across settings. In these settings, EHR-based analyses necessarily answer a different causal question. Sensitivity analyses and diagnostics should therefore be used to evaluate assumptions implied by design choices, not applied generically64. Their role is to assess whether conclusions depend on weakly supported features of the data and to guide interpretation when identifiability is limited.

Importantly, a valid outcome of this framework is the determination that a given causal question is not identifiable using available EHR data, in which case TTE should not be pursued. These considerations are intended to guide study design and analysis planning prior to estimation, rather than to justify results after the fact. By anchoring inference to an explicitly specified target trial and making the emulation step transparent, this framework provides practical guidance for determining when EHR-based TTE can yield interpretable causal estimates, and when it does not.
