A foundation model for multi-task cross-distribution restoration of fluorescence microscopy images
Authors: Qiqi Lu, Xiuli Liu, Qianjin Feng, Shaoqun Zeng, Shenghua Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep learning has demonstrated remarkable abilities in restoring fluorescence microscopy images degraded by noise, blur, or undersampling. However, most existing models are task-specific and trained on limited, homogeneously distributed data, which restricts their generalizability and practicality. Here, we present FluoResFM, a foundation model for multi-task and cross-distribution fluorescence microscopy image restoration in a unified model. FluoResFM leverages textual prior information to adapt to specific tasks and data distributions. Trained on datasets across three tasks (image denoising, deconvolution, and super-resolution) and over 20 biological structures, FluoResFM demonstrates superior restoration performance and enhanced generalization across datasets with varied biological structures and imaging conditions. Through fine-tuning with only a single sample, FluoResFM can further improve its performance on unseen data, achieving results comparable to conventional models trained on hundreds of samples, and be easily adapted to additional tasks, including 3D image restoration, surface projection, isotropic reconstruction, and super-resolution with various scale factors. Moreover, the performance of existing cell/organelle segmentation models can be enhanced using the high-quality images restored by FluoResFM.

Introduction

Fluorescence microscopy is an essential tool for observing the morphology and dynamics of biological structures1. However, fluorescence microscopy images are often compromised by noise, blur, or undersampling due to limitations in light exposure, imaging speed, and spatial/temporal resolution2,3. Recently, deep learning-based algorithms have shown remarkable promise in fluorescence microscopy image restoration, including image denoising4,5, deconvolution4,6,7, and super-resolution5,8,9, enabling more accurate observation and analysis of biological structures and dynamics.

Current deep learning-based fluorescence microscopy image restoration methods are typically task-specific and trained on limited, homogeneously distributed data, which restricts their generalizability and practicality in biological research. Separate models are generally required for each restoration task due to the significant differences in task objectives4. Moreover, imaging objects (e.g., clathrin-coated pits (CCPs) and microtubules (MTs)) and imaging conditions2,10 (e.g., light exposure and type of microscopy) vary widely across research contexts, leading to heterogeneous data distributions. This distribution shift poses a challenge to the generalizability of deep networks. When a deep network trained on a specific data distribution is applied to data with a different characteristic, it may hallucinate details and produce artifacts11. Furthermore, due to the complexity of sample preparation and image acquisition, task-specific homogeneous datasets are often limited, increasing the risk of overfitting11. The inefficiency and impracticality of deploying separate models for each task and dataset highlight the need for a unified model capable of handling diverse tasks and leveraging large, heterogeneous datasets.

Foundation models have gained significant attention in image analysis12–17 and processing18–20 for their effectiveness and strong generalizability, which stems from their pre-training on large-scale, diverse datasets. For image restoration problems, foundation models can handle diverse degradations simultaneously18,19, adapt to unseen tasks or datasets through fine-tuning on limited data20, or serve as effective preprocessing tools for enhancing downstream image analysis tasks18. In fluorescence microscopy, UniFMIR19 utilized task-specific heads and tails along with shared Swin transformer layers to address different restoration tasks in a unified framework. While UniFMIR demonstrated commendable image restoration precision and generalization across different tasks, it did not fully account for the significant data heterogeneity introduced by variations in imaging objects and conditions, potentially leading to confusion between different data distributions.

In this study, we present FluoResFM, a generalizable foundation model for multi-task, cross-distribution restoration of fluorescence microscopy images. FluoResFM enhances the quality of images from diverse biological structures and imaging conditions through denoising, deconvolution, and super-resolution in a unified model. It leverages textual prior information, including task type, imaging object, and imaging condition, to guide adaptation to specific task and data distribution. We trained and evaluated FluoResFM on an extensive collection of real-world datasets (over 4.3 million paired patches for training), spanning three restoration tasks and more than 20 types of biological structures from varying imaging scenarios. Comprehensive evaluations demonstrated its superior restoration performance and generalizability on diverse internal and unseen external datasets across different tasks and data distributions. The textual prior information enabled effective control over image contents and patterns, providing a clear indication of the underlying task objective and data distribution. As a foundation model with strong generalization capability, FluoResFM can achieve improved restoration performance on unseen data through fine-tuning with as few as a single sample, obtaining results comparable to those of conventional deep networks trained on hundreds of samples. FluoResFM can also be readily adapted to other tasks through fine-tuning on a single sample, including three-dimensional (3D) image restoration, surface projection from 3D volume, isotropic reconstruction, and super resolution with various scale factors. Moreover, the cell and organelle segmentation performance of existing automatic segmentation models can be further improved when applied to the high-quality images restored by FluoResFM. To make FluoResFM readily available to the biological research community, we developed a user-friendly napari plugin that allows straightforward integration into imaging workflows.

Results

Inspiration and development of FluoResFM

Training a deep learning model on homogeneous data from a specific task, imaging object, and imaging condition enables the model to learn a strong prior that effectively constrains the solution space of an inverse problem. However, while this specificity mitigates the inherent ambiguity of ill-posed problems, it concurrently limits model generalizability and introduces bias when applied to out-of-distribution data. In fluorescence microscopy, a model trained on data with a specific type of imaging structure often exhibits unexpected bias when applied to structures with different patterns due to distribution shift (Supplementary Fig. 1). For example, the content-aware image restoration (CARE) model trained on data with CCP structures (CARE:CCP) produced incorrect CCP-like structures when restoring MT images (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Only when training and testing data shared the same distribution could the model generate expected structures (Supplementary Fig. 1a) and achieve optimal quantitative performance (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Although training on more heterogeneous data (such as a mixture of CCP, MT, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) structures) alleviated the output bias problem (Supplementary Fig. 1a), it expanded the feasible solution space, increased output uncertainty, and yielded sub-optimal results (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, task conflicts arising from differing objectives across different tasks further complicate image restoration within a unified model. Recent studies have demonstrated that prior information related to tasks21 or image contents22 can serve as a powerful condition for ill-posed inverse problem and guide deep learning models to adapt to different data distributions, which is a promising strategy for improving the generalization ability of models. Motivated by this insight, we designed the architecture of FluoResFM to effectively fuse image features with textual prior information, leveraging the latter to direct the model in adapting to fluorescence microscopy images from different tasks and data distributions (Fig. 1a). FluoResFM was based on the widely-used U-Net architecture23 (Fig. 1b). To incorporate textual guidance, the textual prior information (including task type, structure type, and imaging condition) was encoded into a feature embedding via a text embedder. The embedding was subsequently fused with spatial image features at each scale of the U-Net through cross-attention operation24,25 (Supplementary Fig. 2). We adopted the text encoder from BiomedCLIP26 as the text embedder, which had been pre-trained on diverse biomedical datasets, including generic biomedical illustrations, radiography, digital pathology, and microscopy. FluoResFM was trained on more than 4.3 million paired low and high-quality patches (Fig. 1a) across three tasks (denoising, deconvolution, and super-resolution (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×2 upscaling)) and over 20 types of biological structures (Fig. 1c). To comprehensively evaluate the restoration performance of FluoResFM, we curated 302 internal datasets and 51 external unseen datasets. Performance of FluoResFM on internal datasets

We compared the performance of FluoResFM with its variant trained without textual prior information, and UniFMIR19, a state-of-the-art multi-task fluorescence microscopy image restoration foundation model, on the internal datasets. Across the majority of internal datasets, FluoResFM attained higher average peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR), multi-scale structural similarity index measure (MS-SSIM), zero-normalized cross-correlation (ZNCC), resolution-scaled Pearson coefficient (RSP) values and lower resolution-scaled error (RSE) values (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3a, and Supplementary Data 1). The resolution of images restored by FluoResFM were more consistent with that of ground-truth (GT) images (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Overall, FluoResFM demonstrated superior performance compared to FluoResFM without text guidance and UniFMIR across all three restoration tasks (Fig. 2c). The FluoResFM model effectively suppressed the noise, removed the blur, and enhanced the spatial resolution of the images, producing outputs closer to the high-quality GT images (Fig. 2b). FluoResFM better resolved closely spaced structures (e.g., super-resolution of nuclear pore complexes (NPCs) and MTs) (Fig. 2b, red arrows) and reconstructed more continuous and sharper MTs and ER networks (e.g., deconvolution of MTs and ERs) (Fig. 2b). In contrast, UniFMIR merged adjacent objects or produced fragmented reconstructions of MTs and ERs (Fig. 2b). FluoResFM without textual information guidance tended to deblur all the input images and could not fulfil the denoising task as expected (e.g., denoising of CCPs and F-actin filaments) (Fig. 2b), resulting in noticeably lower PSNR, MS-SSIM, ZNCC, and RSP values and higher RSE on certain datasets for the denoising task (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 3a). These results suggest that simply unifying multiple tasks in a single universal model without any task-related prior information can result in uncontrollable and undesired predictions. Furthermore, in both the deconvolution and super-resolution tasks, FluoResFM without textual guidance was not only quantitatively inferior (Fig. 2a,c and Supplementary Fig. 3a) but also produced visibly blurrier reconstructions compared to FluoResFM (Fig. 2b). Benefiting from the superior restoration performance of FluoResFM, the CCP diameter measured in the restored images was more consistent with that of GT images (Supplementary Fig. 4a,d). Long filaments in the MT network could be tracked more easily (Supplementary Fig. 4b), and more complete skeleton and more nodes of the ER network were detected (Supplementary Fig. 4c) in the restored images. Performance of FluoResFM on external unseen data

To demonstrate the generalizability of FluoResFM without model retraining or parameter adjustment, we evaluated its image restoration performance on 51 external unseen datasets. FluoResFM achieved higher PSNR, MS-SSIM, ZNCC, RSP values and lower RSE values across the majority of datasets (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Data 2). The resolution of images restored by FluoResFM were more consistent with that of GT images (Supplementary Fig. 3d). Overall, FluoResFM exhibited significantly superior quantitative performance across all three tasks (Fig. 3c). FluoResFM without textual guidance still struggled with the denoising task and tended to reconstruct a deblurred image instead of removing noise (e.g., denoising of CCPs and F-actin) (Fig. 3b), which was consistent with the observations on internal datasets (Fig. 2b). Influence of textual prior information on FluoResFM restoration

Textual prior information can serve as an effective condition to guide the image reconstruction process and control the generated image content, which has shown promising results in natural image synthesis24,27, restoration28, and enhancement29. When combined with user-specified restoration task type, such prior knowledge can provide valuable cues for inferring image content patterns and alleviating the inherently ill-posed nature of image restoration. In fluorescence microscopy, imaging objects and conditions are typically known, and this knowledge can serve as a prior for the restoration.

FluoResFM can effectively control the reconstructed patterns of structures by altering the input text prompt (Fig. 4a). For instance, with the “denoising” prompt, FluoResFM only removed the noise from the ER image while preserving its intrinsic blur and low resolution (Fig. 4a, blue). The “deconvolution” prompt deblurred the input image (Fig. 4a, green), and the “super-resolution” prompt further up-sampled the image and restored finer structural details (Fig. 4a, red). Only with the correct structure type prompt, such as “ER” in Fig. 4a, can FluoResFM produce the most accurate restoration. In contrast, when using the “CCP” prompt as input, the ER was mistakenly reconstructed as CCP-like structures, while the “MT” prompt yielded structures resembling evenly thick, crisscrossing tubules rather than the correct reticular structure with uneven thickness (Fig. 4a, red arrows). Furthermore, with the correct structure type prompt, FluoResFM achieved more accurate morphology-aware measurements, such as CCP diameter, filament length in the MT network, and count and degree of nodes detected in the ER network, compared to the raw input (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 5). In contrast, incorrect structure type prompts may degrade the accuracy of these measurements. For example, when “MT” or “ER” prompts were used to restore CCP images, the CCP diameter was overestimated (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Using the mismatched “CCP” prompt led to severe reconstruction distortions in both the MT and ER images (Fig. 4d), resulting in shorter extracted filaments in the MT network on average (Supplementary Fig. 5b), and fewer detected nodes in the ER network (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Due to the structural similarity between MT and ER, the confusion between the “MT” and “ER” prompts did not lead to significant degradation in filament length or node count/degree measurements (Supplementary Fig. 5b-d), although detailed structural features were distorted (Fig. 4a). These results highlight the strong controllability offered by textual priors in guiding the image restoration process. Additionally, the deficiency of prior information decreased the performance of FluoResFM (Fig. 4b,c). Compared to FluoResFM using only the task information (FluoResFM-T), only the task and structure information (FluoResFM-TS), or only the task, structure, and input metadata (i.e., microscope type and parameters used for acquiring input image) (FluoResFM-TS-in) as input, FluoResFM with full textual prior information achieved significantly higher PSNR, MS-SSIM, and ZNCC values on both internal (Fig. 4b) and external unseen datasets (Fig. 4c). The quality of the restoration increased with the addition of textual information (Fig, 4b,c). When detailed target imaging parameters were unavailable, FluoResFM using only the task, structure, input metadata, and target microscope type information (FluoResFM-TS-in-tm) still achieved acceptable restorations, showing relatively good results on internal datasets (Fig. 4b) and no significant differences in PSNR and ZNCC on external datasets (Fig. 4c). Removing the target metadata during both training and inference stages led to a modest performance decrease across most metrics on the internal (Supplementary Fig. 6a) and external datasets (Fig. 4e). Compared to using structured categorical embeddings (Methods), FluoResFM showed slightly degraded performance on internal datasets (Supplementary Fig. 6b) but demonstrated improved generalization to external datasets (Fig. 4f).

Transfer FluoResFM to unseen data via fine-tuning

The availability of training data is a critical factor influencing the performance of deep learning models. Insufficient training data often leads to suboptimal restoration performance11. Transfer learning offers a solution by leveraging prior knowledge learned from large-scale datasets and transferring it to limited, task-specific, homogeneous unseen data that would be inadequate on its own19,30,31. To evaluate the efficiency of FluoResFM in transferring knowledge to unseen data, we fine-tuned the model separately on 12 external datasets with different biological structures. For each dataset, only a single image was used for fine-tuning. For comparison, conventional deep learning-based image restoration models (the CARE network4 and the deep Fourier channel attention network (DFCAN)8) were trained from scratch using the same sample. CARE yielded the lowest PSNR, MS-SSIM, and ZNCC values, even worse than the low-quality input images (Fig. 5a). Although DFCAN achieved better results than CARE, its performance remained substantially inferior than FluoResFM (Fig. 5a). The poor performance of them was primarily attributed to overfitting, resulting from the insufficiency of training data (Fig. 5c). While the overfitting issues of CARE and DFCAN could be mitigated with more training data, they required hundreds of images to reach the performance achieved by FluoResFM fine-tuned on just a single image (Fig. 5d). In contrast, both UniFMIR and FluoResFM exhibited stronger resistance to overfitting (Fig. 5c) and obtained better performance after fine-tuning (Fig. 5a). The fine-tuned FluoResFM model attained significantly higher PSNR, MS-SSIM, and ZNCC values compared to CARE and DFCAN trained from scratch, UniFMIR and FluoResFM without fine-tuning, as well as the fine-tuned UniFMIR model (Fig. 5a). To test its applicability in dynamic imaging, we fine-tuned FluoResFM on the image acquired at 0 s in live imaging of CCPs and lysosomes separately (Fig. 5b). The fine-tuned models were then applied to restore images at subsequent time points. FluoResFM markedly enhanced the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and resolution of raw images, allowing for clearer differentiation of tiny structures and more accurate tracking of their dynamics (Fig. 5b, red dashed boxes). Benefiting from these, the diameter of CCPs (Supplementary Fig. 8a,c) and the count of lysosomes (Supplementary Fig. 8b,d) measured in the restored images were more consistent with those measured from GT images across all the time points and showed reduced spurious frame-to-frame fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 8c).Fig. 5Fine-tuning FluoResFM on a single sample.a Quantitative comparison of the restoration performance of different models trained or fine-tuned on a single sample. Boost automated cellular and organelle segmentation

Accurate segmentation of cell bodies, membranes, nuclei, and other organelles is crucial for many quantitative cell biological applications32,33. Existing automated cellular and organelle segmentation methods, such as Cellpose-SAM34 and Nellie35, have demonstrated impressive versatility across diverse cellular and intracellular structures, yet they show limited robustness to image degradation. Their segmentation performance can be significantly improved when applied to images restored by FluoResFM. We used 72 datasets encompassing a variety of cellular and organelle structures to evaluate the effectiveness of FluoResFM for cellular and organelle segmentation. The low-quality image was first restored using FluoResFM, and then segmented using either Cellpose-SAM or Nellie to obtain object masks (Fig. 7a). Discussion

Most existing deep learning-based models for fluorescence microscopy image restoration4,6,7 were trained on data with specific tasks, imaging objects, and imaging conditions (Fig. 1a). The data distribution bias in practical applications can lead to their performance degradation and increase the risk of generating hallucinations (Supplementary Fig. 1). The pre-trained foundation model UniFMIR19 utilizes task-specific network heads and tails to perform different tasks within a unified model. However, it does not fully account for variations in the image content patterns across different imaging objects and conditions, which can lead to its ambiguity of different structures (Figs. 2b, 3b). To address this limitation, our proposed FluoResFM model leverages textual prior information to guide the model to adapt to different tasks and data distributions. The validation on a wide range of heterogeneous internal and external unseen datasets (Figs. 2,3 and Supplementary Fig. 3) suggests its superior performance in fluorescence microscopy image restoration. The textual prompts can effectively control the structural patterns in the restored images (Fig. 4a) and serve as informative priors about underlying data distributions, thereby enhancing the model’s generalization across diverse imaging scenarios.

Pre-training on diverse data distributions enables FluoResFM to extract generalized representations and achieve better generalization to unseen data. When fine-tuning only a small subset of model parameters using just a single sample, FluoResFM did not experience overfitting and produced accurate restoration results comparable to conventional deep networks trained on hundreds of times more data (Fig. 5). Moreover, it can be readily adapted to additional unseen tasks via fine-tuning, such as 3D image restoration, surface projection, isotropic reconstruction, and super-resolution with various scale factors (Fig. 6). This alleviates the heavy dependence on large training datasets during deep learning-based model deployment. Besides, FluoResFM can serve as a plug-and-play tool to enhance subsequent analysis tasks, such as cellular and intracellular structure segmentation (Fig. 7). The versatility and generalizability of FluoResFM underscore its practical value in biological research. To make FluoResFM more accessible to the biological research community, we have developed a user-friendly napari36 plugin for FluoResFM that enables interactive data preprocessing, model training, fine-tuning, and prediction (Supplementary Fig. 10 and Supplementary Movie 1).

Despite these strengths, several limitations and aspects should be considered in future work to improve the performance and applicability of FluoResFM. First, although FluoResFM can be transferred to restoration tasks unseen during training via fine-tuning, incorporating additional datasets dedicated to these tasks into the training corpus could further improve its performance. However, the quantity of such datasets remains relatively limited at present. Second, while FluoResFM has been adapted to perform 3D image restoration and surface projection, extending it to a fully 3D network may be more effective for modeling volumetric information37. However, the lack of accessible large scale 3D training datasets currently hinders its development. Third, FluoResFM was trained on diverse fluorescence microscopy data and presented promising results, but the covered scenarios remain limited considering the diversity of imaging objects and microscopy techniques1,38. We plan to continuously collect additional data and train the model to further improve its generalizability. Besides, the performance of FluoResFM may be further enhanced by using more advanced network architectures39,40 emerging in the natural image processing domain, which offer improved capabilities for fusing image and text information. Currently, FluoResFM requires relatively large memory usage and long computation time, particularly for high-resolution images (Supplementary Table 8). However, its inference time does not scale proportionally with its parameter count and remains comparable to UniFMIR. Improvements in architecture design, such as SwinIR41 and Retormer42, could enhance its efficiency43 and warrant further exploration.

Methods

Data collection and preprocessing

We collected numerous publicly accessible fluorescence microscopy image datastes4,5,19,44–51 covering diverse imaging objects and conditions across denoising, deconvolution, and super-resolution tasks for model training and evaluation (Supplementary Tables 1–4 and Supplementary Data 3). No cell culture experiments were performed by the authors. Imaging object and condition information was manually curated for each dataset. Both low-quality input and high-quality GT images were normalized to a common intensity range using percentile-based normalization with the 3rd percentile as the minimum and the 99.5th percentile as the maximum value. For the super-resolution task, low-quality images were up-sampled using nearest-neighbor interpolation to match the dimension of high-quality GT images. For internal datasets (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Data 3), we manually divided the datasets lacking pre-defined splits into training and test sets using an approximately 80:20 ratio. To enable simultaneous training across different datasets, we cropped the training images into patches with the same spatial size (64\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×64 pixels) (Fig. 1a). Patches containing only noise were excluded from the training set. The final training data relatively uniformly distributed across the three tasks, with more data for super-resolution given the complexity of this problem (Fig. 1c). The training data encompassed over 20 types of structures (Fig. 1c), including CCP, ER, F-actin, MT, myosin-IIA, nucleus, histone, nuclear-envelope, membrane, granule, tubulin, nucleoid, MreB, Mito, lysosome, survivin, neural crest cell (NCC), NPC, chromosome, and Golgi, imaged with wide-field microscopy, structured illumination microscopy, instant structured illumination microscopy, confocal microscopy, multi-photon microscopy, light-sheet microscopy, two-photon microscopy, and stimulated emission depletion microscopy. The textual prior information consisted of task type, sample type, structure type, fluorescence indicator, and microscopy techniques with their parameter settings used for both the low-quality input and high-quality GT image acquisition, which were concatenated sequentially as a single text input (Supplementary Table 7). The detailed information of internal and external datasets for restoration performance evaluation, as well as datasets for segmentation evaluation were shown in Supplementary Data 4. Each scale of the encoder and decoder contained a residual block and a text-image fusion block (Supplementary Fig. 2). The text-image fusion block utilized cross-attention mechanism25 to integrate textual prior information with image features. Adapting FluoResFM to process 3D data

For the 3D image restoration task, to enable multi-channel input and output, the number of input channels of the convolution layer at the head of FluoResFM and the number of output channels of the convolution layer at the tail were set to match the number of slices of the input volume. For the surface projection task, only the number of input channels of the convolution layer at the head of network was adjusted to match the number of slices of the input volume.

Network training and fine-tuning

The FluoResFM model was implemented using the PyTorch deep learning framework (Pytorch 2.6.0) and optimized by adaptive moment estimation with decoupled weight decay (AdamW)52 optimizer. The batch size was set to 16, and the model was trained for 700,000 iterations. The initial learning rate was set to 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}×10-5 and halved every 100,000 iterations. To maintain the consistency of training conditions across different networks and conduct a fair comparison, the comparison methods followed similar training settings (Supplementary Table 5). During fine-tuning, only parameters in the first and last convolution layers of the U-Net backbone were updated, while all other parameters were frozen (Supplementary Fig. 2). The training settings remained unchanged, except for the total number of iterations (Supplementary Table 5). All experiments were conducted on a computer equipped with an Intel Core i9-14900K processor and a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090D graphic processing card (24 GB of RAM).

Evaluation metrics

We adopted five image quality assessment metrics (PSNR, MS-SSIM, ZNCC, RSE, and RSP) to quantitatively evaluate the image restoration performance of different methods. PSNR was used to measure the pixel-wise difference between restored and GT images and implemented using the scikit-image53 package (version 0.22.0). MS-SSIM54 was employed to quantify the structure similarity between restored and GT images across multiple spatial scales, which was implemented using the pytorch-MS-SSIM package (version 1.0.0). Statistics and reproducibility

All neural networks were trained once for each set of hyperparameters. Random seeds were fixed for network parameter initialization. The training and testing images were randomly split for each dataset. The images within each dataset were acquired from different samples under the same imaging condition. For comparisons between two groups, a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.11.9) and SciPy package (version 1.12.0). No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses.
