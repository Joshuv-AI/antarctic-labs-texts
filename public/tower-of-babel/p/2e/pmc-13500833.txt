Evolutionary trajectory of microbial sulfur oxidation pathways recapitulates Earth’s oxygenation history
Authors: Xin-Yue Ren, Xian-Yong Lin, Tomohisa Sebastian Tanabe, Alexander Loy, Lingfei Hu, Yong-Guan Zhu, Song-Can Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDissimilatory sulfur oxidation mediated by the microbial sulfur-oxidizing (Sox) multienzyme complex has played a central role in Earth’s oxidative sulfur cycle. However, the evolutionary trajectory leading to its current complexity remains poorly understood. Here, we integrated expanded genomic records with geochemical evidence to reconstruct the natural history of the Sox system along a geological timeline. Our analyses traced the origin of the truncated Sox system (SoxABXYZ) prior to the Great Oxidation Event (GOE). This early form was later assembled with reverse dissimilatory sulfite reductases (rDsr), facilitating a primitive pathway for sulfate formation during the anoxic Archean eon. Following the post-GOE atmospheric oxygen rise, the truncated system incorporated SoxCD components, evolving into a more fine-tuned, oxygen-adapted pathway compared to the rDsr-coupled alternative. The subsequent spread of these Sox pathway variants was constrained by the oxygen requirements and phylogenetic backgrounds of their prokaryotic hosts, shaping the modern ecological landscape of microbial sulfur oxidation. The viral recruitment of auxiliary Sox components marked the latest evolutionary events, enabling direct manipulation of sulfur cycling over centuries-to-millennia timescales. Collectively, the reconstructed molecular timeline offers deeper insights into the evolutionary dynamics of the global oxidative sulfur cycle and its billion-year interplay with Earth’s changing environments.Subject terms: Coevolution, Element cycles, Microbial ecology, Molecular evolution

Introduction

Microbial sulfur oxidation (MSO) is a fundamental component of Earth’s sulfur biogeochemical cycle. Alongside chemical processes, MSO facilitates re-oxidation of sulfide—produced during microbial sulfate reduction—back into sulfate, with or without intermediate sulfur species1. Consequently, MSO sustains the cycling of sulfate and sulfide, which in turn exerts far-reaching implications for carbon fluxes, climate dynamics, and redox states of Earth’s exosphere2–7. At the organismal level, MSO is intricately linked to carbon fixation and/or the respiration of electron acceptors such as oxygen, nitrate, manganese(IV), and iron(III), supporting diverse lifestyles, including chemosynthesis, anoxygenic photoautotrophy, and chemolithoautotrophy8–15. These MSO-driven processes underpin primary productivity across diverse ecosystems, forging crucial connections between the sulfur, carbon, oxygen, and nitrogen cycles at a planetary scale16. Microbial fossil records suggest that MSO began its operation as early as 2.5 billion years ago in Earth’s history, evolving alongside—yet still influencing—Earth’s surface environments over billions of years17,18. However, tracing the evolutionary history of MSO remains a grand challenge. While the development of specific sulfur metabolism over geological time (e.g., sulfate reduction) has been inferred from sedimentary sulfur isotopes, these records have limited capacity to resolve the trajectory of MSO evolution19. The limitation arises primarily due to (1) the generally weak isotopic signatures associated with MSO20 and (2) the strong influence of sedimentary parameters on sulfur isotope fractionation during early diagenetic burial environments21. As a result, the origin of primitive MSO and its evolution into modern forms are still not well understood.

MSO is driven by a set of enzymatic machines, and their records in the modern-day genomes provide an alternative window into the evolutionary history of MSO22. Over geological time, diverse MSO pathways have emerged, catalyzed by a complex suite of enzymes19,22. Among these, the sulfur oxidation (Sox) multienzyme system is the most widely distributed pathway, responsible for oxidation of thiosulfate—a key intermediate in the oxidative sulfur cycle7,23,24. The Sox system occurs in phylogenetically diverse prokaryotic lineages with either phototrophic or chemotrophic lifestyle, as well as in viruses, where it serves as auxiliary pathways for sulfur metabolism25,26. Multiple variants of Sox-dependent MSO pathways have been characterized in prokaryotes. The complete Sox system, comprising seven subunits often organized in a gene cluster (soxXYZABCD), facilitates complete oxidation of thiosulfate to sulfate (Fig. 1a). A truncated variant of the system, lacking SoxCD, accumulates reduced sulfur intermediates (e.g., elemental sulfur) during thiosulfate oxidation (Fig. 1a)27. This incomplete system can assemble with alternative MSO enzymatic machines, such as reverse-acting dissimilatory sulfite reductase (rDsr)28 or sulfur-oxidizing heterodisulfide reductase-like complexes (sHdr)29, to achieve complete oxidation of thiosulfate (Fig. 1a). In addition, viruses may carry only some genes of the Sox system, which are proposed to modulate the sulfur oxidation metabolism in infected hosts26. Phylogenetic analysis indicates the evolution of the Sox system is shaped by vertical and horizontal gene transfer (HGT), with the frequency of the latter correlating with increasing oxygen availability over Earth’s history22,30,31. Yet, these previous analyses focused on a restricted/sparse sampling of taxa or on individual components of the Sox system, leaving the evolutionary assembly of the Sox complex largely unexplored. Thus, it remains elusive how and when distinct Sox pathways were assembled and further disseminated across prokaryotes and viruses into their present complexity. Addressing these aspects is critical not only for obtaining a more comprehensive understanding of MSO’s evolutionary history but also has fundamental implications for the evolution of microbial metabolism and its ecological impacts on Earth’s chemical and physical environments.

Here, we leveraged the expanded diversity of the Sox system recorded in the vastly improved resolution of bacterial, archaeal, and viral tree of life. We performed large-scale phylogenetic analysis of all components of Sox systems, together with their associated rDsr and sHdr pathways spanning 80,789 bacteria, 4416 archaea, and 8,700,000 viruses. Phylogenetics were further coupled to molecular dating and machine learning algorithms to dissect the origins and paths that led to the current complexity of Sox systems along the geological timeline. Our results provide broader insights into the evolution of MSO and its intimate interplay with Earth’s surface environments.

Results

Expanded taxonomic distribution of the Sox pathway in bacteria

We conducted an exhaustive homology search for all seven components (soxBAXYZCD) of the Sox pathway across over 85,000 representative genomes of bacteria and archaea (“Methods”). We identified the soxB, an established marker for the Sox pathway and for diversity analyses of sulfur oxidizers31–33, in 4997 genomes (Supplementary Data 1). The other sox genes are often situated in close proximity to soxB (Supplementary Fig. 1a), and together formed 3873 recognizable gene clusters from 3795 genomes (“Methods”), with 77 genomes encoding multiple copies. Over a thousand soxB-hosting genomes (n = 1205) lacked detectable sox gene clusters, due to the fragmented nature of the genome or genuine segregation of sox genes across genomic regions, as observed in Thermochromatium vinosum (formerly known as Allochromatium vinosum) (Fig. 1b). The recognized sox gene clusters exhibit diverse syntenic structure, primarily due to variations in the position of soxA and soxX (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1b). These include gene organization like soxB → A → X → Y → Z with adjacent soxAX, soxB → A → Y → Z → X with separated soxAX, sox(AX) → Y → Z → B with fused sox(AX), soxY → Z → A → B that lacks soxX (Fig. 1b). These patterns reflect the complex rearrangement history of soxA and soxX during gene cluster evolution (Supplementary Note 1). A majority (n = 2533) of the sox gene clusters also harbored soxCD, which were located either upstream or downstream of the other sox constituents (Fig. 1b, c). Beyond the formation of gene clusters, additional paralogs of soxB (n = 65), soxA (n = 978), soxX (n = 1097), soxY (n = 1298), soxZ (n = 1273), and soxC (n = 951), and soxD (n = 803) were found insulated from the recognized sox gene clusters in the genome (Supplementary Data 1), likely resulting from the gene duplication or horizontal gene transfer30. Some genomes with sox gene clusters additionally contained the core genes for either rDsr- (n = 615 genomes) or sHdr-mediated sulfur oxidation pathways (n = 157 genomes) (Supplementary Data 1).

The identified sox gene clusters were derived from 3795 species, all but one affiliated to eighteen bacterial phyla (Fig. 1c). This phylum-level distribution of sox-carrying microorganisms is narrower compared to a previous study that detected sox genes in 21 phyla22, and we attributed this discrepancy to our strict rule in homology search and the requirement for the presence of the recognizable gene clusters. Only one sox gene cluster was derived from an archaeal species, MGIIa-L1 sp002726395, belonging to the phylum Thermoplasmatota (Fig. 1c). The putative archaeal sox gene cluster shares over 80% sequence identity with bacterial homologs, which suggests it resulted from inter-domain horizontal gene transfer or erroneous genome assembly or binning. Among all sox-carrying microorganisms, 77 species (2%) from the phyla Aquificota, Campylobacterota, and Pseudomonadota harbor two or more copies of sox gene clusters (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Such multi-sox systems in the genome might facilitate microbial adaptation to fluctuating pH conditions or different hosts, as demonstrated for Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans34 or for symbiotic sulfur oxidizers35, respectively.

The majority (62%) of sox-carrying genomes are from uncultured bacteria (Fig. 1c), indicating diverse uncharacterized thiosulfate-oxidizing microorganisms across various biomes. For instance, the uncultured microorganisms from the phyla SZUA-79, Methylomirabilota, and Eremiobacterota have only recently been recognized for their thiosulfate-oxidizing potential36–38. Uncultured members of the class Thermodesulfovibrionia and the order Desulfobulbales are discovered to harbor a complete sox cluster for thiosulfate oxidation (Supplementary Data 1), expanding their physiological role in sulfur reduction or disproportionation known from the cultivated counterparts39–43.

Origin of a truncated Sox pathway in the Archean eon

The broader phylogenetic distribution of sox genes across bacterial phyla triggered our efforts to re-examine the evolutionary history of the Sox system. To this end, we performed phylogenetic analyses for all individual components (SoxABXYZCD) encoded in the identified sox gene clusters. The component modules comprising SoxBYZ, SoxAX, or SoxCD showed partly different evolutionary histories in the Pseudomonadota lineage (Supplementary Fig. 2), and therefore, they were analyzed separately. Phylogenies based on concatenated alignment of SoxAX and SoxBYZ share an overall congruence with the reference bacterial phylogeny (Fig. 2a, b, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4a, b). Both exhibit two well-supported clades of Gracilicutes, with Clade I containing Aquificota and Campylobacterota, and Clade II comprising bacterial groups including Nitrospirota, SZUA-79, and Pseudomonadota (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Figs. 3, 4a, b). The major phyla or classes in the two clades are recovered as distinct lineages, with their relationships resembling the organismal tree (Fig. 2a). The overall consistency of topology between gene tree and species tree supports detectable signals of vertical inheritance for SoxAX and SoxBYZ30 and by extension, these components may have already been present in an ancient Gracilicutes member that represented the last common ancestor (LCA) of microorganisms in Clade I and II (Fig. 2a). This interpretation is further corroborated by gene-species tree reconciliation, which revealed the origin of SoxBYZ and SoxAX to the LCA of clade I and II with presence probability (PP) of 1 (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Note 3). A few discrepancies between the SoxAX and SoxBYZ phylogenies were nevertheless visible. For instance, in the SoxAX tree, some Aquificota and Deinococcota members form a well-supported lineage in Clade I together with several Pseudomonadota members (Fig. 2b). Yet, this phylogenetic association is absent in the SoxBYZ tree, and all Pseudomonadota members are placed within Clade II (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4a, b).

To study the timing of the LCA of Clades I and II, we performed a series (n = 14) of molecular clock analyses accounting for alternative molecular clock types, root prior distribution, time calibration sets, and species backbone topologies (Supplementary Tables 3, 4). All analytic scenarios dated the LCA of Clades I and II in Archean eon prior to 2.9 billion years ago (Ga) with >95% posterior probability (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 3, 4; Supplementary Note 4), consistent with five independent bacterial chronology studies (Fig. 3c)44–48. These estimated dates suggest a truncated Sox pathway, comprising SoxABXYZ, may have existed at least 600 million years before the Great Oxidation Event (GOE, ~2.35 Ga). The early origin of the truncated Sox pathway is compatible with a gene birth dating study (Supplementary Note 3), which estimated the emergence of sox genes (e.g., soxBYZAX) at 2.6–2.7 Ga (Supplementary Table 2)22,49. Notably, the source of thiosulfate for the Archean Sox system would likely result from re-oxidation of sulfide produced from dissimilatory reduction of sulfite or sulfate, similar to the thiosulfate-shunt scenario in modern anoxic marine and freshwater ecosystems50,51. The dissimilatory sulfate/sulfite reduction pathway should have already been in operation around the invention time of the Sox system, considering its earlier origin in the Paleoarchean (3.6–3.2 Ga)52. However, the oxidants that could have been coupled with Sox-mediated sulfur oxidation during the Archean eon remain an open question. One possibility is nitrate, given that an aerobic nitrogen cycle driven by cryptic oxygen cycling has been established before 2.7 Ga53. The proposed emergence of denitrification genes napA and narG around 2.8 Ga also suggests the presence and metabolic utilization of nitrate at that time54. Nitrate-driven sulfur oxidation in the anoxic Archean environments is, in principle, analogous to the coupling of thiosulfate oxidation and nitrate reduction observed in the oxygen minimum zone of the modern ocean55. Alternatively, oxygen itself could have served as an electron acceptor in oxygen-rich microenvironments such as Archean marine oxygen “oases”, before the GOE56. In support of these inferences, the early branching microorganisms encoding the truncated Sox system, such as Campylobacterota and Aquificota, can oxidize thiosulfate with oxygen or nitrate57–60, with the genetic potential for both oxygen and nitrate respiration being prevalent among sox-carrying microorganisms (Supplementary Fig. 8). These findings imply the establishment of genetic potential for the oxidative parts of the sulfur cycle before the GOE, in accordance with the presence of sulfur-cycling genes in an early Earth analog61.

Assembly of the truncated Sox system with rDsr in the Archean eon

The truncated Sox system (SoxABXYZ) requires interactions with the rDsr, SoxCD, or sHdr complexes for complete oxidation of thiosulfate (Fig. 1a)62. It has been proposed that the rDsr pathway is an ancient metabolic pathway potentially dating back to the Archean22, yet the timing of its linkage with the truncated Sox system for thiosulfate oxidation remains unresolved. Here, we explored the origin of the evolutionary assembly of two systems through phylogenetic analyses of marker proteins (i.e., rDsrAB) of the rDsr pathway in sox-hosting microorganisms. The resulting rDsrAB phylogeny, rooted by reductive-type DsrAB, revealed overall congruence with the reference bacterial species tree and phylogeny of rDsrAB from all sulfur-oxidizing species (Fig. 2a, c and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4d). The tree contains a split between Nitrospirota and Pseudomonadota at root. The Pseudomonadota clade is further divided into groups of Gammaproteobacteria, Magnetococcia, and Alphaproteobacteria (Fig. 2c), but also contains Bacteroidota of possible lateral origin63. These clustering patterns are consistent with a predominantly vertical inheritance of rDsrAB in sox-hosting Pseudomonadota64, tracing the assembly of rDsrAB with the Sox system back to the LCA of Pseudomonadota. The association between rDsrAB and the Sox system in Pseudomonadota represents a trait that either descended from the LCA of Nitrospirota and Pseudomonadota or was laterally transferred from an unknown ancestor of Nitrospirota. In either case, the linked rDsr-Sox pathway may have already been present in the LCA of Pseudomonadota, as also supported by phylogenetic reconciliation (PP = 1; Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Note 3). The age of the crown group of Pseudomonadota was consistently dated before the GOE according to both our and others’ chronology (Figs. 2d, 3c and Supplementary Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 2–4; Supplementary Note 3, 4)44–48, implying the ancestral rDsr-coupled Sox pathway originated in anoxic environments. This inference aligns with the anaerobic lifestyle of taxa encoding rDsr-coupled Sox metabolism, including Bacteroidota (e.g., Chlorobaculum tepidum; formerly known as Chlorobi) and Nitrospirota65. The anoxic origin of rDsrAB is also consistent with its elevated abundance or transcription levels in oxygen-depleted or low-oxygen compared to the high-oxygen environments24. In contrast to rDsrAB, the phylogenetic analysis of the sHdr system in sox-encoding microorganisms revealed complex evolutionary scenarios that date the coupling between sHdr and Sox system over a broad range of geological timescales, either before or after the GOE (Supplementary Note 5). Expanded taxonomic sampling of microbial species encoding both systems is needed to resolve their time of origin in relation to the GOE.

Expansion of the truncated Sox system following the GOE

The narrower taxonomic distribution of SoxCD compared to SoxABXZY suggested the recruitment of soxCD and the formation of a complete sox gene cluster as a more recent evolutionary event, occurring after the emergence of the truncated Sox system. To test this hypothesis, we first reconstructed a phylogenetic tree using only SoxCD encoded in the complete sox gene cluster (hereafter termed in-cluster SoxCD). Unlike SoxBYZ and SoxAX, the unrooted SoxCD tree is composed primarily of polyphyletic clades of Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria (Supplementary Fig. 11). Comparison between gene tree and species tree yielded equivocal scenarios regarding the evolutionary origin of SoxCD (Supplementary Fig. 11), underscoring the necessity of rooting to clarify alternative scenarios. An initial attempt to root the tree using sulfite oxidase (SO) as an outgroup revealed low bootstrap support for the majority of deep branches, probably due to the long branch attraction caused by high sequence divergence between SoxC and SO (Supplementary Fig. 11). To alleviate these artifacts, more closely related homologs of in-cluster SoxCD—the paralogous copies of SoxCD genomically secluded from the main sox gene cluster—were included in the phylogeny66. The resulting tree showed a well-supported bifurcation between the in-cluster and paralogous SoxCD (Supplementary Fig. 12a), consistent with an ancient gene duplication event giving rise to two groups of SoxCD. The paralogous SoxCD may have experienced functional divergence compared to their in-cluster peers following the duplication event. This is supported by (1) an experimentally validated member from Hyphomicrobium denitrificans XT that lacks functional activities described for in-cluster (canonical) homologs (Supplementary Fig. 12a)67,68, (2) higher dN/dS compared to in-cluster SoxCD indicating relaxed purifying selection (Supplementary Table 5), and (3) consistently shorter SoxD (P < 2.2 × 10−16, r = 0.509; Mann–Whitney U test) with sparse distribution of a potential redox-active motif (CMXXC) conserved among in-cluster SoxD (Supplementary Fig. 12b)69. These properties established paralogous SoxCD as a robust outgroup for resolving the evolutionary relationships of in-cluster SoxCD. Occasionally, paralogous SoxCD (e.g., from Campylobacterota) possessing redox-active motifs are placed within the clade of in-cluster SoxCD (Supplementary Fig. 12a, c), likely representing the relics of recent duplication or gene transfer events of the sox gene cluster30. Deinoccocota SoxCD branches within the paralogous counterparts despite being adjacent to other sox genes (Supplementary Fig. 12a). These soxCD were also likely acquired through gene transfer, as evidenced by the detection of mobile genetic elements (e.g., integrases and transposases) within the same gene context (Supplementary Fig. 12d).

Rooting with paralogous SoxCD revealed a distinct evolutionary history of in-cluster SoxCD compared to SoxAX or SoxBYZ. A clear separation between Clade I and II observed for SoxAX and SoxBYZ is lacking, due to the absence of SoxCD in the Aquificota (Clade I), Nitrospirota (Clade II), and SZUA-79 (Clade II) (Figs. 2a, 3b and Supplementary Fig. 4c). In contrast, the rooted phylogeny of in-cluster SoxCD recovered two large Pseudomonadota clades: one predominantly comprising Burkholderiales from Gammaproteobacteria, and the other encompassing Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria, with Eremiobacterota and Campylobacterota nested within shallow branches (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 4c). The latter resolved most Gammaproteobacteria orders as monophyletic groups, with their relationships largely congruent with species tree (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 4c). Such topology supports an origin of in-cluster SoxCD within Gammaproteobacteria, followed by multiple times of lateral transfer to other taxa, including Campylobacterota, Alphaproteobacteria, and Eremiobacterota. Corroborating this interpretation, likelihood-based tests rejected an alternative supporting the Pseudomonadota origin of SoxCD (Supplementary Fig. 14; Supplementary Table 6), and phylogenetic reconciliation mapped the ancestor of extant in-cluster SoxCD to an ancient organism within the Gammaproteobacteria group (Supplementary Table 1; Supplementary Note 3). Notably, the HGT of soxCD to Alphaproteobacteria likely represents an ancient event, predating the diversification of Gammaproteobacteria into subsequent orders like Methylococcales and Woeseiales. Moreover, this transfer may have led to the formation of a new synteny of sox genes, with soxCD situated in front of soxABXYZ (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 15).

The molecular clock analyses estimated the median age for the LCA of Gammaproteobacteria, corresponding to the origin of in-cluster SoxCD, at ~2.4 Ga under uncorrelated molecular clock, and at ~2.1 Ga under autocorrelated clock (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Tables 3, 4). The 95% credible interval was either overlapping (2.70–2.14 Ga in uncorrelated clock) with or significantly younger (2.45–1.83 Ga based on autocorrelated clock) than the time windows for GOE (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5; Supplementary Tables 3, 4; Supplementary Note 4). These date intervals cover the estimates from previous timing efforts (Fig. 3c)44–48, and support our hypothesis, i.e., the recruitment of SoxCD by the truncated Sox system during or after the GOE, subsequent to the origin of the truncated Sox system. The timing of SoxC is younger than a previous estimate of 2.77 Ga, which may have overestimated the age due to the inclusion of functionally divergent SorA homologs during phylogenetic analysis22. Indeed, re-analysis of the same dataset using our more conservative homolog identification pipeline supports a substantially younger emergence time of in-cluster SoxCD (Supplementary Table 2; Supplementary Note 3). Our revised chronology suggests that the incorporation of SoxCD into the truncated Sox system represents an adaptive innovation to the post-GOE environments. The GOE introduced oxygen as a potent electron acceptor to the biosphere, rendering sulfur oxidation more exergonic (energy-releasing) compared to the anoxic, pre-GOE environments. SoxCD enables host microorganisms to exploit this new thermodynamic landscape by fully oxidizing thiosulfate with oxygen, releasing more electrons for energy production than the truncated counterpart70. The dependence of SoxCD on oxygen likely arises from the high-potential electrons it generates (278 ± 10 mV)71, which require coupling with strong oxidants like oxygen to drive energy recovery. The interplay between oxygen availability and SoxCD emergence is imprinted in the oxygen tolerance trait of sox-carrying microorganisms and their environmental distribution along the redox gradient. SoxCD-containing lineages are often oxygen-tolerant, favoring oxic or suboxic environments, whereas those lacking SoxCD are typically oxygen-intolerant and confined to anoxic habitats (Fig. 1c). This pattern is also evident from the distribution of Sox-hosting microorganisms in aquatic systems, where microorganisms possessing the complete Sox pathway (e.g., Halothiobacillus and Thiomonas) predominantly occupy oxic waters, whereas those utilizing the truncated Sox pathway (e.g., Thiobacillus) dominate anoxic waters24,72,73.

Ecology and phylogeny constrain the dissemination of Sox pathways among bacteria

Following the origin, distinct variants of Sox pathways have subsequently disseminated among bacterial species over time, resulting in marked distribution patterns among extant microorganisms. These include (1) mutually exclusive SoxCD- and rDsr-coupled pathways, with a few exceptions62 (Supplementary Note 7), (2) narrow taxonomic distribution of rDsr- or sHdr-coupled Sox pathways (Fig. 1c), (3) mosaic Sox systems where components have discrete origin30, and (4) varied sox gene cluster arrangements (Supplementary Note 1). We reasoned that the extant patterns result from macroevolution processes such as HGT, operon reshuffling, and adaptive gene loss, which are known to be influenced by ecological preferences, phylogenetic background, and dispersal limitation74. To disentangle the effects of ecology, phylogeny, and geography on the evolution and dissemination of Sox pathways, we constructed a random forest (RF) model to predict the distinct assemblies of the Sox pathways in each species. The model incorporates a range of explanatory factors, including ecological preferences (e.g., pH, oxygen, salinity, and temperature), phylogenetic identities, and the geographic distribution of the host microorganisms (Supplementary Fig. 17; Supplementary Data 6). Phylogenetic identities were modeled as the top three principal components (PCs) of the species phylogeny that explains 77.85% of the variations in phylogenetic distance. Similarly, geographic constraints were modeled by selecting the top three principal components decomposed from the geographic distance matrix, which explain 77.81% of the variation in geographic distance.

Our RF model demonstrated that ecological and phylogenetic factors, rather than geographic location, primarily shape the dissemination of Sox pathway assemblies across bacterial species (Fig. 4a). Oxygen preference of the host microbes emerged as a significant ecological factor (P = 2.5 × 10−2; permutation test, n = 100), contributing significantly to model accuracy (Fig. 4a). This result was robustly supported by an alternative RF model that accounted for potential confounding effects of species phylogeny on oxygen preference (“Methods”). Additionally, we found a significant association between the Sox pathway assembly and oxygen preference (χ²(4) = 258.27, P < 2.2 × 10−16, Cramér’s V = 0.22; χ² test). Specifically, the SoxCD-enabled pathway was over-represented in aerobic vs. anaerobic species (Z-score = 12.74, P < 2.2 × 10−16; two-sided standard residual analysis), while the rDsr pathway was more prevalent in anaerobes (Z-score = 15.37; P < 2.2 × 10−16; two-sided standard residual analysis). Microorganisms with both pathways were mostly facultative anaerobes (Z-score = 3.23, P = 2.2 × 10−3, two-sided standard residual analysis) (Fig. 4b). Additionally, phylogenetic identity of the species was a significant predictor of Sox pathways composition (P = 2.5 × 10−2; permutation test, n = 100) (Fig. 4a), which shows the phylogenetic conservatism of Sox pathway assembly75. This observation is supported by a significant phylogenetic signal for different Sox pathway assemblies (P = 0; permutation test, n = 1000) (Supplementary Table 7). In contrast, geographic components were not identified as significant predictors in our model (Fig. 4a), indicating that biogeography is not an important factor during Sox pathway assembly.

Our analyses revealed that the assembly and dissemination of the Sox pathway are constrained by both ecological and phylogenetic processes. The oxygen requirement was identified as a significant ecological factor determining whether the truncated Sox pathway is assembled with rDsr or SoxCD. This is consistent with the observed niche partitioning of the microorganisms with the two pathways along environmental oxygen gradients in tailing impoundment water, hot spring, and marine environments24,72,73. Our results expand previous studies by showing that the impact of oxygen is not confined to local environments, but likely operates on a longer evolutionary timescale, ranging from the origin of the Sox pathway to its diversification across species. Mechanistically, the ecological constraints imposed by oxygen are likely associated with the intrinsic biochemical properties of different Sox pathways. The truncated Sox and rDsr pathway, known for its higher energy efficiency, is favored in energy-limited anoxic habitats, whereas the complete Sox pathway, with its superior kinetic traits, is more adaptive in oxic habitats76,77. However, the dissemination of Sox pathways is also shaped by phylogenetic inertia78. We found that species used different Sox pathway assemblies depending on their phylogenetic position in the tree (Fig. 4c). The conservatism of a specific Sox pathway in a clade likely arises from the strong co-selection pressures on Sox pathway components, which maintain them as a coherent functional unit from ancestral to descent microbial species. In support of this, we detected significant co-evolutionary patterns of the truncated Sox system with rDsrAB (F(1, 336399) = 3.051 × 106, P < 2.2 × 10−16, R2 = 0.90; two-sided F-tests) and with SoxCD (F(1, 4674243) = 1.465 × 108, P < 2.2 × 10−16, R2 = 0.97; two-sided F-tests), as indicated by the high topological similarity of their phylogenies (Fig. 4d). Functional interdependencies among these pathway components likely drive co-evolution, potentially restricting the integration of divergently evolved modules into existing Sox systems, and limiting the random genetic exchange between the species.

Recent recruitment of bacterial Sox components by viruses on timescales of centuries to millennia

The dissemination of bacterial sox genes is further facilitated by virus-host interactions, where viruses capture the sox genes from the bacterial hosts for reproduction purpose. It has been proposed that the auxiliary sox genes encoded by viruses have the potential to augment the thiosulfate oxidation metabolism in bacterial hosts, and contribute to biogeochemical sulfur cycling at ecosystem levels26. Yet, an overview of the evolutionary trajectory of sox genes in viruses remains lacking. To this end, we searched the latest Integrated Microbial Genomes/Viruses (IMG/VR v7.0) database for the presence of sox genes using the bacterial Sox phylogenies as references (“Methods”). A total of 26 genes, covering all components of the Sox system except SoxC, were found in 24 metagenomic viral contigs (mVCs). These mVCs were recovered from various ecosystems, including aquatic, terrestrial, engineered, and host-associated environments (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Data 7). Taxonomic classification revealed that all mVCs belonged to the Caudoviricetes class, except one affiliated with Faserviricetes (Supplementary Data 7). Twenty mVCs were predicted to follow a lytic lifestyle, whereas the remaining six were classified as lysogenic (Supplementary Data 7). The predominance of a lytic lifestyle is consistent with the tendency of lytic viruses to encode energy-related auxiliary metabolic genes that are beneficial or essential for viral reproduction79. One mVC was identified as proviruses (Supplementary Data 7), providing direct evidence for the genetic exchange between virus and host. Most viral sox genes are encoded in isolated loci on mVCs rather than forming sox gene clusters as in bacterial genomes (Supplementary Fig. 18). This pattern is consistent with previous analyses of auxiliary metabolic genes involved in carbon, sulfur, and phosphorus cycling26,80,81. Notably, the number of viral soxY/Z candidates detected herein (n = 10) was significantly lower than that reported by Kieft et al. (n = 50)26. We attributed the discrepancy to our strict criteria in detecting functional orthologs of viral sox genes (Supplementary Note 8).

Evolutionary placement analysis revealed that viral Sox sequences were broadly distributed within the bacterial Sox phylogenies (Fig. 5b). The detected SoxB/A/X/Y/Z/D were closely associated with bacterial lineages such as Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria in the SoxBYZ, SoxAX, and SoxCD trees (Fig. 5b). Such scattered phylogenetic placement indicates recurrent recruitment of the sox genes by viruses in diverse bacterial taxa. The virus-encoded Sox proteins often were phylogenetic neighbors of those encoded in the predicted bacterial hosts, yet exceptions exist (Fig. 5c). For example, a virus (Ga0182408_119348) was predicted to infect and capture genes from members of Enterobacterales based on CRISPR spacer matches, but SoxZ encoded by the virus was placed within the reference SoxZ clade spanned by Burkholderiales (Fig. 5c). This discrepancy could be explained by inter-virus gene exchange during co-infection or broader host range than currently predicted82,83 (Supplementary Note 9). Notably, most of the viral Sox proteins shared high sequence identity (>80%) with bacterial homologs (Fig. 5a), and were placed near the tips of the bacterial protein phylogeny, with relative evolutionary divergence (RED) values approaching 1 (>0.9) (Fig. 5b). The close phylogenetic association of virus-host Sox protein pairs suggests conserved enzymatic functions, which supports direct utilization of viral proteins to complement host sulfur oxidation processes. Based on the medium nucleotide dissimilarity of the virus-host pairs (0.17 substitutions per site) and the substitution rate of DNA virus (10−8 to 10−6 substitutions per site per cell cycle)84, the observed viral recruitment of the host proteins was estimated to have occurred over the last few hundreds to tens of thousands of years. This suggests genetic exchanges between virus and host occurred at much shorter timescales compared to the billion-year-history of the Sox system (Fig. 3c). The consistently recent nature of the recruited genes reflects a continuous influx of contemporary bacterial sox variants to viral genomes, potentially allowing the viruses to maintain functional compatibility with the evolving bacterial Sox systems. However, this apparent recency does not exclude the occurrence of more ancient genetic exchanges throughout billions of years of virus-bacteria interactions. Rather, these ancient events may have become obscured due to (1) displacement of earlier acquired bacterial genes by more recent homologs or (2) restricted sampling of contemporary virus diversity.

Discussion

Earth’s surface environments have undergone profound changes throughout its 4.5-billion-year history. Given the critical role of MSO pathways in shaping global biogeochemical cycles, understanding their evolutionary trajectory along the geological timeline is fundamental to both biological and Earth sciences. Through history reconstruction using the updated chronology of bacterial life, we traced the emergence of MSO pathways mediated by the truncated Sox system and/or its interaction with rDsr back to the Archean (4.0–2.5 Ga). The inferred date aligns with fossil evidence of sulfur-oxidizing microorganisms at 2.52 Ga17, and informs ongoing debates about whether thiosulfate-cycling genes originated early in the Archean or later in the Proterozoic era until the GOE19,22,49. The early emergence of the thiosulfate-oxidizing Sox system suggests that thiosulfate has already been available as a key intermediate for regulating sulfur fluxes since Archean eon, similar to the thiosulfate shunt established in modern analogs of Archean environments, such as anoxic, low sulfate freshwater ecosystems51,85–87. These findings broaden our view of the Archean sulfur cycle, which has been conventionally considered dominated by organoclastic sulfate reduction19, anaerobic sulfide oxidation via anoxygenic photosynthesis metabolism52, and atmospheric photochemical reactions of gaseous sulfur species (e.g., SO2)88.

Following the GOE, the rise in atmospheric oxygen created ecological conditions that favored the metabolic expansion of MSO pathways. One of such expansions, as revealed by our study, was the recruitment of SoxCD by the truncated Sox system, giving rise to a novel pathway for complete thiosulfate oxidation independent of rDsr. Encoded in an operon-like structure, the newly assembled pathway likely conferred adaptive advantages in oxic environments by enabling fine-tuned regulation of gene expression and more efficient thiosulfate oxidation. The integration of SoxCD into preexisting truncated Sox systems exemplifies a “legacy-plus-innovation” mode of evolution, whereby ancestral metabolic frameworks were augmented by novel functional modules in response to altered environments89. A similar evolutionary mode has been documented across multiple metabolic systems, such as those for photosynthetic carbon fixation and arsenic detoxification90–92, and likely represents a common strategy by which microbial life adapted to Earth’s changing redox landscapes over time. The inferred timing of the metabolic innovation supports a link between MSO pathways and fundamental transitions in the chemical properties of Earth’s surface ensuing the GOE22. For example, the evolution of oxygen-adapted MSO pathways may have facilitated the expansion of oxidative sulfur cycling after the GOE, as evidenced by increased sulfur isotope variation in contemporary sedimentary rocks19. Moreover, the higher acidity-generation potential of the SoxCD-coupled pathway24 suggests its emergence and diversification could have contributed to the acid rock drainage and accelerated land-to-ocean phosphorus fluxes following the GOE93–95.

As Earth became increasingly oxygenated and thiosulfate availability rose, variants of the Sox pathway proliferated across the bacterial tree of life. Our analyses revealed that this dissemination trajectory is constrained by phylogenetic background and oxygen preferences. In particular, more ancient pathways tend to be confined among anaerobes in the context of similar phylogenetic placements. These constraints help explain both evolutionary and ecological patterns of Sox systems, including (1) the intense period of HGT of soxC and related genes, coinciding approximately with the time of Earth’s second oxygenation event (0.85–0.54 Ga)22, and (2) a distinct assemblage of thiosulfate-oxidizing bacteria along the environmental oxygen gradient24. Together, these findings highlight how organismal evolutionary history and habitats influence the variants of Sox pathways they employ. The global principles uncovered here offer a testable framework in predicting the large-scale spatiotemporal patterns of Sox systems, and lays groundwork for future studies in evolution and biogeochemistry of MSO systems.

In all, our findings highlight the complex interplay between ecology, phylogeny, and biogeochemistry that has shaped the modern complexity of MSO pathways over geological times, offering new perspectives on the co-evolution of life and Earth’s surface environments.

Methods

Homology search of genes involved in dissimilatory sulfur oxidation in microbial genomes

Homologs of each protein of the Sox system (SoxXYZABCD) were retrieved from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB release 214) using hmmsearch command from the HMMER package (v3.2.1; http://hmmer.org/). The relaxed cutoff (--cut_nc) of hmmsearch was used to ensure the sensitivity of homology search. As solitary sox genes may not function in thiosulfate oxidation96, all retrieved homologs of the Sox system were subject to a synteny check, and only those found in complete or nearly complete sox gene clusters were retained for subsequent analysis. Syntenic sox gene clusters were required to contain (i) soxB, (ii) at least one of soxA and soxX, (iii) at least one of soxY and soxZ, and (iv) less than four non-sox genes between any two sox genes. This criterion reflects the minimum composition of a functional Sox system69. All sox genes in this study refer to those in the detected sox gene clusters. Microorganisms containing syntenic sox gene clusters in their genomes were considered as sox-hosting microorganisms. The SoxCD homologs present in the sox-hosting microorganisms were classified into two categories depending on their genomic context. The first category comprises SoxCD genes located within the sox gene clusters, i.e., forming a complete sox cluster (soxABXYZCD) and were designated “in-cluster soxCD.” The second category of SoxCD genes was classified as “paralogous soxCD” based on two criteria: (1) the host microorganism encodes a complete sox gene cluster and (2) the homologs are solitary from the other sox genes (e.g., soxABXYZ). Parallel hmmsearch pipelines were further applied to screen for genes involved in sHdr and rDsr pathways in sox-hosting microorganisms. Gene clusters containing (i) shdrA, (ii) at least one of shdrB1 and shdrB2, and (iii) at least one of shdrC1 and shdrC2, were regarded as shdr gene clusters. The rdsr gene clusters were detected using the following criteria: (1) the presence of both rdsrA and rdsrB, or (2) the presence of one of either rdsrA or rdsrB combined with at least one of dsrEFH to account for incomplete genomes that lack one of rdsrA or rdsrB. The metabolic potential of sox-hosting microorganisms for oxygen and nitrate respiration was inferred based on the presence/absence of genes encoding cytochrome c oxidases (ccoNOPQ and coxABCD) and nitrate reductases (narG and napA), using HMM profiles from KoFam97. All the HMMs used in this study have been deposited in a repository (https://github.com/RHarperR/SoxEvolution/). Details for hmmsearch are listed in Supplementary Table 8. Mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were identified using DIAMOND (v0.9.14.115) with the --sensitive parameter to enable sensitive homology searches against mobileOG-db98,99.

Phylogenetic analyses

Homologs of Sox proteins from the sox-hosting microorganisms were dereplicated using cd-hit v4.8.1 with -c 0.8 option100. Representatives of each Sox protein were aligned using MUSCLE (v5) with default parameters and then trimmed with TrimAl v1.4.rev15 using the -automated1 option101,102. To check for topological consistency, phylogenies of individual Sox components were reconstructed with IQ-TREE (v2.2.0) using best-fit substitution models selected by ModelFinder (option -m MFP) and branch support assessed with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates (option -B 1000)103. Based on the overall topological consistency (Supplementary Fig. 2), three concatenated datasets for SoxBYZ, SoxAX, and SoxCD were created to enhance phylogenetic resolution. Phylogeny based on the concatenated alignment of SoxBYZ, SoxAX, and SoxCD was inferred using IQ-TREE, with automated model selection (option -m MFP) and 1000 ultrafast bootstraps (option -B 1000). To account for site-heterogeneous substitution, alternative gene phylogenies were reconstructed using complex mixture models. To achieve this, C10-C60 mixture models were included in the model selection process (option -mset LG + C10-C60), and the optimal model was then used to infer gene trees according to the posterior mean site frequency method104,105. The deep branching patterns of resulting trees are nearly identical to those obtained with basic models, indicating the robustness of our topology against substitution model complexity (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Note 2). The SoxCD phylogeny was rooted using three methods: midpoint ancestral divergence rooting, outgroup rooting, and paralogous rooting. Only paralogous rooting was selected as it yielded the highest phylogenetic resolution in deep branches (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 11). To evaluate the likelihoods of different evolutionary scenarios, the rooted SoxCD tree was further subjected to topology tests, including Kishino-Hasegawa (KH), Shimodaira-Hasegawa (SH), and Approximately Unbiased (AU) tests using IQ-TREE (with parameters -n 0 -zb 1000 -zw -au) to compare alternative tree topologies versus the maximum likelihood topology. Phylogenies of rDsrAB and sHdrAB1B2C1C2 were resolved using analogous workflows. The substitution models selected for each protein set were listed in Supplementary Table 9. To facilitate the inferences of evolutionary history for Sox-related genes, phylogenetic reconciliation of gene tree and species tree was performed using RANGER-DTL (v2.0)106 following the pipeline proposed by Nishihara et al.107 (Supplementary Methods 1). Additionally, an independent cross-validation was conducted by re-analyzing data from a previous study on the evolution of sulfur-cycling enzymes22. Here, Sox-related genes were firstly identified using our conservative homolog identification pipeline, after which the resulting gene trees were reconciled against the dated species tree using ecceTERA (v1.2.4)108 following the protocols and parameter settings of the original study (Supplementary Methods 1).

Reconstruction of the species tree

To perform the molecular clock analyses, we first reconstructed a reference phylogeny of archaea and bacteria through a benchmarked MarkerFinder pipeline developed by Martinez-Gutierrez and Alyward104. The reference phylogeny includes (1) the taxa from a recent study incorporating a wide sampling of major phyla of bacteria and archaea, and (2) members from candidate phyla such as SZUA-79, Eremiobacterota, and Methylomirabilota. All selected genomes were listed in Supplementary Data 5. Applying MarkerFinder pipeline to the selected genome dataset resulted in a trimmed concatenated alignment of 27 ribosomal genes and 3 RNA polymerase genes. Phylogenetic tree inference was performed with IQ-TREE using the LG + R10 model (selected by ModelFinder in IQ-TREE) with 1000 ultrafast bootstraps104. The tree with the highest likelihood (Supplementary Fig. 3a) was manually inspected and refined via pruning and re-grafting using the ETE3 toolkit109 to ensure the monophyly of (1) the Archaea, (2) the DST group comprising phylum Deinococcota, Synergistota, and Thermotogota, (3) the Terrabacteria superphylum, and (4) the Gracilicutes superphylum (Supplementary Fig. 3b)45,104,110. Likelihood-based tests of topologies, including Kishino-Hasegawa (KH), Shimodaira-Hasegawa (SH), and Approximately Unbiased (AU) tests, were performed using IQ-TREE (-n 0 -zb 1000 -zw -au) to assess the fitness of the refined tree to the alignment. No significant difference in likelihood was observed between the maximum-likelihood tree and refined trees (Supplementary Table 10). To assess the robustness of the inferred topology, alternative species trees were reconstructed using both mixture and partition models in IQ-TREE. Phylogenomic inferences based on complex mixture models (LG + C60 + G) followed the same procedure as described for gene trees. For partition analysis, each gene block of the concatenated alignment was allowed to have its own substitution models and evolutionary rates (Supplementary Table 11), with edge length constrained to be proportional across partitions (option -p). In both cases, the resulting trees are highly congruent with those obtained using the site-homogenous model (Supplementary Fig. 3; Supplementary Note 2).

Molecular clock analyses

Dating of the reference species phylogeny was performed using PhyloBayes v4.1c, with a CAT20 substitution model (-cat20), a birth-death process (-bd), and four gamma categories (-dgam 4)111. The CAT20 model was chosen because previous tests showed that the analyses using a full CAT model fail to converge within a reasonable time90. The clocks were calibrated with a set of temporal constraints based on fossil evidence and strictly aerobic metabolisms (Supplementary Fig. 6; Supplementary Methods 2)45. Both the autocorrelated lognormal (-ln) and uncorrelated gamma multiplier (-ugam) relaxed clocks were applied to model the rate variations across lineages. Two root prior distributions were implemented to constrain the age of the last universal common ancestor, respectively: a uniform distribution ranging from 4.40 to 3.46 Ga, and a gamma distribution centered at 3.95 Ga with a standard deviation of 0.23 Ga45,90. For each of the four primary analytic settings (scenario 1–4 in Supplementary Table 3), two independent PhyloBayes Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains were run in parallel for 2 months (>40,000 model cycles), with parameters sampled every 20 cycles (Supplementary Data 8). The convergence of MCMC chains was checked using the program tracecomp. Moreover, a series of additional Bayesian analytic scenarios were set up to ensure the robustness of dating results to alternative temporal constraints (scenarios 5–12 in Supplementary Table 4) and to species backbones reconstructed using site-heterogenous models (scenarios 13–14 in Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Methods 3). These analyses employed an uncorrelated molecular clock, which yielded consistent results with the autocorrelated clock in our primary analyses but likely produced more robust estimates. This is indicated by faster convergence, larger effective sampling size, and smaller discrepancies between independent chains (Supplementary Fig. 5; Supplementary Methods 3).

In addition, we also compiled chronological data from five independent molecular clock analyses that dated the divergence time of bacteria and/or the prokaryotic tree of life44–48. For datasets with available ultrametric tree files, divergence times for key evolutionary nodes were determined using the R package treeio (v1.18.1)112. When tree files were unavailable, we extracted median node ages or 95% confidence intervals directly from the published figures using WebPlotDigitizer (https://automeris.io/).

dN/dS calculation

Non-synonymous to synonymous substitution (dN/dS) ratios for soxC and soxD sequences were calculated using HYPHY (v2.5)113. Selective pressures were compared between paralogous SoxCD and in-cluster SoxCD using branch-site random-effects likelihood (REL) models. For each group, a mean dN/dS value was estimated with the MG94CUSTOMCF3X4 codon substitution model under a three-rate category partition (012345).

Predicting key variables for the assemblies of sulfur oxidation pathways

To evaluate the influence of phylogeny, geography, and ecology on the dissemination of Sox pathways, we developed a set of predictor variables representing environmental dissimilarity, phylogenetic distances, and geographic distances between sox-hosting microorganisms. Phylogenetic distances between the species were derived using the ape (v5.7) package in R from GTDB (r214) tree of life114. The geographic linear distances between the host species were quantified based on latitude and longitude, using the distHaversine function embedded in the geosphere (v1.5-18) package of R115. The location information of each sox-hosting species was collected from the metadata in the NCBI BioSample records using the genome accession number. For species lacking explicit coordinates information, geographic location (e.g., “Yellowstone hot spring”) from NCBI metadata was resolved to coordinates using the GeoNames geographic database (https://www.geonames.org/). Both phylogenetic and geographic distance matrices were decomposed into principal components (PCs) using the philentropy (v0.7.0) package in R116. PCs from PC1 onward were retained until their cumulative explained variance exceeded 75% of the total variation.

Oxygen, pH, temperature, and salinity were selected as the primary ecological predictors of different Sox pathway assembly. The parameters were chosen based on their potential associations with the distribution and activity of sulfur-oxidizing microorganisms24,117–119. The microbial preferences of the selected environmental factors were predicted using the genomic data of sox-hosting microorganisms. Oxygen preference (aerobic, facultative, and anaerobic) was predicted from the genome using a recently developed classifier120. The optimum pH, temperature, and salinity for growth were predicted using GenomeSPOT121.

Sox pathways were categorized into four classes based on genomic presence/absence of key genes: (1) the complete Sox System without rDsr, where genomes contain at least one of soxC or soxD but lack key genes for rDsr pathway (e.g., dsrAB and dsrEFH); (2) the truncated Sox system coupled with rDsr, where genomes contain the key genes for rDsr system but lack soxCD; (3) the complete Sox system coupled with rDsr, characterized by co-occurrence of truncated sox gene cluster, soxC or soxD, and rdsrAB; and (4) the truncated Sox system without rDsr and SoxCD, where genomes lack soxCD and rdsrAB. Collectively, 3321 sox-hosting microorganisms (87.5% of all sox-hosting species) with complete metadata of the phylogenetic, geographic, and ecological predictors were included in subsequent analyses (Supplementary Data 6).

Random forest models were employed to dissect the primary drivers shaping the distribution of Sox pathway variants among species. The modeling was conducted using the randomForest (v4.7-1.1) and caret (v6.90) packages in R122,123. To determine the best-performing model structure, extensive hyperparameter tuning was conducted through a grid search across a differential combination of mtry (2–10) and n_trees (100–2000) parameters, each implemented within a 10-fold cross-validation. The final model, with mtry of 3 and n_trees of 1400, achieved the best performance, with an out-of-bag (OOB) error rate of 19.72%. Variable importance was then assessed using Gini impurity metrics, and statistical significance (Bonferroni adjusted) of predictors was validated via permutation test (n = 100) with the rfPermute (v2.5.1) package124. Moreover, random forest adjustment was conducted to estimate the influence of oxygen after controlling the variation explained by the species phylogeny using the RFA (v0.2) package in R125. This analysis reaffirmed the statistical significance of oxygen in predicting the Sox pathway assembly (t(3319) = −4.09, P = 4.37 × 10−5, effective size = −0.22, 95% confidence interval = [−0.33, −0.11]; two-sided t-test).

In addition, a series of alternative analyses was performed to support the results of the random forest analysis. To quantify the phylogenetic signal of Sox pathway assemblies, we assessed trait conservatism using the D statistic via the caper package (v1.0.3) in R126,127. To test the co-evolutionary signals between the truncated Sox system and SoxCD/rDsr, the similarity among the phylogenies of truncated Sox system, SoxCD, and rDsr was compared pairwise, with the statistical significance calculated using linear regression analysis. Chi-squared test was performed to examine the statistical significance of the association between a specific Sox pathway variant and oxygen preference of sox-hosting microorganisms using chisq.test() function in R package stats (v3.6.2)128. Standardized residuals (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted) derived from the Chi-squared test were used to indicate whether specific Sox pathway assembly was over-represented or underrepresented within specific oxygen niches129.

Identification of viral Sox proteins

To identify viral Sox proteins, HMMs targeting Sox components were queried against the Integrated Microbial Genomes and Virome (IMG/VR) database (v7.0) using hmmsearch with an E-value threshold of 10⁻⁵130. Sequences of candidate viral Sox homologs were placed into the respective Sox reference tree using the RAxML Evolutionary Placement Algorithm (EPA). All the reference trees were deposited to a repository (https://github.com/RHarperR/SoxEvolution). For EPA alignment, MAFFT (v7.407) was executed with the --add parameter to integrate new sequences into existing reference alignments131. Homologs placed within the established sox-like clade, with cumulative placement probabilities ≥0.95, were retained as functional orthologs. To confirm viral origin, sox-containing contigs were validated using VIBRANT (v1.2.0) for annotation, classification of lytic vs. lysogenic lifestyles, and quality control132. Contigs flagged as nonviral were excluded from subsequent analyses. Additional phylogenetic analyses were conducted to discriminate quinoprotein dehydrogenase-associated SoxYZ-like viral proteins from the genuine functional orthologs of SoxY or SoxZ (Supplementary Methods 4). Taxonomy, ecosystem origin, and genomic features of validated viruses were extracted from IMG/VR metadata. Validated viral contigs were assigned to hosts via two approaches: (1) existing metadata provided by the IMG/VR database, which was gained via isolation source, CRISPR spacer matches, or k-mer matches and (2) whole-genome alignment between viruses and bacterial genomes from GTDB (r214) with near-exact matches (96% identity over 1 kb)133.

To trace the evolutionary origins, viral Sox sequences were placed onto reconciled bacterial phylogenies in this study, following the pipeline described above using EPA. The phylogenetic position of each viral homolog was determined by its highest-probability node placement. Relative evolutionary divergence (RED) values for these internal nodes, contextualizing viral gene origins within the timescale of bacterial sox evolution134, were calculated using the castor (v1.8.3) R package135.

Data availability

The sequence data, genome/sequence accession codes, phylogenetic trees, and the custom code are publicly available at GitHub (https://github.com/RHarperR/SoxEvolution/). The metadata and other data necessary to interpret the findings are provided in the Supplementary Information and Supplementary Data. There are no access restrictions to any of the data. Source data are provided with this paper as a Source Data file.

Code availability

The codes used for the phylogenetic reconstruction for the Sox system (SoxABXYZCD), rDsr (rDsrAB), and sHdr (sHdrAB1B2C1C2), the evolutionary placement of viral protein sequences, molecular dating, random forest analyses, and gene-species tree reconciliation have been deposited at Github (https://github.com/RHarperR/SoxEvolution) and Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.20539185).
