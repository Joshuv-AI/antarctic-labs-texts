Caring behaviors and nursing competency among nursing students in Taiwan: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuh-Shiow Li, Hui-Chen Tseng, Yueh-E Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCaring behaviors are essential for providing high-quality, patient-centered care and constitute a core component of professional nursing practice. Ensuring that nursing students demonstrate competent caring behaviors is crucial for preparing them for professional practice. However, there is a lack of research investigating the relationship between caring behaviors and nursing competency simultaneously. This study aimed to explore the association between caring behaviors and nursing competency among nursing students enrolled in three education programs in Taiwan.MethodsA cross-sectional study with an analytical design was conducted with 600 nursing students recruited through random cluster sampling from multiple nursing programs within one institute in Taiwan. After excluding 35 incomplete questionnaires, 565 participants were included in the final analysis. Participants completed the Chinese versions of the Caring Behaviors Scale (CBS) and the Nursing Competency Scale (NCS). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and Pearson’s correlation analysis to examine associations between caring behaviors and nursing competency, with supplementary subgroup analyses for selected demographic variables.ResultsCaring behaviors were moderately positively correlated with overall nursing competency and its subdomains. Among caring behaviors, “knowing the patient” was most frequently reported, including behaviors related to recognizing patients’ unique values, attending to patients’ concerns, and supporting family involvement in decision-making. The highest-rated nursing competencies reflected respect for clients and families, empathic communication, and ethical practice.ConclusionsCaring behaviors were moderately associated with nursing competency among nursing students. These findings suggest that caring may represent an important component of professional development within nursing education. However, given the analytical cross-sectional design, the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal. Integrating caring-focused strategies into competency-based nursing curricula may help support students’ professional readiness and compassionate, person-centered care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04785-8.Keywords: Caring behaviors, Nursing competency, Undergraduate education, Nursing students

Background

Caring is widely regarded as the essence of nursing and a cornerstone of high-quality nursing education [1]. Beyond a moral ideal, caring encompasses the attitudes, values, and behaviors that shape patients’ experiences of being cared for within healthcare contexts. Person-centered care, which emphasizes individual needs, lived experiences, and cultural perspectives, is therefore recognized as a central purpose of the nursing discipline [2]. Caring behaviors include both instrumental components, such as technical and physical care, and expressive components, such as emotional presence and psychological support, both of which contribute to positive patient experiences and clinical outcomes [3]. Consequently, nursing education plays a critical role in intentionally cultivating these caring capacities. Educational approaches that emphasize caring have been shown to support value internalization, emotional growth, and humanistic development, thereby preparing nursing students to deliver compassionate, patient-centered care [4, 5].

In parallel with the emphasis on caring, contemporary healthcare systems increasingly demand high levels of nursing competency. Rapid clinical advances, rising patient acuity, and persistent workforce shortages have intensified expectations for nurses to demonstrate not only technical proficiency but also sound clinical judgment, effective communication, and ethical reasoning. Nursing competency is now conceptualized as a multidimensional integration of knowledge, skills, attitudes, and values that underpin safe and effective practice [6]. Empirical evidence among Taiwanese nursing students further indicates that relational abilities, particularly communication skills, are significantly associated with overall nursing competence, highlighting the importance of interpersonal dimensions in competency development [7]. These workforce-driven demands underscore the need for educational models that prepare students for complex and evolving clinical roles.

Caring behaviors and competency are closely interconnected, jointly contributing to patient dignity, safety, and well-being [8]. For nursing students, the development of both caring capacity and professional competency prior to entering clinical practice is essential. The final year of nursing education represents a critical transition period, during which students are expected to consolidate their knowledge, skills, and professional values in preparation for entry into practice [9].

Recent scholarship has increasingly framed caring as a developmental and assessable component of nursing competency rather than an innate personal trait. Humanistic caring has been described as progressing through identifiable stages involving presence, compassion, respect, advocacy, and moral responsibility, suggesting that caring capabilities can be systematically cultivated through education and practice [10]. Psychological and reflective processes also contribute to competency development; for example, higher levels of self-compassion have been associated with stronger nursing competency among psychiatric nurses in Japan, indicating that intrapersonal resources shape professional behavior and caring engagement [11]. Similarly, research in gerontological nursing highlights holistic competence as a multidimensional construct influenced by ethical attitudes, contextual factors, and continuing education, reinforcing the interplay between individual and environmental determinants of nursing competency [12].

Despite the growing body of literature, existing research has predominantly examined caring behaviors and nursing competency as related yet distinct constructs, often addressing them in isolation. Empirical evidence that simultaneously investigates their interrelationship remains limited, particularly within nursing education contexts that adopt learner-centered pedagogies such as problem-based learning in Taiwan. Moreover, insufficient attention has been given to how caring behaviors are differentially associated with specific domains of nursing competency during the critical transition from student to professional practice. This gap is particularly important in light of ongoing healthcare workforce challenges and the global shift toward competency-based nursing education, where the integration of relational, ethical, and clinical competencies is increasingly emphasized. Without a clearer understanding of how caring behaviors relate to the development of nursing competency, curriculum design may lack the necessary theoretical and empirical grounding to effectively support students’ professional readiness. Accordingly, this study aims to address this gap by examining the association between caring behaviors and nursing competency among nursing students across different educational programs in Taiwan, thereby providing contextually grounded evidence to inform nursing education and curriculum development.

Theoretical framework

This study is guided by a theoretical perspective that conceptualizes caring behaviors as observable and measurable expressions of caring within nursing education, rather than as an abstract philosophical construct or a general competency alone. Drawing on Watson’s Theory of Human Caring and competency-based nursing education frameworks, the present study focuses specifically on how caring is enacted through nursing students’ behaviors in clinical and educational contexts.

Watson’s Theory of Human Caring provides a philosophical foundation for understanding caring as a relational, moral, and interpersonal process central to nursing practice [13]. However, within nursing education, this abstract conception requires operationalization into observable behaviors that can be taught, practiced, and evaluated. These behaviors include demonstrating empathy, responding to patients’ needs, respecting individual values, and engaging in supportive communication. Such behaviors represent the practical enactment of caring among nursing students and serve as indicators of their professional development [10, 14]. A Taiwanese validation study of the Creative Synthesis Inventory identified visualization, discovery, prototyping, and evaluation as key elements of design-thinking competence among nursing students [15].

Conceptually, caring behaviors are hypothesized to influence nursing competency through several interconnected pathways. Relational engagement, characterized by empathy, respect, and authentic presence, facilitates effective communication and trust, which are essential for accurate assessment and clinical decision-making. Reflective practice and self-awareness, fostered through caring-oriented education, support students’ ability to integrate experiential learning, evaluate their actions, and adapt to complex clinical situations. Additionally, the internalization of ethical values and moral responsibility inherent in caring behaviors strengthens professional judgment and accountability, thereby contributing to the development of holistic nursing competency.

Competency-based nursing education frameworks further support this perspective by emphasizing that professional competence is reflected in demonstrable performance, including communication, ethical practice, and patient-centered care [2]. From this viewpoint, caring behaviors are not separate from competency but represent essential behavioral components through which competency is expressed and developed.

In the present study, caring behaviors are operationalized using the Caring Behaviors Scale (CBS), which includes three domains: helping the patient through the illness trajectory, patient advocacy, and knowing the patient. These domains capture specific actions through which nursing students express caring in practice. Conceptually, these behaviors are expected to contribute to nursing competency by facilitating communication, strengthening therapeutic relationships, and supporting reflective and ethical decision-making. In this context, caring behaviors are understood as observable actions that can be appropriately demonstrated by nursing students within supervised learning environments, rather than independent clinical practices requiring professional licensure.

Taken together, these frameworks support the assumption that caring behaviors and nursing competency are interdependent domains of professional development. Watson’s theory positions caring as essential to ethical and relational practice, while competency models emphasize the integration of technical skill, communication, and moral capability. Developmental research suggests that both caring and competency strengthen across academic progression [16, 17]. Patient-perception studies further underscore that caring behaviors - such as kindness, empathy, and respect - form a critical dimension of how competency is experienced in practice [18]. These theoretical and empirical foundations justify examining the relationship between caring behaviors and nursing competency among nursing students.

In Taiwan, cultural expectations of harmony, respect, and relational orientation may shape caring behaviors differently than in Western contexts. Harrison’s work indicates that caring behaviors among Taiwanese nursing students are influenced by gender-role orientation rather than demographic characteristics [19]. Thus, a theoretical model integrating Watson’s caring philosophy and competency-based educational frameworks provides a culturally grounded basis for examining caring behaviors and nursing competency in Taiwanese nursing education.

Caring

In this study, caring is conceptualized as a set of observable behaviors demonstrated by nursing students, rather than solely as an abstract ethical or philosophical ideal. While traditional perspectives emphasize caring as a relational and moral process [13], contemporary nursing education increasingly focuses on how caring is enacted through measurable actions in clinical and learning environments.

Caring behaviors include both instrumental and expressive actions, such as providing timely assistance, advocating for patients’ needs, demonstrating empathy, and recognizing patients’ individual values and preferences [3, 20]. These behaviors are particularly relevant for nursing students, who are in the process of translating professional values into practice.

Empirical studies have demonstrated that caring behaviors are associated with improved patient satisfaction, well-being, and care outcomes [21, 22]. Focusing on caring at the behavioral level therefore allows for a more precise and education-relevant understanding of how caring is developed, assessed, and integrated into professional competency.

In the present study, caring behaviors are assessed using the Caring Behaviors Scale (CBS), ensuring alignment between conceptualization, measurement, and research objectives.

Nursing competencies

Nursing competency encompasses cognitive, technical, relational, and ethical capabilities required for safe and effective practice. AACN competencies highlight that competency is dynamic and responsive to evolving healthcare needs [2]. The American Nurses Association similarly defines competency as an expected performance level integrating knowledge, skills, communication, reasoning, and ethical judgment [2]. Core domains include patient care, medical knowledge, communication, professionalism, systems-based practice, and continuous learning [23]. At the point of graduation, European studies have shown that nursing students generally self-assess their competence at a “good” level, while revealing notable cross-national variation and identifying factors such as prior experience and educational climate as important influences, thereby underscoring the need for systematic assessment and support of competence as students enter practice [24]. Contemporary education also emphasizes innovation and design-thinking competencies; the Creative Synthesis Inventory validated in Taiwan reflects the need for creative, human-centered problem-solving abilities [15]. Educational research additionally indicates that targeted instructional interventions can enhance specific domains of nursing competency. For instance, a quasi-experimental study of nursing students demonstrated that a focused workshop on traumatic dental injuries substantially improved students’ knowledge, confidence, and attitudes toward dental trauma management, highlighting how structured training can strengthen practice-relevant competencies that may not be sufficiently covered in traditional curricula [25].

Caring and nursing competencies

Caring behaviors are closely linked to the development of nursing competency, particularly through their roles in communication, ethical sensitivity, and relational engagement [16, 17]. Students with limited ability to demonstrate caring behaviors may experience difficulties in delivering compassionate and patient-centered care [16]. Patients frequently associate nursing competency with observable caring behaviors, such as attentiveness, empathy, and therapeutic communication [18].

Li et al. identified “knowing the patient” as the most prominent caring behavior among nursing students, while advocacy behaviors were less emphasized [23]. Similar patterns have been observed in Taiwanese nursing students, suggesting that caring behaviors are shaped by both educational and cultural contexts [23]. In addition, individual characteristics—such as gender-role traits and personality preferences—may influence caring behaviors and, consequently, competency development [19, 26].

Experiential pedagogies, such as transcultural simulation and structured evidence-based nursing practice training programs, have been shown to be associated with improvements in nursing competency by supporting clinical decision-making, technical skills, and care quality [27, 28]. Despite extensive global research, empirical studies examining the relationship between caring behaviors and nursing competency among Taiwanese nursing students remain limited [29]. Therefore, examining caring at the behavioral level is essential for understanding how nursing competency is developed within specific educational and cultural contexts. Cultural factors, educational structures, and healthcare system demands may uniquely shape competency development in Taiwan. In addition to educational outcomes, nursing competency has been associated with organizational commitment and workforce stability, highlighting its relevance for human resource management and professional retention within healthcare systems [30]. Thus, examining this relationship is essential for informing evidence-based curriculum design and strengthening person-centered nursing education.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed an analytical cross-sectional design to investigate the relationship between caring behaviors and nursing competency among nursing students in northern Taiwan in 2021. This design was appropriate because the study aimed not only to describe caring behaviors and nursing competency but also to examine associations between these variables using inferential statistical analyses. All participants were enrolled in programs that utilized a problem-based learning (PBL) approach as the primary instructional method, providing a consistent educational context for assessing the study variables. Demographic information, including age, gender, marital status, program type, and prior clinical or work experience, was obtained through a structured one-page survey designed to ensure uniform and comprehensive data collection for all participants. In addition, caring behaviors refer to observable interpersonal and supportive actions demonstrated by nursing students within supervised clinical and educational settings. These behaviors do not involve independent clinical decision-making or unsupervised nursing procedures but are performed under the guidance of licensed nurses and clinical instructors in accordance with institutional and ethical standards in this study.

Participants and sampling procedures

Random cluster sampling was used to recruit nursing students from two primary educational pathways in Taiwan: university/college programs and technical/vocational programs. Random cluster sampling was employed because students were naturally organized into intact classes, making individual random sampling impractical and potentially disruptive. Class-level clusters allowed efficient recruitment while maintaining ecological validity. Proportional selection across program types was applied to enhance representativeness and reduce sampling bias. In the university/college pathway, students complete senior high school and subsequently enter a four-year Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) program, which serves as the foundation for both entry-level and advanced nursing education. In the technical/vocational pathway, students first complete a five-year junior college curriculum leading to an Associate Degree in Nursing (ADN), after which they may pursue a two-year BSN program offered in day, evening or on-the-job formats.

Clusters were defined at the class level within each educational pathway. The researchers compiled a list of eligible classes, assigned random numbers using a computer-generated randomization procedure, and selected clusters proportionally from each program type to ensure representative sampling. A total of 600 participants were initially recruited. After excluding 35 incomplete questionnaires, data from 565 participants were included in the final analysis. The recruitment sample included 159 students from the four-year BSN program, 142 from the two-year BSN day program, 126 from the two-year BSN evening program, and 138 from the two-year BSN on-the-job program. Although Taiwan’s nursing education system encompasses multiple institutions and program structures, the present study was conducted within a single institute; thus, findings should be interpreted within this context.

Exclusion criteria consisted of refusal to participate and questionnaires with substantial missing data. Eligible participants were required to (1) be enrolled in one of the institute’s nursing programs; (2) be able to read and complete Chinese-language questionnaires; and (3) voluntarily agree to participate in the study.

Instruments

The Chinese version of the Caring Behaviors Scale (CBS) was used to assess participants’ caring behaviors [31]. Originally developed by Ou and Lin [32], the CBS comprises 28 items across three domains: (1) helping the patient through the illness trajectory, (2) advocating for the patient, and (3) knowing the patient. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = never, 1 = sometimes, 2 = often, 3 = always), producing a total score range of 0–84, with higher scores indicating more frequent caring behaviors. Prior studies have demonstrated strong psychometric properties when applied to nursing students in both baccalaureate and associate degree programs. Lin reported a content validity index (CVI) of 0.82, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.83–0.87, and test–retest reliability coefficients of 0.80–0.82 across the three dimensions [31]. Pai et al. also documented excellent reliability, reporting a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.94 and test–retest reliability of 0.86 [33].

The Chinese version of the Nursing Competency Scale (NCS) was used to evaluate students’ nursing competency. The NCS includes 24 items across three categories: (1) professional practice skills, (2) professional humanity skills, and (3) self-development skills. Items are scored on a 6-point Likert scale (0 = none, 1 = 1–20%, 2 = 21–40%, 3 = 41–60%, 4 = 61–80%, 5 = 81–100%), with higher scores indicating higher levels of competency (total score range = 0–120). The instrument has demonstrated excellent reliability, with an overall Cronbach’s alpha of 0.97 and subscale alphas ranging from 0.90 to 0.95. Tseng reported a CVI of 0.99, item-level CVIs of 0.90–1.00, a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value of 0.96, and an explanatory variance of 67.85%, confirming strong construct validity [34]. The items are designed to be easily understood and scored, supporting the instrument’s suitability for student populations.

Data collection

All participants were selected by random cluster sampling from classes at each grade level of approximately 50 students in the three programs of one institute in the north of Taiwan. Data were collected in the classrooms of the nursing programs by the study researchers at a time convenient for the students. The researchers explained the design and purpose of the study prior to distributing the questionnaires. Each packet was coded and contained a demographic survey and the two self-report instruments. The researchers administered the survey and questionnaires by distributing the packets to students at each nursing program. The students placed completed sealed packets into a box in the research assistant’s office. This procedure ensured participant confidentiality and maintained consistency in data handling throughout the study.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the university. After approval, one of the researchers provided a verbal explanation of the study’s purpose, design, and procedures to all potential participants. Students were informed that participation was entirely voluntary, that their responses would remain confidential and anonymous, and that declining or withdrawing from the study would not affect their academic performance or course evaluation. All nursing students who agreed to participate were subsequently enrolled in the study.

Data analysis

Completed questionnaires were collected in locked boxes and subsequently retrieved by the research team for scoring. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 28.0. Descriptive statistics—including frequencies, percentages, ranges, means, and standard deviations—were used to summarize demographic characteristics and instrument scores. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize CBS and nursing competency scores across different nursing programs to provide contextual understanding of the sample. Inferential analyses focused on examining the associations between caring behaviors and nursing competency, rather than evaluating differences in program effectiveness. Age was treated as a categorical variable for descriptive analyses and as a continuous variable in inferential statistical analyses where appropriate. Given the relatively narrow age distribution among younger participants and the small number of cases in older age ranges, age was presented descriptively in a format that reflects the underlying distribution of the sample to enhance interpretability and avoid sparse data categories.

Inferential analyses were conducted to examine associations between caring behaviors and nursing competency. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and independent-samples t tests were used for exploratory subgroup analyses of selected demographic variables. However, program-specific values presented in Table 2 were reported descriptively and were not used to evaluate program effectiveness. Potential confounding variables, including age, gender, program type, and prior clinical or work experience, were examined through subgroup comparisons and preliminary correlation analyses to assess their associations with caring behaviors and nursing competency. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were then used to assess the relationships between key study variables. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Results

Consequently, data from 565 participants were included in the final analysis, yielding a valid response rate of 94.2%. The majority of participants were female (93.6%, n = 529), while males accounted for 6.4% (n = 36). Most participants were in their early twenties, and the majority were single (91.2%). Correlation analyses revealed statistically significant positive associations between caring behaviors and nursing competency (Table 3). These findings suggest that caring behaviors are meaningfully, but moderately, associated with nursing competency. Given the cross-sectional nature of the study, these associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causality.

Discussion

This study examined the association between caring behaviors and nursing competency among nursing students engaged in a learner-centered, problem-based learning (PBL) approach in Taiwan. The findings revealed a moderate positive correlation between caring behaviors and nursing competency, providing empirical evidence of an association between these constructs within contemporary nursing education [4, 19].

Importantly, this study extends prior Taiwanese research by concurrently examining caring behaviors and multiple domains of nursing competency within a unified analytical framework. This approach provides a more comprehensive understanding of how caring behaviors are associated with professional practice, professional humanity, and self-development competencies among nursing students.

Consistent with prior Taiwanese studies, knowing the patient emerged as the most frequently practiced caring behavior. Participants endorsed behaviors reflecting attentiveness to patients’ values, serious consideration of patient concerns, and respect for family involvement in decision-making [23]. While similar patterns have been reported previously, the present findings add nuance by demonstrating that knowing the patient also indicated moderate positive associations with key competency domains, particularly professional practice and professional humanity skills (Table 3). This suggests that relational understanding may function as a central linkage between caring behaviors and competency development within Taiwanese nursing education, rather than serving solely as an ethical or affective dimension of care. These findings are consistent with earlier Taiwanese research identifying “knowing the patient” as a core and stable component of caring among nursing students [35].

In contrast, patient advocacy behaviors were less frequently endorsed, a finding that both aligns with and extends earlier Taiwanese studies [23, 35]. While previous research has attributed this pattern to sociocultural values emphasizing harmony, hierarchy, and collective decision-making, the current data further indicate that advocacy behaviors were also less strongly associated with nursing competency domains compared with relational caring behaviors (Table 3). This divergence suggests that advocacy may be differentially cultivated or expressed within educational and clinical contexts, highlighting a potential area for targeted curricular emphasis.

These findings may be understood within the Taiwanese cultural context, where interpersonal harmony, respect for hierarchy, and relational orientation are highly valued. The prominence of “knowing the patient” reflects culturally embedded expectations of attentiveness and relational sensitivity. Conversely, the relatively lower emphasis on advocacy may be influenced by cultural norms that prioritize harmony over confrontation, suggesting that advocacy behaviors may require more explicit educational support.

The observed associations between caring behaviors and nursing competency domains underscore the relevance of educational environments that integrate relational engagement, ethical reflection, and experiential learning. Prior studies have shown that student-centered and inquiry-based pedagogies enhance critical thinking, clinical reasoning, and the integration of theory and practice [35, 36]. Rather than merely replicating these findings, the present study demonstrates that such pedagogical approaches may be relevant when they intentionally support caring-oriented processes—such as reflective practice, empathy development, and relational communication—that are closely linked to competency development.

The findings also contribute to ongoing discussions regarding individual and contextual influences on caring and competency. Previous studies have shown that caring behaviors and competency are shaped by personal characteristics, including gender-role orientation, stress perception, and psychological resources [19, 23, 35]. In contrast, the present study suggests that educational structure and learning approach may play a more prominent role in shaping the caring-competency relationship. By situating students within a consistent PBL-based educational context, this study suggests that caring behaviors and nursing competency may be jointly supported through intentional curriculum design rather than relying primarily on individual predispositions.

Overall, this study highlights the interdependence of caring behaviors and nursing competency and reinforces the importance of educational strategies that integrate relational, ethical, and practical dimensions of nursing practice. These findings align with patient-perspective research indicating that caring behaviors—such as kindness, attentiveness, and respectful communication—constitute a key dimension of perceived nursing competence [23]. The results have direct implications for nursing education in Taiwan, where culturally responsive, learner-centered approaches may enhance both professional readiness and compassionate care delivery.

Future research should build on these findings using longitudinal and intervention-based designs. Prospective cohort studies could examine how caring behaviors and nursing competency co-develop across different stages of nursing education and the transition to professional practice. In addition, experimental or quasi-experimental studies evaluating targeted curricular interventions—such as structured caring-focused PBL modules, reflective practice training, or advocacy-enhancement programs—would help clarify causal pathways and inform evidence-based curriculum development [36].

This study contributes to the international nursing education literature by providing context-specific evidence on the association between caring behaviors and nursing competency within a learner-centered, PBL-based environment. The findings underscore the importance of integrating relational and competency-based dimensions within a unified framework.

From a global perspective, caring behaviors are not only affective attributes but also observable and developable components of professional competence. In the Taiwanese context, cultural values—such as relational orientation, interpersonal harmony, and respect for hierarchy—may shape the expression and prioritization of caring behaviors, particularly the prominence of “knowing the patient.”

Overall, these findings suggest that nursing education should adopt context-sensitive strategies to integrate caring behaviors into competency development and better prepare students for person-centered clinical practice.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, participants were recruited from a single institute in northern Taiwan, which may limit the generalizability of the results to nursing students in other institutions or educational settings. In addition, the small proportion of male nursing students reflects the gender distribution within the sample but restricts the extent to which gender-related differences in caring behaviors and nursing competency can be meaningfully examined. Second, the analytical cross-sectional design precludes conclusions about temporal sequence or causality. Although significant associations were identified, the study cannot determine whether caring behaviors influence nursing competency or whether more competent students are more likely to report caring behaviors.

Third, the reliance on self-reported questionnaires may introduce response bias, including social desirability bias, and may not fully reflect students’ actual clinical performance. Fourth, although demographic variables were collected and examined, potential confounding factors such as learning motivation, clinical placement experience, instructor influence, and institutional culture were not fully controlled. Finally, program-based scores were presented descriptively to provide contextual information only; the study was not designed to evaluate program effectiveness or compare educational pathways. Future longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to clarify developmental patterns and causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

This study found that caring behaviors were moderately associated with nursing competency among nursing students in Taiwan. These findings suggest that caring may be understood as a measurable and developable component of professional competence, rather than solely as an affective disposition. However, given the analytical cross-sectional design, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships, and further longitudinal and intervention-based studies are needed to clarify these relationships.

In particular, the prominence of “knowing the patient” highlights the relational and culturally embedded nature of nursing practice in Taiwan, supporting the importance of integrating caring dimensions into competency-based nursing education [23, 35]. The findings also suggest the potential value of learner-centered and inquiry-driven pedagogies in supporting the integration of clinical reasoning, professional behaviors, and ethical practice. Evidence from health professions education indicates that well-designed and active educational approaches may be associated with improvements in professional competency, supporting the rationale for intentional pedagogical planning within nursing curricula [37, 38].

Overall, this study underscores the importance of developing nursing curricula that deliberately integrate caring behaviors as core learning outcomes within competency-based, learner-centered educational frameworks, thereby supporting the preparation of competent, compassionate, and practice-ready nursing graduates.
