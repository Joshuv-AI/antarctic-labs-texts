GWAS on yield-related traits of Pakistani wheat under post-anthesis heat stress
Authors: Shabbir Hussain, Fanmei Kong, Ishrat Naureen, Madiha Habib, Nida Ghori, Faisal Saeed Awan, Hasham Feroz Ghuman, Kiran Hameed, Xiaocun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heat stress is a major threat to spring wheat (Triticum aestivum) production in the Indo-Gangetic plains of South Asia, particularly in India and Pakistan. Breeding for heat-tolerant cultivars is an effective strategy to improve wheat productivity in stressed environments. To identify quantitative trait loci (QTL) for heat tolerance, we conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) to identify marker-trait associations (MTAs) using single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers generated via genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS). We evaluated a panel of 184 Pakistani spring wheat accessions for 13 yield-related agronomic traits under induced terminal heat stress. GWAS using the Bayesian information and linkage disequilibrium iteratively nested keyway (BLINK) model identified 295 significant MTAs on all 21 wheat chromosomes. Notably, two QTL intervals (QTL), on chromosomes 3D (5 SNPs) and 5B (3 SNPs), exhibited pleiotropic effects on grain weight (GW) and grain per spike (GPS). In addition, three QTL intervals comprising 9 SNPs on chromosome 7 A, 7 SNPs on 3B, and 4 SNPs on 5 A were significantly associated with the heat susceptibility index (HSI). Furthermore, clusters on chromosome 2B (5 SNPs) and 6B (4 SNPs) were linked to kernel weight reduction (KWR). The identified QTLs and candidate genes associated with HSI and KWR provide valuable genetic resources for fine-mapping and the development of breeder-friendly markers for selecting heat-tolerant wheat cultivars.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12699-7.

Introduction

Among all the world’s crops, wheat is cultivated on the most acreage (≥ 240 million hectares) [1]. Wheat single-handedly produced 784.91 million metric tons (MMT) of grain [2] and serves as a major source of nutrition for > 40% global population [3, 4]. As the global human population is increasing 1.5% per annum, the population is likely to reach ~ 10 billion by the year 2050 [5]. To achieve global food security, yields must improve by 1.6% to 2% annually [6]. Pakistan is the 5th most populous country and the 7th largest wheat producer in the world. Bread wheat alone provides 75% of the national caloric needs. Spring wheat was planted on almost 40% of the country’s agricultural land during the most recent Rabi season [7]. However, the wheat demand in developing nations like Pakistan needs to be increased by 2–4% to maintain national food security. The global climate models predicted an increase of 1–3.7 °C in temperature from mid to the end of the 21st century, which leads to the manifestation of infrequent rainfalls accompanied by enlarged, drastic heat waves [8]. The Unavailability of heat-tolerant wheat cultivars and unpredictable weather patterns limit wheat production in some regions [9]. Numerous studies have found a 10–50% decrease in overall wheat production as a result of temperature change [10–14]. Heat stress generally impacts the wheat crop from planting to harvest; however, terminal heat stress has more negative effects on photosynthesis, chlorophyll content, grain filling duration, and assimilate partitioning. At the final stages of reproductive growth, heat stress not only lowers crop yield but also degrades grain quality [15].

As a result of global warming and climate change, South Asian nations like Bangladesh, India, and Pakistan are increasingly facing a serious threat to their spring wheat crops from terminal stage heat stress. In 2022, major wheat-growing regions in India reported yield losses of 15–20% in March and April due to average monthly temperatures exceeding the 100-year average temperature limits [16], while Pakistan experienced a 10% reduction in wheat yield due to record high temperatures exceeding 40 °C. Most areas experience early heat stress, which causes crops to mature one to two weeks earlier and have a much shorter grain filling time, ultimately resulting in output losses [16]. High-throughput NGS-based molecular marker technology has revolutionized the field of molecular breeding both in plants and animals. Various types of DNA-derived markers have been used for the genetic characterization of plants and other organisms. Genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS)-based markers, such as diversity arrays technology (DArT), single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [17–19], generated arrays have been commonly used marker platforms for gene discovery and genome-wide genotyping. These marker platforms make a wider application of SNPs in uncovering the genetic diversity (GD), linkage disequilibrium, population structure (PS), biparental QTL mapping, genome-wide association studies (GWAS), genome prediction (GP), and genomic selection (GS) in plant and animal [20, 21]. Using a panel of 208 durum wheat types and 6,211 DArT seq SNPs [22] identified multiple MTAs for drought stress (DT), heat tolerance (HT), and yield potential (YP) on chromosome regions 2 A, 2B, 7 A, and 3B. A panel of 125 spring wheat accessions from the International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT) were genotyped using GBS and assessed at three distinct sites in Pakistan and a total of 139 significant MTAs were found by GWAS for DH, GFD, PH, SPP, chromosomes 6Y, with respective values of 20, 21, 23, 13, 8, 21, and 33 [23]. 500 significant MTAs for heat stress were found through GWAS analysis conducted on a set of 236 elite soft winter wheat accessions using 27,466 SNP markers. Of these, 10 MTAs on chromosomes 6 A, 2B, 3B, 7B, 1D, and 7D showed a pleiotropic effect [24]. Using a 90 K genotyping array, a GWAS on 688 winter wheat genotypes was conducted, and 335 MTAs were found to be linked to TGW and stress susceptibility index. GBS reduces genome complexity through restriction digestion and sequencing, thereby reducing the complexity of larger genomes, such as those of wheat [25, 26]. A comprehensive GWAS study of drought and heat stress-related traits was conducted on 246 spring wheat genotypes acquired from the International Center for Agricultural Research in the Dry Areas (ICARDA), by using 10 K SNP markers across six locations for two years, 2015–2016 to 2016–2017. Phenotypic screening was performed, and phenotypic data of grain yield-related traits, such as days to heading, days to maturity, plant height, grain yield, and thousand-grain weight, were collected. GWAS was performed using the MLM model, and a total of 65 MTAs across 6 environments and 16 chromosomes were linked with different yield-related traits [27]. A GWAS and genetic diversity study was conducted on 250 ICARDA wheat genotypes to dissect the composition of heat stress-related yield traits. The diversity panel was screened at two different locations, Gezira Research Farm (GRF) and Agricultural Research Corporation (ARC), Wad Madani, Sudan, over two consecutive years, 2016/17 and 2017/18. GWAS was performed using 15 K SNP markers and about 282, 355, and 475 significant MTAs associated with yield traits identified during the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd seasons, respectively. Various highly significant MTAs were identified for biomass, harvesting index (HI), thousand grain weight, number of seeds per spike, days to heading, days to maturity, and plant height [28]. Another data set of 191 ICARDA spring wheat genotypes developed for drought and heat stress tolerance was screened across 7 different environments in Egypt, Sudan, Lebanon, and Morocco against five yield-related traits. A GWAS was conducted using 12,954 SNP markers and identified 37 significant MTAs, 19, 14, 5, 3, 3 for PH, DM, thousand kernel weight (TKW), grain yield (GY), and DM, respectively [29]. To identify climate-resilient alleles and develop heat-tolerant wheat varieties, a comprehensive screening of the available germplasm is urgently needed. The objective of this study is to phenotypically characterize a panel of 184 Pakistani wheat accessions under induced terminal heat stress in consecutive cropping years 2017-18 and 2018-19 using GBS-derived SNP markers. The aim is to identify marker-trait associations of post-anthesis yield-related traits in wheat under heat-stress environments, which could be beneficial for marker-assisted selection, genome selection, and the development of heat-stress-tolerant germplasm.

Materials and methods

Germplasm collection and phenotyping

Seeds of 184 Pakistani wheat accessions (Supplementary Table S1) were obtained from the Wheat Research Institute (WRI), Ayyub Agricultural Research Institute (AARI), Faisalabad, Punjab, Pakistan. The field-based experimental trials with three replicates were grown for two consecutive years (2017-18 and 2018-19) at the experimental area of the University of Agriculture Faisalabad (UAF) (31° 26’ N, 73° 6’ E) in a Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD). Three rows of 1.5 m length were grown in each replication, with plant-to-plant and row-to-row distances of 15.24 cm and 22.86 cm, respectively. However, a 30.48 cm distance was maintained between genotypes. Similarly, a layout was also planned for heat stress treatment and check (control conditions). Heat stress (32 °C) was provided 10 days after anthesis in a tunnel for a consecutive 10 days. The data for HI, biological yield (BY), yield per plant (YPP), thousand-grain weight (TGW), DM, DH, grain filling duration (GFD), single spike grain weight (SSGW), spike weight (SW), grains per spike (GPS), stay green (SG), KWR, and HSI were collected following our previous report [30]. Statistical data analysis

The phenotypic data were statistically analyzed by using PCA (principal component analysis), agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC), and Pearson correlation (R-software v.3.6.1 (https://www.r-project.org/) whereas further details of the statistical analysis are given in [31]. ANOVA and broad-sense heritability were calculated by using QTL IciMapping [32].

Genotyping-by-sequencing of a diversity panel

Hence, for genotyping, seeds were sown into 96-well plastic trays in the greenhouse at Kansas State University (KSU), Manhattan, KS, United States. Three pieces of 15-day-old seedling leaf tissues (2 cm) were collected in 1.1 ml 96-deep-well plates with 3 mm stainless steel beads in each well and immediately freeze-dried for 2 days. Leaf tissues were ground by shaking the plates at 30 cycles per second for 3 min in a Mixer Mill. Genomic DNA was isolated using the standard cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method with a slight modification. DNA quality was checked with 1% agarose gel and quantified in a FLUOstar Omega microplate reader using Quant-I, PicoGreen dsDNA assay kits. The genomic DNA of all samples was normalized at a concentration of 20 ng/µL for preparing the GBS library. The details of GBS library preparation, DNA sequencing, data cleaning, filtration, SNP calling, and downstream data analysis are also given in [4].

Genotypic data analysis

A phylogenetic tree was constructed for the panel using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) distance method in trait analysis by association evolution and linkage (TASSEL) v5.2.67 [33]. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was implemented using the Euclidean distance method in Genetic Analysis in Excel (GenAlEx) v6.5 (https://biology-assets.anu.edu.au/GenAlEx/Download.html) [34]. Population GD, polymorphic information content (PIC), major allele frequency (MAF), and percentage heterozygosity (HZ) were calculated using POWER MARKER v3 [35]. Analysis of molecular variance (AMOVA) calculated using GenAlEx v6.5 (https://biology-assets.anu.edu.au/GenAlEx/Download.html). PS was inferred using TASSEL v2.3.4, and LD was calculated with GAPIT in the R environment [4, 36].

Genome-wide association study

The panel of 184 Pakistani spring wheat accessions was genotyped, and GWAS analysis was conducted by using the BLINK module 13 in GAPIT (Version 3) [37]. MLM was selected and optimized using different data as kinship + principal component (K + P) and kinship + PS (K + Q) [38, 39], to choose the best-fit model for MTAs [40]. Finally, the MTAs were performed using Bayesian information criteria (BIC) with genotypic, phenotypic, principal component (first 3PCs), and kinship (K) data. BLINK is an advanced version that incorporates more statistical power and efficiency to reduce the ratio of false positives to detect significant SNPs associated with desired multi-locus traits. A p-value threshold of 1e-4 was declared as a substantial association cutoff value.

In silico analysis of QTLs/MTAs to identify the candidate genes

In Silico analysis of significant MTAs detected from GWAS was performed using the International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium (IWGSC) Chinese Spring RefSeq 1.1 wheat reference genome in the Ensemble Plants database [41]. Candidate genes associated with selected SNPs within 5 Mb of upstream and downstream regions of the subject SNPs were selected using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) function. The putative functions of the high-confidence protein-coding genes were proposed using the Universal Protein Resource Knowledge Base (UniProtKB) [42] and further confirmed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI).

Results

Statistical summary of phenotypic data

A descriptive statistical summary of variables, such as range, mean, standard deviation (n), variance, and coefficient of variation (%CV), was calculated for both years (2017-18 to 2018-19) and their respective controls. Yield per plant, biological yield, harvesting index, grains per spike, and thousand-grain weight showed higher CVs than their respective controls. However, days to maturity, yield per plant, single-spike grain weight, and spike weight showed declines in mean values compared with the control. The ANOVA results indicate that post-anthesis yield-related traits showed significant differences in mean values between heat treatment and control in both growing seasons. Most of the yield traits showed that heat stress had a major influence on the phenotypic expression of all yield-related traits. Most of the traits were significant at a 1e-4 value. The trait heritability of all traits is given in Table 1. The first two axes of the biplot (F1 and F2) and the principal component axes (PC1 and PC2) explained 17% and 12% of the variability in 2017-18, 16% and 15% in 2018-19, and 14% and 12% under control conditions, respectively. Further phenotypic details are provided in [31].

Yield-related traits and their correlation analysis

A total of 11 yield-related traits were evaluated to assess the responses of 184 wheat accessions under terminal heat stress after anthesis, and variation was observed for all traits among the accessions within each treatment and control. Pearson correlation coefficients of 11 yield-related traits were evaluated under both heat-stressed (treatment) and normal environments in 2017-18 and 2018-19 growing seasons (Fig. 1). DH showed a positive correlation with DM in both years, and control with longer DM ultimately resulted in a longer crop life cycle. DM showed strong positive correlations with GFD for two treatments (r = 0.80 and 0.60, respectively) and the control (r = 0.60), suggesting that early maturity led to minimum GFD and delayed maturity enhanced the GFD and ultimately affected the overall yield. YP was positively correlated with BY, HI, SSGW, SW, and TGW in both treatments and the control. These results indicate that all yield-associated traits possess a cumulative effect on overall yield. The BY showed a strong negative correlation with HI in both treatments and the control. Strong positive correlations were observed among SSGW, SW, GPS, and TGW under heat-stressed and controlled conditions. SG in plants also showed a positive correlation with DM under both treatments and control, suggesting that delayed green color in plants ensures continuous photosynthesis and transfer of nutrients from source to sink under stress conditions. However, the extended SG phenotype delays maturity and also makes the crop vulnerable to early or late-season heat stress. Further details of phenomics characterization were given in [31].

Genotypic data analysis

Genotyping by sequencing (GBS) of 184 wheat accessions generated 202,147,814 sequence reads and 129,180 SNPs with 80% missing data points. A total of 123,596 polymorphic SNPs were retained after removing low-quality SNPs with maf < 0.01, heterozygote rate > 0.2, and imputation using the IWGSC Chinese spring wheat reference genome RefSeq V1.1, for further data analysis. PS distributed the population into three sub-groups (group 1, group 2, and group 3) based on the optimum K = 3. The Delta K value gives the optimal number of subpopulations by plotting a graph with the number of clusters (K). Phylogenetic analysis also divided the population into three different clusters. Group 1 contains 31 accessions, which were dominated by local lines, including 23 Pakistani accessions, and only eight CIMMYT accessions. Group 2 consisted of 33 accessions, including 18 Pakistani and 15 from CIMMYT. Group 3 was the largest group that contained 120 accessions, including 75 local accessions from Pakistan, 42 from CIMMYT, two wild wheat relatives, and one accession of unknown origin. The whole-genome mean MF was 0.87, with 0.86, 0.87, and 0.89 for A, B, and D subgenomes, respectively. Mean GD was observed as 0.20, 0.19, and 0.16 for sub-genomes A, B, and D, respectively. The HZ was 0.07, 0.06, and 0.08 for A, B, and D genomes, respectively, and the PIC value was observed as 0.16, 0.16, and 0.14 for A, B, and D subgenomes, respectively, with a 0.15 cumulative PIC for the whole wheat genome. The results of the current experiment showed that subgenomes A and B do not show any significant differences among MF, GD, HZ, and PIC values. However, the D subgenome showed a higher and significantly different value among the MF, HZ, relatively lower GD, and PIC values compared to the A and B subgenomes. The scatter plot of (r2) and pairwise physical distance (D) revealed that LD decay increases with an increase in marker physical distances on chromosomes or the genome. The average cut-off value r2 = 0.1 was used to determine the LD decay level. The LD decay value (0.34 Mbp) was observed for the whole genome. However, among sub-genomes, the LD decay was the highest on the A sub-genome (0.29 Mbp), followed by the B (0.2 Mbp) and D (0.07 Mbp) sub-genomes, respectively. Complete details of the genotypic data analysis results were given in our previous publication [4].

GWAS for yield-related traits under terminal heat stress

A GWAS was conducted on 13 yield-associated traits (11 phenotypic traits and two derived traits specific to heat stress, the KWR and HSI, as given in [31] with 184 Pakistani spring wheat accessions by using 123,596 SNPs, identifying 295 highly significant MTAs (Fig. 2). The highest number of significant MTAs was identified for GFD (61), followed by TGW (47) and HSI (30); while the lowest number of MTAs was identified for SG/GLR (4), followed by SW (12) and BY (13). Significant MTAs were also observed in DH, DM, GPS, HI, HSI, SSGW, YP, and KWR with 14, 15, 22, 17, 23, 14, and 23, respectively (Fig. 2c). Among those MTAs, 34 larger clusters with at least 3 MTAs were observed for different traits. However, 58 MTAs of single SNPs, 27 of 2 SNPs, 12 MTAs of 3 SNPs, 10 MTAs of 4 SNPs, 6 MTAs of 5 SNPs, 3 MTAs of 6 SNPs, 3 MTAs of 7 SNPs, and 3 MTAs of 9 SNPs clusters were mapped on different genomic regions associated with different traits BY, DM, GFD, HI, HSI, SG, SSGW, KWR, TGW, and YPP, respectively, as summarized in Table 2.

Moreover, 5 SNPs on chromosome 3D, 3 SNPs on chromosome 5B, 1 SNP on 3B, and 1 SNP on chromosome 1D showed a pleiotropic effect on SSGW and GPS. One SNP of chromosome 2D showed a pleiotropic effect on DM and GFD (Table 3). Individual Manhattan plots associated with each trait summarize the results of GWAS. The X-axis of the plot represented the genomic positions of individual SNPs, and the Y-axis showed the –log of the P-value obtained from the GWAS statistical model. Larger skyscraper peaks in the Manhattan plot indicated that the surrounding regions were in strong association with the subject traits. A solid black line in the Manhattan plot represented the standard 1e-4 as a threshold cutoff after application of adjusted false discovery rate (FDR) for GWAS association (Fig. 3). In the current study, a less stringent threshold, the adjusted p < 0.0001 (-log10 (P) > 4), is used to declare the significant SNP-trait association. Furthermore, a quantile-quantile (QQ) plot was used to determine how well the selected model fit with the GWAS and how skillfully it elaborated the genetic population structure and familial relatedness of the subject population. Most of the SNPs in a QQ plot lie on diagonal lines, indicating no association between SNPs and traits of interest. A deviation from the diagonal line suggested a cryptic association due to population structure and familial kinship. SNPs that deviated on the upper-right section of the plot were most likely associated with the desired trait (Fig. 3). In silico analysis of significant and candidate genes associated with heat stress in wheat

A GWAS analysis was performed for 13 yield-related traits to detect the significant heat stress-associated MTAs in a population of 184 Pakistani wheat accessions. About 295 significant marker-trait associations were detected at a threshold cutoff p-value of 0.0001. A total of 286 high or low confidence region IWGSC putative protein-coding genes were identified in 5 Mb upstream/downstream of the specific SNP positions (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Table S3.1). A total of 104 genes are specifically associated with different types of abiotic and biotic stresses in plants. However, a total of 52 genes are linked to various types of abiotic and biotic stress, and about 52 genes are specifically associated with heat stress tolerance in combination with other abiotic stresses like drought and salt in wheat. Moreover, 59 genes located in the 5 Mb upstream and downstream regions of the selected SNP positions are still uncharacterized. About 112 genes identified in this experiment are involved in other structural and molecular functions in plants. On the other hand, 11 SNPs are located within the coding region of different protein-coding genes. Out of these 11 genes, nine are functionally characterized, and two SNPs are present in uncharacterized gene coding regions (Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Table S3.1). Several important major gene families associated with different types of abiotic stress-linked SNPs, particularly heat stress were identified i.e., F-Box, C2H2 associated zinc finger protein, AAA+ATPase (CLPB), Ring zinc finger domain having, gamma-interferon-inducible lysosomal thiol reductase (GILT), glutaredoxins (GRXs), thioredoxin (TRX), heat shock protein (HSP) HSP40, RNA helicase superfamily 2 (SF2), DEAD-box, RNA helicase, Flavin containing monooxygenase, Basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH), bowman-birk inhibitors (BBIs), G protein, Clip ATPases, Nucleoporin, Cyclophilins, DUF gene family are major one given in Table 4.

Significant SNP markers and putative candidate genes linked with heat stress tolerance-related yield traits in wheat

Discussion

Merely South Asia, such as Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh, is producing > 15% of the world’s spring wheat. On the other hand, Pakistan is one of the major countries that is severely affected by climate change. A total of 70 to 80% of wheat in South Asia rice-growing areas is late sown, which is generally prone to terminal heat stress. However, sometimes crops sown within the recommended sowing window are also affected by sudden heat waves due to unexpected climate change [16]. GWAS was performed by using 123,596 polymorphic SNP markers, and a total of 295 significantly linked marker-trait associations were identified. A total of 37 larger clusters of ≥ 3 SNPs associated with 13 traits were mapped on different genomic regions, including A sub-genome 2 A, 3 A, 4 A, 5 A, 6 A, 7 A, B sub-genome 1B, 2B, 3B, 5B, 7B, and D sub-genome 1D, 2D, 3D, 7D of respectively. In the recent past, several GWAS and QTL mapping studies were performed on various populations and traits, showing similar results. Several MTAs 4AL, 5AS, 7AL, 4BS, 2BL, and 4DS [43], 1 A, 2 A, 7 A chromosome of a genome 1B, 2B, 4B, 6B, 7B chromosomes of the B genome [44], 2 A, 5 A, 4 A, 6 A, 7 A and 1B, 2B, 3B, 4B, 3D [23], 3 A, 4B, 6 A, 6B [45], 5 A [46, 47], 5D [47, 48], 2B, 2D, 4 A, 4B, 6 A [49], 2 A, 3 A, 7D [50] 1B, 2 A, 2D, 3B, 4D, 5B, 6B, 7B [51] 4D, 6 A [52, 53]. identify QTLs on 2 A, 3 A, 4 A, 5 A, 6 A, and 7 A, the chromosome of the A genome, 1B, 2B, 3B, 4B, 5B, 6B of the B genome, and 4D of the D genome, respectively. GPS is one of the most important components of yield. In our study under induced terminal heat stress conditions, 22 MTAs related to grain per spike were mapped on 8 chromosomal regions of spring wheat, such as 1 A, 2B, 3B, 5B, 7B, 1D, 2D, 3D, 4D, respectively. Several MTA studies were performed under different growth conditions, and various chromosomal regions similar to our results were mapped in different bi-parental or natural populations, and reported associated QTLs on other chromosomes as 2AL, 3AL, 4AL, 1BS, 2B, 3B, 4BL, 6BL, 7BS, and 2D [43], 2D, 3 A, 3D, 6D, 7B [54], 2 A, 3 A, 6 A, 7B, 2D, 5D, 6D [23], 6 A [47], 5B, 7B, 1D [55].

GWAS results of yield-related traits against terminal heat stress tolerance in Pakistani wheat genotypes

In our study, 15 genes of F-box supergene family located on different chromosomal regions i.e., 2 A, (TraesCS2A02G121400), 5 A, (TraesCS5A02G534800, TraesCS5A02G378400), IB, (TraesCS1B02G475600), 2B, (TraesCS2B02G433100, TraesCS2B02G433100, TraesCS2B02G608700, TraesCS2B02G608700), 6B, (TraesCS6B02G060000, TraesCS6B02G060000), 3D, (TraesCS3D02G127000, TraesCS3D02G305900, TraesCS3D02G306000, TraesCS3D02G306000), 7D, (TraesCS7D02G070800), were found associated with DH, DM, GPS, GFD, HI, SSGW and TGW. E3 ligases are one of the major families of ubiquitously expressed E3 ligases, and the F-box is a main protein subunit involved in abiotic stress regulation [83]. Caseinolytic protease B (CLPB) is a homolog of the heat shock protein (HSP101) gene family linked with the AAA+ATPase superfamily, plays a critical role in plant growth and development, as well as heat acclimation [98]. In the current study, six genes of the AAA+ATPase super gene family were identified on different genomic regions of four chromosomes, i.e.,1B (TraesCS1B02G022200, TraesCS1B02G439200), 2B (TraesCS2B02G019800), 2D (TraesCS2D02G227000, TraesCS2D02G227000), 6D (TraesCS1B02G439200), associated with GPS, GFD, HSI, TGW, and KWR, respectively. In rice and Arabidopsis, the thermotolerance ability of the protein depends on the capability of the CLPB gene to complement the hsp104 mutant of the yeast [99]. Overexpression of rice cytoplasmic CLPB genes enhances the thermotolerance up to 45–50 °C under heat stress conditions [100]. A considerable increase in Insilco’s expression of organellar CLPB in the leaves at the seedling stage under heat stress conditions indicates the potential role of the CLPB gene in the case of heat stress, in collaboration with cytoplasmic CLPB genes [101, 102]. Ring-type is also known as U-box E3 ligases, a part of a large superfamily of E3 ubiquitin ligases. This gene family plays an important function in plant primary growth, development, and counter-response to abiotic stimuli, immunity, growth, hormonal signaling, and photosynthesis in case of temperature, salt, drought, harmful metals, and scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) [103]. RING (Really interesting new gene) or U-box domain is classically divided into two groups (RING-HC and RING-H2) based on its His and Cys fifth metal ligand binding position. About six genes in two chromosomal regions were identified as linked with HSI and GFD. These genes will be potentially associated with abiotic stress tolerance, like that 7 A (TraesCS7A02G308900), 7D (TraesCS7D02G554500, TraesCS7D02G554400, TraesCS7D02G554400, TraesCS7D02G554600, TraesCS7D02G554600). A wheat homolog, TaGW2-A1 of the rice OsGW2 gene located on the short arm of chromosome 6 A, closer to the centromeric region, was mapped as a QTL for TWG in wheat [60, 104, 105].

Gamma-interferon-inducible lysosomal thiol reductase (GILT) plays a crucial role in plant cellular immunity by catalyzing the reduction of disulfide bonds. In this study, three GILT genes (TraesCS4A02G112300, repeated entries possibly due to redundancy) were identified on chromosome 4 A and were found to be associated with days to heading (DH). In Arabidopsis thaliana, GILT family genes such as AtOSH1 and AtOSH2 play vital roles in repairing antioxidant enzymes and enhancing the regulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under various abiotic stress conditions. A set of three genes, named glutaredoxins (GRXs), is a ubiquitous oxidoreductase of the thioredoxin (TRX) gene family, located on the 5B chromosome. They are basically involved in regulating cell internal redox homeostasis as well as redox-dependent signaling pathways. Glutaredoxin genes AtGRXS17, OsGRXS17, and TaGRX in Arabidopsis, wheat, rice, and tomato also play an important role in maintaining ROS as well as ion homeostasis in case of abiotic stress, drought, chilling, and heat stress stimuli [106–108]. In the current GWAS, three genes of the TRX gene family, i.e., 5B, TraesCS5B02G374300, TraesCS5B02G374300, TraesCS5B02G374300, associated with GPS were identified, potentially involved in scavenging abiotic stress tolerance in various plants.

HSPs, also known as molecular chaperones, generally protect protein substrates against conformational damage by interacting with their surfaces or binding to them. They facilitate the folding of unfolded, misfolded, or incipient polypeptides [109]. In our study, one gene (TraesCS7B02G06410) from the HSP40/DnaJ gene family was identified on chromosome 7B and found to be associated with a selected SNP. HSP40 proteins typically act as molecular chaperones and play critical roles in the assembly, folding, translocation, and degradation of cellular proteins associated with HSI in wheat. Additionally, the HSP40 gene contributes significantly to protein refolding and stabilization in various plants, including wheat, under both biotic and abiotic stress conditions.

The basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH) transcription factor family is another important subfamily, and its conserved bHLH domain is widely distributed across various plant genomes. It is commonly involved in numerous stress-responsive biological processes in plants. In this study, a gene from the bHLH transcription factor family (TraesCS5B02G219300), identified as being associated with the SNP S5B_393540092, was found to be potentially linked to the DM trait and abiotic stress tolerance, particularly heat stress, in wheat. Thus, bHLH-type transcription factor genes appear to function as vital regulators of stress responses and plant development in wheat and other crops.

Additionally, a gene linked to the protein arginine methyltransferases (PRMTs) gene family, known to be involved in heat, drought, and salt stress responses in maize (ZmPRMT1-8) and Arabidopsis (AtPRMT4a), was also identified [110]. One such gene, TraesCS2D02G240400, associated with GPS and the SNP S2D_258769220, may represent a potential candidate for heat stress tolerance in wheat.

Another gene linked to a significant SNP on chromosome 4D is a member of the bowmen-birk inhibitors (BBIs), which plays a vital role against various abiotic stresses, like salt, drought, heat, and oxidative stress in wheat and other plants [61]. A gene of the BBI family associated with GFD, TraesCS4D02G063400, was identified, which is significantly associated with selected SNP, S4D_39310955 on chromosome 4D, potentially linked with heat stress tolerance in wheat. One gene named TraesCS1A02G160600 (TaTRX) from the thioredoxin gene family, associated with selected SNP S1A_288540619 on chromosome 1 A, was identified as being linked to GFD. This gene family is commonly involved in various biotic and abiotic stress responses, including powdery mildew, stripe rust, drought, heat, salt, and reactive oxygen species regulation in wheat and other plants. Trx proteins are known as thermostable and low molecular weight redox regulators. Through a GWAS study, a total of 42 Trx genes were identified across the three wheat sub-genomes, with 12 on A, 16 on B, and 14 on D sub-genomes [111]. Trxh5 and Trxh8 are two key genes induced in response to both biotic and abiotic stresses in rice. A unique cis-regulatory element has been shown to control the expression of three important antioxidant genes, such as SOD, Grx, and Trxh, in the rice plant (112).

In addition, the G protein/heterotrimeric G protein family, also known as guanine nucleotide-binding protein, typically consists of three subunits, such as α, β, and γ. A gene, TraesCS5A02G377700, associated with the selected SNP S5A_575543068 on chromosome 5 A was identified in wheat and is linked to HSI. This gene may serve as a potential target for improving heat stress tolerance in spring wheat. Clip ATPases are important members of the AAA+ superfamily, generally function as molecular chaperones, assisting in the assembly and disassembly of protein complexes and reducing the aggregation of damaged protein for degradation under biotic and abiotic stress conditions. Members of this gene family typically form large hexameric, ATP-dependent structures that contain two AAA+ like domains: a RecA-type nucleotide-binding domain and an α-helical domain [112]. In the current GWAS study of heat tolerance in wheat, a gene, TraesCS7A02G076400, belonging to the Clp AAA + ATP-dependent protease proteolytic subunit gene family, was identified on chromosome 7 A associated with SNP marker S7A_41753379. This gene could be a potential source to develop heat-tolerant wheat genotypes.

Nucleoporin (Nup) represents another super gene family, consisting of approximately 30 different proteins organized into various subcomplexes. They are involved in various cellular processes throughout the plant life cycle [113]. To date, only eight nucleoporins: Nup1, Nup75, Nup88, Nup96, Nup133, Nup160, Tpr/NUA, and REA1 have been successfully identified and characterized in tobacco, Arabidopsis, and Lotus Japonicus [114]. A gene, TraesCS4B02G238900 of the NPCs family, significantly associated with SNP marker S4B_495633736 on chromosome 4B, was identified in our current GWAS linked to heat stress tolerance and KWR in wheat. This gene may be a valuable resource for further research into the mechanisms of heat tolerance in wheat. Cyclophilins are a large family of ubiquitous proteins found across a wide range of organisms, from bacteria to higher plants and animals. Different members of this gene family possess a peptidyl prolyl cis-trans isomerization activity for cis-trans peptidyl bond formation and isomerization. In wheat, the TaCypA-1 gene functions as a chaperonin under heat stress conditions [99]. In current GWAS, a gene named TraesCS4A02G064400, associated with the YPP trait, was significantly linked to SNP marker S4A_60989335 and identified as a potential candidate gene for heat stress tolerance in wheat.

Research background conclusion and future outlook

Pakistan is a country of diverse climatic and geographical diversity and is widely divided into different agro-climatic zones, ranging from arid to semi-arid to moderate areas. A larger part of Pakistan, such as Baluchistan and Sindh, falls into arid to semi-arid areas, which encompass < 250 mm annual rainfall and a high evaporation rate, ultimately leading to severe drought. Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) provinces have a moderate climate. Punjab is considered the breadbasket of Pakistan, contributing more than 70% of the total wheat production. Punjab province has favorable soil and temperature conditions for wheat growth, but sudden unpredictable heat waves, as well as the duration and intensity of the monsoon, drastically affect the overall wheat production. Climate change not only alters the wheat growth cycle but also the intensity and frequency of heat waves, severe drought, floods, precipitation patterns, and adaptability-related issues. Under such unique climate conditions, wheat breeders need to develop varieties that possess heat- and drought-tolerance conferring genes to improve the crop’s yield and adaptability. Systematic heat stress during late sowing, along with unpredictable heat waves at the terminal growth stages due to climate change, has a major impact on final wheat production. Creating climate-smart wheat cultivars with improved genetics is the ultimate goal to tackle heat tolerance. In this genomic era, with advances in sequencing technology, GWAS is an important technique for identifying genetic variants associated with different qualitative and quantitative traits to improve crops. Some important major gene families associated with various types of abiotic stress linked SNPs especially heat stress were identified i.e. F-Box, C2H2 associated zinc finger protein, AAA+ATPase (CLPB), Ring zinc finger domain containing, gamma-interferon-inducible lysosomal thiol reductase (GILT), glutaredoxins (GRXs), thioredoxin (TRX), heat shock protein HSP40, RNA helicase superfamily 2 (SF2), DEAD-box, RNA helicase, Flavin containing monooxygenase, Basic helix-loop-helix (bHLH), bowmen-birk inhibitors (BBIs), G protein, Clip ATPases ATP dependent, Nucleoporin, Cyclophilins in our study. These significant SNPs could be further validated by developing a KASP marker in a biparental mapping population.

The current study will help to identify the genetic linkage, parental selection to develop mapping populations (Bi-parental, RILs, NILs, BILs, Indels, NAM), gene pyramiding, QTL fine mapping, and marker-assisted selection (MAS) of heat-tolerance candidate lines. Improving genetic diversity by introducing unique loci in future breeding lines, meta-analysis of available data, phylogenetic prediction of disease, and colocalization of gene analysis (pleiotropic) could be used to develop climate-resilient wheat lines. Functional validation of MTAs will help to dissect the major and minor loci associated with heat tolerance in wheat, and these MTAs could help to develop wheat varieties that can withstand heat stress conditions in Pakistan.
