Intra-arterial delivery of FePt nanoparticles induces ferroptosis and immune infiltration to enhance radiotherapy
Authors: Yaowei Yeh, Yuhsuan Huang, Yanshen Shan, Shangyin Wu, Hsiuyun Wang, Paosheng Hou, Yifu Chang, Wuchou Su, Helen HW Chen, Chiajui Yen, Tsunglin Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advances in nanomedicine provide new opportunities to enhance radiotherapy and overcome tumor radioresistance. In this study, we investigated the therapeutic potential of iron-platinum nanoparticles (FePt NPs) delivered through intra-arterial (IA) administration to improve tumor targeting and therapeutic efficacy. FePt NPs significantly inhibited the proliferation of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells and enhanced radiosensitivity. Mechanistic studies demonstrated that FePt NPs induced ferroptosis characterized by lipid peroxidation (LPO), mitochondrial damage, and downregulation of GPX4, leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including ATP, high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and calreticulin (CRT), thereby promoting ferroptosis-associated immunogenic cell death (ICD). In vitro cytokine array analysis further revealed modulation of immune-related cytokines associated with inflammatory responses and immune cell recruitment. In vivo studies showed that IA administration significantly increased FePt NPs accumulation within tumors compared with conventional intravenous delivery, resulting in enhanced tumor suppression. The combination of FePt NPs and radiotherapy further promoted T-cell and macrophage infiltration within the tumor microenvironment (TME), indicating enhanced immune activation. Importantly, in a comparative canine liver tumor model treated with FePt NPs via transarterial embolization, tumor regression or stabilization was observed together with increased immune infiltration. These findings demonstrate that IA-delivered FePt NPs act as ferroptosis-inducing radiosensitizers that reshape the tumor immune microenvironment and convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors. This approach highlights the translational potential of localized nanoparticle delivery for cancer radio-immunotherapy.Keywords: DAMPs release, FePt nanoparticles, Ferroptosis, Immunogenic cell death, Intra-arterial administration

Introduction

Radiotherapy remains one of the most widely used noninvasive modalities for cancer treatment. However, its efficacy is often limited by pronounced hypoxia in solid tumors [1] and the development of radioresistance, which can enhance tumor invasiveness and metastasis [2]. Immunotherapy has recently emerged as an effective anti-tumor strategy, either alone or in combination with surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy [3]. Despite this, the clinical response rate to immunotherapy alone remains low for most tumor types [4]. Recent advances in nanotechnology have significantly improved the efficacy of cancer therapeutics [5]. Notably, nanomedicine-mediated radiotherapy can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), which directly kills cancer cells and elicits systemic anti-tumor immune responses in several solid tumors [[6], [7], [8]]. ICD is characterized by the extracellular release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as surface-exposed calreticulin (CRT), secreted adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), all of which are recognized by pattern recognition receptors [9,10]. These DAMPs activate the migration of dendritic cells and prime T cells, thereby promoting systemic immunity against tumors [11]. Among the mechanisms underlying ICD—apoptosis, necrosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis—ferroptosis has been closely linked to the efficacy of radiotherapy [[12], [13], [14]]. However, the challenge remains to develop a platform that effectively bridges these processes to maximize clinical outcomes. Recent studies have highlighted the significant potential of advanced nanomedicines in optimizing cancer radio-immunotherapy, in which bimetallic nanoparticles serve as cascade-sensitization amplifiers to alleviate hypoxia and amplify radiation-induced ICD effects [15]. In this study, we hypothesize that iron-platinum nanoparticles (FePt NPs) can serve as potent ferroptosis-inducing radiosensitizers that not only amplify radiation-induced DNA damage but also trigger systemic anti-tumor immunity by initiating a sequential a ferroptosis-ICD-immune activation cascade.

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death triggered by iron accumulation and excessive lipid peroxidation (LPO), has been implicated in the development and therapeutic responses of various tumor types [16]. Nanoparticles (NPs) are recognized as novel inducers of ferroptosis [17]. In various cancer cells, treatment with iron-containing NPs can induce mitochondrial shrinkage with increased membrane density, elevate LPO levels, and promote cell death via ferroptosis [18,19]. Recent breakthroughs in bimetallic nanostructures have shown exceptional potential in orchestrating ferroptosis-driven immune activation [20], reinforcing our hypothesis for the FePt NPs platform. Our previous studies demonstrated that FePt NPs can induce ferroptosis in tyrosine kinase inhibitor-resistant mesenchymal lung cancer cells through CD44-hyaluronan-mediated endocytosis [21]. Since 2012, the role of ferroptosis in anticancer immunity has drawn increasing attention [22], suggesting that enhancing ferroptosis can improve the anti-tumor efficacy of cancer therapies. Immunotherapy-activated CD8+ T cells have been shown to promote ferroptosis-specific LPO in tumor cells, thereby synergizing with the anti-tumor efficacy of immunotherapy [23]. In addition, pharmacological induction of ferroptosis may modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME) by regulating glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) activity, which correlates with immune cell survival and infiltration [24]. However, the systemic delivery of such potent ferroptosis inducers is often hindered by suboptimal tumor enrichment. Advanced strategies, such as charge-conversion systems, have been developed to improve tumor penetration and accumulation [25].

Despite the promising enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect exhibited by metal-based NPs, only a few have been approved for the treatment of solid tumors [26]. Following intravenous (IV) administration, a substantial fraction of NPs is sequestered by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), particularly in the liver, kidneys, and spleen [27]. Consequently, systemic toxicity and myelosuppression remain major obstacles to achieving therapeutic drug concentrations at the tumor site. In contrast, intra-arterial (IA) administration of metal-based NP drugs may reduce systemic toxicity and increase the local tumor concentrations of therapeutic agents [28]. The feasibility, low morbidity, and good tolerability of IA-administered melphalan have previously been demonstrated in a U87MG tumor model [29]. Another promising approach involves the use of albumin-doxorubicin NPs conjugated with microbubbles, which, when administered via the IA route, significantly reduced tumor growth in a VX2 rabbit tumor model while minimizing hepatic toxicity [30].

Furthermore, given the numerous clinical and molecular similarities between canine and human cancers, the use of companion dogs with spontaneous tumors, coupled with a comparative oncology approach, has garnered considerable attention in drug development [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Conventional drug development is expensive and time-consuming, with unpredictable drug responses in xenograft rodent models often contributing to failures in Phase I and II clinical trials [35]. This challenge is particularly pronounced for complex therapeutics such as nanomedicines, where precise drug ratios and stimulus-responsive release are critical for clinical translation [36]. A 2016 news article published in the journal Science, titled “Can clinical trials on dogs and cats help people?,” pointed out that if a drug does not work in a dog, it is unlikely to work in humans. Therefore, the integration of comparative oncology using canine models offers a valuable and translationally relevant platform for nanomedicine development [37].

We aimed to investigate the therapeutic potential of FePt NPs as ferroptosis-inducing agents when delivered via IA administration and to evaluate their ability to enhance radiotherapy outcomes and modulate the tumor immune microenvironment. Using murine and companion canine tumor models, we explored whether this targeted delivery approach improved FePt NPs accumulation, therapeutic efficacy, and immune cell infiltration compared with conventional IV delivery. We sought to establish a translational framework for ferroptosis-based nanomedicine, highlighting the potential of IA-administered FePt NPs as radiosensitizers and immune modulators in future clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Study design

We investigated the therapeutic potential of FePt NPs in enhancing radiotherapy by analyzing a combination of in vitro cell lines and in vivo animal models. The research was conducted using human TNBC cell lines (MDA-MB-231 and Hs578T), a murine TNBC cell line (4T1), BALB/c mice, and canine cases with liver tumors. Data were collected in a series of controlled laboratory experiments, wherein cells were treated with FePt NPs alone, radiotherapy alone, or a combination of both to evaluate cell growth, cell death mechanisms, protein levels, and DAMPs release. For the in vivo mouse study, mice with established tumors were randomized into four groups based on tumor size to receive treatments via either IA or IV injection. Tumor volume was measured to assess therapeutic efficacy, whereas systemic biosafety and organ-specific tissue biodistribution were evaluated through histopathological profiling and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES), respectively. Furthermore, intratumoral immune cell infiltration was evaluated using immunohistochemical (IHC) and iron staining. The translational relevance was further examined in canine subjects treated with FePt NPs via transarterial embolization (TAE), with tumor volume and immune infiltration assessed through computed tomography (CT) imaging and IHC staining, respectively.

Synthesis of FePt NPs

FePt NPs (6 nm) were synthesized as previously described [38]. Briefly, platinum (III) acetylacetonate (97 mg; Acros Organics, Geel, Belgium) and iron pentacarbonyl (66 μL; Acros Organics) were combined with 1,2-hexadecanediol (195 mg; TCI, Tokyo, Japan), dioctyl ether (4 mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), oleylamine (100 μL; Acros Organics), and oleic acid (100 μL; SHOWA, Tokyo, Japan) in a reaction flask. The mixture was heated to 240°C at a rate of 15°C min−1 under a nitrogen atmosphere for 30 min. After cooling to 25°C, the product was precipitated by adding hexane and then collected via centrifugation. For ligand exchange, FePt NPs (100 mg) were dispersed in cysteamine (∼1 g) in ethanol via sonication. The suspension was sonicated for 4 h at 40°C and washed with ethanol to remove unbound ligands. The modified particles were collected and stored in nitrogen-filled bottles. The morphology, magnetic properties, and elemental composition of the synthesized FePt NPs were characterized using Transmission electron microscopy (TEM), Superconducting Quantum Interference Device (SQUID), and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), as shown in Fig. S1.

Cell culture

The human triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell lines MDA-MB-231 and Hs578T, the murine TNBC cell line 4T1, and the murine dendritic cell line DC2.4 were used in this study. MDA-MB-231 cells were cultured in Leibovitz's L-15 medium, whereas Hs578T cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium supplemented with 0.01 mg mL−1 insulin. Both murine 4T1 and DC2.4 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium. For DC2.4 cells, the RPMI-1640 medium was additionally supplemented with 2.5% v/v HEPES (1 M), 1% v/v L-glutamine, 1% v/v MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids (NEAA, 100×), and 0.00054% v/v 2-mercaptoethanol. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U mL−1 penicillin, and 100 μg mL−1 streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified chamber. MDA-MB-231 cells were maintained without CO2, whereas Hs578T, 4T1, and DC2.4 cells were cultured in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Cellular uptake

Cells were treated with a fixed concentration (1 mg mL−1) of FePt NPs for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were collected and digested overnight with aqua regia. The digested samples were then diluted and filtered through a 0.2-μm membrane. The resulting solution was transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and subsequently analyzed. Intracellular iron and platinum (Pt) concentrations were quantified using an iCAP 7400 ICP-OES system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Colony formation assay

A colony formation assay was conducted to evaluate the inhibitory effect of FePt NPs on TNBC cell growth. Cells (2 × 105) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. Following incubation, the cells were treated with various concentrations of FePt NPs (0, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, or 1 mg mL−1) for either 24 h or 48 h to map the toxicity profile as a pre-experimental screening. After the designated treatment periods, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), trypsinized, counted, and reseeded in triplicate at appropriate densities. Colonies were allowed to form undisturbed for seven days under standard culture conditions. At the end of the incubation period, the cells were fixed and stained with crystal violet. At least 50 colonies were selected for further analysis. To assess the radioenhancing effect of FePt NPs in TNBC cells, the cells were pretreated with FePt NPs for 48 h before exposure to ionizing radiation (IR) at doses of 0, 2, or 4 Gy. IR was performed using an X-ray cabinet irradiator (RS 2000; Rad Source, Buford, GA, USA) at the National Cheng Kung University Laboratory Animal Center (Tainan, Taiwan). Following IR, the cells were incubated for 4 h at 37°C, washed with PBS, and then trypsinized according to a previously described procedure.

Cell death identification

The effect of FePt NPs on cell death was assessed using specific cell death pathway inhibitors. Before co-treatment with FePt NPs, the cells were pretreated with the indicated inhibitors at appropriate concentrations for 1 h. The following inhibitors were used to block distinct cell death modalities: ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (10 μM; Sigma Aldrich) for ferroptosis; carbobenzoxy-valyl-alanyl-aspartyl-(O-methyl)-fluoromethylketone (Z-VAD-FMK) (20 μM; Bachem, Bubendorf, Switzerland) as a pan-caspase inhibitor for apoptosis; necrostatin-1 (Nec-1s) (50 μM; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) as a RIPK1-targeted inhibitor for necroptosis; and bafilomycin A1 (BFA) (0.5 nM; Sigma-Aldrich) as an autophagy inhibitor. Treated cells were subsequently processed for the colony formation assay.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates, incubated overnight, and then treated with 1 mg mL−1 FePt NPs. Following treatment, the cells were incubated in culture medium containing 500 nM MitoPerOx dye (ab146820; Abcam) for 30 min at 37°C in the dark to assess mitochondrial lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. For surface CRT detection, cells were stained with a FITC-conjugated anti-CRT antibody (bs-5913R-FITC; Bioss, Woburn, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. After staining, the cells were washed with PBS, resuspended in the appropriate buffer, and analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). For dendritic cell maturation analysis, DC2.4 dendritic cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2.5% v/v HEPES (1M) buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% v/v L-glutamine (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% v/v MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids Solution (100×) (NEAA), 1% v/v Penicillin-Streptomycin (100×), and 0.00054% v/v 2-mercaptoethanol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, US). To perform the functional assay, 4T1 cells were pre-treated with FePt NPs for 24 h or 48 h to generate therapy-induced conditioned media (CM). Subsequently, DC2.4 cells were incubated with the harvested CM for 24 h. To evaluate phenotypic maturation, the treated DC2.4 cells were harvested, washed, and stained with fluorophore-conjugated primary antibodies against FITC anti-mouse CD80 (Cat. No. 104706) and PE anti-mouse CD86 (Cat. No. 105008), obtained from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA), at 4°C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were then washed with PBS and analyzed using flow cytometry to determine the relative median fluorescence intensity (MFI).

Western blotting

Alterations in protein levels related to the ferroptosis pathway were evaluated through western blotting analysis. Cells were treated with or without 1 mg mL−1 FePt NPs and exposed to 6 Gy of IR. Following treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and lysed on ice for 30 min using RIPA lysis buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail]. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (10-50 μg) were separated on 10-15% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at 25°C and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the respective primary antibodies. After washing three times with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 25°C. Protein bands were detected via chemiluminescence using HRP Substrate Luminol Reagent (Millipore).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded into 4-well chamber slides (Millicell EZ Slide; Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) and incubated overnight. Subsequently, the cells were treated with or without 1 mg mL−1 FePt NPs for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, washed three times with PBS (5 min each), and permeabilized with 0.05% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were subsequently incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-HMGB1 antibody (ab18256; Abcam) at a dilution of 1:100, followed by washing with PBS and incubation with an Alexa Fluor 594 secondary antibody (A-11212; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) at a dilution of 1:1000 for 60 min. Counterstaining was performed using Hoechst 33258 (Invitrogen). Fluorescence signals were analyzed using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Quantification of DAMPs release

Cells were treated with 1 mg mL−1 FePt NPs, as described above. Following FePt NPs treatment and co-treatment with IR, culture supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 3 min at 4°C to remove insoluble debris. ATP release was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay kit (G7571; Promega, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were either used immediately or stored at −80°C until analysis.

Measurement of malondialdehyde content

Intracellular LPO was further validated via quantitative evaluation of malondialdehyde (MDA) levels using a commercial MDA Assay Kit (Cat. No. ab118970; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, 4T1 cells (6 × 105 cells) were seeded in a 6-cm dish and treated with FePt NPs for the indicated time points (0, 24, and 48 h). Following incubation, the cells were collected, washed with PBS, and lysed on ice. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min to harvest the supernatant. The raw MDA content was calculated via absorbance measurement at 532 nm using a microplate reader. The final values were normalized to the cell population and expressed as nmol/106 cells.

Cytokine profiling via membrane-based array

To evaluate treatment-induced changes in cytokine secretion, conditioned media collected from 4T1 cells under different treatment conditions were analyzed using a commercial Mouse Th1/Th2/Th17 Array C1 kit (RayBiotech, Inc., Peachtree Corners, GA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, array membranes were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with the collected supernatants mixed with a cocktail of biotinylated detection antibodies. After washing, streptavidin-HRP was applied, and the immunoblotted spots were visualized using chemiluminescence reagents. The spot intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized against the positive control spots on each membrane to yield relative expression levels.

Surgical procedure for IA administration

BALB/c mice were obtained from the National Cheng Kung University Laboratory Animal Center. All experimental procedures and animal handling were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee of National Cheng Kung University (IACUC Approval Number: 112168). To establish the IA administration model, a modified surgical procedure was performed, as described previously [39]. The mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and the hair on the ipsilateral hind leg was then removed. A longitudinal incision was made in the thigh to expose the femoral vessels. A 2-0 silk suture (Ethicon Inc., Somerville, NJ, USA) was placed proximally and distally around the femoral artery. Trypan blue was injected using a 31-gauge beveled NanoFil needle as a blood flow indicator. The surgical site was then closed, and the distribution of the dye in the paw was monitored for 4 h to confirm successful IA delivery.

In vivo anti-tumor effect in mouse models

4T1 cells (5 × 106) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of BALB/c mice. When the tumors reached a volume of 80-100 mm3, the mice were randomized into four groups (n = 5 per group): PBS (control), IR alone (6 Gy), FePt NPs alone (25 mg/kg), and combined FePt NPs (25 mg/kg) combined with 6 Gy IR. PBS or FePt NPs were administered via tail vein (IV) or femoral artery (IA) injection. Approximately 24 h post-injection, the right flank tumors were locally irradiated using lead shielding. Tumor volumes were measured three times per week using digital calipers and calculated using the formula: tumor volume (V) = 0.5 × (length × width2).

Histological evaluation for biosafety

To systematically assess the in vivo biocompatibility and systemic biosafety of the administered treatments, major visceral organs (including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested immediately after the mice were humanely sacrificed. The collected tissues were fixed in 4% buffered formalin overnight, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and subsequently sectioned into 4-μm thick slices. The tissue sections were subjected to standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to evaluate potential histological abnormalities, inflammatory responses, or structural damage induced by FePt NPs. The stained organ architectures were visualized and captured under a high-resolution optical microscope.

In vivo tissue biodistribution evaluation via ICP-OES

To quantify the systemic organ distribution and administration-dependent targeting efficiency, mice from different treatment groups were sacrificed 48 h post-injection. Major visceral organs, including the heart, lungs, liver, kidneys, and spleen, were meticulously harvested, rinsed thoroughly with ice-cold PBS to eliminate residual surface blood, and blot-dried on filter paper. The wet weight of each individual organ was precisely recorded using an analytical balance. Subsequently, each tissue specimen was transferred into a clean digestion vessel, and concentrated aqua regia (a 3:1 v/v mixture of 69-70% nitric acid (HNO3) and 37% HCl) was added. The mixtures were maintained under ambient conditions for 7 days until solid tissue architectures were entirely dissolved and the solutions became completely translucent. The resulting digested matrix was passed through a 0.22 μm membrane filter to remove any residual microparticulates. The filtrate was then quantitatively transferred and further diluted with ultra-pure deionized water to a final volume of 5-10 mL, ensuring an optimized acid matrix suitable for subsequent analysis. The total Pt concentration within each organ profile was systematically quantified via ICP-OES. A multi-point linear calibration curve was freshly established using commercial trace-element standard solutions across a matrix-matched concentration gradient. The final absolute mass of accumulated Pt was normalized to the corresponding wet tissue weight and expressed as micrograms of Pt per gram of tissue (μg g−1 tissue).

IHC staining

After the mice were sacrificed, tumors were fixed in 4% formalin overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The tissues were then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), as well as with the following antibodies: anti-CD3 (ab16669; Abcam), anti-CD4 (D7D2Z; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-FoxP3 (FJK-16S; eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA), anti-CD8 (D4W2Z; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-CD11c (#117302; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA), anti-CD86 (ab53004; Abcam), and anti-CD206 (ab64694; Abcam), to evaluate T-cell and macrophage infiltration in the TME. Stained tissue sections were visualized under a microscope and quantified using the TissueFAXS imaging system (TissueGnostics, Vienna, Austria).

Iron staining

Mice were sacrificed at 4 and 24 h after FePt NPs injection. Tumors were fixed in 4% formalin overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, after which they were incubated for 10 min with a 1:1 mixture of hydrochloric acid and potassium ferrocyanide (SIGMA HT20). The sections were then rinsed thrice with distilled water and counterstained with nuclear fast red for 3-5 min, followed by another three washes with distilled water. The slides were dehydrated, and coverslips were added using a resinous mounting medium.

In vivo evaluation of FePt NPs transarterial embolization in canines

All experimental procedures and animal handling were conducted in compliance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Taiwan Earning Institutional Animal Care & Use Committee (IACUC Approval Number: EA21-001). Regarding the canine model, this study recruited clinical cases with naturally occurring hepatic tumors confirmed via histopathological biopsy and pre-operative CT imaging. Signalment, clinical history, and baseline tumor volumes were recorded. The TAE procedure was performed by a veterinarian. Briefly, the right femoral artery was punctured using a 20-G needle and cannulated with a 4-French introducer sheath. A guidewire and catheter were advanced into the aorta and then into the celiac artery under fluoroscopic guidance. A micro-guidewire was subsequently introduced into the common hepatic artery through the catheter. To distinguish the therapeutic effects of FePt NPs from the mechanical effects of vascular occlusion, a control group receiving pure TAE embolic agents (Gelfoam and Lipiodol only) was established as a baseline. For the experimental group, embolization was performed using FePt NPs mixed with Lipiodol and Gelfoam, which were delivered via a 50 mL syringe. The FePt NPs dose was calculated based on body weight and body surface area (BSA). All dogs were monitored for three months after TAE. Abdominal and dorsal CT images were reviewed monthly using CDVIEWER software to assess tumor volume and evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

IHC staining of canine tumor tissues

Liver tumor tissues were collected from canine cases at defined time points (e.g., 1.5 and 2.5 months post-treatment) for histopathological analysis. The tissues were fixed in 4% formalin overnight, followed by routine paraffin embedding and sectioning at standard thicknesses. The sections were then subjected to IHC staining to assess the infiltration and expression of specific immune cell populations within the TME. The primary antibodies used included those against CD3 (IR252-1; iReal, Hsinchu City, Taiwan), CD4 (GB11064; ServiceBio, Wuhan, China), CD8 (bs-0648R; Bio SB, Goleta, CA, USA), CD20 (BSB5195; BioSB), and CD44 (GTX102111; Genetex, Irvine, CA, USA). Stained tissue sections were observed under a microscope, and quantitative analysis of positive cell populations performed using the TissueFAXS system.

Statistical analysis

All data were derived from at least three independent biological replicates (n ≧ 3) and are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical evaluations were performed using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student's t-test or multiple t-tests were applied. For multi-group comparisons involving a single independent variable, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test or Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was utilized. Statistical significance thresholds were defined as follows: ∗p < 0.05, p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001; "ns" denotes no significant difference. The specific statistical methods and post hoc tests applied for each individual experiment are explicitly detailed in their respective figure legends.

Results and discussion

FePt NPs enhance IR-induced cell killing in cancer cells

Our previous study reported that FePt NPs can enhance the radiosensitivity of lung cancer cells with high hCtr1 expression by inducing mitochondrial dysfunction through ROS-mediated damage due to substantial NP accumulation [40]. Based on these findings, in the current study, we evaluated the cellular uptake of FePt NPs in the following TNBC cell lines: 4T1, MDA-MB-231, and Hs 578T. Micrographs revealed that FePt NPs were visible as distinct black dots owing to their high density. ICP-OES revealed that the intracellular concentrations of FePt NPs were 1.76 ± 0.48, 2.04 ± 0.49, and 1.04 ± 0.14 ng cell−1 in 4T1, Hs578T, and MDA-MB-231 cells, respectively (Fig. 1A). We then evaluated the inhibitory effect of FePt NPs on TNBC cell proliferation and their potential as radiosensitizers. To establish a rational dosing strategy tailored to the distinct biological characteristics of each cell line, we first performed a preliminary dose-response screening across a broad concentration range of FePt NPs (Fig. S2). This screening revealed marked differences in baseline sensitivity. MDA-MB-231 cells were more sensitive to FePt NPs, whereas 4T1 and Hs578T cells exhibited higher resistance. To ensure that downstream evaluations of radiosensitization, ferroptosis, and immunogenic signaling were conducted within a comparable cytotoxic range rather than at an arbitrary absolute dose, cell line-specific dose pairs were selected for subsequent long-term colony formation assays. 4T1 and Hs578T cells were treated with 0, 0.5, and 1 mg mL−1 FePt NPs, whereas MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with 0, 0.1, and 0.25 mg mL−1 for 48 h. After treatment, the cells were cultured in fresh medium for seven days to allow colony formation (Fig. 1B). To assess radiosensitization, the treated cells were exposed to IR at doses of 2 or 4 Gy (Fig. 1C). In 4T1 cells, the surviving fractions (SFs) following FePt NPs treatment alone were 1.0, 0.086 ± 0.036, and 0.003 ± 0.002 for treatment concentrations of 0, 0.5, and 1 mg mL−1, respectively; when combined with 4 Gy IR, the SFs were further reduced to 0.16 ± 0.007, 0.002 ± 0.000, and 0, respectively. Similarly, in Hs578T cells, treatment with FePt NPs alone yielded SFs of 1.0, 0.068 ± 0.005, and 0.008 ± 0.003 for treatment concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.25 mg mL−1, respectively, which decreased further with 4 Gy IR to 0.445 ± 0.059, 0.016 ± 0.003, and 0.005 ± 0.001, respectively. In MDA-MB-231 cells, FePt NPs reduced the SFs to 1.0, 0.264 ± 0.014, and 0.075 ± 0.011 at treatment concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.25 mg mL−1, respectively. Upon exposure to 2 Gy IR, the SFs declined to 0.371 ± 0.033, 0.120 ± 0.017, and 0.049 ± 0.012 for the respective treatment concentrations (Fig. 1D). These findings demonstrate that FePt NPs significantly enhance the cytotoxic effects of radiation in TNBC cells by promoting cell death, suggesting their potential as a promising strategy for improving radiotherapy outcomes.

FePt NPs induce ferroptosis in TNBC cells

Although FePt NPs exhibited potent inhibitory effects on TNBC cell growth, the precise mechanism underlying FePt NPs-induced cell death remained unclear. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the underlying mode of cell death in vitro. To delineate the mechanism of action, TNBC cells were co-treated with FePt NPs and specific inhibitors targeting distinct cell death pathways: apoptosis (Z-VAD-FMK; 20 μM), autophagy (BFA; 0.5 nM), necroptosis (Nec-1s; 50 μM), and ferroptosis (Fer-1; 10 μM). After 24 h of co-treatment, colony formation assays were performed (Fig. 2A). In 4T1 cells, only moderate restoration of colony formation was observed after treatment with inhibitors of apoptosis (47.0% ± 3.42%), autophagy (32.4% ± 1.94%), and necroptosis (42.3% ± 4.52%). In contrast, Fer-1 significantly reversed FePt NPs-induced cell death, increasing colony formation from 33.7% ± 0.67% to 99.1% ± 5.72% (Fig. 2B) relative to untreated controls (set at 100%), suggesting that ferroptosis is the dominant cell death pathway induced by FePt NPs in TNBC cells. While conventional ICD is often associated with apoptosis, our results demonstrate a clear mechanistic departure. The negligible efficacy of Z-VAD-FMK and Nec-1s suggests that apoptosis and necroptosis play only minor roles in FePt NPs-induced cell death. In contrast, the near-total rescue by Fer-1 identifies ferroptosis as the definitive executioner.

To further validate this finding, the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins in FePt NPs-treated TNBC cells was assessed using western blotting at different time points. The expression of GPX4, a peroxidase that mitigates LPO via glutathione reduction, was downregulated, whereas that of prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2, encoding COX-2), a ferroptosis-associated marker, was upregulated. Furthermore, the expression of heme oxygenase-1, which promotes ferroptosis, was significantly upregulated. Transferrin receptor-1 and ferritin heavy chain-1 levels also increased in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2C), further supporting the activation of ferroptosis. However, co-treatment with IR and FePt NPs did not result in significant changes in the expression of ferroptosis-related markers compared with FePt NPs treatment alone (Fig. 2D), suggesting that IR primarily induces DNA damage without affecting ferroptosis. These findings indicate that FePt NPs induce ferroptosis as the primary mechanism of cell death in TNBC cells.

FePt NPs induce ferroptosis via mitochondrial dysfunction and LPO burst

High-resolution TEM revealed that FePt NPs-treated TNBC cells exhibited characteristic subcellular morphological features of ferroptosis, including cell membrane vesiculation or ruptures, smaller and shriveled mitochondria, and reduced or absent mitochondrial cristae (Fig. 3A, black arrows). Iron-containing NPs can release ferrous (Fe2+) or ferric (Fe3+) ions into lysosomes, inducing ferroptosis through the Fenton reaction, which generates ROS and LPO [41]. As LPO accumulation is a hallmark of ferroptosis, we evaluated whether FePt NPs could induce LPO in TNBC cells.

LPO levels were measured by flow cytometry using C11-BODIPY staining (Fig. 3B). Our results demonstrate that combined treatment with FePt NPs and IR significantly increased LPO levels in all TNBC cell lines tested. Specifically, the MFI was 11.2 ± 0.25 in 4T1 cells, 77.0 ± 1.80 in Hs578T cells, and 216.3 ± 4.04 in MDA-MB-231 cells. In each cell line, LPO levels in the FePt NPs + IR group were significantly higher than those in the IR-only group (Fig. 3C), indicating a synergistic effect. To further substantiate this lipid bilayer degradation via an independent biochemical quantification, we evaluated the intracellular accumulation of MDA in 4T1 cells over time. As shown in Fig. S3, the baseline MDA level in untreated cells was low (0.054 ± 0.012 nmol/106 cells). Following incubation with FePt NPs, MDA levels increased in a time-dependent manner, showing a modest elevation at 24 h (0.054 ± 0.012 nmol/106 cells) and a marked increase at 48 h (0.206 ± 0.006 nmol/106 cells). These data suggest that FePt NPs, in combination with IR, promote ferroptosis in TNBC cells by enhancing LPO accumulation.

FePt NPs trigger DAMPs release through ferroptosis activation

A defining feature of ICD is the spatiotemporally coordinated release of DAMPs, such as ATP, HMGB1, and CRT, which interact with the immune system to exert anticancer responses [42]. Given the role of ATP as a key DAMPs, we evaluated its release profile in TNBC cells following FePt NPs treatment. As shown in Fig. 3D, no significant ATP release was observed within the initial 24 h. However, a marked increase was detected at 48 h (p < 0.05), followed by a further significant increase at 72 h (p < 0.001). Notably, combination treatment with IR did not further enhance ATP release compared with that following FePt NPs treatment alone (Fig. 3E), indicating that IR has a minimal impact on ATP dynamics in this context.

Notably, the release pattern of HMGB1—a well-established DAMP—differed from that of ATP, especially during ferroptosis. To explore this, we analyzed the intracellular localization of HMGB1 using confocal microscopy. As shown in Fig. 3F, nuclear HMGB1 fluorescence was reduced in FePt NPs-treated cells compared with that in untreated controls and was further diminished in the FePt NPs + IR group (yellow arrow), suggesting that HMGB1 translocated from the nucleus to the cytoplasm.

CRT surface exposure, another hallmark of ICD, was also evaluated (Fig. 3G). Flow cytometry revealed significant CRT upregulation following FePt NPs treatment in 4T1 (MFI = 61.2 ± 0.29), Hs578T (MFI = 73.5 ± 0.40), and MDA-MB-231 (MFI = 81.1 ± 0.40) cells compared with their respective controls (MFI in 4T1 = 51.2 ± 0.29; MFI in Hs578T = 66.0 ± 0.92; MFI in MDA-MB-231 = 34.3 ± 0.30). In Hs578T cells, CRT expression was highest in the FePt NPs + IR group (MFI = 91.4 ± 0.75), whereas in MDA-MB-231 cells, FePt NPs treatment alone induced a robust CRT response (MFI = 81.1 ± 0.40) comparable to that of the combined treatment group (MFI = 78.0 ± 0.46) and markedly higher than that induced by IR treatment alone (MFI = 41.8 ± 1.00) (Fig. 3H). These findings demonstrate that FePt NPs effectively induce the release or surface exposure of key ICD markers—ATP, HMGB1, and CRT—in TNBC cells, highlighting their potential to promote ICD via ferroptosis activation.

FePt NPs and IR synergistically trigger acute inflammatory signaling and promote dendritic cell maturation

To further investigate whether FePt NPs-induced ferroptosis and DAMPs release are associated with inflammatory signaling, we next analyzed cytokine secretion profiles using a cytokine array (Fig. 4A). FePt NPs treatment alone moderately increased several inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 (0.335), TNF-α (0.182), and TGF-β1 (0.409) compared with untreated controls. In contrast, irradiation prominently induced the chemokine MIP-3α (1.152), which plays a key role in recruiting immature dendritic cells and memory T cells [43], together with increased IL-6 (0.856) and IFN-γ (0.355). Notably, the combination of FePt NPs and irradiation further enhanced multiple cytokines, including TNF-α (0.634), and TGF-β1 (0.747), while maintaining elevated levels of IL-6 (0.863) and MIP-3α (1.039) (Fig. 4B).

Although IL-6 is often associated with tumor progression and immunosuppressive signaling under chronic inflammatory conditions, its function is highly context-dependent [[44], [45], [46]]. In the present study, the observed increase in IL-6 occurred in parallel with ferroptosis induction, DAMPs release, and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α and MIP-3α. This coordinated response suggests that IL-6 may be part of an acute inflammatory signaling cascade triggered by therapy-induced tumor cell damage rather than reflecting a chronic tumor-promoting effect [47,48]. Collectively, these findings indicate that FePt NPs-mediated ferroptosis not only promotes DAMPs release but also enhances cytokine signaling, thereby potentially facilitating immune modulation within the TME.

The upregulated secretomes, including TNF-α, IL-6, and the chemokine MIP-3α, suggests the activation of an inflammatory signaling network that may contribute to immune cell recruitment and activation. MIP-3α (CCL20) acts as a primary homing signal dedicated to recruiting immature dendritic cells (DCs) to sites of stress, while pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and acute-phase IL-6 serve as requisite maturation stimuli that drive DCs into a functional antigen-presenting phenotype. Therefore, the increased levels of these mediators suggested that the secretome of FePt NPs-treated tumor cells may have the potential to promote downstream immune activation.

To experimentally evaluate this possibility, we next examined whether tumor-conditioned media could induce maturation of antigen-presenting cells. To achieve this, 4T1 breast cancer cells were treated with FePt NPs for 24 h or 48 h to generate conditioned media containing therapy-induced cellular factors. Subsequently, DC2.4 dendritic cells were then incubated with the corresponding conditioned media for 24 h, and the expression levels of critical costimulatory molecules (CD80 and CD86) were analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 4C).

As shown in the representative histogram overlays, incubation with conditioned media from FePt NPs-treated 4T1 cells induced a rightward shift in fluorescence intensity for both CD80 and CD86 (Fig. 4D). Quantitative analysis of the relative MFI further showed that direct exposure of DC2.4 cells to FePt NPs alone did not markedly induce maturation, whereas conditioned media from FePt NPs-treated tumor cells significantly upregulated CD80 and CD86 expression. This effect was observed with conditioned media collected from both the 24 h and 48 h tumor treatment groups (Fig. 4E), resulting in approximate 1.3-fold and 1.4-fold increase in CD80 and CD86 expression, respectively, compared with the control group. Collectively, these findings indicate that FePt NPs-mediated tumor stress and ferroptosis promotes the release of immune-stimulating factors that can contribute to dendritic cell maturation.

Preferential accumulation of FePt NPs in tumors of 4T1-bearing mice

Despite the enhanced EPR effect, NP delivery through systemic circulation is often limited by the RES, resulting in <1% of the administered dose reaching the tumor site [49]. To address this limitation, we developed a localized delivery model in BALB/c mice wherein FePt NPs were administered via IA injection. As shown in Fig. S4A, a longitudinal incision was made in the mouse thigh to expose the femoral vessels. Mice were positioned under a dissection microscope, and the superficial femoral arteries were then identified. Nylon (2-0) sutures were looped (not ligated) around the artery, which was then cannulated using a 31-gauge beveled NanoFil needle to inject trypan blue injection as an indicator (Fig. S4A, Step 1). After injection, the needle was removed, and the mouse leg examined for localized staining (Fig. S4A, Step 2, green circle). Approximately 2 h post-injection, blue discoloration in the mouse paw indicated successful IA delivery (Fig. S4A, Step 3, blue arrow).

To evaluate FePt NPs accumulation, 4T1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the distal hind legs of mice. Tumors were established within 7-8 days post-implantation. Tumor-bearing mice were then administered FePt NPs (25 mg kg−1) via either IV or IA injection. At 4 and 24 h post-injection, the mice were sacrificed, and tumor tissues were harvested for H&E staining as well as iron staining (Fig. S4B). Quantitative analysis using TissueFAXS revealed significantly greater iron accumulation in the IA group than in the IV group. At 4 h post-injection, iron-positive areas were 5.65 ± 1.1% in the IA group versus 0.342 ± 0.07% in the IV group. At 24 h, iron levels remained higher in the IA group (2.77 ± 0.63%) than in the IV group (1.18 ± 0.16%) (Fig. S4C). These results demonstrate that IA administration enhances the tumor-targeting efficacy of FePt NPs, offering a promising strategy for improving localized therapeutic delivery.

Enhanced tumor suppression via IA delivery in 4T1-bearing mice

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of FePt NPs delivered via different routes, we employed a 4T1 cell xenograft mouse model, comparing IV and IA administration of FePt NPs alone or in combination with IR. Before treatment, tumor radiosensitivity was evaluated using IR doses ranging from 2 to 10 Gy. Tumor growth, as monitored via mCherry fluorescence imaging and caliper measurements, demonstrated a dose-dependent response. Notably, exposure to 6 Gy IR caused moderate tumor inhibition (14.63-fold change) and was thus selected for use in combination therapy with FePt NPs (Fig. S5).

4T1 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flanks of mice approximately seven days prior to treatment initiation. Treatment commenced (Day 0) once tumor volumes reached approximately 80-100 mm3. Mice were then randomly assigned to experimental groups, and tumor growth and body weight were monitored for 24 days.

In the IV group, mice received FePt NPs (25 mg kg−1) via tail vein injection. In the combination group, 6 Gy X-ray IR was administered 24 h after NP injection. After 24 days, tumors in the saline-treated controls grew by approximately 15.58-fold. The IV-administered FePt NPs alone reduced tumor growth to 14.7-fold, a statistically significant difference compared with the saline control (p < 0.05). Treatment with IR alone and IV FePt NPs + IR both significantly inhibited tumor progression (7.07-fold and 7.36-fold increases, respectively; p < 0.01 vs saline), demonstrating comparable therapeutic effects (Fig. 5A). Body weight remained stable across all IV treatment groups (Fig. 5B), indicating low systemic toxicity.

In contrast, IA delivery of FePt NPs at the same dose as that used in the IV group showed markedly greater therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 5C). IA-administered FePt NPs alone significantly reduced tumor growth (3.17-fold; p < 0.01 vs saline), whereas the combination of IA FePt NPs + IR led to near-complete tumor regression (0.47-fold change; p < 0.001 vs saline), with tumors becoming nearly undetectable. This enhanced efficacy is likely due to higher local NP accumulation at the tumor site following IA delivery. Body weights in the IA group also remained stable (Fig. 5D), supporting the safety profile of the treatment. These results highlight the superior tumor-targeting capability and therapeutic effects of IA-delivered FePt NPs, especially when combined with IR, and support the potential of localized delivery strategies to maximize anti-tumor efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity.

Enhanced immune cell infiltration following IA-administered FePt NPs + IR treatment

Based on our previous findings, FePt NPs induce ferroptosis and stimulate immune activation within the TME through the release of DAMPs [14]. To further investigate immune cell recruitment, we performed histopathological analysis using IHC on excised tumors from both the IV (Fig. 5E) and IA (Fig. 5F) treatment groups. IHC staining targeted key immune markers, including pan T-cell markers (CD3), helper T cells (CD4), cytotoxic T cells (CD8), regulatory T cells (FoxP3), costimulatory molecule expression on antigen-presenting cells (CD86), alternatively activated macrophages (CD206), and dendritic cells (CD11c). Quantitative analysis using TissueFAXS revealed distinct patterns of immune cell infiltration between the IV-treated (Fig. 5G) and IA-treated (Fig. 5H) groups. In the IV-administered mice, FePt NPs alone elevated CD3+ (11.71%) and CD4+ T-cell (13.24%) levels compared with those in the saline control (CD3: 5.58%, CD4: 4.78%). However, when combined with IR, these percentages slightly decreased (CD3: 8.14%, CD4: 6.16%). No notable changes were observed in CD8+ T or FoxP3+ regulatory T cells in the IV group.

By contrast, IA administration of FePt NPs resulted in a markedly higher infiltration of CD3+ (28.24%) and CD4+ T cells (26.55%), which was further increased upon combination with IR, reaching 45.96% for CD3+ and 34.96% for CD4+ T cells. Moreover, the IA FePt NPs + IR group exhibited the highest infiltration of CD8+ T cells (37.65%), indicating robust activation of anti-tumor immunity. IA administration also enhanced intratumoral CD86+ cell infiltration (FePt NPs: 19.33%, FePt NPs + IR: 12.33%), consistent with the activation of M1-polarized macrophages, which are associated with pro-inflammatory and anti-tumor functions [50]. A slight reduction in CD86+ cells in the IA combination group may reflect transient immune suppression following IR or a feedback regulatory mechanism.

Both the IA FePt NPs (29.82%) and IA FePt NPs + IR (27.64%) treatment conditions significantly increased CD206+ M2 macrophage populations, potentially indicating a compensatory immunosuppressive response within the TME. CD11c + dendritic cells were more prominent in the IA-treated group than in the IV group. The observed decline in local CD11c + dendritic cells likely reflects their maturation and subsequent migration from the TME to tumor-draining lymph nodes after capturing antigens released by FePt NPs-induced ICD, a process necessary for priming systemic T-cell responses. However, their overall levels declined in all FePt NPs-treated groups across both administration routes, suggesting that the NPs may affect dendritic cell recruitment, maturation, or migration.

These results underscore the critical role that the administration route plays in shaping FePt NPs-mediated immune modulation within the TME. The IA FePt NPs + IR treatment notably enhanced cytotoxic T-cell infiltration, consistent with ICD induced by ferroptosis. These findings support the potential of combining ferroptosis-inducing nanomedicines with localized delivery and radiotherapy to enhance anti-tumor immune responses.

In vivo systemic biosafety and biodistribution of FePt NPs

Encouraged by the potent transarterial immunomodulatory efficacy, we performed an evaluation of the in vivo systemic biosafety and tissue biodistribution of FePt NPs in healthy murine models to assess their translational potential. Histopathological assessment via H&E staining of major clearance and vital organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) was conducted post-treatment. All examined organ sections from both the IV and IA groups exhibited intact cellular architectures, clear tissue morphology, and no obvious inflammatory infiltration or focal necrosis compared with the saline control group (Fig. 6A), demonstrating favorable tissue-level biocompatibility. In parallel, ICP-OES was utilized to quantify organ-specific Pt deposition (Fig. 6B). Compared with the conventional IV administration, which resulted in higher off-target Pt accumulation in major RES and clearance organs, locoregional IA delivery markedly limited rapid systemic dissemination. This locoregional delivery approach led to a broad and significant reduction in Pt accumulation across healthy vital organs in the IA group compared to the IV group. Furthermore, longitudinal hematological profiles showed no abnormal variations in white blood cell parameters (WBC, NE, LY, and MO) between groups (Fig. 6C). Serum biochemical profiles, encompassing liver metabolic enzymes (GOT and GPT) and renal/systemic markers (BUN, CRE, UA, TP, TCHO, GLU, and TG), likewise revealed no significant deviations compared with the saline control group (Fig. 6D). These collective benchmarks substantiate the robust systemic safety and superior biokinetics of regional FePt NPs administration, providing a solid ethical and empirical foundation for further advancement of this nanomedicine platform into large, spontaneous canine tumor models.

Therapeutic efficacy and safety of FePt NPs in canines

Given the significant pathological similarities between spontaneous tumors in dogs and humans, canine tumor models offer a clinically relevant platform for evaluating the therapeutic potential of NPs [51]. Therefore, we recruited canines with spontaneous hepatocellular carcinoma to assess the efficacy of FePt NPs. Two prospective clinical cases of dogs with spontaneous hepatocellular carcinoma were enrolled. Before treatment, the FePt NPs dose for each animal was calculated based on body weight and BSA and scaled from the maximum systemically administered dose [52]. The FePt NPs were sterilized and confirmed to be endotoxin-free, thus meeting clinical-grade standards.

Case 1: A 50-kg dog was diagnosed with two heterogeneous liver tumors located in the left (12 × 11 × 10 cm) and right (9 × 6.5 × 8.5 cm) lobes, as detected via non-contrast cranio-abdominal multi-detector CT. Based on a BSA of 1.371 m2, the total dose of FePt NPs was calculated as 41.13 mg. For TAE, the FePt NPs were mixed with approximately 1 g Gelfoam in a 50 mL syringe and delivered via the celiac artery using guidewire assistance. The FePt NPs-containing mixture was specifically targeted to the arterial branches supplying the right lobe. Anesthesia lasted for approximately 5 h. CT scans were performed on Day 0 and at 1 and 2.5 months post-treatment to monitor tumor progression (Fig. 7B). At the 1-month period, both tumors showed a reduction in size, decreasing from 966 to 472.5 cm3 in the left lobe and from 535.5 to 382.5 cm3 in the right lobe. However, by 2.5 months, the left lobe tumor had regrown to 1320 cm3, whereas the right lobe tumor remained relatively stable at 497.25 cm3 (Fig. 7C).

Case 2: An 8.2-kg dog underwent the same protocol used in Case 1. The FePt NPs dose was calculated as 12.15 mg and suspended in saline. CT scans were obtained on Day 0 and at 1 and 1.5 months post-treatment (Fig. 7D). The tumor in the left hepatic lobe progressively shrank, decreasing from 180 to 74.25 cm3 (Fig. 7E).

Comprehensive safety evaluations, including hematology, blood chemistry, coagulation profiles, and urinalysis, revealed no systemic toxicity following FePt NPs administration. To evaluate the in vivo blood compatibility and organ toxicity of the FePt NPs, we performed longitudinal blood biochemical monitoring for both Case 1 and Case 2 (Fig. S6). The results indicated that renal function markers (CREA and BUN) remained stable within the reference range throughout the observation period (up to 83 days). While a transient elevation in liver enzymes, particularly ALT, was observed on Day 2 post-treatment, these levels rapidly returned to baseline. This transient peak is consistent with post-embolization syndrome, likely resulting from localized tumor necrosis and the embolic effect of the TAE procedure [53], rather than systemic toxicity of the NPs themselves. Furthermore, the stability of total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), and glucose (GLU) levels suggests that hepatic metabolic function remained intact after treatment. The absence of adverse effects in this large-animal model supports the biosafety and translational potential of FePt NPs for human cancer therapy.

FePt NPs-mediated immune modulation and histopathological profiling in canine tumors

In addition to evaluating tumor growth inhibition, histopathological analysis was performed on liver tissues collected from two canine patients at the end of the post-treatment period. IHC was conducted using markers for T cells (CD3, CD4, and CD8) and B cells (CD20), with the aim of quantifying immune cell populations and characterizing the TME following FePt NPs treatment.

In Case 1 (2.5 months post-treatment), FePt NPs-treated tumors exhibited marked increases in all measured immune markers compared with those in both the pre-treatment and Gelfoam control groups (Fig. 8A). Specifically, CD3+ T cell levels increased from 3.72% to 40.77%, CD4+ from 18.20% to 81.10%, CD8+ from 21.65% to 46.75%, and CD20+ B cells from 25.03% to 73.26%. CD44+ expression also significantly increased from 48.04% to 96.56% (Fig. 8B). Notably, while the Gelfoam control group showed minimal changes in lymphocytic infiltration, the FePt-TAE group demonstrated robust recruitment of adaptive immune cells, particularly cytotoxic T cells. These results indicate robust activation of the adaptive immune response within the TME following FePt NPs administration. The marked elevation in CD3+ T cells, including CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic subsets, suggests enhanced T-cell recruitment and activation. The concurrent increase in CD20+ B cells implies involvement of the humoral immune response, potentially through antigen presentation or local antibody production. Elevated CD44 expression supports its role in FePt NPs uptake and immune modulation within the TME.

In Case 2 (1 month post-treatment) (Fig. 8C), although CD3+ T cell levels moderately increased from 3.80% to 6.52%, CD8+ cytotoxic T-cell levels significantly increased from 0.52% to 4.25% and of CD20+ B-cell levels increased from 3.11% to 12.13% (Fig. 8D). Persistent CD44 expression further indicates ongoing cellular uptake and immune activation within the tumor site.

We present the first application of FePt NPs via localized IA delivery using TAE in a companion animal model. The results underscore the therapeutic benefits of regional FePt NPs delivery, demonstrating significant tumor reduction and potent immune activation in a large animal model. These outcomes were achieved without observable systemic toxicity, representing a key milestone in translational nanomedicine and effectively bridging the gap between the bench and bedside.

Conclusions

Our study highlights the critical influence that the administration route has on FePt NPs accumulation in tumors. Conventional systemic IV delivery, even when combined with radiotherapy, achieved only modest tumor suppression and limited synergistic effects, likely due to suboptimal NP uptake at the tumor site. In contrast, IA administration significantly improved FePt NPs accumulation in tumors, leading to markedly improved anti-tumor efficacy, including tumor regression and stabilization in a canine cancer model. Notably, FePt NPs modulated the tumor immune microenvironment by inducing ferroptosis, which triggered the release of DAMPs, including ATP, HMGB1, and CRT, thereby promoting ICD. This immune-stimulating effect enhanced T-cell infiltration, particularly that of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, in both murine and canine models. Mechanistically, this process triggers a ferroptosis-driven ICD cascade, a pathway distinct from traditional apoptotic-mediated immune activation. By focusing on iron-dependent LPO rather than caspase-mediated pathways, this strategy provides a novel avenue for sensitizing “cold” tumors to radiotherapy. IHC analysis of canine tumor tissues further corroborated these findings, revealing a robust increase in immune cell infiltration following treatment. In conclusion, our study identifies the IA administration route as a critical determinant for maximizing the therapeutic window of FePt NPs-mediated radiotherapy. By bypassing systemic sequestration, IA delivery enables substantial NP enrichment at the tumor site, synergistically amplifying radiotherapy-induced DNA damage and ferroptosis. These results demonstrate that IA-delivered FePt NPs effectively reshape the tumor immune microenvironment, successfully converting immunologically “cold” tumors into highly responsive “hot” tumors. The validation of this approach in both murine and canine large-animal models underscores its significant potential as a dual-function radiosensitizer and immune activator for future clinical translation.
