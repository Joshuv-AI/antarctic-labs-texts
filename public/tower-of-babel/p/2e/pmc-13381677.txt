Son preference and regional inequalities in girls undernutrition in India
Authors: Soumen Barik, Dewaram A. Nagdeve, Anuj Singh, Mayank Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Child malnutrition in India remains among the highest globally, with persistent gender disparities rooted in son preference. Although girls are biologically more resilient, they often receive less food and care in son preferring households. Using nationally representative data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5, 2019–21), this study analysed 112,255 girls aged 0–59 months to examine how son preference influences stunting, wasting, and underweight across India’s regions. Son preference was defined as mothers desiring more sons than daughters in their ideal family composition. Generalized linear models with logit links were used to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs), controlling for child, maternal, and household characteristics, with analyses conducted nationally and across six regions. Nationally, girls in son preferring households had slightly higher odds of stunting (OR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00–1.04), wasting (OR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00–1.04), and underweight (OR = 1.02; 95% CI: 1.00–1.04). Regionally, disparities were more pronounced in Central (stunting: OR = 1.04; underweight: OR = 1.06) and Eastern India (stunting: OR = 1.03; underweight: OR = 1.04), while Southern and Northeastern regions showed negligible or reversed associations. Maternal education, higher household wealth, and better maternal nutritional status were protective factors. These findings indicate that son preference contributes to modest but meaningful disadvantages in girls’ nutritional outcomes, particularly in more patriarchal regions. Targeted, gender-transformative strategies within existing nutrition programs are needed, with priority attention to Central and Eastern India while drawing lessons from more gender-equitable regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-52420-y.

Introduction

Malnutrition remains a leading cause of developmental delays, illness, and death among children under five globally, contributing to approximately 45% of such deaths1,2. Malnutrition affects individual health and society by reducing productivity, impairing cognitive development, and limiting educational achievements, thereby hindering the country’s economic growth3–5. In 2018, an estimated 150.8 million (22.2%) children under five were stunted, 50.3 million (7.5%) wasted, and 38.3 million (5.6%) overweight6. South Asia bears the highest burden of malnutrition7 with India alone accounting for nearly one-third of the world’s stunted children6. According to NFHS-5 (2021), 36% of Indian children under five are stunted, 19% wasted, and 32% underweight figures that reflect persistent systemic failures in nutrition and equity8. Cultural gender preference plays a crucial role in shaping child nutritional status and health outcomes9–12 observed that, after surviving infancy, girls tend to have poorer nutritional outcomes than boys in South Asia like Bangladesh, India, and Nepal13. In contrast14, reported the opposite pattern in National family and health survey five in India, where boys are more nutritionally disadvantaged than girls.

Gender bias in nutrition is concerning because it can extend to other areas, reinforcing inequalities across multiple dimensions. For adolescent girls, malnutrition can also negatively impact reproductive health, creating additional challenges15,16. They face lifelong nutritional risks due to their unique biological trajectory including menarche, pregnancy, and lactation which demand higher caloric and micronutrient intake. When undernourished, girls are more likely to become undernourished mothers, giving birth to low-birth-weight and undernourished infants and perpetuating a vicious cycle of malnutrition across generations17. Extensive research has examined son preference, its expression, and underlying drivers, particularly in East and South Asia9, Arab countries9,18 linked son preference in India, China, and Korea to similar patriarchal family systems that discourage investment in daughters, marginalizing women despite rising incomes, education, and urbanization19. Further demonstrated that cultural gender preferences and differences in perceived returns on investment result in unequal resource allocation among siblings in India. They found that child height disadvantage increases with birth order, reflecting favoritism toward eldest sons, and that this birth-order effect is stronger in regions and communities with high son preference.

Several studies have examined gender-based inequalities in child education, nutrition, and well-being (Borooah 200420), yet evidence linking son preference to nutritional disparities remains limited. In India, as in other patriarchal societies, sons are often valued as economic assets and symbols of family continuity, while daughters are viewed as financial burdens. Such norms drive unequal health and nutrition investments, with girls frequently facing disadvantages in breastfeeding, healthcare, and food allocation21,22. This gender bias not only affects girls’ immediate well-being but also perpetuates intergenerational cycles of malnutrition and poverty23–25.

In a society where cultural norms often prioritize sons, girls frequently receive less care, fewer resources, and inadequate attention10,26,27. This neglect deeply affects their growth and development, contributing to higher levels of undernutrition25–28. Despite extensive research on child malnutrition in India29,30, the specific influence of son preference on girls’ nutritional outcomes as regional level analysis remains understudied posing a critical challenge to achieving SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality). This study contributes to the literature by using the most recent NFHS-5 (2019–21) data to examine the association between son preference and girls’ undernutrition, and by providing region-specific estimates that uncover geographic heterogeneity often masked in national-level analyses. This study aims to investigate the influence of women’s son preference on the nutritional status of girls under five in India through a regional perspective. Previous studies indicate that the intensity of son preference varies regionally being higher in the North, Central, Eastern, and Western regions with stronger patriarchy, and lower in the South and Northeast31,32, where women’s empowerment and matrilineal systems promote more equitable resource allocation and better nutrition for girls. Using the latest National Family Health Survey (NFHS) data, this study examines key indicators of malnutrition within socio-cultural and economic contexts to reveal the hidden gender biases affecting child nutrition. The findings are expected to bridge a crucial research gap and offer evidence-based insights for policy actions promoting gender equity and improved nutritional outcomes among girls in India. Furthermore, policymakers and local government bodies should strengthen nutrition programs such as ICDS and Poshan Abhiyan by ensuring that resources are not uniformly distributed across all regions, but rather targeted toward areas with the most urgent needs and vulnerabilities.

Data and method

Data source

This study utilized data from the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), conducted in India during 2019–21. NFHS-5 is a large-scale, nationally representative household survey that provides comprehensive information on population health, nutrition, and family welfare indicators. The dataset includes anthropometric measurements, child feeding practices, and maternal health indicators, along with relevant socio-demographic variables. Importantly, it also contains information related to son preference, enabling an analysis of its potential association with child undernutrition. For this study, data from 112,255 female children aged 0–59 months were extracted, providing a robust sample for examining the relationship between son preference and nutritional outcomes among girl children in India.

Sample selection procedure

Figure 1 presents the sample selection process used in the study. The analysis initially considered 232,920 children aged 0–59 months from the NFHS-5 dataset. Children who were not alive at the time of the survey (N = 8,702) were excluded, resulting in a sample of 224,218 living children. Further, cases where mothers did not report their ideal number of children were excluded (N = 3,240), yielding 220,978 observations with valid fertility preference information. From this sample, male children were excluded (N = 120,665) to focus on female children, resulting in a final analytical sample of 112,255 female children. Within this final sample, 19,625 cases (16.69%) were classified as exhibiting son preference, defined as instances where the ideal number of sons exceeds daughters, while 92,630 cases (83.31%) were categorized as having no son preference.Fig. 1Flow diagram depicting the sample selection process.

Variable description

Outcome variable

The outcome variable for the nutritional status of children in India, as assessed in the NFHS data, encompasses three key indicators: stunting, wasting, and underweight. Stunting, which reflects chronic malnutrition and impaired linear growth, is measured using height-for-age Z-scores calculated against the WHO growth standards, with children classified as stunted if their Z-score is less than -2 standard deviations (SD). Wasting indicates acute malnutrition and is determined through weight-for-height Z-scores, with children classified as wasted under the same Z-score threshold. Underweight serves as a composite measure of both chronic and acute malnutrition, using weight-for-age Z-scores for classification. All measurements are conducted according to standardized anthropometric methods, utilizing WHO Multicenter Growth Reference Study standards (WHO, 200633;). Data collection focuses on children under five years of age, with Z-scores computed using WHO Anthro software and exclusions applied for implausible values. These indicators are critical for informing nutritional interventions in India, where NFHS-5 reports significant prevalence rates: 35.5% stunting, 19.3% wasting, and 32.1% underweight, highlighting the need for targeted policies addressing child nutrition in relation to socioeconomic factors and maternal health.

Predictor variable

Using NFHS-5, the son preference variable is categorized as a binary measure: Son Preference Yes or Son Preference No, based on women’s responses about their ideal family size and preferred gender composition of children. Women are first asked about their ideal number of children, followed by questions on how many of these children they would prefer to be boys, girls, or of no specific gender. Women expressing a preference for more boys than girls are classified under “Son Preference Yes,” while those preferring an equal number of boys and girls or more girls than boys fall under “Son Preference No.” This binary classification helps analyze the influence of son preference on fertility decisions, family planning practices, and gender biases, providing critical insights into societal attitudes toward gender and informing policies to address gender imbalances.

Further the other variables, are categorized into three main groups: child characteristics, women characteristics, and household characteristics. Child Characteristics include the child’s age in months, categorized into a continuous variable representing the average age, along with birth order classified into three categories: first order, second/third order, and fourth and above. Birth weight is also included as a continuous variable measured in kilograms. Women Characteristics encompass several variables: maternal age is categorized into three groups (15–24 years, 25–34 years, and 35 years and above), while education level is classified into four categories (no education, primary education, secondary education, and higher education). Access to mass media is represented as a binary variable (no access vs. any access). Maternal Body Mass Index (BMI) is categorized into three groups: normal weight, underweight, and overweight/obese. The number of antenatal care (ANC) visits is classified into two categories: three or fewer visits and more than three visits. Household Characteristics include the wealth index, which is divided into five categories ranging from the poorest to the richest. Social caste is classified into three groups: Scheduled Castes/Tribes (SC/ST), Other Backward Classes (OBC), and others. Religious affiliation is categorized into four groups: Hindu, Muslim, Christian, and others. Finally, residence is classified as either urban or rural while region is classified into six geographic zones: Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Southern, and North-Eastern, based on standard NFHS regional groupings (Table 1). Each of these variables provides essential context for understanding the demographic profile of the study sample and its potential influence on child nutritional outcomes. Statistical methods

To examine the association between son preference and undernutrition among girls (stunting, wasting, and underweight), Generalized Linear Models (GLMs) with a logit link function were employed, given the binary nature of the outcome variables. Separate models were estimated for each outcome. The dependent variables were defined as binary indicators, where children were classified as stunted, wasted, or underweight if their respective anthropometric Z-scores were below − 2 standard deviations from the WHO reference population, and 0 otherwise. The key explanatory variable was son preference, coded as 1 if the mother reported a higher ideal number of sons than daughters, and 0 otherwise.

The estimated coefficients were exponentiated and reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for ease of interpretation.

Four models were estimated sequentially: Model 0 (unadjusted), Model 1 (adjusted for child-level characteristics), Model 2 (adjusted for child and women characteristics), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for child, women, and household characteristics).

This analysis is based on observational cross-sectional data and estimates conditional associations rather than causal effects. The identification relies on the assumption that, conditional on the included child, maternal, and household covariates, there are no unobserved factors that simultaneously influence both son preference and child nutritional outcomes. All analyses incorporate NFHS-5 survey design features, including sampling weights, clustering (primary sampling units), and stratification, to obtain nationally representative estimates and robust standard errors. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs), and model fit was evaluated using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 17 .

At the household level, over half (57.9%) lived in families with 5–8 members, and 60.5% reported that distance to a health facility posed a problem. The vast majority of households were male-headed (84.8%) and resided in rural areas (73.4%). In terms of socioeconomic status, 46.6% of households belonged to the two poorest quintiles, while only 15.5% were in the richest. Caste distribution showed that 45.4% of respondents were OBC, 35.1% were SC/ST, and 19.5% were others. The majority of respondents were Hindu (79.2%), followed by Muslims (16.4%). Regionally, the largest shares of respondents were from the Central (28.1%) and Eastern (26.0%) regions, followed by the Southern (16.5%), Western (12.4%), Northern (13.2%), and Northeastern (3.7%) regions. These figures indicate a broad geographical representativeness across India. Overall, the sample profile reflects substantial rural and socioeconomic vulnerability, conditions that often reinforce nutritional inequities among girls.

Regional variation in gender bias in nutritional status

Figure 2 shows the regional variation in the nutritional status of girls under five, comparing those from son preference and no son preference households across India. A clear pattern emerges, showing higher levels of stunting, wasting, and underweight among girls from families expressing son preference in nearly all regions. At the national level, girls in son preference households exhibit higher rates of stunting (42.1%), wasting (19.6%), and underweight (37.7%), compared with their counterparts from no son preference households (33.2%, 18.2%, and 29.9%, respectively). The Eastern (45.5%) and Central (42.9%) regions show the greatest excess stunting among girls from son preference households, followed by the Western (38.8%) region. These regions, characterized by strong patriarchal norms and lower maternal empowerment, also show the highest burden of underweight (East: 45.1%; Central: 34.4%). In contrast, the Southern (34.3%) and Northeastern (35.4%) regions record lower stunting prevalence and minimal differences between son preference and no son preference families. The Northeast, in particular, demonstrates resilience against gender bias in nutrition—likely reflecting matrilineal traditions and higher women’s autonomy. Association between son preference and girls’ undernutrition

Figure 3 presents result from generalized linear models (GLMs) estimating the association between son preference and undernutrition among girls (stunting, wasting, and underweight) at the national and regional levels in India. Four models were fitted sequentially: Model 0 (unadjusted), Model 1 (adjusted for child-level factors), Model 2 (adjusted for child and maternal characteristics), and Model 3 (fully adjusted for child, maternal, and household characteristics). In the unadjusted models (Model 0), girls from son-preferring households exhibited significantly higher odds of stunting (OR = 1.21, p < 0.001), wasting (OR = 1.10, p < 0.001), and underweight (OR = 1.23, p < 0.001) compared with girls from households without son preference (Appendix Tables A5–A7). Adjustment for child, maternal, and household characteristics (Models 3) attenuated the associations, though most remained statistically significant. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), son preference persisted as a modest yet significant predictor of stunting (OR = 1.02), wasting (OR = 1.02), and underweight (OR = 1.02), indicating that gender bias continues to influence girls’ nutritional outcomes even after accounting for socioeconomic and demographic factors.Fig. 3Regional variations in the association between son preference and undernutrition among girls in India: A GLMs analysis.

Regional variations in the association between son preference and undernutrition among girls in India: A GLMs analysis.

The marked regional heterogeneity was observed in the association between son preference and girls’ nutritional outcomes across India (Appendix Tables A8–A25). In northern India, a consistent positive association was evident across all outcomes, with fully adjusted models showing higher odds of stunting (OR = 1.03), wasting (OR = 1.05), and underweight (OR = 1.08) among girls from son-preferring households. These effects remained significant even after adjusting for maternal education (OR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.80–0.88) and household wealth (OR = 0.70; 95% CI: 0.66–0.75), suggesting that gender bias operates beyond socioeconomic status in this region. In eastern India, the association remained positive for stunting (OR = 1.03) and underweight (OR = 1.04), with lower maternal education (OR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.78–0.86) and household poverty (OR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.63–0.72) further amplifying girls’ nutritional disadvantage. In central India, son preference continued to be significantly associated with both stunting (OR = 1.04) and underweight (OR = 1.06), while the compounding effects of low maternal education (OR = 0.83; 95% CI: 0.79–0.87) and limited household wealth (OR = 0.67; 95% CI: 0.62–0.71) reinforced these disparities. In contrast, western India exhibited weaker associations, with only marginal increases in underweight (OR = 1.02), whereas maternal education (OR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.76–0.85) and household wealth (OR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.65–0.74) emerged as stronger protective factors, suggesting that socioeconomic improvements may offset the influence of son preference.

In southern India, no positive association was observed in the fully adjusted models; odds ratios for stunting (OR = 0.97) and underweight (OR = 0.95) were near or below unity, reflecting greater gender parity facilitated by higher maternal education (OR = 0.76; 95% CI: 0.72–0.81) and more equitable wealth distribution (OR = 0.63; 95% CI: 0.59–0.68). Interestingly, in northeastern India, the direction of association was reversed in several models, with girls from son-preferring households showing lower odds of stunting and wasting (e.g., stunting OR < 1 in Model 3), consistent with stronger women’s autonomy, higher maternal education (OR = 0.79; 95% CI: 0.74–0.84), and more egalitarian gender norms. Collectively, these regional contrasts highlight that gender bias in child nutrition remains most pronounced in the north-central belt—where educational and wealth inequalities intersect with cultural son preference—but is substantially mitigated in southern and northeastern India, where social development and gender-equitable norms are more established.

Discussion

This study investigates the association between son preference and three major indicators of malnutrition stunting, wasting, and underweight among girls under five at both the national level and across six regions separately, using generalized linear regression models adjusted for demographic, maternal, and household characteristics. Nationally, son preference was associated with higher odds of all three forms of undernutrition, though the effect attenuated after adjustment. Regionally, the association was strongest in the Central and Eastern regions, where girls from son-preferring households faced significantly elevated risks of stunting and underweight, reflecting entrenched patriarchal norms and limited female empowerment. In contrast, the Southern and Northeastern regions showed negligible or even reversed associations, with girls in son-preferring households sometimes faring better likely due to higher maternal autonomy, better education, and more gender-equitable social structures. The Northern and Western regions exhibited modest but persistent gender-based nutritional disadvantages linked to son preference, underscoring the interplay of cultural norms and socioeconomic conditions.

In India, girls exhibit lower rates of exclusive breastfeeding, immunisation, and healthcare utilisation in comparison to boys have been well documented34–36. However, these inequalities vary across regions. In the Western, Central, Northern, and Eastern states like Rajasthan, Haryana, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Maharashtra, Chittorgarh, and Madhya Pradesh, a strong preference for sons and high-risk fertility behaviour helped that girls get less food, healthcare, and education than boys. This makes them more likely to be malnourished37,38. Many families eat mostly vegetarian meals not properly planned that mostly consist of cereals and legumes with only a few fruits, vegetables, and dairy. This can lead to a lack of iron, zinc, vitamin A, and protein. These nutritional deficiencies hinder growth, cognitive development, and immune function, rendering girls more vulnerable to infections and the use of Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) is relatively limited, Poverty and income inequality make the problem worse by making it harder to get healthy food and medical care. A lack of clean water and sanitation makes the risk of disease even higher, which makes malnutrition worse39–41. Conversely, in the Southern and Northeastern regions, Girls have the same access to food, health care, and education as boys. Non-vegetarian diets are common in this area and provide important nutrients like protein, iron, vitamin B12, and zinc. These nutrients help people grow better and mitigate the risk of anemia-related problems. Eating meat, fish, and eggs on a regular basis helps promote mental and physical development. Also, having access to a variety of foods, such as fruits, vegetables, dairy, and animal products, prevents vitamin and mineral deficiencies. The region’s fertile land and plenty of rain make sure that there is always fresh, healthy food available that is good for your health26,27,42–44.

Despite the challenges, girls have exhibited more rapid nutritional advancements in recent years14. Biologically, boys are more susceptible to infections and necessitate greater energy for recovery, thereby elevating their risk of undernutrition45,46. The increased incidence of diarrhoea and respiratory infections in boys corroborates this trend, whereas girls’ superior immunity and adaptability in precarious environments alleviate their nutritional deficit47,48. In North, Central, and East regions with strong son preference, cultural bias overrides biological resilience, exacerbating girls’ nutritional vulnerability. The effect size is still small, but reducing son preference could significantly reduce the burden of undernutrition among millions of girl’s children.

Maternal education was identified as a significant protective factor for all forms of malnutrition, including stunting, wasting, and underweight. Mothers who have had more schooling are more likely to know more about health, hygiene, and nutrition, which directly affects how they feed and care for their children49–51). Achieving a higher level of education increases women’s capacity to access, interpret, and utilise health and nutrition information, resulting in enhanced complementary feeding and overall child nutrition52,53. Furthermore, it strengthens problem-solving and decision-making skills, enabling mothers to respond effectively to their children’s nutritional needs and seek timely healthcare54–56. Regionally, the protective effect of maternal education is strongest in Southern and Northeastern India. In the South, higher-educated mothers had markedly lower odds of stunting (ARRs ≈ 0.85) and underweight (ARRs ≈ 0.69), reflecting high female literacy, greater autonomy, and gender-equitable norms that enhance the benefits of education28,57,58. Similarly, in the Northeast, higher maternal education was linked to reduced underweight (ARRs ≈ 0.65) and stunting (ARRs ≈ 0.85), supported by matrilineal traditions, higher female schooling, and progressive social structures that empower mothers to act on their knowledge. In contrast, the effect is weaker in Central and Eastern India, where patriarchal norms, limited female agency, and poverty constrain educated mothers’ ability to improve child nutrition28,Borooah 2004) . In Central India, secondary education offers only modest protection (ARRs ≈ 0.95), while in Eastern India, the gains from higher education are offset by gender and structural barriers. Even educated women in these regions may lack the power or resources to prioritize daughters’ nutrition, highlighting how entrenched inequality can blunt the protective potential of maternal education.

Household wealth emerged as a robust protective factor against stunting, wasting, and underweight among girls under five across India, with national fully adjusted odds ratios (ARRs) of 0.54, 0.82, and 0.38, respectively, for the richest versus poorest quintile. Income directly affects a family’s ability to obtain sufficient food, healthcare, and sanitary hygienic conditions57,59,60. Families with greater resources can buy healthy foods, like animal-source foods that are high in protein and micronutrients that are important for a child’s growth and development61,62. Conversely, children from low-income households are more likely to experience undernutrition due to limited dietary diversity, inadequate access to health services, and poor housing, lack of clean water, and poor sanitation increase susceptibility to infections, hinder child growth63–65. This protective effect varied systematically by region. In Central and Eastern India areas marked by high poverty and entrenched son preference wealth exerted the strongest buffering influence: in Central India, the richest girls had 50% lower odds of stunting and 57% lower odds of underweight,in the East, corresponding reductions were 48% and 55%. These findings suggest that economic resources can partially mitigate gender-based nutritional neglect in high-risk settings. In contrast, the wealth–nutrition gradient was flatter in Southern and Northeastern India, where better baseline nutrition, gender-equitable norms, and stronger public health systems diminish the marginal returns to income. Notably, in the Northeast, even middle-wealth households showed strong protection against underweight (ARRs ≈ 0.52, likely due to synergies between income, dietary diversity (including animal-source foods, and women’s autonomy. In Northern and Western India, wealth also significantly reduced undernutrition risks e.g., 15% lower stunting odds in the North highlighting economic empowerment as a viable pathway to counteract son preference where cultural shifts lag.

Maternal nutritional status emerged as a powerful determinant of girls’ undernutrition across all three indicators. Nationally, underweight mothers increased the odds of stunting by 9% and wasting by 14%, while overweight or obese mothers conferred significant protection—reducing stunting risk by 7% and underweight risk by 62%. These patterns reflect the intergenerational transmission of malnutrition: undernourished women are more likely to experience intrauterine growth restriction, anemia, and inadequate gestational weight gain, leading to low birth weight and depleted nutrient reserves in offspring66,67. Consistent with this, girls born with low birth weight faced markedly elevated risks nationally, they had 11% higher odds of stunting and 17% higher odds of underweight, echoing findings from Maharashtra where low birth weight was associated greater stunting risk68. The influence of birth order further compounded these vulnerabilities. Later-born girls (third or higher) exhibited significantly higher odds of stunting (ARRs = 1.14) and underweight (ARRs = 1.11) nationally, a pattern most pronounced in the Central, Eastern, and Northern regions where son preference and resource dilution intersect. In Central India, fourth-or-higher-order girls had 13% higher odds of stunting and 7% higher odds of underweight, consistent with evidence that children following same-sex siblings particularly girls after girls receive fewer resources in son-preferring households69,70. In India, this birth-order penalty was minimal or absent in the South and Northeast, where gender-equitable norms and stronger maternal autonomy buffer against intra-household discrimination. Together, these findings underscore that maternal undernutrition, low birth weight, and high birth order do not operate in isolation but are amplified in regions where patriarchal norms constrain investment in daughters57,59,60.

Overall, this study demonstrates that son preference is associated with modest but meaningful disadvantages in girls’ nutritional outcomes in India, with substantial regional heterogeneity. By employing region-specific models, the findings reveal that Central and Eastern India bear a disproportionately higher burden, where entrenched patriarchal norms, lower maternal education, and economic deprivation amplify gender bias in child nutrition, while Southern and Northeastern regions show negligible or even protective patterns reflecting greater female autonomy and more equitable social structures. Although maternal education, household wealth, and birth order emerge as important protective or risk factors, their effects vary considerably across regions, underscoring the context-specific nature of these associations. These results highlight the need for moving beyond uniform national strategies toward regionally targeted, gender-responsive interventions within existing programs such as ICDS and Poshan Abhiyan, particularly in high-burden regions. At the same time, successful practices from more gender-equitable regions offer valuable lessons for scaling context-adapted solutions. However, given the cross-sectional design, reliance on self-reported measures, and potential unmeasured confounding, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Future research should further examine intra-household dynamics, including paternal attitudes and decision-making processes, using mixed-method approaches to better understand the pathways linking son preference to child nutrition. Addressing both structural inequalities and deeply rooted social norms will be essential to break the intergenerational cycle of gendered malnutrition in India.

This study has several limitations that should be considered while interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design of NFHS-5 limits the ability to draw causal inferences, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as causal relationships. Second, the measurement of son preference is based on women’s self-reported ideal family composition, which may be subject to social desirability bias and may not fully capture the intensity or behavioural expression of gender bias within households. Third, although anthropometric indicators are standardized, potential measurement errors and the exclusion of children with missing or implausible values may introduce selection bias. Fourth, despite adjusting for a wide range of covariates, unmeasured factors such as paternal attitudes, intra-household food allocation, and cultural practices may confound the observed associations. Finally, regional estimates, particularly for the Northeastern region, are based on relatively smaller sample sizes, which may limit statistical precision and comparability. In addition, the analysis relies on identification assumptions that, conditional on observed covariates, no unmeasured factors jointly influence both son preference and child nutritional outcomes; violations of this assumption may bias the estimates. Furthermore, the absence of finer geographic controls and the inability to directly account for community-level norms or intra-household dynamics may limit the precision of the estimated associations.
