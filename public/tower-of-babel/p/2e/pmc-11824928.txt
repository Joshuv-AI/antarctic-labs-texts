Primary Peritoneal Serous Carcinoma with Coexistent Mucinous Cystadenoma of Ovary – A Rare Case Report
Authors: Rajashree Pradhan, Sajeeb Mondal, Upasana Mukherjee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimary peritoneal serous carcinoma (PPSC) is an extremely rare tumor mostly idiopathic in nature arising from the peritoneal layers of the abdominal cavity. We present here a unique case of a 72-year-old female with a history of abdominal swelling and difficulty in micturition and defecation for 3 months. Diagnostic imaging and histopathological examinations led to the diagnosis of PPSC. Simultaneously, the patient also has a space-occupying lesion involving the left ovary which on histopathological examination was found to be mucinous cystadenoma. We present this case as PPSC with synchronous mucinous cystadenoma of the left ovary, an extremely rare phenomenon. This combination has not been reported in the literature yet to the best of our knowledge.

INTRODUCTION

Peritoneal malignancies represent a heterogeneous group of neoplasms with different characteristics in terms of incidence, diagnosis, treatment options, and prognosis.[1] Primary peritoneal serous carcinoma (PPSC) is an extremely rare tumor involving the peritoneum with an incidence of 7 in 1 million cases each year.[2] It usually occurs in elderly and postmenopausal females. The patients usually present with abdominal ascites (63.6%) distension (59.1%), abdominal pain (19%), a sense of fullness, nausea, emesis, constipation, increased urinary frequency, dyspnea, fatigue, and weight loss.[3] This tumor resembles ovarian tumor in many ways making it difficult to diagnose clinically. Since it is a rapidly progressive tumor with an adverse prognosis, early diagnosis is absolutely essential for better patient management. To aid in the diagnosis, along with the routine radiological imaging and ascitic fluid cytology, tumor markers and immunohistochemistry are also warranted. We report a case of PPSC with synchronous mucinous cystadenoma of the left ovary. To the best of our knowledge, this combination has not been reported so far. This case could provide a new paradigm for primary peritoneal tumors associated with mucinous cystadenoma of the ovary. Through this case, we would also like to point out the importance of careful analysis and interpretation of test results in establishing a definitive diagnosis, especially when there are confounding factors.

CASE REPORT

A 72-year-old female attended the outpatient department with a history of abdominal swelling and difficulty in micturition and defecation for 3 months. Ultrasonography showed a cystic space-occupying lesion in the left adnexa. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the whole abdomen showed the presence of a cystic density lesion arising from the left adnexa along with abnormally thickened greater omentum with increased soft tissue density (omental cake). There was also the presence of contiguous loculated fluid collection noted in the left paracolic region. The left ovary was not seen separately. Ascites was noted. Tumor marker study revealed raised cancer Antigen-125 (CA-125) (105.45U/mL), serum lactate dehydrogenase (298.5U/L), carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) (6.01 ng/mL), and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) (4.92 ng/mL). Human chorionic gonadotropin, inhibin A, and inhibin B were within the normal limits. Ascitic fluid cytology showed positive for malignancy. The patient then underwent a total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, and left internal iliac lymph node dissection [Figure 1]. Omentectomy was infracolic. On surgery, there were no palpable regional lymph nodes except the left internal iliac lymph node; complete bilateral pelvic and paraaortic lymphadenectomy were not done. Thorough grossing was done according to the College of American Pathologists protocol. Grossing of the omentum revealed multiple whitish nodules. The left ovary showed a multilocular cystic cavity filled with mucinous fluid. Microscopic features showed serous carcinoma of the peritoneum (high grade) [Figure 2a and b]. The left ovary showed features of mucinous cystadenoma [Figure 2c]. The right ovary, bilateral fallopian tubes, and iliac lymph nodes were free from tumor. After careful analysis and correlation, the diagnosis of primary peritoneal carcinoma (papillary serous type) (PPSC) was made. The pathologic stage was given as pT3N0Mx. Additional findings included mucinous cystadenoma of the left ovary which has made this case a unique one. The patient was further advised for cytogenetic study. Considering the age of the patient, second surgery was not done because according to the standard protocol, cytoreductive surgery was already done (i.e., hysterectomy with bilateral salpingectomy and omentectomy).

DISCUSSION

Primary peritoneal tumors are one of the rarest malignancies with an incidence rate of 7 in 1 million per year.[2] This tumor is of Mullerian origin arising from the peritoneal lining and mostly involves the omentum.[3] The exact pathogenesis is unknown, but it can be caused by germline mutations in the BRCA1 gene which has been reported in 17.6% of the cases.[2] A diagnosis of primary peritoneal carcinoma is based on the Gynecologic Oncology Group criteria: (1) both ovaries are normal in size or enlarged by a benign process; (2) extraovarian involvement is greater than ovarian involvement; (3) there should be no malignancy in either ovaries or the tumor in serosa, and cortex of size <5 mm ×5 mm can be present; and (4) the tumor is of a predominantly serous type.[2] All the criteria were fulfilled in our case; as the omentum was involved by serous carcinoma, the left ovary was involved by mucinous cystadenoma (benign process), histology was of papillary serous type, and bilateral ovaries were free of tumor. The differential diagnosis includes peritoneal metastasis from different primary sites (ovaries and gastrointestinal tract [GIT]), peritoneal tuberculosis, and peritoneal lymphomatosis. General presenting symptoms include ascites (63.6%), abdominal distension (59.1%), abdominal pain (19%), a sense of fullness, nausea, emesis, constipation, increased urinary frequency, dyspnea, fatigue, and weight loss[1] similar to that of ovarian carcinoma. Pelvic peritoneum has the potential to differentiate into Müllerian-type epithelium,[2] leading to a diagnostic challenge. Microscopically, both PPSC and ovarian carcinoma show papillary structures lined by several layers columnar to oval cells with enlarged nuclei and high nuclear–cytoplasmic ratio. Numerous mitoses were seen. Psammoma bodies were present. In our case along with PPSC, the left ovary showed mucinous cystadenoma. No evidence of papillary serous tumor was seen in the uterus, fallopian tube, and ovaries.

Tumor markers play an important role in diagnostic workup including CA-125 which is usually elevated creating a diagnostic challenge keeping ovarian cancer as part of the differential. In our case, it was elevated at 105.45U/ml. Cancer antigen 19-9 (CA 19-9) (mucinous marker) was not elevated in our case. Although CA 19-9 is the most useful marker for ovarian mucinous neoplasm, with respect to benign tumor, i.e., mucinous cystadenoma, CA 19-9 is elevated only in 27% of cases.[4] Other important tumor markers are CEA and AFP. There is lack of literature in this regard, and hence, it is considered an unproven and unhelpful test due to its low sensitivity. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) can be used to differentiate among the tumors, grading, and providing appropriate treatment. The most commonly used stains in peritoneal cancer include calretinin, cytokeratin, and BerEP4. Calretinin- and BerEP4-positive cells indicate their epithelial origin and are used for the diagnosis of PPSC. The metastatic adenocarcinomas show no nuclear staining for calretinin and cytokeratins. The tumors from the upper GIT are positive for CK-7 and show variable results for CDX2/CK20, whereas tumors from lower GIT are negative for CK-7 and positive for CDX2/CK20. This increases the chances of detection of tumor and its pathological subtype from ascites, especially in ambiguous cases. However, a definite role remains unclear, and therefore, it is just used as an adjunct.[2] CT scan is considered the primary diagnostic modality and also helps in guiding the interventional radiologists for conducting biopsies and surgeons for performing cytoreductive surgeries. In the past few years, new treatments and multimodal management strategies helped to improve patient survival rate and quality of life, and the premise that peritoneal malignancies are fatal diseases has been dismissed as management strategies, including complete cytoreductive surgery embedded in perioperative systemic chemotherapy, can provide cure in selected patients. Furthermore, intraperitoneal chemotherapy has become an important part of combination treatments.[1] Rarely, radiotherapy is the treatment of choice. The prognosis of this tumor depends on the positivity of tumor markers, primary debulking surgery, stage at presentation, and functional status. It is considered as a terminal illness and has a survival rate varying from 11 to 17 months.[2]

CONCLUSION

PPSC is a rare and aggressive tumor with a female predominance which often leads to a diagnostic challenge and dilemma due to its similarity with ovarian cancer in various aspects. Imaging studies, ascitic fluid cytology, tumor markers, IHC, and cytogenetics are imperative to differentiate them. Therefore, meticulous analysis and interpretation of the study parameters are necessary to aid in timely diagnosis and expeditious therapeutics. A competitive and cooperative team consisting of nurses, radiologists, surgeons, oncologists, and psychiatrists in addition to the advanced therapeutic modalities is essential in increasing the survival rate and improving the quality of life.
