Cardiovascular Health of Single Mothers: A Systematic Review
Authors: Jaclyn D Borrowman, Lauren M Pageau, Natalie A Cameron, Mercedes R Carnethon, Nicole D Fields
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial drivers of health shape cardiovascular health (CVH), with single mothers facing considerable social adversity. This review aimed to describe single mothers’ CVH and the potential role of social drivers of health.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses guidelines, we included observational studies published in English that assessed at least 1 American Heart Association–defined CVH factor or behavior among parous single mothers (aged >18 years). Searches were conducted in 3 electronic databases, with 2 reviewers independently screening. Study quality and risk of bias were assessed using the Appraisal Tool for Cross‐Sectional Studies and the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Exposures.ResultsOf 2071 search results, 1704 articles were screened, and 36 studies met inclusion criteria. Most studies were cross‐sectional (n=33), with diet and nicotine use the most assessed outcomes (n=26), followed by physical activity (n=9), body mass index (n=7), sleep (n=6), blood pressure (n=4), blood glucose (n=3), and blood lipids (n=2). Overall, findings suggest that single mothers have worse CVH behaviors and factors compared with partnered mothers. Social drivers of health influences were rarely examined with individual‐level factors (eg, income) most common (n=7) and structural‐level factors (eg, access to food outlets) were least (n=1).ConclusionsThe present review suggests that single mothers have worse CVH compared with partnered mothers. More research is needed to identify potential drivers of poor CVH among single mothers to inform interventions targeting specific health behaviors or factors to decrease cardiovascular disease risk. Critically, future studies should examine structural‐level social drivers of health through an intersectional lens to advance health equity for single mothers.Keywords: American Heart Association, cardiovascular health, mothers, women’s healthSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Women

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and death globally, with growing recognition of the critical role social drivers of health (SDoHs) play in shaping cardiovascular health (CVH). 1 The Life’s Essential 8 framework describes 8 key health behaviors and factors associated with CVH, including physical activity, diet, sleep, nicotine use, body mass index, blood pressure, blood glucose, and blood lipids. 2 Beyond these factors, Life’s Essential 8 further acknowledges the influence of SDoHs, describing the individual (eg, income), community (eg, social cohesion), and structural factors (eg, built environment), which drive disparities in CVH outcomes. 2 Given the pervasiveness of health disparities, research that accounts for the multilevel SDoHs influencing health of vulnerable populations is imperative to achieving CVH equity.

One population that experiences a unique set of multileveled influences on CVH are parents. Currently, most literature pertaining to the CVH of parents has centered on pregnancy, attributing to a lack of understanding of the long‐term and multifaceted effects motherhood has on CVH beyond delivery. 3 , 4 Evidence indicates the effects pregnancy has on CVH are shaped by family structure, including marital/partnered status. Moreover, single mothers, compared with partnered mothers, have worse overall CVH. 5 Mechanisms potentially attributing to this observation include the considerable social adversity single mothers face, including limited access to stable employment, income, and educational opportunities, which in turn may increase stress and psychological distress. 6 These challenges directly and indirectly translate to reduced access to health care, healthy foods, safe environments for physical activity, and consistent social support (ie, SDoHs); each of which informs CVH. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 With an estimated 101.3 million single mothers worldwide, this population represents a high‐risk group for CVD in need of further research to promote their CVH. 11 , 12

Although prior research has reported that overall CVH is worse among single mothers, it is unclear which CVH behaviors or factors may be driving this observation. 5 By systematically examining the existing literature, we sought to describe the prevalence of CVH behaviors (physical activity, diet, nicotine exposure, and sleep) and factors (body mass index, blood pressure, blood glucose, blood lipids) among single mothers, how these prevalences compared with partnered mothers, and the potential role SDoHs play in these associations. This review also directly addresses calls by organizations, including the American Heart Association, to expand research that addresses disparities in both maternal health and CVH. 2 Understanding the current landscape of CVH among single mothers is a crucial first step toward informing future research and developing public health strategies, clinical interventions, and policy initiatives that equitably promote CVH.

Methods

Data Collection and Search Strategy

This systematic review was conducted and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses statement with the protocol published in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews. 13 , 14 In consultation with a research librarian, we searched for all relevant studies published on or before December 31, 2024, in PubMed, Scopus, and PsychINFO electronic databases. The search strategy included variations of the following keywords relating to single motherhood and CVH behaviors and factors: single mother, cardiovascular disease, cardiovascular health, diet, physical activity, sleep, nicotine use, blood pressure, blood glucose, body mass index, and cholesterol. Complete search strategies can be found in Table S1. Records were imported into Rayyan, an online platform designed for systematic review data organization, screening, and extraction. 15 Institutional review Board (IRB) approval was waived for the present study, and all supporting data are available within the article and its online supplementary files.

Study Eligibility Criteria

Articles were deemed eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) observational design, (2) sample of parous single mothers aged ≥18 years at the time of study enrollment, (3) ≥1 CVH factors or behaviors measured as defined by Life’s Essential 8 (ie, physical activity, diet, sleep, nicotine use, body mass index, blood pressure, blood glucose, and cholesterol), 2 and (4) published in English. Duplicate publications, incomplete records (eg, conference proceedings), and interventional studies were excluded from the review. The search was not restricted to geographic location, country of study, or date of publication.

Study Screening

Title and abstracts were independently screened by the first author (J.D.B.) and a secondary reviewer (L.M.P.). Any discrepancies were discussed between reviewers until consensus was reached. Full‐text publications were also screened independently by both reviewers, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion. Articles included in the review had information regarding the first author, year of publication, country where the study was conducted, study design, sample size, and participant characteristics were extracted.

Risk of Bias and Study Quality Assessment

The Appraisal Tool for Cross‐Sectional Studies (AXIS) was used to assess the quality of cross‐sectional studies. 16 The AXIS tool consists of 20 components to assess study design, reporting quality, and the risk of bias in cross‐sectional studies. 16 Longitudinal studies were assessed for risk of bias using the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Exposures tool. 17 The Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Exposures tool consists of 7 domains to determine whether there is low, some, or high concern regarding unmeasured confounding, measurement of the exposure or outcome, study population, external influences, missing data, or reported results, which combined, allows for the determination of overall risk of bias. 17

Results

The initial search yielded 2071 records, 367 of which were duplicates and removed before screening. The remaining 1704 abstracts were screened for relevance, leading to the exclusion of 1589 records. The remaining 115 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. After full‐text screening, 36 articles met inclusion criteria and were included in the present review. The Table 1 reports the details of the 36 included articles. Reasons for exclusion included duplications (n=367), populations outside the scope of this review (eg, single fathers; n=60), nonobservational study designs (eg, experimental; n=11), or publication in a language other than English (n=8). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses diagram in the Figure 1 further details the identification, screening, and inclusion of study articles for this review. Significant variability was observed in the quality of studies included, with most classified as either having some or high concern of bias (Tables S2 and S3).

Of the included articles, 9 assessed physical activity, 13 assessed diet, 13 assessed nicotine use, 6 assessed sleep, 7 assessed body mass index, 4 assessed blood pressure, 3 assessed blood glucose, and 2 assessed blood lipids among single mothers. Most studies included were cross‐sectional in design (n=33), with only 3 articles using a longitudinal cohort study design. A total of 14 studies were based in the United States, 5 in Canada, 2 in both Australia and Israel, and a single study was included from each of the following: Chile, Colombia, Finland, Germany, Ireland, Japan, Sweden, Taiwan, the Philippines, and the United Kingdom.

CVH Health Behaviors

Physical Activity

The most common method of physical activity assessment was self‐report (n=7) followed by accelerometry (n=2). Countries where physical activity was assessed among single mothers included Canada, Japan, Taiwan, and the United States. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 50 Prevalence of inactivity among single mothers ranged from 39% to 54%, with 1 study reporting single mothers achieved on average 20.2 minutes of moderate‐ to vigorous‐intensity (MVPA) physical activity per day as measured by a waist‐worn accelerometer, equating to less than the physical activity guidelines, which recommend achievement of at least 150 minutes of MVPA per week. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 23 , 25 , 51 Similarly, another study with waist‐worn accelerometry confirmed this finding, estimating that only 32% of single mothers met physical activity guidelines. 19 When comparing the physical activity of single mothers with partnered mothers, single mothers were at greater odds of self‐reported physical inactivity (odds ratio [OR], 3.04 [95% CI, 1.39–6.66]) and lower odds of being physically active (OR, 0.77 [95% CI, 0.76–0.77]) over the past month. 18 , 23 , 25

Diet

All assessment methods were self‐reported, with food frequency questionnaires and diet recalls the most common tools used for data collection (n=13). Countries where diet was assessed among single mothers included Canada, Sweden, the United States, Ireland, the Philippines, Colombia, and Chile. 22 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 38 , 52 Studies indicated that diet quality was poor among single mothers, with consumption of saturated fat typically above the recommended levels and adequacy of nutritional intake consistently poorer than their children. 28 , 32 Single mothers were also estimated to consume 0.33 cups more sugar‐sweetened beverages (SE, 0.16; P<0.01), with 1 study reporting that single mothers consumed up to 6 times more carbonated beverages than their children (P<0.0001). 31 , 36 Compared with partnered mothers, single mothers reported fewer days of fruit consumption (4.7±2.2 days versus 5.5±2.0 days), vegetable consumption (5.6±1.9 days versus 6.0±1.6 days), and lower protein intake (55 versus 70 g) per week. 27 , 53

Nicotine Use

All studies used self‐reported methods for the assessment of nicotine use (n=13). Countries where nicotine exposure was assessed included the United States, the United Kingdom, Japan, Finland, Australia, Germany, and Canada. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 28 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 54 Prevalence of current smoking status among single mothers ranged from 26% to 58%. 23 , 24 , 25 , 39 , 42 , 43 , 44 In studies comparing smoking habits of single mothers and partnered mothers, all reported single mothers to be at increased odds of smoking (OR range, 1.90–2.99) 23 , 24 , 25 , 39 , 42 , 43 , 44 Further, odds of heavy smoking, defined as consuming >19 cigarettes per day, was also observed to be higher among single mothers compared with partnered mothers (OR, 2.87 [95% CI, 2.16–3.81]). 55

Sleep

A total of 4 studies used self‐reported methods for sleep assessment, and 2 studies used wrist‐worn actigraphy and sleep diaries. Data reported on single mothers’ sleep were from the United States, Israel, and Japan. 22 , 39 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 56 Various dimensions of sleep, including sleep duration, prevalence of sleep problems, sleep quality, and perceived sleep quality, were represented in results reported. 23 , 46 , 47 , 48 Single mothers were observed to be at greater odds of sleep problems compared with partnered mothers (OR, 1.83 [95% CI, 1.18–2.83]), with a lower prevalence of single mothers achieving 7 hours of sleep duration (20% versus 29%), more reporting difficulty staying asleep (28% versus 21%), and not feeling well rested (52% versus 47%). 23 , 46 In the 2 longitudinal studies assessing sleep among single mothers, wrist‐worn actigraphy reflected findings reported in cross‐sectional studies that single mothers had significantly lower sleep duration and more night waking compared with partnered mothers. 46 , 55

CVH Health Factors

Body Mass Index

A total of 4 studies measured height and weight by a trained research staff member, and 3 used self‐reported height and weight to determine participants’ body mass index. Countries in results reported included the United States, Sweden, the Philippines, Chile, and Canada.* Findings regarding differences in body mass index among single mothers, compared with partnered mothers, were mixed. Prevalence of overweight and obesity among single mothers ranged from 29% to 41%. Only 1 study identified significant differences in body mass index, where single mothers were at greater odds for overweight (OR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.34–1.35]) and obesity (OR, 3.48 [95% CI, 3.46–3.50]) compared with partnered mothers. 25

Blood Pressure

Two studies measured blood pressure with a trained research staff member, and 2 others used self‐reported hypertension. All studies conducted were in the United States. 25 , 28 , 49 , 57 A study done by Drake et al estimated the prevalence of hypertension among single mothers to be ≈4.2%. 28 When compared with partnered mothers, single mothers were observed to be at greater odds of reporting hypertension (OR, 2.40 [95% CI, 2.39–2.41]). 25 Only 1 study assessed blood pressure longitudinally, finding that Black single mothers had the highest blood pressure compared with Black and White partnered mothers (systolic blood pressure +2.7 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressure +0.7 mm Hg). 57

Blood Glucose

Three studies assessed the association of single motherhood and blood glucose. All studies conducted were in the United States and assessed blood glucose only via participants’ self‐reported diabetes status and not by fasting blood glucose or hemoglobin A1c levels. 21 , 25 , 28 The prevalence of diabetes was estimated to be 6.3% with differences observed by physical activity level, with inactive single mothers having the greatest prevalence of diabetes (20.5%). 21 , 28 Single mothers were observed to be at higher odds of having diabetes compared with partnered mothers (OR, 1.67 [95% CI, 1.67–1.68]). 24

Blood Lipids

Only 2 studies assessed blood lipids among single mothers and were conducted in the United States. 25 , 28 In these, self‐reported use of cholesterol‐lowering medication and a concentration of total cholesterol from venous blood draws were used. Single mothers were observed to be at greater odds of having high cholesterol compared with partnered mothers (OR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.21–1.22]), with the greatest prevalence of cholesterol levels >200 mg/dL observed in single mothers aged 25 to 49 years (34%) followed by those aged 19 to 24 years (31%). 24 , 28

SDoHs and CVH of Single Mothers

Only 15 of the 36 studies included results that assessed the potential role SDoHs had on CVH behaviors (n=12) and factors (n=3) among single mothers. These studies looked at SDoHs at either the individual, community, or structural level.

Individual‐Level Factors

A total of 7 studies highlighted the influence of individual‐level factors on CVH among single mothers. For physical activity, the main barriers to being active among single mothers in the United States included low self‐efficacy, low outcome expectations, high perceived barriers to activity, and lack of ability to set goals and plan opportunities for physical activity. 20 Odds of nicotine use among Canadian single mothers, compared with partnered mothers, was greater even after stratification by low (OR, 2.99 [95% CI, 1.51–5.90]) and mid‐ to high‐income levels (OR, 3.01 [95% CI, 1.93–4.70]). 24 However, prevalence of overweight and obesity did not differ between Canadian single mothers and partnered mothers when stratified by income. 24 Finally, emotional distress was identified as an important individual‐level factor influencing sleep and blood pressure, with greater distress associated with poorer sleep quality and higher blood pressure among single mothers in the United States. 46 , 57

Community‐Level Factors

Diet was the most common CVH factor observed to be influenced by community‐level factors (n=7), with food insecurity estimated to affect ≈32% to 78% of single mothers in Canada and Colombia, 22 , 27 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 36 subsequently attributing to single mothers having greater odds of experiencing hunger compared with partnered mothers (OR, 1.21 [95% CI, 1.07–1.37]). 33 Food insecurity was also linked to having a compromised diet in Canada and more severe forms of hunger in Colombia, with 27% of single mothers reporting having a compromised diet because of food insecurity and 16% experiencing severe hunger. 26 , 33

Structural‐Level Factors

Only a single study assessed structural‐level influences on CVH among single mothers. Zhou et al assessed fast‐food consumption in Chicago, Illinois, in the United States by incorporating the local community configuration, transportation preferences, and socioeconomic variables. 38 These methods allowed for researchers to both understand the areas served by fast‐food outlets but also how residents’ socioeconomic level related to the number of weekly fast‐food visits. Results indicated that the density of fast‐food locations were highest in areas with a high percentage of single‐mother households, which was positively related to the number of fast‐food visits. 38

Discussion

Findings across studies suggest that exposure to single motherhood is associated with worse CVH, with 15 studies highlighting the potential role SDoHs play in these associations. Overall, CVH behaviors and risk factors were not studied equally. The greatest preponderance of data assessing CVH among single mothers was observed for diet and nicotine use. A smaller set of studies suggested that other health behaviors including physical activity were also worse among single mothers. Limited research on the association between single motherhood on health factors as well as the multileveled influences of SDoHs on CVH were observed.

The present review found that single mothers have worse CVH compared with partnered mothers. There are several hypothesized mechanisms underlying the observation of worse health among single mothers, with SDoHs suspected to be a major contributor to these observations. As recognized by Life’s Essential 8, socioeconomic and structural determinants affect an individual’s ability to optimize their CVH. 2 Single mothers face immense socioeconomic adversity, with more than two thirds living 200% below the poverty line, 17% achieving less than a high school education, 15% currently unemployed, 50% reporting food insecurity in the past year, and 10% without health insurance. 58 , 59 , 60 As described in the framework of SDoHs published by Powell‐Wiley and colleagues, the sociopolitical and economic context that individuals experience defines their access to health care, education, socioeconomic status, or neighborhood environments that support CVH and overall well‐being. 1 SDoHs contribute to disparities through influencing health at multiple levels of the socioecological model. For example, high chronic psychosocial stress due to financial burdens (individual level), poor social health due to social isolation or loneliness (interpersonal level), and residing in a socially vulnerable neighborhood (structural level) have all been independently associated with CVD risk factor development and disease incidence. 61 , 62 However, in our review, less than half of included studies comprehensively assess the role of SDoHs in single mothers’ CVH, highlighting a critical research gap in this population.

Further, research using an intersectional lens is lacking. 63 Intersectionality, a term coined by Kimberlé Crenshaw, describes how the intersection of multiple oppressed identities contributes to disparities beyond those associated with a single oppressed identity. 64 Intersectionality and CVH have been described in the literature previously, where Black women are observed to be at higher risk of CVD compared with White men and women due to the compounding effects of racism and sexism Black women experience. 65 , 66 , 67 Black women who are single mothers, consequently, may experience even more disparities regarding their CVH. However, only 2 studies included in the present review disaggregated data by race to assess CVH among single mothers. 24 , 39 , 57 Given that Black women are also more likely to be single mothers, 50 , 68 intersectional research that contextualizes the lived experiences of individuals is needed to effectively and equitably promote CVH.

Recommendations for Researchers, Clinicians, and Policymakers

In light of the findings from this review, several coordinated actions are recommended to support and improve the CVH of single mothers. Recommendations provided are for researchers, clinicians, and policymakers who may influence the health of this population.

For researchers, future studies should use rigorous and validated measures for CVH behaviors (eg, research‐grade accelerometry for physical activity assessment) and collect longitudinal data on CVH factors. Studies should also account for the diverse pathways into single motherhood (eg, divorce, death of spouse, by choice), as each pathway may be shaped by distinct psychosocial and economic factors. Finally, studies leveraging qualitative methods and community‐based participatory research approaches should be conducted to ensure that solutions are contextually relevant and center single mothers’ voices. These strategies have demonstrated effectiveness at improving and sustaining ideal CVH behaviors across diverse populations. 69

For clinicians, family structure should be recognized as a core component of SDoHs and incorporated into routine care. Embedding SDoH screening tools into clinical workflows can help identify single mothers experiencing high levels of stress, food insecurity, or housing instability. 70 , 71 When these risks are identified, providers should proactively connect patients with community and governmental resources, such as nutrition assistance programs, subsidized child care, and mental health services. Such integrated referrals have been shown to reduce financial and psychosocial stress, which have downstream benefits for CVH. 70 , 72 Clinicians should also address mental health, as chronic stress, depression, and anxiety are linked to increased CVD risk. 73 , 74

For policymakers, supportive workplace policies that reduce work–family conflicts including flexible paid time off, sick leave provisions that cover child illness, and remote work should be implemented given evidence of their associations with improved psychological well‐being and CVH. 75 , 76 Further, federal programs that strengthen household economic stability, including subsidized child care, child care tax credits, and income supplementation, should be promoted. 77 According to the Family Stress Model, financial strain increases caregiver stress, subsequently contributing to worse mental health and adoption of adverse CVH behaviors. 78 , 79 Reducing economic hardship through these programs has been observed to produce significant reductions in severe psychological distress and improvements in mental health. 80 As poor mental health is also associated with increased absenteeism, disability, and reductions in productivity in the workplace, policies that protect the psychological well‐being of single mothers may jointly improve their CVH through reduction of stress and promotion of their economic stability. 81 , 82

Strengths and Limitations

There are various strengths featured in the present review. To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first systematic review to describe CVH among single mothers. Moreover, the assessment of both the health behaviors and factors that describe CVH resulted in an overall more comprehensive understanding of CVD risk‐factor prevalence among single mothers.

Despite these strengths, there are limitations that inform our interpretation of findings. Most studies were cross‐sectional, thus limiting our ability to determine causality in associations between single motherhood and CVH. More longitudinal studies assessing the long‐term trajectories of CVH, drivers of inequities in CVH, and potential protective factors of CVH among single mothers are needed. We were also unable to determine the duration of single motherhood from the studies included, which is a limitation given that the duration of exposure has been documented to have a dose–response effect on health. Teenage single mothers were also excluded from our study, which is an important next step in research given the potential longer duration of exposure to factors deleterious to CVH. Further, although we included publications from outside the United States (n=22), the heterogeneity in the social and political environment across multiple countries can inform the impact of socioeconomic status on health. Heterogeneity was also observed in the quality of measures used for each health behavior and health factor, indicating a need for more research that uses rigorous research methods to assess CVH among single mothers. In the studies included, there was no assessment of the pathway to single motherhood (eg, divorce, widowed, by choice), which may have a differential influence on CVH. Finally, this study excluded articles that were not in English, which may have diminished the generalizability of findings.

Conclusions

The present review described the current findings on the distribution of CVH among single mothers. According to the studies included, single mothers had overall worse CVH compared with partnered mothers. Given the interrelated relationship of health behaviors and factors that describe CVH, further research is needed to disentangle these associations and identify potential factors driving poorer CVH among single mothers. Finally, to equitably improve CVH among single mothers, research must address SDoHs through an intersectional lens that recognizes how diverse experiences shaped by the multiple, overlapping identities held by individuals, outside of motherhood, may influence CVH.
