A classification of radicular grooves from the perspective of periodontology
Authors: Huxiao Li, Zhaowei Tai, Jiachen Dong, Zhongchen Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRadicular grooves are relatively common, yet a standardized classification system and treatment regimen remain elusive. Existing classifications primarily focus on the morphological aspects of dental structures, overlooking the crucial periodontal condition of affected teeth. This oversight hampers the development of effective diagnostic and treatment protocols. Therefore, the article aims to present a classification that integrates the periodontal status of affected teeth and propose corresponding therapeutic approaches.MethodsThe article discusses four classes of radicular grooves based on the location of the deformity and the periodontal status of teeth. Each class is accompanied by a recommended treatment program. For the most severe class, a periodontal-endodontic surgical approach is suggested as an alternative to intentional replantation.ResultsThe article presents the treatment process and outcomes for at least one case report for each class, achieving positive results across all classes. Notably, cases with deep periodontal pockets showed significant postoperative improvement.ConclusionThis paper proposes a novel radicular groove classification system incorporating periodontal status, with treatment protocols specific to each category. Clinical case reports validate the clinical applicability of this approach, demonstrating favorable outcomes across all classification groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07002-1.Keywords: Radicular grooves, Palatal groove, Decisional tree, Periodontal lesion, Tooth developmental anomaly

Introduction

During tooth development, abnormal growth of the enamel organ can lead to dental invagination, where enamel extends deeply into the dental papillae [1]. Radicular groove, a type of dental invagination, ranges from superficial grooves on the tooth surface to deep depressions that traverse the entire root structure. It occurs predominantly in maxillary lateral incisors, occasionally affecting central incisors or canines, with a prevalence rate of about 4.6% [2].

The malformed radicular groove disrupts normal soft and hard tissue closure around the tooth, creating a pathway for bacteria to invade the periodontal tissues. This microbial invasion causes significant localized destruction of periodontal tissues, complicating treatment interventions [3]. Patients often do not notice the deformity until advanced stages marked by periodontal-endodontic lesions, when affected teeth have a poor prognosis and may require extraction due to the challenges of treatment [4].

Currently, a standardized classification system for radicular grooves remains elusive. Clinically, the taxonomy proposed by Goon [5] is widely used to categorize the extent and complexity of radicular grooves. According to this classification, "mild" indicates a subtle depression of enamel on the coronal aspect, stopping at the cemento-enamel junction. "Moderate" describes a radicular groove that extends along the root surface as a shallow fissure but does not reach the root apex. In contrast, "complex" features a deeply fissured radicular groove that spans the entire length of the root and, in rare cases, may even separate an accessory root from the primary axis. The differentiation between "moderate" and "complex" remains somewhat ambiguous.

An alternative classification proposed by Gu relies on the length of the groove along the root surface as observed via cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) [6]. Other classifications based on the mesio-distal position of the sulcus or the depth of the sulcal depression are also documented [7]. These existing classifications primarily focus on the morphological aspects of dental structures and often overlook the periodontal status of the affected tooth.

Effective therapeutic intervention for radicular grooves requires an integrated approach that considers both dental and periodontal conditions. The current classifications [5–7] of radicular grooves lack inclusion of periodontal status, which is crucial for developing accurate diagnostic and treatment protocols. Therefore, the introduction of a classification system that incorporates periodontal status is essential for facilitating the development of effective treatment plans.

Numerous case reports have explored the management of radicular grooves [8–11]. Intentional replantation is often considered a last-resort option when severe periodontal destruction persists despite root canal treatment (RCT), yet the tooth retains some salvageable value [12]. However, intentional replantation carries substantial risks and must be executed swiftly, ideally within 15 min post-extraction [13]. Key procedural steps include debridement, sealing of the radicular groove, reverse root canal preparation and filling, and reimplantation. Throughout these procedures, maintaining root surface moisture, ensuring cleanliness, and avoiding excessive compression of the periodontal ligament are critical [14].

Despite its generally high success rate [15, 16], prolonged observation periods post-replantation may lead to external root resorption [17]. Moreover, in vitro manipulation often results in suboptimal survival of periodontal cells and incomplete regeneration. A case series found a 77.3% incidence of ankylosis among 48 intentional replantation patients [18], underscoring the preference for in vivo procedures when feasible. In 1996, Rankow reported a case where apicoectomy and guided tissue regeneration (GTR) were both used to treat a palatal radicular groove [19], but subsequent reports have been scarce. We consider this treatment a superior alternative for severe cases of radicular grooves.

This paper introduces a case series of several different radicular grooves. Through these cases, we summarize a classification for radicular grooves intended to inform clinical treatment protocols and emphasizes exploration of alternative treatment options to replace or complement intentional replantation.

This paper presents a classification system for radicular grooves, which takes into account the periodontal condition of affected teeth. For each class, the article outlines the recommended therapeutic approaches. Additionally, it provides case reports corresponding to each class, demonstrating the application of these treatment protocols and highlighting the favorable outcomes achieved.

Material and methods

Based on our clinical experience in managing radicular grooves, we propose a classification system that integrates the assessment of periodontal status. This classification necessitates a comprehensive clinical examination and radiographic evaluation to accurately assess the periodontal status of affected teeth, thereby significantly enhancing the clinician's ability to develop a precise and effective treatment plan.

It is crucial to note that this classification specifically applies to cases where radicular grooves are the primary or predominant etiological factors. Other factors such as severe periodontitis, trauma, occlusal trauma, and extensive caries can also influence the prognosis of affected teeth. Addressing the underlying conditions associated with radicular grooves is essential for the successful treatment of the affected tooth without compromising its overall prognosis. However, when these factors cannot be resolved and tooth extraction becomes necessary, the classification of radicular grooves loses its relevance and falls beyond the scope of our discussion.

We categorize radicular grooves into four distinct classes (Fig. 1).

The classification of radicular grooves. Class I: A, B: The groove extends supragingivally or within 2 mm subgingivally. Class II: C, D:The groove extends > 2 mm subgingivally, without any infrabony defect or with a ≤ 3 mm intrabony defect. Class II-a: C: Shallow grooves can be eliminated; Class II-b: D: Deep non- eliminable grooves require obturation. Class III: E: The groove extends > 2 mm subgingivally, with a > 3 mm intrabony defect (non-apical extension). Class IV: F, G: The groove extends > 2 mm subgingivally, with a > 3 mm intrabony defect (apical extension) and/or refractory periapical inflammation

Class I

The groove extends either coronally to the gingival margin or subgingivally within 2 mm (≤ 2 mm) apical to the gingival margin.Pulp symptoms may or may not be present; if they occur, apical periodontitis can typically be managed with root canal treatment. Treatment involves resin restoration of the grooves (Fig. 1 A, B).

Class II

The root surface groove extends subgingivally beyond 2 mm (> 2 mm) apical to the gingival margin, without any infrabony defect or accompanied by an intrabony defect not exceeding 3 mm in depth (≤ 3 mm). Pulp symptoms may or may not be present; if present, apical periodontitis can be managed with root canal treatment. Treatment approach includes flap elevation without GTR. Class II is further subdivided into:

Class II-a

Shallow grooves can be contoured with a high-speed diamond bur (Fig. 1 C).

Class II-b

Deeper grooves require subsequent obturation with bioceramic materials to achieve hermetic sealing of potential infection pathways.. If any bioceramic material is exposed above the gingiva after flap suturing, it can be replaced with composite resin once the gingival margin has stabilized (Fig. 1 D).

Class III

The root surface groove extends subgingivally beyond 2 mm (> 2 mm) apical to the gingival margin, accompanied by an intrabony defect exceeding 3 mm in depth (> 3 mm) that remains confined coronally to the root apex.Pulp symptoms may be present or absent; however, if present, apical periodontitis can be effectively managed through RCT. The treatment protocol involves elevating a flap and applying bioceramic material to seal the groove. This procedure is further enhanced by GTR to restore the compromised periodontal tissues (Fig. 1 E).

Class IV

The root surface groove extends subgingivally beyond 2 mm (> 2 mm) apical to the gingival margin, associated with an intrabony defect exceeding 3 mm in depth (> 3 mm) that demonstrates apical extension to the root apex, and/or presents refractory periapical inflammation uncontrollable by conventional endodontic therapy.The treatment approach typically involves flap elevation, filling of the radicular grooves, GTR, and potentially endodontic surgery. In situations where these combined treatments are not feasible, intentional replantation may be considered as an alternative option (Fig. 1 F, G).

This classification system integrates both the depth of the groove and the presence of infrabony defects, providing a structured approach to diagnosis and treatment planning based on the severity of radicular grooves and associated periodontal conditions.

Results

This case series presents 8 cases of radicular grooves, illustrating all the previously mentioned classes, the application of corresponding treatment protocols, and the outcomes of these treatments. The cases were treated at the Periodontology Department of the Ninth People's Hospital of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine in Shanghai, China between 2021 and 2024.

All patients, with the exception of patient 1, exhibited varying degrees of periodontitis. Notably, the teeth affected by radicular grooves demonstrated a more pronounced attachment loss compared to other teeth within the oral cavity. Importantly, no other causative factors, such as trauma or extensive caries, were identified in these cases.

Case 1

A 22-year-old female was diagnosed with a Class I radicular grooves on a lateral incisor. The groove was limited to the coronal aspect of the gingival margin. The patient exhibited normal pulpal vitality and did not report any spontaneous symptoms. Consequently, the treatment plan involved only the decarving and filling of the groove with resin (Fig. 2).

Case 1 showing lateral radicular groove of Class I. A preoperative clinical examination, the groove led to caries formation and extended supragingivally; B resin restoration of the caries; C postoperative 4 months

Case 2

A 38-year-old female patient was diagnosed with Class II-a radicular grooves on the bilateral maxillary lateral incisors. These grooves extended subgingivally beyond 2 mm apical to the gingival margin. It was determined that the grooves could be effectively contoured using fine-grit diamond burs. The treatment included flap elevation, contouring and finishing, and suturing. This approach resulted in favorable postoperative healing (Fig. 3).

Case 2 showing bilateral radicular grooves of Class II-a. A, G preoperative clinical examination, the grooves extended > 2 mm subgingivally; B, H flap elevation showed no infrabony defect; C, I after contouring and finishing, grooves were eliminated without restoration; D, J suturing with 5–0 thread; E, K postoperative 12 months; F, L preoperative radiograph

Case 3, 4, and 5

The patients, comprising a 36-year-old male (Patient 3), a 42-year-old female (Patient 4), and a 62-year-old male (Patient 5), were diagnosed with Class II-b radicular grooves that extended below the gingival margin but did not present deep infrabony defects. In all cases, flap elevation was performed, and due to the significant depth of the grooves, bioceramic material (iRoot) was utilized to fill them. Following suturing, postoperative healing was satisfactory, as illustrated in Fig. 4.

Case 3, 4, 5 showing radicular grooves of Class II-b. A, F, K flap elevation showing deep radicular grooves of case 3, 4, 5 without infrabony defect; B, G, L preoperative radiograph; C, H, M sealing grooves with bioceramic material (iRoot); D, I, N suturing with 5–0 thread; E, J postoperative at 6 months; O Case 4 showing the probing depth in 2 mm

Case 6

A 36-year-old female was diagnosed with a Class III radicular groove, exhibiting deep infrabony defects after flap elevation. The therapeutic protocol involved obturation of the root surface groove with bioceramic material (iRoot), grafting of the intrabony defect using bone collagen (Bio-oss Collagen), followed by suturing, ultimately achieving favorable healing outcomes with optimal tissue regeneration. (Fig. 5).

Case 6 showing a radicular groove of Class III. A preoperative clinical examination, the groove extended 8 mm subgingivally; B preoperative radiograph; C, D, E flap elevation, debridement and sealing the groove; F filling the infrabony defect with bone collagen (Bio-oss Collagen); G suturing with 5–0 thread; H postoperative 2 weeks; I, J postoperative 9 months

Case 7

A 29-year-old female patient presented with a Class IV radicular groove on a central incisor, which extended to the root apex and was accompanied by significant periodontal tissue destruction, as revealed by CBCT imaging. The treatment plan involved several steps: flap elevation, debridement, labial fenestration for apical resection, retrograde filling, bone grafting, placement of a collagen membrane (Bio-gide), and tension suturing. Postoperative evaluation showed a reduction in probing depths to 2 mm, indicating successful healing, (Fig. 6).

Case 8

A 25-year-old female presented with a Class IV radicular groove on the palatal aspect of a lateral incisor, which had previously undergone root canal therapy at another facility. Despite this prior treatment, the patient continued to experience refractory periapical inflammation and severe alveolar bone resorption, as revealed by CBCT. To address these issues, a comprehensive treatment plan was implemented, involving flap elevation and endodontic surgery. The radicular groove was filled, and GTR was employed to promote healing. Given the moderate mobility of the tooth, postoperative splinting was performed to stabilize it. After one year, the patient exhibited successful healing, demonstrating the efficacy of the treatment approach. (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Since the initial report of radicular grooves over a century ago [20], various treatment approaches and case reports have been documented [21–24]. In recent years, there has been a gradual shift toward multidisciplinary collaborative approaches in the management of complex radicular grooves [25, 26], but the taxonomic framework for radicular groove classification has not undergone commensurate refinement. Existing classification systems for radicular grooves primarily emphasize tooth morphology, often overlooking the critical role of periodontal tissue integrity, which is essential for tooth preservation.

Based on a comprehensive evaluation of both periodontal condition and tooth morphology, we propose a treatment-guided classification system for radicular grooves. This system is designed to facilitate appropriate clinical decision-making. In this classification, Class I encompasses cases that are asymptomatic and can be effectively managed with resin filling treatment alone. In contrast, Classes II to IV denote more complex cases that necessitate surgical intervention due to the involvement of periodontal lesions.

In our case report, Case 2–5 involved maxillary lateral incisors with radicular grooves that extended abnormally below the gingival margin, necessitating flap elevation. In Class II-a cases, after elevating the flap, some radicular grooves can be effectively eliminated using a high-speed diamond bur. Following this debridement process, the gingival flap is repositioned and sutured. However, deeper radicular grooves, classified as Class II-b, may require the application of additional filling materials to seal the infection pathway. Treatment approaches within Class II vary depending on whether root filling is necessary without addressing deeper infrabony defects. The utilization of glass ionomer cement (GIC) [27] and bioceramic materials [28, 29] for radicular groove obturation has been documented in clinical applications. In our study, iRoot BP Plus (Henry Schein, Hongkong, China) a pre-mixed bioceramic paste formulation demonstrating favorable handling properties [30]—was employed as a permanent root canal obturation and surgical repair material. We formally declare no financial conflicts of interest regarding the selection of obturation materials, which were strictly based on experimental requirements without commercial influence.

Class III defects are distinguished by their deeper infrabony characteristics compared to Class II, yet they do not extend to the apical region. The treatment protocol for Class III typically involves several steps: flap elevation, debridement of the infrabony defects, and the removal of granulation tissue and calculus from the root surface. Additionally, GTR is employed to facilitate healing. Due to the presence of radicular grooves in Class III defects, achieving optimal morphology through odontoplasty is insufficient; thus, the use of filling materials is necessary to address these grooves. The prognosis for this type of defect is generally favorable, as evidenced by numerous documented cases in the literature. Various materials, such as demineralized freeze-dried cortical bone (DFDBA), non-porous hydroxyapatite, and enamel matrix derivative (EMD), have been successfully used to fill osseous defects, contributing to positive treatment outcomes [31–36].

Current literature exhibits a notable deficiency in demographic analyses of gender distribution and age stratification pertaining to radicular groove presentations. In our case series, a marked gender predilection was observed, with female predominance (8 cases) significantly outweighing male incidence (2 cases) (gender ratio 4:1). Age distribution demonstrated bimodal clustering, with 90% of cases (n = 9) occurring within the 20–45 year cohort, while a solitary outlier (6.7%) presented at 62 years of age, whose radicular groove configuration belonged to Class II according to the classification system. It may indicate that more severe morphological anomalies prompt earlier patient-initiated healthcare-seeking behavior.

Historically, intentional replantation has been a common approach for managing cases involving radicular grooves [13, 37]. However, in our recommendation, flap elevation combined with endodontic surgery offers distinct advantages by providing enhanced visibility and space for thorough debridement without the need for tooth extraction. This approach is particularly beneficial in cases where radicular grooves extend to the apical region with complex anatomical variations such as C-shaped canals or double canals, which can be effectively managed through apicoectomy and retrograde restoration.

Combination therapy offers several advantages over intentional replantation, particularly in terms of preserving the original periodontal tissues and minimizing the risks associated with tooth extraction. By avoiding extraction, combination therapy reduces the likelihood of external root resorption and promotes a more stable healing process. This approach is especially important in Class IV cases, where severe alveolar bone resorption surrounds radicular grooves. In such instances, it is crucial to regenerate periodontal tissue concurrently with treating the apical segment of the root. Although intentional replantation can effectively address apical inflammation and facilitate gradual healing and bone defect repair in the apical region, it falls short in addressing bone resorption around the grooves.

Although there are limited reports on successful management of radicular grooves using intentional replantation combined with emdogain therapy [38, 39], there is no documentation on intentional replantation combined with comprehensive groove regeneration. Our integrated treatment regimen allows for simultaneous execution of endodontic surgery and GTR, addressing concerns related to tooth ankylosis [18] and optimizing long-term outcomes.

This strategy, while effective, presents certain limitations that must be acknowledged. Firstly, it necessitates a high level of staffing, as the procedure requires the expertise of both an endodontic specialist and a periodontal specialist to be performed effectively. Additionally, the simultaneous execution of multiple procedures extends the duration of the surgery, which can consequently result in more pronounced postoperative swelling.

This case series examines two specific instances of labial and palatal radicular grooves, analyzed within the framework of a periodontal-endodontic surgical approach. The determination of the flap elevation site is primarily influenced by the location of the radicular groove. Typically, due to anatomical considerations and the need for optimal visualization, endodontic surgery is conducted via a labial approach. However, when the radicular groove is situated on the palatal aspect, both labial and palatal flaps are elevated concurrently. This dual elevation technique is exemplified in Case 8, where postoperative bone defects on both sides are covered with a collagen membrane. Conversely, when the radicular groove is located on the labial side, only a labial flap is elevated, as illustrated in Case 7.

In Case 7, it is imperative to exercise caution during apical surgical procedures to prevent compromising the alveolar bone on the crown-side of the tooth. Although removing alveolar bone on the labial aspect of the crown can facilitate debridement, maintaining the original alveolar bone height is crucial. This preservation not only supports the contour but also provides osteoblasts necessary for subsequent GTR. Our case outcomes have demonstrated minimal loss of attachment during patient healing, with probing depths consistently measuring less than 2 mm. Furthermore, for aesthetic considerations, it is essential to preserve the papillae during flap elevation.

The primary objective of establishing a classification system for radicular grooves is to assist physicians in formulating suitable treatment protocols. To improve the efficacy of this classification, we have created a decision path, illustrated in Fig. 8, which systematically guides clinicians through the steps necessary to categorize and address these conditions.

The decision path for the classification of radicular grooves

When assessing a tooth with a radicular groove, the initial step involves identifying any accompanying conditions such as caries, trauma, occlusal trauma, or periodontitis. If these conditions are severe enough to jeopardize the survival of the tooth, extraction may become necessary, rendering further classification and treatment of the radicular groove irrelevant. However, if the prognosis for these conditions is favorable or if they are absent, clinicians proceed to classify the radicular groove based on its extension below the gingival margin.

Class I: Radicular grooves extending within 2 mm below the gingival margin can often be managed with non-surgical periodontal treatment, followed by resin sealing of the groove.

Classes II to IV: Radicular grooves extending more than 2 mm below the gingival margin require flap elevation for further assessment. The classification within this range (II to IV) is determined by the presence and depth of infrabony defects, identified through clinical examination, X-rays, and ideally CBCT.

Class II: No infrabony defect exceeding 3mm. Subcategories include II-a (groove can be ground and leveled) and II-b (groove requires sealing with bioceramic materials). Class III: Infrabony defect ≥3mm without reaching the root apex. Class IV: Infrabony defect ≥3mm extending to the root apex or presenting with apical periodontitis resistant to root canal treatment. Requires comprehensive endodontic and periodontal surgical intervention.

For Class III, GTRCBCT following flap reflection is typically employed to restore compromised periodontal tissues. In Class IV cases, where apical inflammation persists or significant apical defects exist, additional endodontic surgery is necessary. Intentional replantation may be considered as a last resort if other treatments are not feasible.

The classification system delineates the extent of inflammatory damage across critical boundaries: the gingival margin, alveolar bone crest, and root apex. Each classification level corresponds to progressively more complex surgical interventions. By precisely categorizing radicular grooves using this decision tree, clinicians can formulate tailored treatment plans that optimize outcomes for affected teeth, considering both functional restoration and aesthetic preservation.

Conclusion

This article introduces a novel classification system for radicular grooves and presents a series of representative cases demonstrating its application in treatment planning. Based on the observed correlation between groove characteristics (depth, location, extent) and periodontal destruction patterns in our case series, the proposed classification provides periodontists with a structured framework to more accurately assess the periodontal prognosis of affected teeth. This enhanced diagnostic clarity, as demonstrated by the tailored approaches in the presented cases, directly facilitates the development of customized treatment strategies that address the specific anatomical challenges and disease severity inherent to each groove class.

Furthermore, the successful management outcomes achieved in cases where intentional replantation was deemed high-risk (Case 7&8 demonstrating stable attachment at 1 year post-op) highlight the viability of the advocated alternative surgical approach. By preserving the natural tooth and its supporting structures, this approach inherently mitigates the risks of complications such as dental ankylosis and root resorption, which are well-documented sequelae of extraction and replantation procedures.
