Comparison of midline lumbar interbody fusion and minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion for treatment of lumbar degeneration disease
Authors: Xuelei Zhang, Yu Zhang, Zuchao Gu, Guo Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMidline lumbar interbody fusion (MIDLIF) and minimally invasive transforaminal interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) are two minimally invasive lumbar fusion methods that have gained popularity in the past two decades. MIDLIF involves the use of cortical bone trajectory screws, whereas MIS-TLIF uses traditional pedicle screws. However, there is a significant lack of research directly examining the clinical efficacy of these two methods in treating single-segment lumbar degenerative diseases. Hence, the objective of our retrospective study is to assess and contrast the surgical and clinical results of MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF. The study population comprised 133 patients diagnosed with single-segment lumbar degenerative disease that received treatment using either MIDLIF (n = 65) or MIS-TLIF (n = 68) in our department from January 2017 to January 2019. The fusion rates for MIDLIF were consistently lower than MID-TLIF at all post-operative time periods of follow-up, however, the differences between the two groups were not statistically significant. The 1-year fusion rates were 81.5% (MIDLIF) and 83.8% (MIS-TLIF) (P = 0.728), and the 2-year fusion rates were 87.7% (MIDLIF) and 91.2% (MIS-TLIF) (P = 0.513). The final follow-up fusion rates were 93.8% (MIDLIF) and 95.6% (MIS-TLIF) (P = 0.653). MIDLIF had several advantages over MIS-TLIF, including a shorter operative time (135.2 ± 15.70 vs. 160.1 ± 17.2 min, P < 0.001), decreased intraoperative blood loss (147.9 ± 36.4 vs. 169.5 ± 24.7 mL, P < 0.001), and a shorter length of hospital stay (10.8 ± 3.1 vs. 12.4 ± 4.1d; P = 0.014). No significant differences were seen between the groups in terms of the postoperative day of ambulation, Oswestry dysfunction index (ODI) scores, and visual analog scale (VAS) scores for leg and lower back pain (P > 0.05). Although not significant (P = 0.707), MIDLIF (13.8%) had fewer overall complications than MIS-TLIF (16.2%). Therefore, compared to MIS-TLIF, MIDLIF provides perioperative benefits while achieving the same outcomes as MIS-TLIF in terms of fusion rate, pain relief, functional improvement, and complication rate.Subject terms: Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Lumbar degenerative disease (LDD) is a prevalent and costly health disorder that causes disability in middle-aged and older adults. It encompasses conditions such as lumbar spinal stenosis, lumbar disc herniation, and lumbar spondylolisthesis. LDD may result in spinal instability, radiating discomfort in the lower extremities, and lower back pain. Due to population aging, LDD prevalence is rising annually1–4. When conservative treatments fail to provide effective management of symptoms, lumbar fusion surgery, for decompression and stabilization of the involved segments, is the most commonly used treatment approach.

Posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) are commonly employed open surgical procedures for the treatment of degenerative lumbar illnesses. These procedures involve the use of pedicle screw fixation and have shown favorable clinical outcomes5–8. However, the requirement for extensive dissection of paravertebral muscles in this approach increases intraoperative blood loss and can increase the risk for refractory lower back pain post-surgery9,10. In 2003, to avoid the risks associated with paravertebral muscle dissection, Foley et al. introduced the minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) procedure. MIS-TLIF has several advantages over traditional open surgery, including a smaller incision, reduced intraoperative blood loss, decreased risk of postoperative discomfort, faster recovery after surgery, and shorter length of hospital stay11–13. However, a natural drawback of MIS-TLIF is the narrow surgical space and limited field of view, which may lead to insufficient nerve decompression14 and poor surgical and long-term outcomes. In addition, the fixation strength using pedicle screws is significantly reduced when implanted in osteoporotic vertebrae15,16. In 2009, Santoni et al. suggested utilizing cortical screw (CS) fixation as a means to minimize soft tissue damage, enlarge the operative area, and enhance the stability of screws. CS usage results in a 30% higher uniaxial yield pullout load compared to PS when assessed in human cadaver lumbar vertebrae17. CS increases the fixation strength by varying the entry point and screw channel, compared to PS, providing greater surface contact with the cortical bone18,19. Moreover, the insertional torque of CS in human vertebrae is 1.71 times greater than that of PS20. Other studies have also confirmed the improved biomechanical properties of CS21,22. Compared to PS, the entry point of CS is closer to the posterior midline and, therefore, less soft tissue dissection is required to expose the screw entry point and, thus, less blood loss is expected during the dissection process23. In addition, the posterior midline surgical approach for MIDLIF ensures an adequate surgical field.

Since Mizuno et al. first reported the clinical use of MIDLIF in 201424, its safety and efficacy have been confirmed25,26. However, although MIS-TLIF and MIDLIF are commonly employed methods for managing LDD, there is a distinct lack of studies directly comparing the clinical efficacy of these two approaches in treating single-segment LDD. Accordingly, the aim of our retrospective study was to compare the surgical and clinical outcomes of MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF.

Method and materials

Research methodology and ethical declaration

This study was a retrospective cohort analysis that involved patients with single-level lumbar degenerative disorders who underwent either MIDLIF or MIS-TLIF procedures at our hospital between January 2017 and January 2019. Data pertaining to medical records, imaging data, and postoperative follow-up information of all patients were gathered. The study was carried out in compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and its revised version from 2013. The protocol received approval from the Ethics Committee of Chengdu Frist People’s Hospital (project identification code 2020 ZXKT No. 008). The Ethics Committee of the Chengdu First People’s Hospital exempted/waived the need for informed consent from patients due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study sample

The criteria for inclusion were as follows: (1) lumbar disc herniation, lumbar spinal stenosis, and I-II degree lumbar spondylolisthesis have been diagnosed; (2) single-level lumbar fusion surgery, using the MIDLIF or MIS-TLIF technique; (3) age > 18 years; and (4) follow-up ≥ 3 years post-surgery. The criteria for exclusion were as follows: (1) prior surgical intervention on the lumbar spine; (2) lumbar spine infection, trauma, or malignancy; (3) imaging showing congenital hypoplasia or deformity of the spine; (4) severe scoliosis (Cobb angle > 30°); and (5) loss to follow-up.

Surgical procedures

A single senior surgeon performed all surgeries, using the following methods for the MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF approaches.

The MIDLIF was performed following the successful administration of general anesthesia, with the patient positioned facing downward. The surgical segment was identified using C-arm fluoroscopy and marked at the posterior midline with a surgical skin marker pen. Then, the surgical site was thoroughly cleansed and covered with sterile drapes. A 5 cm long incision was made along the midline, sequentially cutting through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and lumbar dorsal fascia. Dissecting the paravertebral muscles revealed the lamina to the inner margin of the articular process to determine the target intervertebral space. The entry point of the screw was located at the place where the vertical line passing through the center of the superior articular process intersects with the horizontal line, positioned 1 mm below the lower margin of the transverse process. A 1.5 mm drill hole was created at the point of entry to break through the superficial cortex. A depth-limited electric drill (2.5 mm and a length of 2.5 cm) was then used to establish a screw channel, oriented in a caudal inclination of 25° and a medial inclination of 15°. A probe was utilized to verify the soundness of the inner pedicle wall of the screw channel. The inferior articular process and spinous process of the superior vertebrae, along with a portion of the superior articular process of the inferior vertebrae, were surgically removed. The vertebral plate was also resected, bilaterally, to the inner edge of the pedicles, and the ligamentum flavum was resected. Herniated disc tissue was removed, the bilateral lateral recesses and nerve root canals were enlarged, and compression of the dural sac and nerve roots was relieved. The surgical area was then irrigated, then cauterized to stop bleeding. The residual nucleus pulposus and cartilaginous endplates were completely removed. Interbody fusion was performed by sequentially implanting autogenous bone and a polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cage filled with autogenous bone into the intervertebral space. Four CS screws were used. The fixation rods were pre-bent, according to the physiological curvature of the lumbosacral region, and a screw-rod internal fixation system was routinely installed. Fluoroscopy was used to confirm the appropriate position of the internal fixation. The incision was irrigated thoroughly with normal saline, with complete cessation of any bleeding. A negative-pressure drainage system was inserted into the surgical site, and the wound was closed sequentially, layer by layer.

The MIS-TLIF was performed following the successful administration of general anesthesia, with the patient positioned face down (prone). The surgical segment was identified using C-arm fluoroscopy, and a surgical skin marker pen was used to indicate the horizontal line that passed through the midpoint of the pedicle and the vertical lines representing the outer margin of the pedicle. The purpose of these lines is to facilitate the accurate placement of Jamshidi needles into the pedicle, in preparation for subsequent screw insertion. Then, the surgical site was thoroughly cleansed and covered with drapes as per standard procedure. A 3 cm vertical incision was made 1 cm lateral to the lateral pedicle line on the affected side, sequentially cutting through the skin, subcutaneous tissue, and lumbar dorsal fascia. Access was created along the gap between the longissimus and multifidus muscles, and working and fiber optic channels were placed. The upper and lower facets of the target segment were exposed. The lower facet and part of the upper facet were resected, and the lateral recess and nerve root canal were enlarged, relieving the compression on the nerve root and dural sac. For patients with bilateral lower extremity symptoms, the operating bed was tilted 15–20° towards the surgeon’s side, and the working channel was adjusted to provide visibility to the base of the superior spinous process. The contralateral ligamentum flavum and inferior portion of the vertebral plate were resected. The contralateral lateral recess was decompressed. The traveling nerve root was exposed and decompressed in the space between the intervertebral disc and ligamentum flavum, pedicle area, and exit area. The herniated disc tissue was removed. The residual nucleus pulposus and cartilaginous endplates were completely removed. The incision was irrigated thoroughly with normal saline, with complete cessation of any bleeding. Interbody fusion was performed by sequentially implanting autogenous bone and a PEEK cage filled with autogenous bone into the intervertebral space. The spinal canal, bilateral nerve root canals, and lateral recesses were explored again to confirm that the decompression was sufficient and that the nerve roots and dural sac were relaxed and free of entrapment. The appropriate pedicle screw entry point was determined and a guidewire was placed. Four percutaneous PS screws were placed using the guidewire. The fixation rods were pre-bent, according to the physiological curvature of the lumbosacral region, and fixed with set screws. Fluoroscopy was used to confirm the appropriate position of the internal fixation. The incision was irrigated, with complete cessation of any bleeding. A negative-pressure drainage system was inserted into the surgical site, and the wound was closed sequentially, layer by layer.

For both procedures, antibiotics were administered for 24 h postoperatively to prevent wound infections. The criterion for drain removal was less than 30 ml of drainage volume in the previous 24 h. The patient was instructed to wear a spinal brace for ambulation after drain removal.

Data collection and follow-up

The following perioperative outcomes were measured: intraoperative volume of blood loss, operative time, days to postoperative ambulation, short-term complications of the surgery and length of hospital stay. Clinical outcomes included change in lower back and lower limb pain, evaluated using VAS score from baseline to postoperative day 3 and 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. Change in function was assessed by measuring the ODI score at various time points: baseline, postoperative day 3, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and the final follow-up. The fusion status was evaluated by radiographic assessment, namely the Brantigan-Steffe-Fraser (BSF) classification, at 1- and 2-years after the surgery and during the final follow-up. As per the BSF categorization27, a mechanically solid fusion is characterized by the presence of bone bridges covering at least half of the fusion region, with a minimum density matching that attained during the surgery.

Statistical analysis

The mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to summarize continuous, discrete, and self-report rating data, whereas categorical variables were presented as a frequency or percentage. The Independent sample t-test was employed to evaluate differences between groups for continuous variables that had a normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, with the exception of the VAS score, which was assessed using the Robust t-test due to the heterogeneity of variance and non-normal distribution of the data. The chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test were employed to analyze categorical variables. The analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and Jamovi (Version 2.3.28.0, The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia). The threshold for statistical significance was established at a P-value of less than 0.05.

Discussion

MIS-TLIF and MIDLIF are two posterior lumbar fusion techniques, introduced by Foley et al. and Mizuno et al., respectively, at the beginning of this century, which have gradually gained widespread clinical application12,14,23–26,28,29. MIS-TLIF is performed through a paramedian incision, utilizing channel instruments and a light source, via the muscle interval between the longissimus and multifidus muscles. By comparison, MIDLIF is performed through a posterior midline incision, with intervertebral decompression and fusion performed after stripping of the paravertebral muscles. Both MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF provide significant advantages over traditional posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) in shortening operative time and length of hospital stay, as well as reducing intraoperative blood loss23,26,30. However, to date, a comparative evaluation of outcomes specific to these two techniques has not been reported. This was our aim.

Intraoperative outcomes were compared between the two techniques. It is well known that shortening the operation time can help reduce intraoperative blood loss and anesthesia accidents. Phan et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 21 articles and found that the mean operative time was 185 min and mean intraoperative blood loss was 177 mL for MIS-TLIF31. Zhu et al. conducted a meta-analysis of 28 articles and found that the mean operative time for MIS-TLIF was 168.9 min, with an average intraoperative blood loss of 160.5 mL32. Upon reviewing 42 patients who underwent single-level MIDLIF, Takenaka et al. found that the mean operative time was 120 min, and the mean intraoperative blood loss was 119 mL33. In our study, we found the MIDLIF group has shorter mean operative time and less intraoperative blood loss compared to MIS-TLIF group. Our findings align with the results of the above studies. We hypothesized that this may reflect the advantages of MIDLIF, such as conventional access, adequate surgical field, less soft tissue dissection, and easiness of achieving bleeding cessation, compared to MIS-TLIF which needs to be performed using a narrow channel which requires a long learning curve34. Furthermore, our findings indicate that the MIDLIF group had a shorter length of hospital stay, aligning with the meta-analysis conducted by Wang et al.23. While the MIDLIF group showed statistically significant advantages in terms of operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and length of hospital stay, some orthopedists typically did not perceive these differences as clinically meaningful. This may be attributed to the following reasons: 1. MIDLIF appears later, and the presence of its learning curve may obscure the advantages of the technology in early patients2. Different locations of the fused level and the number of levels fused may also interfere with the above clinical results; 3. The variations in the proficiency levels of the surgeons in the two procedures also impact the clinical outcome.

Good interbody fusion is a key factor for ensuring positive long-term postoperative outcomes. Previous studies have reported fusion rates for MIDLIF at 1-year postoperatively of 79.3%, 87%, and 92.1%35–37. According to Lee et al., the fusion rate for MIDLIF at 2-years postoperatively was 94.3%38. According to Wong et al., the fusion rate for MIS-TLIF at 1-year postoperatively was 92.5%39, with other studies reporting rates of fusion at 2-years postoperatively of 92–96.7%40–42. In our study, the interbody fusion rates for MIDLIF were 81.5%, 87.7%, and 93.8% at 1- and 2-years postoperatively and at the final follow-up, respectively. These rates were slightly lower than those observed in the MIS-TLIF group, at 83.8%, 91.2%, and 95.6%, respectively. However, these differences were not significant. Both the literature results and our findings confirm that the fusion rate in the MIDLIF group was lower than that in the MIS-TLIF group at the same time after surgery, although this difference was not significant. This observed difference in fusion rates may be related to the weaker compressive force of CS on the anterior column of the lumbar vertebral body than the compressive force achieved using PS43. It is crucial to acknowledge that our study sample size might have been insufficient to identify statistical significance between the two groups. Large, prospective, case-control studies are needed to clarify whether the fusion rate is indeed lower for MIDLIF than for MIS-TLIF.

Pain and functional improvement are important indicators of successful surgical outcome and patient satisfaction. At 1-year post-MIDLIF, Lee et al. reported an improvement in VAS score, from baseline, of 5.6 points for lower back pain and 4.6 points for leg pain, with a concomitant improvement in the ODI score of 24.635. At 2-years post-MIDLIF, Chin et al. reported an improvement of 4.3 points in the VAS score for lower back pain and 4 points for leg pain, and an improvement of 12 points in the ODI score44. At 1-year after MIS-TLIF, Zhu et al. reported an improvement of 5.1 points in the VAS score of lower back pain and 4.7 points for leg pain, with an improvement in the ODI score of 51.5 points, compared to the baseline preoperatively45. At 1 year following MIS-TLIF, Jenkins et al. reported a reduction of 4 points on the VAS for lower back pain, 3.8 points on the VAS for leg pain, and 21.6 points on the ODI 1-year after MIS-TLIF46. At 2-years after MIS-TLIF, Jenkins et al. reported a reduction of 3.6 points on the VAS for lower back pain, 3.5 points on the VAS for leg pain, and 21.1 points on the ODI. In our study, no significant difference in the extent of improvement in VAS and ODI scores was found between MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF. The magnitude of change, shown in Table 3; Fig. 1, being consistent with those previously reported. Based on our findings and those of the aforementioned published studies, we could conclude that MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF are equally effective in achieving postoperative pain relief and functional improvement.

Posterior lumbar surgery is frequently associated with a range of complications, which can be categorized into two main groups: intraoperative and postoperative complications. Intraoperative risks encompass dural tear, nerve injury, and endplate injury, while postoperative complications consist of incision infection, adjacent segment disease, and internal fixation failure. Reducing the incidence of complications is needed to accelerate postoperative recovery and improve patient quality of life. We found the overall complication rate was 13.8% for MIDLIF and 16.2% for MIS-TLIF, rates which were comparable to the results reported by Sakaura et al. and Modi et al.47,48. The difference in complications rate between groups was not significant. Intraoperative complications included dural tears and screw misplacement which were treated promptly in both groups, with no incidence of nerve injury. Postoperative wound infections were treated using intravenous antibiotics combined with dressing changes in both groups. One patient with an epidural hematoma in the MIS-TLIF group required prolonged bed rest, combined with hemostatic drugs, mannitol, and low-dose dexamethasone. Two patients in the MIDLIF group developed symptomatic adjacent segment disease (ASD) at 12 and 16 months, postoperatively. Symptoms were significantly relieved after conservative treatment, with no indication for surgery. Two patients in the MIS-TLIF group developed symptomatic adjacent segment disease at 14- and 22-months post-surgery. One patient experienced symptomatic relief after conservative treatment, while there was no significant relief in the other patient after 3 months of conservative treatment, which required reoperation. One patient in the MIS-TLIF group experienced cage backout 3 months postoperatively, with unbearable radiating pain in the ipsilateral lower limbs prior to surgery. After reoperation, with use of a larger cage, the symptoms were relieved and the patient was able to move without limitations after wearing a lumbar brace.

According to the literature, the incidence of ASD with MIDLIF ranges from 3.2 to 9.1%, with a rate of reoperation of 0 to 3.2%47,49, compared to a rate of ASD of 4–10% for MIS-TLIF, with a rate of reoperation of 0 to 8.3%48,50–53. Hence, it may be inferred that both MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF procedures are likely to exhibit a low incidence of complications and reoperation. Furthermore, the observed low rates of ASD in both groups suggest favorable long-term results.

It is imperative to understand the limitations inherent to our study. First, the sample size was small, which increased type II statistical errors and reduced the ability to detect low-probability events. This also affects our ability to judge whether there is a real difference in the occurrence of events close to each other between the two groups. Second, our patients were relatively young and had a low incidence of osteoporosis, which theoretically reduces the biomechanical advantage of CS over PS in osteoporotic vertebrae. Finally, given the retrospective nature of this investigation, it is important to acknowledge that, despite adequate statistical power for detecting between-group disparities, the presence of inherent biases and confounding variables may potentially influence the overall findings and conclusions of the study. Hence, it is imperative to conduct prospective, large-scale, randomized controlled studies to assess and compare the clinical outcomes of MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF procedures in patients across various age groups, particularly in the elderly population that exhibits a heightened prevalence of osteoporosis.

In summary, both MIDLIF and MIS-TLIF procedures have demonstrated comparable outcomes in terms of fusion rate, pain alleviation, functional enhancement, and complication rate when employed for the management of single-level LDD. However, the MIDLIF procedure has obvious advantages in shortening operation time, reducing intraoperative blood loss and length of hospital stay.
