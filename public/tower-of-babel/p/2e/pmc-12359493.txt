Differences in the management of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction among physicians from Europe, the Middle East and North Africa: an international survey
Authors: Mohamed H Omer, Anastasia Shchendrygina, Omar Ahmad, Clara Saldarriaga, Sydney Goldfeder de Gracia, Mosaad Alhussain, Najmeddine Echahidi, Suleiman M Kharabsheh, Fakhr Ayoubi, Kamal Alghalayni, Jehad Alburaiki, Bahaa Fadel, Feras Bader, Hadi Skouri, Mohamad Hamade, Marta Załęska-Kocięcka, Federica Guidetti, Nathan Mewton, Dania Mohty
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) poses global diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, with potential regional differences in clinical practice that remain underexplored. This study aimed to map physician-reported HFpEF diagnostic and management practices across Europe and the Middle East-North Africa (MENA) region to identify similarities, differences and opportunities for improved care.MethodsAn independent, academically developed survey consisting of 29 questions was designed through expert collaboration to capture detailed information on physician demographics, diagnostic strategies, screening practices, pathophysiological understanding and treatment approaches for HFpEF. The survey was validated by heart failure specialists to ensure relevance and accuracy and distributed widely through professional societies, email and social media channels to reach cardiologists, general practitioners and other physicians involved in heart failure care across Europe and MENA.ResultsA total of 723 physicians participated (77 from MENA, 646 from Europe). The proportion of heart failure specialists was higher in Europe (26%) than in MENA (12%, p<0.001), while general cardiologists comprised a larger share in MENA (81% vs 59%, p<0.001). Natriuretic peptide testing use was lower in MENA (82%) compared with Europe (92%, p=0.023). Pharmacotherapy preferences showed both regional similarities and differences, with SGLT2 inhibitors being the most preferred drug of choice universally. In contrast, there was a higher ranking of mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists in Europe (p=0.007) and greater reported use of angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors in MENA (p=0.03).ConclusionThis large international survey offers a descriptive mapping of HFpEF care practices across Europe and MENA, revealing overall guideline alignment but also regional differences in diagnostic test use and pharmacotherapy preferences. These findings underscore the need for targeted education, improved diagnostic and therapeutic access and tailored guideline implementation to ensure equitable, evidence-based HFpEF management across diverse healthcare settings.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) represents a global health burden as it affects approximately 64 million people worldwide, accounting for the primary cause of hospitalisations in individuals aged 65 and over.1 2 Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) accounts for approximately 50% of all HF cases, and the prevalence of this particular subtype is expected to increase, leading to it becoming the predominant subtype of HF in the coming years.3 4 HFpEF poses a similar 5-year all-cause mortality and hospital readmission rate compared with HF with reduced ejection fraction (EF).5 Indeed, HFpEF represents a disease that is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, limited quality of life and tremendous healthcare resource utilisation.6

HFpEF remains a significantly underdiagnosed entity.7 8 This is due to the diagnostic challenge that HFpEF poses, as there remains a lack of global consensus on the specific criteria and definitions required to diagnose HFpEF. Moreover, the non-specific symptoms, coupled with the older and comorbid patient demographic typically associated with HFpEF, lead to a sizeable proportion of these patients being managed by non-specialists such as primary care or internal medicine physicians, further complicating the diagnostic process.9 10

The management and treatment of HFpEF requires consistent collaboration between non-specialists (eg, primary care and internal medicine physicians) and HF specialists. Primary care physicians and internal medicine doctors are encouraged to promptly identify HFpEF, initiate appropriate diagnostic tests and treatments following medical guidelines, manage comorbidities and refer to a cardiologist when necessary.11 On the other hand, HF specialists are tasked with confirming the diagnosis of HFpEF and optimising treatment according to medical guidelines.12 Nonetheless, significant global variations remain in the knowledge and attitudes of non-specialist and specialist HF physicians when it comes to diagnosing and treating HFpEF.13 14 According to the 2019 Lancet Global Burden of Disease study, the Middle East-North Africa (MENA) region has the fifth-highest regional prevalence of HF.15 In comparison to the developed European region, the MENA region has a distinct population of patients with HFpEF with unique demographic and comorbidity profiles. In addition, the MENA region tends to display poorer healthcare infrastructure and a lack of standardised guidelines for the treatment of HFpEF when compared with its European counterpart. In this study, we sought to conduct a descriptive comparison of physicians’ practices towards HFpEF diagnosis and treatment between Europe and the MENA region. Understanding these patterns can help inform targeted strategies to improve HFpEF care through locally adapted guidelines, education and policy planning.

Methods

Survey preparation and distribution

An independent academic survey was developed and validated through collaboration between HF experts from various institutions, including cardiologists from the Pontifical Bolivarian University, specialists from the Heart Failure Association (HFA) and alumni of the Zurich Post-Graduate Course in Heart Failure (PCHF). The initial design of the survey took place in early 2023, with a dedicated group of HF specialists rigorously reviewing and refining the questionnaire to ensure that it reflected the latest research in the field. The validation process, completed by April 2023, contributed to adjusting the survey questions, ensuring relevance and accuracy in addressing contemporary HF diagnostics and management strategies.

The final version of the survey comprised 29 questions designed to gather comprehensive data on key areas relevant to HF care. These areas included participant characteristics (eg, age, sex, clinical specialty, country), diagnostic approaches (such as the use of natriuretic peptides (NP), echocardiographic parameters, HFpEF scores and diastolic stress tests), the pathophysiological understanding of HF, daily clinical practice phenotypes and pharmacological treatment strategies. The detailed structure of the survey, including its questions, is provided in online supplemental table S1 and appendix S1.

The validated survey was published on the Google Docs platform and launched globally to ensure widespread participation among HF practitioners. All survey questions were marked as required fields, ensuring that there were no missing data in the responses analysed. To maximise reach, initial outreach efforts were made through collaborations with scientific societies in cardiology, including the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), and the HFA and PCHF networks. The survey was disseminated via email, social media platforms and mailing lists, and participants were encouraged to share the survey with colleagues managing patients with HF, such as cardiologists, general practitioners (GP), geriatricians and internal medicine doctors. This broad approach helped ensure the inclusion of a diverse group of physicians from various specialties involved in HF care.

The survey, accessible in English, Spanish and Russian to minimise language barriers and foster inclusivity, was available from 1 May 2023 to 30 June 2023. It aimed to collect insights from a global pool of medical professionals and facilitate the creation of a unified database reflecting contemporary HF management.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted for each item in the questionnaire. Respondents were classified into two geographical groups: Europe versus MENA. Survey participation required responses to all items, resulting in complete data from all participants with no missing data. Continuous variables were screened for normality within each region using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were summarised as mean±SD and compared with Welch’s t-test. Skewed variables were expressed as median (IQR) and compared with the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test. Ordinal ranking items were treated as non-parametric and analysed similarly to non-normal continuous variables, with results reported as mean rank (95% CI). The mean rank is the average numerical rank assigned by respondents; lower values indicate higher perceived importance. Categorical variables were presented as counts (percentages) or percentages (95% CIs). Two-by-two tables were evaluated with Pearson’s χ² test; Fisher’s exact test replaced χ² when any cell count was <5. To control the false discovery rate (FDR) across all bivariate comparisons, raw p values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. All p values are reported as adjusted values (FDR-p). A two-sided FDR-p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata V.18 and GraphPad Prism V.10.0.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 723 physicians participated in the survey, with 77 from the MENA region and 646 from Europe (table 1). The median ages of participants were 43 years (37–53) in the MENA cohort and 41 years (35–48) among European respondents (p=0.08). Male representation was significantly higher among MENA respondents (78%, 95% CI 67% to 86%) compared with 55% (95% CI 51% to 58%) in the European cohort (p=0.004). Most respondents were cardiologists, comprising 81% (95% CI 70% to 88%) in the MENA region and 59% (95% CI 55% to 63%) in Europe (p<0.001), while HF specialists accounted for 12% (95% CI 6% to 21%) in MENA compared with 26% (95% CI 23% to 30%) in Europe (p<0.001).

Income distribution showed a significantly higher proportion of high-income respondents in Europe (82%, 95% CI 79% to 85%) compared with the MENA region (62%, 95% CI 51% to 72%; p<0.001). Medical practice characteristics were similar between regions, with academic institutions employing 52% (95% CI 41% to 63%) of MENA respondents and 61% (95% CI 57% to 64%) in Europe (p=0.22).

The proportion of patients with HFpEF among total patients with HF seen in daily practice was similar between regions, with MENA physicians reporting a median proportion of 37% (95% CI 33% to 41%) and European physicians 38% (95% CI 36% to 39%; p=0.24).

Regarding which specialties managed patients with HFpEF, cardiology specialists represented the primary providers in both cohorts: 79% (95% CI 69% to 87%) in MENA and 59% (95% CI 55% to 63%) in Europe (p=0.006). Internal medicine specialists accounted for 14% (95% CI 8% to 24%) in MENA and 20% (95% CI 17% to 23%) in Europe (p=0.49). Finally, primary care physicians represented 5% (95% CI 2% to 13%) in MENA and 17% (95% CI 14% to 20%) in Europe (p=0.024).

Diagnostic workup

The proportion of physicians using a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) threshold of >50% to define HFpEF was similar between the MENA cohort (58%, 95% CI 49% to 63%) and Europe (70%, 95% CI 67% to 72%; p=0.09) (online supplemental table S2). Utilisation of specific echocardiographic parameters for HFpEF diagnosis, such as left atrial volume index, left ventricular mass and E/e’, was comparable in both regions (MENA: 86%, 95% CI 76% to 92%; Europe: 80%, 95% CI 77% to 83%; p=0.47) (figure 1A). NP use was significantly lower among MENA physicians (82%, 95% CI72% to 89%) compared with their European counterparts (92%, 95% CI 90% to 94%; p=0.023). Both regions reported limited use of diastolic stress tests (MENA: 22%, 95% CI 14% to 33%; Europe: 23%, 95% CI 20% to 26%; p=0.97). The use of HFpEF scores was similar between the cohorts (p=0.57). A notable proportion of physicians in both regions did not use any scoring tools (MENA: 62%, 95% CI 51% to 72%; Europe: 60%, 95% CI 56% to 64%) (figure 1B).

Specialists’ approaches to specific cardiomyopathies that present with HF-like symptoms, preserved EF and requiring disease-modifying therapies varied, reflecting both shared practices and distinct differences across the MENA and European cohorts (table 2). The patterns of coronary artery disease (CAD) screening were broadly similar between the two groups (p=0.115). Screening was most commonly performed in symptomatic patients (MENA: 42%, 95% CI 31% to 53%; Europe: 44%, 95% CI 40% to 48%), followed by asymptomatic individuals at high cardiovascular risk (MENA: 22%, 95% CI 14% to 33%; Europe: 25%, 95% CI 22% to 29%) and routine screening in all patients (MENA: 31%, 95% CI 22% to 42%; Europe: 30%, 95% CI 26% to 33%). Cardiac amyloidosis (CA) screening practices showed more variation (p=0.022). Left ventricular hypertrophy remained the most common criterion for initiating CA screening in both groups, reported by 42% (95% CI 31% to 53%) of MENA respondents and 47% (95% CI 43% to 51%) in Europe (p=0.77). There was no significant difference in the proportion of respondents screening all patients with HFpEF for CA between MENA (16%, 95% CI 9% to 25%) and Europe (8%, 95% CI 6% to 11%; p=0.12). MENA respondents were significantly more likely to use conduction system disorders as a screening criterion (6%, 95% CI 3% to 14%) compared with Europe (1%, 95% CI 1% to 3%; p=0.04). Finally, the proportion of respondents reporting no screening for CA did not differ significantly between the regions (MENA: 10%, 95% CI 5% to 19%; Europe: 17%, 95% CI 14% to 20%; p=0.17).

Pathophysiology and phenotypes

Both regions demonstrated comparable rankings in the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying HFpEF (figure 2A). Chronic inflammation, vascular dysfunction, and fibrosis, as well as systemic involvement, were consistently highlighted as key contributors, with less emphasis on atrial myopathy and arrhythmias. Both cohorts reported similar patterns regarding the most frequently observed HFpEF phenotypes in clinical practice, with the two most common phenotypes identified as ‘elderly/arterial hypertension/low exercise tolerance’ and ‘obesity/diabetes/arterial hypertension/lack of exercise’ (figure 2B).

Pharmacological treatment

In an ideal setting with access to all HF medications, SGLT2 inhibitors were the most preferred option in both regions, with mean ranks of 2.73 (95% CI 2.23 to 3.22) for the MENA cohort and 2.31 (95% CI 2.16 to 2.46) for the European cohort (p=0.29). Loop diuretics were the second most preferred treatment, showing mean ranks of 3.03 (95% CI 2.58 to 3.47) for MENA and 2.62 (95% CI 2.49 to 2.75) for Europe (p=0.26). ACE inhibitors (ACEi) followed with mean ranks of 3.08 (95% CI 2.68 to 3.47) for MENA and 3.10 (95% CI 2.96 to 3.23) for Europe (p=0.96). Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA) showed a significant preference difference, being more favourably ranked by the European cohort (mean rank 3.16, 95% CI 3.03 to 3.30) compared with the MENA cohort (3.86, 95% CI 3.45 to 4.26; p=0.007). The remaining treatments, including beta blockers (BBs), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), had similar preference rankings across both groups without significant differences. Wide CIs were noted in the MENA data, indicating substantial variability in treatment choices (figure 3A, online supplemental table S2).

When constrained to select only one medication for HFpEF treatment, preferences were broadly similar between respondents from the MENA region and Europe (overall p=0.21). SGLT2 inhibitors emerged as the most frequently chosen option, reported by 45% of MENA respondents (95% CI 35% to 57%) and 51% of European participants (95% CI 47% to 55%; p=0.64). Loop diuretics were the second most preferred therapy, with 26% (95% CI 17% to 37%) in the MENA group and 23% (95% CI 20% to 26%) in Europe (p=0.81). MRAs were selected by 6% (95% CI 3% to 14%) in MENA and 5% (95% CI 4% to 7%) in Europe (p=0.80). ARNIs were reported more frequently among MENA respondents (10%, 95% CI 5% to 19%) compared with Europe (3%, 95% CI 2% to 5%), reflecting a significant difference (p=0.03). BBs, ACEis and ARBs were infrequently selected, with no notable regional differences (figure 3B, online supplemental table S2).

Discussion

This international survey provides a descriptive assessment of physician-reported practices for the diagnosis and management of HFpEF across Europe and the MENA region. By capturing perspectives from over 700 clinicians, the study offers important baseline data on real-world HFpEF care in two regions with diverse health system capacities, economic contexts and specialist availability. While many diagnostic and treatment practices appeared broadly aligned with international guideline recommendations, significant differences in specific aspects of care were also observed. Mapping these similarities and differences is essential to identify areas for harmonisation, inform targeted educational strategies and guide policy initiatives aimed at improving equitable access to evidence-based HFpEF management.

Differences in participant characteristics between the European and MENA cohorts are important for interpreting the observed practice patterns. The European sample included a significantly higher proportion of HF specialists, while respondents from MENA were more often general cardiologists with fewer HF specialists overall. This difference may reflect variations in workforce training and availability of dedicated HF services, as highlighted in regional expert consensus, which notes that many MENA hospitals lack formal HF clinics and rely on general cardiologists for advanced HF care.15 The greater presence of internal medicine specialists and GPs among European respondents aligns with healthcare systems that emphasise multidisciplinary and primary care-driven models, including gatekeeper roles for GPs in socialised systems.16 Such integration may support broader awareness and earlier identification of HFpEF, particularly given its prevalence in older adults with multiple comorbidities. Additionally, the higher proportion of respondents from high-income countries in Europe suggests differences in health system resources, access to advanced diagnostics and availability of guideline-directed therapies. Together, these differences in provider type, specialty training and socioeconomic context are likely to influence the diagnostic strategies and treatment patterns reported in our survey, underscoring the importance of tailoring guideline implementation and educational efforts to local healthcare realities.

Diagnostic practices for HFpEF in both Europe and the MENA region reflected overall alignment with international guidelines, but also revealed persistent gaps that may affect early identification and management. In our survey, both groups reported routine use of echocardiography and largely adopted an LVEF threshold of ≥50% to define HFpEF, consistent with ESC and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) recommendations.17 18 However, a significant difference was observed in the reported use of NPtesting. The recent ESC guidelines emphasise the pivotal role of NP testing in improving HF diagnosis and management.17 For HFpEF specifically, the diagnosis requires a combination of characteristic symptoms, LVEF ≥50%, evidence of congestion detected through elevated NP levels and functional or structural heart abnormalities confirmed via echocardiography. Our results illustrate that physicians in the MENA region used NPs to a lower extent when compared with their European counterparts. The lower use of NPs in the MENA region may stem from limited availability of these tests in non-specialist settings or differences in the prioritisation of diagnostic tools due to cost concerns or knowledge deficiencies. Indeed, these findings are supported by data from HF registries within the MENA region. For instance, in the I PREFER registry, encompassing participants from MENA, BNP concentration measurements were documented in only 3.8% of cases.19 Likewise, NP testing was either entirely absent or conducted in very limited numbers among Egyptian patients enrolled in the DELTA-HF registry and the ESC-HF-LT-R, respectively.20 21 Given that guidelines increasingly emphasise these scores to improve diagnostic precision, these results underscore the need for investment in diagnostic capacity, simplified clinical pathways and targeted education to support early and accurate HFpEF diagnosis, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Advanced diagnostic assessment for HFpEF remains underused across both Europe and MENA, despite echocardiography and NP levels being key indicators, with invasive haemodynamic testing as the diagnostic gold standard.22 Over 75% of respondents reported not using diastolic stress testing, highlighting barriers such as limited clinician awareness, lack of training, restricted facility access, costs and absence of standardised protocols. Adoption of HFpEF-specific scoring systems was similarly low (>60% non-use), reflecting their perceived complexity, need for advanced imaging and inconsistent familiarity among generalists.23 24 Addressing these gaps will require targeted clinician education, simplified diagnostic algorithms and investments in diagnostic capacity to make these tools more practical for routine use. Once HFpEF is diagnosed, screening for underlying cardiomyopathies is also essential. While CAD screening practices were similar across regions, MENA physicians reported more frequent universal screening for CA, whereas European respondents used a more selective, marker-guided approach. This may reflect the composition of respondents but also emphasises the importance of recognising conditions with disease-modifying therapies, given the consequences of delayed or missed diagnosis.25 Notably, our survey found no significant differences in physicians’ conceptual understanding of HFpEF pathophysiology, with broad recognition of its multifactorial mechanisms involving obesity, diabetes and hypertension. However, higher regional prevalence of these risk factors in MENA populations underscores the need for tailored prevention strategies, improved diagnostic pathways and context-specific management approaches to effectively address local patient profiles.15

Treatment practices for HFpEF in our survey reflected broad alignment with contemporary guideline recommendations, with both European and MENA physicians identifying SGLT2 inhibitors as a preferred therapy. This is consistent with strong evidence from EMPEROR-Preserved and DELIVER trials demonstrating reductions in HF hospitalisations and cardiovascular death, leading to class IA recommendations in both ESC and ACC/AHA guidelines.17 18 26 27 However, notable differences emerged: European respondents ranked MRAssignificantly higher, while MENA physicians reported significantly greater use of ARNIs. These variations may reflect differences in local guideline adaptation, familiarity with specific trial evidence and formulary availability. Both MRA and ARNI received a class IIB recommendation for HFpEF treatment in the ACC/AHA guidelines based on modest evidence for reducing HF hospitalisations, while they are not formally recommended in the ESC guidelines.18 28 29 For example, MRAs have established benefits in reducing HF hospitalisations, supported by studies such as TOPCAT, while ARNI use, despite cost considerations, may indicate selective adoption in certain MENA centres, potentially driven by access in higher income Gulf states or targeted prescribing for specific patient subgroups.28 Broader access and affordability challenges remain critical barriers, particularly in MENA, where national formularies and reimbursement policies often limit availability of newer HF therapies.15 The WHO’s essential medicines framework highlights these disparities, with many low- and middle-income countries prioritising older HF treatments over newer, evidence-based options.30 Beyond pharmacotherapy, clinicians must also manage comorbidities and employ non-pharmacological strategies such as lifestyle modification, physical activity and weight management, consistent with guideline recommendations. Addressing these gaps will require policy initiatives to improve drug availability and affordability, updated national treatment protocols reflecting the latest evidence and targeted clinician education to support consistent, rational prescribing. These efforts are essential to ensure equitable, evidence-based HFpEF care across diverse health systems.

This study offers important strengths, including its large, international scope and direct clinician-reported insights into HFpEF diagnosis and management across Europe and the MENA region, which has not been systematically compared in prior work. By including over 700 responses and applying rigorous statistical adjustments for multiple testing, the analysis provides a transparent, descriptive mapping of current practice patterns, serving as valuable baseline data to guide guideline adaptation and policy planning. However, several limitations warrant consideration. The voluntary, self-selected nature of the survey introduces potential selection bias, likely over-representing cardiologists and HF specialists who may practise closer to guidelines, thereby limiting generalisability to broader primary care settings. The sample size imbalance, with far fewer respondents from MENA, reduces power to detect differences and risks under-representing the diversity of healthcare systems within the region, which includes both high-income Gulf states and lower income North African countries. Treating MENA as a homogeneous group may obscure important within-region variability in practice patterns, access to diagnostics and medication availability. Additionally, reliance on self-reported practices introduces recall and social desirability biases and may not fully reflect actual prescribing or diagnostic behaviour in clinical settings. While correction for multiple comparisons appropriately limited false positives, it also resulted in many differences losing statistical significance, underscoring the exploratory, hypothesis-generating nature of the analysis. Finally, survey distribution methods and language availability may have further constrained representativeness. Recognising these limitations is essential for interpreting our findings and highlights the need for future work using larger, more balanced regional samples and prospective data collection to better understand and address disparities in HFpEF care.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this international survey offers a descriptive overview of HFpEF diagnostic and treatment practices across Europe and MENA, highlighting both broad guideline alignment and important regional differences. While SGLT2 inhibitors were widely preferred in both regions, significant variations in MRA and ARNI ranking suggest differences in guideline adaptation, training and access. Diagnostic practices were generally consistent with guidelines but showed lower NP testing use in MENA, reflecting cost and availability barriers. Limited adoption of scoring systems and diastolic stress testing across regions underscores the need for education, simplified diagnostic pathways and better diagnostic capacity. Despite a shared understanding of HFpEF’s complex, comorbidity-driven pathophysiology, regional differences in risk factor prevalence and healthcare resources emphasise the need for tailored guideline implementation and policy planning. These insights can inform targeted education, improved access to diagnostics and therapies, and harmonisation of HFpEF care, while future research should validate these findings in larger, more balanced samples.
