Sarcopenia Definitions and Diagnostic Challenges Across Diverse Clinical Populations: A Scoping Review
Authors: Amirahmad Nassiri, Minoo Heidari Almasi, Seyed Amirhossein Salehi, Azin Ebrahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsSarcopenia, characterized by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, function, and strength, has evolved from a descriptive term to a recognized disease with significant implications for individual health and healthcare systems globally.MethodsThis scoping review explores the evolution of sarcopenia definitions and diagnostic criteria and the challenges of diagnosing sarcopenia in diverse clinical populations, including chronic kidney disease (CKD), cancer, liver disease, heart failure, and obesity.ResultsA literature review was conducted using PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, focusing on studies published between 1995 and 2024. Database searches identified 1200 studies. After screening titles and abstracts, 238 studies were selected for full‐text review. Ultimately, 36 of these studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final evaluation.The review highlights the variability in diagnostic criteria proposed by various working groups, such as the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) and the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), and discusses the challenges in defining sarcopenia in specific clinical conditions.ConclusionThe findings underscore the need for condition‐specific definitions and standardized diagnostic tools to improve patient outcomes and advance research in sarcopenia.

Introduction

The term sarcopenia is derived from the Greek words “sarx” (muscle) and “penia” (loss) [1]. It refers to a syndrome characterized by the progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, function, and strength [2]. First conceptualized as a disease by Rosenberg in 1989 [3], has been a topic of considerable progress in the past few decades, transitioning from a descriptive term to a defined disease that has an ICD‐10‐CM code specific to it (M62. 84) in 2016 [4]. This designation was a landmark advancement that established sarcopenia as a treatable condition with far‐reaching implications, not only for individual health but for healthcare systems globally [5].

An improved understanding of sarcopenia is largely based on the increasing evidence of its association with a greater susceptibility to comorbid conditions, functional decline and death, especially in aging populations [6]. Although classically associated with aging, sarcopenia is increasingly recognized across a wide spectrum of chronic diseases independent of chronological age, and its diagnosis in a wide array of clinical conditions, such as CKD, cancer, liver disease, heart failure, and others, is rising [7]. These different conditions introduce challenges in defining and diagnosing sarcopenia empirically, given that the underlying pathophysiology of each condition is different, including the different contributing aspects such as inflammation and metabolic derangements, as well as disease‐specific complications like fluid overload or cachexia [8].

To standardize the definition of sarcopenia, several working groups have proposed diagnostic criteria over the years, including the EWGSOP, the AWGS, and other groups [6, 7]. However, controversies remain on the population‐specificity of diagnostic thresholds and inclusion of functional (rather than solely muscle mass) endpoints [9, 10].

In addition to clinical diagnostic challenges, the translation of mechanistic findings from preclinical studies depends on the use of well‐characterized and standardized animal models [11]. Variability in model selection, age definitions, housing conditions, and phenotypic endpoints may contribute to inconsistencies across studies and can limit the reproducibility and clinical translation of sarcopenia research [12, 13].

This scoping review systematically maps how these issues differ across individual conditions, such as CKD, cancer, and liver disease [14]. By employing a scoping review framework, this paper argues for greater attention to condition‐specific definitions and standardized diagnostic tools to improve patient outcomes and advance research in this important area of medicine [15].

Methods

Study Design

This study was conducted as a scoping review following the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for scoping reviews and reported in accordance with the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) [16].

Search Strategy

A systematic search was conducted on December 31, 2024, across four electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. In brief, the search combined terms related to sarcopenia (e.g., “sarcopenia definition,” “diagnostic criteria,” “muscle mass,” “muscle strength”) with condition‐specific terms (e.g., “chronic kidney disease,” “cancer,” “liver disease,” “heart failure,” “obesity”).

Animal studies were included only when they provided mechanistic insights not available from human data. When interpreting evidence from preclinical studies, consideration was given to the potential impact of differences in animal model selection and experimental standardization on the generalizability and translational relevance of findings [8, 17].

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Briefly, studies were included if they were published in English between January 1, 1995, and December 31, 2024, and addressed the definition, diagnostic criteria, or disease‐specific context of sarcopenia in human populations. Animal studies were included only when they provided mechanistic insights not available from human data. Exclusion criteria included non‐English publications, editorials/commentaries, and studies irrelevant to sarcopenia definition or diagnosis.

Screening and Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers (S.A.S. and A.E.) screened titles and abstracts, followed by full‐text review. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer (M.H.A.). The selection process is summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Results and Discussion

A total of 1200 studies were identified through database searches. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 238 studies were selected for full‐text review. Of these, 36 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The selection process is summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1).

Historical Perspective

The term sarcopenia was initially introduced by Rosenberg in 1989, derived from the Greek roots: sarx (muscle) and penia (loss) to describe the progressive age‐dependent loss of skeletal muscle mass. He proposed the concept after comparing thigh lean muscle mass between a young woman and an older woman [1, 18]. He also noted the remarkable ability of muscles to recover and regain function [3]. Subsequently, in 1994, William J. emphasized on the correlation between muscle mass decline and muscle strength reduction in older adults [4, 9, 19]. The significance of sarcopenia was immediately highlighted due to its individual and economic impacts; however, it was not until 2000 that the world health organization (WHO) recognized sarcopenia as a risk factor for increased susceptibility to various comorbidities in aging individuals [10, 11, 20]. In 2016, sarcopenia gained recognition as an official disease with its own ICD‐10‐CM code (M62.84) [2]. This classification represented a crucial advancement, establishing sarcopenia as a treatable condition [21].

Current Diagnostic Criteria of Sarcopenia (In Order of the Time of Introduction)

ESPEN SIG (2010): The first formal diagnostic framework came from the Special Interest Group (SIG) of the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN). This group pioneered a dual‐criteria approach requiring both (a) muscle mass percentage ≥ 2 standard deviations below the mean of young adults, and (b) gait speed < 0.8 m/s. The choice of gait speed was clinically motivated by its strong association with fall risk and mobility disability in older populations [5, 22].

EWGSOP 2010: Building on ESPEN's work, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People expanded the framework to include three assessment domains: muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance [12, 23]. Unlike ESPEN's binary approach, EWGSOP recommended multiple measurement options grip strength or knee flexion/extension for strength, and SPPB, walking speed, or TUG (Timed Up and Go), for performance. The thresholds were set at two standard deviations below young adult norms: grip strength < 30 kg for men and < 20 kg for women, lean mass < 7.23 kg/h2 for men and < 5.67 kg/h2 for women, and gait speed ≤ 0.8 m/s [13, 14, 24]. This multi‐domain approach was intended to capture different aspects of sarcopenia pathophysiology.

According to the International Working Group on Sarcopenia (IWGS) standardization, sarcopenia is present with walking speed slower than 1 m/s combined with low appendicular lean mass relative to height squared (≤ 5.67 kg/m2 for women and ≤ 7.23 kg/m2 for men) measured via Dual‐Energy X‐ray Absorptiometry (DXA) scanning [3, 25].

The Society for Sarcopenia, Cachexia and Wasting Disorders (SCWD) established their own sarcopenia definition, to create clinically applicable standards, defining sarcopenia as muscle mass loss with reduced mobility, with diagnostic criteria including Appendicular Lean Mass (ALM) relative to height falling two standard deviations below young adult means, plus either walking speed of ≤ 1 m/s or covering less than 400 m in a 6‐min walk test [3, 15, 16, 26] (Figure 2).

Timeline of evolution of sarcopenia diagnostic criteria.

In 2014, the AWGS proposed their consensus definition of sarcopenia, determining diagnostic cutoff values based on evidence from Asian populations. They advised assessing ALM using DXA (< 5.4 kg/m2 for women, < 7.0 kg/m2 for men) or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (< 5.7 kg/m2 for women, < 7.0 kg/m2 for men), with low muscle strength defined as handgrip strength < 18 kg for women and < 26 kg for men, and gait speed ≤ 0.8 m/s as the threshold for low physical performance [6, 27].

The Foundation for the National Institutes of Health introduced another definition in 2014, deriving clinically relevant cutoffs from a large, diverse population [28]. Their threshold for low lean mass was < 0.512 Appendicular Lean Mass indexed by Body Mass Index (ALMBMI) for women and < 0.789 ALMBMI for men, with grip strength cutoffs of < 16 kg for women and < 26 kg for men [7, 29].

In 2018, the EWGSOP updated its definition, emphasizing muscle strength while maintaining three criteria: strength, quantity/quality, and physical function. They introduced a staging system where probable sarcopenia is diagnosed with low strength alone, confirmed sarcopenia requires low strength plus low muscle quantity/quality, and severe sarcopenia occurs when all three criteria are deficient [30]. For grip strength, thresholds are below 16 kg for women and under 27 kg for men, with chair stand assessment as an alternative measure [7].

Subsequently, The AWGS 2019 introduced several key updates to improve the early detection and diagnosis of sarcopenia compared to the AWGS 2014 criteria, the new framework classifies sarcopenia into three stages: probable, confirmed, and severe. Additionally, the handgrip strength cutoff for men was raised from 26 kg to 28 kg, and the gait speed threshold was increased from ≤ 0.8 m/s to ≤ 1.0 m/s to enhance sensitivity. The Chair Stand Test (≥ 12 s) [6, 31].

The Sarcopenia Definitions and Outcomes Consortium (SDOC) proposed a simplified definition in 2020 [8]. Separately, the ICFSR published evidence‐based clinical practice guidelines in 2018, which endorsed existing diagnostic definitions (EWGSOP, Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH)) rather than proposing a new one [9, 32]. A subsequent ICFSR Task Force report (2022) addressed drug development challenges and is not a diagnostic definition [10, 33]. Both criteria emphasize muscle strength and function as key diagnostic markers; however, SDOC simplifies the diagnosis by excluding muscle mass and relying solely on handgrip strength and a gait speed of less than 1.0 m/s, which facilitates easier application in clinical settings [34]. In contrast, ICFSR incorporates muscle mass measurements (DXA, BIA) and integrates frailty and inflammatory biomarkers, providing a more comprehensive assessment [35]. Additionally, ICFSR sets a lower gait speed threshold of 0.8 m/s or less and employs multiple physical performance tests, SPPB and TUG, which SDOC does not include [11, 36].

Controversies and Challenges in Defining Sarcopenia

One of the many controversies is its definition: should sarcopenia be classified as a disease or syndrome? Supporters of the disease classification say the behavior matches the definition of a pathology with identifiable symptoms and health consequences [12, 37]. In contrast, other researchers see it as a syndrome, highlighting its layered pathophysiology and its association with aging, chronic conditions, and lifestyle factors [13, 38]. This debate has implications for research, clinical practice, and health care policy, because disease classification often has an impact on funding, treatment protocols, and public awareness of the disease [39].

Another challenge is the variability in diagnostic thresholds across different populations [40]. Current definitions and criteria for sarcopenia, such as those proposed by the WGSOP and the AWGS, differ in their cut‐off values for muscle mass, strength, and physical performance [6, 7]. These discrepancies stem from variations in population characteristics, including ethnicity, body composition, and lifestyle. This variability complicates the establishment of universal diagnostic standards, hindering global comparisons and consistency in research [41].

A recurring challenge across chronic diseases is that the most important barriers to sarcopenia diagnosis are often methodological rather than biological [42]. Although inflammation and metabolic dysregulation contribute to muscle wasting in many conditions, the practical application of diagnostic criteria is limited by measurement heterogeneity across modalities such as DXA, BIA, CT, and MRI [43]. Device‐specific differences, variations in assessment protocols, and disease‐related factors, including fluid overload, edema, and ascites, can substantially affect estimates of muscle mass and body composition [44]. Furthermore, most currently used diagnostic thresholds were developed in community‐dwelling older adults and have not been validated in populations with CKD, cancer, cirrhosis, heart failure, or sarcopenic obesity. Consequently, applying general‐population criteria to these conditions may result in diagnostic misclassification and inconsistent prevalence estimates [45]. These limitations highlight the need for standardized measurement protocols and disease‐specific diagnostic cutoffs to improve the accuracy and clinical utility of sarcopenia diagnosis [46].

Challenges in Defining Sarcopenia Among Patients With CKD

As previously discussed, sarcopenia is defined according to the standards set by the EWGSOP and the AWGS. These definitions encompass low muscle mass, low muscle strength, and low physical performance. However, these criteria must be adjusted for patients with CKD due to the underlying pathophysiology of kidney disease [4]. The lack of standardized criteria for diagnosing sarcopenia in CKD complicates its application in clinical practice and research. While definitions like EWGSOP were initially developed to predict mobility loss in aging populations, their applicability to CKD patients is questionable, as CKD accelerates aging processes and disproportionately affects muscle health [23]. Therefore, current cutoffs for identifying sarcopenia in the general population may not suit CKD or dialysis patients, emphasizing the need for CKD‐specific criteria.

Fluid overload, a common consequence of CKD, can obscure actual muscle loss when relying solely on muscle mass measurements [47]. Additionally, uremic toxins and metabolic acidosis—two features prevalent in advanced CKD directly inhibit muscle protein synthesis and accelerate muscle degradation, further complicating assessment [4].

A factor that is usually left unseen when assessing sarcopenia in CKD and End‐Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) patients is the fact that sarcopenia in the stated populations is different from the age‐related sarcopenia in terms of underlying metabolic abnormalities and changes in body fat and muscle fibers [48]. Age‐related sarcopenia involves a gradual loss of muscle mass and function, primarily due to reduced protein synthesis and the loss of type II muscle fibers. In contrast, CKD‐related sarcopenia is more severe, with increased protein degradation, elevated energy expenditure, and atrophy in both type I and II fibers [4]. Inflammation and insulin resistance are more common among CKD patients, worsening muscle wasting compared to aging [49]. While aging sarcopenia often leads to increased body fat, CKD‐related sarcopenia shows variable fat changes, sometimes resulting in fat loss [14]. These differences highlight CKD's unique metabolic challenges, like chronic inflammation, which exacerbate muscle loss beyond normal aging [15, 16]. Recognizing these distinctions is key to addressing CKD‐induced sarcopenia effectively [50].

Another significant factor in CKD is the prominent role of inflammation and malnutrition, both of which are frequently associated with muscle wasting [17]. In CKD, the term “protein‐energy wasting” (PEW) describes the combined effects of insufficient nutrient intake, metabolic disorders, and inflammation on muscle and body composition [18]. The overlap between sarcopenia and PEW in CKD patients may necessitate broader diagnostic approaches that include nutritional and inflammatory markers [16, 19]. Efforts have been made to characterize CKD‐specific sarcopenia. For example, additional biomarkers such as serum creatinine or cystatin C, along with urine urea nitrogen, have been proposed to more accurately estimate muscle mass in patients with CKD [17, 20].

Emerging evidence suggests that the interaction between chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction in CKD may be mediated, in part, by immunometabolic reprogramming [21]. Alterations in mitochondrial metabolism and autophagic flux within tissue‐resident macrophages can influence the balance between pro‐inflammatory and reparative phenotypes, thereby contributing to persistent inflammation, PEW, insulin resistance, and muscle loss [22]. This emerging framework provides a more integrated understanding of CKD‐associated sarcopenia beyond the effects of individual cytokines or metabolic abnormalities alone [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28].

In CKD, the primary diagnostic barriers are not mechanistic but practical. Fluid overload common in advanced CKD and dialysis causes DXA and BIA to overestimate lean mass, masking true sarcopenia [13]. Measurement heterogeneity across devices (DXA vs. BIA vs. computed tomography scan (CT)) and protocols (pre‐ vs. post‐dialysis, supine vs. standing) produces non‐comparable results [18]. Validated condition‐specific cutoffs are absent; applying EWGSOP thresholds to CKD patients may misclassify up to 30% [19]. PEW overlaps with sarcopenia but adds nutritional/inflammatory criteria (low albumin, low body mass index (BMI)) that may improve risk prediction [20]. Emerging approaches include using serum creatinine or cystatin C as surrogate markers of muscle mass, though these require adjustment for CKD [21].

One of the major challenges in defining sarcopenia in patients with CKD is the lack of consensus on diagnostic thresholds [22]. Muscle mass can be evaluated using tools such as DXA and BIA [13]. However, because body fluid and composition can vary in CKD, these results may be imprecise [23]. Furthermore, muscle strength, often measured by handgrip strength, may be influenced by complications of uremia or comorbidities, such as diabetes mellitus, which are common among CKD patients [24]. Physical performance tests, such as gait speed and the SPPB, are also relevant but may be affected by high rates of fatigue and physical deconditioning in this population [25].

Historically, sarcopenia was defined primarily by the loss of muscle mass. Still, recent evidence suggests that muscle strength is a stronger predictor of adverse outcomes, such as disability, poor quality of life (QoL), and mortality [26]. In CKD patients, low muscle strength has been shown to predict higher rates of hospitalization and mortality more effectively than low muscle mass alone [27]. Muscle quality, rather than just quantity, also plays a critical role [30]. Studies have indicated that interventions targeting muscle strength, such as resistance exercise, have demonstrated improvements in physical health and function, even without significant changes in muscle mass [28]. So, we can conclude that in CKD, muscle strength and physical function are more clinically relevant than muscle mass alone for predicting patient outcomes and guiding interventions. While muscle mass remains an important indicator of nutritional status, it is the loss of muscle strength and functional capacity that more directly impacts morbidity, mortality, and quality of life [29].

Challenges in Defining Sarcopenia Among Patients With Cancer

It is essential to distinguish between cancer‐associated sarcopenia and cancer cachexia, as these terms are often mistakenly used interchangeably [51].

Cancer‐associated sarcopenia refers specifically to the loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical performance in cancer patients, as defined by standard sarcopenia criteria (EWGSOP, AWGS). It does not necessarily require weight loss or metabolic derangement.

Cancer cachexia, in contrast, has a distinct consensus definition. According to the international consensus published by Fearon et al., cancer cachexia is defined as “a multifactorial syndrome characterized by an ongoing loss of skeletal muscle mass (with or without loss of fat mass) that cannot be fully reversed by conventional nutritional support and leads to progressive functional impairment” [31]. Cachexia is further staged as pre‐cachexia, cachexia, and refractory cachexia [28].

Most patients with cancer cachexia meet criteria for sarcopenia, but not all patients with cancer‐associated sarcopenia have cachexia (e.g., a patient with immobilization‐induced muscle loss without significant weight loss or inflammation) [29].

Recent advances in immunometabolism have also highlighted the role of immune‐cell metabolic reprogramming in cancer‐associated muscle wasting [52]. Changes in macrophage mitochondrial function and autophagic activity may promote a sustained pro‐inflammatory state that interacts with tumor‐induced metabolic disturbances, contributing to progressive muscle loss and functional decline [53]. Although these mechanisms remain under active investigation, they underscore the complex interplay between inflammation and metabolism that characterizes cancer‐associated sarcopenia and cachexia [28].

Unique Features of Cancer‐Associated Muscle Wasting

Cancer‐related muscle wasting (whether termed sarcopenia or cachexia) is driven by systemic inflammation, metabolic disturbances, reduced nutrient intake, and the direct effects of cancer and its treatments [30]. Unlike age‐related sarcopenia, which is a gradual process, cancer‐associated muscle wasting can develop at any age and often progresses rapidly, negatively impacting patients' quality of life, treatment tolerance, and survival outcomes [31].

Diagnostic Challenges Specific to Cancer Patients

Traditional diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia may not fully apply to cancer patients due to their unique pathophysiology. For example, cancer patients may maintain relatively preserved muscle mass while experiencing significant functional decline due to factors such as fatigue, chemotherapy side effects, or tumor‐associated metabolic changes [32]. Therefore, some experts argue that a cancer‐specific definition of sarcopenia should include additional components, such as markers of inflammation (e.g., C‐reactive protein (CRP)), nutritional status, and the stage of malignancy [33]. However, no formal consensus definition for “cancer‐associated sarcopenia” currently exists separately from the Fearon cachexia criteria.

Challenges in Defining Sarcopenia Among Obese People

The link between excess fat and muscle loss complicates the definition of sarcopenia in obese patients, referred to as sarcopenic obesity. Standard diagnostic tests, such as DXA and BIA, tend to overestimate muscle mass in obese patients because high fat mass can mask low muscle mass [13]. For instance, DXA estimates lean mass based on tissue density, but increased fat infiltration within muscles can lead to misclassification of muscle quality [34]. Similarly, BIA relies on electrical conductivity, which is influenced by hydration status and fat distribution, further complicating accurate muscle assessment in obesity [35].

Additionally, obesity‐related metabolic and inflammatory states, such as insulin resistance and chronic inflammation, exacerbate muscle wasting and functional decline, making diagnosis even more challenging [44]. Chronic low‐grade inflammation, driven by elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF‐α, IL‐6), promotes muscle protein breakdown via activation of the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway [36]. Another factor contributing to metabolic dysregulation and muscle atrophy, is the increased release of leptin and reduced adiponectin in consequence of adipose tissue dysfunction in obesity [37].

A study proposes combining methods such as CT/ Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐based measurements of intramuscular fat infiltration alongside traditional metrics to attain improved diagnostic accuracy [38]. Functional assessments, including gait speed, chair stand tests, and grip strength adjusted for body fat, may also enhance sarcopenic obesity identification [39].

Compared to either obesity or sarcopenia, sarcopenic obesity is associated with worse clinical outcomes, including higher frailty rates, physical disability, metabolic complications, and increased mortality [54, 55]. Thus, addressing these diagnostic challenges is of great importance [40]. Emerging research suggests that combining body composition analysis with functional and biochemical markers (e.g., CRP, myostatin levels) may refine sarcopenic obesity diagnosis, though further validation in diverse populations is needed [41].

Challenges in Defining Sarcopenia Among Patients With Other Conditions

In cirrhosis, ascites is the dominant diagnostic barrier. BIA markedly overestimates lean mass in patients with tense ascites; DXA is also affected, though less so. Measurement heterogeneity is severe: CT at L3 is the preferred method, but is not available in all centers; BIA and DXA have unknown accuracy in this population [56]. Hyperammonemia directly impairs muscle protein synthesis, but ammonia levels are not included in any diagnostic criteria [13]. Cirrhosis‐specific cutoffs are lacking; general population thresholds may not predict hepatic encephalopathy, transplant outcomes, or mortality [42]. The Royal Free Hospital index (a creatinine‐based equation) has been proposed but requires validation.

In heart failure, edema and cardiac cachexia create diagnostic confusion. Fluid retention causes BIA and DXA to overestimate lean mass [13]. Cardiac cachexia (involuntary weight loss > 5% in 12 months) overlaps with sarcopenia but adds a weight loss criterion not required for sarcopenia diagnosis. Functional tests (gait speed, 6‐min walk) are confounded by dyspnea and fatigue independent of muscle status; grip strength is less affected [28]. HF‐specific cutoffs are absent; general population thresholds may not predict hospitalization or mortality [43]. Serial assessments tracking mass and strength over time may be more informative than single time points [20, 57] (Table 1).

Conclusion

In conclusion, sarcopenia has evolved from a descriptive term to a recognized disease with an ICD‐10‐CM code (2016). However, translating this recognition into clinical practice faces actionable diagnostic barriers that are consistent across chronic diseases but require condition‐specific solutions.

Since its conceptualization by Rosenberg in 1989, substantial progress has been made in understanding its pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria, and impact on diverse clinical populations. However, challenges remain in defining and diagnosing sarcopenia, particularly due to its association with various chronic conditions such as CKD, cancer, liver disease, heart failure, and obesity. These conditions complicate diagnosis not only through disease‐specific pathophysiological mechanisms but also through practical challenges related to sarcopenia assessment. Measurement heterogeneity across DXA, BIA, CT, and MRI, protocol‐dependent variability, confounding by edema and ascites, and the lack of validated disease‐specific diagnostic thresholds remain major barriers to accurate diagnosis. Addressing these methodological limitations through standardized assessment protocols and condition‐specific cutoff values may be as important as advancing our understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying muscle loss.

Current diagnostic criteria proposed by groups such as the EWGSOP and the AWGS vary in their thresholds and endpoints, reflecting differences in population characteristics and disease‐specific considerations. This variability underscores the need for condition‐specific definitions and standardized diagnostic tools to improve patient outcomes and advance research. Unlike previous frameworks that assumed access to specialized equipment (DXA, BIA) and clinical settings, AWGS 2019 specifically addressed the “implementation gap” by providing a pragmatic pathway for community‐based screening and early intervention, with “possible sarcopenia” serving as a call to action for lifestyle modification rather than a final diagnosis.

Furthermore, the debate over whether sarcopenia should be classified as a disease or a syndrome highlights the necessity for a unified approach to its classification, which has implications for funding, treatment protocols, and public awareness. Moving forward, greater attention to these issues, along with the integration of biomarkers and advanced diagnostic techniques, will be crucial in addressing the challenges posed by sarcopenia across diverse clinical populations.

Drawing on cross‐disciplinary methodological insights from other complex neuromuscular and metabolic disorders, the development of effective management strategies increasingly demands multidimensional intervention platforms. Rather than targeting individual pathways in isolation, future frameworks must address interconnected pathological mechanisms by structurally integrating molecular biomarkers, functional capacities, and patient‐centered endpoints into a unified assessment and intervention model.
