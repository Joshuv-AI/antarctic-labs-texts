Regional Brain Structure Alterations in Diabetes but Not Prediabetes
Authors: Liangqi Wang, Huandong Lin, Zehua Zhao, Lingyan Chen, Li Wu, Ting Liu, Jing Li, Chu-Chung Huang, Chun-Yi Zac Lo, Xin Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextDiabetes (DM) affects brain volume and white matter hyperintensity (WMH), but whether regional gray matter volume (GMV) and tract-specific WMH progress in the prediabetes (PreDM) stage remains unclear.ObjectiveWe investigate brain structural changes across 3 distinct glycemic states.MethodsWe analyzed 512 participants (122 with DM, 109 with PreDM, and 281 controls) using advanced neuroimaging techniques. High-resolution structural T1-weighted magnetic resonance images and FLAIR (fluid-attenuated inversion recovery) images were acquired, complemented by cognitive assessments, grip strength measurements, and gait speed testing. We performed correlational analyses to examine the relationships between observed brain changes, cognitive performance, and motor function across different levels of glycemic states.ResultsWe found substantial changes in GMV in DM, especially in areas responsible for movement and coordination, including the bilateral cerebellum, right precentral gyrus, and left postcentral gyrus (P < .001 uncorrected with cluster size > 500). These brain changes were associated with decreases in cognitive test scores (Montreal Cognitive Assessment; P = .04), gait speed (P < .05), and right-hand grip strength (P < .05)—effects not seen in the prediabetic group. We observed significantly higher WMH index across 20 tracts in DM brains (P < .05; false discovery rate [FDR]-corrected). Glycemic levels positively correlated with WMH index in multiple tracts (P < .05; FDR-corrected).ConclusionThis study illuminates DM as a powerful force in cerebral architecture, challenging the notion of a gradual decline beginning in PreDM. These insights not only underscore the critical importance of DM prevention but also hint at the brain's remarkable resilience in the face of early metabolic challenges.Keywords: cognitive dysfunction, diabetes, MRI, prediabetes, tract-specific WMH, VBM

Diabetic (DM) and prediabetic (PreDM) patients with increasing prevalence among the aging population have caused public health problems. Cognitive dysfunction is increasingly accepted as a comorbidity of DM (1). Many epidemiological studies have reported that DM is a high-risk factor for dementia, accelerating cognitive decline in progress from mild cognitive impairment to dementia (1-4). The underlying mechanism may be microvascular dysfunction in the brain caused by hyperglycemia (5). Adults with PreDM also exhibit cerebral microvascular dysfunction, raising concerns that the onset of cerebral microvascular disease may occur before DM is diagnosed.

Brain atrophy has been found in DM using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with morphological analysis (6-8). Type 2 DM was associated with hippocampal and amygdala atrophy, a characteristic of preclinical Alzheimer disease, suggesting that specific brain region atrophy in type 2 DM affected cognitive function (9, 10). The association of PreDM and changes in brain volume has not been fully elucidated. A previous study showed that DM was associated with lower brain volumes, but not with PreDM (11). Another study suggested that PreDM patients had lower gray matter volume (GMV) than controls in the right anterior cingulate gyrus, the right posterior cingulate gyrus, the left insula, the left super temporal gyrus, and the left middle temporal gyrus, indicating that these regions may be associated with glucose dysregulation in PreDM patients (12). However, it is still largely unknown whether blood glucose fluctuations affect regional GMV in the PreDM stage.

On the other hand, white matter hyperintensity (WMH) is a patchy-like hyperintense lesion detected by FLAIR (fluid-attenuated inversion recovery) MRI (13). Tract-specific WMH analysis provided a method for localizing WM abnormalities in a fiber-specific manner. Vascular risk factors such as blood pressure (BP) were associated with tract-specific WMH (14). WMH has been shown to progress from a normal glycemic state to DM and is associated with the level of glycaemia even in the PreDM stage (15). However, it is not clear whether WMH progresses in specific WM tracts in the PreDM stage.

This study aims to elucidate the complex relationship between blood glucose fluctuations and cerebral structural integrity, addressing gaps in our understanding of PreDM and its neurological implications. Using whole-brain voxel-based morphometry (VBM) and tract-specific WMH analysis, we investigate brain structural changes across 3 distinct glycemic states in a community-based population. Our research examines the correlations between observed brain alterations, cognitive performance, and motor function across varying levels of glycemic states. Contrary to current assumptions, we hypothesize that significant brain structural modifications may be detectable not only in DM but potentially in the PreDM state as well. This approach may offer new insights into the progression of metabolic disorders and their effect on neurological health, potentially revising our understanding of the PreDM condition's effects on neural integrity.

Materials and Methods

Participants

We recruited 589 participants older than 50 years who attended the Shanghai Changfeng Study between July 2017 and January 2020. Seventy-seven participants were excluded for the following reasons: tumor, artifacts, and incomplete clinical data. Participants were divided into 3 groups: DM (n = 122), PreDM (n = 109), and normal controls (NCs, n = 281). The presence of DM was assessed at the baseline of Shanghai Changfeng Study and at the time of the first follow-up (16). The blood glucose measurements conducted at the time of the first follow-up between November 2014 and March 2017 were used for DM diagnosis. Participants were regarded as having DM if they had ever accepted antidiabetic treatment or if they met the World Health Organization 1999 criteria for diagnosing DM (17). PreDM was defined according to World Health Organization 1999 criteria: fasting blood glucose (FBG) and postprandial blood glucose (PBG) of the participant did not meet the standard for DM, but the standard for impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance. Other evaluations included years of formal education and health and medical history. Weight, height, BP, and waist and hip circumferences were also measured. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Individuals received a cognitive assessment with the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). On a 12-meter walking course, gait speed was measured when participants walked in the middle 6 meters. Gait speed was measured twice, and the mean speed was used for analysis. The grip strength on each hand was measured 3 times in kilograms using a Xiangshan EH101 gripping device in a subset of 484 individuals, and the mean strength was used for analysis. The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1975 and approved by the ethics committee of the Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. All participants provided written informed consent.

Image Acquisition

All images were acquired on a 3-Tesla scanner (GE Discovery 750w, GE Healthcare) at Putuo Hospital, Shanghai. A 16-channel head-neck coil with 12 channels for the head was used. A high-resolution structural T1-weighted MRI (T1w) were acquired with a 3-dimensional BRAVO sequence. A standardized FLAIR sequence was also acquired. For T1w images, 68 participants were scanned with a protocol and others were scanned with another. The T1w imaging protocols for 68 participants were: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 8.6/3.2 ms, flip angle 14°, field of view (FOV) = 25.6, 148 slices, slice thickness 1 mm, matrix 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3; for 444 participants were TR/TE = 8.5/3.2 ms, flip angle 12°, FOV = 25.6, 172 slices, slice thickness 1 mm, matrix 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. For FLAIR images, 68 participants were scanned in the sagittal plane with a protocol, and others were scanned with another in the axial plane. The FLAIR imaging protocols for 68 participants were: TR/TE = 6000/90 ms, FOV = 25.6, 292 slices, slice thickness 1.2 mm, matrix 256 × 224; for 441 participants were TR/TE = 8944/118.9 ms, flip angle 111°, FOV = 25.6, 51 slices, slice thickness 3 mm, matrix 256 × 192. All images were acquired parallel to the anterior commissure posterior commissure line. The cushions were filled inside the coil to minimize motion artifacts generated during image acquisition.

Image Preprocessing

The images acquired for each participant were reoriented to have the same spatial orientation, and parts of the neck below the cerebellum were removed using FSL (version: 5.0.11 https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/FslInstallation). Without preassigning the structures to be evaluated, VBM analysis can detect regional structural differences in whole-brain anatomy between groups. VBM analysis was conducted using SPM12 (version 7484, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/) in MATLAB (R2018a). Image were segmented with unified tissue segmentation (18), involving segmentation, bias correction, and spatially normalization. This procedure overcomes the inherently circular problem that registration requires an initial tissue classification, and tissue classification requires an initial registration. Three tissue types, including GM, WM, and cerebrospinal fluid, were segmented in native space according to tissue probability maps, which define the prior probability of finding a tissue type at a particular location. Afterward, all the native-space tissue segments were registered to the standard Montreal Neurological Institute template using the affine registration algorithm with east Asian brain template. Next, the diffeomorphic anatomical registration through exponentiated lie algebra (DARTEL) was used to refine the intersubject registration. The DARTEL registration involves alternating between computing a template, based on the average tissue probability maps of all participants, and warping the GM and WM tissue maps of all individuals into increasingly good alignment with the template (19). In the last step of DARTEL, the GM tissues are modulated to preserve the amount of tissue in each voxel, and areas that are expanded during warping are correspondingly reduced in intensity (20). Finally, images were smoothed with a gaussian kernel of 6 mm.

WMH was assessed on FLAIR images using lesion segmentation toolbox under SPM12 with the lesion prediction algorithm. We used the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) WM tract probability map to evaluate the WMH of 20 tracts, including bilateral anterior thalamic radiation (ATR), corticospinal tract (CST), inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF), inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus (IFO), uncinate fasciculus (UNC), superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) and its temporal part (tSLF), cingulum in cingulate gyrus (CgC), cingulum in hippocampus (CgH), forceps minor (Fmi), and forceps major (Fma). Our calculation was conducted in the individual native T1 space to maintain initially absolute volumes of the WMH. JHU WM tract probability map, FLAIR image, and WMH probability map were transformed to native T1 space, which has been detailed in a previous study (14). Statistical Analysis

The analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used for group comparison of continuous variables; and chi-square statistics were used for categorical data. The differences of GM between DM, PreDM, and NCs were initially assessed using ANCOVA in SPM at a threshold of P less than .05 corrected with family-wise error. We also exploratory performed a less conservative test using an uncorrected threshold of P less than .001. To compare the GM among DM, PreDM, and NCs, the age, sex, and time between blood glucose measurement and MRI examination were included as covariates in the analysis. Clusters of more than 500 statistically significant voxels were considered important and visualized on a standard brain image. Adjusted analysis was further performed by including the BMI, waist circumference, hip circumference, pulse pressure (PP), FBG, and glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) as covariates. Clusters of more than 100 statistically significant voxels were considered important and visualized on a standard brain image. Post hoc t tests were also conducted between each of the two groups.

Linear regression analysis was used to examine the relationship between regional GMV change and cognitive and motor functions, including MoCA, MMSE, and grip strength as well as gait speed, with age, sex, years of education, and total intracranial volume (TIV) as covariates. Mean GM volume in regions that showed significant differences between NCs, PreDM, and DM was summed for correlational analyzes. We first generated masks from the significant regions in the VBM analysis between NCs, PreDM, and DM; we applied the masks to the GM images of each participant and calculated the mean GM volume only in these significant regions; finally, we tested the relationships between the mean GM volume with MoCA, MMSE, and grip strength as well as gait speed.

ANCOVA was used to test differences in tract-specific WMH between groups and post hoc analysis was performed. The age, sex, and time between blood glucose measurement and MRI examination were included as covariates in the analysis. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between tract-specific WMH and the level of glycemia. Two-tailed P less than .05 was considered statistically significant. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) correction. FDR correction is a measure of false-positive risk, defined as the expected proportion of false positives among detections (21). All statistical analyzes were performed on the MATLAB (R2018a) and R Studio (version 1.4.1106) platform.

Tract-specific White Matter Hyperintensity Analysis

Due to the failure of imaging preprocessing, there were 509 participants included in the WMH analysis (NC = 279, PreDM = 108, DM = 122). The group differences in WMH index between the NC, PreDM, and DM groups were statistically significant across 20 tracts after FDR correction (P < .05, respectively). Post hoc analysis showed that the WMH index was higher in the DM participants than in NCs across 20 tracts after FDR correction (P < .05, respectively; see Fig. 5). We did not observe differences in WMH index in patients with PreDM relative to NCs, or in patients with DM relative to PreDM after FDR correction. FBG was positively correlated with WMH index in left ILF, bilateral ATR, CST, CgC, CgH, IFO, SLF, UNC, tSLF, Fma, and Fmi after FDR correction (Table 2). Discussion

In this community-based population, our results reveal a considerable effect of DM on brain structure, while PreDM showed no notable effects. In people with DM, we found substantial changes in GMV, especially in areas responsible for movement and coordination, including the bilateral cerebellum, right precentral gyrus, and left postcentral gyrus. These brain changes were associated with slight but notable decreases in cognitive test scores, gait speed, and right-hand grip strength. However, these effects were not seen in the PreDM group. Additionally, we observed widespread alterations in the WM of DM brains, with significantly higher WMH index across 20 tracts, suggesting potential disruptions in the brain's communication pathways. FBG and HbA1c levels positively correlated with WMH index in multiple tracts. These findings highlight the stark contrast between the effects of DM and PreDM on brain health.

The findings of this study extend the previous concept of association between brain atrophy and hyperglycemia (9, 11, 23, 24). GMV loss has been frequently reported, but the specific regions associated with hyperglycemia are inconsistent (9, 11, 23, 24). Results of a few previous studies showed that DM is associated with total GMV loss, regional GMV loss, and hippocampal and amygdala atrophy (9, 11, 25). To our knowledge, only a few studies were designed to use VBM analysis (24, 26, 27). These VBM approaches showed that GMV loss is associated with DM in the right temporal lobe. One study found that regional atrophy of GM mainly occurred in the right superior temporal gyrus and left inferior occipital gyrus in DM (27). VBM analysis is performed without priori hypothesis, and the grouped areas in the brain are not prechosen. Our VBM results show that DM has a lower GMV in regions of the bilateral cerebellum, right precentral gyrus, and left postcentral gyrus. These regions are known to be motor cortex involved in planning, controlling, and executing voluntary movements, or related to motor function (28). Our correlation analysis further confirmed that regional GMV is associated with gait speed and grip strength.

In a previous study, PreDM patients had lower GMV in the right anterior cingulate cortex compared with NCs (12). However, in our study, no statistically significant differences in GMV were found between PreDM and NCs. Similarly, the ARIC-NCS study did not find specific regional brain atrophy in PreDM (11). In accordance with previous studies, our findings suggest that brain structural changes do not appear in the early stage of hyperglycemia. The vulnerability of regional GM to blood glucose fluctuation was not identified in PreDM. A threshold effect that full-blown DM triggers significant GM atrophy may exist. Instead of gradually declining from the PreDM, regional GMV was reserved in the early stage of glucose intolerance, suggesting the brain resilience presented under disrupted homeostatic pressure. Based on this threshold effect, DM rather than PreDM could be the therapeutic objective to mitigate the progression of brain atrophy.

Several studies have suggested that DM increased the risk of cognitive decline (2, 3), but a longitudinal study also showed that atrophy did not mediate the association between DM and cognitive dysfunction (25). Consistent with the longitudinal study, we explored that GM atrophy in most regions was not associated with neurophysiological performance. A slight decrease in cognitive test scores was found in regional GMV between PreDM and DM participants, suggesting potential cognitive dysfunction. We found that regional GM atrophy was correlated with gait speed. A previous study showed that gait performance deteriorated among patients with DM peripheral neuropathy, suggesting that these patients may have a greater tendency to fall (29). Previous studies have demonstrated that individuals with DM peripheral neuropathy generate similar maximum palmar grip strength (30, 31). However, our results showed that grip strength on the left hand was stronger in DM patients, and a similar trend on the right hand was observed. The inconsistency may be attributed to the different study population. In this study, not all DM individuals developed complications of DM peripheral neuropathy. The significant regional volume between groups was positively correlated with grip strength on the right hand. We analyzed the regional brain volume using the VBM method and found between groups significant differences in the cerebellar that were involved in motor performance. Hyperglycemia could cause motor deficits through morphological changes in the brain. Regional GM atrophy in the bilateral cerebellum, the right middle frontal gyrus, and the right precentral gyrus may serve as an imaging marker of motor performance of the upper and lower extremities.

The WMH index was higher in DM patients than in NCs across 20 tracts, but the differences were not statistically significant between PreDM individuals and NCs. A previous study suggested that PreDM and DM were significantly associated with the larger volumes of WMH (15). Inconsistent with the previous study, our findings showed that the WMH index is not affected during the early stage of hyperglycemia, challenging the long-held notion that WMH was susceptible to glucose intolerance. Instead, the WMH showed a remarkable resilience to hyperglycemia insult in the PreDM state. Hyperglycemia may present a threshold effect on the WMH index. Similar to regional GMV, the WMH index showed a marked deterioration in DM, demonstrating a common structural alteration manner in the brain. The results showed that DM is a powerful force in cerebral architecture, emphasizing the importance of treatment of DM. FBG and HbA1c levels were positively correlated with WMH index in multiple tracts, indicating a tract-specific pattern of hyperglycemic injury in WM.

The strengths of our study include that it is a population-based design that enabled comparison among NCs, PreDM, and DM patients; acquisition of high-resolution T1w images using 3T MRI, VBM analyses, and tract-specific WMH analysis; and using both FBG and PBG to accurately assess glucose metabolic status. There are limitations to our study as well. The cross-sectional design of this study limits the explanation of causality between brain atrophy and hyperglycemia. The time passed between blood test and MRI examination may influenced the results mentioned earlier, but we have taken the interval time as a confounder in the analyses. A subset of participants (68 individuals) was scanned using a different protocol, which may have influenced the comparability of the imaging data. Different DM medications can have varying effects on brain health and cognitive function, but as an observational study, we could not clarify the potential effects caused by different medications. The latest DM medications, including glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and sodium-glucose cotransporters inhibitors, have shown a promising effect on reducing cardiovascular risks and may have neuroprotective effects in DM patients. However, since these medications were not available to participants in the Changfeng study in China prior to 2017, we could not elucidate the effect of these medications on brain structure in the present study. Future research should consider the potential effect of these medications on brain health in DM patients. Finally, our study is based on a Chinese population older than 50 years, so this should be considered when extending our results to other population.

In conclusion, this study illuminates DM as a powerful force in cerebral architecture, challenging the notion of a gradual decline beginning in PreDM. Instead, we revealed a threshold effect by which full-blown DM triggers substantial brain alterations. These insights not only underscore the critical importance of DM prevention but also hint at the brain's remarkable resilience in the face of early metabolic challenges. Our findings open new frontiers in understanding the complex interplay between metabolism and the mind, potentially paving the way for novel protective strategies and targeted interventions in the battle against DM-induced brain changes.
