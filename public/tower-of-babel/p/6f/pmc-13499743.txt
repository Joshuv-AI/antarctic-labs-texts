Usability and Acceptance of Autoinjector and Prefilled Syringe Devices by Patients with Generalized Myasthenia Gravis in a Human Factors Study
Authors: Kelly G. Gwathmey, Panru Jing, Scott Laorr, Joe Koo, Sanjay Rakhade, Emmett Alton Sartor, J. T. Tibung, Jason Murray, Christina Laskar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionGeneralized myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a chronic autoimmune disorder that causes muscle weakness. This human factors (HF) validation study aimed to demonstrate that use of a prefilled syringe with needle safety device (PFS-SD) or autoinjector (AI) is safe and effective for patients with gMG, caregivers, and healthcare professionals (HCPs) to administer injectable medication in the intended use environments without preventable use errors.MethodsPatients with gMG, caregivers, and HCPs were randomized to the PFS-SD or AI. No training on either device use was provided. The safety and usability of both devices were assessed using simulated use scenarios, knowledge-based questions, and participant feedback.ResultsThe simulated use scenario was successfully completed by 94% and 96% of participants using the PFS-SD and AI, respectively, while 90% and 87% of participants, respectively, successfully administered their assigned dose. Device acceptability was reported by 93% and 100% of patient participants for the PFS-SD and AI, respectively.ConclusionThis HF validation study demonstrated that the PFS-SD and AI are safe and effective for use by patients with gMG, caregivers, and HCPs in the intended use environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03663-8.

Introduction

Generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) is a rare, chronic, and debilitating autoimmune disorder characterized by impaired neuromuscular transmission and muscle weakness that worsens with activity [1–6]. Patients experience symptoms such as diplopia, ptosis, bulbar muscle weakness, limb muscle weakness, fatigue, and respiratory muscle weakness [2–4]. When it is severe, respiratory muscle weakness may result in potentially life-threatening myasthenic crisis necessitating intubation and mechanical ventilation [2, 3, 7]. At a minimum, the persistent functional limitations resulting from gMG symptoms can substantially affect a patients’ ability to perform activities of daily living and to work, and can compromise their quality of life [5, 8–10]. Circulating auto-antibodies against the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor (AChR) are present in 80–90% of patients with gMG [1, 11]. Binding of auto-antibodies to the AChR activates the complement cascade, resulting in damage to the postsynaptic neuromuscular junction membrane. As such, suppression of terminal complement activation of the complement cascade through targeted inhibition of complement component 5 (C5) is a potential therapeutic option for patients with gMG [12, 13].

Gefurulimab, a novel dual binding nanobody that inhibits complement C5 cleavage [14], is in development for the treatment of AChR antibody-positive (AChR-Ab+) gMG [15]. The low molecular weight of gefurulimab (~ 28 kDa) enables delivery through subcutaneous administration via two delivery device options: the prefilled syringe with needle safety device (PFS-SD) and an autoinjector (AI) [14]. The gefurulimab PFS-SD and AI are prefilled, single-dose, disposable delivery device options with sharps projection features that enable patients with gMG to feasibly self-administer their prescribed medication independently without the need for regular clinic visits. Human factors (HF) studies were performed to evaluate the overall usability and comprehensibility of the user interface (including device design, labeling, instructions, and packaging) of the gefurulimab PFS-SD and AI delivery device options by their intended users [16]. Device usability by patients is important, as symptoms associated with gMG, such as muscle weakness and diplopia, may impair the ability of patients to self-administer injectable therapies [4, 11].

Leading up to this final HF validation study, five formative HF studies with 157 participants representing the intended users [72 patients with gMG or surrogates, 41 caregivers, and 44 healthcare professionals (HCPs)] were conducted to assess and refine the product design [16]. The objective of this final HF validation study was to demonstrate that the PFS-SD and AI can be used safely and effectively by patients with gMG, their caregivers, and HCPs in the intended use environments without patterns of preventable use deviations which could result in harm to the user or patient.

Methods

Participants

The study recruited participants in accordance with US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance on HF studies, including the recommendation of inclusion of a minimum of 15 participants for each intended user population [17]. The targeted minimum enrollment was 75 eligible participants each assigned to one of five groups: (1) patients with gMG assessing the PFS-SD, (2) patients with gMG assessing the AI, (3) caregivers of patients with gMG assessing the PFS-SD, (4) caregivers of patients with gMG assessing the AI, and (5) HCPs assessing the AI only. Patient and caregiver participants were randomly assigned to the PFS-SD or AI group and counterbalanced evenly within the user groups to one injection or two injections. All HCPs were assigned to only the AI device under the guidance of FDA recommendations on this protocol.

Key inclusion criteria for patients and caregivers were aged ≥18 years, US residence, and the ability to speak English fluently and to read and understand English and Westernized Arabic numerals. Patients were required to have a diagnosis of gMG, and caregivers had to provide care for a patient diagnosed with gMG. Patients and caregivers could not have worked as HCPs at any time. Key inclusion criteria for HCPs were current employment as a medical professional working in a related field (i.e., neurology, neuromuscular medicine, ophthalmology, nephrology, rheumatology, or dermatology), prior injection experience, and experience training patients on the use of injection devices. Key exclusion criteria for patients and caregivers were participation in market research studies involving injection devices within the past 6 months; occupation involving market research, advertising, or a pharmaceutical company; and sharing a household with a person working in one of these fields. Participants could be withdrawn from the study if they were unwilling to participate, and they were allowed to leave the study at any time without cause or explanation and without forfeiting compensation.

The protocol, informed consent form, and recruitment materials were approved by an institutional review board (protocol number: ALEXIO141-01; Castle IRB, Chesterfield, MO, USA) prior to study initiation. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice, and all applicable legal and regulatory requirements. All participants provided written informed consent.

Study Design

Test Environment

The study was conducted at nine sites across eight states in the United States from July to August 2024 in a market research facility, usability laboratories, and participants’ homes. The study was designed in accordance with applicable sections of FDA guidance (Applying Human Factors and Usability Engineering to Medical Devices [17] and Application of Human Factors Engineering Principles for Combination Products: Questions and Answers [18)], the American National Standards Institute/Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation [19], and the IEC 62366–1:2015 Medical Devices—Part 1: Application of Usability Engineering to Medical Devices [20].

Each testing room included a table, chairs, a refrigerator, and typical injection supplies (e.g., alcohol swabs, bandages, cotton balls, sharps container), similar to the home or clinical environment where the devices are to be used; lighting and ambient noise also simulated the actual use environment. A study moderator, present in the testing room and seated across from the participant, conducted and oversaw the study sessions, which included reading the simulated use scenario prompt, ensuring the patient understood the scenario, taking notes on participant interaction with the device, and conducting the post-device interview. Participants were instructed to administer the injection(s) as if the moderator was not present to ensure an effective data capture, while maximizing representation of how tasks would be completed in the actual use environment.

Injection Devices and Study Samples

The PFS-SD consists of a single-dose prefilled syringe assembled with a needle safety device, an add-on finger flange, and a plunger rod (Fig. S1i). The AI consists of a single-dose prefilled AI device (Fig. S1ii). Each device is designed to contain a 2-mL volume of medication.

Samples evaluated in the study were representative of the intended commercial product. Study samples included the full intended product user interface, including the delivery device (PFS-SD or AI), device label, carton, carton labeling, instructions for use (IFU), and a mock prescribing information leaflet. For this study, both devices were filled with a surrogate solution, which mimicked the physical properties of the drug (i.e., appearance and viscosity) and did not contain active drug product.

Study Procedure and Simulated Use Scenario

The PFS-SD and AI were evaluated through simulated use and knowledge task question scenarios (Fig. S2). Additional subjective feedback on participants’ experiences with their assigned device was collected at the end of the study. Study participants received no training on the use of the device to which they were assigned. All study participants had access to product labeling or the IFU but were not directed by the moderator to review them. Study participants were able to request assistance from trained personnel by calling a helpline during the simulated use scenario, as indicated in the IFU, if needed. In such situations, answers to participant questions were provided based only on information available in the IFU and product labeling, and the incident was documented in the study report.

Simulated Use Scenario

During the simulated use scenario, objective data evaluating performance on each use task for the PFS-SD or AI were collected for each study participant. Each simulated use scenario included one study participant and lasted approximately 1 h. Study participants administered at least one injection, with participants assigned to two injections administering an additional injection using a second device immediately following the first injection. All injections were made into an injection pad that represented the injection site. HCPs and caregivers administered their injections into an injection pad attached to a mannequin. If errors were observed, participants were asked to perform a second simulated use trial (a “probing trial” that could be used only to help characterize use errors and determine whether the participant could self-correct). If a device malfunction occurred during normal use of the PFS-SD or AI, the instance was not counted against the participant and the product was set aside so that the manufacturer could assess the cause. The participant was asked to repeat the scenario with a different device only if the malfunction prevented the participant from completing the scenario. If the participant had an observed use deviation, the moderator asked questions to determine the root cause but avoided questions that could influence knowledge task performance. All sessions were videotaped from multiple angles to enable review. After each simulated injection, the study moderator conducted a post-scenario interview to determine the root cause of use deviations.

During the simulated use scenario, simulated tasks and knowledge tasks related to PFS-SD and AI storage, removal, inspection, administration according to dosing schedule, and disposal were evaluated (Table S1). Tasks were classified as either critical or noncritical. Critical tasks were defined as essential, product-specific user actions that, if performed incorrectly or omitted, could require medical intervention or compromise medical care. Noncritical tasks were defined as those that are not product-specific, do not affect proper dosing, are not essential to achieving therapeutic benefit, and do not pose a risk of harm requiring medical intervention.

Knowledge Tasks and Assessment

Study participants were asked to complete 12 knowledge tasks to determine if users could locate and understand important information provided in the product labeling (Table S2). The moderator asked participants targeted, knowledge-based questions about the product label and IFU (e.g., number of times the device can be used, storage information, dosing procedures). After the study participant answered these questions, the moderator probed on any questions answered incorrectly or instances in which the participant had difficulty finding information to determine the reasons why. At the end of the knowledge task scenario, the moderator inquired about any remaining deviations from the simulated use scenario that were not previously discussed before the knowledge task assessment.

Debrief/Post-Device Interview

After the simulated use scenario and knowledge task assessment were completed, the moderator interviewed the participant about their experience and requested feedback on their assigned device.

User Experience Questionnaire

After the simulated use scenarios, patient participants completed a user experience questionnaire during which they rated their experience with the device (PFS-SD or AI) and product user interface based on six attributes: ease of use, intuitiveness, level of comfort, confidence, convenience, and acceptability (Supplementary Material, Exhibit 1). Participants rated each attribute on a scale from 1 to 5, with 1 being low, 3 being moderate, and 5 being high. Each participant selected the top three reasons from a prepopulated list to clarify their selection.

Trained and experienced HF moderators collected and analyzed observational user performance data to evaluate use-related safety and effectiveness of the PSF-SD and AI. Performance datapoints of use deviations for all tasks were documented throughout testing, and sessions were videotaped and reviewed for details, clarification, and verification of events. All data analyses were descriptive and summarized, when appropriate, using descriptive statistics. For tasks in simulated use scenarios, observational user performance data included the number of participants who correctly completed a task independently (success); who experienced close calls and/or difficulties; and who made use errors (including omissions) that could result in harm or compromised medical care, incorrectly completed a task, or needed assistance to complete a task. All use deviations were counted and categorized after root cause analysis. Subjective data were evaluated for trends of comments.

Results

Study Participants

The HF validation study enrolled 75 participants (30 patients, 30 caregivers, and 15 HCPs) representing five distinct user groups, with 15 participants assigned to each user group (Fig. 1; Table S4). Among the participants assigned to the PFS-SD group, 8 patients and 8 caregivers were assigned to one injection and 7 patients and 7 caregivers were assigned to two injections. Among the participants assigned to the AI group, 7 patients, 7 caregivers, and 8 HCPs were assigned to one injection and 8 patients, 8 caregivers, and 7 HCPs were assigned to two injections. The majority [68/75 (90.7%)] of participants were right-handed and 60.0% were female (36/60); 60.0% (18/30) of patient participants and 43.3% (13/30) of caregiver participants assigned to each device were experienced with injection. The simulated use tasks with the highest incidences of observed use deviations were “Pinch skin and insert needle (at an approximately 45° angle)” (simulated use task 21; n=13) followed by “Let the prefilled syringe(s) sit at room temperature for 45 minutes before use” [simulated use task 16; n=12 (Table S5)]. Use observations for “Pinch skin and insert needle” included study participants not pinching the skin (injection pad) or inserting the needle at the recommended angle before administering the dose. This use deviation may increase the probability of intramuscular dose delivery, although the clinical impact on efficacy from an intramuscular injection is expected to be minimal. The primary root causes of use deviations associated with this task were negative transfer (use deviation resulting from participant reliance on prior injection experience), IFU-related (IFU overlooked, forgotten, or considered not salient to the participant), and experimental artifact (nervousness due to simulated use scenario evaluation or failure to perform the task because this was a simulated use scenario and not a real-world setting). These use deviations were reviewed, and participants were able to demonstrate an understanding of the need to follow the IFU to ensure proper injection technique. In the second most common use deviation, the failure to allow the prefilled syringe(s) to warm to room temperature for 45 min before use would not affect efficacy but could affect injection comfort. The primary root causes of this use deviation were negative transfer (use deviation resulting from participant reliance on prior experience with another refrigerated medication), IFU-related (IFU not used), experimental artifact (participant determination that the task was not necessary in a simulated use scenario or the deviation was due to perceived time constraints), and willful noncompliance (participant understood the task but expressed a possible unwillingness to wait the full 45 min in a real-life setting). These product use risks were identified and assessed. After determining the primary root causes, the residual use-related risks were determined to be acceptable. No additional product user interface modifications were considered necessary, as the primary root causes for these use deviations could not be fully mitigated through design changes when the product is used as intended per the IFU.

Knowledge Task Performance

Across the patient and caregiver user groups, 97.2% of datapoints for all PFS-SD knowledge tasks were answered correctly, and the incidence of use deviations was low (Table 1). Knowledge tasks with the highest incidence of use deviations were finding or understanding information on the appropriate appearance of medication that is good to use (knowledge task 8, n=6), understanding where to administer a second injection (knowledge task 7, n=2), and finding or understanding information about what to do if the carton was damaged (knowledge task 10, n=2).

AI Task Results

Simulated Use Performance

Overall, 96% of the datapoints for all AI simulated use tasks were successfully completed, and the overall incidence of use deviations was low (Table 1). Eighty-seven percent of participants (39/45 total; 15/15 patients; 13/15 caregivers; 11/15 HCPs), including all patients with gMG, successfully administered their assigned dose using the AI, as defined by successfully determining the number of injections for the prescribed dose (simulated use tasks 5 and 24) and lifting the device from the injection site only after the injection had been completed (simulated use task 23). Two study participants (1 caregiver and 1 HCP) lifted the AI device from the injection site before completing the injection (simulated use task 23) based on their reliance on experience with another injectable medication instead of instructions in the IFU. One caregiver participant did not deliver the second injection because of incorrect recall (simulated use task 24), despite verbally confirming understanding the need for two injections at the beginning of the simulated use scenario. The participant indicated that in a real-world setting they would confirm the dosage with the patient. Three HCPs administered one injection instead of two (simulated use task 24) as they did not confirm they were assigned to administer two injections at the start of the simulated use scenario. All three participants reported that in a real-world clinical setting the routine practice is to confirm the prescribed dose in the patient’s chart or electronic medical record, an event that was not simulated in the study session. The product use risks stemming from these dose administration-related use observations were assessed and the residual use-related risks were deemed acceptable. No additional product user interface modifications were proposed, as none were expected to further effectively mitigate these risks.

The simulated use tasks with the highest incidences of use deviations were “Let autoinjector(s) sit at room temperature for 45 minutes before use” (simulated use task 16; n=23) and “Choose injection site” (simulated use task 18; n=8; Table S6). Use observations for “Let autoinjector(s) sit at room temperature for 45 minutes before use” were IFU-related (IFU not salient to participant), experimental artifact (failure to wait 45 min due to perceived time constraints of the study), and negative transfer (participant reliance on prior injection experience). The majority of use observations for “Choose injection site” involved administering the two injections within 1 inch (c. 2.54 cm) of each other. The primary root causes of use deviations associated with this task were IFU-related (relevant information in IFU overlooked or not salient to the patient), experimental artifact (participant determination that task was not necessary when injecting into an injection pad), and negative transfer (participant reliance on prior injection experience). The product use risks with the AI identified from these use observations were assessed, and the residual use-related risks were determined to be acceptable. No additional product user interface modifications were considered necessary, as primary root causes for these use deviations could not be fully mitigated through design changes when the product is used as intended per the IFU.

Knowledge Task Performance

Across the three user groups (patients with gMG, caregivers, and HCPs), 99% of datapoints for all AI knowledge tasks were correctly answered, and the incidence of use deviations was low (Table 1). Knowledge tasks with the highest incidence of use deviations were finding or understanding information on the appropriate appearance of medication that is good to use (knowledge task 8, n=4) and understanding where to administer a second injection (knowledge task 7, n=2).

Discussion

This HF validation study demonstrated that the PFS-SD and AI could be safely and effectively used by patients with gMG, caregivers, and HCPs in the intended use settings, despite the participants receiving no protocol-required training and not being required to read the IFU before using the device. Overall, study participants completed 94–99% of the simulated use and knowledge tasks datapoints evaluated for both devices (PFS-SD, 94% of simulated use datapoints and 97% of knowledge task datapoints; AI, 96% of simulated use datapoints and 99% of knowledge task datapoints), and the incidence of use deviations (use errors, close calls, and use difficulties) was low.

Despite the use deviations observed, 90% of participants using the PFS-SD and 87% of participants using the AI successfully administered their assigned dose. Use deviations observed with both the PFS-SD and AI were primarily related to participant reliance on prior injection experience, failure to follow device IFU, and the simulated nature of the scenario. No consistent patterns of critical use deviations (i.e., repeated observations of a use error leading to elevated user or patient harm) were identified. All participants assigned to the PFS-SD were able to push the plunger rod completely and expel the full contents of the syringe, and all patient participants were able to confirm their assigned dose and administer the correct number of syringes. Excluding use errors related to experimental artifact, 96% of participants assigned to the AI successfully administered a simulated injection. Knowledge task deviations were primarily related to understanding where to place a second injection when assigned to administer two injections (PFS-SD, n=2; AI, n=2) and finding or understanding information on how to determine if the medication is good to use based on its appearance (PFS-SD, n=6; AI, n=4). Patient participants gave highly positive feedback for ease of use, intuitiveness, level of comfort, confidence, convenience, and acceptability for both the PFS-SD (87%–93% across all attributes) and the AI (93–100% across all attributes). Although ≥ 10% of patients in each group did not successfully administer their assigned dose, device acceptability was reported by 93% of patient participants assigned to the PFS-SD and 100% of the patient participants assigned to the AI.

The use deviations identified in this study are expected to have a low likelihood of occurring in real-world setting and are not unique to the devices tested. All participants who administered an incomplete dose with the PFS-SD or AI recognized the issues resulting in incomplete dose administration immediately and sought clarification, suggesting that the likelihood of similar events going unnoticed and resulting in repeated incomplete dosing in real-world settings is low. Administration of two injections in the same location is not expected to impact drug efficacy. Experimental artifact and negative transfer root causes cannot be completely eliminated through optimization of the product user interface as the product is intended to be used with the provided IFU. The ability of the majority of users to find and understand information in the IFU and product labeling and to correctly answer knowledge task questions suggests that the IFU and product labeling effectively communicate the appropriate use of the PFS-SD and AI devices [21–23]. For example, some study participants did not believe that waiting 45 min was part of the simulated use scenario despite this being clearly stated in the IFU, suggesting that the IFU is effective when followed and that such deviations are unlikely to occur in real-world conditions.

A potential limitation of this study is that baseline characteristics describing disease severity for patients with gMG were not collected. However, although disease severity can vary widely for patients with gMG, the intent of this study was to assess whether these self-administration devices can be used safely and effectively in a population of patients with gMG capable of using such devices. Caregiver participants were included in this study to assess their ability to use these devices to simulate the real-world instances in which the disease severity of gMG in a patient would prevent self-administration and therefore require the assistance of a caregiver. In real-world practice, patients with gMG and caregivers would be expected to discuss physical capabilities with the physician to determine whether self-administration devices are appropriate for use and whether the patient or the caregiver would be expected to administer the injection.

Other study limitations include those inherent in the simulated nature of the study, such as the use of injection pads, which may have contributed to some use errors and the possibility that study participants’ self-injection performance differed between study performance and real-world use.

Conclusions

This HF validation study demonstrated that the PFS-SD and AI devices can be used safely and effectively for self-injection in the intended use settings by untrained patients, caregivers, and HCPs without patterns of preventable use deviations that could result in harm to the user or patient. Both devices were also highly rated by users across several attributes, including ease of use, intuitiveness, level of comfort, confidence, convenience, and acceptability. These HF validation study results support the PFS-SD and AI as convenient and user-friendly options for self-administration of medication by patients with gMG and their caregivers.
