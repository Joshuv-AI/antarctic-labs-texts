Cecal bacterial communities and lung immunity in layers fed omega-3 fatty acids or yeast bioactives under different space allowance
Authors: Paul Kibaba Waliaula, Xinhua Yin, Dion Lepp, Lindsey Clairmont, Elijah G. Kiarie, Moussa S. Diarra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The poultry-rearing environment and diet can influence gut microbiota and immunity. However, the long-term effects of early-life nutritional interventions on gut heath in layers under various spacing allowances (SA) are not well understood. This study investigated how early-life dietary yeast bioactives (YB) or co-extruded full-fat flaxseed, a source of omega-3 fatty acids (N-3 FA), from placement to 16 weeks of age (woa) influenced the development of cecal microbiota and immune function in Lohmann LSL Lite pullets reared under different SA, with follow-up to 72 woa. The experiment involved 2,832 newly hatched chicks reared in an enriched cage system under high (HSA, 348 cm2/bird) or low (LSA, 284 cm2/bird) SA and fed either control diet (C), C + 3 % N-3 FA, or C + 0.05 % YB. Ceca were sampled at 4, 8, 16, 35, and 72 woa for bacterial plate counts, 16S rRNA sequencing, and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) quantification. Lungs were analyzed for immune gene expression. At 4 and 16 woa, β-diversity (P = 0.01) revealed dissimilarity between the bacterial communities of birds under HSA and LSA groups. High Bacteroides abundances, propionic and iso-butyric acids concentrations were observed in LSA at both sampling points (P<0.05). At 16 woa, the highest and lowest n-butyrate concentrations were noted in N-3 FA- and control-fed birds, respectively (P < 0.05), regardless of SA. Barnesiellaceae positively correlated with n-valeric and acetic acid in N-3 FA-fed birds at 4 woa. In YB-fed pullets, Lactobacillaceae and Enterobacteriaceae showed a positive correlation with n-butyric acid. In the lungs, about 15 genes, including IL1β,IL2, and IL8, were differentially expressed depending on SA and diets (P<0.05). Early dietary YB or N-3 FA under different SA modulated cecal bacterial community diversity and structure, SCFA profiles, and enhanced lung immune responses in layer.

Introduction

Pullets and laying hens are mainly reared in conventional or enriched cage systems under different stocking densities (Casey-Trott et al., 2017; van Staaveren et al., 2018). Stocking density, defined as the spacing allowance (SA) per bird, plays a key role in shaping poultry welfare, stress response, and performance (Casey-Trott et al., 2017). Studies suggested that low SA (LSA) induced chronic oxidative stress, impaired intestinal functions and microbial diversity, thereby increasing susceptibility to metabolic and bacterial diseases (Abo-Al-Ela et al., 2021; Schwarzer et al., 2022). Given that gut microbiota undergoes continuous maturation during the pullet phase, the rearing environment plays a decisive role in shaping microbial communities that support long-term gut health and optimal laying performance (Makagon et al., 2024; Sun et al., 2021). However, the extent to which SA modulates gut microbiota development in pullets remains poorly understood. Thus, the present study investigated the impacts of SA on gut microbiota development and its implications for health of pullets.

The poultry gastrointestinal tract (GIT) is densely colonized with diverse microbial communities that produce bioactive compounds, including lipopolysaccharides, bacteriocins, secondary metabolites, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), which modulate host physiology, including immune functions (Zhen et al., 2021). In laying hens, the gut microbial composition naturally shifts with age and production stage, while rearing conditions further shape its structure (Bajagai et al., 2024). Although, SA and dietary components (YB or N-3 FA) are known to influence poultry gut microbiota, their combined effects on gut microbial structure, composition, and downstream immune responses during early life remain poorly understood in pullets, with potential long-term impacts in laying hens, underscoring the need for this study.

Understanding the interplay between gut microbiome composition and gut health is essential for optimizing poultry welfare and performance (Zhen et al., 2021). Functional feed additives have been explored as a nutritional intervention to promote growth, enhance gut health and immunity (Neijat et al., 2019c, b; a; Kiarie et al., 2024; Dowling et al., 2024; Shani and Irani, 2024; Waliaula et al., 2024). This study provides insight into how such additives can strengthen lung immunity via the lung-gut axis in pullets.

Flaxseed is a plant source of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), containing high levels of α-linolenic acid (N-3 FA, C18:3n-3). This is an essential fatty acid that can be converted in the body to long-chain decosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6n-3), through a series of desaturation and elongation reactions, which is important in poultry health, reproductive, and performance (Whittle et al., 2024). Dietary supplements with N-3 FA have been reported to improve the antioxidant and immune status in birds, particularly under a high stocking density (Li et al., 2019). Dietary N-3 FA have been shown to improve performance, eggshell quality, and the production of omega-3-enriched eggs (Ma et al., 2020). PUFA, particularly N-3 FA and DHA, have also been shown to enhance gut microbial diversity by increasing the population of beneficial gut microbes in humans and animals (Calder, 2006). Dietary supplementation with PUFA has been associated with increased SCFA levels, which support immune responses and gut health (Chen et al., 2015). Therefore, understanding the effects of N-3 FA on microbial composition, SCFA production, and immunity is essential for developing feeding strategies that improve poultry health and performance (Lee et al., 2016).

Yeast bioactives have been shown to exert a broad range of beneficial biological effects, including immunoregulatory, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antiviral, antioxidant, and toxin-absorbing properties (Zhen et al., 2021). Yeast extracts from the Saccharomyces cerevisiae cell wall are rich in β-glucans, a type of polysaccharides consisting of a β–1,3–linked d-glucopyranosyl backbone with β−1,6-linked side chains. Studies in both humans and animals, including poultry, have confirmed these functional properties of yeast β-glucans (Yitbarek et al., 2012; Zhen et al., 2021; Alagawany et al., 2023). In laying hens, dietary yeast has been associated with improved egg production (Qiu et al., 2024), and enhanced microbial diversity. Specifically, supplementation with 1 – 2 g/kg yeast culture improved egg production performance in both young and older hens (Qiu et al., 2024; De Cloet et al., 2023), while yeast β-glucans in breeder hens enriched ileal microbial diversity and increased beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus (Zhen et al., 2021). However, detailed SCFA data in pullets and laying hens are inadequate; changes in cecal microbial profiles, especially a decrease Proteobacteria and an increase in Actinobacteria, suggest shifts in fermentation potential (Qiu et al., 2024).

Alpha linolenic acid (ALA), a source of N-3 FA, and yeast bioactive (YB) are functional feed additives that modulate gut microbiota and microbial metabolites in animal and poultry (Lyons et al., 2017; Shani and Irani, 2024). Early dietary supplementation with these additives may have a long-lasting impact on gut health, immune functions, and performance (Videnska et al., 2014). This study investigated the effects of early-life dietary supplementation with YB or N-3 FA, from placement to 16 weeks of age (woa), on cecal microbiota development and lung immune genes profile in pullets reared under different SA in an enriched cage system, with follow-up to 72 woa to evaluate the long-term impact on immunity and performance.

Material and methods

All experimental procedures performed in this study at the Arkell Poultry Research Center (Guelph, ON, Canada) were approved (protocol #4518) by the University of Guelph Animal Care Committee according with the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines (CCAC, 2009) and Canadian code of practice for the care and handling of pullets and laying hens (NFACC, 2017).

Birds and housing

A total of 2,832-day-old Lohmann LSL lite pullets were procured from a commercial hatchery (Archer Poultry Farm Ltd, Brighton, Canada). Birds were housed in 48 enriched cages (Farmer Automatic, Clark Ag Systems, ON, Canada), each measuring 239 × 80 × 75 cm, and equipped with a 5,950 cm² perching platform (Khanal et al., 2021). The cages were of a combi-aviary design, but the foldable doors were kept closed throughout the experiment, allowing them to function as enriched cages (Fig. 1), as described elsewhere (Habinski et al., 2017). Each cage had a feeder trough along the length of the cage floor (width: 12 cm, depth: 9 cm), positioned 23 cm from the front wall and 52 cm from the back wall, allowing access to feed from both sides. A parallel water line, located behind the feeder, was fitted with 12 nipple drinkers plus an additional 4 nipple drinkers over the platforms.Fig. 1Housing system for pullets: combi-cage brooding (0 –16 weeks) and laying hens in enriched cages (17 – 72 weeks).Fig. 1

Housing system for pullets: combi-cage brooding (0 –16 weeks) and laying hens in enriched cages (17 – 72 weeks).

Each room contained 24 cages, with birds placed under two spacing allowances (SA): high SA (HSA, 348 cm2/bird, 50 birds/cage, 24 cages) or low SA (LSA, 284 cm2/bird, 68 birds/cage, 24 cages) (Fig. 1). The room temperature was set at 35°C for the first 3 days, then gradually reduced to 20°C by the 5th week and maintained thereafter (through the end of the pullet and laying phases). Relative humidity ranged from 50 – 60 % for the entire study. Lighting (30 lux) was provided continuously for the first two days, then gradually decreased according to the lighting program of LSL-Lite as described (Lee et al., 2023). At hatch, chicks were vaccinated against Marek's and Newcastle (ND) diseases via subcutaneous neck injection. At 3 woa, pullets were spray-vaccinated with Infectious bronchitis vaccine (IBV) followed by the first booster for ND and IBV at 6 woa. Vaccination against fowl laryngotracheitis virus (via wing web), avian encephalomyelitis, and fowl pox (via eye drop) was done at 8 woa. Second and third boosters for IBV and ND vaccine were administered at 11 woa (spray) and 15 woa (thigh injection), respectively.

Experimental diets

The three dietary treatments consisted of: (1) basal mainly corn and soya bean meal control; (2) the control diet supplemented with 3 % of co-extruded full fat flax seed and pulse mixture (FFF, 1:1 wt/wt;) as the source of N-3 FA (linPRO, O &T Farms Ltd., Regina Canada), and (3) control diet supplemented with 0.05 % of YB (CBS Bio Platforms Inc., Calgary Canada). The FFF contained 11 % α-linoleic acid, 25 % crude protein, and 23 % neutral detergent fibre (Thanabalan and Kiarie, 2021), and its energy and nutrient contribution was accounted for in feed formulation. The YB from hydrolysis of Saccharomyces cerevisiae by β−1,3-glucan hydrolase (Canadian Bio-Systems Inc., Calgary, AB, Canada) contained 30 % crude protein and 40 % total non-starch polysaccharides, of which more than 95 % are mannans and β−1,3-glucans (Lu et al., 2019; Patterson et al., 2023). The inclusion levels of FFF and YB used in the present study were based on previous experiments (Lee et al., 2023). Diets were provided in three feeding phases: starter (0 – 4 woa), grower (5 – 8 woa), and developer (9 – 16 woa) (Table 1) (Lee et al., 2023). All diets were formulated to meet nutrient specifications for Lohmann LSL Lite hens (Lohmann, 2016). Experimental design, and samples collection

The experiment was a 2 (SA) x 3 (diets) factorial arrangement. Diets were allocated to SA in a completely randomized design, with 8 replicate cages per SA and diet combination. Treatments were applied from placement to 16 woa. At 17 woa, birds were transferred to laying house where they retained their rearing treatment identification and were fed commercial diets until 72 woa (Fig. 2). The birds had a free access to feed and water. As shown in Fig. 2, birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation at predetermined ages to collect lung and ceca digesta samples.Fig. 2Timeline of the present feeding trial. L = Lung collection for gene expression; C = Ceca samples collection for bacteriological analysis by culture-dependent and culture independent approaches.Fig. 2

Timeline of the present feeding trial. L = Lung collection for gene expression; C = Ceca samples collection for bacteriological analysis by culture-dependent and culture independent approaches.

Sample collections

Four hundred fifty-six birds at woa 4 (n = 48), 8 (n = 96), 16 (n = 96), 35 (n = 108), and 72 (n = 108) were sacrificed to collect cecal digesta, and lung samples at 16 woa. Cecal samples were transferred into sterile Whirl-Pak plastic bags (Nasco, Fort Atkinson, WI) and immediately placed in cooler boxes before transporting them to the laboratory for bacteriological analysis and SCFA quantification. Lung tissues were excised aseptically using sterile forceps and scissors and placed in the tubes containing RNA stabilization solution (AM7021, ThermoFisher Scientific) at a ratio of 1 mg tissue to 1 mL solution for gene expression profiling. All samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Culture dependent bacteriology

Cecal digesta were analysed for Lactobacilli, Enterococcus, and E. coli using selected media as described (Diarrassouba et al., 2007; Islam et al., 2019). Briefly, the Lactobacillus population was enumerated on Lactobacilli MRS agar (Oxoid, Nepean, ON, Canada), incubated at 37°C for 48 hours under anaerobic conditions. Enterococcus populations were enumerated on KF Streptococcal agar CM0701 (Oxoid), incubated at 37°C for 48 hours. E. coli bacteria were enumerated on CHROMagarTM incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Cecal bacterial counts were Log10 transformed before statistical analysis.

Culture independent bacteriology: 16S rRNA sequencing

DNA extraction: Genomic DNA was extracted from cecal samples using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Venlo, Netherlands) according to the manufacturer guidelines. The DNA quality and concentration were determined using 1.0 % agarose gel electrophoresis and a Qubit Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) assay kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA), respectively. DNA samples were then normalized and pooled to have 8 replicates per group of treatment/week except for samples from Week-35 and 72, which were pooled to have 3 replicates per group.

Sequencing and analysis: Amplicon sequencing libraries were prepared according to the 16S Metagenomics Sequencing Library Preparation Guide (Illumina, Inc.) Rev B using primers Bakt_341F and Bakt_805R, as previously described (Das et al., 2020). Libraries were pooled at equimolar ratios to a final concentration of 8 pM and sequenced on a MiSeq instrument using the MiSeq 600-cycle v3 kit (Illumina, MS1023003). Microbiota analysis was performed using Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology (qiime2-amplicon-2024.2) (Caporaso et al., 2010). Briefly, 300 bp paired-end reads were processed with DADA2 (Callahan et al., 2016) to denoise reads, remove chimeric sequences and singletons, join paired-ends, and de-replicate sequences to produce unique amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomic classification of the resulting ASV feature table was performed with VSEARCH (Rognes et al., 2016) using the Greengenes 99 % operational taxonomic unit (OTU) sequences (McDonald et al., 2012) as reference. Multiple sequence alignment of ASV representative sequences was performed with multiple alignment using fast fourier transform (MAFFT) (Katoh et al., 2009), and a rooted phylogenetic tree constructed with FastTree (Price et al., 2009).

Core diversity analyses were performed to calculate alpha-diversity metrics, taxonomic relative abundances, and beta-diversity dissimilarity matrices (weighted and unweighted UniFrac) for principal coordinate analyses (PCoA). Differential abundance (DA) testing was conducted with analysis of composition of microbiomes (ANCOM) accounting for the compositional nature of relative abundance data (Mandal et al., 2015). Relative frequencies of bacterial abundances were calculated at each taxonomic level. Pairwise statistical tests were used for alpha- (Kruskal-Wallis) and beta-diversities (PERMANOVA).

Short-chain fatty acid analysis

Cecal digesta samples collected at 4 and 16 woa were analyzed for seven SCFA (SCFA: lactic, Acetic, propionic, iso-butyric, n-butyric, n-valeric, isovaleric acid) as described in our previous method (Maina et al., 2024). Briefly, cecal digesta were thawed, and 0.1 g of each sample was suspended in 1 ml of 0.005 N H2SO4, vortexed and centrifuged at 20, 200 x g for 15 min (Thermos Scientific Sorvall, Germany). Supernatant (400 μl) were transferred to amber high pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) vials and diluted with equal volume of 0.005 N sulphuric acid (H2SO4) and analyzed on an HP1100 series HPLC system (Woldbronn, Germany) equipped with an ROA LC column (300×7.8 mm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) and an Agilent 1260 infinity RID detector (Woldbronn, Germany). A 20 μl injection volume was run isocratically at 60°C with 0.005 N H2SO4 as the mobile phase at 0.5 ml/min for 42 min. SCFA were identified and quantified using a certified reference standard (CRM46975 volatile free acid, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), to establish baseline concentration. Final concentrations were corrected for dilution factor and extract volume, and expressed in μmol/ml.

Lung RNA extraction and gene expression profiling

Lung samples (n = 24) were collected at 16 woa from birds fed the control basal diets (n = 8), N-3 FA (n = 8), and YB (n = 8) under HSA (n = 12) or LSA (n = 12). Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy® Mini Kit (Cat. No./ID:74104 Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 mg of the lung tissues were excised using sterile forceps and surgical blades and then transferred into 600 μl RLT buffer with 1 % β-mercaptoethanol (Fisher Scientific). The tissue was homogenized using a Pro 200 homogenizer (Pro Scientific). Homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 3 min, and the supernatant was transferred to Eppendorf tubes, followed by the addition of 70 % ethanol. RNA was eluted using the RNeasy Mini column (Cat. No./ID:74104 Qiagen). The RNA quality and purity were measured with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (260 and 260/280 nm, respectively). The absence of genomic contamination was confirmed by running RNA samples with five housekeeping genes (ACTB, H6PD, HMBS, RPL4, and UBC) by real-time polymerase chain reaction (rt-PCR).

Real-time PCR

cDNA synthesis was carried out using the Qiagen RT2 Profiler PCR Array Handbook (11/2018) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 mg of total RNA from each sample was synthesized using the RT2 First Strand Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) and stored at −20°C until required. For PCR, cDNA samples were mixed with molecular-grade water and RT2 SYBR Green ROX qPCR Mastermix (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols and dispensed into a 96-well plate from the Chicken Innate & Adaptive Immune Response PCR array (PAGG-052ZA, Qiagen). This array profiles the expression of 84 genes involved in innate and adaptive immune response pathways. Gene expression was normalized by five housekeeping genes automatically selected by RT2, Profiler PCR Array & Assay data (Das et al., 2020). Real-time PCR was performed using the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-time PCR system with 7500 software v2.3. Fold changes in gene expression between control and the N-3 FA and YB diets groups fed birds under HSA or LSA were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Statistical significance was determined by student’s t-test at P < 0.05.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed according to the factorial design using the General Linear Mixed Model (GLIMMIX) procedure in SAS (SAS, 9.4, 2009), with the dietary treatment groups, SA, and sampling age as fixed effects and individual cage as the experimental unit. Data from bacterial enumeration were log-transformed (CFU/g), and SCFA concentration was expressed in μmol/mL before analysis. Least squares mean (LSmeans) were compared using the Tukey test when the overall treatment effect was statistically significant. Non-parametric correlations relationships between SCFA and bacterial taxa were estimated. The differences in fold changes (FCs) of lung gene expressions between control and feed supplement groups were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and the Student’s t-test. ǀFCǀ ≥ 2 were considered significantly influenced by feed supplementation. The p-value of 0.05 was used to declare a significant difference.

Results

Enumeration of viable cecal bacteria: E. coli, Enterococcus and Lactobacillus spp

Analysis of 456 ceca digesta showed no significant effects of diets and SA on E. coli counts at 4, 8, and 35 woa (P > 0.05). However, at 16 woa the lowest E. coli counts were found in LSA (P < 0.0001), with N-3-FA-fed and YB-fed pullets having the lowest numbers than control-fed pullets (P = 0.007). No interactions between diets and SA for E. coli count were found at any sampling weeks (P > 0.05). No significant SA or diet effects were observed at any sampling week for Enterococcus counts, but at 8 woa LSA tended to have the lowest number of this bacterium (P = 0.078). At 8 (P = 0.009) and 35 (P<.0001) woa, cecal Lactobacillus counts were lower in LSA than in HSA pullets. At week 35, interactions (P = 0.053) between the SA and diets were noted (Table 1).

Cecal bacterial composition based on 16S rRNA sequencing

At Week 4, no significant differences were observed between the SA and diets for alpha-diversity, as indicated by the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indexes (P > 0.05). However, beta-diversity analysis using the Unifrac unweighted and weighted methods revealed a clear dissimilarity between the cecal bacterial communities from birds under HSA and LSA (P < 0.05; Fig. 3A), with different clustering also observed among feeding groups (Fig. 3B). The phylum Proteobacteria tended to be more abundant in the cecal of birds fed a control diet under LSA, although the difference was not statistically significant. Correlations between cecal bacterial families and SCFA concentrations

Correlations were estimated between bacterial families and SCFAs at weeks 4 and 16. At week 4, positive correlations were observed in cecal digesta of control-fed birds between Rikenellaceae and Dehalobactericeae families with n-valeric acid, while Bacteroidaceae and Enterobacteriaceae were negatively correlated with propionic acid and iso-butyric acid, respectively (P < 0.05). Discussion

Rearing environment and functional dietary feed additives play a key role in shaping gut microbiota and immunity, which contribute to improved health, flock uniformity, and performance in layers (Stanley et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2021). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate that early-life dietary supplementation with N-3 FA or YB to Lohmann LSL Lite pullets under different SA in an enriched cage system modulates cecal microbiota composition and SCFA profiles. These bacterial and metabolic shifts are associated with downstream effects on lung immune gene expressions, providing novel insight into the potential gut-lung axis influenced by both nutritional interventions and environmental factors. Though the use of functional dietary feed additives to enhance gut health, microbiota, and performance in broilers has been extensively studied (Stanley et al., 2013; Das et al., 2020; Jadhav et al., 2024).

Escherichia coli and Lactobacillus are early colonizers of the gut, serving as indicators for harmful and beneficial bacteria, respectively. In the present study, viable bacterial counts revealed that SA and N-3-FA- and YB-fed pullets showed lower E. coli counts than control-fed pullets at 16 woa. Saccharomyces cerevisiae-derived YB contains mannan-oligosaccharides, which affect E. coli by breaking down bacterial membranes while maintaining beneficial bacteria (Xu et al., 2021). Dietary supplementation with prebiotics mannan-oligosaccharides increased Lactobacillus count and mitigate stress in broilers under LSA (Kridtayopas et al., 2019). Similar to the present study, the use of dietary N-3 FA in pigs increased the abundance of Lactobacillus, which suppresses E. coli through competitive exclusion and changes in enzymatic activities in the gut (Leung et al., 2018; Ndou et al., 2018). Conversely, Lactobacillus counts – indicative of beneficial gut bacteria were notably higher in birds housed under HSA than in those under LSA at 8 and 35 woa, respectively. These findings are consistent with those of a previous study reporting fewer Lactobacillus count in broilers chicks at high stocking density as compared to low stocking density (Cengiz et al., 2015).

The gastrointestinal tract of newly hatched chicks undergoes rapid colonization, forming a complex cecal microbiome as the birds age (Sun et al., 2021). In the present study, beta-diversity analysis revealed a clear dissimilarity between the cecal microbiota of pullets reared under LSA and HSA at 4 woa providing a clear indication that SA plays a key role in shaping the cecal bacterial composition in pullets. Control-fed birds under LSA showed the most abundant phylum, Proteobacteria, which is often associated with inflammation. This finding indicates that LSA might promote the growth of potentially harmful bacteria in the gut (Fathima et al., 2023). Interestingly, the highest abundance of Bacteroides fragilis from this study was observed in LSA. Some species within this bacterial genus can be pathogenic; however, others are considered beneficial bacteria that contribute to the degradation of complex carbohydrates and increase SCFA production, thereby reducing gut inflammation (Liu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2025).

At 8 woa, our data revealed different bacterial clustering and increased beneficial bacterial families, including Bifidobacteriaceae, Coriobacteriaceae, Barbesiellaceae, Bacteroidaceae, Lachnospiraceae, Erysipelotrichaceae, Lactobacillaceae, Rikenellaceae, and Ruminococcaceae, suggesting improved gut health with YB or N-3 FA dietary supplementation under LSA. Improved gut microbiota and intestinal integrity were reported in laying hens fed on omega-3 fatty acid-supplemented diets (Zhang et al., 2025). Our findings are consistent with other studies in highlighting dietary interventions as effective strategies to mitigate SA-associated stress and promote a balanced microbiota (Wulandari and Syahniar, 2018; Dong et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025)

Positive influences of dietary N-3 FA on gut health, microbial composition, and immune response in laying hens have been reported (Neijat et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2024). In-feed YB has also been found to improve gut integrity and bacterial diversity, which are important in enhancing digestive function and poultry health (Liu et al., 2021). Results from the present study show significant clustering of cecal bacteria from N-3 FA-fed LSA birds compared to those from other groups. Previous studies have also shown the potential of omega-3 fatty acids in enhancing gut health in birds under stressful conditions, such as LSA (Wan et al., 2023). The high abundance of Firmicutes and Bacteroidales in HSA birds indicated that SA impacts microbiota composition and diversity. The enrichment of Bacteroides fragilis in LSA birds deserves particular attention. Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is a metabolic disorder impacting laying hen (Zhang et al., 2025). It has been reported that Bacteroides fragilis exerts an anti-fatty liver hemorrhagic syndrome effect through modulation of oxylipin metabolism and gut microbiota stability, with a pivotal role played by arachidonic acid. Thus, data from this study suggest that feeding birds N-3 FA or YB under LSA could increase Bacteroides abundance, which could prevent fatty liver Hemorrhagic syndrome (FLHS) in layers (Zhang et al., 2025). Further research is needed to determine the benefits of N-3 FA or YB in feed on the effects of Bacteroides in layers.

Little is known about the role, persistence, type, and evolution of intestinal tract microbiota in layers. Such knowledge revealed by this study can help in the development of efficient feeding interventions (Bajagai et al., 2024). In older birds from week 35, culture-dependent bacteriology revealed that neither SA nor conventional diets showed a significant impact on ceca bacterial community structure and diversity, indicating a relatively resilient and stable microbiota after week 16. The predominant phyla identified include Firmicute, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria, which is consistent with other studies on the impact of stocking density, prebiotics, and probiotics on gut microbiota and health of laying hens (Neijat et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2021). Growing evidence has revealed that Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria play an essential role in fermenting carbohydrates, balancing the gut microbiota, and inhibiting pathogen colonization. (Wang et al., 2021). The presence of minor phyla, such as Synergistetes and Deferribacter, suggests a maintained microbial balance, independent of dietary interventions. The findings suggest that early dietary interventions supplemented with functional feed additives could exert a beneficial and long-term influence on microbial adaptation, immunity, and overall performance.

Acetate, propionate, and butyrate are the major SCFA generated simultaneously by bacterial fermentation of complex carbohydrates and soluble fibers in the gut (Nicholson et al., 2012). In the present study, SCFA concentrations were influenced by bird age, SA, and dietary interventions. A clear age-related trend was observed, with the highest SCFA levels detected at week 35, in agreement with other studies, indicating a bacterial community shift and improved gut health during different growth periods of laying hens (Sun et al., 2021). Birds reared under LSA exhibited elevated concentrations of propionic acid and iso-butyric acid at week 4 and 16, a pattern consistent with the previous report in laying hens under high stocking density (Wang et al., 2021), suggesting enhanced microbial fermentation efficiency, reduced stress associated with SA, and increased abundance of SCFA-producing bacteria taxa such as Lactobacillus. At week 35, the findings from this study under HSA demonstrated increased levels of acetate and propionate, which are known to support gut barrier function, energy metabolism, and immune modulation (Sergeant et al., 2014). However, LSA birds showed elevated lactate levels, indicative of enhanced epithelial integrity and immunity.

Dietary intervention also played a significant role in shaping the SCFA profile, as evidenced by improved n-butyrate production at week 16 following N-3 FA dietary supplementation. Butyrate is a critical metabolite serving as a primary energy source for colonocytes, strengthening gut barrier function and exerting immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects (Khan et al., 2020; Al-Khalaifah and Al-Nasser, 2021). The increased n-butyrate concentration in N-3 FA-supplemented birds suggests a selective enrichment of butyrate-producing bacterial taxa, potentially improving pullet gut health and resilience (Akhtar et al., 2022). Studies have shown that gut microbiota is associated with SCFA production, as evidenced in the present study, which provides insight into the complex microbial-SCFA metabolite interaction through dietary treatment. In N-3 FA-birds, a positive correlation between the family Barnesiellaceae and propionate indicated that N-3 FA might favor the proliferation of the propionate-producing bacterial group.

Furthermore, a positive correlation between Desulfovibrionaceae and Veillonellaceae and n-butyrate has also been observed in other studies involving a mouse model (Hong et al., 2021). These findings highlight the potential roles of these bacteria in butyrate metabolism. In YB-fed birds, Bifidobacteria showed a strong positive correlation with lactic acid production, consistent with the known prebiotic effect of yeast in promoting the growth of beneficial lactic acid-generating microbiota (Zhen et al., 2021). Interestingly, a negative correlation between Enterobacteriaceae and iso-butyric acid was observed, which may indicate the suppression of opportunistic pathogens in the gut under LSA YB-fed birds. These findings highlight the multifactorial influence of age, SA and targeted dietary intervention (YB or N-3 FA supplementation) on microbial ecology and SCFA production in the gut of layers. By modulating the abundance and activities of key microbial taxa, these strategies contribute to the enhanced gut barriers integrity, overall performance and enhanced immune functions in pullets before laying period.

Increasing SCFA production has been shown to attenuate lung injury or damage by modulating macrophage immune response (Wu et al., 2020; Verma et al., 2024). Peng et al. showed that dietary supplementation with baicalin in day-old chicks protects them against avian pathogenic E. coli (APEC), inducing inflammation by increasing the synthesis of SCFA in the gut (Peng et al., 2021). The gut microbiota and SCFA metabolite production can influence immune gene expression and development (Song et al., 2023). However, there are limited studies on the effect of functional dietary components on the development of the immune system in pullets and layers. Given the emerging link between the gut and lung, we hypothesized that SA and dietary intervention (N-3 FA or YB) might influence lung immunity in pullets. Our data showed that N-3 FA in birds under HSA significantly upregulated cytokine and IL2, which is essential for activation of T cells and natural killer cells (Kogut et al., 2020). Both basal and N-3 FA-supplemented diets fed to pullets upregulated IL1β, a key pro-inflammatory cytokine. This finding appears to contrast with that reported in mammals, in which dietary N-3 FA (EPA and DHA) has been shown to downregulate IL1β (Calder, 2006). However, avian species may exhibit species-specific immune responses. Sijben et al. (2001) reported that dietary fish oil reduced lymphocyte proliferation in broilers (Sijben et al., 2001), implying a suppression of T cell activity. Moreover, age-associated immune dynamics in pullets, especially during the early developmental stage, may influence their sensitivity to dietary components. It is also possible that basal diets contained an immunostimulatory element that primed an inflammatory response, which was further enhanced by N-3 FA supplementation. Therefore, the upregulation of IL1β in pullets suggests early-stage immune activation where N-3 FA enhances the immune system’s readiness, reflecting complex interactions between diets, species, and physiological status that warrant further investigation.

In the present study, the observed significant upregulation of IL8 expression in 16 woa pullets with YB under LSA indicates an enhanced pro-inflammatory response. This observation aligns with previous findings that demonstrate yeast beta-glucan increases IL8 expression in heat-stressed broiler birds (Zhang et al., 2020). These results collectively suggest that yeast-derived components may help pullets fight against infections and injuries by inducing pro-inflammatory cytokines (Zhang et al., 2020). On the other hand, the present study revealed MPO expression was downregulated at 16 woa in HSA YB-fed birds and pullets fed HSA- and LSA supplemented N-3 FA-diets. MPO, is an oxidative enzyme expressed in neutrophils, plays a key role in host defense by producing reactive oxygen species during inflammation. However, its excessive production is associated with oxidative tissue damage and inflammation (Wu et al., 2023). Consistent with current findings, fish oil-derived N-3 FA PUFA has been reported to suppress monocyte activity and lymphocyte proliferation in poultry (Al-Khalifa et al., 2012). Similarly, dietary yeast has been shown to decrease oxidative damage and immune activation cytokines (Gao et al., 2008), suggesting reduced neutrophil activation in poultry. These results highlight the immunomodulatory potential of N-3 FA- and YB-fed in pullets, whereby reduced MPO expression and associated oxidative damage may contribute to decreased inflammation and enhanced resilience to stress, especially in commercial poultry where SA can compromise welfare and performance. The cytokine IL1β plays a significant role as an anti-inflammatory agent (Dinarello, 1996), and its downregulation indicates a reduced inflammatory burden. Concurrently, the decrease in the anti-inflammatory IL10 expression suggests a balanced immune state. Yeast-based diets have been shown to decrease respiratory inflammation in poultry exposed to pathogens or stressors (Shanmugasundaram et al., 2013). The components, such as β-glucan, modulate cytokine expression in broiler birds, potentially limiting tissue damage while maintaining their function (Gao et al., 2008). The significant suppression of IL10 and IL1β in the lungs of HSA-YB-fed pullets indicates reduced immune activation. These findings suggest that HSA plays a role in maintaining lung immune homeostasis, potentially enhancing respiratory resilience and performance in commercial birds (Yitbarek et al., 2012).

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that SA and early dietary N-3 FA or YB influence cecal bacterial community structure, SCFA production, and lung gene expression in pullets. Enrichment of beneficial bacterial population in the ceca correlated with an increased SCFA levels and potential immunomodulatory effects in the lung. However, further gene expression and dose-response studies are needed to determine whether the tested N-3 FA and YB dosages were suboptimal. Large-scale trials are also recommended to validate the findings under a commercial set up.
