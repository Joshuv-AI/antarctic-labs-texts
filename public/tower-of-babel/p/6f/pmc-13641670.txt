Therapeutic landscape evolution for steatotic liver disease: from lifestyle interventions to precision pharmacotherapy
Authors: Shirley Huey Shin Bong, Kevin Kim Jun Teh, Vincent Wai-Sun Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSteatotic liver disease (SLD) is a growing global epidemic, with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease now the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and closely linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Lifestyle modification remains the cornerstone of management, with the Mediterranean diet as the preferred dietary intervention and emerging evidence supporting additional dietary and exercise strategies. Pharmacotherapy has advanced rapidly in recent years, with two U.S. Food and Drug Administration-approved options, resmetirom and semaglutide, for noncirrhotic metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis, alongside multiple agents in development targeting different disease mechanisms. In metabolic dysfunction- and alcohol-associated liver disease, evidence remains limited, but management essentially focuses on alcohol cessation and optimization of cardiometabolic risk factors, with resmetirom, incretin-based therapies, and fibroblast growth factor-21 analogues representing promising therapeutic agents that have yet to be fully evaluated in clinical trials. The field is increasingly moving toward a precision medicine paradigm integrating pharmacologic and lifestyle interventions, tailored to disease phenotype, genetic risk, and gut microbiome. Key challenges include heterogeneity in treatment response, assessment of alcohol intake, and real-world implementation barriers. This review summarizes current and emerging therapies and highlights the role of precision medicine in advancing individualized care across the SLD spectrum.Keywords: Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, Alcohol-related liver disease, Resmetirom, Semaglutide

INTRODUCTION

Steatotic liver disease (SLD), redefined by the 2023 Delphi consensus, encompasses three major categories, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), metabolic dysfunction- and alcohol-associated liver disease (MetALD), and alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD), distinguished by the presence of metabolic dysfunction and varying levels of alcohol intake [1]. SLD is a rapidly escalating global epidemic. MASLD is now the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide, with prevalence rising from 25% in 1990–2006 to 38% in 2016–2019 [2] and projected to exceed 50% by 2040 [3]. MetALD prevalence varies (1.7–17%) [4], and is expected to rise due to worsening cardiometabolic risk profiles [5] and persistent alcohol consumption [6].

SLD has major hepatic and extrahepatic conditions. In the United States, MASLD is now the leading indication for liver transplantation among women and individuals with hepatocellular carcinoma [3]. MASLD independently increases cardiovascular risk [7-9], particularly with advancing fibrosis [8,10]. Cardiovascular risk is even higher in individuals with MetALD [11]. Furthermore, SLD is associated with an increased risk of multiple cancers [12,13].

Recent therapeutic advances include the approval of resmetirom (2024) and semaglutide (2025) by U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for treatment for noncirrhotic metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) with moderate-to-advanced fibrosis. Numerous additional agents targeting metabolic and fibrotic pathways are under development. Despite these pharmacological breakthroughs, lifestyle interventions remain fundamental. This review summarizes current therapies and highlights the expanding role of precision medicine in SLD.

LIFESTYLE INTERVENTIONS

MASLD

Lifestyle interventions, including dietary modification and physical activity, remain central to MASLD management (Fig. 1). There is a clear dose–response relationship between weight loss and histologic improvement: ≥3–5% weight loss improves steatosis, ≥7–10% is typically required for resolution of steatohepatitis and inflammation, and ≥10% offers the greatest likelihood of fibrosis regression [14-16]. However, sustained weight loss in real-world settings remains challenging, with only a minority of patients reaching clinically meaningful targets [17].

The Mediterranean diet [15] is widely recommended, emphasizing minimally processed plant-based foods, lean proteins, and healthy fats, and is associated with reductions in hepatic steatosis and liver stiffness. Emerging approaches, including time-restricted eating, show promise in improving hepatic fat and metabolic parameters [18-20], although long-term sustainability and safety remain uncertain.

Dietary implementation is often limited by cultural and socioeconomic factors, underscoring the importance of culturally tailored dietary strategies [21]. Emerging studies are evaluating integrated approaches combining pharmacotherapy with personalized nutrition, such as a Mount Sinai study investigating resmetirom with medically tailored meals and dietitian-guided, culturally adapted nutrition based on Mediterranean diet principles [22].

Physical activity provides benefits independent of weight loss, including improvements in insulin sensitivity, hepatic steatosis, and cardiovascular risk. Guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity exercise weekly, incorporating aerobic and resistance training [15]. However, adherence remains suboptimal [23], and sedentary behaviour independently contributes to disease progression [24,25]. Novel approaches, such as the “weekend warrior” pattern, consolidating physical activity into one or two days per week, may confer comparable benefits as regular exercise [26].

A major limitation of lifestyle interventions is the response heterogeneity, influenced by individual factors such as baseline body mass index (BMI), disease severity, metabolic phenotype, and access to structured support [17]. Precision nutrition and exercise studies demonstrate substantial inter-individual variability in response to identical interventions, driven by differences in genetics, insulin resistance, and gut microbiome composition [27-32]. Mechanistically, host–microbiome interactions and immune–metabolic pathways further influence treatment response, supporting integration of multi-omic and microbiome-informed approaches into clinical care [33,34]. Collectively, these data support moving beyond a one-size-fits-all approach toward individualized, culturally adapted, and precision-based lifestyle interventions in MASLD.

MetALD and ALD

Lifestyle management in MetALD and ALD requires addressing both alcohol exposure and metabolic dysfunction. In MetALD, standard strategies mirror MASLD but must be combined with alcohol abstinence due to synergistic disease progression [35,36].

In ALD, sustained alcohol abstinence is the primary determinant of prognosis. Behavioural therapies—including cognitive behavioural therapy and motivational enhancement [37]—form the foundation of treatment, with pharmacologic therapies considered in selected patients [38].

Evidence on mild alcohol intake in MASLD is conflicting but favours caution. Earlier observational studies suggested mild alcohol consumption may be associated with lower risks of steatohepatitis and fibrosis [39-43], but these findings are limited by confounding. More recent data indicate that even low levels of alcohol may worsen liver outcomes, including fibrosis progression [44] and incident liver disease [35], with synergistic effects alongside metabolic dysfunction and insulin resistance [35,36]. Genetic factors, particularly the patatin-like phospholipase domain-containing 3 (PNPLA3) risk allele, further amplify risk [45,46]. Alcohol intake is also associated with a linear increase in hepatocellular carcinoma [40,47] and extrahepatic cancer risk [48]. Overall, no level of alcohol intake can be considered unequivocally safe in MASLD.

Achieving sustained abstinence remains challenging. Pharmacologic treatments are underutilized due to concerns regarding safety, stigma, and limited clinician experience [38]. Psychosocial interventions remain first-line, while emerging digital strategies—including remote monitoring and personalized feedback—offer promising adjuncts to improve engagement and long-term adherence [49].

Multidisciplinary approach and digital strategies in SLD

Given the multisystem nature of SLD, a multidisciplinary approach is essential. Integration of hepatology, endocrinology, primary care, and allied health services has been shown to improve both liver-related and cardiometabolic outcomes [50-53]. In parallel, digital health tools—including smartphone-based interventions, wearable devices, and artificial intelligence-driven platforms—offer scalable solutions to support behaviour change, improve adherence, and enable personalized lifestyle modification, with early studies demonstrating benefits in weight reduction and liver stiffness [54-56].

PHARMACOTHERAPY

Approval pathways

Currently, the US FDA and the European Medicines Agency consider conditional approval for drugs in noncirrhotic MASH if it resolves histological MASH and improves liver fibrosis in a Phase 3 study [57]. Full approval requires demonstration of benefit on major adverse liver outcomes (MALO), defined as progression to cirrhosis, hepatic decompensation, liver transplantation and liver-related death. Some studies also include the development of high-risk varices as a liver outcome. Conversely, there is no pathway for accelerated approval in MASH cirrhosis; thus, the focus remains on reducing MALO.

Reliance on liver histology and clinical outcomes presents several key challenges for drug development. First, while histological fibrosis correlates with future MALO, it is imperfect due to sampling, intra-rater, and inter-rater variability. Since histological response is based on two liver biopsies, this compounded variability undermines reliability of endpoint ascertainment [58]. Stringent inclusion criterion of MASH with F2-F3 fibrosis leads to high screen failure rates and patients’ reluctance to undergo repeated biopsies makes MASH drug development expensive and slow. Furthermore, chronic liver disease progresses over decades, requiring large patient cohorts followed over extended periods to detect MALO. Additionally, patients with F2 fibrosis at baseline are unlikely to develop MALO during the study.

Fortunately, recent studies indicate that many noninvasive tests (NITs) are noninferior to histology in predicting MALO, and clinically meaningful changes in some tests have been better defined [59-61]. The FDA has indicated openness to proposals for using NITs as surrogate endpoints in clinical trials [62]. It has also received letters of intent to qualify vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) and magnetic resonance elastography as reasonably likely surrogate endpoints (Fig. 2), The SYNERGY-Outcomes study (NCT06215716) is the first Phase 3 study to use NITs exclusively for patient enrolment. Liver biopsies and endoscopies will be performed based on NIT results to detect incident cirrhosis and high-risk varices, respectively, which will count as MALO. Ultimately, as reasonably likely surrogate endpoints become validated through correlation with MALO in trials, it may be possible to achieve drug approval based on NITs alone (Fig. 3).

There is currently no established approval pathway for drugs evaluated for treatment of MetALD, although many principles for MASH apply. Given the lack of precedent, this is a prime opportunity to establish a standard that prioritise NITs over liver biopsies in MetALD trials. Assessment of alcohol consumption will be a critical component in MetALD trials.

Resmetirom

Resmetirom is an oral, liver-selective thyroid hormone receptor-β (THR-β) agonist that enhances hepatic lipid metabolism, reducing liver fat accumulation and lipotoxicity (Table 1) [63]. Its efficacy in MASH has been consistently demonstrated across phase 2 and phase 3 clinical trials. In a 36-week phase 2 trial, resmetirom significantly reduced hepatic fat as measured by magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF) and multiple atherogenic lipids and lipoproteins compared with placebo [64].

The phase 3 MAESTRO-NASH trial further confirmed the efficacy of resmetirom. At 72 weeks, MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis was achieved in 25.9% and 29.9% of patients receiving 80 mg and 100 mg respectively, compared with 9.7% in the placebo group. Fibrosis improvement without worsening of MASH occurred in 24.2% and 25.9% of participants in the 80-mg and 100-mg groups, respectively, versus 14.2% among placebo recipients [65]. A secondary analysis including patients with “possible MetALD,” defined by elevated carbohydrate-deficient transferrin >2.5% or phosphatidylethanol (PEth) >20 ng/mL, demonstrated similar treatment responses regardless of alcohol use, suggesting efficacy across the MASLD-MetALD spectrum [66].

Resmetirom was generally well tolerated, with mainly mild gastrointestinal symptoms, including diarrhoea and nausea, while serious adverse events occurred at rates comparable to placebo.

Glucagon-Like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs)

GLP-1 RAs mimic the action of native GLP-1, inducing insulin while reducing glucagon secretion. Their hepatoprotective effects involve both weight-dependent and weight-independent mechanisms. By suppressing appetite and delaying gastric emptying [67], they promote weight loss [68]. Weight-independent factors include intracellular modulation resulting in improved fatty acid oxidation and lipid clearance from hepatocytes, suppression of lipid synthesis, reduction in hepatic expression of proinflammatory cytokines and down-regulation of macrophage-driven fibrogenic signalling [69]. In addition, the holistic benefit of GLP-1 RAs in reducing overall mortality and cardiovascular outcomes has been well-demonstrated [70].

Phase 2 and 3 trials have demonstrated promising histological outcomes (Table 2). Patients who received subcutaneous liraglutide 1.8 mg once weekly were 4.3 times more likely to achieve MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis compared to placebo [71]. 59% of patients who received subcutaneous semaglutide 0.4 mg daily achieved MASH resolution with no worsening of fibrosis [72] compared to 17% in the placebo group. However, in a separate phase 2 study evaluating compensated MASH-related cirrhosis [73], once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide 2.4 mg did not reverse cirrhosis or improve MASH resolution compared with placebo. Nonetheless, owing to semaglutide’s longer half-life and suitability for once-weekly dosing [74,75], the phase 3 ESSENCE trial (biopsy-proven MASH, F2 or F3 fibrosis) showed significant improvement in hepatic outcomes over placebo. MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis occurred in 62.9% versus 34.3% in placebo, while reduction in liver fibrosis without worsening of MASH was seen in 36.8% versus 22.4%.

GLP-1 RAs were generally well tolerated, with mild gastrointestinal and injection-site reaction being the most common adverse effects. Important safety considerations include contraindications in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2, and risks of pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, hypoglycemia (with co-administration of insulin or insulin secretagogues), renal impairment and diabetic retinopathy.

In MetALD, GLP-1 RAs were shown in a large retrospective cohort study [76] to have potential beneficial effect on incidence and recurrence of alcohol use disorders. This may be mediated by modulation of the brain dopamine reward system and stress responses. Randomized clinical trial data, albeit limited by sample size, showed the reduction of brain activation to alcohol cues [77] (based on functional magnetic resonance imaging), weekly cravings and thus alcohol consumption [78]. Larger trials are needed to further evaluate their role in MetALD.

Resmetirom versus GLP-1 RAs

Treatment selection between resmetirom and GLP-1 RAs in MASH should be individualized based on disease drivers and metabolic profile. Resmetirom targets hepatic lipid metabolism and demonstrates antifibrotic effects, making it best suited for patients with significant fibrosis (≥F2) where liver disease predominates, even without obesity or diabetes. In contrast, GLP-1 RAs provide systemic metabolic benefits, including weight loss and improved glycaemic control, and are therefore preferred in patients with obesity, type 2 diabetes, or increased cardiometabolic risk, although antifibrotic effects are less consistent [79,80].

An additional advantage of resmetirom is its oral administration, which may improve tolerability compared with GLP-1 RAs that require subcutaneous injection, making it a suitable option for patients who are intolerant to GLP-1 RAs.

Real-world treatment decisions are also shaped by access and healthcare system factors. Resmetirom is currently available mainly in the United States and selected European countries, limiting its use. GLP-1 RAs are widely available globally and already integrated into metabolic disease management, making them a more practical option in routine care and a pragmatic first-line therapy worldwide.

Other incretin therapies

The primary incretin hormones [81] are glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1. Dual-agonist agents targeting GIP and GLP-1 (tirzepatide [82]), glucagon and GLP-1 (survodutide [83], pemvidutide [84] and cotadutide [85]) and a triple-agonist targeting GIP, glucagon and GLP-1 (retatrutide [86]) have been evaluated in phase 2 studies. All agents were administered subcutaneously once weekly. Survodutide, pemvidutide and cotadutide differ in receptor potency ratios – pemvidutide is balanced while survodutide and cotadutide have higher GLP-1 ratios. Dual and triple receptor activation resulted in synergism driven by direct metabolic effects, central nervous system satiety signalling and improved systemic lipid metabolism [87].

Tirzepatide demonstrated substantial improvements in MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis, with up to a 52 percentage-point difference versus placebo. Similar benefits were observed with survodutide (62% in 4.8 mg versus 14% in placebo) and pemvidutide (38 percentage points in 1.2 mg). Cotadutide showed significant improvements in hepatic fat fraction, serum aminotransferases and metabolic parameters versus placebo [85], with phase 2b data demonstrating both MASH resolution without worsening of fibrosis and fibrosis improvement without worsening of MASH [88]. Retatrutide resulted in significant liver fat reduction (up to 82.4% in 12 mg), abdominal fat and metabolic measures associated with improved insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism [86].

Combination incretin agonists also achieved meaningful body weight reduction. Tirzepatide 15 mg was associated with 15.6% weight reduction, while retatrutide was associated with up to 25.9% weight reduction at 48 weeks, versus 0.1% with placebo. Gastrointestinal and injection-site reactions were common, with comparable serious adverse event rates across agents, despite the higher frequency of gastrointestinal symptoms during initial treatment with dual-agonist agents [79]. Results from ongoing phase 3 studies will be pivotal in establishing the role of combination incretin therapies for MASH treatment.

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) analogues

FGF21 is hormone primarily secreted by hepatocytes in response to stress from excessive ingestion of fructose or alcohol [89]. It exerts beneficial metabolic effects by increasing peripheral insulin sensitivity and enhancing lipid clearance from hepatocytes [90]. FGF21 modulates inflammatory pathways by shifting macrophages toward a pro-repair phenotype and suppressing differentiation of hepatic stellate cells into myofibroblasts [91]. Chronic elevation of endogenous FGF21 in MASH may result in a state of FGF21 resistance [90], providing the rationale for developing long-acting analogues.

Efruxifermin, administered weekly, achieved fibrosis improvement without worsening of MASH in 30–49% of patients with F2-F3 fibrosis, compared with 19% in placebo [92], although the same outcome in established cirrhosis was not achieved [93]. Pegozafermin showed dose-dependent improvements, with 14–20 percentage point increases in fibrosis responses versus placebo [94]. Efimosfermin, a longeracting FGF21 analogue achieved fibrosis improvement in 45% of patients at higher doses compared with 21% in placebo [95,96].

FGF21 analogues are generally well tolerated, with mild gastrointestinal symptoms and injection-site reactions being the most common adverse events. A transient increase in appetite has been reported. Concerns regarding bone health [97] and increased tone of the hypothalamic-pituitaryadrenal and sympathoadrenal axes [98] remain, although causality remains uncertain due to lack of reproducibility and confounders. Ongoing phase 3 trials with longer follow-up will better inform long-term safety.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) agonists

PPAR activation regulates adipogenesis, improves insulin sensitivity [99] in adipocytes and stimulates systemic fatty acid oxidation, resulting in reduced triglyceride accumulation [100]. MASH patients have reduced hepatocyte PPAR expression, with PPAR activation being associated with hepatic stellate cell inactivation [101]. Pioglitazone, a PPARγ agonist, was evaluated alongside vitamin E against placebo, with pioglitazone showing significant improvement in transaminases, steatosis and lobular inflammation, without improvement in fibrosis [102]. Saroglitazar, a dual PPAR agonist (α and γ), has shown significant alanine aminotransferase (ALT), liver fat, homeostatic model assessment-insulin resistance and triglyceride improvement in phase 2 studies [103]. Lanifibranor, a pan-PPAR agonist (α, δ and γ), has demonstrated promising results in phase 2 trials, with nearly half of treated patients achieving MASH resolution or fibrosis improvement compared with placebo, and is currently in phase 3 evaluation for F2 and F3 fibrosis [104]. Evaluated PPAR agonists have been found to be tolerable, with common adverse events being diarrhoea, nausea, peripheral oedema and weight gain [105].

De-novo lipogenesis (DNL) inhibitors

In addition to resmetirom, therapeutic agents targeting alternative mechanisms involved in DNL are under development, aimed at reducing hepatic production and accumulation of fatty acids. DNL inhibition has also been postulated to lower ceramide-modulated paracrine signalling associated with hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis [106]. These agents include fatty acid synthase inhibitors (denifanstat [107]), acetyl-CoA carboxylase inhibitors (firsocostat [108], clesacostat [109]), diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 2 inhibitors (ervogastat [109]) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 modulators (aramchol [110]). Notably, however, the combination of ervogastat and clesacostat in the phase 2 clinical study was associated with increased levels of serum triglycerides, apolipoprotein C3 and E, raising concerns for increased risk for cardiovascular events. Inadequate diabetes control and diarrhoea were observed with ervogastat and clesacostat use, the former largely due to the need to reduce metformin dosage in light of potential drug-drug interaction, while dry eye symptoms and alopecia were reported in denifanstat. Results from further studies are needed to ascertain the safety and efficacy of DNL inhibitors in MASH treatment.

Gene therapy

Gene-targeted therapies using small interfering RNA (siRNA) and antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) targeting PNPLA3 [111] and hydroxysteroid 17-beta dehydrogenase 13 (HSD17B13) [112] have entered clinical development, aiming to induce loss-of-function protective effects against MASLD and MASH.

Early-phase data are promising. The siRNA agent AROPNPLA3 (JNJ-75220795) showed approximately a 40% reduction in liver fat content by MRI-PDFF at 400 mg, without any serious adverse events [113]. The ASO agent AZD2693 [114] targeting PNPLA3 showed a 12.2% relative reduction in hepatic steatosis at 50 mg although subsequent phase 2 data (FORTUNA) did not meet expectations. Two siRNA agents targeting HSD17B13, ARO-HSD (GSK4532990) [115] and rapirosiran [115], have shown substantial reductions (~78%) in hepatic mRNA expression and favourable safety profiles in phase 1 studies, with ongoing phase 2 evaluation. Across studies, adverse events have been mild, primarily injection-site reactions. However, key concerns remain, including potential host immune response, insertional mutagenesis from unintended effects of gene editing [116] and off-target effects. Further studies are required to establish the clinical efficacy and role of gene-based therapies in MASH treatment.

Microbiome therapy

Gut dysbiosis plays a key role in the pathogenesis of MASLD through disruption of the gut–liver axis, promoting inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and fibrogenesis. Alterations in microbial composition—characterised by depletion of beneficial taxa and enrichment of pro-inflammatory species—contribute to increased intestinal permeability, endotoxemia, and altered bile acid and lipid metabolism [117-120].

These mechanisms provide a rationale for microbiome-targeted therapies. Prebiotics, probiotics, and synbiotics have demonstrated modest improvements in hepatic steatosis and metabolic parameters, primarily through restoration of short-chain fatty acid production and gut barrier function [121-125]. However, clinical evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, with a lack of standardised formulations and variable treatment responses across individuals [34,126].

Emerging strategies, including engineered microbial consortia, faecal microbiota transplantation, and metabolite-based therapies, aim to more precisely modulate gut microbial function.127 Within a precision medicine framework, integrating host–microbiome interactions with clinical and metabolic profiling may enable more targeted and individualised interventions. Nonetheless, further large-scale and long-term studies are required to establish efficacy and safety before routine clinical implementation.

PATIENT SELECTION FOR TREATMENT

While Phase 2b and 3 studies typically enrol patients based on strict histological criteria, requiring liver biopsy before treatment initiation is unrealistic in real-world practice. Consequently, the US FDA has endorsed starting treatment based on NITs in the labels for resmetirom and semaglutide. The American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) recommends using VCTE, magnetic resonance elastography, or enhanced liver fibrosis tests to select patients for treatment [128,129]. These tests identify patients with likely F2-F3 fibrosis while excluding those with cirrhosis. As data on the efficacy and safety of certain therapeutic agents in compensated cirrhosis emerge, exclusion of cirrhosis may become unnecessary, streamlining the selection process.

Notably, while trials often enrol patients with MASH and F2-F3 fibrosis, the recommended NITs focus solely on fibrosis. This is appropriate, as NITs for fibrosis are more established than those for MASH [130]. Longitudinal studies suggest that MASH provides limited incremental prognostic value over fibrosis alone [131]. The remaining question is whether some drugs may be less effective in patients with significant fibrosis but low disease activity, as such patients are currently excluded from trials.

TREATMENT MONITORING

Although various NITs have proved diagnostic and prognostic [59-61,130], their performance as response biomarkers is less established. This is primarily because response biomarkers can only be evaluated in the context of effective treatments, which have only recently become available. Consequently, while AASLD guidance suggested indicators of treatment response, treatment cessation was recommended only when NITs indicated worsening liver disease [128,129]. It is important to recognis that while histological responses in clinical trials are considered binary, actual responses exist on a spectrum. For instance, a patient showing no steatosis, no lobular inflammation, and a ballooning score that decreases from 2 to 1 after treatment would not be classified as having MASH resolution in a clinical trial, despite it being a clear treatment response.

Current NITs for MASH resolution show variable performance. A relative reduction in MRI-PDFF of ≥30% and a decrease in serum ALT of ≥17 U/L have consistently correlated with histological response [132]. The FibroScan-AST score combining liver stiffness measurement (LSM), and controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) from VCTE and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) has demonstrated reductions associated with MASH resolution [133]. A newer NIT, fibrotic MASH resolutionVCTE index, which integrates baseline and dynamic changes in the acFibroMASH score (comprising serum creatinine, serum AST, LSM, ALT), is used to identify fibrotic MASH resolution and predict clinical outcomes, demonstrating good diagnostic accuracy, and outperforming FAST while also demonstrating strong prognostic value [134].

As for NITs for fibrosis, they take longer to reflect improvements. Additionally, some biomarkers like VCTE may be confounded by adiposity, leading to misleading reductions in liver stiffness without actual fibrosis improvement in patients undergoing weight loss treatments such as GLP-1 RAs [133].

ALCOHOL ASSESSMENT

Assessing alcohol consumption is crucial for classifying SLD. MetALD is defined by the presence of at least one cardiometabolic risk factor and increased alcohol consumption (30–59 g per day for men and 20–49 g for women), while ALD involves excessive alcohol intake (≥60 g per day for men and ≥50 g for women) [1]. Patients with MASLD often underreport their alcohol consumption, which can be both unintentional and intentional due to stigma and cultural factors [135].

Alcohol consumption is traditionally assessed through self-reporting and structured tools, such as the Timeline Followback method, often deemed the gold standard [136]. However, this time-consuming method is rarely used in routine practice. The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, particularly the condensed 3-item AUDIT-C questionnaire, is more practical. However, self-reported measures are frequently affected by underreporting. Recently, several alcohol biomarkers have gained traction in liver research. Notably, blood PEth levels reflect alcohol consumption in the past 2–4 weeks and can complement other assessments, roughly correlating with MetALD and ALD at cutoffs of 20 and 200 ng/mL [135,137]. POTENTIAL FOR PRECISION MEDICINE

Since the association between PNPLA3 polymorphism and MASLD was discovered in 2008 [138], at least 15 single nucleotide polymorphisms linked to MASLD and its severity have been identified [139]. As noted earlier, gene silencing strategies for PNPLA3 and HSD17B13 are now in clinical development. If genetic treatments become available, patient selection will depend on genotype. Notably, it remains to be seen whether MASLD genes also influence responses to other therapeutic agents. Several studies indicate that carriers of the PNPLA3 G allele experience a greater reduction in hepatic fat during lifestyle interventions [140]. In the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, the protective effects of a healthy diet and coffee consumption on liver-related mortality were more pronounced in those with the GG genotype [141]. Additionally, G allele carriers showed greater ALT reduction after semaglutide treatment [142].

The underlying pathophysiology differs across SLD. MASLD is primarily driven by metabolic dysfunction, particularly insulin resistance, leading to hepatic steatosis, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation. Emerging data highlight heterogeneity within MASLD, with subtypes associated with differing risks of MALO and cardiovascular events, suggesting potential variable therapeutic responses [143,144]. ALD results from direct ethanol-related hepatotoxicity, including acetaldehyde-mediated injury, oxidative stress, and gut barrier disruption. MetALD represents a synergistic overlap, where metabolic and alcohol-related mechanisms amplify inflammation and fibrosis, conferring a higher risk of disease progression.

These mechanistic differences guide treatment strategies. MASLD management focuses on metabolic optimisation including weight loss and control of cardiometabolic risk factors. ALD requires alcohol abstinence as the cornerstone of therapy, supported by addiction and nutritional interventions. MetALD necessitates a dual approach, targeting both metabolic dysfunction and alcohol intake.

Clinically, outcomes follow a risk gradient, with worsening prognosis from MASLD to MetALD to ALD, associated with higher risks of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma [145,146], and cardiovascular and cancer-related mortality [145,147-149]. This underscores the need for risk-adapted management, including intensified monitoring and broader screening for complications as alcohol contribution increases.

Lean MASH represents a distinct phenotype in nonobese individuals, characterized by visceral adiposity, metabolic dysfunction, and genetic susceptibility despite normal BMI [150-152]. Altered lipid metabolism, bile acid signalling and gut microbiota contribute to disease pathogenesis [151,153]. Management differs in that modest weight loss (3–5%) may achieve remission, and patients are more likely to sustain improvements in weight [154]. Pharmacotherapy data remain limited as this group is often excluded from trials. While incretin-based therapies may benefit cardiometabolic risk, agents targeting intrahepatic pathways (e.g., THR-β agonists, FGF21 analogues, PPAR agonists) may be more mechanistically appropriate.

Collectively, these insights emphasize the need for individualized, mechanism-based management strategies across the SLD spectrum, reinforcing the shift toward precision medicine. Failure to account for underlying disease etiology may lead to suboptimal therapeutic response, misinterpretation of treatment efficacy, and missed opportunities to address key drivers of disease progression, such as ongoing alcohol exposure.

REAL-WORLD IMPLEMENTATION OF MANAGEMENT

Accessibility, eligibility, equity, and cost-effectiveness are critical considerations in the implementation of SLD therapies. While lifestyle modification is broadly applicable, its effectiveness is often limited by socioeconomic barriers, health literacy, access to structured programmes, and challenges with long-term adherence. Multidisciplinary care models and community-based interventions may promote more equitable access to care. For pharmacologic therapies, clinical use is influenced by eligibility criteria (e.g., fibrosis stage and comorbidities), cost, reimbursement policies, and healthcare system variability. Although newer agents show promise, their uptake may be constrained by affordability and implementation challenges, particularly in resource-limited settings.

The most practical and effective approach to SLD management involves a stepwise, risk-stratified strategy. Initial management should prioritise lifestyle interventions, including achievable weight loss targets, dietary modification, and promotion of regular physical activity, supported by simple tools such as brief counselling printed resources, or digital platforms. Use of non-invasive tests (e.g., FIB-4 and elastography where available) enables identification of patients at higher risk of advanced fibrosis, who may benefit from specialist referral and consideration of pharmacotherapy.

Pharmacologic treatment should be selectively applied to patients with more severe disease or those unable to achieve adequate response with lifestyle measures alone. Multidisciplinary and community-based models—incorporating primary care, dietitians, bariatric surgeons and allied health professionals—can enhance feasibility and adherence. Overall, an individualised yet pragmatic approach that balances clinical benefit with resource availability is most suitable for real-world implementation.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Future directions in SLD will focus on advancing diagnostic frameworks and therapeutic strategies to enable more precise care. A key priority is validation and regulatory acceptance of NITs as clinical trial endpoints, allowing reliable assessment of disease activity and treatment response without liver biopsy. Increasingly, NITs are evolving into composite, multi-domain models integrating imaging and biochemical markers, incorporating baseline values and dynamic changes over time. This approach enhances diagnostic accuracy while improving prognostic and clinical utility, enabling better prediction of long-term outcomes and more informed treatment decisions.

In parallel, the field is shifting toward a personalized medicine paradigm that integrates pharmacologic and lifestyle interventions, with treatment tailored to individual liver disease characteristics, metabolic phenotype, genetic risk, and gut microbiome profile. There remains a critical need for research in underrepresented populations, including patients with MetALD, ALD, and lean MASH, who are often excluded from current trials despite distinct pathophysiology and clinical trajectories.

Finally, addressing real-world implementation challenges, such as accessibility, treatment eligibility, health equity, and cost-effectiveness, will be essential to ensure that advances in SLD translate into meaningful improvements in population health outcomes.

CONCLUSION

Thanks to the concerted efforts of basic scientists and clinical researchers, resmetirom and semaglutide have received accelerated approval for treating MASH, with additional agents in late-phase development. As our understanding of the biology and natural history of SLD expands, the potential for precision medicine becomes increasingly viable. To realise this potential, ongoing collaboration among researchers is essential to define the optimal use of noninvasive fibrosis tests, genetic assessments, and new biomarkers, paving the way for precision treatment strategies. Meanwhile, MetALD is a newly recognised disease entity, and its optimal management requires further studies.
