Dye Removal Utilizing ZIF-71 and Hydrophobic ZIF-71(ClBr): Kinetic and Equilibrium Studies
Authors: Martín Martínez-Salazar, Norma Tiempos Flores, Arturo Obregón-Zúñiga, Oscar I. Arillo-Flores, Nancy E. Dávila-Guzmán, David J. Michaelis, José Raziel Álvarez, Eugenio Hernández-Fernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the present work, methylene blue (MB) was removed from aqueous solutions using ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) as adsorbents. The ZIFs were synthesized at room temperature, and their structures were confirmed by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). Nitrogen physisorption experiments showed high specific surface areas of 831.24 m2 g–1 and 815.17 m2 g–1 for ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr), respectively. Adsorption optimal conditions of pH and contact time were evaluated. The adsorption data fitted well with the pseudo-first-order and the Langmuir isotherm model for ZIF-71, whereas ZIF-71­(ClBr) fitted well with the pseudo-second-order and the Freundlich isotherm model. The Langmuir model showed the ZIFs’ maximum adsorption capacities (qm ) of 597.92 mg g–1 and 191.56 mg g–1. Kinetic analysis showed that ZIF-71­(ClBr) reaches equilibrium faster than ZIF-71, revealing the effect of differences in their pore sizes. Moreover, density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate that the minimal structural deformation required for ZIF-71­(ClBr) plays an important role in achieving this behavior. The efficiency of both ZIFs was evaluated by performing adsorption–desorption cycles, reaching four cycles.

Introduction

Water contamination is an environmental problem of significant importance globally, where one main challenge for society is the selective removal of trace contaminants, such as dyes and pharmaceuticals. Dyes are one of the major pollutants, whose annual production worldwide is more than 2 million tons due to their use in plastic, food, paper, textiles, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical industries. , The textile industry alone uses more than 10,000 tons of dyes annually, generating many different wastes containing high concentrations of dyes discharged into streams. Many dyes in industrial wastewater are toxic and carcinogenic. Among the most common dyes found in contaminated water is methylene blue (MB), which is used in the textile industry for dyeing silk, wool, and cotton. This compound can cause burns, nausea, vomiting, and permanent eye injuries. For this reason, it is essential that we learn to reduce the concentration of these dyes in industrial waste and remove them from natural water sources.

Various strategies are available for dye removal, including advanced oxidation, membrane separation, and adsorption. Adsorption into porous materials is considered one of the most effective methods for removing inorganic substances and organic contaminants from effluents due to its ease of operation, low cost, and high efficiency. In this sense, adsorption requires porous solid materials that have high specific surface areas, large pore sizes, fast adsorption kinetics, and good thermal and mechanical stability. One of the most used adsorbents is activated carbon (AC) because of its high adsorption efficiency, high surface area, and large pores in its structure; however, it is a very expensive adsorbent due to its high production and regeneration costs. In addition, AC is difficult to separate from water, which may introduce secondary contaminants into the wastewater being treated. Therefore, the discovery of more economical alternatives, such as zeolites and clays, is an important area of research.

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are porous materials formed by three- and two-dimensional networks of metal complexes with organic ligands. Because of their high surface area and uniform and modifiable pore size, they are considered potential materials for adsorption, separation, and catalysis. Zeolitic imidazole frameworks (ZIFs) make up a class of MOFs that possess high thermal and chemical stability. They are formed by transition metal cations, commonly Zn­(II) and Co­(II), and imidazole derivatives as ligands. Substitution with different functional groups on the imidazolate linkers significantly influences adsorption performance through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic and dispersion forces.

Another critical factor in adsorption is the hydrophobicity of the adsorbent. Hydrophobic adsorbents do not interact with water molecules, enhancing the interaction between the adsorbent and the adsorbate. The water contact angle (θw) is a parameter that measures the angle formed between the solid and liquid surface. High values of θw (>90°) refer to hydrophobic adsorbents, whereas lower values (<90°) are considered hydrophilic.

Among all ZIFs, ZIF-8 and ZIF-67 are the most studied and used for the adsorption of pollutants. Their high usefulness derives from their high surface areas of approximately 1200 m2 g–1 for ZIF-8 and 1700 m2 g–1 for ZIF-67, whose surface area depends on the different conditions of synthesis. ZIF-8 presents a water contact angle (θw) of 97°, while ZIF-67 is 58°. These materials are more hydrophobic than common activated carbon adsorbents (θw = 38°) and can be used as adsorbents for several dyes, including methylene blue, , methyl orange, and Congo red. ,

Despite their success, the development of ZIFs with even higher hydrophobicity remains limited, restricting their potential to enhance the adsorption performance of pollutants. To date, studies have not fully explored the relationship between the hydrophobicity and dye adsorption efficiency. In the search for improved hydrophobic adsorbents, ZIF-71 emerges as a promising material as it exhibits a higher θw of 108°. Owing to its hydrophobic nature, Li et al. demonstrated that this property enhances the adsorption of Congo red.

To address this gap, ZIF-71­(ClBr) was synthesized and tested in recent work for the adsorption of butanol, resulting in a θw of 126.79°, greater than those reported for ZIF-71, ZIF-8, and ZIF-67. To the best of our knowledge, there are no recent reports of the removal of methylene blue using ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) as adsorbents. Within this framework, this study aims to investigate the adsorption performance of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr), exploring the correlation between hydrophobicity and adsorption capacity. Evaluating these materials provides novel insights into the potential of highly hydrophobic ZIFs for dye adsorption and pollutant removal.

In this study, ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) were synthesized at room temperature and used as promising adsorbents that exhibit notable performance for the removal of methylene blue in an aqueous solution. ZIFs were characterized using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), and nitrogen physisorption techniques. The effects of pH, initial dye concentration, and temperature on adsorption performance were investigated through batch experiments, with measurements conducted using UV–vis spectroscopy. Additionally, kinetics and isotherms were studied to provide deeper insights into the adsorption mechanism. The reusability of the materials was also evaluated to determine their practical applicability. Finally, the stability of the ZIF frameworks was carried out by measuring the concentration of leached Zn2+ ions using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) and by analyzing structural changes postadsorption using FTIR. Complementarily, DFT calculations were conducted to better understand and describe the adsorption mechanism of the adsorbate molecule on the surface of the ZIFs.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr)

The synthesis of ZIF-71­(ClBr) was carried out following our previously reported work, and ZIF-71 (Figure ) was synthesized following the procedure of Babu et al. with slight modifications. White powder solids were obtained with a yield of 72.6% and 48.5% for ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr), respectively, according to the initial mass of the starting material.

Chemical structures of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr).

The functional groups on the surfaces of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) were identified by FTIR analysis (Figure ). Neither ZIF exhibited a stretching band of N–H, indicating that the reaction proceeded as expected. Additionally, an intense band from C–N bond absorption appeared at 1035 cm–1, and the 1194–1225 cm–1 band corresponded to the imidazolate ring’s in-plane bending. , The medium band at 663 cm–1 corresponded to the out-of-plane ring deformation.

FTIR spectra of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr).

XRD analysis was carried out to investigate the phase structures of the two ZIF samples. As shown in Figure , the diffraction patterns of both ZIFs exhibited the characteristic peaks of ZIF-71. The diffraction peaks at 2θ = 4.3°, 6.2°, and 7.5° represented the (001), (002), and (112) planes of ZIF-71. No additional peaks were detected for ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr), as reported in the literature. ZIF-71­(ClBr) shows lower intensity peaks compared to the ones observed in ZIF-71.

Diffraction pattern of ZIF-71 ref , ZIF-71, and ZIF-71­(ClBr).

Finally, N2 physisorption results indicated a typical Type I isotherm (Figure ), as classified by IUPAC, which is characteristic of microporous materials. The surface area was calculated by applying the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method according to the criteria of Rouquerol. The obtained surface area was 831.24 m2 g–1 and 815.17 m2 g–1 for ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr), respectively, consistent with values previously reported in the literature. The determination of the average pore size showed that ZIF-71 has a larger pore size of 2.22 nm in comparison to that of ZIF-71­(ClBr), which is 0.69 nm. ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) showed a pore size distribution centered at 15.03 Å, which is higher than the 13 Å from the original ZIF-71 (Figure S1).

Effect of pH

The methylene blue adsorption capacity of both ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) at different pH was first tested. As shown in Figure , the removal percentage for MB decreased as the pH increased from 4 to 9, indicating that MB adsorption is most effective under acidic conditions. According to the literature, the pK a value of MB is 3.8, meaning that at pH levels above 3.8, the cationic form of the dye predominates. Additionally, the reported isoelectric point of ZIF-71 is 9.6, indicating that at lower pH values, the surface charge of ZIF-71 is predominantly positive, leading to electrostatic repulsions with the cationic dye.

Adsorption of MB by ZIFs (conditions: Co = 30 mg L–1, time = 120 min, adsorbent = 150 mg, and pH = 4–10).

The optimal pH value for adsorption with both ZIFs was 4, with removal efficiencies of 85% for ZIF-71 and 97% for ZIF-71­(ClBr). This is attributed to the fact that at pH 4 (close to the pK a value of the MB), approximately 50% of the MB molecules are in their neutral form.

Effect of Temperature

The adsorption thermodynamics were analyzed to evaluate the adsorption of MB, and the parameters are summarized in Tables and . The Gibbs free energy (ΔG 0) values are negative across all studied temperatures, indicating that the adsorption process is spontaneous. The positive enthalpy (ΔH 0) indicates that the adsorption of MB onto the ZIFs augments with the temperature increase, likely due to the energetic cost of liberating the cationic MB from its solvation shell as it approaches the hydrophobic adsorbent surface to allow interactions between MB and the imidazolate linkers of the ZIFs′ framework to be established. The values of ΔG 0 and ΔH 0 being less than 20 kJ mol–1 and 40 kJ mol–1, respectively, indicate that the adsorption mechanism predominantly involves physical adsorption. , Particularly, the positive values obtained for entropy (ΔS 0) confirm that the organization produced at the liquid–solid interface is surpassed by the randomness gained from solvent molecules exiting solute solvation shells.

The activation energies calculated for ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) were 23.685 kJ mol–1 and 6.171 kJ mol–1, respectively. Both values are significantly below 40 kJ mol–1 which differentiates physical adsorption from chemical adsorption; such classification points out that the adsorption process is mainly dominated by physical interactions rather than other chemical processes. Thermodynamic insights reveal that the early stages of adsorption can markedly change the energetics of the overall process, underlying the importance of the adsorbate properties in solution.

Effect of Initial Concentration

The MB adsorption capacity of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) was determined. As shown in Figure , the MB adsorption capacity sharply increases with the initial concentration of MB. For ZIF-71, at a high concentration of MB of 2695 mg L–1, the removal of MB was 79%, with an adsorption capacity of 426.28 mg g–1; however, at an initial concentration of 3367 mg L–1, the removal decreased to 63%, and the adsorption capacity reached a maximum of 421.28 mg g–1. For ZIF-71­(ClBr), at a high concentration of MB of 1160 mg L–1, the removal of MB was 75%, with an adsorption capacity of 174.43 mg g–1. Similarly, at an initial concentration of 1576 mg L–1, adsorption decreased to 54%, and the adsorption capacity decreased to 171.00 mg g–1. This behavior might be attributed to the difference in pore size between the compounds, in which ZIF-71 exhibits a larger pore size than ZIF-71­(ClBr), allowing for greater dye adsorption into the pore cavities and not just at the surface.

Effect of initial concentration of MB by ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) (conditions: pH = 4, adsorbent = 150 mg, time = 75 min, Co = 30–2500 mg L–1). Error bars represent means ± standard deviation from the mean of a triplicate experiment.

In addition, FTIR analyses were conducted to study the integrity of the materials (Figure S2). The characteristic bands of the ZIFs remain stable after the adsorption of MB molecules, suggesting that the framework structure of the ZIFs is preserved during the adsorption process. This stability indicates that the adsorption primarily occurs on the surface or within the pores without causing significant disruption of the coordination bonds between the metal ions and imidazolate linkers.

Adsorption Kinetics

The adsorption kinetics of MB onto ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) were evaluated at an initial MB concentration of 30 mg L–1, pH 4, and 25 °C. As shown in Figure , the decay of the MB concentration increased over time, from 0 to 120 min. Within the first 10 min, 34.3% of MB was removed by ZIF-71, while ZIF-71­(ClBr) achieved 80% removal. ZIF-71­(ClBr) reached equilibrium faster than ZIF-71, 15 and 70 min, respectively. In addition, at equilibrium, ZIF-71­(ClBr) removed 96.7% of MB, compared to 88.4% removal by ZIF-71.

Kinetics of MB adsorption by ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr). Error bars represent means ± standard deviation from the mean of a triplicate experiment.

These results indicate that at an initial MB concentration of 30 mg L–1, ZIF-71­(ClBr) exhibits faster kinetics and a higher adsorption capacity than ZIF-71. The enhanced hydrophobic nature of ZIF-71­(ClBr), compared to ZIF-71, has been shown to increase its affinity for MB molecules. To gain further insight into the adsorption kinetics of these materials, a mass transfer model that includes a lumped kinetic rate constant (k 1), which accounts for both external and internal mass transfer resistances, was applied. As expected, ZIF-71 exhibited a lower k 1 value (2.81 × 10–4 L/g·s) compared to ZIF-71­(ClBr) (3.13 × 10–3 L/g·s), indicating that ZIF-71 faces greater restrictions to mass transfer.

The molecular size of MB (1.7 nm × 0.8 nm) is smaller than the pore size of ZIF-71 (2.22 nm), which facilitates diffusion into the pores of ZIF-71, resulting in a slower adsorption process to reach equilibrium. In contrast, the pore size of ZIF-71­(ClBr) (0.69 nm) is smaller than that of the MB molecule, suggesting that adsorption predominantly occurs on the surface of the adsorbent, thereby reducing the time required to reach equilibrium.

Additionally, the external film mass transfer coefficient (k f) can be estimated from the initial stages of the adsorption process. The k f values for ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) were found to be 3.05 × 10–8 and 2.38 × 10–6 m/s, respectively, further confirming that the adsorption of MB occurs more rapidly on ZIF-71­(ClBr) than on ZIF-71 at an initial MB concentration of 30 mg L–1.

Adsorption Mechanism

The adsorption of methylene blue onto ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) can be driven by physical interactions (Figure ). The high hydrophobicity of these materials facilitates the exclusion of water molecules from the surface, enhancing the interaction between the dye and the adsorbent. This hydrophobic interaction likely plays a dominant role, particularly for ZIF-71­(ClBr), with its superior θw of 126.79°.

Proposed mechanism of ZIFs and MB molecules.

In addition to hydrophobic interactions, other physical interactions significantly contribute to the adsorption mechanism. Studies by Santoso et al. and Zhang et al. have highlighted common interactions between dyes and various ZIFs. These works have proposed that the approach of the adsorbate toward the ZIF surface is mediated by the nitrogen atoms in the linkers through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and π–π stacking interactions, depending on the dye′s ionic state in solution.

Adsorption–Desorption Cycles

As shown in the FTIR spectra (Figure S2), the consistent presence of the ZIF characteristic bands reinforces the robustness of the ZIF materials under the experimental conditions and highlights their suitability for repeated adsorption cycles. Therefore, after dye adsorption, the recovered adsorbent was washed with methanol to remove the adsorbed dye and then dried. The collected ZIFs were reused for another adsorption cycle (Figure ). For ZIF-71­(ClBr), four cycles were performed, where the level of removal of methylene blue remained above 95% after three cycles and then decreased to 71%. Moreover, the MB molecule did not desorb in the case of ZIF-71. After the sample was washed with fresh methanol and dried according to the same procedure, only 2.5% removal was observed, assuming a strong interaction between the dye and ZIF-71.

Cycles of reuse for ZIF-71­(ClBr). Error bars represent means ± standard deviation from the mean of a triplicate experiment.

The safe disposal of spent adsorbents is a critical consideration in environmental applications, particularly for materials that may release toxic components upon degradation. For ZIF-based materials, proper disposal strategies must account for the potential leaching of metal ions and adsorbed pollutants. Despite this, regeneration steps would help reduce waste and minimize environmental impacts; alternatively, some other treatments can be applied, like carbon treatment in several studies. While this study primarily focuses on the adsorption performance of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr), future research could explore the development of disposal or recycling protocols designed for ZIF-based materials, ensuring their safe and sustainable use in environmental applications.

Stability in Solution of ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr)

To further evaluate the chemical stability of the ZIFs, atomic absorption spectroscopy was conducted to understand the integrity of the materials through the determination of the Zn2+ concentration in the aqueous solution. Table summarizes the final Zn concentrations for all of the study samples, revealing that they remain below the WHO permissible limit of 5 mg L–1, with an exception in the case of ZIF-71­(ClBr)/Ads. Interestingly, the interaction between the ZIF framework and MB molecules appears to enhance the leaching of Zn2+ ions. This phenomenon may be attributed to the weakening of the Zn-linker coordination bonds caused by these interactions, potentially leading to a partial breakdown of the framework. Further detailed experiments are required to investigate the nature and extent of this interaction, which falls beyond the scope of the present study.

DFT Results

Before analyzing the adsorption energy, charge transfer processes, or intermolecular interactions, it was essential to perform geometric optimization of the structures being studied. The considered adsorbate–adsorbent systems display two distinct interaction modes between the adsorption sites of the ZIFs and MB, as illustrated in Figure . When the molecular plane of MB approaches the top of two imidazole rings (M1), it becomes more aligned with their planes, and halogen atoms interact directly with sulfur and methyl groups; this motif presents higher interaction energies. However, this arrangement resulted in a significant reduction of the dihedral angle among the C1–N1–N3–C4 atoms (Figure S3). Notably, ZIF-71 exhibited a greater deviation, 37%, in the intramolecular dihedral angle compared to ZIF-71­(ClBr), 20% (Table ). The slight larger interaction observed for ZIF-71 may be attributed to lower steric hindrance of chlorine atoms on the imidazole ligand in comparison to the bromine atom in ZIF-71­(ClBr), as shown by the distances between halogen atoms and MB functional groups.

The orientation with lower interaction energy (M2), which presents the MB interacting near the edge of imidazole ligands, exhibits a smaller reduction in the dihedral angle (Table ). These computational results may also explain the faster removal efficiency of MB by ZIF-71­(ClBr), as it requires less structural deformation to interact with the adsorbate through both interaction patterns. In the case of ZIF-71, the motif M1 entails more stress within the MOF framework; therefore, it is less likely to occur experimentally. Interaction between ZIFs and MB involves polarization and dispersion. The polarization component can be noticed by the change in atomic charges of interacting atomic pairs adsorbate/adsorbent shown in Tables S1 and S2; this effect is reinforced by an amount of charge transferred from the ZIFs toward the adsorbate ∼0.21e (Table ), which redistributes within the MB’s rings and hydrogen atoms on N–H and C–H of methyl groups, increasing their bond polarity. The dispersion component is manifested in specific interacting pairs where both atoms gain electronic charge, as in the case of Cl···S, this nonbonding interaction with an average distance of 3.48 Å has been reported to govern the orientation of encapsulated charged species with neutral complexes. , The formation of these sulfur···halogen interactions is more attainable inside the larger pore cavities of ZIF-71, which explains its 3-fold value for its maximum adsorption capacity.

Conclusions

ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) were synthesized and characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, powder X-ray diffraction, and nitrogen physisorption. These techniques allowed the elucidation of the structure and properties of the ZIFs, such as the BET surface areas of 831.24 m2 g–1 and 815.17 m2 g–1 for ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr), respectively. Optimal conditions of pH 4 and a contact time of 75 min were applied for the adsorption of the MB dye by ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr). Both ZIFs were performed with a pseudo-first-order kinetics model according to our experimental data. Isotherm analysis showed that ZIF-71 followed the Langmuir model, whereas ZIF-71­(ClBr) followed the Freundlich model. ZIF-71 and ZIF-71­(ClBr) exhibited maximum adsorption capacities (qm ) of 597.92 mg g–1 and 191.56 mg g–1, respectively. While ZIF-71­(ClBr) achieved faster adsorption kinetics due to higher hydrophobicity, ZIF-71 had much higher adsorption capacities due to the adsorption of the MB molecules into the pores of the ZIF. Adsorption–desorption experiments showed that ZIF-71­(ClBr) maintains high adsorption performance after 4 cycles, whereas ZIF-71 only showed a low performance after 2 cycles, likely due to the strong interaction between MB molecules and ZIF-71 pores.

Theoretical DFT calculations provide insights into the intermolecular interactions governing the adsorption mechanism, which involve polarization and dispersion forces when hydrogen bonds and sulfur···chlorine pair contacts are established between MB and ZIFs. Calculated interaction energies (−22.5 to −15 kcal/mol) fall within the threshold range for physisorption.

While this study demonstrates the potential of both ZIFs as highly hydrophobic adsorbents for MB, some limitations should be noted. The study primarily focuses on batch adsorption under controlled conditions, which may not fully replicate real cases involving complex dye mixtures or industrial effluents. Future work should explore the performance of dynamic adsorption systems.

Experimental Section

Synthesis of ZIF-71

Two solutions in 50 mL beakers were prepared. In the case of solution 1, 4,5-dichloroimidazole (1 g, 6.4 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (20 mL), whereas for solution 2, zinc acetate dihydrate (0.4 g, 1.8 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (10 mL). Both solutions were sonicated to ensure complete dissolution. Solution 2 was transferred to solution 1 dropwise without stirring. It was left to rest for 24 h. Then, it was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 6 min, the liquids were decanted, and the solids were washed with methanol (3 × 5 mL). Finally, the product was dried under vacuum at 50 °C for 4 h.

Synthesis of ZIF-71­(ClBr)

Two solutions in 50 mL beakers were prepared. In the case of solution 1,4-bromo-5-chloroimidazole (1 g, 5.5 mmol) and sodium formate (0.184 g, 2.7 mmol) were dissolved in methanol (20 mL), whereas for solution 2, zinc acetate (0.252 g, 1.4 mmol) was dissolved in methanol (10 mL). Both solutions were sonicated to ensure complete dissolution. Solution 2 was transferred to solution 1 dropwise without stirring. It was left to rest for 24 h. Then, the mixture was centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 6 min, the liquids were decanted, and the solids were washed with methanol (3 × 5 mL). Finally, the product was dried under vacuum at 50 °C for 4 h.

Stability in Solution Studies

Experiments were conducted using 50 mg of adsorbent, and 10 mL of solvent was added to a 25 mL beaker. The mixture was continuously stirred for the specified time under three different conditions: (1) stirring for 24 h with water as solvent, denoted as “ZIF/H 2 O”; (2) after the adsorption process, denoted as “ZIF/Ads”; and (3) replacing water with methanol as the solvent and stirring for 24 h, denoted as “ZIF/MeOH”. Following the designed periods, the samples were analyzed by using atomic absorption spectroscopy to quantify the concentration of Zn2+ ions.

Adsorption Experiments

The experiments were carried out in triplicate. In a 50 mL beaker, 150 mg of the adsorbent was added to 30 mL of MB solution with an initial concentration of 30 mg L–1. The pH of the solution was varied from 4 to 10 by using 0.1 M NaOH and 0.1 M HCl. The samples were stirred for 120 min. Once the time had elapsed, they were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance obtained was measured at 665 nm. The adsorption capacity (eq ) and the removal (eq ) were calculated with the concentrations obtained before and after removal. Moreover, the effects of pH, initial concentration of the MB, and contact time were evaluated to obtain the best conditions for the adsorption process.

For the kinetic experiments, 400 mL of a 30 mg L–1 solution of MB was prepared, and 2 g of the adsorbent was added. The solution was adjusted to the optimum pH condition and kept under constant stirring for the established time.

Adsorption studies were conducted at different temperatures, specifically at 25, 35, and 45 °C. In a 50 mL beaker, 150 mg of the adsorbent was added to 30 mL of a 100 mg L–1 MB solution. The solution’s pH was adjusted to the optimum condition and kept under constant stirring for the established time. The temperature was monitored during the whole experiment using a thermometer.

Density Functional Theory Calculation and Method

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to investigate the adsorption mechanism in ZIF-71­(ClBr) and ZIF-71 with cationic (1+) MB at the microscopic scale. Each structure was analyzed using plane-wave DFT calculations as implemented in the SIESTA (Spanish Initiative for Electronic Simulations with Thousands of Atoms) package. Exchange and correlation effects are accounted for using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). The Troullier–Martins pseudopotentials were used to describe the core electrons of all species, while valence electrons were represented by using a double-ζ basis set with polarization functions (DZP). Geometry optimizations were performed at the Γ-point using the conjugate gradient algorithm until atomic forces were reduced to less than 0.04 eV/Å. Atomic partial charges were determined using the Hirshfeld method, which calculates charge values through an analysis of the electrostatic potential surface.
