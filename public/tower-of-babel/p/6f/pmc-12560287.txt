Time-series datasets of meteorological variables and influenza incidence in Kawasaki City, Japan
Authors: Keita Wagatsuma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAlthough a wealth of studies has explored the impact of individual meteorological variables on influenza transmission, investigations of these effects within key subgroups—such as viral type, sex, and age—remain scarce. Moreover, long-term daily time-series influenza surveillance data are exceptionally limited. To address these shortcomings, we herein present a meticulously curated daily time-series dataset spanning approximately 11 years from Kawasaki City, Japan, to facilitate comprehensive analyses of the short-term association between meteorological variables and influenza incidence. This resource also aims to aid researchers, educators, and students by fostering the application of time-series regression modelling in both research and educational contexts.Data descriptionDaily total number of influenza cases and ambient meteorological data—mean, minimum, and maximum temperature (°C); relative humidity (%); wind speed (m s⁻¹); rainfall (mm); sunshine duration (hours); and vapour pressure (hPa)—were collated for Kawasaki City, Japan (March 2014–April 2025). Influenza cases were stratified by virus type (A/B), sex (male/female), and age (0–5, 6–11 months; 1–9 years in single-year bands; 10–14, 15–19, 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, ≥ 80 years).Keywords: Environmental health, Climate change, Environmental epidemiology, Time-series regression, Short-term effect, Influenza, Meteorological variables, Distributed lag non-linear model

Objective

Environmental epidemiology examines the relationships between environmental exposures and health outcomes within human populations [1]. For decades, time-series regression analysis has been a cornerstone method, enabling the quantification of short-term relationship between environmental variables (e.g., meteorological variables and air pollution) and health outcomes (e.g., mortality and morbidity) [2]. Such study offers critical epidemiological insights into lag effects and thresholds, which are essential for shaping effective public health policies and interventions. Recent advancements in statistical methods have propelled the widespread adoption of distributed lag non-linear models (DLNMs) in time-series analyses [3]. These models have played a pivotal role in unravelling complex exposure–lag–response relationships, further cementing their significance in environmental epidemiology. Although highly significant, examples highlighting the proper implementation of DLNMs remain relatively scarce. Furthermore, datasets tailored for applying this classical time-series regression framework to infectious diseases are notably limited [4]. To address this gap, this study introduces a meticulously curated time-series dataset designed to assess the short-term association between multiple meteorological variables and influenza incidence in a Japanese city. We previously conducted a preliminary assessment of the association between influenza types and ambient temperature, utilizing a subset of the daily time-series data available for this Japanese city [5]. This dataset aspires to be a vital tool for researchers, educators, and students, fostering the use of time-series regression modeling in both academic research and educational contexts. The dataset al.so acts as a benchmark for comparing similar modeling approaches and allows researchers to extend analyses by integrating additional components, minimizing redundant effort.

Data description

Kawasaki City, located in Northeastern Kanagawa Prefecture adjacent to Tokyo, Japan, is in the western Pacific region at 35°N latitude and 139°E longitude [5, 6]. The area experiences a temperate climate with four distinct seasons: hot and humid summers from June to August and cold, dry winters from December to February. In this study, we collected daily time-series data on influenza incidence and meteorological variables gathered over 11 years from March 2014 to April 2025 in this city. The Kawasaki City infectious disease surveillance system enables real-time monitoring of influenza incidence [5–7]. All medical institutions in the city have been legally required to report daily influenza cases by rapid diagnostic test kits since March 2014. For this study, daily total number of influenza case counts were obtained from March 2014 to April 2025. During the study period, a total of 309,759 influenza cases were recorded in Kawasaki City. Furthermore, to enable detailed stratified analyses, we obtained case counts disaggregated by virus type (A and B), sex (male and female) and the following age groups: 0–5 months, 6–11 months, 1 year, 2 years, 3 years, 4 years, 5 years, 6 years, 7 years, 8 years, 9 years, 10–14 years, 15–19 years, 20–29 years, 30–39 years, 40–49 years, 50–59 years, 60–69 years, 70–79 years and ≥ 80 years. Daily ambient meteorological variables—including mean, minimum, and maximum temperatures (°C), relative humidity (%), wind speed (m s⁻¹), rainfall (mm), sunshine duration (hours), and vapour pressure (hPa)—were acquired from a single monitoring station operated by the Japan Meteorological Agency in Kawasaki City [8]. The dataset additionally comprised temporal identifiers (reporting date, year, month, and day) to facilitate time-series analysis. All data management was undertaken in R version 4.2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) (Table 1).

Overview of data sets

The dataset assembled for this study is available to researchers in diverse fields—public health, epidemiological statistics, medicine, infectious diseases, climate change, and global health (Data set 1). It comprises a comprehensive 11-year daily time-series from a Japanese city, enabling the assessment of short-term associations between multiple meteorological variables and influenza incidence. By characterizing seasonal variation in these exposures (Data file1, 4) and applying time-series analyses in conjunction with DLNMs, investigators can generate detailed outputs—three-dimensional exposure–lag–response surfaces (Data file2, 4), overall lag-cumulative exposure–response relationships and stratified analyses (Data file3, 4)—essential for inferring causal links between meteorological variables and influenza occurrence. The findings will equip public health policymakers with crucial evidence to formulate and implement targeted strategies for reducing community-level influenza risk. Moreover, they could help identify the most vulnerable populations in Japan and guide adaptation policies to lessen the influenza burden attributable to climatic variability.

Limitations

This dataset has several limitations. First, because our dataset was confined to Kawasaki City, Japan, the extent to which our findings are generalizable to other cities or countries remains uncertain. Second, although multiple meteorological variables were included, data on potentially influential air pollutants—such as particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter of ≤ 2.5 μm (PM2.5) and ozone (O₃)—were not collected, which may have affected influenza transmission dynamics [10, 11]. Third, rainfall in particular—exhibited substantial missingness (43.7%; 1785 of 4079 daily observations), which may have introduced bias into our time-series estimates. Forth, owing to data constraints, meteorological exposures were collected at the city rather than the individual level, potentially introducing Berkson-type measurement error and attenuating the statistical power of our time-series analyses [12]. Fifth, in this dataset the date variable denotes the reporting date of influenza cases; therefore, there may be a time lag relative to the actual dates of infection, symptom onset, and diagnosis/testing. Finally, the dataset includes time-series data spanning the COVID-19 pandemic. During this period, non-pharmaceutical interventions (e.g., school closures and mask use), behavioral changes, altered mobility, pressures on healthcare services, expanded testing capacity, and subsequent rebounds in the susceptible pool following periods of suppressed circulation may all have influenced transmission dynamics and reporting intensity. Our models include a year indicator to account for broad annual shifts; however, many of these determinants vary at finer temporal resolutions (e.g., daily or weekly) and are not measured in our data. Residual confounding therefore cannot be excluded, and estimates for the pandemic period should be interpreted with caution.
