Development and Validation of HEPCA-Lat: A Bilingual Hepatitis C Virus Knowledge Scale for Latino Men
Authors: Cho-Hee Shrader, Juan Arroyo-Flores, Edda Rodriguez, Lacey Despres, Daniel Castaneda, Isaac Nkrumah, Mariano Kanamori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatitis C virus (HCV) knowledge is alarmingly low among Latino/Latinx/Hispanic populations (thereby Latino), and commonly used HCV knowledge scales have not been updated with recent innovations in HCV treatment. Latino men who have sex with men (LMSM) and Latino seasonal farmworkers (LSW) are two groups at the highest risk of HCV. In response, we developed and validated “HEPCA-Lat: Prueba de Conocimientos sobre Hepatitis C para Adultos Latinos,” a bilingual English/Spanish HCV knowledge scale. First, we identified 14 unique HCV scales. Then, experts and community health workers identified 43 items to include in HEPCA-Lat. From 2018 to 2020, we recruited participants from community health organizations in South Florida to complete a cross-sectional questionnaire. We conducted Item-Total Correlation, Principal Component, and reliability (random splits, Cronbach’s alpha, mean inter-item correlation) analyses to develop and validate the final HEPCA-Lat. We used regression analyses to examine associations with HCV knowledge. Of 208 participants, 89 were LMSM and 127 were LSW. Of 43 unweighted items identified for initial inclusion, a correlation threshold of >0.5 was used, and 20 items were retained. Items loaded onto 2 components: “HCV prevention” and “Living with HCV” (55.4% of variance). HCV knowledge was low: LMSM correctly answered 44.6% and LSW 14.5% of items. Future research could focus on (a) applying the HEPCA-Lat scale in pre- and post-intervention assessments to evaluate changes in HCV knowledge and (b) refining the HEPCA-Lat scale to a concise version for broader use among the general Latino population.Keywords: Hispanic or Latinos, hepatitis C, reproducibility of results, Florida, farmers, sexual and gender minorities

Introduction

Hepatitis C, caused by the hepatitis C virus (HCV), is the most commonly reported bloodborne infection in the United States (Schillie et al., 2020). Although Hepatitis C is a curable infection, no vaccine, or biomedical pre- or post-exposure prophylaxis is currently available (Office of Infectious Disease and HIV/AIDS Policy [OIDP], 2022). HCV is severely underreported: despite only 4,848 new cases of acute HCV being reported to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in 2022, there were an estimated 67,400 new acute HCV cases and 93,805 newly reported cases of chronic HCV (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024; Klevens et al., 2014). As of 2022, Florida is the U.S. state with the highest number of newly reported acute HCV cases (n = 1,463) and newly reported cases of chronic hepatitis C cases (n = 8,540), and ranks in the top 10 states for the highest rate of acute HCV with 6.6 cases per 100,000 (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). However, due to undertesting, cases in Florida are expected to be significantly higher than reported (US Census Bureau, 2024).

Males and those between the ages of 30 and 39 years are two of the most at-risk groups for acute HCV (3), and the most commonly reported exposure is injection drug use (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2024). A large proportion of people who formerly and currently inject drugs (PWID) test positive for HCV (Martin et al., 2015). Alcohol misuse also places individuals at elevated vulnerability for HCV (Taylor et al., 2016). In addition, men who have sex with men (MSM) living with HIV have been identified as an emerging population at risk of HCV (Bradshaw et al., 2013; Martin et al., 2015). Further, two populations who may be disproportionately impacted by HCV but are unaware of their status are Latino/Latinx/Hispanic populations (thereby Latino) seasonal workers (LSW) and Latino MSM (LMSM). The elevated HCV vulnerability among LSW and LMSM is linked to historically high rates of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and substance use disorders (SUDs) among these subgroups of men (García, 2007; Kim-Godwin & Bechtel, 2006; Sánchez, 2014; Valverde et al., 2015; Winkelman et al., 2013).

LSW report high rates of alcohol misuse and opioid misuse, with up to 50% of some communities reporting substance use and abuse (García, 2007; Kim-Godwin & Bechtel, 2006; Sánchez, 2014; Winkelman et al., 2013). Men of racial/ethnic minoritized backgrounds, such as Latino men, experience economic marginality and gendered coping responses to stress, which can further lead to elevated levels of substance use (Williams, 2008). The disproportionate HCV risk among LSW has been linked to a variety of factors, including (a) self-medicating behaviors to alleviate musculoskeletal injuries caused by the hazardous nature of their work (Brock et al., 2012; Bureau of Labor Statistics & US Department of Labor, 2019; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention & The National Center for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), 2019), (b) inadequate access to health care (Hansen & Donohoe, 2003; Holmes, 2013), and (c) depression and psychological stressors related to extreme living conditions, immigration and acculturative stress, and discrimination (Hansen & Donohoe, 2003; Holmes, 2013; Kanamori et al., 2020; Pulgar et al., 2016; Winkelman et al., 2013). LMSM report high rates of polydrug and alcohol misuse, placing this subgroup at elevated HCV risk (Kanamori et al., 2021; Zaller et al., 2017).

Exacerbating this syndemic among Latino men are societal gender norms, which can influence men’s perceptions and beliefs, such as self-reliance and emotional restraint, about health (Oliffe, 2023). These norms have been described to make men less likely to seek medical care or publicly discuss their health concerns (Palmer et al., 2024), and this could influence their knowledge of health care and adversity to health-protective behaviors (Williams, 2008). More specific to HCV, a qualitative study reported that men, relative to women, did not acknowledge the impact of HCV and relevant needs for HCV-related education and social support (Temple-Smith et al., 2004). Instead, men downplayed their HCV infection and avoided seeking out health care. Considering these gendered factors and the high HCV prevalence in Florida, LSW and LMSM living in Florida may be disproportionately affected by HCV and the negative outcomes of HCV.

HCV knowledge among LSW and LMSM is understudied. Accordingly, it is vital to understand the correlates of HCV knowledge to identify opportunities to engage LSW and LMSM in HCV screening and education. We developed and validated “HEPCA-Lat: Prueba de Conocimientos sobre Hepatitis C para Adultos Latinos,” a bilingual English and Spanish HCV knowledge scale for Latino men in Florida. We also identified associations of HIV knowledge, hazardous alcohol use, insurance status, and health care access with HCV knowledge among LSW and LMSM.

Method

Participants and Recruitment

HEPCA-Lat was developed using cross-sectional data from two Latino populations living in South Florida: LSW and LMSM. Participants were recruited and data were collected between July 2019 and March 2020. All participants completed an assessment on HCV knowledge and HCV risk behaviors. Assessments were provided in English and Spanish, with both languages available for each question. Community health workers (CHW) administered assessments verbally in the participant’s preferred language.

LSW Sample

LSWs were recruited using convenient sampling by CHWs of a large Latino-serving community-based health organization (CBO) in South Florida. Eligibility criteria included: (a) male sex assigned at birth, (b) Latino, Latinx, or Hispanic ethnic identity, (c) being a seasonal worker or a part of the seasonal worker community, and (d) using drugs and/or alcohol in the past 12 months. Additional participants were recruited using peer referral sampling.

LMSM Sample

LMSM were recruited by an HIV-service CBO in South Florida using respondent-driven sampling. Eligibility criteria included: (a) male sex assigned at birth; (b) HIV-negative status; (c) sex with a man in the past 6 months; (d) Latino, Latinx, or Hispanic ethnic identity; and (e) indication for PrEP prescription based on PrEP Clinical Practice Guidelines (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2018). Additional information about study procedures can be found elsewhere (Kanamori et al., 2022).

HCV Knowledge Scale Instrument Update

To develop HEPCA-Lat, we first conducted a literature review from 2000 to 2018 using the search string “HCV Knowledge Scale” on PubMed and identified 14 unique HCV knowledge scales (Allison et al., 2016; Aluzaite et al., 2018; Balfour et al., 2009; Buffington et al., 2000; Chen et al., 2013; Denniston et al., 2012; Du et al., 2012; Heimer et al., 2002; Krans et al., 2018; Lowe, 2008; Pundhir et al., 2016; Soto-Salgado et al., 2011; Sultan et al., 2018). Most studies used a modified version of either the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES; Denniston et al., 2012) or the Balfour et al. Brief HCV Knowledge Scale (Balfour et al., 2009). We consulted an HCV subject matter expert (SME) and LSW and LMSM CHWs to review the 14 unique HCV knowledge scales and select items to include for our initial scale. The scale was translated to Spanish using a back-translation method standard in cross-cultural research (Brislin, 1970).

The initial version of the HEPCA-Lat scale was reviewed by an HCV SME, three CHW working to address LSW’s health disparities, and seven CHWs in LMSM’s health disparity prevention to establish content validity. We pilot tested the HEPCA-Lat scale with CHWs serving each population. Based on their feedback, a total of 43 items were retained. Of 43 items, 20 items were “True/False” items, 10 items were multiple-choice items, and 1 item was a “select all that applies” item with 13 response options (coded as binary). The scale’s total score was calculated by awarding one point for each correct response and no points for incorrect and “Do not know” responses. The initial scale potential score ranged from 0 to 43, with higher scores indicating greater HCV knowledge.

HEPCA-Lat Scale Validity and Reliability

Item analysis – Individual items were assessed for construct validity using the Item-Total Correlation. Items with a correlation lower than 0.5 were removed from the scale. A total of 20 items were retained for the final scale.

Construct validity – A principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess construct validity, data reduction, and the identification of underlying factors of our scale (Abdi & Williams, 2010). To conduct the PCA, we used the FactoMineR package (Lê et al., 2008) on the R environment (R Core Team, 2023).

In addition, we collected the following information to understand associations of HIV knowledge, hazardous alcohol use, insurance status, and health care access, with HCV knowledge among LSW and LMSM.

Sociodemographic Measures

We collected participants’ age (years), race (referent: White, Black, another race [i.e., Native American, multiracial, and other]), region of birth [referent: North America (United States), Central America (Colombia, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Mexico, Nicaragua, Peru), South America (Brazil and Venezuela), and the Caribbean (Jamaica, Dominican Republic, and Puerto Rico)], education (referent: some high school, high school degree, some college, college graduate), marital status (referent: single, married or in a stable relationship), employment (referent: employed full-time, not employed full-time), income (measured as continuous), county of residence (Miami-Dade County or Palm Beach county), years residing in the United States (measured as continuous in years), and health insurance status (referent: uninsured, any health insurance).

Acculturation (Immigration and Discrimination) Stress

We measured acculturation stress using the Immigration Stress (9-item; α = .88) and Discrimination Stress (10-item; α = .88) subscales of the Hispanic Stress Inventory Version 2 (Cervantes et al., 2016). We calculated subscale scores using the mean value for all items.

HCV Risk Measures

Participants indicated if they had tested positive for a sexually transmitted infection (STI), such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, genital herpes, genital warts, or syphilis, in the past year. Participants indicated if a health professional or a doctor had ever spoken to them about HIV prevention or how to have safe sex. We assessed participants’ ability to afford health care in the past year by asking, “In the past year, were you ever unable to see a doctor because you could not pay for it?” (referent: cannot afford health care, can afford health care). We used the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) to assess participants’ alcohol consumption (a score of 8 or higher indicated hazardous alcohol use; α = .85; Bush et al., 1998).

In addition, participants reported their HIV knowledge based on a truncated Brief HIV Knowledge Questionnaire (Carey et al., 1997; Carey & Schroder, 2002). We excluded the following items: “A woman can get HIV if she has anal sex with a man,” “A woman cannot get HIV if she has sex during her period,” and “There is a female condom that can help decrease a woman’s chance of getting HIV.” The study team (MK, CS) deemed these items inappropriate for our samples, as the LSW may disproportionately have more knowledge about these female-centric items than the LMSM group.

Statistical Analysis

We compared the LMSM and LSW samples using bivariate analysis to identify significant differences. We included only participants’ information if they provided complete data. We then used regression modeling to identify associations between explanatory variables and the outcome variable of the raw score of correct answers to our HCV knowledge scale after item analysis reduction. We used the same explanatory variables to identify associations with HCV knowledge, stratified by sample. Thus, we fitted three models using the same explanatory variables. We examined associations of the outcome of HCV knowledge score for (a) the entire sample of Latino men using multilevel modeling nested by sample, (b) the LSW subset using linear regression, and (c) the LMSM subset using linear regression.

We used the STROBE cross-sectional checklist when writing our report (Von Elm et al., 2008).

Ethical Approval

A member of the study team obtained written consent from participants in English or Spanish, depending on the participant’s language of preference. Participants were provided with a $40 gift card or cash for their participation. The parent studies were approved by the institutional review board of the University of Miami.

Results

Sociodemographic Characteristics of Samples

The 127 participants from the LSW sample (M = 34.8 years, SD = 11.4) were significantly older than the 89 participants from the LMSM sample (28.6 years, SD = 4.1) (t = −5.64, p < .001). Relative to LSW, a significantly higher percentage of LMSM participants identified as White Latino (LSW = 62.2%; LMSM = 82.0%; χ2 = 10.16; p = .006). Approximately half of the participants in the LMSM sample were born in the United States (56.2%) and the Caribbean (24.7%), which differed from the LSW sample, among whom most were born in Central America (65.4%) and the United States (16.5%), differing significantly (χ2 = 72.31; p < .001). LSW reported living in the United States for an average of 9.4 years (SD = 9.9) and significantly differed from LMSM, who reported living in the United States for an average of 13.9 years (8.5; t = 2.74; p = .007). Participants also differed based on their county of residence: most LMSM lived in Miami-Dade County (93.3%), and most LSW lived in Palm Beach County (78.7%), differing significantly (χ2 = 127.82; p < .001). Most LMSM were employed full-time (92.1%) relative to over a third (35.4%) of LSW who were employed full-time, differing significantly (χ2 = 67.1; p < .001). Income also varied by group, with 90% of LMSM reporting that they earned $25,000 or more annually, and 93% of LSW reported earning less than $25,000 (χ2 = 156.2; p < .001). Most participants reported a single relationship status (75%) with no significant differences among groups (χ2 = 2.68; p = .101). Evaluation of Psychometric Properties of the HEPCA-Lat Scale

Item analysis – Of the 20 total items, 6 items were answered incorrectly by 90% or more of the sample. This presents a floor effect; however, our validity and reliability scores were satisfactory, as detailed below. The floor effect may not be attributed to how items were phrased and/or asked but rather due to the lack of HCV knowledge in our sample and target population.

Validity – The results of the PCA indicated that all items loaded onto two components (accounting for 55.4% of the variance). The larger component explaining 46.9% of the variance mainly focused on items regarding the symptoms and potential HCV complications. In comparison, the smaller component (8.5%) focused on transmission, diagnosis, and treatment.

HCV Knowledge Correlates

In the full sample, nested by group, there was a significant and positive relationship between reporting being a college graduate relative to having no high school degree (B = 3.05 p = .03), having health insurance (B = 2.92, p = .001), not being able to afford health care (B = 1.97, p = .008), HIV knowledge score (B = .19, p = .019), and discrimination stress score (B = .95, p = .019). There was a significant and negative relationship between immigration stress score (B = −.71, p = .047) and HCV knowledge score. The variance of 0.44 indicates that 44% of the difference in HCV knowledge score may be attributable to differences between the samples (i.e., LSW or LMSM).

Within the sample of LSW, there was a significant and positive relationship between having health insurance (B = 4.51, p = .010), not being able to afford health care (B = 5.68, p = .007), and discrimination stress score (B = 3.57, p = .009). There was a significant and negative relationship between immigration stress score (B = −2.86, p = .022) and HCV knowledge score.

Within the sample of LMSM, there was a significant and positive relationship between HCV knowledge score, age (B = .08, p = .001), and HIV knowledge score (B = .26, p < .001). There was a significant and negative relationship between region of birth and HCV knowledge score, with LMSM born in Central America (B = −2.91, p = .001) and South America (B = −4.30, p = .019) having lower HCV knowledge scores relative to those LMSM born in the United States. Discussion

This study sought to develop and validate HEPCA-Lat: a bilingual English and Spanish HCV knowledge scale then identify sociodemographic and contextual associations with HCV knowledge. Although previous HCV knowledge scales has been psychometrically validated to assess HCV knowledge, it has been suggested that these scales should be regularly updated with the most current research on HCV risk factors and medical treatments (Balfour et al., 2009). In this context, we are presenting HEPCA-Lat, a psychometrically sound bilingual English/Spanish HCV knowledge scale that can be used for pre- and post-test assessments for HCV knowledge interventions. Notably, we provide an updated 20-item psychometrically valid HCV scale in English and Spanish based on current research on HCV risk factors and treatment advances. In addition, we identified differences in HCV knowledge scores between LSW and LMSM. Finally, we found significant differences between LMSM and LSW on associations with HCV knowledge.

HCV knowledge is associated with HCV screening (Samuel et al., 2018) and HCV treatment (Younossi et al., 2013). Using the HEPCA-Lat scale, we found that both LSW and LMSM subgroups had low HCV knowledge indicating a need for HCV prevention education. Our tests for validation and reliability regarding our expert-reviewed knowledge scale were satisfactory; however, our study revealed that the respondents in our samples lacked knowledge about HCV. Given our analysis of the scale, we propose that the floor effect may be due to the lack of HCV knowledge in our sample and target population or perhaps because some items may be too clinically technical for community use. Items such as “Your screening for hepatitis C can be positive even if you don’t have it,” (4.2% of participants answered correctly), “If you had hepatitis C once, you can’t get it again,” (9.3% of participants answered correctly), “There is a vaccine to prevent hepatitis C,” (8.8% of participants answered correctly), and “The treatment of hepatitis C means that you have to take medication for hepatitis C the rest of your life” (8.2% of participants answered correctly) were not overly technical and we believe may be due to lack of knowledge. Items that may be too technical could include “What does knowing the type of hepatitis C genotype tell you?” (6.4% of participants answered correctly) and “Which of these diagnose the stage of liver problems?” (9.7% of participants answered correctly). Because over 90% of participants incorrectly answered these 6 items, some of these items may also be considered for exclusion in future studies, which could parse down the 20-item scale to a 14-item scale. However, items addressing treatment, reinfection, and vaccination may require familiarity with medical terminology, which men may be less likely to access as indicated in previous research (Palmer et al., 2024). Retained items may function better as diagnostic indicators of fundamental knowledge gaps and identify populations with limited HCV information access where foundational education is needed. Still, further research is needed to confirm and better expand upon how future users could interpret very low scores in intervention or surveillance contexts. In addition, an expansion of this study could inform the implementation of prevention programs that would address this gap in HCV knowledge among Latino men.

Although both samples possessed low HCV knowledge, LMSM were generally better informed about HCV relative to LSW. We found that LMSM have higher income and educational attainment relative to LSW, which could increase access to information in general and perhaps health-related information such as HCV information. These indicators suggest higher privileges in society; thus, individuals may have more capacity to obtain health-related knowledge. In addition, LMSM with higher HIV knowledge were found to also have higher HCV scores: this could be because these individuals could acquire HCV knowledge when they received HIV prevention information. Future interventions can identify whether it is meritorious to integrate HCV knowledge interventions with existing HIV knowledge interventions, as Ibañez et al. (2016) have for Latino criminal justice clients. Although HIV and HCV have differing etiologies, there are overlapping risk factors that result in acquiring both. If individuals are already receiving HIV information, it may be low burden to also include an HCV component. Alternatively or concurrently, LMSM may have possessed higher HCV knowledge than LSW and experienced associations between HIV and HCV knowledge because LMSM have more exposure to sexual health information through access to a CBO that, among their services, provides HIV and HCV prevention and treatment programs. Notably, among LMSM, those born in Central or South America also had significantly lower HCV knowledge relative to those LMSM born in the United States. This could be due to a lack of HCV-related information being available in these countries, or perhaps a lack of interest as these participants may experience more prevalent needs as they migrated to the United States.

Among LSW, we found that having health insurance, not being able to afford health care, and discrimination stress were positively associated with HCV knowledge. Having health insurance could increase access to HCV knowledge, and if LSW cannot afford health care, they may be proactive in gathering health care information. Interestingly, discrimination stress was positively associated with HCV knowledge – this could be due to the items that could have assessed discrimination experienced at health care agencies, as has been reported by previous studies (Arreola et al., 2022; Williams et al., 2003). Our finding of immigration stress being negatively associated with HCV knowledge suggests that those individuals who experience higher immigration stress may cause LSW to be afraid to seek out medical information or interact with medical providers, especially those providers who are not Latino (Philbin et al., 2018); thereby, reducing access to HCV knowledge.

There were several limitations to this study. First, we prioritized two Latino communities at the highest risk of HCV who were men, and our findings may not be generalizable to the larger Latino community. As Latinos in the United States are a heterogeneous group, the concept of generalizability may be impaired for this diverse group. Because our participants were limited to men, we do not know how well our scale would measure HCV knowledge among other genders, such as women. Second, we found a floor effect; however, this may be due to a lack of knowledge of HCV instead of an actual floor effect. Thus, some participants may experience recall or social desirability bias, which may influence our results toward the null. Fourth, we recruited participants in the LMSM sample using respondent-driven sampling while using peer referral to recruit LSW, but we did not use an estimator to account for the potential bias in each of these groups. We recognize sampling bias as our sample is not random, and there will be dependencies within the data due to the closeness of both communities – for example, there may be clusters of LMSM who share similar HCV knowledge scores, with this knowledge being due to group differences, relative to LSW. A future study could examine measurement invariance across Latino subgroups to ensure that our scale functions similarly across populations regardless of recruitment strategy. In addition, because participants were recruited at CBOs serving Latino communities, our findings may differ from those seeking care at other clinics not tailored to the Latino community.

Public Health Implications

Currently, there are few HCV prevention programs offered for LSW and LMSM populations. Once diagnosed with HCV, the morbidity and mortality of Latinos are seven times higher relative to HCV-negative Latinos, when compared to other races/ethnicities (Emmanuel et al., 2017). As such, it is important to prevent HCV among Latinos- and especially those experiencing multiple marginalization such as LSW and LMSM. Previously shown effective HCV prevention programs should not only include formal patient education programs (Surjadi et al., 2011) but also be appropriate for Latino subgroups such as the LSW population and LMSM (Ibañez et al., 2016). HCV prevention programs can consider using HEPCA-Lat to measure and provide HCV-related knowledge through a culturally competent, low-barrier care setting. The HEPCA-Lat scale was developed and validated among Latino men assigned male at birth. Although the present study focused on LSW and LMSM, given the similar structural barriers to health care access among transgender or gender-expansive individuals (Isano et al., 2023), this scale could also be used to assess HCV-related knowledge among other gender minoritized groups who identify as men or were assigned male at birth. However, the extent to which HEPCA-Lat functions similarly among transgender or gender-diverse Latino individuals has not yet been evaluated. Future validation work could assess whether the scale demonstrates appropriate reliability, validity, and measurement equivalence in these populations or whether adaptations are needed to ensure cultural and gender inclusivity.
