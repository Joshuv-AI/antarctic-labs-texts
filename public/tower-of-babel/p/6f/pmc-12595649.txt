Identification and validation of key genes related to apoptosis in multiple organ dysfunction syndrome
Authors: Jian Zhang, Zhi-ying Wen, Yan-xiao Li, Hui-ping Sun, ying-ying Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundApoptosis occupies a core position in the pathogenesis of multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS). Therefore, exploring key mechanisms of action of apoptosis-related genes (ARGs) in MODS will play a positive and promoting role in the diagnosis and treatment of MODS.MethodsWe obtained MODS-related data from public databases, and analyzed the disparately expressed genes between MODS and controls, as well as the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) genes most related to MODS. The intersection with ARGs was then used to obtain candidate genes. After that, by combining Cytoscape software, machine learning algorithms with expression verification, key genes were obtained, and a nomogram model was constructed and evaluated. Next, centering on key genes, gene set enrichment analysis, immune infiltration analysis, small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) analysis, regulatory network construction, and drug prediction were carried out. At last, the expression of key genes in clinical samples was validated.ResultsAfter screening, S100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 were identified as the key genes of MODS. They were all significantly highly expressed in MODS and jointly participated in "oxidative phosphorylation" signaling pathway. The nomogram constructed based on the key genes had excellent predictive ability. There were 15 types of differentially infiltrated immune cells between MODS and controls, and they were correlated with the key genes. In addition, each of the key genes had two or more SUMOylation sites, and multiple miRNAs (hsa-let-7d-5p) and lncRNAs (XIST) were predicted. Subsequently, the key genes also jointly predicted potential drugs (curcumin). Finally, in clinical samples of MODS, the key genes also showed high expression.ConclusionS100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 were the key genes of MODS in terms of apoptosis. The constructed nomogram had an excellent predictive value. It offers a novel approach and potential targeted therapy for the clinical diagnosis and treatment of MODS.

Introduction

Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) is a clinical syndrome triggered by severe infections, trauma, burns, or other acute illnesses, manifesting as dysfunction or failure in two or more organs or systems0. The pathogenesis of MODS is intricate, featuring pathological damage that affects multiple organs, systems, levels, and targets[2, 3]. Even with the advancement of life-support technologies today, MODS still features high incidence rates, high mortality rates, and significant social and economic pressures. When only two organs fail, the mortality rate hovers around approximately 30%. In contrast, when three to four organs are impaired, the mortality rate surges to a range of 50% to 70%[4]. However, due to the complex and multifactorial nature of MODS, modern medicine has yet to discover effective prevention and treatment methods[5, 6]. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of MODS can help us deepen our understanding of MODS and provide a theoretical basis for the prevention and treatment of MODS.

Apoptosis, which is also referred to as programmed cell death or cellular suicide, represents a process during which cells undergo active death under the control of genes[7]. Apoptosis is a pervasive phenomenon in biology and usually occurs in normal physiological and pathological states. It has a major impact on embryonic advancement, morphogenesis, tissue stability, and immune responses[8]. Apoptosis is responsible for the removal of damaged or functionally obsolete cells, which contributes to the maintenance of normal tissue and organ development as well as the stability of the internal environment[8, 9]. Research indicates that the pathophysiology of MODS is associated with stress-induced apoptosis, and inhibiting this stress-induced cell death may improve outcomes[10].

The current research shows that apoptosis acts as a double-edged sword in the development of MODS. In the early stages of the disease, apoptosis serves as a critical mechanism that modulates the immune response and promotes inflammation. This process helps activate the immune system, clearing out damaged cells and pathogens, and can be beneficial in the initial phase of the disease, as it aids in tissue repair and sets the stage for recovery[11]. However, when apoptosis genes are overexpressed, particularly under sustained stress conditions, this process can become maladaptive. Excessive apoptosis leads to an overproduction of inflammatory mediators, further exacerbating the inflammatory response. This runaway inflammation can significantly damage tissues and organs, worsening the overall condition and contributing to the progression of organ failure [12]. The dysregulation of apoptosis shifts from a protective mechanism to a pathological contributor, emphasizing the importance of balancing cell death and survival signals in preventing or managing MODS.

Despite recognition of apoptosis as a central mechanism in MODS, the specific expression patterns and regulatory roles of apoptosis-related genes remain incompletely understood. Therefore, further clarifying the expression and functional impact of apoptosis genes in MODS could provide insights into disease progression and identify potential targets for intervention. Key apoptosis-related genes in MODS were screened and validated in this study by integrating data from public databases and applying multiple bioinformatics approaches, including differential expression analysis, WGCNA, and machine learning. Their functions, regulatory mechanisms, and potential clinical value were further analyzed, providing novel insights and candidate targets for the diagnosis and treatment of MODS.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

GSE66099 (GPL570), GSE26440 (GPL570), and GSE144406 (GPL18573) were datasets related to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and all of them were collected from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Respectively, GSE66099 served as training set, and the samples of septic shock and sepsis in it were combined and defined as MODS, with a total of 199 MODS samples (MODS) and 47 control samples (controls). GSE26440 served as validation set 1, which included 98 MODS samples of septic shock and 32 control samples. GSE144406 served as validation set 2, and the extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO)−0h and MODS-0h samples in it were combined and defined as MODS, with a total of 23 MODS samples and 4 control samples. The sample types in all of the above three datasets were whole blood.

The apoptosis-related genes (ARGs) were collected from the literature. The ARGs in different literatures were combined, and then the duplicate genes were deleted. Retained genes

Screening of candidate genes

In training set, the "limma" package (v 3.54.0)[13]was employed to analyze the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between MODS and controls (|log2fold change (FC)|> 1, adj.p < 0.05). Generally, it was believed that when the log2FC value of DEGs was greater than 1, its expression in MODS was upregulated. When the log2FC value of DEGs was less than 1, its expression in MODS was downregulated. Subsequently, the volcano plot of DEGs was drawn in the "ggplot2" package (v 3.4.3)[14], and the names of the top 10 upregulated and downregulated DEGs with respect to |log2FC| value were labeled. The heatmap of the expression levels of DEGs labeled in volcano plot was drawn in "ComplexHeatmap" package (v 2.14.0)[15].

Then, taking MODS and controls as the traits, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed by "WGCNA" package (v 1.70.3) [16]in training set. Firstly, the genes with the median absolute deviation (MAD) values in the bottom 50% in training set were removed. And the unqualified genes and samples were removed by utilizing the goodSamplesGenes function. The hclust function was employed to cluster the samples so as to remove the outlier samples. Subsequently, to make the constructed network as close to a scale-free network as possible, the network connectivity R2 was set to 0.85, and the soft threshold that first exceeded 0.85 was set as the optimal soft threshold. The co-expression matrix was constructed through optimal soft threshold, with minModuleSize set to 50 and mergeCutHeight set to 0.25 to obtain gene modules. Finally, MODS and controls were introduced as traits, and Pearson correlation between the traits and the modules was calculated (|correlation (cor)|> 0.3, p < 0.05). Then, the module that had the strongest correlation with the traits was selected as WGCNA genes.

The common genes among the DEGs, WGCNA genes, and ARGs were taken as candidate genes, and an intersection diagram was drawn in "VennDiagram" package (v 1.7.3) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=VennDiagram) to display them.

Functional exploration of candidate genes

In the "clusterProfiler" package(v 4.7.1.003)[17], Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were carried out on the candidate genes to preliminarily explore the functions and signaling pathways involved in candidate genes (p < 0.05). Among them, GO includes molecular functions (MFs), cellular components (CCs), and biological processes (BPs). The top 5 entries with the most enriched genes in each part of GO and the top 10 functions with the most significant p-values in KEGG were presented.

Acquisition of hub genes

To explore whether the candidate genes have interactions at the protein level, these candidate genes were admitted into Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins(STRING) database (https://string-db.org). After removing the discrete proteins, a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed (confidence score = 0.15). Subsequently, the importance of the candidate genes was calculated with the help of the maximal clique centrality (MCC), density of maximum neighborhood component (dNNC), and degree algorithms in the cytoHubba plugin of Cytoscape software (v 3.7.1)[18]. The hub genes were obtained by taking the intersection of the top 10 candidate genes from three algorithms by "VennDiagram" package (v 1.7.3).

Screen key genes by combining machine learning algorithms with expression level verification

In the training set, characteristic genes strongly associated with MODS were identified using multiple machine learning algorithms. The input for these algorithms was the original expression matrix from GSE66099. After probe-to-gene mapping and filtering of missing values, the hub genes were used directly as input without log2 transformation or Z-score normalization. Three algorithms––least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), support vector machine recursive feature elimination (SVM-RFE), and Boruta––were subsequently applied to screen for key genes associated with MODS.

Specifically, LASSO is a regression-based method that performs variable selection and regularization simultaneously to enhance prediction accuracy. It was implemented by "glmnet" package (v 4.1–4)[19]. It limited the complexity of the model by introducing a regularization term. When the value of the regularization coefficient (lambda) was at its minimum, the constructed model was considered to have the smallest sum of squared residuals. At this time, the hub genes included in the model were registered as LASSO genes. SVM-RFE is a machine learning algorithm that iteratively removes the least important features to identify the most relevant predictors. It was implemented by "caret" package (v6.0–93)[20]. A fivefold cross-validation was elected to calculate the accuracy under various features. When the accuracy rate reached its maximum, the genes included in the model were regarded as SVM-RFE genes. Boruta is a feature selection method based on random forests that evaluates the importance of each variable by comparing it with randomized shadow features. It was run in "Boruta" package (v 8.8.0)[21]. After multiple iterations, the hub genes that were still marked as "confirmed" were recorded as Boruta genes. Then, the intersection of the genes included in three algorithms was taken in "VennDiagram" package (v 1.7.3), and the common genes obtained were the characteristic genes.

Lastly, the Wilcoxon test was applied, respectively, in training set, validation set 1, and validation set 2 to analyze the variations in expression levels of characteristic genes between MODS and controls (p < 0.05). The characteristic genes that had expression variations and showed coherent trends across the three datasets were designated as key genes.

Construction of a nomogram

To intuitively demonstrate the relationship between key genes and MODS, a nomogram was constructed by "rms" package(v 6.7–1) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=rms) in the training set with the key genes as variables. Each key gene corresponded to different range values, and the total score corresponded to a greater probability of suffering from MODS.

Subsequently, decision curve analysis (DCA) was carried out in "ggDCA" package (v 1.2) (https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/ggDCA/versions/1.1), and the DCA curve was plotted to analyze the clinical application value of nomogram. Similarly, with the help of "rms" package (v 6.7–1), the calibration curve of the nomogram was drawn, and degree of coincidence between the predicted effect of the model and the actual effect was judged by the p-value and the slope of the curve (p > 0.05). In "pROC" package (v 1.18.0)[22], the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of nomogram was drawn, and the accuracy of predicted results of nomogram was judged by the area under the curve (AUC) value (AUC > 0.7).

Similarity analysis and localization analysis

To explore whether there is similarity among the key genes, we conducted Spearman correlation analysis (|cor|> 0.3, p < 0.05) and functional similarity analysis (score > 0.5) on the key genes in "psych" package (v 2.2.9) (https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=psych) and "GOSemSim" (v 2.24.0) [23]package, respectively. And the results were displayed through "Pheatmap" package (v1.0.12) [24]and "ggplot2" package (v 3.4.3), respectively. Meanwhile, the key genes were admitted into the GeneMANIA database (http://www.genemania.org/) to search for genes with similar functions to the key genes as well as the functions they participated in jointly. And a gene–gene interaction (GGI) network diagram was drawn to display them.

The positions of key genes on human chromosomes were localized using the "RCircos" package (v 1.2.2)[25]. Meanwhile, subcellular localization-related information was downloaded from the Genecards database (https://www.genecards.org/), and visualized by plotting with the "ggplot2" package (v 3.4.3). Finally, the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) was explored to investigate the tissue-specific expression of key genes (species: human).

Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

In training set, the background gene set "c2.all.v7.5.1.symbols.gmt" (MSigDB database) was preset. GSVA analysis was performed on all samples using the "GSVA" package (v 1.46.0), and the differences in gene set scores between MODS and controls were compared using the "Limma" package (v 3.54.0) (|t|> 2, p < 0.05). The top 5 upregulated and downregulated pathways were depicted in a bar chart, sorted by their |t| values in descending order.

In addition, GSEA was employed to explore the signal pathways in which each key gene was involved in training set. Firstly, the Spearman correlation coefficients between key genes and all the genes in training set were calculated by "psych" package (v 2.2.9) and then sorted in descending order. Then, "c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt" was downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/) as the reference gene set. Finally, GSEA was executed in "clusterProfiler" package (v 4.7.1.003) (p < 0.05). The top 5 pathways with the most significant p-values for each key gene were displayed by "enrichplot" package (v 1.18.3)[26].

Immune features and immune pathway analysis

In training set, CIBERSORT algorithm was adopted to evaluate the immune infiltration levels between MODS and controls. And the Wilcoxon test was employed to analyze the differences in immune cell infiltration between MODS and controls (p < 0.05). The immune cells with differential infiltration were recorded as differentially infiltrated immune cells (DICs). Next, the Spearman correlation coefficients between key genes and DICs were calculated in "psych" package (v 2.2.9) (|cor|> 0.3, p < 0.05). Subsequently, the association between key genes and immune-related pathways[27] was further explored. In the MODS samples of the training set, "GSVA" package (v 1.46.0)[28]) was employed to conduct single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) on MODS samples to evaluate the scores of immune-related pathways for all MODS samples. After that, the Spearman analysis was performed through the "cor" function to assess correlation between key genes and scores of immune response-related pathways (p < 0.05).

Subsequently, 67 immune checkpoints were obtained from the literature[29]. The differential expression of all immune checkpoints between MODS and controls was analyzed by Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05), and the top 20 results with the greatest significance were displayed in the "ggplot2" package (v 3.4.3).

Small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) modification analysis

SUMOylation modification plays roles in regulating inflammation, apoptosis, immune cell function, and so on in MODS [30, 31]. Therefore, this study analyzed the SUMOylation modification sites of the key genes. The FASTA files of the proteins corresponding to the genes were downloaded from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). By inputting the protein FASTA into GPS-SUMO 2.0 database (https://sumo.biocuckoo.cn/), the SUMOylation and SUMO interaction sites of the key genes at the protein level could be obtained.

Regulatory relationships of key genes

For the transcription factors (TFs) that regulate key genes, predictions were carried out by position analysis software tool for alignment of annotations (PASTAA) database (https://trap.molgen.mpg.de/PASTAA.htm). The p-value was calculated by the hypergeometric distribution to assess the correlation between key genes and the TFs (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, the TFs that had the most significant correlation with the key genes were input into JASPAR database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) to predict the DNA-binding sites of the TFs.

Meanwhile, TFs targeting key genes were predicted using the JASPAR plugin in the NetworkAnalyst database (https://www.networkanalyst.ca/), and a TF-key gene network was constructed in Cytoscape software (v 3.7.1).

Next, the microRNAs (miRNAs) of the key genes as well as the long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) upstream of the miRNAs were predicted in order to construct an lncRNAs–miRNAs–mRNAs network. Specifically, MicroCosm database (https://mycocosm.jgi.doe.gov/mycocosm/home) and TargetScan database (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) were employed to predict the miRNAs targeted by key genes. And the miRNAs in the union of these two databases were regarded as the key miRNAs. In starBase database (https://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/), the lncRNAs that regulate the key miRNAs were screened according to the criterion of clipExpNum > 4, and the network diagram was drawn in Cytoscape software (v 3.7.1).

Besides the regulatory relationships among genes, we also explored the interactions between key genes and compounds as well as proteins in the STITCH database (http://stitch.embl.de/) (confidence = 0.4) and constructed a key gene–compound–protein network.

Disease and drug prediction of key genes

The relationship between key genes and nervous system-related diseases was analyzed in Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD, http://ctdbase.org/), and the top five diseases associated with each key gene were displayed [32]. Similarly, the potential drugs 1 targeted by key genes were predicted in CTD database and a key gene–potential drug network was drawn in Cytoscape software (v 3.7.1). Given that the CTD database primarily focuses on predicting toxicants, potential drugs (denoted as "potential drugs 2") targeting the key genes were retrieved using the DSigDB plugin in the Enrichr database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/). Concurrently, a key gene-potential drug network was constructed. Subsequently, based on the interaction scores between drugs and key genes, the drug with the highest score for each key gene was selected for molecular docking.

The 3D structures of the key genes were obtained from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/entry/) and used as receptors. The 3D structures of the drugs used for molecular docking were retrieved from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) as SDF format and used as ligands. Both receptors and ligands were uploaded to the CB-Dock online server (https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock/php/blinddock.php) for molecular docking. The number of cavities for docking was set to 5. CB-Dock performs semi-flexible docking using the built-in AutoDock Vina engine, where the protein is treated as rigid, and all rotatable bonds of the ligand are freely sampled within the search space.

AutoDock Vina calculates the initial binding conformation based on the input structures and estimates the corresponding initial energy as a scoring basis. The conformations are then optimized using the BFGS algorithm to minimize energy, and the resulting lowest energy is reported as the Vina score. The Vina score reflects the binding affinity between the receptor and ligand, with lower scores indicating higher binding affinity. For each cavity, the docking results are ranked by Vina scores, and the top-ranked conformation is considered the best binding mode. The corresponding Vina score is used to represent the binding energy of the ligand–ligand at the predicted binding site.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

In this study, whole blood samples from 5 patients with MODS and whole blood samples from 5 normal controls were collected at First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University. This study had already been approved by the Ethics Committee of The ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University approved this research, Approval No.:240723–66.

To achieve the relative quantification of key genes, firstly, the total RNA of the samples was extracted by TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, the total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with the help of the SweScript First Strand cDNA synthesis kit (Servicebio, China). The reverse transcription was carried out by the S1000™ Thermal Cycler general PCR instrument (BIO-RAD, USA). Finally, PCR reaction system was configured according to the instructions of Hifair® III 1 st Strand cDNA synthesis supermix for qpcr (gdna digester plus) (Yeasen, China). The results of PCR were analyzed by the t-test in GraphPad Prism 5 to calculate the p-values (p < 0.05) of the key genes between MODS and controls, and then graphs were drawn. Each reaction was performed in triplicate, and melt curve analysis was conducted to confirm amplification specificity (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses in the bioinformatics part were conducted in the R language (v 4.2.2). The differences between MODS and controls were analyzed by the Wilcoxon test (p < 0.05). S100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 were the key genes

When Lambda.min = 0.002382346, the error rate of LASSO model was the lowest, including 6 LASSO genes, namely, S100A9, S100A8, ITGAM, IRAK3, HGF, and BCL2A1 (Error! Reference source not found.A-B). In addition, in Boruta model, HGF, S100A8, IL1R1, S100A9, BCL2A1, ITGAM, and IRAK3 were marked as "confirmed" and serve as Boruta genes (Error! Reference source not found.D). Among three machine learning algorithms, S100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 were the common characteristic genes (Error! Reference source not found.E). In training set, validation set 1, and validation set 2, S100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 continued to exhibit a consistent trend of differential expression (p < 0.05). They were significantly upregulated in MODS (Error! Reference source not found.F–H). The discovery of key genes provided new targets for MODS in terms of cell apoptosis.

Signaling pathways enriched by key genes (BCL2A1, S100A8, S100A9)

Multiple DICs and pathways related to immune responses were involved in MODS

Among 22 types of immune cells, neutrophils had the highest infiltration abundance in the samples of the training set (Error! Reference source not found.7A). Moreover, there were 16 DICs between MODS and controls. Among them, the infiltration levels of neutrophils, monocytes, plasma cells, and M0 macrophages were significantly increased in MODS, while the infiltration levels of DICs such as naive B cells, resting natural killer (NK) cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, naive CD4 T cells, and CD8 T cells were decreased in MODS (p < 0.05) (Error! Reference source not found.7B). The correlations between key genes and DICs showed consistency. Mainly, the key genes were significantly positively correlated with neutrophils and M0 macrophages (cor > 0.3, p < 0.05), while they were significantly negatively correlated with CD8 T cells, resting memory CD4 T cells, naive CD4 T cells, and naive B cells (cor < −0.3, p < 0.05) (Error! Reference source not found.7C). This consistency strengthens the connection between key genes and the immune environment of MODS.

In the correlation analysis of pathways related to immune responses, "proteasome," "pathogenic escherichia coli infection," "sphingolipid metabolism," and "pantothenate and CoA biosynthesis" related to key genes were significantly upregulated in MODS. On the other hand, "intestinal immune network for IgA production," "primary immunodeficiency," "aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis," and "ribosome" related to the key genes were significantly downregulated in MODS (p < 0.05) (Error! Reference source not found.7D-F). This implied that patients with MODS have alterations in multiple immune pathways. Utilizing key genes to regulate these immune pathways could help correct abnormal immune responses.

Among the 67 immune checkpoints, 27 were differentially expressed in the training set samples (p < 0.05). Key genes mostly had SUMOylation sites

When SUMOylation and SUMO interaction were high, S100A8 and S100A9 had 2 and 4 SUMOylation sites, respectively. For example, S100A8 had a SUMOylation site at the 77th amino acid position of the protein sequence. The peptide EFLILVIKMGVAAHK had a SUMOylation score of 0.8807, which was higher than the Cut-off value (0.82), indicating that this prediction was reliable. At the 25th amino acid position of the gene S100A9, the SUMOylation prediction score of the peptide TFHQYSVKLGHPDTL was 0.8804, which was higher than the threshold of 0.82, also indicating the reliability of the prediction result (Error! Reference source not found.8A-B). Regrettably, no results were predicted for BCL2A1 under the same conditions. Therefore, when the criteria of SUMOylation and SUMO interaction were set to medium, BCL2A1 had 5 SUMOylation sites and 1 SUMO interaction site. At the amino acid positions of 59–63, the prediction score of SUMO interaction for the peptide LKSCLDNVNVVSVDTARTL was 0.7454, which was higher than the threshold of 0.5. Additionally, at the 32nd amino acid position, the prediction score of SUMOylation for the peptide QPGSGPSKTSRVLQK was 0.7345, which was higher than the threshold of 0.67 (Error! Reference source not found.8C). These SUMOylation sites could affect various properties of proteins and might help regulate inflammatory response and restored immune balance in patients with MODS [33].

Discussion

MODS continues to pose a significant clinical challenge, despite extensive efforts by researchers to advance its diagnosis, treatment, and management. Characterized by complex pathophysiological mechanisms and high mortality rates, this study focuses on cellular death processes and reports, for the first time, that BCL2A1 and S100 family genes exert a synergistic effect in MODS, with their joint involvement in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway. This finding addresses the limitation of "isolated gene analysis" in existing research and offers a new framework for exploring the functional roles of key genes in MODS. In this study, we initially employed well-recognized public datasets, machine learning techniques, and human blood samples to identify S100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 as key genes associated with MODS. A nomogram was subsequently constructed to enhance their clinical utility, and the results of the nomogram indicated that S100A8/9 and BCL2A1 are viable blood biomarkers for MODS. Further investigations revealed that these three key genes collectively participate in the "oxidative phosphorylation" and "T cell receptor signaling pathways," which are closely linked to immune regulation and cellular metabolism. Notably, modulation of the SUMOylation modification sites of these genes holds promise for regulating cell apoptosis, thereby controlling disease progression, suggesting their potential as novel therapeutic targets. Additionally, based on the key genes identified, we predicted potential therapeutic agents, including abrine and curcumin. Molecular docking analyses demonstrated high binding affinities between these drugs and the proteins encoded by the key genes. Collectively, these findings provide new directions for the diagnosis and treatment of MODS.

S100A8 and S100A9, both members of the S100 calcium-binding protein family, are released by neutrophils and monocytes and play a decisive role in the progression of inflammation. At low concentrations, they promote tissue proliferation and repair, while at high concentrations, they exert detrimental effects on inflamed tissues [35]. Increased expression of S100A8 and S100A9 has been observed in diseases that can induce MODS, such as acute liver injury, acute lung injury, and sepsis [36]-[39], which is consistent with the findings of our study. In inflammatory responses, S100A8 and S100A9 can bind to TLR-4/RAGE, thereby activating the NLRP3/NF-κB pathway and contributing to inflammatory cytokine storms and mitochondrial dysfunction [40]. Regulated cell apoptosis, also known as mitochondrial apoptosis, relies critically on mitochondria [41]. S100A8 and S100A9 can induce mitochondrial membrane depolarization by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), followed by the inhibition of BNIP3 (an atypical pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family member) and activation of aBak. Following the translocation of aBak and BNIP3 to mitochondria, cytochrome C, Smac, and HtrA2 are concomitantly released from mitochondria into the cytoplasm. These molecules activate caspase-3 either directly or in an IAP-dependent manner to induce cell apoptosis [35]. Therefore, we can regulate the modification status of S100A8 and S100A9 genes through SUMOylation, which may influence the pathophysiology of MODS. This is supported by evidence from several inflammation-related studies, which have shown that downregulating the expression of S100A8 and S100A9 or blocking their binding to TLR-4/RAGE can ameliorate acute pancreatitis, septic nephropathy, myocardial injury during sepsis, and lung injury in peritoneal sepsis [42]-[44].

BCL2A1 (BCL2-Related Protein A1), encoding a protein belonging to the BCL-2 family, is primarily localized to the outer mitochondrial membrane, though it can also be distributed in other membrane structures such as the endoplasmic reticulum [45]. Its main function is to inhibit cell apoptosis [45]. When cells are stimulated by apoptotic signals, BCL2A1 protein can bind to pro-apoptotic proteins (e.g., Bax, Bak), preventing them from forming channels in the inner mitochondrial membrane, thereby inhibiting the release of apoptotic factors such as cytochrome C and preventing the occurrence of cell apoptosis [46]. The role of BCL2A1 is opposite to that of S100A8 and S100A9, as it can reduce mitochondrial damage. A study by Sun et al.[47], suggested that the expression of BCL2A1 is lower in patients with sepsis compared to healthy individuals, indicating a decreased anti-apoptotic effect in sepsis patients. However, our research findings differ from theirs, even showing an opposite trend. Therefore, we hypothesize that BCL2A1 exerts a dual role in MODS: high expression of BCL2A1 may alleviate tissue and organ damage by inhibiting excessive apoptosis, but its overactivation might also lead to abnormal cell survival and exacerbate inflammatory responses. Thus, the functional role of BCL2A1 protein expression in MODS requires further investigation for clarification.

The three key genes identified in this study were consistently upregulated in MODS across the training set, validation cohorts, and RT-qPCR experiments. S100A8 and S100A9, due to their secretory nature, could be readily measured in peripheral blood, representing promising non-invasive biomarkers for early screening or dynamic monitoring, while BCL2A1, although not secreted, exhibits significant transcriptional changes that may serve as an auxiliary molecular marker. A nomogram based on these genes demonstrated excellent predictive performance for early risk stratification and dynamic monitoring of MODS. By converting gene expression into a quantitative score, the nomogram enables standardized identification of high-risk patients, guides timely interventions, and allows real-time monitoring of disease trajectories in clinical practice. These genes also hold potential as biomarkers for early diagnosis and routine monitoring and may serve as therapeutic targets to mitigate organ injury. Notably, all analyses were conducted using blood samples, which are minimally invasive, widely accessible, and suitable for repeated collection, facilitating real-time assessment and broader clinical translation compared with tissue-based approaches.

Our study revealed that S100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 are involved in the "T cell receptor signaling pathway" and "oxidative phosphorylation" pathway. S100A8 predominantly impairs oxidative phosphorylation function by inhibiting the activity of mitochondrial complex I, interfering with the PGC-1α/NRF1 signaling pathway, and inducing excessive mitochondrial fission [48, 49]. Concurrently, it activates the T cell receptor (TCR) signaling pathway either directly or indirectly through interactions with the IL-17 signaling pathway and NF-κB signaling pathway. This activation promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, ultimately leading to immune dysregulation and organ damage [50, 51]. S100A9 facilitates excessive mitochondrial fission and respiratory dysfunction by activating the ERK1/2-Drp1 pathway, which further impairs the oxidative phosphorylation process [49]. Moreover, S100A9 indirectly modulates T cell activity by regulating the function of dendritic cells (DCs). Studies have demonstrated that S100A9 can suppress the expression of B7 co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80 and CD86) on the surface of DCs, thereby reducing antigen presentation to T cells and inhibiting T cell activation. In contrast, the deletion of S100A9 enhances the antigen-presenting capacity of DCs, which in turn exacerbates T cell activation and inflammatory responses [52]. However, the association of BCL2A1 with oxidative phosphorylation and TCR signaling remains unclear, and further investigation is required to elucidate this relationship.

Based on BCL2A1, S100A8, and S100A9, we predicted potential therapeutic drugs that are co-related to these key genes, including abrine, acetamide, azathioprine, calcitriol, curcumin, and diclofenac. Curcumin, a natural polyphenol responsible for the yellow color of turmeric, has garnered significant attention in the medical field due to its anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-tumor properties. These effects stem from its ability to regulate molecular targets such as pro-inflammatory cytokines and transcription factors [53]–[55]. Studies have shown that curcumin can inhibit lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced NF-κB activation and reduce the production of inflammatory cytokines in LPS-treated rat vascular smooth muscle cells and RAW264.7 cells [56, 57]. Additionally, a study demonstrated that curcumin-treated mice exhibited reduced severity of MODS, as evidenced by alleviated systemic toxicity induced by paclitaxel administration, reduced weight loss, and decreased mortality [58]. Therefore, we can proceed to the next phase of clinical trials with these drugs to evaluate their beneficial effects on the prognosis of MODS patients, thereby providing new insights into the pharmacotherapy of MODS, with BCL2A1, S100A8, and S100A9 serving as potential therapeutic targets. However, the clinical translation of curcumin is limited by its low bioavailability, rapid metabolism, and poor systemic absorption. To realize its translational potential, further in vivo and clinical studies are needed, along with optimization of its pharmacokinetic profile and delivery strategies.

This study provides the first insight into the contribution of cell apoptosis to MODS pathogenesis through genetic analysis. To ensure the robustness and generalizability of our findings, multiple independent datasets were integrated and analyzed. This multi-dataset validation strengthens the reliability of results and their applicability across different sample sets, providing a solid basis for further mechanistic studies and potential clinical translation. However, several limitations exist. First, the current clinical sample size is small and derived from a single center, which may introduce regional or population bias. Second, the functional analysis of key genes (S100A8, S100A9, BCL2A1) primarily relies on bioinformatics predictions, lacking validation through cellular experiments or animal models. Third, we have not directly demonstrated whether downregulating these genes can reduce cell apoptosis and improve organ function in MODS models, thus failing to establish a direct causal relationship of "key genes → apoptosis → MODS." Fourth, the association between key genes and MODS prognosis remains unvalidated. The study did not include long-term follow-up data of patients, making it impossible to clarify whether the expression levels of key genes are associated with prognosis, which limits their clinical application value as "prognostic biomarkers." Finally, the specific roles of key genes in different organs have not been explored. MODS involves multi-organ damage, but this study only analyzed gene expression in whole blood samples, without clarifying the differences in expression and function of these genes in target organs such as the lung, liver, and kidney, thus failing to explain their specific regulatory mechanisms in "multi-organ" injury.

Future directions include the following: Validating the direct regulatory effects of these genes on apoptosis, immune cell function, and the oxidative phosphorylation pathway through cellular experiments (e.g., constructing S100A8, S100A9and BCL2A1 knockout, or overexpression cell models). Exploring the impact of targeted inhibition or activation of these genes on organ function and survival rate through animal models (e.g., MODS mouse models) to clarify their causal role in MODS. Expanding the clinical sample size (multi-center, multi-etiology MODS cohort) to validate the diagnostic and prognostic value of S100A8/S100A9/BCL2A1 as blood biomarkers, broaden their universal applicability, conduct external validation of the nomogram’s accuracy, and promote their integration into clinical testing systems. Conducting in vitro pharmacodynamic validation and animal model treatment experiments based on predicted potential drugs (such as abrine and curcumin) to evaluate their safety and efficacy. Laying the foundation for subsequent clinical trials. Exploring the specific expression and functional differences of key genes in MODS induced by different inducers, combined with their association with "oxidative phosphorylation" and "T cell receptor signaling pathway," to improve the generalizability of the research results. Optimizing the prediction model by combining the nomogram with other clinical indicators to enhance its clinical utility.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first comprehensive identification of S100A9, S100A8, and BCL2A1 as apoptosis-related key genes in MODS. These genes were not only consistently overexpressed but also linked to oxidative phosphorylation, immune infiltration, and SUMOylation, indicating their central roles in MODS pathogenesis. The predictive nomogram constructed from these genes demonstrated robust performance, and the identification of curcumin as a potential drug further enhances translational relevance. Collectively, our findings provide novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets, paving the way for improved diagnosis and individualized treatment strategies in MODS.
