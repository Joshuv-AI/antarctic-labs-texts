Immunosenescence and organoids: pathophysiology and therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Amirhossein Kamroo, Mahsa Hosseini Kakroudi, Amirreza Jabbaripour Sarmadian, Ayda Firouzabadi, Shaghayegh Mousavi, Niloufar Yazdanpanah, Kiarash Saleki, Nima Rezaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunosenescence is the gradual deterioration in the functionality of the immune system that has various clinical manifestations, such as a weakened response to vaccination, higher susceptibility to viral/bacterial/fungal infection, and higher incidence rates regarding the ageing-based disorders. Conventionally used models, like animal or 2D models for unraveling the interactions between ageing and immunity are unable to depict the complexity of bodily environments. However, this major limitation can be addressed via using the organoid technology. For clarification, organoids are tissue-resembling 3D structures that are generated from stem cells; they have the major superiority of preserving the human physiology, multicellular intricateness, and the capability of dynamic interactions between the existing elements. Organoid-based technology has been applied for the study of different organ-specific immunosenescence, such as in the intestine, brain, liver, and skin. Besides, organoids offer the bright innovative future of senolytic CAR T lymphocytes and other regenerative-based therapies. This review narrates the cutting-edge application of organoids and the mechanisms involved in the detailed molecular processes of organ-specific immunosenescence.Keywords: Immunosenescence, Organoids, Inflammaging

Introduction

Aging influences both neurological and immunological mechanisms. Immunosenescence refers to the gradual deterioration of immune system function with increasing age, as the natural aging process alters the physiological behavior of both innate and adaptive immune cells [1–4]. These alterations result in a wide range of clinical consequences, such as chronic systemic inflammation, also known as inflammaging [5], increased susceptibility to autoimmunity [6], reduced vaccine effectiveness [7], heightened vulnerability to viral, bacterial, and opportunistic infections [8], the development of degenerative disorders [9–12], compromised anti-tumor immunity [13], an elevated risk of malignancies [14], and increased mortality [15]. With increasing lifespan, the gap between longevity and health expectancy has placed immunosenescence under the spotlight as a public health concern [16, 17]. Therefore, understanding its pathophysiology and molecular mechanisms is of utmost significance to address this growing dilemma.

However, research in this field is faced with numerous limitations. For example, human and long-lived vertebrate populations'studies tend to be cross-sectional, gender-related, and constrained by the limited number of longitudinal studies [18–23]. Moreover, studies on mice are less applicable to humans because mice have an evolutionarily unique pattern of aging, and their aging process is other than ours [24]. Traditional two-dimensional (2D) cell culture methods also fail to simulate the human aging process since dynamic interactions in the normal microenvironment of living organisms are lacking [25]. All these shortfalls in clinical, animal, and 2D cell culture research help substantiate the unfulfilled promise of new models to more effectively investigate immunosenescence, which may be fulfilled through the use of organoids [26–28].

Organoids denote in vitro self-organized 3D mini-cell clusters that exhibit more efficient and available models for advanced research. They uniquely preserve organ-specific characteristics and replicate both in vitro functions and preclinical biological structures, thereby modeling the anatomical, physiological, and biological complexity of various organs [29, 30]. The concept of cellular self-organization was first proposed by Henry Van Peters Wilson in 1907, when he showed that isolated sponge cells could reassemble into functional tissue [31]. In 1975, James G. Rheinwatd and Howard Green established long-lasting cultures of epidermal cells derived from human keratinocytes, providing a solid basis for further research [32]. The first pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) were isolated from mouse embryos in 1980 s by Martin J. Evans and Matthew H. Kaufman [33], and independently by Gail R. Martin, who invented the term embryonic stem cells (ESCs) [34]. In late 1990 s, human ESCs originated from human blastocysts by Thomson and his team [35]. In 2008, Eiraku and colleagues utilized 3D culture systems to transform ESCs into self-organizing cortical tissues, which is a major breakthrough in organoid research [36]. Furthermore, Sato et al. developed intestinal organoids by culturing mouse crypts in a matrix gel, sustaining their 3D structure for extended periods [37]. In 2010, Huh and colleagues engineered the first-ever organ-on-a-chip, a microfluidic platform aimed at replicating the complicated structure and functionality of human organs at a microscale level. Scientists engineered a lung-on-a-chip device that mimicked breathing-induced mechanical strain and the air-blood interface. This innovation pioneered a new class of micro-physiological systems [38].

Organoids are now typically derived from various stem cells, including PSCs, such as induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) and ESCs, as well as adult stem cells (ASCs) obtained from both healthy and tumoral tissues [39]. These organoids are increasingly being applied in precision medicine, chemical toxicological testing, drug screening, and modeling of numerous human organs, such as the brain, retina, thyroid, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, colon, liver, pancreas, kidney, lung, muscle, skin, cartilage, artery, mammary gland, prostate, and endometrium [26, 27] (Fig. 1).

Over the past few years, these models have attracted a lot of interest from scientists and have been used more and more in aging research [28, 40]. This review provides an overview of organoid models used to investigate aging, with a particular focus on immunosenescence across various systems. It also discusses current challenges and limitations associated with these models and explores the future potential of organoid-based therapeutic strategies targeting the underlying mechanisms of immunosenescence.

Overview of immunosenescence

Immunosenescence and inflammaging are key aspects of aging, representing two sides of the same coin [41]. These two phenomena are closely associated and are suggested to be the primary cause of various age-related diseases. Although inflammaging is considered a hallmark of immunosenescence, recent evidence suggests that the relationship between these two phenomena is not unidirectional but reciprocal, where inflammaging causes immunosenescence, and vice versa [42, 43]. Immunosenescence is a complex and dynamic process. For many years, it has been regarded as a harmful process, as it might cause inflammaging and the progressive decline of humoral and cellular immunity [44]. Recently, researchers have revised its negative definition [45]. Indeed, from an evolutionary perspective, age-related alterations in the immune system can be described as adaptive remodeling rather than purely detrimental [46]. In this context, aging can be seen as a spectrum: on one end, age-related diseases where inflammaging plays a central role; on the other end, centenarians are spared from such conditions [47]. Consequently, it can be considered that age-related immune changes may represent a combination of adaptation/resilience and maladaptation [48].

Hallmarks of immunosenescence

Although the biological mechanisms underlying immunosenescence are not yet completely understood, several hallmarks of change are generally observed. For example, thymic involution, dysregulated characteristics of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), imbalanced naïve/memory ratios in B and T lymphocytes, altered proportions of immune cell subsets, increased burden of senescent cells, inflammaging, stress responses, compromised responses to new antigens, DNA instability, and mitochondrial dysfunction are commonly noted [49–54].

Thymic involution

As individuals age, the thymus gland undergoes anatomical, histological, and functional changes, a process known as age-related thymic involution, which occurs maximally around the age of 50 [55]. Thymic involution is one of the most significant changes associated with immunosenescence and is notably responsible for the imbalanced ratio of immune cells, especially T lymphocytes [3, 56]. Many studies suggest that the main cause of thymic involution is the age-related degeneration of thymic stromal cells [57]. Histological changes are characterized by the replacement and accumulation of adipocytes instead of lymphoid tissue, disruption of epithelial cells, thymocyte apoptosis, and the accumulation of senescent cells [58]. Age-related thymic involution leads to a reduced output of new naïve T lymphocytes, decreased export of naïve T lymphocytes into the periphery, a diminished peripheral “T cell receptor” (TCR) repertoire [59–61], and an elevation in peripheral late-differentiated memory T cells [62].

Thymic aging and involution are regulated by a variety of molecules, factors, and signaling pathways, including the following [58]; (I) a decline in the transcription factor Foxn1, which is essential for thymic epithelial cell maintenance and inhibits age-related thymic degeneration [63, 64], (II) downregulation of the WNT signaling pathway, due to over-expression of peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) by epithelial cells, which results in epithelial to mesenchymal transition and promotion of adipogenesis [65–67], (III) decreased expression of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), a pro-longevity hormone that prevents immunosenescence by slowing age-related thymic involution [68], (IV) increased expression of factors such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), oncostatin M (OSM), stem cell factor (SCF), and leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF), which are associated with thymic involution [69], (V) elevated secretion of the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-1 (IL-1β) by macrophages also contributes to thymic involution [70]. In addition, administration of keratinocyte growth factor (KGF), interleukin-22 (IL‐22), and interleukin-7 (IL‐7) in humans has been shown to inhibit thymic involution [71]. Studies have also demonstrated a direct association between sex hormones and thymic involution: with the onset of puberty and increased serum levels of sex hormones, thymic decline accelerates [72].

Inflammaging

The chronic, systemic state of low-grade inflammation accompanied by elevated levels of inflammatory markers is termed “inflammaging”, which is a key characteristic of immunosenescence and a major driver of aging [52]. It develops through the accumulation of injured macromolecules and endogenous cellular debris, which cause persistent tissue damage and contribute to the progression of inflammaging [73]. Senescent cells play a key role in prompting inflammaging by secreting the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which includes an excess amount of pro-inflammatory factors, thereby inducing inflammaging [74–76]. Furthermore, during immunosenescence, the immune system shows a reduced ability to eliminate senescent somatic cells (SSCs), which leads to the exacerbation of inflammaging [77, 78].

Senescent cells tend to secrete SASP, through which they affect nearby and distant cells by inducing inflammation, accelerating senescence, and recruiting immune cells. SASP contains major components such as soluble signaling molecules, including inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1α, IL-1β), chemokines (interleukin-8 (IL-8)), growth factors (transforming growth factor- β (TGF-β)), extracellular matrix proteases (matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)), and insoluble extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins [78, 79]. IL-6 is particularly important and plays a central role in inflammation related to DNA damage and oncogenic stress-induced cellular senescence [80, 81].

Cellular metabolic changes

Another hallmark of immunosenescence is the aberrant metabolism observed in senescent immune cells, characterized by the upregulation of glycolysis, increased levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and mitochondrial dysfunction [82]. In addition, senescent T lymphocytes display altered metabolic activity, marked by a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis, which results in insufficient ATP production and dysregulated immune cell function, such as impaired proliferation and cytokine secretion [83]. Moreover, ROS hierarchically impact immunosenescence. At low levels, ROS initiate lipid peroxidation chain reactions, triggering apoptosis and autophagy in cells damaged by oxidation [84], while at high levels of ROS play a major role in oxidative stress, cellular damage, reduced proteasome activity, inflammaging, cellular senescence, and age-related diseases [85–87]. Furthermore, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) undergoes significant changes in both quality and quantity during aging. These alterations result in decreased mitochondrial biogenesis, compromised energy production capacity, and impaired cellular function [88].

Innate immunosenescence

A handful of studies have revealed that the innate immune system undergoes major changes with aging [89]. Innate immune senescence is primarily characterized by a decline in the efficacy of antigen processing and presentation, which eventually results in a weaker response to stimulation [1]. The innate immune system is composed of various cell types, including NK cells, dendritic cells, neutrophils, and monocytes/macrophages [90]. In this article, we provide an overview of the senescence-associated alterations observed in each of these cell types.

Natural killer cells

Along with aging (from newborn to adult), NK cells increase the expression of the killer cell immunoglobulin-like receptor (KIR) family and, conversely, decrease expression of the inhibitory natural killer Group 2 member A (NKG2A) receptors [91]. The reduction in NKG2A expression leads to reduced inhibitory signaling and, consequently, increased lysis and destruction of healthy cells, which can initiate autoimmunity [92]. This major change stabilizes in young adulthood, and most elderly individuals exhibit a steady KIR/NKG2A composition. However, in some individuals, this NK cell repertoire may be altered due to age-related factors such as human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) infection or ineffective hematopoiesis, like myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) [93].

The distribution of NK cell subtypes changes with age. Aged individuals show an elevated number of CD56dim mature cells, especially those that are late-differentiated and express CD57 (a late-differentiation marker), as well as CD56−CD16+ cells [94]. Notably, the CD57+ CD56 dim CD16+ subset is considered a hallmark of NK cells'immunosenescence. These cells are characterized by high KIR expression, low NKG2A expression, strong cytotoxic capability, reduced sensitivity to cytokines, mature and late-differentiated, and reduced proliferative capacity [95]. Conversely, the immature CD56 bright NK cell subset decreases over aging [96].

Senescent NK cells exhibit reduced capacity to secrete cytokines and cytotoxic activity [97] In addition, impaired NK cells are also defined by decreased NK cell cytotoxicity (NKCC), reduced production of perforin and granzyme [98], as well as diminished secretion of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) [98]. IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), secreted by NK cells, are essential for the recruitment of other immune cells [99, 100]. Recent evidence indicates that NK cells play a key role in immunosurveillance by eliminating senescent cells and alleviating inflammaging. However, senescent NK cells might not function efficiently, resulting in the accumulation of senescent cells and the development of inflammatory disorders [97].

Macrophages

Senescent macrophages, like other senescent cells, are characterized by typical changes such as increased activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) and p16Ink4a [101], along with secretion of SASP [102]. Studies suggest that senescent macrophages derived from diabetic mouse wounds secrete SASP factors containing high levels of CXC motif chemokine receptor 2 (CXCR2), its expression in human dermal fibroblast promotes p21-driven senescence [103].

A. Ramirez et al. reported that macrophages from older mice produce lower levels of pro-IL-1β compared to those from young mice [104]. Pro-IL-1β is the inactive precursor of the active pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β; this impaired cytokine production is associated with decreased immune responses to pathogens [92]. As mentioned earlier, IFN-γ is an important cytokine for immune regulation and also tissue regeneration, C. Zhang et al. showed that decreased IFN-γ responses in macrophages from old tissue led to a reduced tissue regeneration [105]. Furthermore, macrophages from wounds in aged mice, when compared with those in young mice, exhibited decreased proliferative capacity and an aberrant upregulated inflammatory response which were associated with delayed wound repair [106].

Other cells

Dendritic cells (DCs), as major antigen-presenting cells (APCs), also undergo changes during aging. In the study by A. Agrawal et al., DCs from older individuals displayed different functions in contrast to those from young subjects, including I) a significantly diminished capacity to phagocytose antigens, II) a decreased in vitro migratory response to cell recruitment signals mediated by the macrophage inflammatory protein 3β (MIP-3β) and stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), and III) Monocyte-Derived Dendritic Cells (MDDCs) from elderly individuals, when stimulated with Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), showed significantly upregulated phosphorylation of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinases (p38 MAPK) and increased production of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 [107].

Neutrophils, the most abundant subtype of leukocytes, are essential for the first line of immune defense. The increased incidence of infections in the elderly may result from age-related changes in neutrophils. M.H. Perskin et al. found that neutrophils from elderly individuals, compared to young controls, showed structural alterations such as decreased plasma membrane viscosity and an age-related reduction in adhesion capacity [108].

Another age-related change involves the expression of different CXC chemokine receptors. A study by Weisel et al. showed that the expression and chemotactic function of CXC chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4) increase with aging. Conversely, the IL-8 receptor (CXCR2) is downregulated [109]. CXCR4 and CXCR2 are essential for neutrophil retention in bone marrow and their migration to inflammatory sites, respectively [110]. This imbalance may contribute to reduced neutrophil infiltration at sites of infection, which might impair inflammatory response.

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) are a group of immature immune cells that suppress the immune response. MDSCs increase with aging and are also expanded during inflammaging. These cells primarily suppress the expansion and functional activity of T and B cells by releasing anti-inflammatory cytokines like TGF-β and interleukin-10 (IL-10), as well as ROS, which result in a decline in immune response [111, 112].

Adaptive immunosenescence

B and T lymphocytes are central component of adaptive immune system which play the crucial role in pathogens clearance and response to vaccination [113]. Aging is not the only driver of immunosenescence; various factors such as cellular senescence, chronic inflammation, and the tumor microenvironment (TME), can also contribute to the development of immunosenescence [114].

B cell

Several studies suggest that aging has a significant effect on the B cell remodeling process [115]. First of all, circulating mature B cells decrease due to reduced B lymphopoiesis and also impaired mechanisms that support and survive mature B cells in the periphery [116]. This happens because of aged bone marrow (BM) which is characterized by diminished density, and altered vascularization and components. In addition, reduced B lymphopoiesis is also a consequence of the direct suppressor impact of inflammation on lymphopoiesis by inhibiting the localization of B progenitors for maturation [117–119]. Studies have shown that aged individuals exhibit a reduced capacity to produce immunoglobulins IgD and IgM. In addition, the number of naïve B cells decreases, while memory B cells are expanded. Furthermore, elderly individuals showed a shift in antibody subtypes from IgM, primarily secreted by naïve B cells, to IgA or IgG, secreted by memory B cells. This shift away indicates the impact of accumulated exposure to pathogens over the years [120]. Consequently, in aged subjects, there is a decreased B cell diversity or a repertoire composed of a large proportion of antigen-experienced cells. Therefore, these changes result in a compromised immune capacity to respond against new antigens [121]. Functional studies showed the weakened ability of memory B cells to secrete antibodies, reduced capacity to produce high-affinity antibodies, and decline in the differentiation of plasma cells in aged people [122–124].

As above discussed, aging affects the metabolic pathway of energy production in B cells. Some aged B cell subsets exhibit mitochondrial age-related changes by its expanded mass and increased mitochondrial ROSs. This leads to a decline in energy production, disfunction in the synthesis of amino acids, nucleotides, and production of antibodies, as well as, activation of B cells [125].

T cell

As humans age, T lymphocyte immunosenescence is marked by a decrease in the number of naïve T lymphocytes and an expansion of terminally-differentiated CD28− memory T lymphocytes, which are typically considered senescent cells [126]. Emerging data indicate that the diversity and number of CD4+ T lymphocytes are consistently maintained during aging, whereas CD8+ T lymphocytes display more pronounced age-related changes [127]. However, CD4+ T lymphocytes are continuously stimulated by a broad variety of antigens throughout aging, resulting in, impaired telomerase induction, telomeric shortening, and the onset of replicative senescence [128].

Senescent T lymphocytes display marked features, including the loss of proliferative capacity, and elevated expression of SA-β-Gal [129]. CD8 + T lymphocytes with elevated levels of SA-β-Gal exhibit telomere dysfunction-induced senescence and p16-mediated senescence [130]. Furthermore, senescent T lymphocytes exhibit phenotypic changes including, increased expression of senescence-associated markers such as CD57, killer cell lectin-like receptor subfamily G member 1 (KLRG-1) [131], inhibitory co-receptor like T lymphocyte immunoglobulin domain and mucin domain protein 3 (Tim-3), and CD45RA.

CD57 is present on late-differentiated CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, indicating an inability to proliferate, mitochondrial dysfunction, increased ROS expression, MAPK activation, and DNA damage. Similarly, the inhibitory receptor KLRG1 expressed on CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, and NK cells [132], plays a role in inhibiting cellular proliferation [133]. In addition to aging, persistent infection and cancers have been shown to increase KLRG1 expression. Tim-3 serves as an inhibitory regulator of T lymphocyte activity and contributes to immune suppression [134].

Senescence T lymphocytes that upregulate CD45RA tend to exhibit strong cytotoxic functions and produce pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ and TNF-α [135]. One of the remarkable changes is the significantly reduced expression of co-stimulatory molecules CD27 and CD28, which are cell surface proteins, that primarily help T lymphocytes to activate and differentiate [136, 137].

Several discoveries have demonstrated that persistent antigen stimulation is associated with immunosenescence [138]. For example, individuals with latent cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection show a decreased number of naïve and early memory T lymphocytes, along with an increased pool of CD8+ T lymphocytes with CD28−CD57+ phenotype, which reflects a deterioration in T lymphocyte activity [139]. Indeed, long-lasting antigen stimulation results in clonal expansion of terminally-differentiated, antigen-specific CD8+ T lymphocytes, characterized by loss of CD28 (CD28−) and increased expression of CD57 (CD57+). These T lymphocyte subsets have shortened telomeres, have lost their proliferative ability (through the process of replicative senescence), and are considered senescent cells [140, 141]. Furthermore, CD28−CD57+ T lymphocytes express higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in comparison to CD8+CD28−CD57− and CD8+CD28+CD57− T lymphocyte subsets [142].

Another mechanism that alters T lymphocyte function has been observed within the TME of various tumor types [143]. In the TME, there is an elevated number of tumor-associated regulatory T lymphocytes (Tregs) [144], and tumor-specific effector T lymphocytes are inhibited by the accumulation of hypoxia-induced cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and activation of the p38-MAPK pathway [145, 146]. Furthermore, elevated Treg cells consume more glucose than effector T lymphocytes, leading to stress-induced damage in the effector T lymphocytes by triggering ATM-related DNA damage. This process also induces extracellular regulated protein kinases 1/2 (ERK1/2) and p38 signaling pathways, activates signal transducer and activator of transcription 1/3 (STAT1/3), and eventually upregulates the expression of p21, p16, and p53 resulting in the T lymphocyte cycle arrest and senescence [146, 147]. Moreover, lack of glucose activates the adenosine 5′monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) pathway, which contributes to altered telomerase activity and further activation of p38 signaling, finally leading to DNA damage and T lymphocyte senescence [148].

SASP

Senescence cells exhibit a tendency to secrete SASP, through which senescent cells affect nearby and distant cells by inducing inflammation and senescence, and recruiting immune cells. it contain major components such as soluble signaling molecules including inflammatory cytokines ((IL-6), (IL-1β)), chemokines ((IL-8) or (CXCL-8)), growth factors (growth factor-β (TGF-β)), extracellular matrix proteases (matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)), and insoluble extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins [78, 79]. IL-6 is important and plays a key role in inflammation linked to DNA damage and oncogenic stress-induced senescence [80, 81]. Many studies demonstrated the decreased capacity of elderly human leukocytes to proliferate and secrete cytokines [149].

Organoid models for aging

Organoid models for aging

It has been challenging to extrapolate the cellular and molecular basis of aging from studies on traditional human models. The development of organoids has the potential to overcome the limitations of monolayer cell culture and close the gap between human and animal models [150]. ESCs and reprogrammed adult cells, like iPSCs, will generate PSC-derived organoids through step-by-step differentiation protocols that mimic signals involved in gastrulation and organogenesis, using growth factors or inhibitors to initiate the development of different germ layers. Since PSC-derived organoids have aging-related genetic variations, they can provide powerful modeling method to study the pathophysiology of various aging-based disorders, named diabetes [151–153], cancer [154, 155], arthritis [156–158], and neurodegenerative diseases [159, 160], such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), ataxia-telangiectasia (A-T), and Huntington disease (HD), even though these organoids often resemble fetal-stage tissues and are mostly used to investigate organogenesis and early developmental events. Additionally, the increased maturity, complex culture environment, and enhanced inter-cellular interactions in iPSC-derived organoids allow for the re-creation of more intricate and diverse disease features. This in vitro disease modeling capability enables studies to investigate early disease events, discover novel molecular signatures, and conduct effective drug screenings, as well as identify new therapeutic targets. The integration of mathematical modeling with iPSC-derived organoids further enhances the identification of optimal drug targets [39, 150, 161–163]. Besides iPSC organoids, ASC-derived organoids represent the homeostatic or regenerative conditions of their original tissue. Comparing ASCs to organoids from juvenile and aged samples allows researchers to study stem cell exhaustion, a key factor in aging. Brain organoids

AD, as one of the major causes of mortality around the world, especially in older adults, presently has no drug treatment that are capable of healing this condition. similarly, PD is the second most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder following AD [182]. Meanwhile, Organoid systems derived from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) and neural stem/precursor cells (NSCs) exhibit significant potential for modeling neurodegenerative diseases such as AD and PD [183–187]. Human brain organoids can be produced from PSCs, such as iPSCs, especially in NDDs modeling, or ESCs [150, 159, 188]. In 2013, Lancaster et al. developed a human whole-brain organoid derived from PSC, including multiple brain areas: the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, and retinal tissues, through Matrigel without the use of patterning growth factors [189]. The Pasca group advanced Lancaster’s work by exposing embryoid bodies to neural induction and differentiation factors, resulting in the generation of dorsal [190] and ventral [191] forebrain organoids, as well as various region-specific organoids, including midbrain [192], cerebellum [193], striatal [194], choroid plexus [195], spinal cord [196], and hypothalamic arcuate organoids [150, 197]. Whole-brain and forebrain organoids with genetic variants linked to neurodegenerative disorders have been developed as a potential model for investigating the early pathological characteristics of these diseases [150]. Culture settings, including the exposure of organoids to human serum to simulate blood–brain barrier leakage, are used to model sporadic Alzheimer's disease (AD) patients [187]. Due to astrocytes'limitation in differentiating into brain organoids, a brain organoid comprising neurons and astrocytes was created to demonstrate how astrocyte dysfunction contributes to various NDDs [190, 198, 199]. The complex organoid system with both neurons and astrocytes with different genetic backgrounds offers a rare chance to analyze the roles of different cell types in the pathophysiology of NDDs [166]. Additionally, a platform for electrophysiology of brain organoids has been developed [186]. In addition to recapitulating early pathogenic aspects of NDDs, the use of brain organoids can give us important insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying these disorders by breaking down mature brain organoids into individual neurons and providing a powerful platform for identifying effective preventative or therapeutic interventions [167, 200].

Gastrointestinal organoids

The major part of gastrointestinal (GI) organoids, including intestinal, gastric, liver, and pancreatic ones, are still made from ASC. Healthy aging is possible by increasing the regeneration ability of the stem cells. Therefore, the first step in creating efficient anti-aging treatment is unraveling the underlying mechanism of stem cell aging [150]. Stem cells live in a specific environment known as the stem cell niche, which regulates stem cell functions and facilitates self-renewal. It is composed of a cellular matrix, niche cells, and secreted fluids. Organoids through stem cell niche can partially re-create the intricate cellular interactions within organs and the molecular environments encountered by stem cells [201]. ASC-derived organoids use sample from various age groups in aging study models, but they face some critical challenges in finding high-quality ASCs in different ages. To overcome this challenge, various methods such as surgical excision, biopsy, and using advanced cutting-edge technologies, such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting and microdissection based on high-resolution laser capture, can be used. In the surgical excision or biopsy method, ASCs are extracted from either normal or malignant human tissues, but it is difficult to guarantee the number and quality of the tissues. In some ASCs (such as intestinal stem cells) that are tiny, have a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, with no reliable surface markers, Isolating ASCs directly can be challenging. Thus, using advanced technologies, such as fluorescence-activated cell sorting and microdissection based on high-resolution laser capture, can improve organoid results [150, 202, 203].

Skin organoids

In contrast to organoids derived from PSCs or ASCs, skin organoids are produced by co-culturing skin fibroblasts and keratinocytes on specific scaffolds in vitro. Skin aging results from a combination of intrinsic and extrinsic factors that ultimately compromise the structure and physiological function of the skin [204]. In the skin organoid model derived from fibroblasts and keratinocytes, aging can be stimulated by mitomycin C treatment or by prolonging the skin culture duration to 120 days [176, 177]. Extending the duration of in vitro organoid culture or speeding organoid aging via external stimuli appears to be two viable approaches to induce aging. Despite the challenges posed by the variety of cell lines used for skin organoids, they offer an innovative 3D model for examining the molecular and cellular mechanisms behind skin aging [150].

Tendon organoids

Tendons are connective tissues that maintain the strength and function of the musculoskeletal system. Aging and degeneration of tendons result in a significant phenotypic alteration in tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs), accompanied by impaired cellular activities.

The study by Yan et al. established a cell sheet model for in vitro tenogenesis, comparing the sheet formation of TSPCs derived from young/healthy (Y-TSPCs) and aged/degenerative (A-TSPCs) human Achilles tendon biopsies. This organoid study indicates that A-TSPCs show significant challenges in generating 3D tendon tissue organoids, thereby providing the cell sheet model appropriate for exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying tendon aging and degeneration, as well as for evaluating innovative pharmacological approaches for the regeneration of aged cells [205].

Lung organoids

The sudden rise of the elderly population has resulted in an increasing number of age-related conditions, especially the increased prevalence and mortality of chronic pulmonary disorders, including idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). Cellular senescence is a significant characteristic of aging and is more common in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF). The work by Lehmann et al. seeks to describe the alveolar epithelial cells of aged mice and evaluate the impact of continuous WNT/β-catenin signaling on the senescence of alveolar epithelial type II (AT II) cells [206].

Some tissues (e.g., joints, skin) are difficult to model using only PSCs or ASCs. Co-culturing various cellular lines on bio-engineered scaffolds is a solution [176]. Additionally, aging is a body-wide process affecting multiple organs. Organ-on-a-chip innovation—using microfluidic contraptions to mimic organ interactions—is increasingly used to study this systemic aspect of aging. This technology simulates the crosstalk between different organs [207] and allows researchers to investigate the pathophysiology of age-related diseases involving multiple organ systems, like diabetes [151], neuromuscular diseases [208], rheumatoid arthritis [209], and hypertensive nephropathy [210].

Gastrointestinal immunosenescence

Almost half of patients with “colorectal cancer” (CRC) suffer from “peritoneal carcinomatosis” (PC), which is regarded as an extensive metastatic disease that is resistant to chemotherapy and immunotherapy. PC tumor cells exhibit senescence features, considered a potential anti-cancer mechanism that stops cells from dividing. PC tumoral cells present features of arresting cell division. For clarification, the arresting cell division is mediated via the suppressors of the “cyclin-dependent kinase” (CDK), namely p21CIP1 (CDKN1A) and p16INK4a (CDKN2A); amplified amounts of p21CIP1 and p16INK4a, accompanied by the poor proliferation record. In addition, PC samples revealed a decreased level of proliferation indicator of Ki67 and their senescence characteristics were also preserved by epigenetic modifications in the “histone 3 lysine 9” (H3K9me3). Meaningfully correlated with senescence indicator of H3K9me, another feature associated with senescent PCs is the overexpression of stem cell markers of CD44, CD133, “leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5” (Lgr5), which lead to the resume of continuous proliferation. Along with the boosted proliferation, senescent cells exhibit a stemness phenotype through increased activity of “senescence-associated β-galactosidase” (SA-β-gal). An important immune evasion of PC was witnessed in the TME. Senescent PC cells actively secrete proinflammatory factors such as SASP of “vascular endothelial growth factor” (VEGF) and “transforming growth factor-β” (TGF-β) inducing the senescence characteristic in adjacent microenvironments particularly immune cells (immunosenescence) which resulted in significantly reduced infiltration of immune cells such as CD4+ T lymphocytes and CD8+ T lymphocytes and reduced their anti-tumor defense in peritoneal metastasis compared to liver and lung metastasis. Senescent T lymphocytes revealed the expression of Tim-3, “cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein-4” (CTLA-4), “programmed cell death protein-1” (PD-1), and “V-domain Ig suppressor of T cell activation” (VISTA), and the expression of inflammatory signals such as TNF, IL-2/6, and IFN-γ. These mechanisms can explain the chemo- and immunotherapy failure. To explore more, a murine model was made to receive orthotopic transplantation of APTAK organoid. The organoid model resembled the clinical setting of human PC, which supported the earlier clinical results. These findings suggest the senolytic treatment approach to reduce senescence in PC and immune cells and prevent cancer progression [221].

Inflammaging is the mild age-related inflammation of cells, that causes tissue dysfunction and the onset of diseases. The mechanisms of cell-intrinsic and extrinsic factors that interact to cause this phenomenon are still unknown. A recent analysis of the mouse intestinal epithelium, using organoid cultures, has explained how these factors contribute to inflammaging, which is mainly induced by cell-intrinsic factors, most importantly, the transcriptome-based up/downregulations that contribute to the process. Single-cell analyses revealed that inflammaging developed in different ways along the crypt-villus axis. Genes with significant downregulations include: alpha-defensins (Defa3/17/24/26), Wnt3 and the meaningfully upregulated genes were Ccr2, Igtp, Ifitm3, Ly6e, Ifi47, P2rx7. Importantly, aged “intestinal stem cells” (ISCs) exhibited pronounced expression of “major histocompatibility complex class II” (MHC-II) genes, including Ciita as its transcriptional activator. The upregulation of MHC-II is responsible for the escalated interactions with CD4+ T lymphocytes and govern the inflammaging in the “ageing immune microenvironment”. In addition to genome-level alterations, ageing-based signaling elements such as “mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1” (mTORC1), TNF-ɑ, and IFN-γ/ɑ revealed enhancement. Organoids excrete SASP-associated cytokines; however, their secretion levels did not meaningfully differ between younger and older organoids. These findings indicate that the secreted cytokines alone are not associated with signs of organoid aging. Therefore ISC-intrinsic inflammaging is enhanced by chromatin-level priming. Collectively, inflammaging in intestinal cells of rodents is primarily regulated via a cell-intrinsic process, constantly sustained by ISCs, and linked to disruptions in immune balance. Notably, this inflammaging property was permanently maintained in organoid cultures derived from aged “intestinal stem cells” (ISCs), suggesting the presence of a cell-intrinsic inflammatory memory. This memory correlated with increased upregulation of inflammation-related genes both in vivo and ex vivo. Noteworthy, STAT1 signaling was identified as a critical regulator of inflammatory gene expression in this context [217]; the aforementioned signaling element might be a suitable candidate for therapeutic procedures.

Furthermore, the SASP, commonly observed in senescence, serves as a significant source of cytokines that may amplify inflammatory responses and reinforce the inflammaging state. Innate immune system comprises important receptors involved in senescence such as Toll-like receptors and inflammasomes. Inflammasomes are innate immune receptors that mediate IL-1 family cytokines production [222–229]. SASP members such as TGF-β, VEGF, chemokines are responsible for both activating the immune monitoring and amplifying the senescence, however, SASP has pro-oncogenic characteristics. Importantly, the IL-1 and inflammasomes govern the initiation of paracrine senescence [230]. Another experiment showed significant aging-related changes in CD4+ T lymphocytes within the “intestinal intraepithelial lymphocytes” (IEL). A significant increase in these lymphocytes was observed in aged mice, along with downregulation of CD27 and CD28, markers associated with aging systemic T lymphocytes, exhaustion of T lymphocytes, and malfunctioning co-stimulation of T lymphocytes. Moreover, Functional examinations revealed that aged IEL T lymphocytes exhibited greater cytotoxic activity against organoids of intestinal tumors in vitro compared to their younger T lymphocytes, however their ability for proliferation decreased sharply in comparison to younger ones. Single-cell RNA sequencing showed a switch from naïve to memory T lymphocytes in splenic populations, while intestinal T lymphocytes showed the prominent characterization of a CD8αα+CD4+ T lymphocyte subset which was exclusive to senescent mice. Additional assessment of aging IEL CD4+ T lymphocytes showed 2 distinct subsets. Subset 1 demonstrated pro-inflammatory features, characterized by IFN-γ expression and upregulation of “nuclear factor kappa light-chain enhancer of B-cells” (NF-κB) signaling pathways. Subset 2 lacked IFN-γ expression but represented an amplified expression of suppressive factors like Tigit and Lag3, and “natural IEL” (nIEL) bio-markers such as Fcer1g, Cd200r1/r2, klrd1/e1, and Tnfrsf9. These subsets have features in common, such as the expression of granzymes and Cd8a, and inhabited opposite clusters in UMAP evaluation, with special TCR repertoires. Such subsets could suppress intestinal senescence and boost longevity through anti-tumor immunity and the removal of senescent and stressed cells in the senescent intestinal environment [231]. As an innovative method, this subset might be mass-produced and applied for therapeutic options.

A recent study used organoid models to reveal the molecular mechanism of aging, which influences colonic epithelial cell repair after tissue injury. The study found that the aging process decreased the repair ability and proliferation of epithelial cells of aged organoids, which was correlated with upregulation of TGF-β. TGF-β is essential for intestinal development, and its signaling through the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway led to the enhancement of cell cycle arrest. In addition to the Smad-dependent mechanisms of TGF-β, JNK is involved in the Smad-independent signaling of TGF-β. Moreover, they observed decreased expression of “inhibitor of DNA binding” family such as ID1/2/3, which are proteins regulating cell division, and upregulation of p16INK4a; altered expression in these proteins was correlated with increased cell cycle arrest. Additionally, the epithelial recovery of the colon and the growth and/or formation of epithelial organoids were reduced in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-colitis mice, most probably due to increased expression of TGF-β-Smad3-p16 INK4a axis. Importantly, the inhibition of TGF-β was able to counteract the ageing related procedures [175]; this option could be applied for therapeutic interventions.

A systematic comparison of transcriptional signatures of young and senescent epithelial cells in vivo, as well as ex vivo through intestinal organoid cultures, revealed that epithelial cells of senescent tissues showed an inflammatory phenotype with increased MHC-II. This hyperinflammation was linked to raised expression of the immune tolerance molecules such as PD-1/PD-L1 in the senescent tissue microenvironment, suggesting disrupted immune balance. Intriguingly, intestinal organoids originating from senescent rodents sustained this hyperinflammatory signature even following extended culturing, coinciding with enhanced chromatin accessibility at hyperinflammation-related loci [217].

Precisely, an organoid-based revealed that aged ISCs markedly enhanced the expression of genes for secretory markers and genes implicated in the antigen-presenting pathway, leading to a significant increase of MHC-II antigens in the aged intestinal cells. Consequently, the rise of inflammatory cells like CD4+ T lymphocytes and “type 2 innate lymphoid cells” (ILC2s) in the lamina propria was identified as the major cause of the increased level of IFN-γ in the aged intestine, which resulted in the triggering of Stat1 activity in intestinal stem cells. Therefore, it led to abnormal differentiation and increased antigen presentation in intestinal cells. Furthermore, systemic suppression of the IFN-γ pathway—at its high dosage—fully reverts these aging characteristics and reinstates the regenerative capability of the aged intestinal epithelium. IFN-γ has however a dual role in governing the intestinal epithelium: depending on the duration and concentration of IFN-γ, it can either accelerate the apoptosis and reduce the epithelial viability in higher concentrations, or it can enhance the proliferative measures in lower concentrations. Ultimately, this study demonstrated the intricate nature of immune signaling complex governing the viability of the ISCs [232].

“Yes-associated protein 1″ (YAP1) is a major regulator of the Hippo signaling process, a conserved process involved in controlling organ size, cell growth, differentiation, and programmed cell death. YAP1 chiefly functions as a transcriptional co-activator, transferring back and forth between the cytoplasm and nucleus to mediate gene expression. Therapeutic potential of targeting the “Ataxia-telangiectasia mutated” (ATM)/YAP1/”precursor Interleukin-18″ (pro-IL-18) bio-signaling pathway in the patients’ epithelium of the intestinal “inflammatory bowel disease” (IBD) identifies this signaling axis as a pivotal element in the development of IBD, particularly in cases with a later-onset. In addition, examination of intestinal tissue biopsy samples and organoids from later-onset IBD patients demonstrated cooperation of telomere malfunction together with the activation of the ATM/YAP1/pro-IL-18 signaling axis within the intestinal epithelium. Induced telomere dysfunction in organoids derived from healthy individuals confirmed the activation of this inflammatory mechanism to support the idea that telomere damage triggers inflammation. Furthermore, in organoid models generated from cases with Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, pharmacologic interventions, including telomerase activity restoration, DNA damage signaling inhibition, and YAP1 suppression, resulted in attenuated pro-IL-18 generation. Overall, telomere dysfunction in the intestinal epithelium is an essential initiator of inflammation in cases of IBD and highlights potential therapeutic strategies for managing the disease [233, 234].

Senescent “healthy non-tumorous human colon cells” (hNCC) originated from surgical specimens, and intestinal organoids produce local “non-pituitary growth hormone” (npGH). This npGH acts as a SASP element and inhibits p53. In addition to npGH, other SASP members such as SA-β-gal, p16, and “growth hormone” (GH) revealed upregulation. The inhibition of p53 by npGH within autocrine or paracrine signaling settings leads to senescence escape by inhibiting the DNA repair mechanisms and resumes the cellular cycle to increase the cellular proliferation. These post-senescent cells undergo “epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition” (EMT) associated with higher cellular motility. Nu/J mice with GH-secreting human colon cancer cells (HCT116) xenografts and post-senescent hNCC injected intra-spleenically showed four times higher metastases than those with control xenografts, which implies that paracrine npGH induces post-senescent cellular transformation. In contrast, aging cells with inhibited npGH show reduced Ki67 levels along with lower colony formation efficiency. The following sequence of events may clarify the transformation of cells associated with aging: The senescent cells secrete SASP chemokine CXCL1 which provokes immune cells to cellular expiration. At the time, the locally produced npGH secreted by the senescent cells inhibits CXCL1 by suppressing phospho‐NF-κB, leading to reduced expression of this SASP cytokine. In line with these findings, a study on mice lacking growth hormone receptors showed amplified levels of phospho-NF-κB and CXCL1 in the colon, whereas GH abundance was associated with diminished CXCL1. The findings clarify the signaling procedures for regional endocrinal orchestration of microenvironmental alterations in DNA-damaged non-cancerous epithelial cells and highlight a previously underappreciated role of GH that promotes age-related epithelial cell transformation [235].

Organoid culture models many cellular features of the actual tissue and is preserved by growth stimulators that simulate in vivo conditions. Even though the cellular procedure is not completely comprehended; specifically, those characteristics influenced by external factors may be lost during in vivo culturing. To reduce the organoid model's limitations, a comprehensive transcriptome-wide analysis was performed on mouse gut organoids derived from various intestinal regions, genders, and age groups. RNA sequencing revealed that the in vitro culturing process substantially impacts the transcriptome of intestinal epithelial cells, accounting for approximately 60% of the observed variance. Numerous transcriptomic characteristics associated with compartment, age, and gender were lost after culturing, suggesting that they depend heavily on factors external to the cells themselves (systemic or niche bio-factors). Nonetheless, particular cell-fundamental features were preserved; specifically, some compartment-specific features and a small subset of age- and gender-related characteristics remained consistent between the organoids and the original tissue, indicating their plausible potential for studying this system. Approximately 12% of pathways associated with aging were retained in organoid cultures after 14 days. A small group of aging-related pathways such as ones involved in intracellular signaling, immune response, cellular growth/proliferation/degradation, and metabolism retained too, but compared to in vivo tissue, their enrichment was significantly reduced. Interestingly, while these signaling networks were developed in various in vivo situations (e.g., different parts of the gut or sexes), their development in organoids was often limited to single conditions. This suggests that in certain contexts (e.g., male-intermediate conditions), aging may have a stronger influence on the transcriptional landscape, consequently requiring extended culture durations to be recovered. The remaining aging-associated pathways were absent after culturing. Overall, findings through exploring antigen presentation pathway, T-helper differentiation, neuroinflammation bio-signaling axes, CD28 bio-signaling in T-helper lymphocytes, B lymphocyte Development, autoimmune thyroid disease signaling, necroptosis signaling pathway, Nur77 signaling in T lymphocytes, IL-4 signaling, MIF regulation of innate immunity, MIF-mediated glucocorticoid regulation, IL-12 bio-signaling and production in macrophages, inflammasome pathway, IL-15 production pathways suggest that organoids maybe not be an optimal model for studying aging-associated transcriptional phenotypes of the intestinal epithelium, as only a small fraction of aging-related pathways retain their original activity after culturing. On the other hand, the evidence could also be interpreted as an intrinsic ability to restore a more youthful transcriptional profile [218].

A novel concept in therapeutic organoid research involves the architecture of senolytic CAR T cells, which are used to eliminate the accumulated senescent gut cells. Senescent gut cells have been demonstrated to impose a negative influence on intestinal homeostasis via disrupting epithelial layer integrity under conditions of natural aging, injury, and colitis. The elimination of senescent cells in the intestines using senolytic CAR T cells, both in vitro and in vivo, significantly improved the regenerative capacity of aged ISCs. This therapeutic approach also improved epithelial barrier function and mucosal immune responses. Senolytic CAR T cells can collectively refurbish the intestinal microenvironment, showing that targeted cellular therapies can improve tissue regeneration in aging systems [236] (Fig. 2).

Skin and hair immunosenescence

The process of immunosenescence plays a critical role in the aging of skin and hair, altering their structure, function, and ability to regenerate. Recent advances in organoid technology, enabling the development of miniature 3D models that closely mimic real skin and hair follicles, have opened new avenues for studying immunosenescence in these organs.

ACSs including epidermal stem cells and melanocytes, play a critical role in tissue repair and regeneration, and therefore are suitable choice to have skin organoids with most similarity to natural skin structures [237]. Lee et al. tried to form skin organoids in vitro, using epidermis and dermis of a newborn mouse skin. Throughout this study, cells transitioned through different stages until terminal differentiation into hair-producing skin, which gave rise to functional hair follicles after transplanting to the mouse [238]. PCS potentially can differentiate into all skin cell lineages. This makes them ideal to generate organoids with fetal skin development capacity and a platform for wound healing research and applicants [239].

Senescence in skin organoids is modeled by a diversity of methods that simulate distinct aspects of aging. A common method utilizes chemotherapy candidates or DNA-damaging agents such as doxorubicin, which induces DNA injury and subsequently triggers cellular senescence [240]. Aged cells are distinguished using bio-markers such as cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2 A [241] and senescence-associated β-galactosidase, as well as observing alterations in cellular morphology. These skin organoid systems provide a potent platform to investigate the excretion of SASP bio-signals, which are implicated in promoting tissue decay and inflammation, and for developing potential treatments that choosingly target and remove senescent cells [242, 243].

Co-culturing immune cells—including macrophages, T lymphocytes, and dendritic cells—with skin organoids creates a platform to study the dynamic interplay between aging skin cells and the immune system [244]. This interaction drives inflammaging, a persistent, low-grade inflammation that speeds up skin aging and exacerbates conditions like atopic dermatitis, chronic spontaneous urticaria, and bullous pemphigoid—diseases that are more common and severe in older adults. Additionally, aged skin exhibits fewer and less functional Langerhans cells (LCs), which are essential for antigen presentation and immune monitoring. This decline compromises the skin’s ability to prevent infections and neoplastic growths [245]. Incorporating advanced organoid systems, significant insights into the mechanisms can be provided, through which chronic inflammation undermines skin tissue stability, augments vulnerability to infections, and damages skin injury recovery [246, 247].

The microenvironment of aging hair follicle is characterized by increased oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, which impair stem cell function and promote senescence. Hair follicle organoids can model these conditions, enabling the study of molecular pathways involved in inflammation-induced stem cell exhaustion and hair follicle degeneration [248]. Interplays of hair follicles with the surrounding vascular tissue have a functional role in mediating angiogenesis and hair regeneration in the process of aging [249]. The proximity and interaction between “dermal papilla” (DP) cells and “endothelial cells” (ECs) decrease as organisms age, culminating in diminished signaling activity. Especially, the EDN1-EDNRA signaling, which involves EDN1 excreted by ECs binding to EDNRA receptors on DP cells, is reduced. This signaling is mandatory for enhancing taurine (TA) metabolism, which supports hair regeneration. In a similar fashion, the CTF1- “IL-6 signal transducer” (IL-6ST) signaling pathway, where CTF1 from DP cells attaches to IL6ST receptors on ECs, is reduced with age, slowing “alpha-linolenic acid” (ALA) metabolism, augmenting angiogenesis. Experimental activation of these pathways in senescent mouse skin and organoid cultures demonstrates that enhanced EDN1-EDNRA-TA signaling can markedly enhance hair regeneration, whereas augmented CTF1-IL6ST-ALA signaling supports angiogenesis. These findings highlight the reciprocal ECs-DP cellular crosstalk: EC-derived EDN1 mediates TA metabolism to trigger hair growth, whereas DP-released CTF1 improves ALA metabolism to boost vascular regeneration. This work identified critical signaling pathways contributing to the interplay between hair follicles with the surrounding vascular tissue in both normal and aging skin [250] (Fig. 2).

Brain immunosenescence

A major gap exists in understanding the role of immune-driven mechanisms in aging, particularly concerning the brain, because of the limited availability of in vitro models that replicate neuroimmune interactions. Recently, researchers introduced a human brain organoid “micro-physiological analysis platform” (MAP) to explore the ever-changing processes underlying immune-associated brain senescence. This MAP leverages 3D printing to restrain organoid development while facilitating cellular and nutrient perfusion, which enhances organoid development and ensures differentiation into forebrain-exclusive constructs. In addition, the platform integrates a dynamic rocking flow system to enable the perfusion of primary monocytes from both juvenile and senescent donors, along with human cortical organoids, as a result of modeling neuro-immunological interactions. The findings indicate that senescent monocytes infiltrate organoids more readily and enhance senescent-related bio-markers, such as p16 expression, suggesting a contributory function of aged monocytes in developing brain senescence [251]. In addition to monocyte infiltration, brain organoids cultured for prolonged periods (10–13 weeks) show increased neuronal senescence. This is evidenced by upregulation of senescence-associated genes, including LRP10, CCND3, TAP1, SLC9A7, and ELMOD1, and downregulation of protective factors like the anti-aging protein Klotho (KL). These changes suggest that brain organoids can model intrinsic neuronal aging processes alongside immune-driven effects [252].

In the study of Shaker et al., hPSCs-derived brain organoids cultured for extended durations, showed signs of increased cellular senescence, which aligns with a decline in KL gene expression. This senescence is characterized by elevated levels of SA-β-gal activity, p21CIP1/WAF1 protein production, and higher p16INK4A mRNA expression. KL expression in this organoid rises progressively during the first 8 weeks of culture, but markedly reduces by weeks 10 and 13, the same duration during which senescence markers fully manifest. The study also demonstrated that SPARCL1 and COL20A1—two senescence associated genes—were downregulated subsequent to KL regression in Gen2C iPSCs-KRAB-derived organoids, indicating KL’s role in the regulation of cortical neural senescence. Regarding these findings, KL overexpression was identified as a potential therapeutic strategy to prevent neural senescence in adults [252].

Additionally, the relationship between aging and the gut-derived cellular metabolite “trimethylamine N-oxide” (TMAO) is highlighted, which is increased in the midbrain of the elderly and some neurological disorders like “Parkinson’s disease” (PD) [253]. Introduction to TMAO in midbrain organoids triggered molecular changes associated with aging, including higher expression levels of senescence-indication bio-markers like p21 and p16, accumulation of p53, along with epigenetic modifications. Furthermore, midbrain organoids treated with TMAO showed neurodegenerative features, such as damaged “brain-derived neurotrophic factor” (BDNF) signaling, loss of astrocyte activation, dopaminergic cells, and neuromelanin accumulation. TMAO contributes to midbrain aging and neurodegenerations such as PD. This model serves as a promising system for investigating late-onset diseases using iPSC-derived organoids. Notably, TMAO exposure significantly elevated GFAP expression in treated groups. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ and IL-6 were enhanced in the treatment group. It has been shown a marked rise in TNF-α levels has been shown after TMAO administration. Collectively, these results indicate that TMAO may elicit astrocyte-driven inflammatory responses in midbrain organoids. Overall, it is concluded that TMAO can induce hallmarks of brain aging, triggering increased cellular aging, epigenetic changes, neuronal dysfunction, glial activation, and neuroinflammation [254, 255] (Fig. 2).

The use of immunocompetent brain organoids with integrated microglia and peripheral immune cells opens new opportunities for advanced aging research. These models allow for the investigation of neuroimmune dysregulation associated with aging, particularly the impact of inflammaging in neurodegenerative conditions such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and multiple sclerosis [256]. For instance, applying stressors like ionizing radiation to brain organoids can trigger senescence markers and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, effectively replicating aging-related pathologies and disease progression [257–259]. Additionally, organoid MAP technology enhances translational research by offering a human-relevant platform for drug screening. This platform is particularly valuable for testing therapeutics designed to regulate immune activity and mitigate neuroinflammation in aging. It also serves as a powerful tool for exploring autoimmune and neuroimmunological disorders, where immune system aging is a key contributing factor [260, 261].

Liver immunosenescence

Liver organoids are 3D cultures derived from liver progenitor cells which contain multiple liver cell types, including hepatocytes, hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs), and immune cells, enabling the study of complex liver biology, disease modeling, and drug testing [262]. Using organoid technology allowed researchers to generate liver organoids from both PSCs and ASCs, which closely mimic the liver’s structure and function. Liver-derived ASCs can be cultured to produce 3D organoids without showing senescence markers for months [263, 264]. A key strategy to establish long-term cultures involves “isolating epithelial cellular adhesion molecule” (EpCAM+) cells from liver biopsies, as they are enriched for ASCs [265].

Exposure to stressors such as free fatty acids (oleic acid, palmitic acid) or TGF-β1 in liver organoids triggers inflammation and fibrogenic pathways, closely mimicking age-related liver disease progression. this involves upregulation of inflammatory mediators such as TGF-β and TNF superfamily, activation of HSCs into myofibroblasts, and increased extracellular matrix deposition—hallmarks of chronic inflammation and fibrosis seen in aging livers. Single-cell transcriptomics of stimulated liver organoids reveals dynamic changes in cell–cell interactions and immune signalling, with distinct ligand-receptor expression patterns like PDGFB/PDGFRB in TGF-β1 models and CXL2/DPP4 in OA models [266].

Recent research has focused on delineating how different stressors influence senescence-associated changes, including alterations in phenotypic markers, molecular signaling pathways, and energy metabolism, using “hepatocyte-derived liver organoids” (HepOrgs) and “primary mouse hepatocytes” (PMH). Each stressor elicited a distinct pattern of senescence markers in PMH. In the evaluated agents, doxorubicin ranked as the top effective inducer of senescence, followed by hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and cisplatin, with no evidence of apoptotic effects. All agents were found to disrupt mitochondrial function and activate DNA damage responses, independently of MAPK and AKT pathways. To investigate how different senescence-triggering agents influence cytokine release, the expression of various cytokines and chemokines were analyzed, such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, CCL2, CXCL2, PAI-1, MMP13, TNF-α, TGF-β1, and TIMP. Even though doxorubicin and etoposide both trigger topoisomerase II which leads to DNA injury, their effects on the SASP varied significantly. Both agents elevated CCL2, TIMP, and MMP13 levels; however, doxorubicin increased TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10, which were downregulated by etoposide. In addition, doxorubicin enhanced CXCL2 and suppressed TGF-β1 expression. At the signaling level, doxorubicin induced MAPK and mTOR signaling while inhibiting AKT; however, etoposide improved mTOR/AKT meanwhile suppressing MAPK. Cisplatin – as the DNA cross-linking agent – elevated IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, CXCL2, and MMP13 while suppressing PAI-1, CCL2, TGF-β1, and TIMP. These influences were modulated via MAPK activation and AKT suppression. In a similar fashion, 5-azacitidine, which triggers DNA hypermethylation, upregulated TIMP, IL-6, and, IL-10 while suppressing IL-1β, TNF-α, CXCL2, and MMP13 via suppression of mTOR, AKT, and MAPK signaling. Telomerase inhibition by BIBR-1532 impacted SASP expression, augmenting IL-10, TIMP, and CCL2 while reducing PAI-1, CXCL2, IL-6, and MMP13 levels through the inhibition of MAPK and AKT pathways. Both ethanol and H₂O₂ via inducing oxidative distress had distinct impacts on cytokine expression. Both stressors increased IL-6, IL-10, CCL2, TGF-β1, TIMP, and TIMP. However, H₂O₂ uniquely elevated CXCL2 and TNF-α, while ethanol selectively reduced IL-1β and TNF-α. Mechanistically, H₂O₂ enhanced the expression of AKT yet reducing AKT’s phosphorylation, in addition to downregulating mTOR, while ethanol suppressed the MAPK/mTOR/AKT pathway. Moreover, intrusion with nucleotide pools through agents including galactosamine and hydroxyurea affected SASP profiles. Galactosamine depleted uridine and increased CCL2, CXCL2, IL-6, as well as IL-10, however reducing TNF-α, TGF-β1, TIMP, and PAI-1 via MAPK and mTOR inhibition. Hydroxyurea, an inhibitor of ribonucleotide reductase, elevated TIMP, CCL2, IL-10, IL-6, and MMP13 while decreasing PAI-1, IL-1β, CXCL2, TGF-β1, and TNF-α through suppression of mTOR, MAPK, and AKT. Besides, p53 activation was further studied via Nutlin-3a, an MDM2 inhibitor. Nutlin-3a accelerated TIMP, CCL2, and IL-10, however reducing TNF-α, TG-Fβ1, MMP13, and IL-6 levels through activation of AKT/mTOR and inhibiting MAPK signaling. These results portray the intricate regulation of SASP by different aging-activating stimuli, highlighting the variability and complexity of the senescence phenotype. Notably, CCL2 and IL-10 were invariably increased across conditions, while PAI-1 represented an invariable decrease [267] (Fig. 2).

Most current liver organoid models lack a fully functional immune compartment, that restricts their ability to recapitulate the full spectrum of immunosenescence observed in aging hepatic tissue. Progress in co-culture systems and bioengineering, such as incorporation of Kupffer cells and lymphocytes, are beginning to address these gaps, enabling more accurate modeling of immune aging and its molecular underpinnings [268–270].

Endometrial immunosenescence

IL-17 receptor B (IL17RB), a transmembrane receptor that primarily binds to the pro-inflammatory cytokines of IL-17B and IL-17E, remains a potential bio-marker for hyperinflammation, malignancies, and endometrial aging [219, 220]. To study its probable relation with endometrial immunosenescence, organoids derived from human hysterectomy tissues were exposed to SASP cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-8, which are elevated in terms of IL-17RB expression. As a result, only IL-1β meaningfully lessened the organoid-forming potential and boosted p21 expression compared to unexposed organoids. However, simultaneous exposure to IL-1β and a JNK inhibitor restored both organoid-forming potential and p21 expression [220] (Fig. 2).

Hematological immunosenescence

Recent advancements in thymic organoid technology have highlighted its potential for the clinic. Specifically, thymic organoids derived from iTEPC-coFoxn1 mice demonstrated a wider TCR repository compared to mice transplanted with iTEPC-mTurq2 or MEF. These findings support the potential for generating thymic organoids from autologous iPSCs, which could aid in treating primary immune deficiencies such as DiGeorge Syndrome and acquired immune deficiencies such as AIDS, assist in rebuilding the T lymphocyte-based immunity after “allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation” (HSCT), besides helping to reverse the immunosenescence effects [271]. The development of bioengineered thymus organoids provides hope for restoring thymic function and fostering donor-exclusive immunological tolerance in transplant conditions. Restoring and maintaining thymic function is key for neutralizing the effects of thymic involution and sustaining immune health. Edge-cutting research incorporating the up-to-date bio-engineered thymus organoids combined with growth-stimulating cytokines and bio-factors such as IL-21 has shown promising results in murine models, significantly rejuvenating peripheral T lymphocyte pools and restoring immune functionality. However, despite these encouraging findings, a comprehensive understanding of thymic restoration for clinical use remains incomplete, and there are still questions about the feasibility of consistently producing functional naïve T lymphocytes [271–274]. Single-cell multi-omics RNA and sequencing of ATAC in juvenile and aged “hematopoietic stem cells” (HSCs), defined by “Lineage-CD34−CD48−CD150+Sca−1+Kit+”, revealed 2 distinct subsets of CD117 (Kit) HSC subclass: Kithi and Kitlo. These distinctive subsets displayed peculiar transcriptional signatures, as for the Kithi subtype of HSCs characterized by a tendency toward platelet lineage—also known as the quiescence and low-expressed gene elements. In contrast, the Kitlo subtype of HSCs showed an augmented capacity of multi-lineage added to high-expressed gene elements. Kitlo subtype of HSCs was found to be less frequent in older mice, suggesting a decline with age. While the Kitlo subtype of HSCs has previously been linked to accelerated self-cell-renewal, their potential for T lymphocyte production and all the underlying mechanisms driving lymphoid differentiation were not well defined. Further S17 stromal assays, in vitro studies, and “artificial thymic organoid” (ATO) analyses demonstrated that the Kitlo subtype of HSCs produced increased numbers of lymphoid progenitors and T lymphocytes across all age groups. In the competitive allogeneic HCT models, the Kitlo subtype of HSCs facilitated embittered thymus-based recovery and post-HCT T lymphocyte reconstitution, irrespective of the donor's age. This highlights the capacity of the Kitlo subtype HSCs as an age-conserved subtype supporting and encouraging the production of T lymphocytes added to the restoration of thymic. Notably, analogous the Kitlo subtype of HSCs was also identified in human specimens which emphasizes their clinical relevance for mitigating aging-related immune decline and therapeutic-based immune suppression [275].

To explore factors regulating germinal center activity, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based immune tissue organoids were explored to study how the manner of antigen presentation, and integrin ligands affect germinal “center-like” B lymphocyte epigenetics and characteristics. Results indicated that while PEG vinyl sulfone and acrylate polymers did not support primary immune cell viability, “maleimide-functionalized PEG” (PEG-4MAL) promoted B lymphocyte proliferation and germinal “center-like” formation. RNA sequencing analyses of data from lymph node stromal and germinal center B lymphocytes revealed variability in integrin-related gene expression linked to the niche. The assimilation of niche-resembling polypeptides revealed that collagen-1 enhanced the germinal “center-like” behavior besides epigenetic changes. PEG-4MAL organoids were used to study the differential effects of membrane-embedded versus soluble antigens from K. pneumoniae, maintaining a reaction from juvenile types and aged types rodents [276].

While naïve T lymphocyte aging is thought to be linked to disruptions in cellular homeostasis, the precise connection between naïve T lymphocyte states and human aging remains unclear. Emerging evidence suggests that certain subsets of naïve T lymphocytes may contribute to immunosenescence and weaker vaccine responsiveness, highlighting their potential role in age-related immune decline [277]. A lymphoid organoid system was developed to sustain resting naïve T lymphocytes with high CD45RA expression for more than two weeks. Detailed phenotypic analyses revealed that a decrease in CD45RA density is a distinguishing feature of aging. CD45RAhigh transformation to CD45RAlow was replicated inside the organoid structure by constructional disintegration without contributions from the senescence of stromal cells or changes in the lymphocyte denseness. This conversion was driven by alternative splicing of CD45, indicating that external lymph node factors may promote phenotypical alterations of naïve T lymphocytes in older adults [278] (Fig. 2).

While generating T lymphocytes from hESCs and iPSCs is viable, the produced TCR repertoire persist unpredictable due to random VDJ recombination. Despite this limitation, engineered TCRs and CARs have enabled iPSC-derived T lymphocytes to target tumor antigens, infiltrate solid tumors, and delay cancer progression in xenotransplant model [279, 280]. Additionally, reprogramming may offset replicative senescence by improving telomerase activity and restoring telomerase length, and thus extending T lymphocyte longevity [281].

Challenges and future therapeutic outlook

In spite of significant progresses which have been made in the field, many challenges in translating organoids in the context immunosenescence-related research. Notably, immunosenescence extends beyond the immune system itself, and a close understanding of how senescence influences different organ systems as well as their complicated interaction with immunocompetence is required [282, 283]. Even though organoid models remain key tools as far as organ-specific alterations are concerned, these tools lack the dynamic organ-to-organ communication and cellular cross-talk necessary to robustly recapitulate the systemic nature of immunosenescence [284].

Gut microbiota is also a key regulator of the immune function [285, 286], which itself undergoes profound fluctuations during the natural senescence process [287]. These microbial shifts in composition and diversity contribute to immunosenescence through modulation of chronic inflammation, immune response, and overall immune function [288, 289]. However, organoid models remain restricted in their ability to replicate the complex, bidirectional relationships between the gut microbiota and the immune system, as well as their systemic implications [290, 291].

Another essential yet underaddressed element is the local microenvironment, such as oxygen levels, nutrient availability, signaling gradients, mechanical forces, and fluid circulation within the target tissue, all of which are disrupted during aging [292, 293]. Individual variations in reduced blood flow and impaired lymphatic drainage disrupt tissue homeostasis, hinder immune cell trafficking, and impair waste clearance. These alterations exacerbate oxidative stress and stimulate the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby sustaining a state of chronic inflammation. Collectively, these factors accelerate the progression of immunosenescence [294–296]. Current organoid models often fall short in replicating the dynamic, finely regulated physiological conditions of living tissues. This shortcoming substantially limits their ability to faithfully recapitulate the complex, multifactorial processes that drive aging and immune system decline [297].

In order to fix these restrictions, the integration of organoid-based co-culture systems with microfluidic organ-on-a-chip platforms indicates a promising approach for more precisely modeling the complexity of neurophysiological processes (Fig. 2). Such platforms combine the structural and functional improvements achieved in 3D organoid cultures with the precise environmental control of microfluidic technology [298]. This synergistic integration enables the co-culture of multiple organoids, supporting the simulation of inter-organ communication and the dynamic cellular and molecular interplay between immune and non-immune cells. It also allows for the exploration of host-microbiome interactions, controlled regulation of fluid dynamics, and real-time modulation of the microenvironment. Together, such capabilities significantly improve physiological relevance and improve the reliability of immune-aging modeling [299–301].

In addition, they provide a strong platform for advancing precision medicine due to proper capturing of patient-specific variations in immunological responses and disease features. This enables the development of personalized treatment strategies with enhanced efficiency and minimized adverse sequelae [302]. The addition of genome editing techniques further enhances these platforms by enabling precise manipulation of genetic elements, as a result enabling the investigation of specific molecular pathways and regulatory networks involved in processes such as immunosenescence. This capability enables researchers to model patient-exclusive mutants, help with decoding of gene-microenvironment relatipnships, and evaluate the functional influence of age-related genetic variants [303]. Furthermore, these platforms enable the identification of markers and potential drug targets, thereby accelerating the translation of research findings into clinical applications. This will not only facilitate the development of more accurate diagnostic tools and targeted therapies, but also enhance patient stratification, treatment effectiveness, and clinical outcomes in the long run [304, 305].

Overall, the combination of organoid-based co-culture systems with microfluidic organ-on-a-chip platforms offers a powerful and adaptable framework for advancing our understanding of immune aging. Through the recapitulating complicated inter-organ communication within a controlled and physiologically relevant microenvironment, these integrated systems could fill in the gap between available models and human physiology (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Shared mechanisms of immunosenescence across organs

Despite the differences, many of the main immunosenescence features take place in different organs, regardless of their entity: 1) Inflammaging: Most bodily tissues demonstrate the low-grade inflammation of senescence and senescence markers of SASP, like IL-6/1β [267], TNF-α [254, 255], and TGF-β [175] in gut, brain, skin, liver, and thymus [41]. 2) The aggregation of immune cells: Across the different organoids applied for the modeling of immunosenescence, the aggregation of specific immune components like T lymphocytes and macrophages with the discharge of senescence markers and a deterioration in functionality is witnessed [28, 74–76]. 3) Altered immune surveillance: The status of immune surveillance is altered in different tissues, such as the weakened cytotoxicity of the NK cells in skin and liver [89, 90], modified antigen presentation in the gastrointestinal system [217], and upregulated expression of inhibitory signals in brain [254, 255]. 3) Genetic and epigenetic alterations: organoid models revealed the alterations in different loci as in MHC-II in the gastrointestinal system [217] and the enhanced accessibility of senescence genes in brain [252].

Organ-specific differences

Despite the commonalities, each organoids revealed tissue specific alterations in terms of immunosenescence: 1) Gastrointestinal tissue: IFN-γ–STAT1 mechanism governing a part of the immunosenescence [217], distinguished cell-intrinsic inflammaging in ISCs [217], IEL senescence [231], intestinal tumors with specific senescence-inducing features [221]. 2) Brain tissue: the role of infiltrating monocytes in accelerating the senescence [252], TMAO-mediated senescence and neuroinflammation [254, 255], dopaminergic neuron susceptibility [192]. 3) Skin/Hair tissue: concurrent senescence of papilla, endothelium and their interaction [249, 250], EDN1-EDNRA signaling axis in follicles [250]. 4) Liver tissue: fibrogenesis incidence in hepatocytes [266], limited presence of immune components in most organoids [268–270]. 5) Thymus/Hematopoietic tissue: depletion in naïve T lymphocyte [271–274], decline in Kitlo HSC [275], impaired ability to regenerate the thymus [273].

Therapeutic perspectives

Insight over the shared and unique features of organ-specific immunosenescence can be applied for both organ-specific and pan-organ therapeutic approaches.

Organ-Specific Strategies: 1) Gastrointestinal tissue: blocking IFN-γ–STAT1 signaling, enhancing ISC endurance. 2) Brain tissue: enhancement of Klotho signaling, proper modulation of microglial cells. 3) Hair tissue: recovering the signaling between endothelium and papilla. 4) Liver tissue: the proper modulation of SASP network. 5) Thymus tissue: IL-21 delivery.

Pan-Organ Approaches: Expanding the organoids for further investigation of the intricate axes and cross-talks of the immune system is suggested. For example, formulating the culture for lymphatic nodes and thymus as the lymphatic organs and the main site for lymphocyte maturation is suggested. Organoids of the thymus can represent the thymic atrophy associated with immunosenescence. CRISPR-Cas9 gene modification tools or RNA-based treatments can be widely used in future investigations. The organoids offer an enclosed micro-environment for the cleansing of the senescent cells or their associated DNA duplication and repairment malfunctions. Besides, more studies on cellular reprogramming systems are suggested to resurrect the stemness of the stem cells. The modern computer-based bioinformatic tools can be incorporated into the concept of organoids. Genomics, Proteomics, and Transcriptomics integrated with modern AI-based systems, such as AlphaFold, may further accelerate organoid-based research of the biomolecular signaling elements, axes, and networks. Spatial omics can also be applied for a further assessment of the cell-to-cell interplays and the following negative or positive modifications of the immune cells during senescence. Recent advancements have led to the development of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells capable of safely and effectively targeting and eliminating senescent cells. CAR T cells redirect the effector function of T cells towards specific cell-surface antigens, allowing for precise and selective elimination of target-expressing cells. Senolytic CAR T cells have been engineered to recognize and lyse cells expressing the urokinase-plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR). uPAR has been shown to be highly expressed on senescent cells across multiple models in both mice and humans, including in natural aging, where uPAR-positive cells are a significant contributor to the senescence burden in aged tissues [306, 307]. Furthermore, while cellular senescence is known to reduce the regenerative capacity of the intestines, the in vivo consequences of senescent cells on intestinal health remain inadequately understood. Recent studies have demonstrated that the accumulation of senescent cells within the aging gut impairs epithelial integrity and disrupts overall intestinal homeostasis. Using senolytic CAR T cells to selectively remove these senescent cells in vivo and in vitro has been found to enhance the regenerative potential of aged intestinal stem cells (ISCs), promoting better epithelial integrity and improved mucosal immune function. These findings put emphasis on the potential of senolytic CAR T cell therapy to rejuvenate the intestinal niche, offering a novel approach for addressing age-related declines and facilitating regenerative processes [236, 308].

For the future, it is suggested that studies utilize computational approaches to screen or engineer novel immunomodulatory candidates to target immunosenescence mechanisms [309–320].

Conclusions

Immunosenescence is considered one of the main hallmarks of ageing, denoting to the gradual decline in the immunological mechanism with a wide range of clinical comorbidities. Traditionally used models lack sufficient precision for imaging the immunosenescence, and the new-rising technology of organoids can compensate for some of the shortcomings of the traditional models by maintaining the tissue structure. Different organoid-based tissues have been applied for the study of organ-specific immunosenescence, such as the brain, intestine, liver, endometrium, skin, thymus, and hematologic tissues. Different studies have imaged the role of immunosenescence features such as the SASP, immune system dysfunctionalities, pathogenic transformation in the organs, and the role of different signaling elements and cascades. In addition, emerging therapeutic innovations like senolytic CAR T cells and bio-engineered organoids have depicted the possible future applications of organoids. Despite all the advantages over traditional models, organoids lack some aspects of an all-inclusive body physiology. For example, organoids are unable to fully image the systemic immunologic interactions or microbial commensalism. Future studies in this matter shall focus on the application of organ-on-chip systems, co-culturing with microbiota, and other state-of-the-art solutions to resolve the current limitations of organoids. In conclusion, organoids depict themselves as a translational approach for ageing research and studies of dynamic molecular interactions in organ-specific details.
