Pioneering integration of combinatorial chemistry and machine learning to accelerate the development of tailored LNPs for mRNA delivery
Authors: Xi He, Pingyu Wang, Linbo Qing, Xiangrong Song
Published: 2024

Ionizable lipid (IL), the most important component of lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) for messenger RNA (mRNA) delivery, is closely related to the mRNA expression and targeted property of LNPs. Recently, a pioneering research led by Li et al.1 published in Nature Materials reported a novel approach to the high-throughput screening of novel ILs for mRNA delivery via combinatorial chemistry and machine learning (ML) (Fig. 1). 119-23, a newly structured IL, was finally designed by this strategy, which outperformed commercial ILs (MC3 and SM102) in transfecting muscle cells and immune cells in several tissues. mRNA therapeutics can be programmed to produce antigens that trigger immune responses against tumors and infectious diseases, as well as to generate functional proteins for the treatment of genetic diseases or other diseases2. The central success of mRNA-based therapies is the plenty of functional protein/peptide expression. LNPs, as efficient and remarkable delivery systems have facilitated mRNA applications in the clinic, in which IL showed great influence on mRNA delivery in vivo3. The chemical structure of ILs can be mainly divided into the hydrophilic head group, hydrophobic tail, and linker. The structure variation vastly impacts the biological function of LNPs. Although the high-throughput synthesis method has been explored, the type of chemical reactions they used will limit the designed ILs with structural diversity and flexibility. Therefore, developing new ILs remains a bottleneck for the optimization of LNPs.

To address this, Li developed a one-pot four-components reaction (4CR) platform based on Ugi reaction to high-throughput synthesis ILs. This 4CR platform was an advancement compared to the 3CR system they reported before4 and facilitated the creation of a wider array of ILs. Based on the 4CR platform, a chemically diverse library of 584 ILs was rapidly created. This new synthesis approach makes the chemical reaction capable of being carried out in biocompatible solvents without requiring a catalyst or cumbersome protection/deprotection steps, which is compatible with the high-throughput synthesis of ILs. It's worth noticing that all the ILs mentioned above with high-throughput synthesis included ones in this study were used without any purification in the primary screening process, but the impurities unremoved may result in the inaccurate evaluation of the ILs. What's more, a limited number of chemical reactions is compatible with the high-throughput synthesis of ILs, which can be carried out in biocompatible solvents without requiring a catalyst or cumbersome protection/deprotection steps. Thus, developing novel multicomponent reactions and high-throughput purifying platforms may further greatly speed up the manufacturing efficiency of ILs in the future.

On the other hand, an available candidate may be obtained after screening from the hundreds of synthesized ILs. However, this traditional strategy is usually time-consuming, labor-intensive, and susceptible to failure. ML techniques were introduced by Li and coworkers5 to improve the screening efficiency of available ILs, which have demonstrated considerable promise in drug discovery and chemical compound screening. To obtain a useable ML algorithm, the chemical structural data of the 584 ILs mentioned above and their relative mRNA transfection results served as the foundational dataset for ML algorithms training. Next, a bigger library of 40,000 ILs was designed and used to screen high-value lipids with the trained model Boost. Finally, a fire-new IL 119-23 outstood and exhibited comparable mRNA transfection capabilities in the muscle and immune cells compared to commercial benchmarks MC3 (Alnylam's Onpattro) and SM102 (Moderna's Spikevax). It's worth noting that the linker of IL 119-23 contained a bulky adamantyl group, departing from the structure of classical ILs. These results provided a new insight into the structural design of ILs and exhibited the successful prediction ability of the ML algorithm. However, the model XGBoost in the current study was developed based on traditional ML algorithms and trained by the small-scale datasets of LNPs, resulting in the limitation of their predicting capabilities. Since all features were input into traditional ML algorithms and some noise information within features could mislead the model inference, these ML algorithms were unable to be further generalized into the unseen samples from the large-scale datasets. In the future, developing models based on deep learning algorithms will be a valid approach to boosting their generalization capability in the design and optimization of LNPs.

In addition to predicting the chemical structure of ILs, ML algorithms have also been applied to optimize the formulation of LNPs and pharmacodynamic properties of mRNA therapeutics. Wang et al.6 have built an ML model to predict LNP formulations for mRNA vaccines against infectious disease, and about 400 data points were collected from 22 studies for ML training. Ding's team7 created a dataset of 572 LNPs along with their level of mRNA transfection efficiency, and then developed an ML model to predict the transfection efficiency of newly constructed LNPs. These studies have demonstrated the potential of ML in improving the performances and screening efficiency of LNPs. However, a serious problem has emerged: the amount of available data was far from meeting the training requirement of ML algorithms. The availability of large-scale, high-quality data is often a bottleneck for biomedical applications, including the optimization of LNP-based delivery systems. Therefore, we hope that a comprehensive dataset of LNPs or other mRNA delivery systems can be created with the joint efforts of researchers in the future. The dataset can include different kinds of information, such as chemical structures, physicochemical properties (e.g., particle sizes, zeta potential, mRNA encapsulation efficiency), mRNA transfection efficiency, biofunctions, etc. More wet test data with high quality will help improve the accuracy and precision of ML algorithms to obtain more efficient LNPs.

Except for the efficient delivery of mRNA in vivo, safety is also critical for the clinical translation of mRNA nano-drugs. Some side effects (pain, swelling, fever, sleepiness, etc.) have been reported in humans vaccinated with the SARS-CoV-2 mRNA vaccine from Moderna or Pfizer/BioNTech8. This is mainly associated with the highly inflammatory nature of LNPs, especially the ILs. Thus, future studies should give equal attention to the efficacy and safety of LNPs for the rational design of IL9. A good balance between the effectiveness and safety of LNPs will facilitate more mRNA-based therapeutics to enter clinical trials and even get marketing approval from the regulatory authority.
