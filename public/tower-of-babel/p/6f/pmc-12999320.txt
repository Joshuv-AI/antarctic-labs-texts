SLC22A3 deficiency leads to cognitive impairment through the cardio-neuroinflammatory axis mediated HA/H1R/NLRP3 pathway in heart failure mice
Authors: Zhitian Wang, Lv Zhou, Na Zhao, Tianxiao Li, Fan Geng, Jingting Kong, Yahao Zhang, Zhijun Zhang, Qing-Guo Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heart failure (HF) affects over 64 million individuals worldwide and is strongly associated with cognitive impairment (CI), yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we identify solute carrier family 22 member 3 (SLC22A3) might be a candidate gene for HF-induced CI through Mendelian randomization and bioinformatics analysis. To investigate its functional role in vivo, we established a mouse model of HF after myocardial infarction (MI). Cognitive performance was evaluated using the Morris water maze. Expression of SLC22A3, blood-brain barrier (BBB) integrity, and neuroinflammatory signalling were examined via immunofluorescence and Western blotting. The involvement of the HA/H1R/NLRP3 signalling pathway was further evaluated using cardiac-specific SLC22A3 overexpression mice, hippocampal-specific H1R knockdown mice, NLRP3 knockout mice, and BV2 cell assays. Consistent with the findings in HF patients, cardiac SLC22A3 expression was dramatically downregulated in HF mice, accompanied by an increase in peripheral histamine (HA) levels, while HA levels in the mouse brain were also significantly raised. Using cardiac-specific SLC22A3 overexpression in HF mice, we demonstrated that restoring SLC22A3 reduced HA accumulation and improved cognitive performance. Mechanistically, HA breached the compromised BBB in HF mice, activating hippocampal microglia H1 receptor (H1R) and the NLRP3 inflammasome. In BV2 cells, HA stimulation elevated NLRP3 expression in a dose-dependent manner, an effect blocked by H1R antagonist. Knockdown of H1R or NLRP3 in the hippocampus attenuated neuroinflammation and rescued HF-induced CI. Our findings unveil a novel cardio-neuroinflammatory axis driven by SLC22A3 deficiency, highlighting HA/H1R/NLRP3 pathway as a therapeutic target for HF-induced CI.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is one of the most daunting challenges in contemporary medicine, affecting 64 million individuals globally [1]. It is distinguished by decompensation of cardiac function, which means that the heart cannot pump enough blood to the brain and peripheral organs, resulting in functional deficits on several levels, including cognition [2]. This severe clinical state has recently been identified as one of the primary causes of hospitalisation and mortality. Furthermore, non-traditional comorbidities associated with HF, particularly cognitive impairment (CI), represent one of the most common and serious consequences of the condition [3]. Epidemiological analyses indicate that the prevalence of CI in HF patients may reach 43%, which is 1.67 times higher than that observed in the non-HF population [4]. Compared to the normal population, people with HF suffer from significant overall cognitive decline and show significant impairment in several cognitive domains, such as processing speed, executive function and verbal memory [[5], [6], [7]]. Moreover, neuroimaging has revealed that HF is associated with structural brain abnormalities linked to CI, including hippocampal atrophy, grey matter loss, myelin degradation, and increased white matter lesions [[8], [9], [10]]. HF and CI are intimately connected, and patients with both disorders have a worse prognosis, greater care expenses, and place a major burden on individuals, families, and society.

However, despite the prevalence of HF-induced CI, the underlying pathological mechanisms remain poorly understood. Currently, two main hypotheses have been proposed: insufficient cerebral perfusion/hypoxia and neurohormone activation [11,12]. HF induces cerebral hypoperfusion due to decreased cardiac output, and its high metabolic properties render sensitive brain areas like the hippocampus more vulnerable to injury. Chronic hypoxia activates the hypoxia-inducible factor-1 signalling pathway, upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor-1 expression, and disrupts the structural integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) by degrading tight junction proteins (TJs). This compromise in barrier function permits the influx of neurotoxic substances, ultimately establishing the pathological foundation for CI [13]. In addition, activation of the sympathetic nervous system and renin-angiotensin system (RAS), as well as elevated levels of catecholamines, are associated with cognitive decline in patients with HF [14]. Recently, increasing evidence has emerged to support the notion that neuroinflammation occurs in many brain areas during HF and may play a role in the onset and progression of HF-induced CI [15,16]. Both preclinical models and population studies consistently demonstrate that systemic inflammatory markers are markedly elevated during the progression of HF, and that heightened levels of pro-inflammatory substances in the peripheral circulation are associated with cognitive decline. Inhibiting peripheral inflammation appears to be closely associated with treating HF-induced CI [17,18]. Moreover, microglia, the central nervous system's (CNS) innate immune effector cells, are activated to a pro-inflammatory phenotype in response to peripheral inflammatory signals, releasing CNS-specific inflammatory mediators. This cascade-activated neuroinflammatory response causes further synaptic plasticity impairment and exacerbates CI [[19], [20], [21]]. Nevertheless, the specific molecular pathways through which peripheral and central neuroinflammation contribute to HF-induced CI are still poorly defined.

Based on Mendelian randomization analysis, we discovered a causal relationship between HF exposure and cognitive results. Solute carrier family 22 member 3 (SLC22A3) might be a candidate gene for HF-induced CI. SLC22A3 is capable of transmitting a variety of molecules across cell membranes, primarily associated with the uptake of monoamine neurotransmitters such as histamine (HA) and epinephrine [22]. Bioinformatics analysis revealed a significant downregulation of SLC22A3 expression in cardiac tissues of HF patients, accompanied by a significant increase in peripheral HA concentration. To explore the specific mechanism in depth, we constructed a mouse model of HF. Overexpression of cardiac SLC22A3 could reduce HA concentrations while improving cognitive function in HF mice. HA exerts its pro-inflammatory effects primarily through the H1 receptor (H1R). H1R has a multidimensional regulatory impact on NLRP3 inflammasome. Stimulation with HA at varying concentrations dose-dependently upregulated the expression of NLRP3, whereas this effect was significantly suppressed by the H1R antagonist in BV2 cells. Knockdown of H1R or NLRP3 could decrease the neuroinflammatory response in the hippocampus of HF mice, eventually improving cognition. While cerebral hypoperfusion is a recognized contributor to CI in HF, our findings identify the HA/H1R/NLRP3 signalling axis as a pivotal mechanism. The convergent neuroprotection achieved by independently modulating different components of this axis indicates that its activation constitutes a specific pathological pathway, potentially acting in parallel to or independently of generalized cerebral blood flow reduction.

Materials and methods

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (male, 6–8 weeks, 25–28 g) and NLRP3-knockout (NLRP3−/−) mice with a C57BL/6 genetic background were obtained from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University and The Jackson Laboratory, respectively. The mouse experiments were conducted under an approved animal use protocol (License Number: SCXK-2025-0012). All animals were kept in specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facilities at the Experimental Animal Center of Southeast University Medical School, under controlled environmental conditions: temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity (55 ± 5%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle, with ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Southeast University (Approval No. 20230919041) and followed the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals established by the NIH.

Cell culture

The mouse microglial BV2 cell line was cultured in DMEM basal medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin antibiotics, and 1% Mycoplasma removal reagent, and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. HA was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (New Jersey, USA), and the H1R antagonist cetirizine dihydrochloride was purchased from Tocris Bioscience. BV2 cells were treated with different concentrations of HA (0.01, 0.1, and 1 μg/ml) for 24 h. H1R antagonist cetirizine dihydrochloride (10 μM) was added to BV2 cells alone or 30 min before addition of histamine (0.1 μg/ml) for 24 h.

HF surgery and echocardiography

Both C57BL/6 and NLRP3−/− mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane and subjected to oral endotracheal intubation. The model used in this study is HF after MI. HF models were established by ligating the proximal left anterior descending coronary artery, as previously described [5,23]. The success rate of left anterior descending coronary artery ligation was (85%), and the perioperative mortality rate was (5%). Cardiac function was assessed via transthoracic echocardiography using a high-resolution ultrasound system (Vevo2100, Visual Sonics; equipped with an MS550 linear array transducer) under the same aesthetic regimen. M-mode echocardiographic images were acquired in the left parasternal long-axis view to measure left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVIDs) and end-diastolic diameter (LVIDd). Ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) were calculated using the Teicholz formula.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

Spatial learning and memory in mice were evaluated using the MWM test [24]. The apparatus consisted of a circular pool (diameter: 120 cm, height: 40 cm) filled with water (depth: 30 cm, temperature: 21 ± 1 °C). A transparent circular platform (diameter: 10 cm) was submerged 1.0 cm below the water surface in a fixed quadrant. During the 5-day acquisition phase, mice underwent daily training sessions starting sequentially from four predefined entry points (one per quadrant) in a randomized order. Each trial allowed 60 s for free exploration, with escape latency (time to locate the hidden platform) recorded. Mice remaining on the platform for 30 s post-discovery were allowed to observe environmental cues. If a mouse failed to find the platform within 60 s, it was gently guided to the platform and retained for 30 s (escape latency recorded as 60 s). On day 6 (probe trial), the platform was removed, and mice freely explored the pool for 60 s. Swimming speed, path trajectory, time spent in the target quadrant, and the number of platform crossings were analysed using dedicated animal behaviour tracking software.

Tissue and blood collection

After the completion of behavioural tests, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under anesthesia (4% isoflurane). Whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture, centrifuged to obtain serum, which was then stored at −80 °C for subsequent Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) assays. Brain tissues were rapidly dissected, with hippocampal tissues preserved at −80 °C for Western blot and ELISA analyses. The remaining brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for histopathological observation and immunofluorescence detection.

Western blot

Hippocampal tissues and cells were collected and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer containing protease/phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, China) on ice for 30 min. Protein concentrations were measured using a BCA assay kit (EpiZyme, China). Aliquots (40 μg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes at 300 mA constant current. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 2 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: SLC22A3 (1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich), PSD93 (1:2000, Abcam), PSD95 (1:1000, Abcam), Claudin-5 (1:500, Proteintech), Occludin (1:1000, Proteintech), ZO-1 (1:500, Abcam), NLRP3 (1:2000, Proteintech), H1R (1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich), H2R (1:1000, Proteintech), H3R (1:1000, Absin), H4R(1:2000, Abclonal), β-actin (1:1000, Abcam), and GAPDH (1:1000, Abcam). After three 10-min TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit/mouse, 1:5000, Pierce) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate (Millipore, USA) and analysed with ImageJ software, with band intensities normalized to β-actin or GAPDH.

Measurement of BBB permeability

Evans Blue (EB) dye is widely utilized for assessing both vascular permeability and BBB permeability [25]. Mice received 2% EB dye (4 mL/kg) via tail vein injection 3 h before anesthesia. Following transcardial perfusion with 150 mL ice-cold PBS under anesthesia with 1.5% isoflurane, brains were rapidly excised. Hippocampi were dissected, fixed in 1 mL formamide at 55 °C overnight, and supernatants were collected. EB content was spectrophotometrically quantified at 632 nm. BBB permeability was calculated as the ratio of EB concentration to hippocampal tissue weight.

FITC-conjugated histamine (HAfluo) detection

HAfluo (Ruixitech, China) was employed as a fluorescent tracer. Experimental procedures were conducted as follows: HAfluo stock solution (1 mg/mL) was prepared in saline and stored protected from light at 4 °C. C57BL/6 mice were immobilized on a thermostatically controlled heating pad (37 ± 0.5 °C) for 5 min prewarming to induce vasodilation before experimentation. The HAfluo solution was subsequently administered via slow bolus injection through the tail vein using an insulin micro syringe at a dosage of 3 μL/g body weight. Animals were maintained under physiological conditions for 30 min to allow systemic circulation of the tracer. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under anesthesia (4% isoflurane), and brain tissues were immediately subjected to perfusion fixation and gradient dehydration. For fluorescence microscopic analysis, 5 μm thick coronal cryosections were prepared and mounted with antifade medium. Fluorescence signals were acquired through the FITC-specific channel using a laser scanning confocal microscope system, with concurrent nuclear counterstaining using DAPI for anatomical localization.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) delivery in vivo

This study employed recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV9/cTNT::SLC22A3-3 × FLAG.WPRE.T2A.EGFP) for cardiac-specific overexpression of SLC22A3 in mice. The construct utilizes the AAV9 serotype driven by the cardiac troponin T (cTnT) promoter, which has been extensively documented in the literature to confer high cardiac specificity and efficiently target cardiomyocytes [26], with an integrated green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter gene for monitoring transduction efficiency. Viral vectors were packaged by Genkai Gene Medical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Both the SLC22A3 overexpression group (SLC22A3OE) and empty vector control group (Ctrl) were standardized to a viral titer of 5 × 1011vg/ml. Myocardial tissue-specific transduction was achieved through systemic circulation following tail vein injection of 200 μl recombinant viral suspension.

This study employed AAV-delivered short hairpin RNA (shRNA) technology to achieve histamine H1 receptor (H1R) knockdown in the hippocampal region. Two engineered viral vectors were utilized: the experimental AAV-H1R carrying H1R-targeting shRNA (8.33 × 1012 vg/ml) and the control AAV-GFP expressing scrambled sequences with GFP reporter (11.4 × 1012 vg/mL), both constructed by Shanghai Genkai Gene Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. Stereotaxic procedures were performed as follows: continuous anesthetized (1.5% isoflurane) mice were immobilized in a stereotactic frame (David Kopf Instruments, USA). Hippocampal coordinates were determined relative to bregma (AP: 2.0 mm, ML: ±2.0 mm, DV: 2.0 mm), according to the murine brain atlas. Bilateral microinjections (1.0 μl per hemisphere, total 2.0 μl) were administered through a cranial burr hole using a nanoinjector system at 0.2 μl/min. The micropipette remained in situ for 10 min to prevent viral reflux.

After viral injection, transgene expression stabilizes in vivo after approximately 2–3 weeks. All subsequent experiments were conducted commencing from the 4th week post-injection, with in vivo gene transduction efficacy systematically validated through fluorescence microscopic imaging and Western blot analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded brain sections underwent heat-mediated dewaxing at 60 °C, followed by sequential xylene-based dilapidation and graded ethanol rehydration. Microwave-assisted antigen retrieval was performed in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), with subsequent PBS rinsing after natural cooling. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) before overnight incubation at 4 °C in a humidified chamber with anti-Iba-1 monoclonal antibody (1:300, Abcam). Following PBS washing, sections were incubated with fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, Abcam) under light-protected conditions at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclear counterstaining was achieved using DAPI, followed by antifade mounting. Fluorescence signals were visualized and captured through fluorescence microscopic observation and image acquisition using a fluorescence microscope with a dual-channel configuration.

ELISA

According to the manufacturer's instructions, the levels of histamine and epinephrine were detected using ELISA kits (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China). The concentrations of interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) were measured using ELISA kits (Elabscience, Wuhan, China).

Golgi staining

Golgi staining of mouse brain tissue was performed using the FD Rapid Golgi Stain Kit (FD Neuro Technologies, Columbia, MD). Immediately after saline perfusion, the brain was removed and immersed in the A and B mixed solution from the kit, followed by incubation in the dark for 14 days. The brain was then transferred to solution C and incubated in the dark for an additional 5 days. Sections of 50 μm thickness were obtained using a vibrating microtome, air-dried at room temperature in the dark, and stained with the D and E mixed solution. Neurons in the hippocampal were first identified under low magnification. Dendritic segments were selected for imaging, and the number of dendritic spines per 10 μm dendritic length was calculated for each neuron using ImageJ.

Masson staining

Mouse hearts were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 h, followed by dehydration through a graded ethanol series and infiltration with paraffin. The tissues were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices using a microtome. The sections were sequentially stained with celestine blue, Mayer's haematoxylin, ponceau fuchsin, and aniline blue. After mounting, the sections were observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope. The collagen volume fraction in the heart was measured using ImageJ.

Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis

MR was employed to assess causal relationships using single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs). Genetic data for heart failure (HF) were sourced from the IEU Open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/, ID: ebi-a-GCST009541), comprising 47,309 HF cases and 930,014 controls of European ancestry. To better reflect the CI outcome, eight cognitive indices were examined among European participants: cognitive performance (ebi-a-GCST006572), cognitive functions (ieu-b-4838), mean time to identify matches (ukb-b-16287), fluid intelligence questions attempted (ukb-b-2988), symbol digit matches (ukb-b-15625), prospective memory (ukb-b-4282), numeric memory test rounds (ukb-b-16872), and time to answer (ukb-b-14360). As a reanalysis of published data, no additional ethical approval was required. MR studies prioritize careful selection of IVs based on significant SNPs (P < 5 × 10−8 for large GWAS studies, P < 1 × 10−5 for smaller sample sizes), independent SNPs (clumped using the 1000 Genomes reference panel), SNPs with an effect allele frequency >0.01 and an F-statistic ≥10, and potential confounders. These criteria ensure robustness and avoid weak instrument bias, and are screened using Phen Scanner V2 (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) to avoid potential confounders. MR analyses are performed using R software and the inverse-variance-weighted (IVW) method [27]. To address limitations, four additional methods are employed: weighted median estimation [28], weighted mode, MR-Egger regression [29], and simple mode. For robust causal inference, consistent directional results across all five methods are required.

Bioinformatic analysis

We conducted a phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) on rs1510226 using PheWeb [30]. The dataset that was obtained for a bioinformatic study from the GEO database was GSE57338. Visit the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) for further details. Gene symbols were placed onto the probes using platform annotations. To eliminate duplicates from genes with multiple probes, one probe was randomly selected, resulting in the gene expression matrix. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were found using the “limma” R package [31] with a significance level of P < 0.05 and |log2 FC| > 0.585. We recommend the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for DEG identification with high sample sizes due to its good FDR control and power [32]. The gene was then analysed for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment using the “cluster Profiler” R package [33] to determine the primary pathways and functions associated with the target gene. Differential expression analysis of metabolites was performed using data from the Metabolomics Workbench (https://www.metabolomicsworkbench.org/, ID: ST002717), identifying significant metabolites with a P-value ≤0.05 and fold-change ≥1.5.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with independent experiments repeated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Statistical methods were selected based on data types and distribution characteristics: For comparisons between two groups, after performing the Shapiro-Wilk test, normally distributed data with homogeneity of variance were analysed using unpaired Student's t-test, while non-normally distributed data were assessed by the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis’ test. Multiple group comparisons were analysed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. Time-dependent data were analysed using repeated measures two-way ANOVA. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Significantly impaired cardiac and cognitive function in HF after MI mice

Given the pathological damage of HF to the CNS in previous studies and the potential clinical value of its standardised treatment in delaying CI [34], we evaluated eight SNPs as IVs by Mendelian randomization and discovered an association between cognitive outcomes and exposure to HF (Fig. 1A, Table S1). Next, we built the HF mouse model (after MI) and tested its cognitive function. The schematic diagram of the experimental design is shown in Fig. 1B. Representative images of cardiac ultrasound in two groups of mice are shown in Fig. 1C. Cardiac function was significantly impaired in HF mice compared to controls, as evidenced by a significant decrease in EF, downregulation of FS, and significant increases in LVIDs and LVIDd (Fig. 1D–G). The differences in cognitive levels between HF mice and Sham mice were evaluated through the MWM test. In the initial 5-day training stage, HF mice had a longer escape latency than Sham mice (Fig. 1H). In the probe test with the platform removed on day 6, there was no significant difference in swimming speed between the two groups of mice (Fig. 1I). HF mice took considerably less time to find the platform in the target quadrant (Fig. 1J) and crossed it fewer times (Fig. 1K). Fig. 1L depicts the matching heat map for the swimming path. The heat map shows that the Sham mice swim around the platform, while the HF mice swim in the region away from the platform. The expression of synapse-related proteins PSD93 and PSD95 in the hippocampus of HF mice was significantly reduced (Fig. 1M). Golgi staining further demonstrated a marked decrease in dendritic spine density in the hippocampal region (Fig. S3A and B). Peripheral accumulation of HA into the brain via the damaged BBB

To further explore the specific molecular mechanism of HF-induced CI, we found the nearest genes to each of the eight SNPs as IVs on the genome, seven of which could encode proteins. Fig. 2A and Fig. S1A depict the differential gene volcano map and expression heat map of HF patients against the normal population. Five of these genes with substantial expression changes between HF and normal populations were identified using differential gene analysis (Fig. 2B, Fig. S1B–G). GWAS and PheWAS studies indicated that the SNP locus rs1510226 was strongly linked with the development of HF, and the gene closest to it was SLC22A3 (Fig. S1H–I). HF patients had considerably reduced expression levels of SLC22A3 in their cardiac tissues compared to the normal control group (Fig. 2B). Animal studies revealed a substantial reduction in SLC22A3 expression in cardiac tissues of HF mice (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, GO analysis of SLC22A3 indicated that the processes of HA absorption and metabolism were highly enriched and significant (Fig. 2D). Metabolite expression analysis revealed that HF patients had considerably greater levels of HA compared to normal controls (Fig. 2E, Fig. S1J). HA concentrations were significantly elevated in both the blood (Fig. 2F) and hippocampus (Fig. 2G) of HF mice by ELISA kits. While epinephrine levels were higher in the blood (Fig. S2A), no significant change was detected in the hippocampus (Fig. S2B). Previous studies have found that BBB integrity is impaired in patients with HF [35]. To assess BBB permeability, we measured EB content in the hippocampus using absorbance photometry. Fig. 2H exhibits the typical EB-stained brains from each group. Compared to the Sham group, the HF group showed considerably higher EB leakage in the brain. The expression levels of BBB-related proteins in HF mice were measured by Western blot (Fig. 2I), and the results showed that the expression levels of Claudin-5, Occludin, and ZO-1 were significantly reduced. To investigate if peripheral HA could reach the brain, HAfluo was administered as a tracer to the periphery. The findings revealed that HAfluo leaked considerably into the hippocampus of HF mice (Fig. 2J). The above findings suggest that SLC22A3 expression is significantly downregulated in cardiac tissue after HF and is accompanied by a marked increase in peripheral HA concentration. Accumulated HA may enter the brain via the damaged BBB to affect cognition. Cardiac overexpression of SLC22A3 significantly improves cognition and reduces HA concentration in HF after MI mice

We verified the ameliorative effect of SLC22A3 on HA metabolic regulation and CI in a mouse model of HF by cardiac-specific overexpression of SLC22A3. Myocardium-targeted AAV vector was injected via the tail vein to achieve cardiac-specific overexpression of the SLC22A3 gene in the HF mouse model, and the schematic design of the experiment is shown in Fig. 3A. Fig. 3B shows the effect of gene transfection by fluorescence. Fig. 3C displays representative images of cardiac ultrasound in the two groups. Overexpression of SLC22A3 significantly improved cardiac function and significantly up-regulated EF in HF mice (Fig. 3D). During the MWM test, HF-SLC22A3OE mice demonstrated shorter escape latencies, spent significantly more time in the target quadrant, crossed the former platform location more frequently, and exhibited swimming paths more focused on the target area compared to HF-Ctrl mice, indicating enhanced spatial learning and memory retention (Fig. 3E–I). SLC22A3 expression levels were measured by Western blot (Fig. 3J), and SLC22A3 expression was significantly increased in cardiac tissues of HF mice overexpressing SLC22A3. There was no significant difference in SLC22A3 expression in the brain and liver between the HF-Ctrl and HF-SLC22A3OE mice (Fig. S4A and B). To investigate the synaptic functions of the two mouse groups, the levels of synaptic-associated proteins were assessed using Western blot (Fig. 3K) and Golgi staining (Fig. S3C and D). In the hippocampus of HF mice overexpressing SLC22A3, PSD93 and PSD95 expression levels were substantially higher than those of the HF group, a significant increase in dendritic spine density in the hippocampal region. We also quantified the HA concentrations in blood and hippocampus of the two groups of mice by ELISA kits, and found that overexpression of SLC22A3 resulted in a significant reduction of HA concentrations in both blood (Fig. 3L) and hippocampus (Fig. 3M) of HF mice. The expression levels of BBB-related proteins were measured. The findings indicated that following SLC22A3 overexpression, HF mice's hippocampal expression levels of Claudin-5, Occludin and ZO-1 rose dramatically (Fig. 3N). In conclusion, the concentration of HA in the blood and hippocampus drops when SLC22A3 is overexpressed in the heart, and this can greatly enhance the cognitive function of HF mice. Discussion

The primary findings of this study are as follows (Fig. 7): 1) SLC22A3 may be a candidate gene for HF-induced CI; 2) SLC22A3 modulation of HA can enhance HF-induced CI; and 3) The neuroinflammatory response caused by the HA/H1R/NLRP3 pathway may be a key molecular mechanism in HF-induced CI. These results indicate that SLC22A3 and HA may play an important role in HF-induced CI, emphasising the potential therapeutic focus of their treatment.Fig. 7Summary of the entire study.Fig. 7

Summary of the entire study.

SLC22A3 is of the organic cation membrane transporter family. It encodes a protein that is produced in organs such as the heart and brain and can engage in the transmembrane transport of different substances, facilitating absorption and clearance [39]. Previous studies have shown that SLC22A3 is also linked to cardiovascular disorders. The SLC22A3 gene cluster has been discovered as a significant risk locus for coronary heart disease [40], and its polymorphism influences atherosclerosis via controlling lipid levels and inflammatory responses [41]. While several studies on SLC22A3 and cardiovascular outcomes have been compiled, there have been scant data on the involvement of SLC22A3 in HF-induced CI. Our study discovered a causal association between HF exposure and cognitive outcomes, and the SNP locus rs1510226 was strongly associated with the development of HF, with the closest gene being SLC22A3. Cardiac SLC22A3 expression is significantly lower in HF patients and mice. SLC22A3 overexpression in cardiac tissue could improve cognitive performance in HF mice. In keeping with the findings of previous studies, our results suggests that SLC22A3 may indeed play an integral role in HF-induced CI.

SLC22A3 primarily regulates the absorption of monoamine neurotransmitters such as epinephrine and HA, as well as brain homeostasis [42]. The GO analysis of SLC22A3 in our study also demonstrated that the processes of epinephrine uptake, HA uptake, and metabolism were highly enriched and significant. A significant increase in epinephrine concentrations in the blood of HF mice was not significantly altered in the brain. This may be related to the following factors: HA has a smaller molecular size and higher lipophilicity, resulting in more efficient passive diffusion. In contrast, epinephrine is more susceptible to efflux by transporters such as P-glycoprotein, which actively pumps it back into the bloodstream and prevents its CNS accumulation [43]. Furthermore, the central histaminergic system can be readily activated by peripheral HA, whereas CNS epinephrine is primarily synthesized locally by central neurons. A rise in peripheral epinephrine is often induced by stress, which in turn triggers negative feedback to inhibit its central synthesis, thereby preventing excessive cerebral stimulation. These differences collectively influence their central bioavailability. The concentration of HA in the blood and hippocampus of HF mice rose dramatically. Cardiac SLC22A3 overexpression resulted in considerably lower HA concentrations in the blood and hippocampus. It appears that HA is a more deserving media.

HA is an endogenous biogenic amine generated by mast cells, and the process of high-concentration storage and release in the heart contributes significantly to the incidence and progression of cardiovascular illnesses [44]. Previous studies have indicated that the level of HA in the plasma of HF patients is higher, and the concentration of HA in the plasma is thought to be significant in predicting the development of HF [45]. Furthermore, HA is a kind of monoamine neurotransmitter that is widely distributed throughout the brain by axons originating from the papillary tuberoid nucleus and accomplishes neurotransmitter release in a sleep-wake cycle-dependent way [46]. In addition to influencing neuronal discharge patterns, brain HA can also modulate glial cell action. HA has been shown to have a variety of impacts on microglial function both in health and illness [47]. Current research on the effects of HA on cognition is controversial. Some studies have revealed that increased HA in the brain induces neuroinflammation and leads to cognitive deficits, while others have demonstrated that intracerebral injections of HA improve spatial memory [48,49]. A recent study has revealed that SLC22A3 may transport HA in bile into cells for breakdown, limiting the growth of cancer cells triggered by HA [50]. Its insufficiency causes extracellular HA accumulation. Meanwhile, SLC22A3 regulates HA production, which controls monocyte inflammatory response [51]. Hence, we hypothesise that the primary mechanism of HF-induced CI is HA accumulation, which is mediated by the absence of SLC22A3. Under pathological conditions, the expression and function of SLC22A3 can be dynamically regulated, directly modulating the local concentration and clearance rate of HA, thereby influencing its spatiotemporal distribution [52]. HA entering the CNS from the periphery may activate HA receptors and other pathways, subsequently triggering downstream neuroinflammatory cascades and affecting synaptic plasticity, ultimately leading to CI.

Abnormal cerebral blood flow after HF is associated with hypoxia in brain tissue, and chronic hypoxia may lead to structural and functional disruption of the BBB by affecting its TJs [53]. Furthermore, investigations have revealed that patients with HF frequently have an inflammatory state, which may compromise the integrity of the BBB via inflammatory substances in the bloodstream [54]. The BBB is a dynamic structure consisting of brain microvascular endothelial cells, astrocyte peduncles, pericytes, basement membranes, and TJs, whose structural and functional integrity maintains brain homeostasis [55]. The BBB is an essential barrier that separates the periphery and the brain, and it may play a role in HF-induced CI. The results of our investigation demonstrated that the expression of BBB-related proteins in HF mice was dramatically reduced, whereas the quantity of EB in the hippocampus tissue was significantly elevated. Under physiological settings, peripheral HA has a tough time penetrating the BBB; however, we found that fluorescently labeled HA on the periphery of HF mice can permeate brain tissue. Our results suggested that the accumulated HA in the periphery can enter the brain through the compromised BBB. It is noteworthy that previous studies have indicated that HA can activate vascular endothelial H1R and increase BBB permeability [56]. This active effect may involve processes such as the release of inflammatory factors and downregulation of TJs expression. Therefore, in the context of HF, there may also exist a bidirectional aggravation vicious cycle between HA accumulation and BBB disruption: BBB damage allows HA to enter the brain, and the entry of HA may further exacerbate BBB dysfunction. In future studies, we will further validate the direct causal impact of HA on BBB integrity to elucidate the specific role of this cyclic mechanism in HF-induced CI.

HA mediates a variety of physiological functions, including allergy, inflammatory response, immunomodulation, gastric acid secretion, and neural signalling, through four different G protein-coupled receptor subtypes (H1R, H2R, H3R, and H4R) [57]. After being released into the surrounding tissues, HA can connect to certain receptors and initiate a series of cascade events [58]. According to studies, HA mostly promotes inflammation via H1R [59,60]. In primary cultured microglia, HA can selectively upregulate H1R expression in a dose-dependent manner [61]. HA may activate microglia in a dose-dependent manner, resulting in increased production of pro-inflammatory molecules TNF-α and IL-6 [62]. Inhibiting HA release can lower pro-inflammatory factors in both the peripheral circulation and the hippocampus following an ischaemic stroke [63]. In the present study, we found that the expression of H1R elevated significantly in HF mice, and knocking down H1R can reduce neuroinflammatory responses in HF mice and improve cognition. Moreover, the decrease in H3R expression levels is interesting, which may reflect compensatory regulation within the histaminergic system. H3R primarily functions as a presynaptic auto receptor that negatively feedback inhibits the synthesis and release of HA. Its downregulation under pathological conditions of sustained H1R activation may weaken this self-inhibition, thereby potentiating histaminergic signalling [64]. The specific mechanism of H3R deserves further exploration.

HA may play a pro-inflammatory function in HF-induced CI via activating H1R in the brain. This mechanism highlights brain H1R as a potential therapeutic target. In this context, the CNS accessibility of H1R antagonists is a key consideration. It is well established that CNS penetration represents a critical pharmacological characteristic of H1R antihistamines [65]. First generation agents such as diphenhydramine readily cross the BBB and exert sedative effects, whereas second generation drugs like cetirizine are structurally optimized to minimize CNS exposure, thereby reducing central side effects. In contrast, the present study focuses not on the central actions of exogenous H1R antagonists, but rather on elucidating an endogenous, heart-derived signalling pathway that mediates cross-talk between the heart and the brain via systemic circulation. Our data demonstrate that under HF conditions, cardiac-released HA circulates as a humoral signal and, through a compromised BBB, modulates hippocampal function. These findings propose a novel conceptual framework: future strategies aimed at modulating peripheral HA bioavailability, rather than direct CNS drug delivery, may provide a viable approach to indirectly regulate brain function, potentially circumventing adverse effects associated with direct central targeting.

The NLRP3 inflammasome is currently the most extensively studied innate immune complex, with a three-part structure: the sensor protein NLRP3, the bridging protein ASC, and the effector protein pro-caspase-1, which activates a downstream inflammatory cascade [66]. According to several studies, the NLRP3 inflammasome plays an important regulatory role in the onset and progression of cognitive impairment [67,68]. In a human chondrocyte model driven by advanced glycation end products, the H1R antagonist loratadine effectively reduced the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [69]. Pathological models demonstrated that the HA/H1R axis dramatically increased lipopolysaccharide-induced inflammatory infiltration and bone resorption in mouse periodontal tissues via activating the NLRP3/Caspase-1 signal cascade response [70]. H1R may exert a multifaceted regulatory influence on the NLRP3 inflammasome. Our study discovered that the level of the NLRP3 inflammasome in HF mice increased dramatically, and that knocking down H1R might reduce the level of the NLRP3 inflammasome in HF mice. The HA in the brain may activate the NLRP3 inflammasome by binding to microglial H1R, initiating the neuroinflammatory response. To preliminarily investigate the role of NLRP3 in HF-induced CI, we employed an NLRP3 global knockout mouse model. Following global knockout, hippocampal inflammation and cognitive function were both improved, suggesting that NLRP3 activation in the hippocampus may be involved in this process. These results provide preliminary systemic evidence for the therapeutic potential of targeting NLRP3. Further precise validation using cell-specific knockout models is required in subsequent studies.

There are several limitations in our study. Firstly, this study mainly focused on the role played by the SLC22A3 gene in HF-induced CI. We acknowledge that other differentially expressed genes such as Bcl-2-associated athanogene 3 (BAG3) and Cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (CDKN1A) identified may likewise be instrumental in the core pathological processes of heart failure. They are an important apoptosis regulator and a cell cycle inhibitor protein, respectively. Although their expression changes significantly in heart failure, suggesting their important roles in cardiomyocyte survival and cardiac remodelling, their primary functions are largely confined to the pathological processes within the cardiac tissue itself. The potential contributions of BAG3 and CDKN1A to HF-induced CI merit further exploration in separate studies. Second, while the findings of this work suggest that exogenous HA can seep into the brains of HF mice, they do not necessarily imply that the same is true for endogenous applications. Future studies will focus on determining the precise method by which brain HA operates in the HF model. Third, our investigation, focus on the neuroinflammatory pathway, logically prioritized microglia as the principal cellular mediator of H1R-dependent hippocampal injury. However, the possible contributions of H1R in neurons or other cell populations are not precluded. Definitive identification of the critical cellular substrates for H1R action will require future approaches utilizing cell-type-specific conditional knockout models or single-cell sequencing analyses. Finally, the findings are primarily based on animal models, with limited validation in human samples or clinical datasets. Further studies should consider integrating available patient samples, systematically assess the value of dynamically monitoring plasma HA levels in HF patients, combined with neuropsychological evaluations, to validate its potential as a predictive marker for cognitive decline risk.

In conclusion, the findings of our study illustrate that down-regulation of SLC22A3 expression in the heart drives an accumulation of peripheral HA. The accumulating HA enters the brain through the weakened BBB, activates microglial H1R, and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, activating neuroinflammatory responses and eventually resulting in HF-induced CI. For the early intervention and treatment of HF-induced CI, targeting the SLC22A3 and HA signals may offer a viable therapeutic approach.
