From childhood shadows to adult aches: a prospective cohort study on adverse childhood experiences, depressive symptoms, and incident arthritis in middle-aged and older Chinese adults
Authors: Bo-wen Lu, Jia-cheng Li, Ming-tao Wen, Yu-qi Guo, Gang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study investigates the impact of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and depressive symptoms on incident arthritis in middle-aged and older Chinese adults, examining the mediating role of depressive symptoms in the ACEs–arthritis relationship to uncover psychological mechanisms underlying arthritis development.MethodsWe used 2011–2020 data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and included 4,184 participants aged ≥ 45 years who were free of arthritis at baseline (2011). ACEs were evaluated with 11 types of childhood adversity, classified into quartiles, while depressive symptoms were measured using the CES-D scale. Multivariable logistic regression and stratified analyses assessed associations between ACEs, depressive symptoms, and incident arthritis. Restricted cubic splines and mediation analysis were applied to explore dose–response relationships and the mediating role of depression.ResultsAmong 4184 participants, 969 developed incident arthritis. Individuals in the highest ACEs quartile (Q4) had a significantly higher arthritis risk (OR 1.78, 95% CI 1.43–2.21, P < 0.001). This association was stronger in females and individuals aged 45–64. Depressive symptoms also increased arthritis risk, with a nonlinear dose–response relationship. Mediation analysis showed depressive symptoms partially mediated the ACEs–arthritis relationship, accounting for 12.4% of the effect.ConclusionsIn this national cohort, higher ACEs burden and greater depressive symptom severity were significantly associated with incident arthritis, and depressive symptoms partially mediated by the ACEs–arthritis association. Targeting childhood adversity and later-life mental health may help reduce arthritis risk in aging populations.Keywords: Arthritis, Adverse childhood experiences, Depressive symptoms, CHARLS database, Mediation analysis

Introduction

Arthritis (AR) refers to the inflammation or swelling of one or more joints, arising from a variety of etiologies, including inflammation, infection, degeneration, trauma, or other factors. This condition encompasses several subtypes, notably osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and psoriatic arthritis [1]. Arthritis represents a major public health challenge. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 15 million new cases of AR are diagnosed annually worldwide, with the incidence increasing by 9.3% compared to the 1990 s [2], imposing a substantial burden on both individual quality of life and the broader socio-economic landscape. Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) refer to traumatic and recurrent events that children encounter during early life, including physical and emotional abuse, family conflict, and peer violence [3]. A large body of evidence indicates their long-term adverse effects on adult health [4, 5]. However, whether ACEs are associated with the onset of arthritis—and whether, and to what extent, psychological factors mediate this association—remains unclear.

Notably, ACEs not only cause short-term health harm during childhood but also exert lasting adverse effects on mental health in adulthood. ACEs are considered key contributors to various psychological issues, including depression, anxiety, and obsessive symptoms, as well as related somatic diseases [6, 7], and are strongly linked to maladaptive psychological behaviors. Individuals exposed to high levels of ACEs often experience internal conflict and emotional turmoil, making them more susceptible to mood disorders, such as depression and anxiety [8]. They are also more prone to impulsive emotional behaviors [9]. Depression, characterized by a low clinical cure rate and high relapse rate, has become a major global mental health concern. Importantly, depressive symptoms are considered a key risk factor for arthritis, and the two conditions exhibit a bidirectional causal relationship [10]. Prior studies have also found that long-term trajectories of depressive symptoms may be a strong predictor of arthritis [11]. Meanwhile, a cross-sectional study suggests that ACEs are positively associated with multiple chronic diseases, including arthritis, with similar patterns observed across different demographic and socioeconomic strata [12]. However, prospective evidence in Chinese populations remains limited.

Against this backdrop, using a prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, this study aims to: (1) evaluate the association between cumulative ACEs and incident arthritis and (2) quantify the mediating effect of depressive symptoms in this association. By jointly considering the exposure (ACEs), the outcome (incident arthritis), and the potential psychological mediator (depressive symptoms), this study will not only deepen understanding of the long-term health impacts of ACEs but also provide actionable psychosocial evidence to inform arthritis prevention and mental health interventions.

Methods

Study population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) collects high-quality microdata from individuals aged 45 and above in China to support interdisciplinary research on the country's aging population. In 2011, CHARLS conducted a nationwide baseline survey using a multi-stage probability proportional sampling method, covering approximately 10,000 households across 28 provinces and 150 counties, with over 17,000 respondents. Since then, CHARLS has conducted face-to-face interviews every 2–3 years at participants' residences. We designed a prospective cohort study based on 2011 data, selecting individuals who were free of arthritis at baseline, and followed them for the incidence of arthritis in 2020. For each outcome, we selected participants with complete records and gathered data from eligible individuals. After screening, a total of 4184 participants were included in the analysis, comprising 969 new arthritis cases and 3215 non-arthritis cases. Figure 1 illustrates the study design process. The CHARLS data set is available for download from the official CHARLS website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en). The collection of CHARLS data has been approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB 00001052-11015), and all participants provided informed consent.

Assessment of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs)

We assessed 11 distinct types of Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs), which include intrafamilial abuse, family dynamics, loss or threat of loss of family members, socioeconomic status, and community environmental quality (see Table S1). In addition, these 11 ACEs were conceptualized as a cumulative score, calculated based on the total number of ACEs an individual experienced. Each ACE was classified dichotomously as either “Yes” or “No,” and the responses were summed to generate an individual’s cumulative ACE score, ranging from 0 to 11. This methodology was based on the studies by Bellis et al. [13–15], which facilitates a more comprehensive evaluation of the potential health impacts of Aces. To further analyze the impact of cumulative ACEs, we categorized ACE scores into quartiles and divided participants into four groups: Q1 (0–3 points, 36.88%), Q2 (3–4 points, 26.34%), Q3 (4–5 points, 20.15%), and Q4 (5–10 points, 16.63%).

Assessment of depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). This scale consists of 10 items designed to evaluate the subject’s depressive mood and behaviors over the past week. Each item is rated on a four-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 to 3 (0 = " < 1 day," 1 = "1–2 days," 2 = "3–4 days," 3 = "5–7 days"). The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. In this study, depressive severity was categorized into four groups: 0–9 points for normal, 10–14 points for mild depression, 15–19 points for moderate depression, and 20 or more points for severe depression. The validity and reliability of the CES-D have been confirmed in Chinese populations [16].

Assessment of arthritis

The presence or absence of arthritis was determined based on the questionnaire item: “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor with arthritis or rheumatism?” A response of “Yes” was assigned a value of 1, while “No” was assigned a value of 0.

Assessment of covariates

CHARLS covers a broad range of topics, including basic demographic information, family composition, health status, healthcare and insurance, employment, income, expenditure, and assets. Notably, between 2011 and 2015, researchers collected fasting venous blood samples (after at least 12 h of fasting) from participants. These biomedical procedures were strictly performed by certified professionals according to standardized protocols. Blood samples were stored at 4 °C and immediately transported to the Beijing Center Laboratory (Capital Medical University Youngman Clinical Laboratory) for advanced diagnostic assessments. Various indicators, including glucose concentration (GLU), uric acid (UA), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and C-reactive protein (CRP), were measured using enzyme colorimetric methods [17].

In this study, baseline data on various covariates were collected. These covariates include sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviors, and current health status. Sociodemographic characteristics encompass age, sex, education level, marital status, and place of residence. Lifestyle behaviors include smoking and alcohol consumption. Current health issues are categorized as (Yes/No) for hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, heart disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and falls (see Table 1 for details). Laboratory test results include glucose concentration, uric acid (UA), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP), and body mass index (BMI).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are represented by frequency and percentage. In this study, the levels of Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) and depression severity were considered as exposure factors to explore their relationship with incident arthritis. In the prospective cohort analysis, multivariable logistic regression was used to examine the relationship between ACEs levels, depression severity, and the risk of incident arthritis. To calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the risk of incident arthritis, four logistic models were used: base model: unadjusted crude model. Model 1: Adjusted for sociodemographic variables and lifestyle behaviors, including age, sex, education level, location, marital status, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Model 2: Adjusted for current health conditions, including (Yes/No) for hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, chronic lung disease, liver disease, heart disease, gastrointestinal disorders, and falls. Model 3: Further adjusted for laboratory indicators, including HbA1c, UA, GLU, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, CRP, and BMI.

In addition, we used the restricted cubic spline (RCS) method in all four models to visually display the dose–response relationship between ACEs score, CES-D score, and the risk of incident arthritis. Subgroup analyses and interaction assessments were also performed for a more thorough examination of these relationships. Finally, we conducted mediation analysis to explore the mediating role of depression symptoms in the relationship between ACEs and incident arthritis. The mediation effect was tested using the Baron and Kenny method, bias-corrected bootstrap, and Sobel test to ensure the robustness of the results. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.2, with a significance level set at α = 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants. Among the 4184 individuals included in the study, 969 were diagnosed with incident arthritis. Among the participants, 48.26% were male, and 51.74% were female. Comparative analysis showed that, compared to non-arthritis patients, those with incident arthritis had the following key characteristics: more females, lower education level, rural residence, alcohol consumption, more severe depression, higher ACEs scores, a history of liver disease, kidney disease, digestive system disease, and falls (p < 0.05). In terms of laboratory test results, patients with incident arthritis had higher blood glucose levels.

We also described the baseline characteristics of study participants based on ACEs levels and depression severity, as shown in Table 2. Regarding ACEs levels, significant differences were found across various baseline characteristics, including age, sex, education level, residence, smoking, chronic lung disease, heart disease, history of falls, depression severity, and uric acid levels (p < 0.05). For example, the proportion of males increased with the level of ACEs, while the proportion of urban residents gradually decreased. No significant differences were observed for other variables across ACEs groups.

Dose–response relationship between ACEs, CES-D score, and incident arthritis

Figure 2 shows the results of the restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, with the x-axis representing the continuous variation of different indicators and the y-axis representing the corresponding predicted values (OR). The dashed line indicates the median of the study data. In the fully adjusted model, a significant linear dose–response relationship was observed between ACEs and the risk of incident arthritis (P overall < 0.001, P non-linear = 0.5718). As ACEs increased, the risk of arthritis gradually increased. A non-linear dose–response relationship was found between CES-D scores and the risk of incident arthritis (P overall < 0.001, P non-linear = 0.0174). Further segmented regression analysis (Table 6) showed that when the CES-D score was below 6, there was no significant association between the score and the risk of incident arthritis (OR 1.027, 95% CI 0.956–1.103, p = 0.474). However, when the score reached or exceeded 6, the risk of arthritis significantly increased with the score (OR 1.025, 95% CI 1.006–1.045, p = 0.012). The likelihood ratio test (p = 0.025) indicated that the segmented model had a better fit than the traditional logistic regression model, suggesting that depression symptoms may have a more significant impact on arthritis risk once they reach a certain threshold.

Mediating role of depressive symptoms in the relationship between ACEs and incident arthritis

After adjusting for all covariates, the results of the mediation analysis showed that the total effect of ACEs on incident arthritis was significant (TE 0.02, 95% CI 0.015–0.023, P < 0.001) (Table 8, Fig. 5). After adjusting for depressive symptoms, the direct effect of ACEs on arthritis remained significant (DE 0.017, 95% CI 0.012–0.020, P < 0.001), indicating an independent association between the two. The indirect effect mediated by depressive symptoms was 0.002 (95% CI 0.001–0.020, P < 0.001), accounting for 12.4% of the total effect (95% CI 7.3–20.4%, P < 0.001). These results (see Fig. 5) suggest that ACEs directly increase the risk of incidental arthritis, and depressive symptoms partially mediates this relationship, emphasizing the importance of psychological factors in the development of arthritis.

Discussion

Based on the CHARLS cohort, we included 4184 middle-aged and older adults free of arthritis at baseline in 2011 and followed them through 2020; the 9 year cumulative incidence of incident arthritis was 23.16%. A higher ACEs burden and greater depressive symptom severity were each significantly associated with increased risk of incident arthritis, and depressive symptoms partially mediated the ACEs–arthritis association. Findings were consistent across multiple models and subgroup analyses. In addition, we observed a trend, whereby lower educational attainment and rural residence were associated with higher arthritis risk, potentially reflecting differences in environmental exposures, lifestyle, healthcare access, and socioeconomic conditions [18, 19].

ACEs and arthritis

The findings of this study regarding the relationship between ACEs and arthritis are consistent with previous research [20–22]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Some studies suggest that prolonged exposure to stressful environments, such as ACEs, during childhood may trigger adaptive responses in the body’s endocrine, immune, and nervous systems [23]. Excessive stress during childhood can keep the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis chronically hyperactive, with increased cortisol secretion and impaired negative-feedback regulation [24]. Other studies have shown that ACEs are independently associated with elevated systemic inflammation in midlife and older age, an association partially mediated by heightened sympathetic activity, abdominal (central) obesity, smoking, and insufficient physical activity [25]. These mechanisms may increase susceptibility to osteoarthritis in adulthood, particularly in middle and later life. In addition, studies have shown that ACEs are associated with DNA methylation in key genes and telomere shortening, which may contribute to greater age-related disease risks [26–28]. Allostatic load, which is the long-term result of the failure to adapt, is a comprehensive indicator used to assess the physiological dysregulation of multiple systems in the body under chronic stress [29]. Thus, allostatic load may be a key factor in explaining new-onset arthritis. In China, the incidence of arthritis among middle-aged and elderly individuals increases with the intensity of ACE exposure, which may be due to the positive correlation between the number of ACEs and the level of allostatic load [30].

ACEs, depressive symptoms and arthritis

ACEs may influence the onset and progression of arthritis through psychosocial mechanisms [15, 31]. In this study, after controlling for other covariates, depression still mediated the relationship between ACEs and arthritis in middle-aged and elderly individuals. Specifically, 12.4% of the effect of ACEs on new-onset arthritis was mediated by depression. This finding is consistent with previous research, where a meta-analysis suggested that ACEs are associated with the risk of various physical and mental health problems, with a particularly significant impact on subsequent psychological issues [15]. Other studies have shown that ACEs are linked to depressive symptoms in later life [32, 33]. Regarding the mechanisms through which ACEs lead to multimorbidity, there are two main perspectives. Some researchers suggest that individuals who experience ACEs are more likely to develop depression, which in turn increases the risk of physical diseases (pain, falls, chronic diseases, multimorbidity, or functional limitations) in later life [14, 34, 35]. Other researchers propose that individuals with ACEs are more prone to physical diseases, which then lead to depressive symptoms [36–38]. In a causal mediation analysis, Ziyang Ren et al. explored the relationship between ACEs, depression, and physical health, and their causal mediation effects. The study concluded that although there is a bidirectional relationship between depression and physical health, in the Chinese middle-aged and elderly population, ACEs tend to induce depression, which then leads to poor physical health [39]. In addition, socioeconomic factors may also play a role in the relationship between ACEs and arthritis [40]. Individuals who experience family poverty or low educational levels during childhood often face fewer medical resources and poorer health management capabilities in adulthood, which may lead to neglecting early symptoms of arthritis, accelerating disease progression.

Because harmful environmental exposures during childhood can alter human physiological functions [41] and exert irreversible effects on physical health, they warrant greater societal attention and discussion. Preventing the occurrence of ACEs and curbing their development is a practical necessity for safeguarding children’s growth and maintaining population health. An urgent priority is to design effective interventions. First, at the community level, strengthen family economic supports, promote parenting-skills and parent–child relationship programs, and implement school-based social and emotional learning [42], to protect children from ACEs. Second, screen and assess ACEs risk across the entire life course, identify vulnerable groups early, and initiate psychosocial interventions to prevent the escalation of mental and physical problems. Based on the present study, resources should be prioritized for high-risk groups, such as middle-aged adults, women, rural residents, and those with lower educational attainment. For these populations, expand access to psychological interventions—for example, trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (TF-CBT), mindfulness-based interventions, and family therapy—to enhance emotion regulation and social functioning [43–45]. Such interventions may alleviate depressive symptoms and thereby reduce the risks of both depression and arthritis. Ultimately, the goal is to formulate universal public health policies and strategies that encourage proactive health management, thereby effectively reducing the adverse health consequences that individuals exposed to ACEs may face in mid- and later life while preventing and lowering the incidence of arthritis.

Strengths and limitations

This study is based on the CHARLS database, which offers a broad and reliable data source encompassing health and socio-demographic information from individuals aged 45 and above in China. The study utilized a multi-stage probability proportional sampling method, ensuring the representativeness of the sample and the broad applicability of the research findings. The prospective cohort design allows for the exploration of causal relationships, not just associations, and the data collection was strictly conducted following standard operating procedures, ensuring the high quality of biological samples and biomedical testing data. Furthermore, multiple statistical analysis methods were employed, such as multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic splines, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of the relationship between exposure factors and the incidence of new arthritis. Mediation analysis was also performed to explore the potential mechanism of depressive symptoms in the relationship between adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and new arthritis. Notably, this study is the first prospective cohort study on the Chinese population examining the impact of ACEs on the development of arthritis in middle-aged and elderly individuals.

However, there are some limitations to this study. First, the reliance on self-reported data for ACEs, depressive symptoms, and arthritis diagnoses may introduce recall bias or social desirability bias, potentially affecting the accuracy of the data. Despite controlling multiple potential confounders, there may still be unidentified confounders influencing the results. In addition, the CES-D scale's limitations may not fully capture all depressive symptoms, particularly those in individuals with severe depression. While a prospective design was used, causal inference should still be made cautiously due to the involvement of multiple complex factors. The assessment of ACEs through cumulative scores may not fully reflect the quality and depth of individual experiences, and the external validity of the findings may be influenced by regional and cultural differences, so the conclusions should be further validated in other populations. Finally, due to data missingness, a large proportion of participants were excluded from the analysis, and caution should be taken when interpreting the representativeness of the study results.

Conclusion

This study utilized a prospective cohort analysis to explore the impact of adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) and depressive symptoms on incident arthritis. The findings indicate a significant positive association between an increasing number of ACEs and the risk of developing arthritis, with this trend being more pronounced in females. In addition, worsening depressive symptoms were closely linked to a higher risk of incidental arthritis. Mediation analysis revealed that ACEs directly increased the risk of arthritis, with depressive symptoms playing a partial mediating role, underscoring the critical influence of psychological factors in the onset and progression of arthritis. Stratified analysis further demonstrated that the effect of ACEs was more significant among females and middle-aged individuals (45–64 years) but weakened in older adults (≥ 65 years). Moreover, among males, a significant increase in arthritis risk was observed only when ACE levels were high, whereas in females, even lower levels of ACEs were associated with an elevated arthritis risk. Regarding depressive symptoms, arthritis risk in females increased progressively with the severity of depression, whereas in males, only severe depression was associated with a substantial rise in risk.

This study provides the first prospective evidence from a Chinese population, highlighting the long-term effects of ACEs and depressive symptoms on incident arthritis in middle-aged and older adults. The findings suggest that arthritis prevention strategies for aging populations should consider the long-term health consequences of childhood adversity and emphasize the importance of mental health management to reduce the risk of arthritis development. Subsequent studies will conduct comprehensive intervention trials centered on psychological interventions for high-risk populations to evaluate their preventive effects on arthritis.
