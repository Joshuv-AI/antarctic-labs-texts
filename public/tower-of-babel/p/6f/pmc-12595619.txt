Profiles of risk taking behaviors among hookah smokers and associations with life satisfaction
Authors: Sima Afrashteh, Roya Farokhi, Davoud Adham, Hassan Ghobadi, Aso Rostampanahi, Hajar Amini, Abbas Abbasi-Ghahramanloo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe clustering of high-risk behaviors among different populations is growing. The current research aims to determine profiles of risk-taking behaviors among hookah smokers and associations with life satisfaction.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was performed on 424 hookah smokers. The sample was selected through multistage random sampling from five municipal districts in Ardabil city. All participants completed three sets of checklists and questionnaires. Data analysis was conducted using chi-square and latent class analysis.ResultsFour latent classes were identified; namely, “low-risk” (27.3%), “moderate risk” (27.2%), “high-risk” (26.2%), and “very high-risk” (19.3%). After adjusting for other studied covariates, higher age significantly decreased the odds of being in the high-risk class (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.82–0.92) compared to the low-risk class. Similarly, having a higher life satisfaction score significantly decreased the odds of membership in high-risk (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.24–0.76) and very high-risk (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.23–0.69) classes in comparison to the low-risk class. On the other hand, being male, compared to being female, increased the odds of membership in moderate risk (OR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.16–4.44) and very high-risk (OR = 6.53, 95% CI: 2.56–16.60) classes compared to the low-risk class. Also, existing substance users among family members increased the odds of belonging to moderate risk (OR = 4.45, 95% CI: 3.17–9.36), high-risk (OR = 2.30, 95% CI: 1.28–4.11), and very high-risk (OR = 5.25, 95% CI: 3.01–9.17) classes in comparison to the low-risk class.ConclusionResults showed that 45.5% of the participants fell under high- risk or very high-risk classes. Consequently, higher life satisfaction is often associated with lower levels of risk-taking behaviors in hookah smokers.Keywords: Risk taking behavior, Latent class analysis, Hookah, Life satisfaction

Introduction

Risk-taking behavior is defined as any behavior that can directly or indirectly increase the risk of a specific disease, ill health, and discomfort [1]. These behaviors are associated with many physical, psychological, and social consequences in people, which include various behaviors such as alcohol consumption, high-risk sexual behaviors, assaults, high-speed driving, tobacco smoking, and suicide attempts [2]. Despite all the efforts that have been made in the past decades to prevent them, these behaviors are one of the most important concerns around the world. As a result,71% of premature deaths in the youth group are due to risky behaviors [3]. Evidence shows that hookah smoking is most prevalent among young people in the Middle East, the United States, South Asia, and various European countries [4]. A recent scoping review found that the prevalence of hookah use is 26.4% in low- and middle-income countries [5]. In Iran, the prevalence of hookah use was 16.36% [6].

In the field of research worldwide, the clustering of high-risk behaviors among different populations is growing and expanding [7]. When risk- taking behaviors cluster with each other, a clustered effect is observed [8]. Several studies have explored clustering of risk-taking behaviors in different age groups. This often focuses on a narrow range of behaviors such as various substance use and clustering of substance use with sexual risk behavior [1, 9, 10]. Previous studies have shown that high-risk behaviors occur in co-occurrence and the Latent Class Analysis (LCA) is a suitable approach for explaining the different patterns of these behaviors and, clustering in different populations [11, 12]. LCA is a focused technique that divides individuals into unique subgroups regarding indicator variables and offers beneficial facts for analyzing the clustered characteristics of health-associated behaviors [13].

Life satisfaction is defined as the degree to which a person positively assesses the quality of life [14]. Life satisfaction as a person’s cognitive evaluation of their life is influenced by social, psychological, and individual characteristics [15, 16]. Evidence suggests that life satisfaction, subjective well-being, and happiness are often used interchangeably [14]. Low life satisfaction is linked to the use of drugs, alcohol, cannabis, and smoking [15]. In addition, the results of previous studies show that smoking has an inverse relationship with happiness [14, 17] Also, people suffering from mental illnesses and people with low levels of happiness and great sadness use hookah because of the happiness associated with using hookah and to avoid sadness and sadness and to overcome problems [18]. It seems that the level of life satisfaction decreases in people involved in risky behaviors due to the lack of appropriate coping strategies and poor social adaptation [16].

The studies conducted on the use of hookah have mostly investigated the prevalence and factors affecting it. However, the increasing trend of risk-taking behaviors, their consequences, and their relationship to life satisfaction among hookah smokers have highlighted the urgent and effective necessity for measures to reduce these behaviors. Therefore, the current research aims to determine profiles of risk-taking behaviors among hookah smokers and associations with life satisfaction.

Method

This cross-sectional research was carried out in 2023 among hookah users living in Ardabil city, located in northwest Iran. A multistage random sampling strategy was applied to recruit participants. Ardabil is divided into five municipal districts; in the first phase, three hookah cafés from each district were randomly chosen. In the second phase, based on the population size of each district and the number of customers in the selected cafés, 424 individuals who smoked hookah were randomly recruited. All smokers were eligible to take part. Data collection was conducted using a structured questionnaire and face-to-face interviews. Prior to participation, respondents were informed about the voluntary nature of the study, anonymity of responses, and confidentiality of their information. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Ardabil University of Medical Sciences, and informed consent was signed by all participants.

Participants completed three sets of forms and questionnaires. The first section captured demographic characteristics such as age, gender, and marital status. The second section assessed risk-taking behaviors including hookah use status (occasional vs. regular), lifetime risk-taking, history of self-injury, cigarette smoking during the past year, alcohol use during the past year, illicit drug use during the past year, and involvement in physical fights during the past month. The questions related to substance use were derived from the World Health Organization’s Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST), adapted to the context of substance use in Iran [1, 19]. The content validity of the tool had been confirmed in previous studies [19], and a preliminary pilot test was carried out among 27 hookah users with similar characteristics to the main sample to ensure reliability and applicability. Based on the results, necessary modifications were made.

In addition, participants were directly asked about their cigarette, alcohol, and drug use within the previous year. Life satisfaction was measured using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), which includes five items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Total scores range from 5 to 35, with higher values reflecting greater life satisfaction [20]. The categorization was as follows: extremely satisfied [31–35], satisfied [26–30], moderately satisfied [21–25], neutral [20], slightly dissatisfied [15–19], dissatisfied [10–14], and extremely dissatisfied [5–9]. For this study, individuals scoring above 20 were considered to have life satisfaction [20, 21].

Latent class analysis (LCA) was conducted by fitting models with one to seven latent classes [22]. LCA aggregates observed variables (indicators) to reveal unobserved categorical variables [1]. To ensure model identification, each model was estimated 20 times using different initial values. The optimal model was selected based on statistical indices including likelihood-ratio statistic (G²), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Entropy. Lower G², AIC, and BIC values together with higher Entropy indicated better model fit. Interpretability of classes was also considered. Seven dichotomous indicators—hookah smoking status, lifetime risk-taking, self-injury, cigarette smoking, alcohol use, drug use, and physical fighting—were used to define latent classes. After identifying the best-fitting model (four-class solution), LCA with covariates was performed to examine predictors of class membership. Covariates included age, sex, marital status, presence of substance use among family members, substance use among close friends, and life satisfaction. The “low-risk” class served as the reference group.

Descriptive statistics and chi-square tests were conducted using SPSS version 16, and LCA was performed with PROC LCA in SAS 9.4. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Out of 450 distributed questionnaires, 424 were returned (response rate = 94.2%). The mean age of participants was 28.17 ± 9.28 years (range 13–61). The majority were male (86.8%), and about 52% were unmarried.

Based on model selection indices (Table 2), the four-class model provided the best fit. The identified groups were: low-risk (27.3%), moderate-risk (27.2%), high-risk (26.2%), and very high-risk (19.3%). Members of the low-risk group had low probabilities of engaging in any risky behavior. The moderate-risk group showed higher likelihood for certain behaviors such as regular hookah smoking, general risk-taking, cigarette smoking, and alcohol use. In the high-risk group, most behaviors were common except illicit drug use and physical fights. The very high-risk class showed elevated probabilities across all risk-taking behaviors (Table 3).

Four predictors were found to significantly influence class membership (Table 4). Older age reduced the likelihood of belonging to the high-risk class (OR = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.82–0.92) relative to the low-risk group. Higher life satisfaction was associated with decreased odds of being in the high-risk (OR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.24–0.76) or very high-risk classes (OR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.23–0.69). Male participants were more likely than females to fall into the moderate-risk (OR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.16–4.44) or very high-risk classes (OR = 6.53, 95% CI: 2.56–16.60). In addition, having substance users among family members significantly increased the odds of belonging to the moderate-risk (OR = 4.45), high-risk (OR = 2.30), and very high-risk (OR = 5.25) groups compared with the low-risk group.

Discussion

The present study was conducted to investigate high-risk behaviors in hookah users and their relationship with life satisfaction. Of these, life satisfaction was 37.7%, and low life satisfaction was prevalent in regular hookah users compared to experimenter ones. We identified four profiles of risk-taking behavior in our study population using LCA, which represented low risk (class 1), moderate risk (class 2), high risk (class 3), and very high risk (class 4). According to our knowledge, this is the first article that examines the clustering of high-risk behaviors in hookah users. However, some studies have used the LCA technique to cluster high-risk behaviors in other populations. For example, in a study aimed at investigating these behaviors in male workers, Abbasi-Ghahramanloo et al. identified 4 latent classes, including low risk, very low risk, medium risk class (high probability of smoking, hookah smoking, and alcohol) and high risk class [1]. Mohammadpur’s et al. also identified four latent classes in students, including low risk, cigarette and hookah smokers, sexual and drinking risk-takers, and high risk [23].

We found that substance use among family members and being a male were positively associated with hookah smoking, cigarette smoking, and alcohol use. In addition, having life satisfaction and increased age were negatively associated with all risk-taking behaviors. These findings could be used for future research.

We observed that for a one-year increase in age, the odds of being in the high-risk class significantly decreased compared to the low-risk class. Although the relationship between age and risk-taking is culture-sensitive, risk-taking is generally highest among teenagers worldwide [24]. The results of another study showed that the tendency to take risks usually decreases with an increase in age [25].

According to the present study, being male significantly increased the odds of membership in the moderate, high-risk and very high- risk classes. Similar studies support this finding [26, 27]. Girls’ level of risk-taking is lower than that of boys in our current society because of the special mental framework they are given and their caution [28]. Zakiniaeiz et al. believe that men engage in risky behaviors more than women, and this gender difference can be explained by biological and socio-cultural differences. Moreover, women are confronted with more stigma and less social support than men [29].

We also observed that existing substance users among family members increased the odds of belonging to moderate risk, high-risk, and very high-risk classes in comparison to the low-risk class. Understandably, exposure to drug use in the family and society increases the possibility of drug use in individuals, so it is important to implement programmatic interventions at the family and community level to reduce the risk of substance use [30]. Also, studies show that children who grow up in homes with parents who are addicted to substances or alcohol are more likely to engage in risky behaviors due to abuse, neglect, behavioral and, emotional problems [31, 32]. These findings show that early recognition of the family situation and appropriate interventions can prevent future problems in people’s lives.

The finding that having life satisfaction has a protective effect on risk- taking behavior is consistent with previous studies. For example, Kroencke et al. found that low life satisfaction may motivate people to engage in health-risk behaviors, such as substance abuse [33]. In addition, the results of another study showed that low life satisfaction is an important predictor for the use of tobacco, alcohol, marijuana, and likely concurrent use of more than one substance [34]. According to the evidence, people who are dissatisfied with life, due to their inability to manage stress and deal with problems in difficult situations, have a greater tendency to adopt risky behaviors, especially alcohol consumption (due to escape from reality) [35]. Life satisfaction is a valuable target for enhancing mental health that can prevent tobacco smoking, alcohol use, illicit drug use and experience of self-injury [34, 36–38].

Limitations

Due to the cross-sectional design of the study, causality could not be assessing. Second, the study relied on self-report data; thus, underreporting of risk-taking behaviors was expected, even assuring the participants of the anonymity of the questionnaires. Finally, because the samples were taken from hookah bars, the results of this study may not be generalizable to all hookah consumers in Iran.

Conclusion

The results of this study showed four classes for clustering risky behaviors and their co-occurrence in hookah users. Results showed that near to half (45.5%) of the participants fell under t high risk or very high-risk classes. In addition, low life satisfaction was prevalent in regular hookah use than to experimenter use. The results demonstrated that substance use among family members and being a male was positively associated with hookah smoking, cigarette smoking, and alcohol use. However high life satisfaction was a protective factor for all risk-taking behaviors. Consequently, higher life satisfaction is often associated with lower levels of risk-taking behaviors in hookah smokers.
