Seizures as the initial presentation of cerebral venous sinus thrombosis in an elderly patient: a case report and literature review
Authors: Chang Liu, Wenze Li, Yanru Chen, Kunhua Li, Yangmei Chen, Yuqing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is a rare cerebrovascular disorder with heterogeneous clinical presentations. Although headache is the most common symptom, atypical presentations without headache may lead to diagnostic challenges, particularly in elderly patients. Seizures are a recognized manifestation of CVST but are occasionally the sole initial presentation.Case presentation:We describe a 64-year-old man with recurrent seizures and transient right-sided weakness, without headache. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed left frontal encephalomalacia, which initially complicated the diagnostic evaluation. Markedly elevated D-dimer levels and bilateral calf vein thrombosis were identified, while subsequent multimodal imaging, including computed tomography venography, demonstrated superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with prominent collateral venous channels. The seizures were considered to be related to a combined mechanism, in which the pre-existing encephalomalacic lesion may have increased seizure susceptibility, and CVST may have acted as a precipitating factor. Anticoagulation therapy and appropriate antiseizure treatment led to seizure resolution and clinical improvement.Clinical discussion:This case highlights a diagnostic pitfall in which competing but plausible findings may delay recognition of CVST. In elderly patients with atypical presentations, reliance on a single explanatory finding may contribute to premature diagnostic closure. Persistent or unexplained neurological symptoms should prompt reconsideration and further vascular imaging.Conclusion:CVST should be considered in patients with unexplained seizures, even in the absence of a headache. Early recognition and timely anticoagulation are essential to improve outcomes.

Introduction

Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is a relatively uncommon cerebrovascular disease characterized by thrombosis of the dural venous sinuses and/or cortical veins, with the superior sagittal and transverse sinuses being the most commonly affected sites[1]. Its annual incidence is estimated at 2–5 per 10 million individuals, accounting for 0.5–3% of all strokes[2,3]. The clinical manifestations of CVST, such as headache, visual disturbances, and motor deficits, vary depending on the site of thrombosis and the extent of collateral circulation. Headache is the most common symptom of CVST, occurring in nearly 90% of patients and often serving as an important clue for early diagnosis[4]. Seizures are a recognized manifestation of CVST but are occasionally the sole initial presentation[3]. In such cases, more common etiologies, including ischemic stroke, brain tumors, encephalitis, or metabolic disorders, are often considered first. This may lead to diagnostic delay. Here, we report a case of CVST manifesting as isolated seizures without headache in an elderly patient, highlighting an important diagnostic pitfall in clinical reasoning, in which atypical presentations and competing findings may delay recognition of CVST. Case presentation

A 64-year-old right-handed man was admitted to our emergency department due to intermittent twitching of the right arm and face. Twenty-one days prior, he was found unconscious by a neighbor with incontinence. He regained consciousness within 2 minutes but had no recollection of the events. Eleven days before admission, while walking with his family, he abruptly stopped, began talking to himself, and subsequently developed limb twitching with rightward gaze deviation. These symptoms lasted 3–5 minutes, and he was amnestic for the episode. He was then admitted to a local hospital, where brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed encephalomalacia with peripheral gliosis in the left frontal lobe. Chest computed tomography (CT) revealed bilateral interstitial changes with multiple intrapulmonary lesions suggestive of possible infection. He was treated with oxcarbazepine and lamotrigine (Table 1) but continued to experience intermittent right-sided limb twitching and episodes of loss of consciousness, occurring up to 10 times daily. Two days before referral to our hospital, he had a witnessed generalized tonic-clonic seizure lasting at least 5 minutes, which was terminated with intravenous diazepam, followed by continuous intravenous sodium valproate. Post-seizure, he exhibited slurred speech and right-sided weakness. The patient denied any history of head trauma, prior seizures, known neurological disorders, recent infection, toxin exposure, prior surgery, or family history of similar conditions. On neurological examination at admission, the patient was drowsy with slurred speech. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score was 11 (E3V4M4). Pupils were equal and reactive to light bilaterally, measuring approximately 3 mm in diameter. Motor examination showed muscle strength of 0/5 in the right upper and lower limbs and 5/5 in the left upper and lower limbs, according to the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale. Sensory and coordination examinations were limited due to poor cooperation. Deep tendon reflexes were normal (2+) bilaterally, and bilateral Babinski signs were present. Blood tests revealed mildly elevated C-reactive protein (CRP: 11.69 mg/L) and high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP: > 5.0 mg/L), as well as a significantly elevated plasma D-dimer level of 2122.5 ng/mL. Complete blood count, electrolytes, blood glucose, procalcitonin, liver and renal function, and coagulation profile were unremarkable. Lower limb Doppler ultrasonography showed intermuscular venous thrombosis in both calves. Video electroencephalogram demonstrated epileptiform discharges, including frequent low- to high-amplitude spikes and sharp waves, occurring sporadically or in bursts, predominantly in the left central and parietal regions, with electroclinical correlation to the patient’s episodes of confusion.

The patient was initially diagnosed with symptomatic seizures, Todd’s paresis, and lower limb venous thrombosis. The seizures were initially attributed to encephalomalacia in the left frontal lobe. The neurological examination was repeated the following day. The patient’s level of consciousness improved, and he was alert and oriented, with slightly slowed responses. The GCS score improved to 15 (E4V5M6). Motor deficits showed partial recovery, with right-sided strength improving to 1/5 on the MRC scale. Sensory examination was normal bilaterally. As part of the treatment plan, intravenous sodium valproate was replaced with sodium valproate sustained-release tablets, and levetiracetam was added. Considering the presence of intermuscular venous thrombosis, low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) therapy was initiated.

The patient underwent a contrast-enhanced brain MRI, which revealed prominent tortuous vessels in the bilateral cerebral hemispheres (Fig. 1). To further characterize these vascular structures, multimodal head CT, including non-contrast CT (NCCT), CT perfusion (CTP), CT angiography (CTA), and CT venography (CTV; Supplemental Digital Content Video, available at: https://links.lww.com/MS9/B302), was performed (Fig. 2A–D). CTP showed markedly hypoperfusion in the left frontal lobe. CTV revealed a filling defect in the superior sagittal sinus with prominent collateral venous channels, suggestive of superior sagittal sinus thrombosis with collateral venous drainage. Comprehensive thrombophilia testing and autoimmune screening were performed. The results were positive for anti-endothelial cell antibody, SSA, complement C4, and lupus anticoagulant screening test, while the genetic thrombophilia profile was negative. Improvement in right-sided muscle strength was observed 12 days later, supporting the diagnosis of Todd’s paresis. A follow-up CTV performed 20 days later showed a reduced thrombus burden in the superior sagittal sinus (Fig. 2E). Due to elevated alanine aminotransferase (ALT, 97 U/L) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST, 57 U/L), sodium valproate was replaced with oxcarbazepine. The patient was discharged after 28 days with favorable clinical outcomes and was prescribed rivaroxaban for at least 6 months of anticoagulation therapy. No further seizures occurred, although mild cognitive impairment was noted.

Discussion

New-onset epilepsy is common in older adults, and identifying the underlying etiology is essential for management[6,7]. In this case, an elderly male presented with seizures without an obvious precipitating factor. Differential diagnoses included stroke, brain tumor, encephalitis, and metabolic disturbances. However, imaging showed no arterial infarction, hemorrhage, or enhancing lesion, and laboratory findings did not support infection or metabolic causes. CTV demonstrated superior sagittal sinus thrombosis, and the subsequent clinical improvement after anticoagulation supported a probable contributory role of CVST in seizure occurrence.

An important diagnostic pitfall in this case was premature diagnostic closure. The markedly elevated D-dimer level appropriately prompted evaluation for venous thromboembolism, and the identification of lower limb deep vein thrombosis appeared to provide a sufficient explanation for the elevated D-dimer level. This may have created a false sense of diagnostic completeness, leading clinicians to attribute the elevated D-dimer to peripheral thrombosis alone, without considering concurrent intracranial venous involvement. At the same time, the seizures were initially attributed to the pre-existing encephalomalacic lesion rather than being considered in conjunction with the elevated D-dimer and systemic thrombosis. This fragmented interpretation of findings, rather than a truly integrative diagnostic approach, may have delayed recognition of an underlying prothrombotic state and the diagnosis of CVST.

CVST presents with heterogeneous clinical manifestations and is often underdiagnosed. While headache is the most common symptom, it is less frequent in elderly patients, contributing to diagnostic delay[8]. Seizures in CVST are classified into acute symptomatic seizures and late seizures. Acute symptomatic seizures typically occur within 7 days of CVST onset and are associated with specific lesion types, whereas late seizures occur beyond 7 days and are often unprovoked[9–11]. In this case, the presence of collateral venous channels suggests a degree of chronicity of the venous outflow obstruction. Although the exact timing of CVST onset could not be determined, the clinical presentation may be more consistent with late seizures. Moreover, CVST is a dynamic condition, and acute or subacute manifestations may still occur. The pre-existing cortical encephalomalacic lesion may have increased seizure susceptibility, and the possibility that it represents encephalomalacia secondary to a previous venous infarction cannot be excluded. Overall, the seizures may be explained by a combined effect, with the pre-existing lesion serving as a susceptible substrate and CVST acting as a precipitating factor.

The etiologies of CVST include hypercoagulable states, infections, autoimmune diseases, malignancies, hereditary thrombophilia, certain hematologic conditions, and medications[12]. In elderly patients, the most common risk factors include inherited or acquired thrombophilia, malignancies, and hematological disorders[8]. In this case, laboratory findings suggested a possible autoimmune-related prothrombotic tendency; however, in the absence of corresponding clinical features, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require follow-up.

Anticoagulation remains the cornerstone of CVST treatment. Current guidelines recommend 3–12 months of oral anticoagulation depending on etiology and risk factors, with longer or lifelong treatment considered in patients with persistent hypercoagulable states or recurrent CVST[2,3,12,13]. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety to warfarin, with greater convenience and fewer drug interactions[14–16]. In this case, given the potential interaction between warfarin and antiseizure medications, a DOAC was preferred, and at least 6 months of anticoagulation was recommended. Endovascular treatment may be considered in cases of clinical deterioration or treatment failure[3,12].

Epilepsy as a long-term consequence of CVST is infrequent, and evidence regarding seizure prevention remains limited[2]. Antiseizure medications (ASMs) are recommended in patients with symptomatic seizures and parenchymal injury, whereas management is less clear in other scenarios[12,17]. Additionally, the optimal duration of antiseizure therapy is unclear. Newer ASMs, such as lamotrigine and levetiracetam, have comparable efficacy to older agents with better tolerability, particularly in elderly patients[6,18]. Although oxcarbazepine and lamotrigine are both first-line treatments for focal epilepsy, their concurrent use may increase the risk of adverse events due to similar mechanisms[19,20]. In this case, seizures were effectively controlled after anticoagulation and ASM adjustment. In elderly patients, careful monitoring of adverse drug reactions and organ function is essential.

This case highlights several practical points. CVST should be considered in elderly patients presenting with new-onset or unexplained seizures, even in the absence of headache. Although an elevated D-dimer level may appropriately prompt evaluation for venous thromboembolism, the identification of peripheral venous thrombosis should not preclude consideration of concurrent intracranial venous thrombosis, particularly in patients with neurological symptoms. Early multimodal vascular imaging is essential for accurate diagnosis. Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Fundoscopic examination and susceptibility-weighted imaging were not performed, limiting further assessment of papilledema and susceptibility-related venous or hemorrhagic changes. Malignancy screening was not comprehensive, as only prostate cancer markers were evaluated. In addition, advanced investigations for seizure focus localization were not performed, limiting further evaluation of whether the seizures were related to the encephalomalacic lesion.

In conclusion, CVST may present with isolated seizures in elderly patients and can be easily overlooked, particularly when competing structural brain lesions are present. In such cases, seizures may reflect a combined mechanism rather than a single definitive cause. Early recognition and timely anticoagulation are critical for improving outcomes.
