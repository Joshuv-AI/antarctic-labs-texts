Comparing treatment strategies for placental site trophoblastic tumour (PSTT): study protocol for a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Jiandi Ma, Yixiao Zhou, Jun Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionPlacental site trophoblastic tumour (PSTT) is a rare type of gestational trophoblastic malignancy. Despite different guidelines recommending surgery, chemotherapy or immunotherapy, there has been no optimal first-line treatment owing to its rarity. Direct comparisons of efficacy and safety across interventions remain limited. We aim to perform a systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) to explore each PSTT treatment and to help develop individualised, evidence-based clinical decisions.Methods and analysisThis systematic review, registered on PROSPERO and adhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-P 2015 guidelines, will evaluate all available treatments for PSTT. Databases (PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science) will be searched from inception to 31 May 2025 using Medical Subject Headings terms and keywords related to PSTT. The inclusion criteria will be studies reporting confirmed PSTT patients and treatment outcomes; the exclusion criteria will be non-human studies, abstracts and reviews. Two researchers will independently screen studies, resolving discrepancies via discussion or a third reviewer. Manual reference checks will complement searches. Data on study characteristics, interventions (chemotherapy, surgery or other treatments) and outcomes (overall survival (OS), progression-free survival, adverse events, fertility) will be extracted. The primary outcome will be the OS. Quality will be assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies and V.2 Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (RoB 2) for randomised controlled trials. Statistical analyses will be conducted via Review Manager 5.4.1 and Stata 16.0. The ORs, along with 95% CIs will be calculated with random-effects or fixed-effects models depending on the heterogeneity between studies. The sensitivity analyses and publication bias (funnel plots, Begg’s/Egger’s tests) will be assessed if indicated.Ethics and disseminationAs this study analyses previously published data, no additional ethical approval is required, nor will patient safety be compromised. The collected results will be submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed journal following PRISMA-NMA guidelines. No datasets will be generated or analysed during the current study.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251013082.Keywords: Placental site trophoblast tumor (PSTT), treatment, overall survival (OS), systematic review, network meta-analysis (NMA)

Introduction

Placental site trophoblast tumour (PSTT) is a rare and unique form of gestational trophoblastic neoplasm (GTN) that arises from the placental site trophoblast.1 2 The trophoblast cell is a specific cell type that normally anchors the placenta to the endometrium during pregnancy.2 It typically occurs in women of reproductive age, manifesting with various clinical presentations, including abnormal uterine bleeding, pelvic pain, but can be asymptomatic in some cases. PSTT accounts for only 1%–2% of all GTN cases or 1/100 000 pregnancies.1 2 It is distinct from other forms of GTN, such as choriocarcinoma and hydatidiform mole, in terms of its clinical behaviour, histological features, prognosis and treatment.2,4

The aetiology of PSTT remains elusive despite decades of research. It is most likely to involve a complex interplay of molecular, genetic, hormonal and environmental factors.5,7 While the precise mechanisms driving PSTT pathogenesis are unclear, emerging evidence suggests a potential association with dysregulation of tumour suppressor genes, such as TP53, and oncogenic pathways linked to trophoblast differentiation.6 Hormonally, elevated human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels and aberrant expression of placental growth factors may contribute to tumour persistence,5 6 8 though their roles remain ambiguous. Environmental risk factors, including advanced maternal age and prior molar pregnancies, further complicate the aetiological landscape.6 In clinical practice, PSTT poses unique challenges due to its rarity, heterogeneous behaviour and resistance to conventional chemotherapy regimens used for other gestational trophoblastic neoplasias.5 Current management strategies vary widely, ranging from uterine-sparing surgery to aggressive multimodal therapies combining hysterectomy, platinum-based chemotherapy and immunotherapy. However, the rarity of the condition hindered the application of randomised trials used to be adopted by the guidelines.5 This has led to inconsistent outcomes, particularly in metastatic or recurrent cases.2 4 5 This knowledge gap underscores the urgency of synthesising global evidence to clarify optimal therapeutic approaches.

As PSTT is not correlated with the FIGO (The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) scoring system, the treatment varies across different regions.6 Primary treatment often centres on hysterectomy, given the tumour’s origin in the placental implantation site, with uterine conservation considered cautiously in select early-stage cases desiring future fertility.3,5 Adjuvant chemotherapy, typically incorporating regimens such as EMA/CO (etoposide, methotrexate, actinomycin D, cyclophosphamide, vincristine) or TE/TP (paclitaxel/cisplatin), is reserved for high-risk features,3,5 including deep myometrial invasion, metastatic disease or prolonged interval from antecedent pregnancy. However, significant regional disparities exist: some centres prioritise surgery alone for localised disease, while others advocate for combined modality approaches, particularly in advanced stages.3 5 6 9 Emerging immunotherapies, such as anti-PD-1 agents,5 10 have shown promise in chemoresistant cases, though evidence remains sparse. This variability is compounded by limited comparative studies, small sample sizes and heterogeneous reporting of outcomes, hindering consensus on risk-adapted protocols.2,5 Furthermore, disparities in healthcare resources and diagnostic capabilities globally exacerbate inconsistencies in treatment accessibility and quality. Addressing these challenges requires a comprehensive synthesis of existing data to identify context-specific strategies and optimise therapeutic paradigms for this rare malignancy.

Therefore, in this systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA), we will include studies that reported treatment for PSTT to compare different therapeutic strategies and to find out the most appropriate treatment for this rare disease.

Methods and analysis

Study protocol

A systematic review will be conducted to evaluate all available treatments for PSTT following the protocol registered on PROSPERO (No. CRD420251013082) and will be reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA)-P 2015 guidelines (online supplemental file 1).11 According to the latest version, we will search four databases of PubMed, Scopus, Embase and Web of Science, covering all relevant studies from inception to 31 May 2025. The search strategy will include a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free text words, such as ‘gestational trophoblastic neoplasia or placental site trophoblast tumo* or PSTT or gestational trophoblastic disease or (trophoblastic neoplasms)[Mesh]’. The complete search strategies for all databases can be accessed in online supplemental file 2).

Inclusion criteria

Studies that report patients with histopathologically confirmed PSTT and their treatments and the results. The manuscript can be written in any language.

Exclusion criteria

Women with concurrent cancerous status. Studies with insufficient data on treatment and/or outcomes. Conference abstracts, editorials, letters, reviews, expert opinions, case reports with fewer than five cases and non-human studies.

Study selection

The two researchers will independently review all papers according to the search strategy mentioned above. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion. If no consensus was agreed, a third researcher will determine the results. We will also manually check the reference lists of the included publications and previous meta-analyses to find more potentially eligible studies. Data collection and analysis

After thoroughly reviewing the full text of the studies, data extraction will be performed independently by two researchers, and discrepancies will be resolved by consensus decision. Data will be extracted from included studies on study characteristics, including region, type of study and population.

Types of interventions and outcome measure

For the treatment regimen, we will further extract data of chemotherapy, surgical management and other therapies in detail. The follow-up results will include overall survival (OS) as the primary outcome, progression-free survival (PFS), remission rate, recurrence rate, adverse events (such as chemo-related events, surgical complications), fertility outcomes (if fertility-sparing strategies were used).

Quality evaluation

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) will be applied for case-control, cohort and cross-sectional studies. We will rate the articles according to a nine-star scoring system. The score of seven or higher indicates good quality of the study, while a score of three or lower is suggestive of poor study quality. Moderate quality is defined as in-between. Randomised controlled trials will be assessed with V.2 of the Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (RoB 2). The overall assessments will be graded in three categories: low risk of bias (low), some concerns and high risk of bias (high). Moreover, the cross-studies bias will be evaluated using Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis, assessing domains including within-study bias, indirectness and incoherence.12

Statistical methods

We will calculate results and perform data analysis via Review Manager 5.4.1 (The Nordic Cochrane Center, Copenhagen, Denmark) and Stata V.16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). The overall results will be calculated in ORs with 95% CIs. The I2 will be used to test the heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 50% indicates significant heterogeneity). The heterogeneity variance (τ2) will be estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method. The forest plot is applied to demonstrate the overall results, with the random-effect model being adopted for calculation as the heterogeneity was considered significant. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis will be performed to assess whether any single study could affect the whole outcome. Publication bias will be visualised via funnel plot with Begg’s test and tested with Egger’s linear regression. Network plots will be generated using STATA V.16.0 and the network package. For each clinical outcome (such as OS, PFS, complete remission rate, adverse events), a network plot will illustrate the comparisons between treatment strategies (such as hysterectomy, EMA/CO chemotherapy, TE/TP regimens, immunotherapy). Node size will correspond to the total patient population receiving each intervention, while edge thickness will reflect the number of studies directly comparing two treatments. To evaluate consistency in closed loops (such as triangular comparisons between surgery, chemotherapy and immunotherapy), a node-split model will assess discrepancies between direct and indirect evidence. Transitivity will be assessed by evaluating the distribution of key effect modifiers (FIGO stage, time since antecedent pregnancy, metastasis status, hCG levels, prior treatment, fertility-preservation intent) across treatment comparisons. Balance of categorical modifiers (such as FIGO stage) will be quantified using contingency tables, and continuous modifiers (such as time since pregnancy) using standardised mean differences (SMD). An SMD >0.4 will trigger meta-regression adjustment or stratified analysis. Where fertility-sparing strategies are exclusively used in early-stage disease, direct comparisons with hysterectomy in advanced stages will be excluded. To address potential confounding in non-randomised studies, we will (1) use node-split models to compare direct and indirect evidence; (2) apply design-adjusted analysis (Efthimiou’s approach A)13; (3) quantify study contributions via matrices and (4) monitor heterogeneity changes. Sensitivity analyses will exclude high-risk-of-bias studies.

Ethics and dissemination

Given the nature of secondary analysis, no additional institutional ethics committee approval is required. Furthermore, this methodological approach inherently ensures that patient safety remains uncompromised, as no new clinical interventions or primary data collection involving human subjects are being conducted. We explicitly affirm that no original datasets will be generated, collected or subjected to analytical procedures. On completion of the analysis, the synthesised results will be systematically prepared and submitted for publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal, adhering strictly to the PRISMA-NMA guidelines. The primary intention of submitting the study results for publication is to disseminate the findings widely and facilitate their broad accessibility within the scientific community and healthcare providers.

Discussion

In this study, we described the rationale and protocol for a systematic review and NMA aiming to specifically compare different treatment choices for patients with PSTT. The network approach will provide evidence to guide future personalised treatment in highly selective cases. This systematic review and NMA will present a unique perspective on treatment and outcomes of PSTT, including the most comprehensive collection of studies. The approach of systematic review and NMA is essential in studying rare diseases and outcomes.

There have been two published systematic reviews.3 4 Deng et al published their Cochrane review in 2013, which has been 12 years since the last update.3 In contrast, Albright et al published more up to date, but this review excluded reports of PSTT and epithelioid trophoblastic tumours.4 Therefore, this study will be a valuable addition to the existing studies, possibly providing evidence for future guidelines.

This study will have several strengths. First, this study aims to provide healthcare providers and patients with the latest evidence synthesised from the most comprehensive search. PSTT is exceptionally rare, limiting the feasibility of large, randomised trials. By pooling fragmented data from small studies and case series, this NMA maximises statistical power and provides clinically meaningful insights despite the scarcity of direct comparisons. We also take advantage of the NMA to compare different treating modalities as conventional meta-analysis needs a predefined comparator to observe the effect difference between the intervention group and the control group. However, different study designs could lead to the inclusion of completely different control groups. This will affect the estimation of effect size. In contrast, NMA integrates both direct and indirect evidence across a network of interventions, eliminating the need for a fixed comparator. This will be the first systematic review and NMA study for PSTT. Second, the NMA framework generates probability-based rankings of treatments (such as hysterectomy vs fertility-sparing surgery, chemotherapy regimens), highlighting optimal strategies for efficacy (such as OS) and safety (such as adverse events, fertility preservation). This supports personalised treatment planning, particularly for patients desiring future pregnancies. Third, the study protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO and will adhere to PRISMA-P and the study will be written following PRISMA-NMA guidelines. Rigorous risk-of-bias assessment will be performed to ensure reproducibility and minimise bias. Sensitivity analyses will further strengthen confidence in the findings.

However, there are also a few limitations. First, the original research included will present possibly significant heterogeneity in study designs, how the outcomes were measured and the adverse events defined and recorded. These may affect the pooled effect and the generalisation and extrapolation of the study conclusions. Second, the study may not be able to cover all available treatments. As we focused on rare tumour types, some treatments, such as complementary or herbal medical treatments may not be included as there were few cases published or reported. These might lead to biased results reporting. Third, despite a systematic screening and reviewing of relevant studies, we may still miss some studies. This can cause publication bias, but we will try to address this by a careful review and search on all references of the included studies.

This systematic review and NMA will evaluate all available treatments via a state-of-the-art approach of NMA to personalise the optimal treatment for patients with PSTT. The results may change the shared decision-making, encourage future clinical research and eventually improve the prognosis of this rare disease.
