Triamcinolone acetonide nanoparticles ameliorate interstitial cystitis and anxiety/depression by targeting the bladder–metabolic–brain axis
Authors: Zhicheng Liu, Qian Ju, Yuanyuan Yang, Qudong Lu, Rong Huang, Jing Yang, Ying Bao, Tao Jiang, Yiji Peng, Yuting Liu, Dinglin Zhang, Wenhao Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Interstitial cystitis (IC) is a refractory bladder disorder for which there are limited therapeutic options due to poorly defined pathogenic mechanisms and suboptimal drug delivery. In addition to bladder pain and urinary dysfunction, patients frequently develop comorbid anxiety and depression. Using metabolomics, we identified D-gulono-1,4-lactone as a pivotal metabolic mediator linking bladder inflammation to neuropsychiatric comorbidities in a cyclophosphamide (CYP)-induced IC mouse model. To target IC, we developed cRGD-functionalized, triamcinolone acetonide (TA)-loaded, pH/ROS dual-responsive nanoparticles (TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs) for intravesical delivery. These NPs promoted mucoadhesion, tissue penetration, and responsive drug release, leading to marked amelioration of bladder inflammation, mast cell infiltration, and urinary symptoms. Furthermore, by suppressing hippocampal neuroinflammation, these NPs alleviated anxiety/depression-like behaviors and consequently normalized D-gulono-1,4-lactone levels. Together, these results uncover a bladder–metabolic–brain axis in IC and propose a dual-action therapeutic strategy that simultaneously targets bladder pathology and associated neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Introduction

Interstitial cystitis (IC), a complex and poorly understood bladder condition characterized by pelvic pain, urinary urgency and frequency, substantially decreases the quality of life of patients [1]. Specifically, IC predominantly affects female patients, with prevalence estimates ranging from 0.45% to 6.5% across populations, thereby posing a significant urological challenge and spurring extensive global research efforts [2]. Although the precise etiology of IC remains incompletely defined, available evidence indicates that the pathogenic mechanisms are multifactorial, including, but not limited to, urothelial barrier dysfunction [3], neurogenic inflammation [4], mast cell activation [5], and autoimmune responses [6]. In particular, distinctive pathologic conditions include disruption of the glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer [3], increased mast cell infiltration in the urothelium [5], elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, and a dysfunctional bladder microenvironment marked by tissue hypoxia, low pH, and elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels [7]. Notably, in addition to bladder manifestations, IC is frequently accompanied by psychiatric comorbidities, including anxiety and depression, suggesting potential communication along a bladder–brain axis [4,8].

The current refractory IC therapeutic landscape remains unsatisfying [1]. Fundamentally, two major challenges are seriously hampering the development of effective, targeted therapies: one is our incomplete understanding of the disease's underlying pathogenic mechanisms, and the other is poor efficiency in drug delivery [9]. IC presents considerable molecular complexity, marked phenotypic heterogeneity, and intricate interplay between peripheral and central nervous systems (CNS). These features have impeded a full understanding of its pathophysiology and, as a result, have hindered the development of mechanism-based targeted therapies [1,4,7]. Fortunately, metabolomics has increasingly been regarded as a potent approach for unraveling IC pathophysiology, providing insights into its diagnosis, subtyping, and mechanism. For instance, Kim et al. identified a diagnostic panel comprising purines, phenylalanine, 5-oxoproline, and 5-HIAA [10], whereas Parker et al. proposed Etio-S as a novel biomarker [11]. Furthermore, Zhu et al. demonstrated distinct metabolic signatures between Hunner-type IC (HIC) and non-Hunter-type IC (NHIC), particularly perturbations in steroid hormone biosynthesis and amino acid metabolism pathways, together with subtype-specific microbiome–metabolite interactions [12]. We performed untargeted metabolomic profiling in a cyclophosphamide (CYP)-induced IC mouse model. This model recapitulates hallmark features of human IC, including urothelial damage, mast cell infiltration, bladder pain [7], and associated anxiety and depression [13]. Our analysis revealed that D-gulono-1,4-lactone was a crucial metabolic mediator significantly linked to disease progression. These findings not only support molecular classification but also implicate key mechanisms such as oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Overall, the accumulating evidence underscores the considerable potential of metabolomics in advancing precision diagnosis and elucidating disease mechanisms in IC.

Although some advances have been made in understanding its pathophysiology, clinical management remains significantly limited by the poor drug delivery efficiency [9]. Indeed, according to the American Urological Association (AUA) guidelines, cystoscopy-guided triamcinolone acetonide (TA) injection combined with hydrodistention is one of the recommended treatments [1]. Notably, we were the first urology center in China to implement this technique and achieved favorable clinical outcomes [14]. However, this approach remains inherently invasive, requires anesthesia, and carries procedural risks such as bladder perforation and infection. Alternatively, intravesical instillation is a non-invasive option, yet it is largely limited by rapid drug washout from urinary voiding and poor drug retention due to inadequate mucosal adhesion and continuous dilution by urine [15]. Thus, these compounded shortcomings contribute to low patient compliance and inadequate long-term symptom control, collectively highlighting the urgent need for improved and intelligent drug delivery strategies.

Nanotechnology is driving notable progress in the treatment of bladder disorders through the rational design of innovative drug delivery platforms. Specifically, these nanosystems can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy by improving mucoadhesion, prolonging drug residence time and enabling site-specific controlled drug release in the bladder [16]. Among these advancements, stimuli-responsive nanoparticles (NPs) enable precise drug release within pathological microenvironments [17]; similarly, urease-powered self-propelling NPs enhance active targeting and localized accumulation [18], and ligand-functionalized nanoformulations significantly improve cellular recognition and intracellular delivery efficiency [19]. More importantly, the integration of nanotechnology with disease-specific biology has led to the development of multifunctional platforms capable of not only microenvironment-triggered drug release and receptor-mediated uptake but also reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging. Collectively, these advances enable spatiotemporal control over drug delivery, extend local retention, and ultimately pave the way for intelligent, highly efficient localized therapies for bladder diseases.

On the basis of the aforementioned advancements and existing challenges, we further hypothesized that targeted nanotherapeutics could simultaneously alleviate urinary symptoms and anxiety/depression-like behaviors via the bladder–metabolic–brain axis. To test this hypothesis, we developed Arg-Gly-Asp-Cys (cRGD) peptide-functionalized, TA-loaded, pH/ROS dual-responsive nanotherapeutics for the targeted treatment of IC. Specifically, cRGD displayed a strong affinity for αvβ3 integrin, which is overexpressed in inflamed bladder tissues, thereby enabling efficient accumulation of the nanotherapeutics at the lesion site. Notably, our results demonstrated that D-gulono-1,4-lactone is closely related to IC development, and more importantly, TA-loaded nanotherapeutics can potentially relieve both urinary and mental symptoms via the bladder–metabolic–brain axis.

Materials and methods

Cells and animals

RAW264.7 macrophages and SV-HUC-1 human uroepithelial cells were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) and Ham's F12K medium (F12K) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 100 U/mL penicillin streptomycin, respectively. The cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For in vivo experiments, female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from ENSIWEIER Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Chongqing, China). All animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) barrier facility under controlled environmental conditions, including a temperature of 22–25 °C, a relative humidity of 50–60%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle. The animals had free access to food and water. All experimental procedures involving animals were performed in strict accordance with the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Army Medical University (Chongqing, China) (Approval No. AMUWEC20232474).

Preparation and characterization of TA-loaded NPs

TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs, TA/CA-αCD NPs and blank CA-αCD NPs were fabricated by a nanoprecipitation/self-assembly method as previously reported by our team [20,21]. Briefly, lecithin (6 mg) and DSPE-PEG2000 (6 mg) were codispersed in a mixture of ethanol and deionized water, heated at 65 °C for 30 min, and then allowed to cool to room temperature (RT). 50 mg CA-HPAP-αCD (blank CA-αCD NPs) and TA (5 mg) were dissolved in 400 μL methyl alcohol and 200 μL DMSO, and the resulting drug-containing solution was added dropwise to the lipid dispersion under gentle stirring. The mixture was then stirred rapidly for 3 min, followed by slow stirring at RT for 2 h to allow self-assembly. The resultant suspension was centrifuged at 15,000×g for 10 min, and the pellet was washed with 5% (w/v) Pluronic F-127 solution. The washed pellet was then resuspended in 200 μL of ultrapure water to obtain the final TA/CA-αCD NPs suspension. For the preparation of TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs, the same procedure was applied, except that DSPE-PEG2000 (3 mg) and cRGD-PEG2000-DSPE (3 mg) were used to form the lipid dispersion. The particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of the NPs were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Morphological characterization was performed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1400, JEOL, Japan). To evaluate the colloidal stability, freshly prepared NPs were diluted in ultrapure water or 10% (v/v) FBS, and their size and PDI were monitored at designated time intervals.

In vitro release profiles of TA

We measured the in vitro release of TA from TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs and TA/CA-αCD NPs using a dialysis-based setup. NPs containing 5 mg TA were placed in dialysis bags (MWCO 3500 Da) and immersed in 40 mL of release medium. The medium consisted of PBS (pH 7.4), PBS with 1 mM H2O2, PBS at pH 5.0, or PBS at pH 5.0 plus 1 mM H2O2. Samples were kept at 37 °C with constant shaking at 100 rpm. At scheduled time intervals, we withdrew 4 mL of medium and replaced it with the same volume of fresh solution to keep sink conditions. TA concentrations in the samples were determined by HPLC (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), and we calculated cumulative release from these values.

Cytotoxicity of the TA-loaded NPs

RAW264.7 cells and SV-HUC-1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in 100 μL complete medium and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. The cells were then treated with free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs at various concentrations (0, 1, 2, 5, 10, or 20 μg/mL) and further incubated for 24 h. After the removal of the medium and three washes with 1 × PBS, 100 μL CCK-8 solution (CCK-8:DMEM/F12K = 1:9) was added, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Cell viability was assessed by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Varioskan Flash, USA).

Cellular uptake of NPs

RAW264.7 cells and SV-HUC-1 cells were seeded in confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h, stimulated with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 12 h, and then coincubated with free Cy5, Cy5-labeled TA/CA-αCD NPs, or Cy5-labeled TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs (final Cy5 concentration: 1 μg/mL) for 2 or 4 h. After being washed 3 times with PBS to remove uninternalized NPs, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, mounted with DAPI-containing medium, and imaged by CLSM (LSM780, ZEISS, Germany). Alternatively, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS, detached by trypsinization, pelleted at 1000 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD FACS Aria III, USA) to quantify the uptake efficiency.

Apoptosis assay

RAW264.7 and SV-HUC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 105 cells per well. After 12 h of LPS stimulation (1 μg/mL), cells were treated with free TA, blank CA-αCD NPs, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs for another 24 h. Cells were then collected, resuspended in 500 μL PBS, and stained with 5 μl FITC-annexin V and 5 μl PI from the apoptosis detection kit obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China; Cat. No. C1062M). Apoptosis was assessed by flow cytometry (BD FACSAria III, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and Western blot analysis

We plated RAW264.7 and SV-HUC-1 cells in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells/well) and allowed them to attach for 24 h. Following 12 h of LPS stimulation (1 μg/mL), cells were given PBS (positive control), blank CA-αCD NPs, free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs for 24 h. Levels of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CXCL13 were measured by ELISA kit (Cat. No. EK0410, EK0411, EK0525, EK0527, EK0392, EK0394, EK0739, and EK0740) purchased from Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China), with each treatment run in triplicate. Protein samples were prepared in 5 × SDS loading buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai; Cat. No. P0015) and separated by 12% SDS-PAGE. After transfer to PVDF membranes (Merck, Billerica, MA), membranes were blocked with FastBlock (Beyotime, Shanghai; Cat. No. P0240) for 15 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies against IL-6, TNF-α, ITGB3, and GAPDH (all from Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were used at dilutions of 1:1000, 1:1000, 1:1000, and 1:5000, respectively, and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:2000) for 1 h at room temperature.

Establishment of IC mouse model and treatment

An IC female C57BL/6 mouse model was induced via three intraperitoneal injections of cyclophosphamide (CYP, 75 mg/kg, Sigma) administered on days 1, 4, and 7, as previously described [22,23]. For each CYP injection in the experimental group, the controls received the same volume of PBS i.p. IC model mice were established and randomly divided five groups. Normal mice were observed without intervention. IC model mice received PBS, blank CA-αCD NPs, free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs via intravesical instillation for 3 consecutive days. All TA-containing groups received an equivalent TA dose of 5 mg/kg.

Retention and permeability of TA-loaded NPs in the urothelium

NPs were labeled with the fluorescent probe Cy5 using our reported method [24]. IC model mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 5 per group): (I) Saline, (II) free Cy5, (III) Cy5/CA-αCD NPs, (IV) Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs, and (V) cRGD + Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs (free cRGD peptide pre-treatment followed by administration of Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs), in which the corresponding treatments were administered via intravesical instillation at a Cy5 dose of 20 μg per mouse. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 0, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h postinstillation using a Live Imaging System IVIS Spectrum CT (PerkinElmer, Waltham, USA) with an excitation filter at 625 nm and an emission filter at 680 nm. The mice were sacrificed at 24 h for subsequent analysis. Bladder tissues were collected from IC model mice for fluorescence intensity analysis to evaluate NP distribution. Subsequently, cryosections were prepared from the bladder tissues of randomly selected mice (3 out of 5 per group), mounted with DAPI-containing anti-fluorescence quenching medium, imaged by CLSM, and quantified using Living Image.

Histological and immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded bladder tissues were sectioned at 10 μm. Sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) following standard procedures. Mast cells were visualized by toluidine blue staining (0.5%, pH 2.8, 8 min) with acidic differentiation. For immunohistochemistry, we carried out antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using microwave heating. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CXCL13. After washing 3 times, we applied HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, then counterstained with hematoxylin.

Von Frey filament test

Pelvic pain responses were evaluated using von Frey filaments (DanMic Global, LLC., San Jose, USA) with a calibrated series of forces: 0.02, 0.04, 0.16, 0.4, 1.0, 1.4, 2.0, 4.0, 6.0, and 8.0 g. The up-down method was adopted with tests performed on the suprapubic region with a standardized filament series. Positive responses included licking/scratching at the stimulation site, sudden rising, or rapid jumping.

Void spot assays

Urinary frequency and mean voided volume were studied using the void spot assay (VSA), which was conducted daily during a fixed time window (10:00 a.m.–2:00 p.m.). Mice were gently placed into metabolic cages lined with Whatman No. 1 circular filter paper and provided with standard food and water. The 4-h assay was performed in a quiet environment. After testing, the mice were returned to their home cages, and the filter papers were collected for UV imaging. Urination frequency was determined by the number of urine spots on the stained filter papers, and the total urine output was reflected by the total area of the urine spots.

Behavioral tests

To minimize potential experimenter bias, all behavioral assessments were conducted under a single-blind procedure. Experimental data were coded prior to analysis. Quantification of behavioral parameters was performed by investigators who were unaware of the group allocations. The analysts were not involved in animal handling or group assignment, ensuring the objectivity and reproducibility of all experimental results.(1)Open Field Tests

Metabolomic analysis

The metabolomic data were processed as follows: the raw files were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard MSConvert, followed by peak detection, retention time correction, and alignment with XCMS under specified parameters (ppm = 15, peak width = 5–30, mzdiff = 0.01, method = “centWave”). Signal drift was corrected using the QC samples, and metabolites whose RSDs were >30% were excluded. Metabolite identification was performed by matching accurate mass and MS/MS spectra against HMDB, MassBank, KEGG, LipidMaps, mzCloud, and a custom database. Multivariate analyses (PCA, PLS-DA, and OPLS-DA) were conducted using the ropls package, with significantly altered metabolites selected on the basis of a p value < 0.05, a VIP >1, and the fold change. Enriched pathways were identified through hypergeometric testing and visualized via KEGG Mapper.

K-means clustering

In the mass spectrometry-based metabolomic profiling of bladder tissue, we used K-means clustering for unsupervised grouping of samples. Metabolite intensity data were Z-score normalized, and we chose the optimal cluster number (K) based on the silhouette coefficient. Samples were then grouped into K metabotype-homogeneous subgroups by iterative calculation. To reveal hidden sample groupings from metabolic profiles, we visualized the clustering results with principal component analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining of hippocampal regions

Mice were anesthetized, cardiac perfusion was performed with PBS, brains were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and tissues were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose at 4 °C until they had sunk. The brains were cut into 20 μm sections, washed three times in PBS, blocked with an immunofluorescence blocking agent for 1 h at 25 °C, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GFAP (GFAP, 1:200; #16825-1-AP, Proteintech) and Iba1 (Iba1, 1:200; #10904-1-AP, Proteintech). After three washes in PBS, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies in the dark at 20–25 °C for 1 h. Hippocampal regions were imaged by fluorescence microscopy. Fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test were used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test. Significance was defined as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001; and ns, no statistically significant difference.

Results and discussion

Metabolic reprogramming in CYP-induced IC bladders

To study IC-related metabolic changes and clarify mechanisms behind urinary and neuropsychiatric symptoms, we used a mouse model with repeated CYP injections and analyzed bladder tissue metabolomics (Fig. 1A). Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis revealed 1639 metabolites in bladder tissues from both normal mice and mice with CYP-induced IC. Multivariate statistical analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA), were performed in both positive and negative ion modes. These analyses revealed distinct metabolic clustering among the experimental groups, demonstrating clear separation between normal controls and mice with CYP-induced IC in all the model score plots (Fig. 1B). Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were constructed to visualize global metabolic patterns in response to CYP induction (Fig. 1C), and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed the dysregulated pathways underlying these patterns (Fig. 1D). Volcano plots were then constructed to pinpoint the individual metabolites driving these pathway-level changes (Fig. 1E). Comparative analysis showed that the linoleic acid, citrate cycle, ferroptosis, and sphingolipid metabolism pathways were significantly enriched with fold change >2 and p < 0.05 in the CYP-induced IC group (Fig. 1D). To get a clearer picture of the metabolic shifts, we constructed comparative heatmaps incorporating both pathway-associated compounds and other metabolites displaying marked fold changes. This approach allowed us to systematically capture key differences between groups. Integration of these data highlighted a subset of metabolites with pronounced changes and potential links to inflammation, neurological impairment, and pain modulation. These metabolites were selected for further investigation. Their expression patterns across the experimental groups are shown in Fig. 1F. The coordinated alterations observed across these pathways likely reflect an interplay among biological processes that contributes to the development of CYP-induced cystitis. For instance, disruption of linoleic acid metabolism gives rise to pro-inflammatory mediators and algogenic lipids, which can fuel inflammation and heighten pain sensitivity [25]. Meanwhile, accumulated citrate cycle intermediates (e.g., succinate) amplify inflammatory signaling and oxidative stress [26]. Ferroptosis pathway enrichment, indicated by increased oxidized glutathione and certain phospholipid derivatives, points to a role for lipid peroxidation-driven cell death in urothelial barrier dysfunction [27]. Altered sphingolipid metabolism might affect neuroimmune crosstalk and pain transmission via the ceramide/S1P axis [28]. We also noted elevated D-Gulono-1,4-lactone, an enantiomer of L-Gulono-1,4-lactone, which could reflect a compensatory antioxidant reaction to oxidative stress. Such a response has been tied to the onset of neurological conditions involving cognitive decline and neuroinflammation [29].

Collectively, these metabolic alterations suggest that oxidative stress, inflammation-associated cell death, and neural activation may form an interconnected pathogenic network that drives key features of IC [30]. Our results also revealed that ROS play a critical role in both the onset and progression of IC, as reported previously [7]. These insights also indicated that targeted modulation of the oxidative stress–inflammation–neural activation triad may serve as a novel strategy for controlling multiple pathological processes in IC. Herein, we developed TA-loaded nanotherapeutics for the targeted treatment of IC and alleviation of depression in mice by modulating the oxidative stress–inflammation–neural activation triad.

Preparation and characterization of the TA-loaded NPs

On the basis of the key role of oxidative stress in IC, we developed pH/ROS dual-responsive NPs for intravesical TA delivery to overcome the limitations of invasive administration. TA-loaded and cRGD-functionalized NPs were fabricated using a modified nanoprecipitation self-assembly method (Fig. 2A). DLS revealed that the average hydrodynamic diameters of the blank CA-αCD NPs, TA/CA-αCD NPs, and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs were 202 nm, 185 nm, and 189 nm, respectively (Fig. 2B). All NPs exhibited negative zeta potentials (from −16.4 to −21.8 mV) and a low polymer dispersity index (PDI) (PDI <0.2), indicating excellent colloidal stability and monodispersity (Fig. 2C–Table S1). TEM confirmed the spherical morphology of the NPs (Fig. 2D). The TA loading content was 12.5% in TA/CA-αCD NPs and 10.2% in TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs (Table S1, Supporting Information). These NPs remained stable in pure water or 10% FBS for 24 h (Fig. 2E and F). Drug release profiles revealed that TA release was significantly accelerated under acidic conditions (pH 5.0) and in H2O2 (0.5 mM) compared with in PBS (pH 7.4). Interestingly, TA release was obviously accelerated under acidic/ROS-rich conditions (pH 5.0 + 0.5 mM H2O2) (Fig. 2G and H). These results confirmed that the pH/ROS dual-responsive NPs may achieve controlled TA release in the pathological IC microenvironment. In vitro cell assays verified that at high TA concentrations (20 μg/mL), both free TA and TA-loaded NPs slightly reduced SV-HUC-1 cell viability (71.5 ± 4.6% for free TA, 74.8 ± 3.4% for TA/CA-αCD NPs, and 73.8 ± 3.6% for TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs), indicating the low cytotoxicity of both TA and TA-loaded NPs to SV-HUC-1 cells. However, all the TA-loaded NPs had minimal effects on macrophages (Figs. S2 and S3). In summary, we successfully developed NPs loaded with TA and modified with cRGD, which demonstrated favorable physicochemical properties. These NPs enabled controlled release of TA in response to acidic pH or elevated ROS levels and exhibited low cytotoxicity.

Cellular uptake and anti-inflammatory activity of TA-loaded NPs

Efficient cellular uptake of NPs is essential for achieving site-specific accumulation and sustained drug action at pathological sites [31]. To evaluate the cellular uptake of NPs by bladder-resident cells, we assessed the internalization of Cy5-labeled NPs by Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-treated SV-HUC-1 and RAW264.7 cells, which are widely used to simulate IC in vitro [32]. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) analysis revealed stronger Cy5 fluorescence signals from both the Cy5/CA-αCD NPs and Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs than the free Cy5 after 2 and 4 h of incubation (Fig. 3A and B). Semiquantitative analysis also confirmed that compared with the free Cy5, the Cy5-labeled NPs displayed a continuously greater fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3C and D). Interestingly, flow cytometry analysis also demonstrated consistently greater uptake of cRGD-modified NPs by RAW264.7 and SV-HUC-1 cells than non-targeted NPs (Fig. 3E). LPS-stimulated SV-HUC-1 and RAW264.7 cells were used to evaluate the anti-inflammatory and antiapoptotic effects of TA-loaded NPs. Western blot analysis confirmed that LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the expression of αvβ3 in both cell types compared with untreated controls (Fig. S5), indicating enhanced integrin availability in the inflamed bladder cell model. As shown in Fig. 3F, LPS stimulation significantly increased the apoptosis of these cells, whereas the normal control group exhibited minimal apoptosis. Among these treatments, compared with free TA, both TA-loaded NPs and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs displayed significantly increased anti-apoptotic activity (Fig. 3F). The expression of inflammatory mediators (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CXCL13) was markedly increased in SV-HUC-1 and RAW264.7 cells after LPS stimulation (Fig. 3G–J). Inflammatory mediators were slightly downregulated by treatment with blank NPs because blank NPs can release cinnamaldehyde, which has certain anti-inflammatory activity. As expected, the levels of all inflammatory mediators significantly decreased with TA treatment, demonstrating that TA clearly has anti-inflammatory activity. Compared with TA, TA-loaded NPs, especially TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs, distinctly downregulated inflammatory factor expression in LPS-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 3G–J, Fig. S6), indicating that the anti-inflammatory activity of TA was enhanced by the formulation of the NPs. This was further confirmed by Western blot analysis of IL-6 (Fig. 3K) and its quantitative results (Fig. 3L), as well as TNF-α (Fig. 3M) and its quantitative results (Fig. 3N), all of which showed a similar trend to the ELISA data. While LPS-stimulated cell model cannot fully mimic the immune microenvironment and chronic pathology of IC, this system enables reliable assessment of anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects in urothelial and immune cells. The bladder mucosa in IC exhibits prominent urothelial denudation in lesions, accompanied by aberrant cell fate and subepithelial inflammation [1,5]. Disruptions of the urothelial barrier allow urinary irritants and inflammatory substances to directly activate bladder afferents, likely driving pain and urgency/frequency [5,7]. The in vitro results suggested that TA-loaded NPs, particularly the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs displayed enhanced antiapoptotic and anti-inflammatory efficacy, which may ameliorate the pathogenesis of IC by simultaneously preserving urothelial integrity.

However, in vivo outcomes critically depend on the mucoadhesive retention and tissue penetration capabilities of therapeutics within the bladder [34]. Conventional intravesical administration is often limited by rapid urine clearance and the mucosal barrier, resulting in short residence times and low bioavailability [35]. Therefore, the mucoadhesion and retention efficacy of NPs in mice with IC should be evaluated.

Mucoadhesion and retention of NPs in mice with IC

To evaluate the mucoadhesive properties and retention efficacy of the NPs in mice with IC, a live imaging system was used to detect the biodistribution of the Cy5-labeled NPs in vivo. The observed area was the lower abdominal region around the bladder. As shown in Fig. 4A, free Cy5 administered via intravesical instillation showed rapid fluorescence attenuation, suggesting that the small-molecule dye was quickly removed from the urine. Compared with the fluorescence observed after treatment with free Cy5 and nontargeted NP, strong fluorescence intensity was observed in the urinary system of mice 2 h after the intravesical administration of cRGD-modified NPs, indicating that cRGD modification can enhance mucoadhesion and retention of NPs in the bladder (Fig. 4A). When animals were pre-treated with excess free cRGD peptides before administration of Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs, the fluorescence intensity in the bladder region was reduced compared with the group given Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs alone, highlighting the integrin-specific nature of the enhanced retention (Fig. 4A). To further investigate the biodistribution of the NPs in mice with IC, we sacrificed the animals 24 h after instillation and collected their bladders for imaging. In the free Cy5 group, ex vivo bladder tissue showed barely detectable fluorescence (Fig. 4F). In contrast, Cy5-labeled NPs showed clear fluorescence signals in the bladder tissues (Fig. 4F). Notably, among all treatments, the cRGD-modified NP group produced the strongest fluorescence intensity in bladder tissues (Fig. 4F). In line with in vivo data, fluorescence in the cRGD + Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs group was significantly lower than in the Cy5/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs group, confirming that free cRGD inhibits integrin-mediated retention (Fig. 4F). Cryosection imaging by CLSM further showed that cRGD-modified NPs reached the submucosa and muscularis layers, while the untargeted NPs stayed mostly in the superficial region and free Cy5 remained in the lumen (Fig. 4G). A semiquantitative analysis likewise showed higher fluorescence intensity in bladder tissue for the cRGD-modified NP group than for the others (Fig. 4H). Quantitative fluorescence analysis also confirmed that cRGD-modified NPs had greater mucoadhesion and deeper tissue penetration in bladder tissues (Fig. 4I). These findings likely reflect the strong affinity of cRGD for integrins that are overexpressed on inflamed cells [33,36]. This targeted drug delivery strategy enhances local drug retention in the bladder and overcomes the limitations of intravesical therapy. In addition, the accumulated NPs in the submucosal and muscular layers may contribute meaningfully to IC treatment, for example by acting on neural hyperplasia and chronic inflammation in the superficial mucosa [5,7,37]. In summary, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs can penetrate into bladder lesions, which may increase the efficacy of TA for treating IC.

Anti-inflammatory effects of TA-loaded NPs in mice with IC

As noted earlier, cRGD-modified NPs can improve bladder retention and enhance tissue penetration, so we went on to evaluate their anti-inflammatory effects in an IC murine model. C57BL/6 mice were given intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of 75 mg/kg CYP on days 1, 4, and 7 to establish the IC model [22,23]. On days 8, 9, and 10, the mice received various treatments via intravesical instillation, including PBS (CYP group), blank CA-αCD NPs (vehicle control), free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs (Fig. 5A). In the treatment groups, each mouse received an intravesical dose of TA at 5 mg/kg. Mice in the CYP group lost weight progressively, whereas those given free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs hit their lowest weight around day 8 before starting to recover gradually (Fig. 5B). Compared with mice in the CYP group, mice in all the TA-containing groups, especially the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP group, displayed a gradual increase in body weight (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, even mice given blank NPs regained some weight, hinting that the blank formulation had a modest therapeutic effect in this IC model. On day 12, these mice were euthanized and bladders collected for weighing. Bladders from the CYP group were the heaviest (Fig. 5C), consistent with marked edema and severe inflammation [38]. The bladder weight decreased with TA treatment, pointing to clear anti-inflammatory activity against IC (Fig. 5C). Notably, compared with that in the TA group, the bladder weight in the TA-loaded NP group was significantly lower, indicating that the anti-inflammatory activity of TA was enhanced via nanoplatform delivery (Fig. 5C). Hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemical analysis of the bladder tissue provided direct visual evidence of inflammatory severity. Mucosal thickness served as an objective indicator of bladder edema; we graphed the group-wise values in Fig. 5D, based on HE-stained sections from all groups (Fig. 5E(i)). Mice in the CYP group had a marked increase in mucosal thickness, with values 3.8 times greater than those in normal mice. Interestingly, the bladder mucosal thickness tended to gradually decrease in the blank CA-αCD NP, free TA, TA/CA-αCD NP, and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP treatment groups compared with that in the CYP group. Among these, blank CA-αCD NPs also brought about a small but measurable drop in thickness, which matched the known anti-inflammatory properties of the base material. Although the reduction in mucosal thickness was not significantly different between the free TA and TA/CA-αCD NP groups, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs demonstrated significantly greater efficacy than TA/CA-αCD NPs did. Notably, thickness in the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD group was close to that in normal mice, indicating that bladder morphology returned to normal after treatment. These phenomena are consistent with our indirect verification of bladder edema severity through bladder weight measurement.

Edema severity only gives a basic readout of inflammation. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of how TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs work, we carried out further analyses. Compared with normal mice, CYP group bladders showed progressive hemorrhagic lesions (black triangles) and epithelial denudation (black arrows) (Fig. 5E(i)). Clearly, these pathological changes improved to varying degrees in mice given free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs. These observations confirm that the TA-containing formulations effectively ameliorate bladder pathological alterations in IC. Furthermore, the pathological damage in the TA-loaded NP group was less severe than in the free TA group, pointing to better efficacy of TA when delivered via nanoplatform. Notably, mice in the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP group showed almost no difference from normal mice, suggesting successful normalization of bladder tissue after treatment (Fig. 5E(i)).

Mast cell infiltration and proinflammatory cytokines drive immune dysregulation and persistent symptoms in IC [30,39]. While initial histopathological analyses revealed improved bladder tissue damage, evaluating mast cell activity and cytokine levels was essential to fully elucidate the therapeutic mechanisms of TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs. Toluidine blue staining is a standard method for visualizing mast cells. Compared with normal control mice, mice in the CYP group exhibited substantial mast cell infiltration. Compared with the control treatment, the free TA and TA-loaded NPs markedly reduced mast cell infiltration. In contrast, blank CA-αCD NPs resulted in only a slight improvement, suggesting that while blank CA-αCD NPs have minor anti-inflammatory effects, the therapeutic benefit primarily stems from the TA payload itself. Importantly, the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs resulted in the most effective reduction in mast cell infiltration (Fig. 5E(ii)), which is consistent with our hypothesis regarding their targeted suppression of this key inflammatory driver in IC. Semiquantitative analysis further confirmed that compared with all the other groups, the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP group achieved the most pronounced reduction in mast cell infiltration within bladder tissue. These findings align with our direct histological observations, in which the blank CA-αCD NPs exhibited a minimal therapeutic effect, whereas the therapeutic efficacy progressively increased across the free TA, TA/CA-αCD NP, and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP groups. Notably, while compared with normal control mice, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP-treated mice showed slightly greater mast cell infiltration, this formulation achieved the greatest therapeutic effect, yielding nearly healthy bladder tissue (Fig. 5F).

Inflammation driven by proinflammatory cytokines plays a central role in the pathogenesis of IC, as evidenced by previous studies documenting their elevated expression in affected bladder tissues [40]. Our immunohistochemical analysis (visualizing IL-6, TNF-α and IL-1β as red triangles) revealed that these cytokines were significantly upregulated in mice with IC compared with normal controls (Fig. 5E(iii-v)), which is consistent with the characteristic inflammatory profile of this model. These findings prompted us to investigate whether TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs could modulate this cytokine network. While the free TA and TA-containing formulations demonstrated cytokine-suppressing effects, the cRGD-modified NPs exhibited superior performance in attenuating this inflammatory cascade. Specifically, TA-loaded NPs significantly reduced cytokine levels, but the decrease in cytokine levels was most pronounced in the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP group. In contrast, blank CA-αCD NPs showed only marginal effects, highlighting that the therapeutic benefit is derived primarily from the TA payload rather than the NP carrier itself. Notably, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs not only outperformed the other formulations but also resulted in cytokine levels closest to those observed in healthy bladder tissue, despite remaining slightly elevated compared with those in normal controls. This pattern mirrors our observations with mast cell infiltration, wherein this formulation achieved the most effective reduction. A semiquantitative analysis likewise showed that among all treatment groups, the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs suppressed cytokine levels the most, reinforcing the targeted anti-inflammatory action of the NPs in IC (Fig. 5G–I). Further analysis found that blank CA-αCD NPs lowered IL-1β only, while IL-6 and TNF-α remained largely unchanged compared with the control treatment, suggesting the therapeutic benefit came mainly from TA rather than the NP carrier. These findings add to the case that cRGD modification helps boost TA's therapeutic effect by delivering it more precisely. Taken together, these findings demonstrate that TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs exert substantial anti-inflammatory effects in vivo, alleviating edema, reducing immune cell infiltration, and suppressing proinflammatory cytokine expression. We then turned to examining whether these anti-inflammatory gains could translate into real relief of urinary symptoms and neuropsychiatric behaviors.

Mice with IC in the CYP group had a clearly lower pelvic pain threshold than normal mice, confirming that the pain model was successfully established. Blank CA-αCD NPs and free TA helped raise the pain threshold slightly, but the change was not statistically significant. These findings hint that while low-dose TA can reduce inflammatory factor levels, ease mast cell infiltration, and improve bladder mucosal morphology, these improvements do not yet lead to a macroscopic reduction in pain sensitivity. Compared with free TA, TA-loaded NPs showed better pain relief, with a clear rise in pain threshold versus the CYP group (Fig. 6B). Notably, among the TA-loaded formulations, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs resulted in even greater pain relief than TA/CA-αCD NPs did. Curiously, free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs all contained the same dosage of TA, yet their therapeutic effect rose stepwise. These findings demonstrate that the NP carrier itself can amplify the therapeutic effects of TA, with cRGD-modified targeted NPs showing the most pronounced enhancement.

The VSA was conducted in metabolic cages, as illustrated in Fig. 6C, with urinary behavior monitored in all the mice from 10:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m. The results from the VSA revealed that compared with normal mice, IC model mice exhibited a 2.58-fold increase in urinary frequency, with episode counts increasing from 3.8 to 9.8 during the observation period (Fig. 6D). Total voided volume over the 4-h period showed no significant difference between groups (Fig. S7). However, the voided volume per micturition (Fig. 6E) was significantly reduced in IC model mice (64.7 ± 7.7 μL) compared to normal controls (174.9 ± 32.8 μL). Interestingly, we observed that compared with that in IC model mice, the voiding frequency in all treatment groups gradually improved. Although compared with free TA (6.8 episodes; 96.2 ± 10.1 μL), TA/CA-αCD NPs (5.6 episodes; 116.8 ± 12.6 μL) further decreased the voiding frequency and increased the volume per micturition, this difference did not reach statistical significance. This lack of significant difference may be attributed to the delivery advantages of nontargeted NPs being offset by urine flushing interference [15]. In contrast, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs significantly improved both urination frequency (4.0 episodes) and single voided volume (161.9 ± 25.0 μL) compared to TA/CA-αCD NPs (5.6 episodes; 116.8 ± 12.6 μL). These findings suggest that compared with TA/CA-αCD NPs, the active targeting of TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs to inflammatory lesions effectively counteracts the negative impact of urine flushing. Importantly, the total urinary volume was unchanged across all the mouse groups, suggesting that it had no effect on overall urine production. Urodynamic analysis yielded results consistent with the voiding spot assay, showing a similar trend in improved voiding parameters, which further supports the reliability of the observed therapeutic effects on urinary function (Fig. S8). In summary, TA-loaded NPs, particularly TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs, effectively alleviated pelvic pain and normalized the urinary frequency in mice with IC.

TA-loaded NPs improve anxiety- and depression-like behaviors

We next investigated whether the efficacy of TA-loaded NPs in alleviating somatic symptoms extends to anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. The open field test (OFT) was used to evaluate anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in mice [43]. As illustrated in Fig. 6G, mice were tested in an OFT arena (50 cm × 50 cm), with the central and peripheral zones clearly marked. Surprisingly, the OFT results revealed no significant group differences in the total distance traveled (Fig. S9), confirming that locomotor activity remained unaffected. However, as shown in the OFT exploration heatmaps (Fig. 6H) and quantitative analyses (Figs. S10 and S11), mice with IC exhibited obvious anxiety- and depression-like behavior, characterized by central zone avoidance and reduced central-to-total exploration time. Specifically, they spent significantly less time in the central zone (Fig. 6H, Fig. S10) and had a lower central exploration ratio than normal mice did (Fig. S11). These findings indicate that while the locomotor function of IC model mice was unaffected, they displayed markedly reduced exploratory and locomotor drive. Interestingly, we also observed that mice treated with blank CA-αCD NPs showed a negligible improvement in exploratory behavior compared with IC model mice. In contrast, both free TA and TA-loaded formulations demonstrated therapeutic effects, suggesting that the anxiolytic and antidepressant benefits are derived primarily from the TA itself rather than the NP carrier. Notably, TA/CA-αCD NPs and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs both significantly enhanced the therapeutic effects of TA on exploratory behavior, with the efficacy of the cRGD-modified formulation being superior to that of the nontargeted TA/CA-αCD NPs (Fig. 6H, Figs. S10 and S11). Collectively, IC model mice showed normal locomotion but anxiety/depression-like behaviors characterized by central avoidance and less exploration. Among all treatments, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs resulted in the greatest improvement in exploratory behavior, demonstrating that cRGD-modified NPs can alleviate both somatic symptoms and mood disorders.

The elevated plus-maze (EPM) test [43], another well-established assay for assessing anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, yielded results consistent with the OFT findings. The mice were placed in the EPM apparatus to evaluate exploratory behavior. The EPM has a central platform with two open arms and two enclosed arms radiating from it (Fig. 6I). Compared with normal mice, IC model mice exhibited a striking reduction in exploration of the open arms, as demonstrated by significantly decreased time spent in and entries into these aversive zones (Fig. S12). Motion trajectory analysis and heatmaps (Fig. 6J) further revealed this avoidance behavior, confirming their anxiety-and depression-like behavior. Importantly, the open-arm exploration behavior in the TA/CA-αCD NPs and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs groups was significantly improved. In contrast to free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs provided better results, and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs delivered the most significant enhancement, clearly outperforming all the other formulations.

To validate the improvements in anxiety- and depression-like behaviors observed in the OFT and EPM test, we next carried out the forced swimming test (FST) and tail suspension test (TST). As expected, IC model mice in the FST and TST spent longer immobile (Fig. 6K–N), consistent with their anxiety/depression-like phenotype. Compared with control mice, all treated groups showed clear therapeutic effects. While both free TA and blank CA-αCD NPs resulted in measurable improvements, statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between them in the FST (Fig. 6L) and TST (Fig. 6N). This pattern might reflect the intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties of blank CA-αCD NPs. Furthermore, both TA/CA-αCD NPs and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs cut immobility time substantially. Of note, immobility levels in the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP group were close to normal in both the FST (Fig. 6L) and TST (Fig. 6N), pointing to a successful easing of depression-like symptoms after treatment.

In summary, these comprehensive behavioral evaluations demonstrated that TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs exhibit remarkable therapeutic efficacy in ameliorating anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in mice with IC. Notably, the cRGD-mediated active targeting mechanism confers significantly superior efficacy compared with that of nontargeted formulations, thereby confirming that our TA delivery system effectively synergizes the management of both somatic symptoms and mood disorders.

To identify potential metabolic mediators of these effects, we next conducted a metabolomic analysis across treatment groups to identify differentially abundant metabolites associated with therapeutic outcomes. A clear pattern of change was observed for 12,13-dihydroxy-octadecadienoic acid (12,13-DiHOME) and D-gulono-1,4-lactone across various treatment groups (Fig. 7C). To further elucidate the intrinsic metabolic response patterns without classification bias, we ran an unsupervised K-means clustering on the 1609 detected metabolites, grouping them into 9 distinct clusters (Fig. 7D, Fig. S13). Cluster 8 had the clearest link to therapeutic outcomes, indicating treatment-responsive metabolic reprogramming among its constituent metabolites. Specifically, as shown in the heatmap, the top 30 most dynamically altered metabolites of cluster 8 exhibited distinct expression patterns across treatment groups (Fig. 7E). Within this set, the patterns of D-gulono-1,4-lactone and 12,13-DiHOME still showed consistent positive correlations with the observed therapeutic outcomes, highlighting their potential as core metabolic mediators.

The strong correlations of D-gulono-1,4-lactone and 12,13-DiHOME with therapeutic outcomes underscore their role as potential metabolic nodes, which require further exploration. 12,13-DiHOME itself can exacerbate inflammatory pain by amplifying transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1)-dependent thermal hyperalgesia [44]. Although its levels differed between the CYP and free TA groups, TA, TA/CA-αCD NP, and TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NP groups did not differ noticeably, indicating a weak link to treatment efficacy (Fig. 7F). More notably, D-gulono-1,4-lactone showed larger changes across the experimental groups (Fig. 7G). Furthermore, similar to D-gulono-1,5-lactone, D-gulono-1,4-lactone serves as an intermediate in gluconate biosynthesis and is typically rapidly converted to gluconate [45]. Metabolomic profiling revealed that compared with those in normal mice, D-gulono-1,4-lactone levels in IC model mice were significantly elevated, suggesting a potential association with disease pathogenesis. Treatment with free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs lowered D-gulono-1,4-lactone levels progressively: mild with free TA, moderate with TA/CA-αCD NPs, and the largest drop with TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs. Interestingly, although the decrease in the TA/CA-αCD NP group was greater than that in the free TA group, the difference was not statistically significant. In contrast, the TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs group showed the greatest decrease in this metabolite, outperforming both free TA and TA/CA-αCD NPs. These results add to the evidence that cRGD-modified NPs can enhance TA's biological efficacy. This gradient of D-gulono-1,4-lactone modulation aligns closely with the previously observed symptom improvement across treatments, further supporting a strong correlation between D-gulono-1,4-lactone and IC symptom severity.

D-gulono-1,4-lactone as a mediator linking bladder inflammation to neuropsychiatric comorbidities

D-gulono-1,4-lactone is consistently associated with IC progression and treatment efficacy, suggesting that it has systemic effects beyond the bladder. These findings prompted us to investigate whether D-gulono-1,4-lactone drives systemic inflammatory responses through metabolic dysregulation, thereby influencing neuropsychiatric states. Given the key role of hippocampal neuroinflammation in anxiety and depression [46], we investigated whether TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs could alleviate CNS inflammation driven by metabolic dysregulation. Previous research has demonstrated that D-gulono-1,4-lactone exerts antioxidant activity and functions as a metabolic intermediate in the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), contributing to gluconate biosynthesis [45]. Under inflammatory conditions, PPP activation serves as a physiological defense mechanism by generating NADPH, the primary cellular reducing equivalent required for antioxidant responses, to counteract oxidative stress induced by inflammation [47]. However, pathological metabolic dysregulation disrupts this homeostatic balance, leading to the aberrant accumulation of these lactone metabolites. Its levels not only reflect the severity of IC but also correlate closely with treatment outcomes. To validate this, we stained hippocampal sections for immunofluorescence to quantify neuroinflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 8B). Hippocampal neuroinflammation was assessed by immunostaining for glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP, an astrocyte marker) and ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (Iba1, a microglial marker). The data showed that IC model mice had far more activated microglia and astrocytes in the hippocampus than normal mice did (Fig. 8B). TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs markedly dampened hippocampal glial cell activation. While both free TA and TA-loaded NPs alleviated neuroinflammation, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs strongly inhibited microglial and astrocyte activation (Fig. 8B). These observations aligned with the findings that TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs alleviated anxiety/depressive-like behaviors (Fig. 6G–N) and trimmed pathological D-gulono-1,4-lactone accumulation (Fig. 7G), suggesting that these NPs have the potential to limit the entry of hazardous substances into the systemic circulation and across the blood–brain barrier. We next quantified the immunofluorescence signals. The percentage of Iba1-positive cells is shown in Fig. 8C, and that of GFAP-positive cells in Fig. 8D. Interestingly, we found that treatment with free TA, TA/CA-αCD NPs, or TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs lowered the proportions of Iba1-positive and GFAP-positive cells. Among these, free TA showed the least reduction, while TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs demonstrated the greatest reduction, reaching levels closest to those in the normal group. These findings indicate that TA effectively reduces the infiltration of neuroinflammatory cells in the hippocampus and that NP carriers, particularly cRGD-modified targeted NPs, can enhance this effect. Crucially, the severity of neuroinflammation, as reflected by the infiltration of activated GFAP+/Iba1+ cells, strongly positively correlated with D-gulono-1,4-lactone levels. Concurrently, treatment with TA-loaded NPs significantly reduced both the proportion of GFAP+/Iba1+ cells and the level of D-gulono-1,4-lactone. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that D-gulono-1,4-lactone serves as a critical mediator linking bladder inflammation to neuropsychiatric comorbidities by driving neuroinflammation in the hippocampus via metabolic dysregulation.

The TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs show promise for IC therapy, yet key limitations constrain clinical translation. The chemical-induced IC model fails to recapitulate the autoimmune heterogeneity or sex bias characteristic of human IC. Long-term toxicological profiling and sex-stratified safety assessments are absent, weakening the translational viability of the nanoformulations. The association between D-gulono-1,4-lactone and hippocampal homeostasis remains correlational, as the molecular signaling pathways remain uncharacterized. Future studies must validate the safety and efficacy of TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs in physiologically relevant models, integrate longitudinal and sex-inclusive experimental designs, and delineate the molecular targets of D-gulono-1,4-lactone within the “bladder-brain” axis.

Conclusion

This work points to a bladder–metabolic–brain axis in IC, where disturbed D-gulono-1,4-lactone links bladder inflammation with neuropsychiatric comorbidities. Metabolomic analysis let us pinpoint this metabolite as a key mediator in IC progression and how patients respond to treatment. To tackle poor drug delivery, we developed TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs for the targeted intravesical delivery of TA. These NPs boosted mucoadhesion, tissue penetration and responsive drug release, helping cut bladder inflammation, mast cell infiltration and urinary symptoms. Importantly, TA/cRGD-CA-αCD NPs also alleviated anxiety- and depression-like behaviors by damping the hippocampal neuroinflammation triggered by microglial and astrocytic activation. This therapeutic effect was associated with the normalization of D-Gulono-1,4-lactone levels. Our findings suggest that targeting this bladder–metabolic–brain axis can simultaneously ease both bladder pathology and neuropsychiatric symptoms, offering a promising strategy for treating IC.
