Resection versus cordal implantation for degenerative mitral regurgitation: Devil in the details
Authors: Christopher Rumer, James S. Gammie, Rachael Quinn
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Lang and colleagues1 provide a thought-provoking 15-year analysis of mitral valve repair outcomes from the German Heart Center Munich. They report that artificial cordal repair (ACR) was associated with inferior long-term durability, including significantly greater rates of more-than-moderate recurrent mitral regurgitation (MR) and reoperation compared with leaflet resection (32.7% vs 10.1% at 15 years). Although these findings contribute to the debate around optimal surgical techniques for repairing degenerative mitral regurgitation (DMR), the authors’ results may be related to technical aspects of cordal implantation.

Lang and colleagues report recurrent greater-than-moderate MR in one third and reoperation in almost 20% of their patients undergoing artificial cordal repair (MR greater than mild in 4.3% at discharge). This is in contrast with other series of nonresectional mitral valve repairs, with reoperation rates of 4.2% and recurrent MR rates of 14.1% at 20 years.2 We and others have reported that a standardized, nonresectional approach facilitates high repair rates and yields excellent durability.2,3 It may be that the less-than-optimal results reported in the Munich series are related to the surgical techniques of cordal replacement.

The normal human mitral valve has 25 to 30 primary chordae tendinae, and we believe that an expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE)-based repair works best when respecting this natural architecture. Our rule of thumb is to space ePTFE cords every 3 to 4 mm along the free edge of any prolapsed leaflet segment. The authors report that patients in their series received a mean of 2.7 artificial cords. In our institutional experience, we used a median of 4 pairs of ePTFE per patient with 95% freedom from repair failure at 5 years,3 and among patients with Barlow valve currently implant median of 7 pairs per patient.4 David uses a running loop technique and effectively implants a mean 8 cords/patient2 whereas the nonresectional technique of Lawrie and colleagues uses a mean 8.8 cords per patient with freedom from reoperation of 96% and freedom from severe MR of 94%.5 Results in these series were excellent and contribute to our hypothesis that there is a signal between durability of ACR and the number of cords implanted.

In conclusion, experienced mitral valve repair surgeons can achieve effective repair of DMR using either resectional and/or artificial cordal approaches. ACR yields a substantially larger surface of coaptation, which is an important predictor of repair durability. With experience, surgeons have evolved to using more, rather than fewer, ePTFE cords.2, 3, 4, 5 Clearly more information is needed to inform best practices in DMR repair.
