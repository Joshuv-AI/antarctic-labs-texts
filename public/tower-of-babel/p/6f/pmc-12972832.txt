Spirituality and Happiness in Haemodialysis Patients: The Mediating Roles of Life Attitude and Posttraumatic Growth
Authors: Hsiang‐Chun Lee, Yi‐Chien Chiang, Kun‐Hung Tsai, Chizimuzo Okoli, Tsung‐Lan Chu, Ya‐Chu Hsiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo examine the relationships among spirituality, happiness, life attitude and posttraumatic growth in haemodialysis patients, and to test whether life attitude and posttraumatic growth acted as potential mediators in the association between spirituality and happiness.DesignA cross‐sectional correlational study.MethodsWe recruited 299 maintenance haemodialysis patients from nine dialysis clinics in Taiwan using convenience sampling. Participants completed validated self‐report questionnaires measuring spirituality, happiness, life attitude and posttraumatic growth. Pearson's correlation coefficients and mediation analyses using the PROCESS 4.2 macro for SPSS were conducted to test associations and mediating effects.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 60.90 years (SD = 12.39), and the average duration of haemodialysis was 5.32 years (SD = 5.30). Spirituality was significantly positively correlated with happiness, life attitude, and posttraumatic growth (r = 0.571, 0.643 and 0.473; all p < 0.001). Mediation analysis revealed a significant total effect of spirituality on happiness (B = 0.196, 95% CI [0.164, 0.228]) and a direct effect (B = 0.060, 95% CI [0.024, 0.096]). Life attitude and posttraumatic growth significantly mediated this relationship (B = 0.082, 95% CI [0.055, 0.110]) and (B = 0.054, 95% CI [0.035, 0.075]), respectively.ConclusionsSpirituality is associated with higher happiness in haemodialysis patients, and this pattern was consistent with potential indirect pathways via positive life attitudes and promoting posttraumatic growth. These findings offer insight into the psychological mechanisms linking spirituality and well‐being in chronically ill patients.ImpactIntegrating spiritual assessment and tailored interventions into clinical care may be relevant for psychological well‐being and overall quality of life in this population. This highlights the importance of addressing spiritual dimensions in chronic care settings.Reporting MethodStudy methods and results were reported in accordance with the STROBE checklist.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06098924Keywords: haemodialysis, happiness, life attitude, posttraumatic growth, spirituality

Introduction

According to the United States Renal Data System (2023), the global prevalence of End State Renal Disease (ESRD) continues to rise. Taiwan ranks among the top nations worldwide in both the incidence and prevalence of patients treated for end‐stage renal disease, with approximately 90% of these patients receiving regular haemodialysis. Additionally, medical expenses for dialysis account for approximately 8.6%–8.9% of total National Health Insurance (NHI) expenditures, representing about 24.6 times the average per capita NHI spending (National Health Research Institutes and Taiwan Society of Nephrology 2024). Patients with ESRD experience high rates of depression (64.2%), anxiety (36.2%), and other psychological issues (Fradelos et al. 2021; Khalil et al. 2022). Although prior studies have focused extensively on negative psychological outcomes in this population, positive psychological outcomes, particularly happiness, remain underexplored. This gap in the literature limits the development of targeted supportive nursing strategies for managing ESRD.

Among positive psychological determinants of health, happiness is often considered one of the most important goals in life. Defined as an emotion characterised by joy, gladness, satisfaction and well‐being (American Psychological Association 2014), happiness is increasingly recognised as a critical outcome for patients with chronic illness. According to the Integrated Model of the Determinants of Happiness (Singh et al. 2023), happiness is influenced by health, hope, and harmony. Among these influences, health has been identified as a foundational determinant (Steptoe et al. 2015). For patients with ESRD, the physical burden of symptoms, lifelong dependence on haemodialysis, and strict lifestyle limitations can lead to psychological distress and a diminished sense of well‐being (Machaca‐Choque et al. 2024), potentially impairing the experience of happiness.

To provide a theoretical basis for this study, we draw upon Reed's Self‐Transcendence Theory (2024), which posits that individuals facing vulnerabilities, such as chronic illness, can enhance their well‐being by expanding the boundaries of the self through introspection, interpersonal connection, and spiritual awareness. Within this framework, spirituality functions as a key resource that facilitates growth, encourages re‐evaluation of life purpose (captured as life attitude), and supports positive transformation in response to adversity (captured as PTG).

Literature Review

Spirituality is a multidimensional human experience involving the pursuit and expression of meaning, purpose, and connection with oneself, others, nature and a transcendent entity (Chagas et al. 2023; Hsiao et al. 2013). Spirituality is a significant coping strategy for patients with ESRD by promoting acceptance of their disease and adhering to prescribed treatment, while also alleviating psychological distress and improving quality of life (Ling et al. 2021). However, few studies have examined spirituality's contribution to happiness for patients with ESRD, and even fewer have explored the underlying psychological mechanisms through which this relationship might operate.

In further understanding key influences on spirituality and happiness, life attitude is a focus of study. Life attitude reflects a person's sense of and motivation to find purpose and meaning in life (Reker et al. 1987). A positive life attitude is shaped by factors such as communication in families, relationships with peers, social capital and predicts psychological well‐being (Malinowska‐Cieślik et al. 2019; Wolfram 2023). Life attitude is associated with greater spirituality (Ivtzan et al. 2013) and is positively associated with happiness (Li et al. 2021). However, it is unknown how life attitude is associated with spirituality and happiness among patients with ESRD.

Another important factor associated with spirituality and happiness is posttraumatic growth (PTG). PTG refers to positive changes or ‘growth’ resulting from the experience of adversity (Tedeschi and Calhoun 1996; Li et al. 2021). PTG is influenced by the extent to which traumatic events challenge an individual's fundamental assumptions about the world's benevolence, controllability, and predictability (Tedeschi and Calhoun 2004). PTG is associated with spirituality (Casellas‐Grau et al. 2017) and improves a person's sense of happiness (Mostarac and Brajković 2022). Thus, examining the relationship between PTG, spirituality and happiness is crucial in determining ways to improve quality of life and treatment among patients with ESRD.

Therefore, this study proposes that spirituality may be associated with higher happiness through its influence on life attitude and PTG. Accordingly, this study aimed to explore the relationships among spirituality, happiness, life attitude, and PTG in patients undergoing haemodialysis, and to test the mediating roles of life attitude and PTG simultaneously in a parallel mediation model (i.e., estimating the two specific indirect effects while adjusting for the other mediator), based on the following hypotheses (as shown in Figure 1):

Spirituality would be associated with happiness, life attitude, and PTG.

Life attitude would mediate the relationship between spirituality and happiness.

PTG would mediate the relationship between spirituality and happiness.

Proposed model for hypothesis testing.

Exploring these mediating roles provides insight into how inner psychological strengths may be associated with better adaption to the challenges of ESRD and support patients' well‐being.

Methods

Design

This was a cross‐sectional correlational study. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement guidelines were used to report this study.

Aims

The aim of this study was to examine the relationships among spirituality, happiness, life attitude and posttraumatic growth (PTG) in patients undergoing haemodialysis. Specifically, the study tested a hypothesized mediation model in which life attitude and PTG were examined as potential mediators of the association between spirituality and happiness.

Sample and Participants

Patients undergoing haemodialysis were recruited using convenience sampling from nine dialysis clinics in Taiwan. Patients were eligible to participate if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged over 20 years; (2) had been receiving haemodialysis for more than 3 months; (3) were able to communicate and complete the questionnaires; and (4) provided informed consent to participate. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) a diagnosis of cognitive impairment or severe psychiatric illness (e.g., schizophrenia), (2) currently taking antidepressant medications, or (3) hospitalisation or undergoing acute medical interventions during the data collection period. Patients on antidepressants were excluded because pharmacologic treatment may independently influence affective outcomes such as happiness, which was the primary outcome variable in this study. A total of 320 patients met the inclusion criteria and were invited to participate; 21 declined, citing reasons such as fatigue or a preference not to interrupt their treatment. In total, 299 patients agreed to participate and provided signed informed consent, yielding a response rate was 93.4%.

The sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1.9.2 software, based on Cohen's (1988) recommendations that an effect size f 2 = 0.15 represents a moderate effect in behavioural sciences research. A total of three primary predictors (spirituality, life attitude, PTG) and six covariates based on relevant literature (age, gender, education, marital status, dialysis duration and comorbidities) were included in the model. With α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 114. A larger sample was ultimately recruited to improve the statistical robustness and generalizability of the findings. For a conservative power estimate, we assumed inclusion of all candidate covariates; however, the final model retained only years of education based on the prespecified screening procedure.

Instruments

Spiritual Health Scale‐Short Form

Spirituality was assessed with the 24‐item Spiritual Health Scale‐short form, developed by Hsiao et al. (2013) for individuals in Taiwan. The self‐report instrument is comprised of five subscales, each representing a dimension of spirituality: connection to others, meaning derived from living, transcendence, religious attachment and self‐understanding. Items are scored on a 5‐point Likert scale with responses ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree); total scores range from 24 to 120 with higher scores indicating better spirituality. The Cronbach's alpha for the total scale is 0.93 (Hsiao et al. 2013). In this study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.94.

Chinese Happiness Inventory

The variable of happiness was assessed with the 10‐item Chinese Happiness Inventory (CHI; Lu 2010), which evaluates positive affect, life satisfaction, and interpersonal relationships through self‐reported statements. The scale has been demonstrated to be correlated with measures of friendship, positive emotions, social support, self‐acceptance, and self‐reported health status. Statements are scored on a 4‐point Likert scale from 0 (strongly disagree) to 3 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 0 to 30; higher total scores indicate greater happiness. The scale has been validated across different groups, showing internal consistency ranging from 0.92 to 0.94 and a one‐month test–retest reliability coefficient of 0.66 (Lu 2010). In this study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.91.

Life Attitudes Profile

Attitudes towards life were assessed with the 39‐item Life Attitude Profile developed by Ho (1990). Each item is a statement about one's perspective towards life's meaning and purpose and one's motivation to find meaning and purpose in life. The profile assesses six dimensions of life attitude: life purpose, existential vacuum, will to meaning, life control, death acceptance and suffering acceptance. Items are scored on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Total scores range from 39 to 195 with higher scores indicating a more positive attitude towards life. Cronbach's alphas for the six subscales range from 0.65 to 0.87, with a 3‐week test–retest reliability coefficient of 0.85 (Ho 1990). In this study, Cronbach's alphas ranged from 0.79 to 0.95.

Posttraumatic Growth Inventory

PTG was assessed with the 25‐item expanded version of the posttraumatic growth inventory developed by Tedeschi et al. (2017). The self‐report scale assesses positive outcomes experienced by individuals coping with a traumatic event and reconstructing their life. Each item is a statement about changes related to a trauma event. Items are scored on a 6‐point Likert scale for the perceived amount of change associated with long‐term haemodialysis from 0 (I did not experience any change) to 5 (I experienced a significant degree of change). Total scores range from 0 to 125; higher scores indicate a greater level of PTG. This study used the Mandarin version of the expanded PTG scale developed for cancer survivors in Taiwan (Ho et al. 2022), which has good reliability as demonstrated by a Cronbach's alpha of 0.96. In this study, Cronbach's alpha was 0.96.

Demographic Information

Demographic Information: Included age, gender, years of education, marital status, years of haemodialysis and comorbidities.

Data Collection

Participants who consented to join the study were given the option to complete the questionnaires during their clinic visit at a time that suited their schedule. Each participant received a packet of self‐report questionnaires. For those unable to complete the forms independently, members of the research team assisted by reading each question aloud and recording their responses. Completing the questionnaire took approximately 20–30 min. Data collection occurred between September 2023 and May 2024.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Version 24 (IBM Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were utilised to summarise the demographic and clinical characteristics of participants using the mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages (n, %) for categorical variables. Scores for the psychological variables of spirituality, happiness, life attitude, and PTG were also reported as the mean and SD.

The hypothesized parallel mediation model (Figure 1) was tested using the PROCESS macro (Model 4; Version 4.2) with bias‐corrected bootstrapping (5000 resamples) to estimate specific indirect effects and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) (Hayes 2013; Preacher and Hayes 2008). Indirect effects were considered statistically significant when the bias‐corrected 95% CI did not include zero (Hayes 2013). Unstandardized coefficients (B) are reported as the primary effect estimates, whereas standardised coefficients (β) are provided in Table 3 for reference. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs).

Ethical Considerations

Results

Participant Characteristics

The mean age of the 299 participants was 60.90 years (SD = 12.39); 59.9% were < 65 years old; slightly more than half were male (n = 173, 57.9%); and 59.5% were married. The duration of haemodialysis treatment ranged from 3 months to ≥ 5 years, with a mean duration of 5.32 years (SD = 5.3). Most participants reported having at least one medical comorbidity (n = 262, 87.6%). Characteristics of participants are summarised in Table 1.

Mediation Analysis

To identify potential covariates, we screened sociodemographic variables (age, gender, marital status and years of education) and clinical variables (dialysis duration and comorbidities) for bivariate associations with happiness. Age, gender, marital status, dialysis duration, and comorbidities were not significantly associated with happiness (all p > 0.05) and were therefore excluded from the final model to ensure parsimony. In contrast, years of education were significantly associated with happiness (F = 5.615, p = 0.004) and were retained as a covariate. Because depressive symptoms and social support are plausible confounders but were not assessed in this study, they could not be adjusted for; this is acknowledged as a limitation.

Before testing the mediation model, collinearity diagnostics were performed. The variance inflation factors (VIFs) for all predictors ranged from 1.11 to 1.87, which were well below the threshold of 5 (Hair et al. 2019), indicating that multicollinearity was not a concern.

The final mediation model is shown in Table 3 and Figure 2. Spirituality demonstrated a significant total effect on happiness (B = 0.196, 95% CI [0.164, 0.228], p < 0.001) and a significant direct effect (B = 0.060, 95% CI [0.024, 0.096], p = 0.001). Spirituality also significantly predicted life attitude (B = 0.830, 95% CI [0.718, 0.941], p < 0.001) and PTG (B = 0.761, 95% CI [0.607, 0.914], p < 0.001). In turn, both life attitude (B = 0.099, 95% CI [0.071, 0.127], p < 0.001) and PTG (B = 0.071, 95% CI [0.051, 0.091], p < 0.001) had significant direct effects on happiness.

Discussion

Our study has two notable strengths. First, it is the first to examine and demonstrate the interrelationships among the psychological variables of spirituality, happiness, life attitude, and PTG in patients undergoing haemodialysis treatment, with a specific focus on the positive psychological construct of happiness. Second, the results reveal that spirituality had a statistically significant positive association with happiness, both directly and indirectly, through the mediating roles of life attitude and PTG.

The significant associations among all primary study variables support our first hypothesis, which proposed that spirituality would be positively associated with happiness, life attitude and PTG. These findings are consistent with previous research. For instance, a positive relationship between spirituality/religiosity and happiness has been demonstrated among Brazilian haemodialysis patients (Siqueira et al. 2019). Similarly, spirituality has been shown to predict higher levels of PTG and better psychological adjustment in patients with cancer (Yang et al. 2023). In addition, life attitude has been positively associated with greater subjective well‐being (Li et al. 2021), while PTG has been linked to improved health‐related quality of life among cancer survivors, highlighting its role in fostering adaptive coping (Liu et al. 2020). Collectively, these findings underscore the interconnectedness of spirituality, happiness, life attitude and PTG, as proposed in our theoretical model.

These findings align with Reed's Self‐Transcendence Theory (2024), which posits that individuals facing chronic health vulnerabilities can enhance their well‐being by expanding their inner sense of meaning, purpose and spiritual connectedness. Within this framework, life attitude and PTG serve as psychological expressions of such self‐transcendence, with life attitude reflecting an existential‐evaluative orientation and PTG reflecting growth‐related change following adversity. Furthermore, the results imply that spirituality may promote happiness by fostering a purposeful and meaningful life attitude, consistent with Frankl's existential theory (1963) and Park's (2010) meaning systems framework. Spirituality may also promote psychological growth by helping individuals reframe traumatic experiences in a more constructive and meaningful way. Patients undergoing haemodialysis who report higher levels of spirituality may adopt more positive attitudes towards life and reinterpret illness as an opportunity for growth, which was associated with higher happiness through the indirect roles of life attitude and PTG. Thus, our findings not only echo earlier theoretical work, such as Reed's Self‐Transcendence Theory (2024), Park's (2010) meaning systems model and Koenig's (2012) review of health‐related pathway but also fill a gap in the empirical literature by demonstrating how spirituality supports long‐term psychological adaptation in the context of chronic illness.

In addition, although spirituality, life attitude, and PTG were moderately intercorrelated, they are theoretically distinguishable constructs and were modelled as parallel mediators for different conceptual reasons. Spirituality reflects perceived connection to the sacred/transcendent and meaning beyond the self (Reed 2024). Life attitude captures an existential‐evaluative stance towards life (e.g., meaning/purpose and acceptance) rather than a trauma‐related change process (King and Hicks 2021; Costin and Vignoles 2020). In contrast, PTG refers to positive psychological changes that may emerge from struggling with highly challenging events (Tedeschi and Calhoun 2004). Thus, spirituality may shape a meaningful life attitude, whereas PTG reflects growth‐related outcomes through coping and reappraisal. Collinearity diagnostics further suggested that overlap was unlikely to distort estimates (VIFs = 1.11–1.87), supporting the appropriateness of modelling these mediators in parallel.

Although our findings supported all three hypotheses, the relatively low levels of happiness and PTG observed in this sample warrant further attention. The mean score of happiness in our study (M = 11.42, SD = 6.48), suggests a moderate‐to‐low level of well‐being within the score's possible range (0–30). While direct comparisons are difficult due to differing instruments, this level appears notably lower than general population norms reported in Taiwan. For instance, recent national data consistently rank Taiwan's general happiness levels as the highest in East Asia (e.g., scoring ~6.5/10 in the World Happiness Report; Helliwell et al. 2024), and community‐dwelling older adults in Taiwan typically report higher CHI scores (e.g., M = 15–17; Chen et al. 2023) compared to our sample. This discrepancy potentially reflects the specific burdens of haemodialysis.

Furthermore, international comparisons suggest our participants experienced lower happiness even within the context of chronic illness. For instance, Siqueira et al. (2019) reported relatively high well‐being (M = 19.7, range 4–28) among Brazilian haemodialysis patients. Although differing scales preclude statistical comparison, this contrast with our sample's moderate‐to‐low levels likely reflects cross‐cultural differences in emotional expression or specific unmeasured burdens, underscoring the need for targeted interventions.

A similar pattern was observed for PTG. Although the PTG score in this study was comparable to that reported among patients with chronic kidney failure (Aykut et al. 2024), it was considerably lower than scores reported in patients with heart disease (Zahra et al. 2024) and breast cancer (Wan et al. 2024). These variations may reflect differences in illness trajectory and psychological processing. Notably, haemodialysis is a lifelong treatment without a definitive endpoint. Distinct from patients with heart disease or cancer who may complete treatment and identify as ‘survivors’, those undergoing dialysis often face ongoing physical, emotional, and social challenges with no expectation of full recovery. This chronicity and lack of closure may hinder opportunities for processing trauma and sustaining PTG. Future programmes should integrate ongoing spiritual care, continuous meaning making opportunities, and supportive dialogue tailored to the lived experience of haemodialysis patients (Alexandru et al. 2019; Yangöz et al. 2025).

From a clinical perspective, nurses should play a proactive role in assessing patients' spiritual needs, facilitating meaning‐centred conversations, and integrating emotional and spiritual support into routine care for individuals undergoing haemodialysis. Providing integrative care plans that address life attitude and the emotional impact of chronic illness may help patients find meaning in their disease journey and improve their overall well‐being. However, future longitudinal studies are also needed to explore how these variables evolve over time and to identify sustained predictors of happiness and PTG in patients undergoing long‐term haemodialysis. Such studies will be important in guiding nursing interventions to deliver holistic models of care for ESRD patients undergoing dialysis treatment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross‐sectional design limits the ability to draw causal inferences among variables; longitudinal studies are needed to explore these relationships over time. Second, the use of convenience sampling and the exclusion of participants taking antidepressant medications may limit the generalizability of the findings. While this exclusion criterion was necessary to prevent pharmacological confounding of the happiness measure, it likely resulted in an underestimation of psychological distress and means our findings may not apply to patients with clinically diagnosed severe depression, who might benefit most from spiritual interventions. In addition, non‐participation due to fatigue or treatment preferences may have introduced selection bias, potentially underrepresenting individuals with greater physical or psychological distress. Although participants were recruited from nine haemodialysis units across Taiwan, the sample may not fully capture the diversity of the broader haemodialysis population globally. Third, while several sociodemographic and clinical variables were examined for potential association with happiness, only years of education showed a statistically significant relationship and was included as a covariate in the final mediation model. However, unmeasured or residual confounding due to other unexamined factors cannot be entirely ruled out. Lastly, psychological variables were assessed using self‐report instruments, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability effects, potentially limiting the accuracy and objectivity of the responses. Although VIFs suggested limited multicollinearity, the constructs were moderately intercorrelated; future studies should further evaluate discriminant validity and test alternative specifications (e.g., sequential mediation) using longitudinal data.

Conclusions

Patients with ESRD undergoing lifelong haemodialysis frequently experience substantial physical, psychological and spiritual distress, which may be related to reduced levels of happiness. This study contributes to the existing literature by demonstrating that higher levels of spirituality were significantly associated with greater happiness among haemodialysis patients, with results consistent with potential indirectly pathway through life attitude and PTG. In addition, the study findings indicated low levels of happiness and PTG in patients undergoing haemodialysis.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

Based on this study finding, nurses may consider incorporating spiritual assessment and meaning‐focused supportive strategies into routine care. Such approaches may help support patients' well‐being and could be explored in future longitudinal or interventional studies. In addition, we suggest that assessing patients' spiritual needs and offering supportive strategies that encourage a positive life attitude and facilitate posttraumatic growth may be associated with higher happiness among patients undergoing haemodialysis. These strategies may also be associated with greater happiness and better quality of life in this population.
