Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) has defined “zero hunger” and “good health and well-being” as sustainable development goals (SDGs) two and three [1]. Ensuring a sustainable supply of nutrients is a key contribution to achieving these goals. Amino acids (AAs) represent a core group of nutrients, as they serve critical roles in cell metabolism and regulation, which is why their scalable and sustainable manufacturing is highly relevant. For example, ornithine is relevant in nitrogen metabolism [2], γ-amino-butyric acid (GABA) is a neurotransmitter [3] and plants produce over 250 different non-proteinogenic AAs of different function [4]. However, native proteins consist almost exclusively of 22 proteinogenic α-L-amino acids, of which 20 are encoded in the DNA, while two are incorporated via specialized translation mechanisms [5].

Commercial interest, especially in proteinogenic AAs, emerged in the 20th century with monosodium glutamate, the sodium salt of glutamic acid (Glu, E), being first used as a flavor enhancer in 1908 [6]. Until 2025, the global AA market was estimated to be 12.3 million metric tons with an expected annual growth rate of 3.80% [7], suggesting over-proportional growth in demand when compared to the annual population growth rate of approximately 0.85% [8]. The projected total market value for 2025 is >25 billion EUR [9] with food, feed and biotechnology being major sub-markets in descending order of absorbed AA mass (Table 1). In this review, we address the current bottlenecks and innovations in biotechnological amino acid production. First, we discuss amino acid market distribution across major applications and individual amino acids. Next, we analyze different sources of amino acids with a focus on their implications for subsequent downstream processing. Following that, we introduce the typical unit operations used for the purification of amino acids, assess properties such as recovery, purity, cost, scalability and speed. Thereafter, we compare the performance of entire manufacturing processes, highlighting potential limitations and advantages. Finally, we put a spotlight on new techniques that can streamline amino acid purification and bring down manufacturing costs. We would like to point out that, due to the strong industry relevance of the topic, a substantial fraction of the relevant data was contained in patents. As these documents typically do not undergo peer-review, we treated the according information with caution.

Amino acid markets and applications

Food applications

The prime application of AAs is food. Based on mass, the Glu market is the largest for a single AA, accounting for approximately 40% of the total global AA sales [13], primarily due to the production of monosodium glutamate, which is mostly used for food applications in Asia [14]. AAs are also used as food and dietary supplements to boost recovery after athletic performance, counteract muscle loss and improve physical performance [[15], [16], [17]]. Overall, the increasing awareness of a healthy lifestyle and the growing popularity of vegetarian and vegan diets stimulate the demand for both proteinogenic and non-proteinogenic AAs [18,19]. Unlike proteins, which must first undergo digestion, consumed AAs are directly absorbed from the small intestine into the bloodstream [20]. However, AA-enriched diets need to be consumed with caution as prolonged high intake of AAs can lead to imbalances in blood AA levels and potential alterations in metabolic pathways [21]. For example, arginine (Arg, R) doses of 21–30 g d-1 can cause gastrointestinal distress, headaches, or nosebleeds [22]. In human endothelial cell culture, continuous exposure to Arg induces oxidative stress and at the same time tolerance to the substance [23]. However, the overdosing risk in vivo is low because the “no observed adverse effect limit” (NOAEL) for daily intake can be up to 25 times higher than the estimated dietary intake [24].

Feed applications

The second major AA market is animal feeds, where AA supplementation reduces the need for high protein meals (e.g. derived from soybean), and improves nitrogen utilization and productivity [25,26]. Within this market, Lysine (Lys, L), methionine (Met, M) and threonine (Thr, T) are dominant and accordingly they are the 2nd, 3rd and 4th most-produced single AAs overall, respectively [13]. These three AAs are common components of poultry and pig feed, in some instances complemented by tryptophan (Trp, Y), valine (Val, V), isoleucine (Ile, I) or leucine (Leu, L) [25]. In cattle, Lys and Met are considered to be limiting factors for milk production [27], and their supplementation in a 3:1 ratio can increase milk protein production by 15% [26].

Interestingly, AA-enhanced feeds have a low environmental impact. For example, when compared with standard, legume-based and low-impact pork feeds, AA-enhanced preparations had the smallest footprint [28], as based on the assessment of their individual components [29,30]. For example, replacing soybean farming with higher-yielding corn production and using a fraction of the harvested biomass as a carbon source for microbial AA production reduced the land use by 67% [25]. However, the environmental impact of increasing biotechnological or synthetic production of free AAs appears to be more complex. Specifically, the reduction in dedicated land use can be heavily offset by increased energy and resource consumption for fermentation equipment and processing. Thus, detailed life cycle assessments will be necessary to precisely and carefully compare the impact of new animal feeds.

Biotechnological and pharmaceutical applications

The third AA market is for biotechnological and pharmaceutical applications. For example, AAs can be used as low-density stabilizers during protein refolding, purification, formulation and storage [[31], [32], [33]]. Specifically, the melting temperature of lysozyme (tmelting = 72-76°C [34]) increases by 4°C in 0.9 M Gly and up to 9°C in 1.0 M Glu; likewise, the melting temperature of BSA (tmelting = 56-69°C [35]) increases by up to 13°C in 1.0 M Gly [[36], [37], [38]]. Furthermore, the presence of AAs in the mobile phase of a chromatography operation can improve protein binding [39] and elution [40,41], or reduce nonspecific interactions of solutes with the stationary phase [42].

In medicine, AA-enhanced diets are used in treatment of patients with various medical conditions, such as inherited metabolic diseases [43], and chronic diseases associated with reduced AA bioavailability [44,45], significantly improving their quality of life [46,47].

Product requirements and feed stream properties

The purity and quantity required for a given AA product depends on the application, which in turn affects the selection of the type and sequence of unit operations to be used during manufacturing. For example, a purity of 70–90% can be sufficient for AA mixtures applied as animal feeds [48], 98%-99% for food-grade, whereas a purity >99% can be necessary for single AA products to be used in pharmaceutical manufacturing or analytics [49]. GMP requirements apply to food and pharma products and are especially strict for the latter, for example in terms of impurity levels, process qualification, clean room environment, quality control, batch records and cleaning validation [50].

Regardless of the application, a manufacturing process is often structured into upstream processing (USP), where AAs are formed in biological or chemical processes, and downstream processing (DSP), which includes recovery, purification and formulation. Consequently, the DSP must also be tailored to the specifics of a USP (e.g., impurity profiles, initial product concentration) and it is thus important to understand the feed stream properties. Specifically, the five approaches of AA production that are well-documented in the literature include chemical synthesis, chemical hydrolysis, enzymatic hydrolysis, enzymatic conversion and biotechnological processes (Table 2), which we will briefly introduce in the next sub-sections. Chemical synthesis

Chemical synthesis produces AAs through a series of well-defined chemical reactions, resulting in consistent composition and a small number of impurities, which streamlines subsequent DSP. A limitation of this approach is a frequent lack of stereoselectivity, resulting in the production of racemic mixtures of D- and L-AAs. Due to the demand for L-AAs accounts for over 99% of the total AA market [53], optical resolution and racemization of D-AA are required, increasing the overall cost and complexity of the method [54]. Enantioselective methods for chemical synthesis of AAs with high purity and yield have been developed [55,56]. However, no records of large-scale enantioselective synthesis of AA were found, which can indicate unfavorable economics of catalysts and reaction substrates. The only two proteinogenic AAs currently produced by chemical synthesis are achiral Gly [57,58] and a DL-Met racemate, which has the same bioavailability as L-Met [59]. From an environmental perspective, the production of synthetic methionine releases 2.96 kg carbon dioxide (CO2) per kg of AA produced [60]. Notably, organic farming regulations limit the maximum amount of synthetic components in feed, driving demand for alternative sources of Met, such as fermentation and enzymatic conversion [61].

Chemical and enzymatic hydrolysis

In this context and according to EU regulation 32021R1165, amino acids derived by chemical or enzymatic hydrolysis of biological material are not regarded as synthetic components [62]. Such hydrolysis can be achieved biotechnologically using enzymes or chemically, with the latter being one of the first methods used to produce mixtures of AAs [63]. Typical hydrolysis substrates include residual food, animal, and plant biomass [64]. Conducting hydrolysis on waste products can reduce waste streams and carbon footprint [65,66]. Mechanical pretreatments like cutting, homogenization, or milling are often applied as a first step to increase substrate accessibility and hence to increase the speed of hydrolysis [67,68]. Other pretreatments such as degreasing, addition of surfactants, or a combination of chemical/enzymatic treatment can increase the hydrolysis degree by up to 50% [67,69].

Chemical hydrolysis itself can be performed using various agents, such as strong acids, bases or sub-critical water (SCW) [70]. Whereas acidic hydrolysis using 6 M hydrogen chloride at 110°C for 24 hours is common for analytical purposes with a hydrolysis degree of 95% [71,63], industrial hydrolysis of animal waste products often relies on SCW, high temperatures (200-280°C) and medium incubation times (5-60 minutes) with an AA recovery of up to 15% based on total feed dry mass, or over 50% in terms of total protein mass [72]. Prolonged incubation times with SCW increase costs and the environmental footprint of the corresponding processes but did not increase AA yields [65]. Instead, different additives can improve SCW hydrolysis increasing AA yields by up to 44% as reported for sodium bicarbonate [73].

Proteinaceous substrates can also be hydrolyzed using proteases and peptidases [74], such as alkaline serine proteases, neutral Bacillus licheniformis proteases, acidic Aspergillus oryzae or Rhizopus niveus proteases [75], several of which are well established from other applications such as laundry detergents. The enzymatic reactions typically take place at pH 6-8 for 1-2 h at a temperature between 25-60°C [76] with an AA yield of 30-70% of total protein mass.

Regardless of the process, the degree of hydrolysis is often well below 100% with the main limitations being the digestibility, variability and heterogeneity of the feed biomass. For example, the protein content of dry plant biomass can range from ∼8% in ryegrass to 34% in microalgae or 37% in soybean [77]. Likewise, the AA composition within one type of biomass can change and depends on age and size in case of animals. Specifically, Lys, His, and Cys concentrations in growing pigs can vary by 15% within a time span of 8 weeks [78], and even up to 50% in feathers of growing broilers over a 14 week development [79].

In addition, proteins in animal by-products are often rich in connective tissues composed of proteins with a high content of proline (Pro, P), hydroxyproline and Gly [80], which often renders the proteins resistant to hydrolysis [81]. AAs Trp and tyrosine (Tyr, Y) are often underrepresented in these proteins too, which results in a nutritional imbalance in the corresponding hydrolysates and can necessitate the costly and tailored addition of these AAs [82,83].

These limitations contribute to costs of 5.2 EUR kg-1 of AAs derived from feathers (neglecting substrate costs) [84], or 24.4 and 38.8 EUR kg-1 AAs from fish wastes obtained by chemical or enzymatic hydrolysis, respectively [85] (costs corrected for inflation to the time of publication). These costs are substantially higher than typical AA sales prices (Table 3), which limits protein hydrolysis mostly to analytical applications for foods and feeds [86]. Likewise, there is a substantial environmental footprint for hydrolysis-based AA production. For example, producing 1 kg of acidic protein hydrolysate derived from leather wastes or feathers releases 1.53 kg and 1.20 kg of CO2, respectively [87,84]. Biotechnological processes - enzymatic conversion

Instead of hydrolyzing proteins, isolated enzymes can also be used to stereo-selectively synthesize L-AAs. Enzymes used for this purpose include tryptophan synthase, L-aspartic-β-decarboxylase, aspartase, or asparagine synthetase and are produced through microbial fermentation [[88], [89], [90], [91], [92]]. Genetically engineered microorganisms like Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas spp. are often used in these processes to obtain high titers of recombinant enzyme [93]. Using immobilized enzymes or even whole cells increases the biocatalyst’s half-life, thereby reducing process costs. For example, aspartase overexpressing cells immobilized in carrageenan retained enzymatic activity for up to 2 years and produces 200 mmol L−1 L-aspartate per hour per gram of cells [88].

The substrates necessary for enzymatic conversion are produced by chemical synthesis [93] or fermentation (Table 2) and the corresponding processes can be referred to as biocatalysis. Enzymatic conversion is suitable to produce AAs which cannot be effectively overproduced in microorganisms (see section 3.4) and for which substrate costs are low. This applies to asparagine (Asp, N), cysteine (Cys, C), alanine (Ala, A) and partially to Trp production [13]. Additionally, synthetic DL-Met can be enzymatically converted to L-Met by D-amino acid oxidase and leucine dehydrogenase overexpressed in recombinant E. coli [94,95]. Compared to fermentation, enzymatic conversion for AA production results in higher purity and volumetric productivity along with lower byproduct formation (Table 2) facilitating a simple DSP, often consisting only of a crystallization step [88].

Biotechnological processes - fermentation

Microbial fermentation is currently the primary method for L-AA manufacturing, accounting for approximately 85% of total production (Table 3). The key organism used is Corynebacterium glutamicum which was isolated in 1956 by Drs. Shukuo Kinosita and Shigezo Udaka at Kyowa Hakko Kogyo Co., Ltd and naturally produces large quantities of glutamate, hence the name [96]. Various strains of C. glutamicum and other bacteria like E. coli have been genetically modified to overproduce L-AAs with titers ranging from 10–181 g L-1 [97,98], which is suitable for industrial production [99]. The substrates used in these processes include glucose, sucrose, xylose, and other simple sugars [100,101], with average prices in the range of 0.4–1.5 EUR per kg [102,103], yet alternatives such as lignocellulose biomass can be used too [104]. Current microbial production strains approach the maximum theoretical conversion rates for these carbon sources. For example, C. glutamicum JL-69 achieves a glucose conversion of 64.6% with 66% being the theoretical maximum [98]. In addition, alternative hosts like methanogenic archaea utilizing carbon dioxide as a carbon source are now being investigated [105]. Such processes can help to reduce the environmental footprint of fermentative AA production in the future, which is currently ∼4.30 kg CO2 per kg of Lys and Threonine (Thr, T) [60].

DSP – downstream processing.

The sequence of the following sub-sections corresponds to the current relevance of the unit operation in amino acid purification, with chromatography being the core of most AA downstream processes found in over 65% of industrial processes (see section 5). Next, membrane-based processes represent an emerging alternative to chromatography, offering improvements such as lower operational costs, decreased waste generation and energy consumption [170,171]. The extraction-based techniques that follow are losing popularity at present, mainly due to their large environmental footprint and high cost. Finally, techniques for solid product recovery, predominantly used as final formulation step, are described. For each technique, we first describe the separation principle and examples, followed by advantages and drawbacks.

Chromatography

Despite some predictions of a diminishing relevance [172], chromatography remains a major workhorse of process-scale DSP in biotechnology. The range of chromatographic techniques (Table 5), together with extensive know-how in industry and comprehensive modeling frameworks ensures its current dominant position. In the case of AAs, chromatography can be used to separate a single type of AA from impurities and other AAs due to selective interaction of the solutes (i.e. AAs and impurities) in the mobile phase (i.e. feed) with a ligand-functionalized stationary phase (also called resin). Separation can be achieved due to selective binding from the feed, stringent wash conditions and fractionated elution, typically by changing the mobile phase properties like the ionic strength, polarity, or pH [173]. Chromatography can remove harmful substances and purify AAs up to 99%, which typically complies with food, feed, and pharmaceutical requirements. Different types of chromatography can be combined to improve AA purity, if necessary, at best using orthogonal separation mechanisms (i.e. based on different AA properties such as size and charge) [174]. The product recovery usually ranges from 70% to >90% [175]. Next, we will introduce individual chromatographic techniques in the context of AA purification.

Ion exchange chromatography

Ion exchange is the most common type of chromatography used for large-scale production of AAs, present in processes accounting for ∼43% of the amino acid market share [195]. Separation by ion exchange chromatography (IEC) is based on the interaction and binding of charged molecules in an aqueous mobile phase with oppositely charged ligands immobilized on the solid phase at a conductivity <1 S m-1. If the feed exceeds this conductivity, desalting (e.g. by electrodialysis), buffer exchange (e.g. by nanofiltration) or broth dilution may be necessary. IEC can be operated in flow-through mode to bind impurities while the product passes through the column or in bind-and-elute mode, where a product is bound and then eluted from the column.

Product binding

The binding can be achieved through interaction with anion or cation exchange ligands with cation exchangers typically binding AAs if pH <pI, whereas anion exchangers interact with AAs if pH >pI [179]. Therefore, feed stream pH and the resulting expected charge of the target AA and impurities (including other AAs) guide the choice of ion exchange resin. Adjusting the feed pH before chromatography is possible but increases acid and base consumption and therefore costs.

For both anion and cation exchangers, there are strong and weak ligands available. Specifically, strong IEC ligands are often used to purify and perform bulk separations of AAs and operated in simulated moving bed (SMB; see 5.4), ion-exclusion (i.e. retention of AAs at their pI and rejection of ionic impurities; purities and recoveries over 99% [196]) and bind-and-elute mode. The strong ion exchange ligands are also efficient in removing negatively charged solutes like organic acids present in fermentation broth (Table 2) [197] even without a need for pH adjustment [198,137]. Strong acid cation exchange resins (SACER) are currently the most common type of resin used in feed-grade AA separation (especially Lys) [[199], [200], [201], [202], [203], [204], [205]] because of their close-to-constant charge over a wide pH range (e.g. 3-12). For example, a single column achieved purities >98% and recoveries in the 70-80% range when loaded with solids-free fermentation broth [203,[205], [206], [207]].

In contrast, the charge of weak IEC ligands will vary with buffer pHs which provides an opportunity to fine-tune binding selectivity for polishing steps, for example when separating AAs with similar properties [177] and facilitates resin regeneration [208]. The capacity of weak ion exchanger operated at optimal pH is typically higher compared to their strong counterparts (e.g. ∼90% and ∼20% for cation and anion exchangers, respectively [209,210]. However, outside the optimal pH range the capacity of weak ion exchanger can be significantly reduced [211].

There are also amphoteric resins that carry positive and negative charges providing unique selectivity and even allow chelation [212]. This can be helpful for pigments and polymers removal, or to separate similar AAs, such as Leu and Ile [213,214,202].

Product elution

After a target AA was bound, the column is washed to remove residual, unbound impurities from the column. Then, elution occurs by weakening electrostatic interactions between AAs and charged ligands on the IEC resin. This is achieved by adding mobile phase modifiers like salts. The addition can be carried out using stepwise or time-dependent gradients. The latter often use a linear increase in salt concentration over time but are mostly limited to analytical purposes [180]. Stepwise elution is preferred at production scale, as it reduces the process time and eluent consumption and simplifies the complexity of chromatography devices (e.g. no buffer mixer and fewer pumps required) [180]. The elution buffer components can be cheap, common bulk chemicals such as phosphate and sodium chloride [176]. To address mass transfer limitation, volume flux can be halted (e.g. for 15 min) after a column is brought in contact with an elution buffer and the target AA is first detected at the column outlet (a technique known as soaking). This can improve purity and peak concentration by 3% and 5%, respectively [200]. However, the selective detection of individual AAs in real time can be challenging and limit applicability and reproducibility at large-scale.

Compared to other techniques, advantages of the IEC are high capacity (e.g. 200 g L-1 [179]), the ability to separate most individual AAs and low costs. The latter are 2-10 EUR L-1 for food-grade resins. Some theoretical process cost estimates for Lys production at a scale of 1 Mt a-1 report the total costs of raw materials for IEC (i.e. resin, ammonium hydroxide, and hydrochloric acid) and equipment to be ∼20% and ∼7% of the overall material and equipment costs, respectively [215]. The limiting factor in IEC is the separation of AAs with similar pI and side chains, such as aliphatic AAs (e.g., Leu, Ile). Additionally, whereas charged impurities like DNA fragments from the fermentation broth may be removed via IEC, they may also unintentionally co-elute with some AAs [178,216]. Since the DNA fragments coeluting with AAs are often <10 bp in size [216], they are unlikely to encode functional proteins and therefore should pose only a small safety risk.

Normal and reversed-phase chromatography

Instead of charged ligands, other surface functionalities can be used as well to purify AAs either in bind-and-elute or flow-through mode. Normal-phase chromatography (NPC) is the oldest chromatographic technique and does not use ligands on the resin surface [217]. The compounds are separated based on polarity using a polar stationary phase, typically silica, and a nonpolar mobile phase such as aliphatic hydrocarbons and ethers. Accordingly, sample loading to NPC requires that fermentation-derived feeds are extracted first (see 4.3). Hydrophilic interaction chromatography (HILIC) is a variant of NPC employing a hydrophilic solid phase and a hydrophobic mobile phase, where solute retention increases with its hydrophilicity [218]. Both NPC and HILIC can be used to purify and separate AAs, even with similar pI, and from broths containing polar impurities. For example, AAs were separated using a normal-phase silica gel and an isopropanol-methanol-ammonia mobile phase achieving recoveries from 96% to 99% [178]. Likewise, it was possible to separate Leu and Ile as well as Lys and Gln by HILIC [181]. However, a full separation of all 20 AAs has not been reported.

Reversed-phase chromatography (RPC) also relies on organic mobile phases (e.g. acetonitrile, methanol, or acetone) but the stationary phases are decorated with aliphatic or aromatic hydrocarbons which interact with hydrophobic surface area of a product. RPC is mostly an analytical tool for High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) and multiple successful AA separations were reported, often requiring pre-column derivation [[182], [183], [184], [185], [186]]. There are hybrid RPC resins available that contain ion-pairing groups to improve the separation of AAs by including two interaction modes. Strong acid ion-pairing group resins were used in both analytical [219] and semi-preparative processes [220]. Surprisingly, the semi-preparative scale displayed better resolution than the analytical column, indicating possibility to employ this technique on a large scale.

Both NPC and RPC suffer from environmental and safety risks associated with storage and handling the toxic, flammable and volatile organic solvents (which can form explosive fumes) in volumes required for large scale production [221]. For example, HILIC uses >60% v v-1 of organic solvents [218,181]. Furthermore, resins costs of 100-200 EUR kg-1 for silica and 600-1000 EUR kg-1 for decorated resins are probably prohibitively high for food and feed products.

Other types of chromatography

Multiple other chromatography techniques have been proposed for amino acid purification and separation including supercritical fluid chromatography (SFC) and hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) [187,188,181,194].

SFC uses supercritical mobile phases such as CO2 at 31°C and 7.3 MPa [222], which is a non-polar solvent under these conditions similar to hexane [188]. While SFC is most suitable for separation of hydrophobic AAs, co-solvents or additives, often organic solvents, can enable separation of hydrophilic analytes too [191], at the expense of increased environmental burden and costs [190]. SFC can even be used for chiral separation of AAs [187]. The advantages of SFC compared to NPC and RPC are the use of non-toxic mobile phases and the ease of their removal from the eluate (e.g. by evaporation), a 3-5-fold reduced operation time with 60-80% lower pressure drop [223] and lower costs [224,188]. However, SFC is currently limited to a throughput of about 12.5 g h-1 [225] and vessel volumes around 40 L [189].

Hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC) is a well-established technique in peptide and protein purification [192,193]. The solutes bind to the hydrophobic stationary phase ligands in the presence of high salt concentration (e.g. >3 M sodium chloride) in an aqueous mobile phase, which makes HIC an orthogonal method to IEC. Especially, HIC elution conditions are compatible with those for IEC loading and vice versa. A possible application of HIC can be the separation of AAs with similar pI and different hydrophobicity, such as Leu, Ile, Val, Ala. However, only Phe and Tyr were found to bind to available HIC ligands [194], which substantially limits the applicability of the method.

Membrane separation and centrifugation

Membrane separations like microfiltration (MF) and ultrafiltration (UF) are well-established to intensify continuous upstream manufacturing [226,227] as well as to remove cells, their debris and proteins from fermentation broth during downstream processing and to concentrated feeds [[228], [229], [230]]. Centrifugation is used to effectively remove cells during clarification, often preceding membrane separation of finer cellular debris and other solids. Due to advances in nanofiltration (NF) [231], reverse osmosis (RO) and electrodialysis (ED) [232], membrane separation can also be used for the purification of AAs.

Electrodialysis

ED is an electrochemical separation process in which a static electric field is applied to induce an ion flux across a semi-permeable membrane [233]. The membrane’s selectivity is defined by its charge facilitating the permeation of ions of opposite charge, thereby separating the ion mixture in a feed. For example, multi-step ED processes can consist of a first desalting step removing ions with higher mobility than AAs [234]. In a second step, separation into basic, neutral and acidic AA fraction is achieved and individual AAs are isolated in a final step [235,236]. Typically, 95% of the salts are removed in such a process whereas 85-99% of AAs are recovered [237,236]. The exceptions are Phe and Arg with ∼70% recovery, probably because these AAs can damage the ED membranes by reducing the water content in the membrane [[238], [239], [240], [241]].

Compared to IEC, ED consumes less chemicals and thus requires ∼65% less energy based on a cumulative demand [242]. The primary energy costs for IEC result from sodium hydroxide production required for resin regeneration. If the latter is performed by electrochemically induced pH shift, the advantage of ED may be less pronounced [243]. Interestingly, the electrochemically induced pH shift relies on an ED device too. The widespread use of ED is currently limited by device and operation complexity and the small number of commercially available membranes, which could provide the required selectivity.

Microfiltration and Ultrafiltration

MF and UF are used to clarify fermentation broth before AAs purification (see 5.2). MF and UF typically retain colloids in the 0.1-5 μm and 0.01-0.1 μm range respectively [244]. Accordingly, MF is suitable to remove cells and cell debris. This can be achieved by cake filtration in continuous setups such as pre-coat and drum filters. Feeds can be processed at flowrates >300 L m-2 h-1 (>8.3 × 10-5 m s-1) with solids contents >1% m v-1 and filter areas as large as 200 m2 [244]. The main drawbacks are increased complexity of the device and limitation of driving pressure difference due to operation in a suction setup (i.e. pressure difference <0.1 MPa), even though positive pressure devices exist as well. The technology is also susceptible to cake deformation and cracking, as well as ineffective scraping [245]. In addition to cell removal during primary recovery, drum filters can also be used to recover crystal slurry as part of a final formulation (see section 5.2).

Both MF and UF can be operated in a crossflow setup too, with tangential flowrates of 1-3 m s-1, fluxes of 50-600 L m-2 h-1 (0.14-1.67 × 10-4 m s-1) and 15-100 L m-2 h-1 (0.28-4.17 × 10-5 m s-1), and driving pressures of 0.07–0.20 MPa and 0.20–1.00 MPa, respectively [246,244]. The price of MF or UF membranes for general applications (e.g. wastewater treatment) is typically 10-500 EUR m-2, and pharma-grade equipment can cost 1000-5000 EUR m-2 [247]. The MF/UF devices are often modular and single use, facilitating high throughput and device rapid setup [248]. Still, MF/UF can be almost twice as labor intensive (assessed in labor cost) compared to devices such as disc-stack centrifuges with cleaning-in-place (CIP) and sterilization-in-place (SIP) functionality [249]. Main disadvantages of MF and UF are concentration polarization and membrane fouling [250], which reduce trans-membrane flux, product separation as well as recovery and increase process costs. Increasing the flux perpendicular to the membrane may reduce the issue but the corresponding increase in shear stress can damage cells present in the feed and thereby increase the effective burden with fine particles [249]. Alternatively, the fouling of MF or UF can be reduced by device modifications (e.g. vibrating membranes), feed pretreatment (e.g. flocculation) or preprocessing by centrifugation or filtration [251].

Interestingly, and despite the pore sizes typically being larger than the diameter of AAs, some UF membranes can separate AAs due to Donnan effects [252], though, for the separation of individual AAs, NF proves to be more feasible [253].

Nanofiltration

NF emerged in late 1980s as a low-pressure (0.2–4.0 MPa vs >8 MPa) alternative to RO (see 4.2.4) for wastewater treatment and applications in beverage industry [171]. NF membrane pore sizes typically range from 150–3000 Da (∼0.6-3 nm), with 150–300 Da (∼0.6–1.1 nm) being most prevalent [254,171,227] with flow rates up to 30 L m-2 h-1 (8.3 × 10-6 m s-1) [255]. NF devices are modular facilitating different geometries (e.g. spiral wound, hollow fiber, flat sheet, or tubular) flow patterns (e.g. cross-flow, inside-out) to minimize membrane fouling [256]. Such membranes allow AAs purification and to a certain extend also AAs separation [[257], [258], [259],253]. Whereas separation by size is often impractical [257], solute rejection ratios can be tuned by varying the degree of membrane surface ionization facilitating charge based separation [260], even though the latter may not be as effective as for IEC. Specifically, the use of NF for separation of AAs with similar pI (e.g. neutral AAs) is limited, and applications focus mainly on acidic and basic AAs. Accordingly, NF has been successfully used to purify proteins, peptides and AAs [261,253]. AA recovery is often >90% with a purity around 95% [262].

Compared to IEC, NF can consume less water and chemicals, especially if feed stream adjustment is not required, and produces less acidic or basic wastes [108]. For example, Glu can permeate NF membranes at pH ∼3.7 with a rejection rate of 0.15 (97% purity). In contrast, Glu binds to commercially used SACER at pH 1.5 (95% purity, 80% recovery), which requires ∼44 g of sulfuric acid per 1 L of Glu fermentation broth (100 g Glu L-1) to achieve the necessary pH adjustment from 3.7 to 1.5 [207]. Also, IEC requires 12 g of ammonia per kg of Glu produced to elute the product from the column and ∼22 L of water for elution and wash, resulting in wastewater with pH 2-4 and high amounts of ammonium sulphate. In theory, no additional water is required for permeating products in NF [227]. Recalculated to CO2 footprint (Global Warming Potential100, IPCC2021), IEC produce approximatelly 0.1 kg more of CO2 compared to NF (calculated from Ecoinvent database entries [[263], [264], [265]]. Also, NF membrane cleaning is carried out only when the performance drops to, for example, 90% of the initial flowrate or feed pressure increases [266]. Furthermore, NF can prevent the conversion of Gln to Glu that typically occurs on ion exchangers [259]. The reduced pressure compared to RO can save up to 40% of energy [267]. A challenge in NF applications is the imperfect rejection rate for AAs (i.e. >0 and <1) [261], reducing the recovery in both retentate and permeate product scenarios. This reduction in the NF effectivity can be mitigated by integrated continuous process layout with waste stream recycling [227]. On the downside, and like UF, NF can suffer from concentration polarization, scaling and membrane fouling [268]. High-performance membranes with a throughput of 5–30 L m-2 h-1 (1.4-8.3 × 10-6 m s-1) cost 30–60 EUR m-2 [255], in some cases even 1000 EUR m-2 [269]. Although we are not aware of a large-scale NF process for AA purification, the technology seems readily scalable, as demonstrated by various processes for waste-water treatment operated at >10 m3 h-1 [270,271].

Reverse osmosis

RO is a common demineralization technique, used mainly for the desalination of seawater or the production of purified water for analytical and pharmaceutical applications. A pressure difference up to 7 MPa is forcing water molecules across a membrane with pore sizes of ∼0.1-1.0 nm with flux of 10-80 L m-2 h-1 MPa-1 (0.28–2.22 × 10-5 m s-1 MPa-1) [272,244]. Accordingly, it can be used to concentrate AAs up to 4-fold, achieving ∼10 g L-1 [[273], [274], [275], [276]] while reducing the energy consumption compared to evaporation systems by up to 76% [277,278]. In some processes, RO reduced the DSP costs by up to 50% [273]. However, RO is ∼60% more energy demanding (∼9–15 MJ m-3 feed, salt concentration ∼ 30 g L-1 [279]) than low-pressure process like NF and even more prone to fouling and pore blocking than these, which necessitates extensive sample pre-treatment, especially if fermentation broths are to be processed.

Centrifugation

Centrifuges can be used as the sole clarification step (see 5.2) with multiple designs available. Disc stack centrifuges (DSCs) are the most common type employed in large scale bioprocessing [249]. They consist of multiple stacked conical discs inside a chamber. Suspensions are fed to the bottom of the centrifuge, where the less dense phase (e.g. fermentation broth) flows radially inward and to the top of the chamber [249]. The denser phases such as cells accumulate the outermost radius of the rotor usually equipped with side solid discharge enabling (semi-)continuous operation using little to no consumables. This reduces costs of operation 2.9 times compared to filtration at a 5000-L scale [249].

However, solid discharge must be properly timed to ensure a smooth operation and is always accompanied by some loss of liquid and thus AAs. Additionally, low-density solids may not be removed effectively, especially at large scale, because, in contrast to filtration, the operation does not scale linearly [249,280]. Capital cost of DSCs can be 3.5 times higher comparted to equivalent filtration devices [249].

Extraction

In the chemical industry, purification is often achieved by mechanical, physical and chemical extraction [281]. The latter is mostly limited to aqueous solutions in case of bioprocessing because products like proteins or applications such as food and feed are incompatible with organic solvents. In case of AAs, their hydrophilic functional groups limit solubility to <6 g L-1 in common non-polar solvents [282]. Even polar solvents like n-hexylamine exhibit partition coefficients of only ∼1 in combination with aqueous solutions limiting extraction to only a few some AAs. Additionally, multiple processing steps are necessary to achieve AA recoveries of 90% with simultaneous desalination [198]. Alternatively, hydrophobic AAs like Phe enrich at, for example, the gas-liquid interfaces of aqueous systems and can be recovered in an overlaying organic solvent phase (recovery >75%; initial concentration ∼3 mg L-1) [283]. Due to the moderate recoveries and substantial environmental footprint of the organic solvents, alternative extraction methods have been developed for AAs.

Aqueous two-phase systems

Specifically, aqueous two-phase systems (ATPS) form upon the addition of either a polymer-polymer or polymer-salt mixture to water [284]. AA partitioning between the two immiscible phases is driven by charge, polarity, hydrophobicity, bio-specific affinity, size, density as well as a combination of these [285]. The formation and properties of two-phase systems are affected by the molecular mass and concentration of polymer, salt concentration, the pH and buffer substance, and especially temperature [286]. Since there is currently no single mechanistic model that can describe the impact of all these parameters, optimizing ATPS for a specific separation setting requires substantial experimental efforts [287].

Nevertheless, an ATPS based on a pH-responsive polymeric deep eutectic solvent (PDES) was successfully use to separate aromatic AAs [288]. The PDES contained carboxylic groups that undergo pH-dependent (de-)protonation, thereby affecting the solubility of the polymer [289], which facilitated AA recovery and polymer recycling [288]. Specifically, the reported extraction efficiency for Tyr, Phe and Trp was 95%, 99%, and 99%, respectively, with recoveries greater than 88%.

The absence of organic solvents in ATPS reduces environmental impact assessed in CO2 equivalent by 60% compared to conventional chemical extraction. Moreover, whereas the latter produces 0.2–0.5 kg of hazardous waste per kg of product, it is <0.01 kg kg-1 product for ATPS [286,290]. However, salts and polymers used in ATPS are difficult to recycle and therefore increase impact scores compared to chromatography depending on the study scope (i.e. inclusion of the resin production and lifecycle) [291].

Reactive extraction and derivatization

The limited solubility of AAs in organic solvents can be overcome if the former are forced to react with cationic and anionic lipophilic extractants or macrocyclic compounds, such as phosphoric acid derivatives, quaternary aliphatic amines, or crown-ethers [292].

For example, 73% of Phe and 80% of Val were extracted from an aqueous or fermentation feed, respectively [293,294]. Specifically, the cationic extractant di-(2-ethylhexyl) phosphoric acid (D2EHPA) in combination butyl acetate was used for a multistage extraction that facilitated a pI-based separation of AAs into four pools. Highest recoveries up to 90% were observed for hydrophobic AAs and at pH 2–3 where extractant reactivity was highest even though AA charge decreased with decreasing pH [292]. Similar extraction rates were found when D2EHPA was combined with other organic solvents like n-Octane [295]. Trioctylamine (e.g. in n-Octanol) is an alternative extractant achieving 86% recovery for Asp [296]. Most AAs can be extracted in a 40–80% [v v-1] D2EHPA, 5-20% [v v-1] 2-ethylhexanol, and 0-55% [v v-1] kerosene system by adjusting the pH. Phe can be specifically extracted at pH 10 using quaternary ammonium salts in a sulfuric acid medium. All 20 AAs can be separated by a system of consecutive extractions with high yield (90-99%) and purity (>99%), at scales ranging from 0.1–1000 L h-1 thanks to high scalability [297,206].

Operating the extraction in hollow-fibers modules increases hydrogen ions transport from stripping to organic phase and feed, which increases AA separation by 40% [298] and is referred to as supported liquid membrane [299]. Extraction process can be also integrated as an emulsion or sandwich layout (emulsion liquid membrane) consisting of aqueous/organic/aqueous layers [300,301]. First, the product reacts with the extractant in the primary aqueous feed phase, then transitions into the organic from which it is released into a defined aqueous phase. The costs are reduced compared to conventional extraction by up to 80% due to faster and intensified transport processes and thus reduced solvents consumption [[301], [302], [303]]. Liquid membranes also facilitate chiral AA separations [304].

However, reactive extraction devices are often complex, with 13 countercurrent extraction columns required in one of the patented process designs [297] and provide only dilute process intermediates that require concentration. The excessive use of organic solvents, which can range from 15–73 L kg-1 of AAs extracted, results in a large environmental footprint. Although up to 99.9% of solvent can be recovered and reused [297], the recovery requires energy-demanding (membrane) distillation [305]. Alternatively, hydrochloric acid solution can be also used for solvent recovery but it is hazardous too [295]. Limiting factors in emulsion liquid membrane approaches is the stability of the emulsion, leakage from the emulsion globule and swelling of the latter [306]. Accordingly, the competitiveness of reactive extraction as whole is currently limited compared to other techniques such as chromatography.

Similarly, AAs may be chemically derivatized to facilitate separation. For example, benzenesulphonic acid and p-toluenesulphonic acid can be used to derivatize Leu and Ile to facilitate their respective separation during fractional crystallization [307]. The technique requires 1.2 kg of reagent per kg of crude Leu and requires IEC step for final purification from reagent and reaches yield of 70%. Similarly, AAs can be acetylated with acetyl chloride (0.6 kg per kg of AA) and recrystallized and selectively hydrolyzed to crystalize pure Leu with yield of 52% [308].

In general, reactive extraction techniques require large amounts of specific chemical reagents for derivatization and subsequent recovery of the authentic AAs, which drive up the cost and substantially increase its environmental footprint. The approach was mostly proposed for the separation of AAs with similar physical properties that were challenging to separate by IEC at that time (e.g. Leu and Ile). Accordingly, reactive extraction is mostly used for analytical purposes, typically to improve resolution in RP-HPLC and facilitate detection (e.g. ninhydrin, o-phthalaldehyde) [309].

Techniques for solid product recovery

Most AAs are sold in a dried or solid state. Since AAs are thermally stable up to 150–200°C [310,311], different options for solid product recovery are available. The most common technique is crystallization, whereas spray-drying is preferentially used in the production of low-purity feed-grade AAs. Lyophilization, or freeze-drying are other options too, but energy consumption can be 10 times higher compared to spray drying [312,313].

Crystallization and precipitation

Crystallization is often used to obtain a product in solid form after purification. Additionally, it can be applied to high-titer broths immediately after fermentation to reduce the complexity and costs of downstream process (see Section 5.).

AA crystallization can be initiated by cooling, addition of salts, adjusting the pH close to the pI of the product or by increasing the concentration of the latter, for example by evaporation of the solvent. This is energy demanding, so cooling crystallization at low pressure and membrane distillation are energy-saving alternatives [314]. For example, integrated membrane crystallization can reduce energy consumption by up to 33% compared to standalone crystallization [315]. Isoelectric crystallization [207] and crystallization by salt addition [316,317] have a low energy demand as well but increase the consumption of acids, bases, or inorganic salts [207]. For example, acidification 1 m3 of Glu fermentation broth (pH 6.5, 100 g L-1) to pH 3.2 requires 40 kg of sulfuric acid, producing similar amounts of salts (e.g. ammonium sulfate) as a solid waste [207]. The addition of solvents like ethanol can enhance AA recovery [203,318]. For example, addition of 50 g of ethanol per liter (∼6.5% v v-1) of fermentation broth reduces Gly solubility by 15%. Furthermore, RO can be used for AA pre-concentration if necessary [314]. RO is in general most suitable for low concentration conditions (<150 g L-1) [314] which are present in most of the reported processes.

Crystallization or precipitation as a purification step is most suitable for AAs with extreme pI (as few other AAs will co-crystallize or precipitate) and feeds that contain >50 g L-1 of single AA with low concentrations of other AAs or impurities (e.g. organic acids). Accordingly, crystallization is especially suitable for Glu (pI 3.2) (see Section 5.3.1). Crystallization is scalable with equipment facilitating a throughput up to 20,000 L h-1 [319,320] and established continuous processes operation [321].

If triggering crystal formation is impractical, precipitation with organic solvents is an alternative facilitating fractionated separation of individual AAs from mixtures based on AA polarity and hydrophobicity using increasing solvent concentrations [322], although co-precipitation of impurities can occur.

Spray drying

Spray drying is used in several food and feed processes [323] with a throughput of >10 000 L h-1 [324]. In this operation, water is removed from the feed by nebulizing the latter into a container typically heated to 150°C with residence times of ∼25 s [244]. The approach is especially suitable if all chemical and biological components of the AA feed stream are safe to use in the intended application because then spray drying can suffice as the only DSP step [325,326]. On the other hand, spray drying is one of the most energy demanding unit operations in DSP, typically second to evaporators, which often precede spray drying to concentrate the feed [327]. Steam production is the main cost driver with 90% share of whole lifecycle expenses [328] and average consumption of 4.8 MJ per kg of product [[329], [330], [331], [332], [333]].

Adsorbers

As an alternative to synthetic resins (see 4.1), natural adsorbers like activated carbon and zeolites can be used for AA purification too. Zeolites act as a molecular sieve for selective AA purification as studied based on a mixture of D-glucose and Lys from which 85% of the latter were recovered [334]. The adsorption mechanism is based on the interaction between the protonated sidechain of Lys and the negatively charged surface of zeolites whereas glucose is rejected due to the size exclusion effect. Notably, glucose is often a limiting source in fermentation (i.e. near zero concentration in the DSP feed) rendering a glucose-AA separation unnecessary. In a similar manner, zeolites can be used for the separation of AAs from aminosulphonic acid, di-, tri-, and oligopeptides [335].

Pretreatment with activated carbon adsorption is often performed after clarification, mainly for decolorization or removal of organic compounds [336,201,318,137]. Activated carbon adsorption can also be used to capture and purify aromatic AAs, for example up to 98% in case of Phe [198,205]. The main adsorption mechanisms in this case are hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions [337]. Whereas recovery is often >90% for many AAs [236], it is only ∼60% in case of Phe and therefore lower compared to most IEC methods. Furthermore, elution from activated carbon requires organic solvents such as 9% [v v-1] ethyl acetate [205] which increases the environmental footprint and reduces compatibility with food applications.

Downstream processes

The performance of individual unit operations is important (Table 4), but a unit operation that performs best as a first step of a purification process may not result in the best overall process, for example because it is incompatible with operations to be used at subsequent steps. Therefore, it is important to assess entire processes in terms of AA purity, recovery and production costs. Also, AA production has undergone a substantial change over time as biomass hydrolysates have been replaced by biotechnological fermentation as the dominant upstream method (Table 3, see 3.). Accordingly, the primary recovery of most downstream processes reported after 1960 is tailored to feeds containing fermentation media components (e.g. salts and carbohydrates), cells, as well as related impurities such as organic acids, DNA, proteins and other amino acids [338].

Early processes – hydrolyzation and crystallizations

Inspired by chemical-industry processes with often simple, well-defined impurity profiles, single unit operation processes were developed for AA purification in the early 20th century (Fig. 1). For example, fractionated precipitation and crystallization (see 4.4.1) were used [341]. However, the AA purity achieved with these processes was often <90% because multiple components of the crude plant and animal hydrolysates co-precipitated with the product, which necessitated repeated processing, slowing down manufacturing. Specifically, a 1909 production process for monosodium glutamate used acidic hydrolysis of wheat gluten for 20 h, followed by three consecutive filtration and crystallization steps, with DSP taking over one month, resulting in 85% product purity with an unreported recovery [340]. Consequently, multi-unit operation processes were developed in the mid-20th century to specifically address the needs of primary recovery, purification and formulation.

Multi-step processes including primary recovery, purification and formulation

Extraction based processes

For biomass hydrolysate feeds, a combination of LLE, chromatography and reactive extraction (see 4.3) was developed in the 1950s [198]. The primary AA recovery via LLE using n-hexylamine was 90% and simultaneous desalination of the hydrolysate feed was achieved facilitating the subsequent IEC. Since the latter allowed only a group separation into neutral, acidic, and basic AAs, multiple reactive extractions (D2EHPA, 2-ethylhexanol, kerosene system) were used to isolate individual AAs with purities and recoveries both >99%. The latter are higher compared to even the latest manufacturing approaches (Fig. 1), but at the expense of high consumables costs (e.g. 10-15 EUR kg-1 of extraction system depending on exact composition [342,343]) and a substantial environmental footprint. For example, the use of D2EHPA releases an equivalent of 50-500 kg CO2 kg-1 AA, depending on the required amount for each specific AA(Global Warming Potential100, IPCC2021) [344].

Fermentation as a disruptive feed: implications for purification

In the mid-1960s, the isolation of C. glutamicum was a disruptive event for AA production process design because it became possible to synthesize selected AAs at double-digit gram quantities per liter fermentation broth (see 3.4, Fig. 2). First, cells and cell debris as well as other dispersed particles need to be removed from the fermentation broth, for example to prevent chromatography column clogging (see 4.2). For this purpose, various microfiltration [[345], [346], [347],199,196,200,316,348,201,349,137,206], ultrafiltration [202,205,326] and centrifugation steps were developed [203,318]. The latter is useful to remove cells and cell debris >1 µm, which facilitates subsequent filtration [249]. Whereas centrifugation is easy to implement at laboratory scale of <50 L liquid, scaling to bulk volumes >1000 L h-1 can be challenging [350], for example due to a substantial increase in mechanical forces.

Filtration technologies like pre-coat and drum filtration have been used as alternatives in AA production processes [215] because they can improve the scalability and reduce energy consumption as well as investment cost but process complexity may further increase [351]. Particles <1 µm are often removed by a series of membrane filters with decreasing pore sizes. This stepwise approach can reduce fouling of the individual membranes, especially when operated in crossflow mode, and improve filter train capacity by 15% [352] but further increases process complexity.

A trend in large-scale AA production that can be observed since the 2000s is that primary recovery and purification steps are combined by implementing IEC or crystallization operations that can handle the particle concentrations of the biotechnological feed [339,207]. In such cases, centrifugation, flotation, or filtration are then implemented after crystallization to recover AAs crystals from the mother liquor and remove residual cells and cell debris [353]. Such product recovery and clarification can be performed on different devices (e.g., filtration and centrifugation), consecutively using the same device (e.g., a centrifuge for clarification and solid product isolation) or, under certain conditions (see 5.2.5 and 5.3) omit clarification and focus solely on solid product isolation on a single device (e.g., centrifugation, see 5.2.4).

IEC as the dominant purification platform

In common with primary recovery, purification steps have undergone modifications as feed streams shifted from hydrolysates to fermentation broth. Specifically, reactive extraction has been superseded by more cost-effective separation methods, especially IEC. Initially, technical limitations such as resin capacity and stability as well as confidentiality due to military use had restricted the use of IEC for AA purification till the 1950 [198,354]. Following the substantial improvement in resin stability and capacity from the late 1960s to early 2000s, AA recovery during IEC increased (Fig. 2) and the technology became the main AA purification and separation method [[199], [200], [201], [202], [203], [204], [205]] resulting in process platformization around the technology (Fig. 1, Table 6).

For example, a single SACER column is capable of coarse AAs separation and removal of most impurities (see 4.1.1). Corresponding processes can reach purities up to 98% and recoveries in the 70-80% range when loaded with solids-free fermentation broth [203,[205], [206], [207]]. Additionally, multi-column settings consisting of anion exchange resins and SACERs can separate AAs and remove negatively charged solutes like organic acids present in fermentation broth respectively (Table 2) [197], resulting in product purity >98.5% [198,137]. Weak acid cation exchange resin (WACER), weak base anion exchange resin (WBAER), or amphoteric resin can be used as orthogonal techniques too and reach purities >99% [202].

An exception may be acidic Glu or basic Lys as their pIs differ from other canonic AAs (see Table A1) which can necessitate an additional crystallization step. Though IEC is suitable, alternative processes based on isoelectric crystallization are more cost efficient and sustainable (see Section 5.3.1).

Likewise, HIC can be used as an orthogonal purification method in case of Met, Phe and Tyr, achieving more than 99% purity. In theory, the separation performance of HIC can be improved by adjusting the temperature during loading and elution [355,356,348,318], but this seems impractical to implement at manufacturing scale, for example due to low surface-to-volume ratios of columns limiting temperature control and the small impact of the parameter on retention of Met, Phe, Tyr, and other hydrophobic AAs is minimal [357].

Process intensification

The conventional batchwise operation of bind-and-elute mode limits volumetric productivity of chromatographic purification and causes substantial upfront investment costs, for example because large column dimensions and quantities of resin are required to provide the necessary binding capacity to capture AAs at large-scale. Continuous operation can resolve this drawback as is reported for other biotechnological products like antibodies [358].

Specifically, periodic counter-current chromatography (PCCC) [359,360] and simulated moving bed (SMB) chromatography can be used, which has been successfully used to purify AAs (Fig. 2) [345,346,361]. Notably, a single-resin-type PCCC setup improved the process yield up to 93% [347,339]. Specifically, a series of three 1000-L SACER columns achieved a purity of 98.4% and a recovery of up to 97.1%, with a throughput of 2500 L of fermentation broth per hour [339]. When Lys is detected at the outlet of the 3rd column, the 1st column is disconnected and eluted while a fresh column is added at the end of system.

Interestingly, clarification of the fermentation broth was not required before feeding it to the column and cells were backflushed from the latter after elution. PCCC can also reduce freshwater consumption, waste water production and eluate volume by 93%, 65% 50% respectively compared to a single-column process [362].

The industrial-scale application of SMB for Lys purification was reported as early as 1997 [363]. The setup consisted of two carousels of chromatography columns. The first was used for primary recovery of Lys from unfiltered broth utilizing SACER at pH 3–4, reaching a purity of 97.5%, and the secondary carousel purifying the eluate with WACER to reach purity of 98.5%. The countercurrent operation facilitates a recovery up to 99%, and resin utilization is 90% higher compared to an equivalent fixed-bed setup as can be derived from what is known as the triangle theory and its expansions [364,365]. Despite the increased complexity of a two-carousel-setup, the authors reported mechanical robustness and reliability after one year of operation. It was disadvantageous that resin backwashing (i.e. operation in backward flow to remove solids from the resin) depended only on feed volume and not product concentration, thereby increasing waste buffer volume when low-concentration feeds were processed. Regardless of the high complexity and expensive equipment, SMB remains well-established and the most cost-effective process in large-scale chemical production [366,367]. For example, 60% of total p-xylene production of 80 Mt a-1 [368] relies on SMB adsorption and achieves >99% purity with 98% recovery [[369], [370], [371]]. For comparison, fractionated crystallization, used in the remaining 40% of the processes, reaches recoveries of 70% [372].

In many patents, the final formulation is often omitted from the process sequence. Probably because it is considered a matured technique with limited improvements in recovery in recent years (Fig. 2). The selection of the most suitable solid formulation step depends on the AA and the conditions after purification (e.g. concentration, pH, aggregate state). Multiple orthogonal crystallization steps are often used to improve AA recovery. For example, isoelectric crystallization at pH 3.2 recovery 83% of Glu and a subsequent low-pressure evaporation and cooling crystallization recovers additional 70% of Glu from the remaining mother liquor, achieving a total recovery of about 95% [207].

Chromatography-based integration of primary recovery and purification

It is possible to omit primary recovery (e.g., filtration or centrifugation) and subject the fermentation broth directly to chromatographic purification. To do so, resins with average particle sizes of 330–1100 μm were used and cells were removed by backwashing the column as part of the cleaning procedure. A purity >98%, and recovery >90% can be achieved with throughput up to 2500 L h-1 [339,373], which is equivalent to conventional processes relying on primary recovery. A similar approach can be applied for SMB (purity 97.5%, recovery 99%), relying on a short packed bed (0.3-1.0 m) and space above the packed bed to allow for up to 30% expansion in the backwash zone [363]. Notably, the capacity of the reported processes is limited by the resin binding capacity rather than by cell retention, despite relatively low AA concentration in feed for the first process (16 g L-1). In case of expanded bed adsorption (EBA) chromatography, developed in 1990s, the cells are in the flowthrough [374]. Flowthrough of the cell can increase the capacity for particulate matter as well as mass transfer rate, which may result in up to 2-fold higher productivity compared to packed bed using unclarified broth from antifungal peptide production [375]. On the other hand, equipment and process control are elaborate and error-prone. Although promising as an alternative to packed bed, EBA chromatography has no reported applications for amino acid purification as of the time of writing. Depending on the microorganism used in upstream production, the cultivation conditions and media composition, extensive fouling can occur during column loading up to the packed bed clogging or collapse of expanded bed. For example, anion exchange resins are prone to cell adsorption due to electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell membranes and high cell loads increase the cell adsorption in general [375]. This can necessitate a reduction in resin capacity and flow rates, increasing development efforts and reducing productivity. Therefore, chromatography with unclarified broth is applicable mainly for low to medium cell loads and basic AAs (e.g. Lys), which can use SACERs [339].

Despite the aforementioned improvements, chromatography still accounts for about 25% the of the costs if used for AA production [215]. Accordingly, various attempts were made to replace or omit chromatography in AA manufacturing.

Chromatography-free setups using crystallization and membrane separation

Crystallization based processes

It is also possible to crystalize AAs directly from filtered or even unfiltered fermentation broth. This is suitable mainly for basic AAs such as Lys and acidic AAs such as Glu due to their distinctive pI values (9.7 and 3.2, respectively, see Table A1), which facilitates selective isoelectric crystallization. In large-scale Glu production, the unfiltered broth is isoelectrically crystallized at pH 3.2 and then centrifuged. Resulting crystals have 95% purity and 75% recovery [207]. The mother liquor is usually processed further, either by ion exchange chromatography with SACER and ammonia elution (80% recovery, total yield 95%; rendering the process not “chromatography-free”), or, preferably, by another crystallization step, recycling the chemical agents used, reducing the sulfuric acid consumption by 50%, and completely removing the ammonia consumption, while maintaining 97.5% purity and 95% overall process yield [207].

If sulfate ions are present in the broth, the addition of chloride salts, such as potassium chloride, calcium chloride, to the filtered broth will result in the formation of solid sulfate salts and AA chlorides (Lys, Arg), obtaining crystals with 99% purity and a 90% recovery rate [316]. Lys and Arg can be crystallized with an equimolar amount of phosphoric acid at room temperature (i.e. 20°C to 25°C), consuming no energy for heating [317]. Selective crystallization and L/D separation of AAs can be achieved by seeding L-AA crystals in racemic mixtures [376]. Crystallization can be operated continuously via a combination of Mixed-Suspension, Mixed-Product-Removal (MSMPR) crystallizer with a tubular crystallizer system, facilitating segregated seeding and crystal growth [321]. To reduce water consumption in animal feed supplement production, direct spray drying of filtered Lys fermentation broth containing no harmful substances is possible, resulting in a feed supplement with 50-80% AA content [326]. To reduce energy demands (see Section 4.4.2), solutes should be present in high concentrations, thus the omission of chromatography or other alternative purification and concentration step in lower titers (<50 g L-1) may not be feasible.

Membrane based processes

If the starting AA concentration is <50 g L-1, ED and NF can be used as energy-saving alternatives to crystallization. ED currently represents a line of development that ceased in the 1990s (Fig. 1). The displayed process consisting of filtration, AC adsorption, and ED achieved up to 90% recovery with 80% energy efficiency of the device [236]. The main advantage is in-place desalting and parallel separation of AAs into multiple fractions.

However, feeds require extensive pretreatment, for example with aforementioned filtration and AC adsorption, as ED cannot remove non-charged impurities such as microorganisms and organic contaminants [377]. Similarly, these impurities can increase ED membrane fouling and scaling [378]. Though an ED device typically consists of multiple premanufactured stacks, like modules in other membrane processes, device operation is complex. Nevertheless, even with effective freshwater demineralization in place [377], high ion concentrations present in the feed (i.e. fermentation broth) substantially increase the energy consumption. For example, an increase in conductivity from 1.8 to 5.4 S m-1 doubled the energy demand [379]. Furthermore, desalting performance can be worse for such high conductivity compared to IEC [380]. It is expected that for these reasons there are limited records of ED based AA purification process in literature after 2000 and reported processes were conducted at laboratory scale only.

As a preferred alternative, pressure driven membrane processes were proposed. For example, a continuous Glu production process utilizing MF and NF was described [227]. A cross-flow MF module was fused first for cell separation to avoid fouling of the subsequent NF membrane which allowed Glu to permeate to the product stream whereas glucose from the fermentation media was rejected due to size exclusion and recycled. The maximum purity and yield of the membrane-integrated reactor system were 97% and 95%, respectively, while the maximum productivity was 8.6 g Glu L-1 h-1 [227]. The integration of NF and stream recycling can limit the product loss for both permeating and retained products (e.g. removal of monovalent ions).

Similarly, high pressure RO can be integrated with in situ product recovery from bioreactors, reducing production cost and greenhouse gas emissions of the subsequent LLE by 53% and 46%, respectively [273]. However, membrane-based concentration faces trade-offs between concentration factor and product recovery, which can also affect specific energy consumption per kg of product recovered. For example, even with 95% membrane rejection, up to 40% product loss can occur at only 10 diafiltration volumes [381]. Accordingly, process throughput costs and energy consumption should be carefully assessed [273] to establish a full understanding of how such a unit operation will affect parameters like the process mass intensity.

MF is also used for preparation of peptones, such as ones used for cell culture media. For example, plant or yeast lysates are filtered to remove particles, optionally combined with AC adsorption followed by UF. UF membranes with a MWCO of 3 kDa can be used to separate bioactive compounds, including AAs and peptides, from yeast lysate [382]. However, chemically defined media are the preferred option for animal cell culture, where AAs are included through exact amounts of pure single AA [383].

A potential platform process for amino acid purification

Food and feed applications

Food and feed applications can permit reduced or even minimal processing as purities of <60% can be acceptable (Fig. 4). Therefore, processes may change rapidly as technologies evolve that can meet product requirements. Accordingly, downstream processes of AA manufacturing have changed substantially over the last century (Fig. 1). After the first, heterogenous industrial AA production, processes platformized around IEC between 1965 and 2000, as the unit operation was compatible with all AAs. The advent of isoelectric crystallization and nanofiltration has allowed for more cost and energy-efficient processes to be developed, especially for basic and acidic AAs. To which extent crystallization and NF can also outperform IEC in terms of process mass intensity will require additional investigation. In the absence of post-2014 peer-reviewed applications for large-scale NF and AA purification in general, the conclusions drawn are inherently uncertain to a degree. Currently, the most common process layout seems i) biotechnological process feed, ii) filtration, iii) chromatography, iv) crystallization (Fig. 3, Table 6), with some alternative pathways emerging. Accordingly, it can be helpful to develop heuristics based on the current knowledge that can guide the selection of downstream processes to be developed in the future (Fig. 4), specifically accounting for the feed stream properties.

Although minimal processing (e.g. spray drying only) can be sufficient for highly concentrated feeds and products with moderate purity requirements, membrane technologies hold great potential to achieve intermediate purity (60–98%) with only a few process steps, especially in combination with crystallization. For the highest purities, chromatography will probably remain an indispensable unit operation.

Specifically, we think that multistage orthogonal crystallization (e.g. isoelectric and cooling) is the most cost and resource-effective DSP for basic or acidic AAs at a concentration >50 g L-1 with low impurity levels of ∼0.6 g L-1 as observed with C. glutamicum [385] and a target purity <98%. This is because a three-step DSP is often sufficient for processes with an initial AA concentration in the 3–100 g L-1 range and common impurities levels in fermentation broths of 0.5–5.0 g L-1 [385]. In the primary recovery step, the feed is clarified to remove the cells; this can be done via centrifugation, filtration, or combination of both. UF is especially beneficial, if high purity of the product is required. If colorization is present, activated carbon adsorption is suitable. The purification step consisting of multi-column IEC with SACER is recommendable, as IEC is the best all-round technique, and multi-column layout decreases the resources consumption while increasing the purity and recovery.

For large-scale production, a PCCC or SMB setup can be an alternative. Other types of chromatography (e.g. HIC, NPC, RPC, HILIC) cannot be recommended due to their environmental burden, high cost, or limited binding of AAs. If buffer exchange or desalination is required due to high conductivity, electrodialysis may be used, or IEC can be complemented or replaced (mainly in case of basic and acidic AAs) by NF. For final formulation, low-pressure cooling crystallization is most prevalent and offers feasible combination of energy demand and chemicals consumption. As bulk AAs are generally low-value products, highest possible yields should not impair cost-effectiveness and sustainability of the DSP. For example, the use of IE crystallization or NF instead of IEC if possible. For complex feeds or high purity requirements, the focus should be given to purification step improvement, mainly through means of different resin types (anion exchange, strong/weak) and process layout.

Importantly, if mixtures or groups of amino acids constitute the final product, unit operations with reduced selectivity but improved scalability and cost-efficacy like membrane filtration may outperform chromatographic setups.

Pharma applications

In contrast to food and feed, pharma applications typically require product purities in the 95–99% range or above [48,49]. For the latter to be achieved, a series of unit operations is required that is tailored to the product properties and unique impurity profiles originating from the upstream process. As such, identifying a certain unit operation that is advantageous over another is impractical. Instead, typical sequences can be identified. For example, membrane processes are often deployed before or in between chromatographic steps to facilitate concentration or buffer exchange [386], whereas the high selectivity of chromatography or crystallization can be exploited to achieve high product purity especially towards the end of the manufacturing process (Fig. 4).

In addition to the mere process performance in terms of product purity and recovery, additional safety aspects need to be considered for amino acids to be used in pharma applications. Specifically, processes need to comply with requirements such as good manufacturing practice (GMP) which are jointly developed by the International Council for Harmonisation of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH; representing the European Union, Japan and the USA) and then transferred to national or regional regulations. For manufacturing processes, the ICH quality guidelines are most relevant, for example Q7 (GMP), Q8 (pharmaceutical development) and Q13 (continuous manufacturing of drug substances and drug products). Specific aspects of these regulations include the validation of cleaning procedures (for example of multi-use membranes and chromatography columns), the identification, characterization and control of impurities as well as extractables and leachables.

Perspective

Although AA production is a well-established industry supplying products from bulk, moderate-purity animal feed supplements to high-purity pharmaceuticals, we think that new technologies can have a substantial impact on DSP design. Specifically, product profiles dictate the downstream process requirements and current tendencies in DSP improvement are focused mainly on cost reduction and process intensification including continuous operation. Sustainability aspects, such as the carbon footprint, water consumption, and the use of inorganic salts, are also critical aspects of these improvements and will become more relevant in the future due to an aggravating resource scarcity. Here, membrane technologies hold great potential due to simplicity, continuous operation and resource-efficacy. Whereas the latter is well established in terms energy consumption, it will require more detailed analyses with respect to product recovery and process mass intensity.

Specifically, we expect that future developments will strive to improve membrane performance to replace chromatography with integrated UF and NF alternatives, or IE crystallization-based processes, mainly for acidic and basic AAs. If chromatography is necessary to meet product specifications, we think that efforts will focus on process intensification by means of continuous operation such as PCCC or SMB. New chromatography stationary phases, membrane-, fiber-based, and cryogels are now being investigated for the purification of proteins and large molecules. For example, fiber-based chromatography allows for significantly higher throughput with capacity independent of residence time [387]. Though these techniques exhibit lower capacities for small molecules. Once these limitations have been addressed, such materials are likely to find their way into AAs purification too.

Modeling and process control play a crucial role in the efforts to intensify and optimize DSP. Mechanistic, hybrid, or machine learning models can improve our understanding of the process, enable fast in-silico process optimization, or reduce the number of required experiments in process development and we expect that these will facilitate the replacement of generic heuristics (Fig. 4).

Such improvements to both manufacturing and development are urgently needed because the demand for AAs as food and feed supplements will likely continue to increase, for example due to an increasing population, healthy diet awareness, sustainable farming, and natural resource scarcity. We also expect that additional markets will emerge, for example, AAs can be critical components of fermentation media for protein production, but also interestingly for cultured meat production media [388]. AAs are being included as an important supplement in various treatments, such as autologous cell therapies [389] and benefits of AA in cosmetics are being confirmed [390].

Although the large-scale amino acid manufacturing processes are well established, growing demand, sustainability goals and cost reduction continue to drive the research in this field. Specifically, we may see the emergence of new biotechnological feeds (e.g. using CO2-fixating archaea) which can be a disruptive shift in upstream processes and thus trigger a new phase of downstream process adaptation.
