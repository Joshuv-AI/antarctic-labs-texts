Cryo-EM structures of human P2X2/3 heteromer channel reveal the structural basis of ligand selectivity
Authors: Xinyu Cheng, Wei Zhang, Tong Che, Hongmin Guo, Weiwei Nan, Yuting Zhang, Ying Fu, Shuangyan Wan, Jin Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
P2X3 receptors are key mediators of adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP)–evoked cough reflexes and validated drug targets, but first-generation antagonists such as gefapixant are limited by off-target inhibition of P2X2/3 heteromers in taste pathways. Here, we report the first cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the human P2X2/3 heteromer in multiple ligand-bound states. The structures resolve its debated stoichiometries (1:2 and 2:1), reveal asymmetric pore organization, and identify heteromer-specific rearrangements that modulate ATP binding and gating. Structural and functional analyses show that gefapixant binds a conserved interfacial allosteric pocket, explaining its poor selectivity and taste-related side effects, whereas the next-generation antagonist camlipixant exploits a divergent vestibular site unique to P2X3, conferring >10,000-fold selectivity and improved tolerability. These findings establish a structural framework for P2X2/3 assembly and drug recognition and provide a roadmap for rational design of selective P2X modulators with enhanced safety and efficacy.

INTRODUCTION

Refractory or unexplained chronic cough (RUCC), defined as a persistent cough lasting longer than 8 weeks despite optimal treatment of underlying conditions, affects nearly 10% of the global population and imposes a profound burden on patients’ quality of life through sleep disturbance, social isolation, and psychological distress (1–3). Current therapies—many used off-label—offer only modest relief and are often accompanied by undesirable central nervous system or taste-related side effects, underscoring a critical unmet medical need for more effective and better-tolerated antitussives (4, 5).

Once considered merely a symptom of respiratory disease, RUCC is now recognized as a distinct clinical entity characterized by cough hypersensitivity syndrome (CHS) (6). CHS involves exaggerated responses to normally innocuous stimuli, driven by both peripheral and central neural sensitization (7). A central mediator of this hypersensitivity is the purinergic signaling system: Extracellular adenosine 5′-triphosphate (ATP), released during inflammation or mechanical stress, activates P2X receptor channels on vagal sensory neurons, thereby triggering and amplifying the cough reflex (8). Among the seven P2X subtypes, the homomeric P2X3 and the heteromeric P2X2/3 receptors are most abundantly expressed in airway-innervating sensory fibers and have emerged as key drivers of pathologic coughing (9).

The P2X family comprises trimeric cation channels formed by either three identical subunits (homomers) or combinations of two subunit types (heteromers) (10, 11). Unlike most ligand-gated ion channels, such as N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, which predominantly function as heteromeric complexes, P2X receptors primarily assemble and function as homotrimers. Only certain subtypes, notably P2X2 and P2X3, are capable of forming functional heterotrimers (12, 13). Among these, the P2X2/3 heterotrimer exhibits distinct pharmacology, characterized by higher agonist sensitivity and slower desensitization than homomer alone, thereby prolonging signal transmission in sensory neurons (14–16). However, its precise stoichiometry (1:2 versus 2:1) and assembly mechanism remain controversial, and the absence of high-resolution structures has hindered efforts to exploit heteromer-specific features for therapeutic design (17–19).

Since the first crystal structure of zebrafish P2X4 was reported in 2009, structural biology has greatly advanced our understanding of P2X receptor gating and ligand recognition (10). Cryo–electron microscopy (cryo-EM) studies have since resolved multiple conformations of human P2X3 and provided structural insights into P2X4 and P2X7, thereby revealing both orthosteric and allosteric ligand-binding sites that shape receptor pharmacology (20–28). For example, P2X3 homomer structures have revealed fine features of the orthosteric site and identified the allosteric pocket targeted by gefapixant, located at the interface of the left flipper, lower body, and dorsal fin domains (22, 26, 27). These studies highlighted a high degree of structural homology across P2X subunits, especially within the conserved orthosteric ATP-binding pocket, which complicates the design of subtype-selective ligands (29). More recently, a cryo-EM structure of human P2X3 bound to a selective antagonist revealed an additional allosteric site within the transmembrane domain that stabilizes the desensitized channel and displays partial selectivity between homomers and heteromers (30). These findings demonstrate that subtle conformational and surface differences can indeed be leveraged for therapeutic selectivity. Despite these advances, all structural insights have come from homomeric receptors. In contrast, no high-resolution structures of heteromeric P2X assemblies have been reported. This gap leaves fundamental questions unresolved: How do different subunits coassemble into functional trimers? What stoichiometries occur in vivo? How does heteromerization reshape gating, ligand recognition, and pharmacological profiles? Addressing these questions is particularly critical for the P2X2/3 heteromer, a receptor central to sensory signaling and a validated clinical target, where the lack of structural knowledge continues to impede rational development of subtype-selective modulators.

Clinical validation of P2X3-containing receptors as antitussive targets has come from trials of allosteric antagonists such as gefapixant and camlipixant, which significantly reduce cough frequency (31, 32). However, their clinical utility is constrained by on-target dysgeusia, experienced by over half of treated patients (33, 34). Initial hypotheses attributed taste disturbance to blockade of P2X2/3 heteromers in taste buds and drove efforts to enhance selectivity for P2X3 homomers (35). Unexpectedly, recent human tissue analyses reveal that taste nerves predominantly express P2X3 homomers, with P2X2 largely absent, indicating that dysgeusia arises from P2X3 inhibition per se rather than heteromer blockade (36). Thus, achieving tissue-specific functional selectivity—attenuating airway P2X3 activity while sparing taste pathways—presents a major design challenge.

Here, we present high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the human P2X2/3 heterotrimer in both the ATP-desensitized and gefapixant-bound states, alongside the closed-state structure of P2X3 in complex with camlipixant. We further resolve the conformational trajectory of the human P2X2 homomer, from apo to open and desensitized states, illuminating the full gating cycle. Comparative structural and pharmacological analyses reveal the asymmetric architecture and subunit stoichiometry of the heteromer, distinct ligand-binding modalities, and allosteric networks that govern receptor activation and inhibition. These insights establish a comprehensive blueprint for the rational design of next-generation P2X modulators, aimed at potent antitussive activity without inducing dysgeusia, and pave the way for therapeutic strategies to address the urgent unmet need in refractory chronic cough.

RESULTS

Biochemistry and electrophysiology of the P2X2/3 heteromer

To investigate the structure and function of the P2X2/3 heteromer ion channel, we coexpressed full-length human P2X2 and a truncated form of human P2X3 (residues 1 to 364) in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells (22). Voltage-clamp recordings revealed that application of α,β-methylene ATP to cells coexpressing P2X2 and P2X3 elicited biphasic inward currents, consisting of a rapidly desensitizing component followed by a slowly desensitizing phase (fig. S1A). Notably, reapplication of ATP within a short interval abolished the rapidly desensitizing component, consistent with use-dependent desensitization of the P2X3 subunit and previous reports (18). In contrast, expression of P2X2 alone generated only a slowly desensitizing current (fig. S1B), confirming the distinct gating contributions of each subunit within the heteromer.

For single-particle cryo-EM analysis of the heterotrimeric P2X2/3 channel, we coexpressed P2X2 and P2X3 in HEK293S GnTI− cells, fusing maltose-binding protein (MBP) to the N terminus of each subunit to enhance expression and solubility. Calcium flux assays and voltage-clamp recordings demonstrated that MBP fusion had no measurable impact on receptor function (fig. S1, C to F), validating the constructs for downstream structural studies. To selectively isolate the P2X2/3 heteromer and eliminate homomeric assemblies, we introduced orthogonal N-terminal affinity tags—FLAG on P2X2 and Strep on P2X3—and used tandem affinity purification. This strategy successfully enriched for complexes containing both subunits (fig. S2A). Subsequent size exclusion chromatography yielded a single, symmetrical peak, and SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis analysis of peak fractions confirmed coelution of P2X2 and P2X3, consistent with the formation of a stable heteromeric assembly (fig. S2, B and C). Last, protein mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of the purified complex revealed an ~1:1 molar ratio of P2X2 to P2X3 (fig. S2D), suggesting the coexistence of heterotrimeric assemblies with different stoichiometries.

The cryo-EM structure of the P2X2/3 heteromer has 1:2 or 2:1 subunit stoichiometry

To determine the cryo-EM structure of the P2X2/3 heteromer, we performed multiple rounds of heterogeneous refinement without symmetry constraints on a total of ~8,000,000 selected particles and identified three distinct classes of density maps (fig. S3, A and B). Two of these exhibited clear asymmetries between subunits, whereas the third displayed no discernible differences across subunits, suggesting a symmetric architecture.

Final nonuniform and local refinements yielded three cryo-EM maps at global resolutions ranging from 2.5 to 4.0 Å, all reconstructed with C1 symmetry (fig. S4 and table S1). During model building, we used differences in side-chain densities and glycosylation patterns between P2X2 and P2X3 to assign subunit identities (figs. S2E and S5). The two asymmetric classes were confidently determined to represent heteromeric assemblies of P2X2 and P2X3 subunits. In contrast, the third map showed uniform side-chain density consistent with P2X2, lacking any indication of P2X3-derived features. We therefore concluded that this class likely corresponds to residual homomeric P2X2 contamination.

To confirm this interpretation, we performed additional refinement on this symmetric class with C3 symmetry and built a high-resolution homomeric P2X2 model (fig. S4, C and D). As a control, we also solved the cryo-EM structure of full-length human P2X2 alone (fig. S6, A to D). Comparison of P2X2/3 and P2X2 homomer maps revealed that while the asymmetric P2X2/3 maps were structurally distinct, the side-chain densities of symmetric maps from both datasets were nearly identical. Given the negligible differences, we adopted the C3-symmetric P2X2 map, which reached a resolution of 2.2 Å, as the representative structure for the homomer (figs. S6, E and F, and S7A). Last, detailed inspection of the asymmetric P2X2/3 maps allowed unambiguous subunit assignment based on their structural signatures. Our findings indicate that the P2X2/3 heteromer adopts asymmetric trimeric assemblies, most likely in 1:2 or 2:1 stoichiometries, offering a structural basis for its unique functional properties.

Overall architecture of the P2X2/3 heteromer

All structures of the human P2X2/3 exhibit the canonical trimeric architecture typical of P2X receptors, with well-defined densities corresponding to ATP observed in the orthosteric ligand-binding sites (Fig. 1, A and B, and fig. S7B). Notably, no exogenous ATP was introduced during purification, suggesting that the bound ATP likely originated from endogenous sources released during cell lysis, a phenomenon also reported in previous P2X3 structural studies (22). No ATP density was observed in the separately expressed P2X2 homomer, which adopts an apo-closed conformation (fig. S7A).

Although the overall trimeric architecture is largely conserved upon superposition of the P2X2/3 heterotrimer with either the P2X2 or P2X3 homotrimer (fig. S8, A to D), the heterotrimer displays distinct structural features attributable to incorporation of the P2X3 subunit. Structural alignment shows that the hP2X2/3 (1:2) and hP2X2/3 (2:1) assemblies exhibit Cα root mean square deviation (RMSD) values of 1.624 and 1.657 Å, respectively, when aligned with the hP2X2 homotrimer [Protein Data Bank (PDB) accession code 9UWY; this study]. Similarly, alignment with the hP2X3 homotrimer (PDB accession code 9IK1) yields Cα RMSD values of 1.405 and 1.500 Å for the (1:2) and (2:1) assemblies, respectively. In each heteromer assembly, transmembrane helix 2 (TM2) from all three subunits lines the central ion conduction pore. In the P2X2/3 heteromers, the pore constriction is formed by V354 (P2X2) and V334 (P2X3), with measured pore radii smaller than the ~1.5-Å threshold characteristic of the desensitized P2X3 state—too narrow to permit passage of hydrated Na+ ions (Fig. 2, D, E, and O; Fig. 2P for sequence alignment). In contrast, the homomeric P2X2 pore is constricted by residue L358, with an even narrower radius of <0.5 Å (Fig. 2F), reinforcing its nonconductive, desensitized state.

Heteromer assembly not only preserves pore closure but also introduces pronounced asymmetry in TM2 positioning. Structural alignment of the heteromers with the desensitized P2X2-ATP structure (PDB accession code 9UWY; this study) revealed conformational perturbations driven by P2X3 incorporation. In the P2X2/3 (2:1) configuration, the single P2X3 subunit induces an ~2-Å counterclockwise rotation in its TM2 helix, disrupting trimeric symmetry (Fig. 2, G to I). The asymmetry is further exacerbated in the 1:2 heteromer, where TM2 helices from P2X3-I and P2X3-II undergo ~2- and ~5-Å counterclockwise displacements, respectively (Fig. 2, J to L). This progressive asymmetry is correlated with distinct positional shifts in the conserved gating residue V334 in P2X3 across heteromeric states (Fig. 2N).

Distinct intersubunit interactions of the P2X2/3 heteromer

The high conformational similarity between P2X2 and P2X3 subunits in the P2X2/3 heteromer (Fig. 2, G and J) suggests that P2X2-P2X3 intersubunit interactions closely resemble those between adjacent subunits in P2X2 or P2X3 homotrimers. This structural homology, together with conserved interface residues, enables P2X2 and P2X3 to substitute for each other, forming a stable heterotrimer whose overall architecture closely resembles that of the P2X2 homomer (PDB accession code 9UWY; this study). Nonetheless, detailed analysis reveals subtle but functionally significant differences in intersubunit interactions across the heteromer, particularly within the head, lower body, and upper body regions (fig. S9, A and B).

In the head region, two pairs of conserved electrostatic contacts are preserved (fig. S9, C and D). At the P2X2–P2X3-I interface of the P2X2/3 (1:2) structure, D94 (P2X2) forms a salt bridge with R295 (P2X3), while D97 (P2X2) engages W152 (P2X3) through van der Waals contacts. Similarly, at the P2X3-II–P2X2 interface, D76 and D79 (P2X3) interact with W175 and R315 (P2X2), respectively. These interactions were highly stable in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, with the D94-R295 salt bridge retained in >80% of frames, comparable to the P2X2 homotrimer (fig. S9D).

In the lower body, heteromer-specific modifications were evident (fig. S9, E and F). In the P2X2 homotrimer, E75-R286 is the canonical salt bridge. At the P2X2–P2X3-I interface of the P2X2/3 (1:2) structure, R286 (P2X2) engages both E57 and E187 (P2X3). By contrast, at the P2X3-II–P2X2 interface, substitution of R286 with T263 weakens the E75 (P2X2) interaction. This is partly compensated by a new E75-K242 salt bridge and an S267-H204 electrostatic contact. At the P2X3-I–P2X3-II interface, only the E57-K242 salt bridge is conserved, highlighting asymmetry in the lower-body network.

In the upper body, conserved interactions ensure structural stability (fig. S9, G and H). Notably, the D174-R308 salt bridge and N176-Y310 hydrogen bond (P2X2-P2X3) were maintained in >90% of MD frames, identical to the P2X2 homotrimer (fig. S9H). These interactions anchor the upper body to the extracellular domain (ECD), preserving trimer integrity despite differences below.

Overall, the P2X2/3 heteromer retains the core intersubunit network of P2X2 homotrimers in the head and upper body, while introducing lower-body modifications that create asymmetry. This combination of conserved and heteromer-specific interactions explains why P2X2 and P2X3 can form a stable heterotrimer: The conserved regions ensure overall structural compatibility, and the adaptive differences at lower-body interfaces and ATP binding sites stabilize the heteromeric assembly and confer unique functional properties.

ATP-binding pocket of the P2X2/3 heteromer

Among the P2X receptor family, rapidly desensitizing subtypes (such as P2X1 and P2X3) generally display higher sensitivity to ATP than slowly desensitizing subtypes like P2X2 (37). P2X2 can form functional heteromers with these rapidly desensitizing subunits (38). However, the ATP sensitivity of the P2X2/3 heteromer is intermediate between that of P2X2 and P2X3. This was confirmed in the present study by calcium influx assays using α,β-methylene ATP, a more stable analog of ATP. The median effective concentration (EC50) of α,β-methylene ATP for the P2X2/3 heteromer was determined to be 1.0 μM (fig. S1C), consistent with previously reported values (18).

The cryo-EM structure of the ATP-bound P2X2/3 heteromer revealed extensive ligand-receptor interactions and pronounced geometric differences between the ATP-binding pockets formed by P2X2 and P2X3 subunits (Fig. 3, A and C). Stoichiometry-dependent variation in orthosteric binding sites is a well-recognized phenomenon in ligand-gated ion channels, such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and NMDA receptors, and may contribute to differential ligand selectivity. Consistent with this, comparative analysis of the P2X2/3 (2:1) (PDB accession code 9UX2) and P2X2/3 (1:2) (PDB accession code 9UX1) assemblies revealed distinct ATP-binding environments. At the P2X2-I–P2X3 interface of the P2X2/3 (2:1) complex, ATP forms hydrogen bonds with P2X2 residues N300/R302/K319 and P2X3 residues K63/K65/T172, together with hydrophobic contacts involving P2X2-L150, P2X3-F174, and P2X3-I215. In contrast, at the P2X3-II–P2X2 interface of the P2X2/3 (1:2) complex, ATP engages six conserved hydrogen bonds (P2X3-N279/R281/K299; P2X2-K81/K83/T196) and an additional interaction with P2X2-K224. The hydrophobic network also diverges: P2X3-F174 present in the P2X2/3 (2:1) complex is replaced by P2X2-L198 in the P2X2/3 (1:2) complex, while P2X2-L150 is substituted by P2X3-P128, which repositions away from the binding pocket (Fig. 3, B and D). These differences likely arise from sequence variations and local rearrangements around the loop region above the binding site, including insertions and deletions. To better characterize these differences, we aligned and compared the P2X2 and P2X3 subunits within the P2X2/3 (1:2) heterotrimeric structure (Cα RMSD = 2.0 Å). Structurally, P2X2 adopts the conserved P2XR fold, featuring a large ECD, two transmembrane helices (TM1 and TM2), and intracellular N- and C-terminal regions. The overall topology resembles other P2X receptors and can be metaphorically described as a “dolphin” breaching the membrane, with anatomical analogs for the head, fins, body, and tail (Fig. 3E). Despite the overall structural similarity between P2X2 and P2X3, we observed distinct conformational differences in a loop located directly above the orthosteric ATP-binding pocket (fig. S8E). In P2X3, this loop is positioned lower compared to its counterpart in P2X2 and engages in magnesium-mediated coordination with ATP via residue D158 (Fig. 3F) (27). Sequence alignment confirms that this loop is poorly conserved across P2XR subtypes (Fig. 3G), and the cryo-EM density maps clearly support its divergent conformations in P2X2 versus P2X3 (Fig. 3, H and I). Notably, the ATP-binding pocket in P2X3 is more compact than that in P2X2, which may underlie the increased ATP sensitivity and rapid desensitization of P2X3.

Human P2X2 receptor gating cycle

To elucidate the complete gating mechanism of P2X2, we determined its structure in the ATP-bound open state, complementing our previously resolved structures of the apo-closed and desensitized conformations. To capture the open state while minimizing rapid desensitization, 100 μM ATP was added before cryo-EM grid preparation. Following data collection and processing, we obtained a 2.4-Å resolution structure of ATP-bound P2X2 (Fig. 4, A and B, and fig. S10).

Although the structure exhibited key features of an activated channel, the characteristic cytoplasmic cap observed in some P2X family members was absent, which may reflect construct-specific factors (N-terminal MBP tag and linker) and/or subtype-dependent variability, and suggests that the current structure may represent an intermediate along the activation-desensitization pathway rather than a canonical open state (39). Functionally, calcium influx assays determined an EC50 of 424 nM for ATP (fig. S1F), indicating a higher sensitivity than previously reported, likely reflecting variations in experimental conditions.

The ATP-binding mode in the open-state structure closely resembled that observed in the desensitized state, with ATP engaging conserved polar residues. Specifically, K81, K83, R302, and K319 formed salt bridges with the phosphate chain, while T196 and N300 formed hydrogen bonds with the adenine and ribose moieties (Fig. 4C). Functional validation using single-alanine mutagenesis revealed that substitutions at K81, K83, T196, N300, and R302 significantly decreased ATP potency, confirming their essential roles in ligand recognition and channel activation (Fig. 4, D and E). In addition to polar interactions, several hydrophobic residues—L198, L223, and I238—surrounded the adenine ring of ATP, forming a shallow hydrophobic pocket. Alanine substitutions at these positions also reduced ATP potency (Fig. 4, D and E), suggesting that both hydrophobic and polar interactions contribute to ligand efficacy. To define structural transitions during channel gating, we compared the apo-closed and ATP-bound open states of P2X2. In the closed state, I343 and T350 formed the extracellular and intracellular constriction points, respectively, with pore diameters of 0.3 and 0.5 Å, which are too narrow for dehydrated sodium ions to pass (Fig. 4, F and H). In the open state, the channel displayed a continuous transmembrane pore with a minimum radius of 2.5 Å, sufficient to accommodate partially hydrated sodium ions (Fig. 4, G and H), confirming its conductive state. Structural superposition revealed that upon ATP binding, I343 and T350 undergo upward and outward movements toward the extracellular side, rotating away from the pore axis to open the channel (Fig. 4I). These conformational shifts define the physical gating mechanism by which ATP binding drives P2X2 activation.

The selective mechanism of drug molecules

To elucidate the molecular basis of taste-related side effects associated with the marketed drug gefapixant, we first evaluated its pharmacological profile. Calcium influx assays yielded median inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of 56 nM for P2X3 homomers and 474 nM for P2X2/3 heteromers (fig. S11), confirming its poor selectivity between these receptor forms. We next determined the cryo-EM structure of the P2X2/3 heteromer bound to gefapixant at 2.6-Å resolution (2:1 stoichiometry; fig. S12). Despite the presence of ATP in the orthosteric pocket, the receptor remained closed, indicating that gefapixant stabilizes the desensitized state. Gefapixant was observed occupying a negative allosteric site at the interface of P2X2 and P2X3 subunits (Fig. 5A), consistent with previous reports (26). Notably, density was resolved only at one P2X2–P2X3-II interface, suggesting asymmetric binding (Fig. 5B and fig. S13).

To determine the binding mode of gefapixant in the P2X2/3 heteromer, we analyzed the structure and found that gefapixant forms hydrogen bonds with K200 and N214 (P2X2), electrostatic interactions with D266 and S267 (P2X3), and hydrophobic contacts with I78 (P2X2), I215 (P2X2), V238 (P2X3), and V274 (P2X3) (Fig. 5E). Comparison with the P2X3 homomer revealed near-complete conservation of the binding pocket, apart from I78/I215 in P2X2 versus V61/L191 in P2X3 (Fig. 5F), explaining gefapixant’s low selectivity. Mutational validation further confirmed critical roles for K200 (P2X2) and S267 (P2X3), as substitutions at these positions markedly reduced drug potency, while D266A nearly abolished inhibition (Fig. 5G). An additional steric constraint at P290 (P2X2) versus S267 (P2X3) partially limited gefapixant binding in heteromers (Fig. 5, H and I, and fig. S13, A and B). Sequence alignment revealed that these sites are not conserved across the P2X family, consistent with gefapixant’s lack of activity at other subtypes.

In contrast, camlipixant, a next-generation P2X3 inhibitor, showed striking selectivity. Calcium influx assays demonstrated potent inhibition of P2X3 homomers (IC50 = 6 nM) but negligible activity at P2X2/3 heteromers (IC50 = 59 μM; fig. S11). The cryo-EM structure of camlipixant bound to the P2X3 homomer at 2.6 Å (fig. S14) revealed a distinct allosteric site in the extracellular vestibule, different from the gefapixant pocket (Fig. 6, A and D). Camlipixant binding induced an ~5-Å clockwise rotation of the upper body and head domains, blocking ATP access and locking the channel in an apo-closed state (fig. S15A). At this vestibular site, camlipixant engages in hydrogen bonds with K284 and E293, a critical cation-π interaction with R73, and extensive hydrophobic contacts with M75, I93, M96, V98, F282, and L298 (Fig. 6E). Functional assays confirmed their importance: R73A nearly abolished activity, whereas K284A, E293A, and Y285A markedly reduced inhibition (Fig. 6H). Structural comparison with P2X2/3 heteromers revealed substitutions that disrupt camlipixant binding: R73 (P2X3) is replaced by K91 (P2X2), and hydrophobic residues (M75, M96, V98, and L298) are substituted by bulkier or slimmer residues (W93, V114, A116, and I318), disrupting the hydrophobic network (Fig. 6F). At the P2X2–P2X3-II interface, E293 (P2X3) is replaced by T313 (P2X2), further weakening ligand interactions (Fig. 6G). The M75W mutation, mimicking the homologous residue in P2X2, also completely abolished camlipixant inhibition, confirming the critical role of this residue in determining binding specificity (Fig. 6H).

DISCUSSION

P2X receptors are ATP-gated trimeric ion channels that mediate essential physiological processes, including sensory neurotransmission, inflammation, and nociception (40). These receptors can assemble as homomeric or heteromeric complexes, each exhibiting distinct functional and pharmacological profiles (41). Among them, P2X3 has emerged as a validated therapeutic target for chronic cough, due to its prominent expression in vagal afferents of the respiratory tract and its role in ATP-evoked cough reflexes (42). However, the clinical utility of first-generation P2X3 antagonists such as gefapixant has been hindered by off-target inhibition of P2X2/3 heteromers, which are expressed in taste pathways, leading to dose-limiting taste disturbances such as hypogeusia and ageusia (43). A major barrier to developing more selective P2X3-targeting therapies has been the lack of structural information on the P2X2/3 heteromer, which has precluded rational drug design strategies aimed at minimizing off-target effects. In this study, we report the first high-resolution cryo-EM structures of the human P2X2/3 heteromer in multiple ligand-bound states, unveiling critical insights into its subunit stoichiometry, asymmetry, and allosteric modulation.

Our structural analysis reveals that the P2X2/3 heteromer adopts a canonical trimeric arrangement but exists in two distinct stoichiometries: P2X2/3 (1:2) and P2X2/3 (2:1). This directly addresses the longstanding debate over P2X2/3 subunit composition observed in prior biochemical and electrophysiological studies, which reported inconsistent stoichiometry (17–19). The structures exhibit pronounced subunit asymmetry, with progressive deviations in TM2 positioning depending on the relative number of P2X3 subunits. These asymmetric features reshape the central ion-conducting pore, potentially contributing to the heteromer’s unique desensitization kinetics and ATP sensitivity, which are intermediate between those of the P2X2 and P2X3 homomers. A particularly notable structural feature is a nonconserved extracellular loop above the ATP-binding pocket of the P2X2 subunit, which undergoes significant positional rearrangements upon heteromerization. This loop modulates both the geometry and electrostatics of the orthosteric site, potentially enhancing ATP binding affinity in the heteromer compared to the P2X2 homomer, where ATP density was absent in the apo-closed conformation. These findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the unique biophysical properties of the P2X2/3 heteromer and reinforce the notion that subunit composition and arrangement can allosterically fine-tune channel function, as observed in other heteromeric ligand-gated ion channels such as NMDA receptors (44–48).

Our structural and functional analyses of gefapixant and camlipixant provide important insights into the molecular basis of antagonist selectivity at P2X receptors. Gefapixant, the first clinically approved P2X3-targeting drug, binds to a conserved allosteric pocket at the P2X2-P2X3 subunit interface and stabilizes a desensitized channel state, even in the presence of ATP. While effective in suppressing P2X3 activity, the high conservation of this pocket across homomeric and heteromeric assemblies explains gefapixant’s limited selectivity and associated taste-related side effects. Subtle sequence differences between homomers and heteromers, such as the substitution of S267 in P2X3 by P290 in P2X2, introduce steric constraints that partially alter drug efficacy but are insufficient to confer specificity (26). These findings indicate that small variations within a largely conserved pocket cannot fully resolve the therapeutic trade-off between efficacy and tolerability.

In contrast, camlipixant achieves remarkable subtype selectivity by exploiting a structurally distinct allosteric pocket located in the extracellular vestibule, spatially separated from the gefapixant site (49). This vestibular site is stabilized by hydrogen bonding, cation-π interactions, and extensive hydrophobic contacts, but critically, it is poorly conserved among P2X subtypes (including P2X4 and P2X7) (23, 50). Substitutions of key residues such as M75 in P2X3 with bulkier side chains (e.g., W93 in P2X2 and W/F in most other P2X subtypes) introduce steric hindrance that effectively precludes camlipixant binding in heteromeric or non-P2X3 assemblies. This divergence provides a clear structural rationale for camlipixant’s >10,000-fold preference for P2X3 homomers and its improved clinical tolerability. Our analysis of the resolved P2X family structures further confirms this point (fig. S15, C to F) (39, 51, 52).

Together, these findings highlight two contrasting pharmacological paradigms: Targeting a conserved allosteric pocket, as exemplified by gefapixant, yields broad but poorly selective inhibition with undesirable off-target effects, whereas engaging a structurally divergent vestibular site, as seen with camlipixant, enables exquisite subtype selectivity and an improved safety profile. More broadly, our results establish a framework for the rational design of P2X modulators by linking site conservation and subunit context to pharmacological outcomes. Future work, including mutational dissection of vestibular residues and exploration of analogous pockets across P2X family members, will be critical to determine whether the selectivity principles uncovered here can be generalized to other heteromeric ligand-gated ion channels.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Electrophysiology

All P2X2 constructs were inserted into the pCDH-EF1-copGFP vector, which allows for the coexpression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP). All P2X3 constructs were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector, and mCherry was additionally added at the N terminus. Mutagenesis was performed using site-directed mutagenesis with designed missense primers. Site-directed mutagenesis used manually designed primers. The sequences of all channel constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing.

HEK293-T cells (American Type Culture Collection) were maintained according to the supplier’s recommendations. The cells were seeded in six-well plates for whole-cell patch-clamp recordings. The following day, the plasmid was transfected using the transfection reagent polyethylenimine (PEI; MKbio), as detailed in the manufacturer’s protocol. Coexpression of P2X2 and P2X3 was achieved using a transfection ratio of 1:2, unless mentioned otherwise. Membrane currents were recorded with a standard patch-clamp technique in the whole-cell configuration. The pipettes were filled with intracellular solution containing (in millimolar) 142 NaCl, 5 BAPTA [1,2-bis(2-aminophenoxy)ethane-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid], 5 EGTA, and 10 Hepes at pH 7.4. The pipette resistance was from 2.0 to 5.0 MΩ. The bath solution contained (in millimolar) 142 NaCl, 10 EGTA, 10 Hepes, and 10 glucose at pH 7.4. To increase the speed of recovery from the hP2X3 receptors, we additionally used a bath solution containing (in millimolar) 142 NaCl, 10 Hepes, 10 glucose, 2 CaCl2, and 1 MgCl2. The saturation of activation was determined with ATP at 10 μM. The currents were recorded with a HEKA EPC 10 amplifier in combination with the patchmaster software. The sampling rate was 10 kHz, and the recordings were on-line filtered at 2.9 kHz using a four-pole Bessel filter. Cells with both eGFP green fluorescence and mCherry red fluorescence were selected to record currents at a constant holding potential of −50 mV, and the series resistance was compensated with the patchmaster software up to 80%.

Cell-based calcium influx assays

All constructs of P2X2 and P2X3 were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector. Mutagenesis was performed using site-directed mutagenesis with designed missense primers. Site-directed mutagenesis used manually designed primers. The sequences of all channel constructs were confirmed by DNA sequencing. Coexpression of P2X2 and P2X3 was achieved using a transfection ratio of 1:2, unless mentioned otherwise.

Human P2X2, P2X3, or P2X2/3 receptor-expressing HEK293 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Corning) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Corning) and 1% (v/v) antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco) at 37°C with 5% CO2. The 96-well black plate (Corning) and poly-d-lysine (PDL; Gibco) were prepared, and each 96-wells were coated with 50 μl of PDL to be flattened in each well. After 5 to 6 hours, the PDL layer was sucked from the 96-well black plate, and the plate was dried in room temperature for at least more than 6 hours. For the cells seeding on the dried plate, the dish filled with more than 80% of cells was prepared. The DMEM used for cell culture was sucked from the dish, and 2 ml of Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Corning) was applied to the dish for washing. The PBS was removed, and the cells on the dish were detached by 1 ml of trypsin/EDTA (Gibco). The cells were collected in a conical tube and subsided by centrifugation with 1000 rpm for 5 min. The DMEM layer was sucked, and the cells were gently resuspended by adding 1 ml of DMEM. The prepared cells were applied to the plate with 100 μl per well, resulting in 4.8 × 104 cells per well. After incubation of the cell-seeded plate for 18 hours, Fluo-5 dye (FLIPR Calcium 5 Assay Kit) prepared according to protocol of the kit was added to the plate with 100 μl per well. The plate was incubated for 1 hour, and 50 μl of various concentrations of compounds was applied to each well. The final concentration of compounds was 1/10 of the prepared concentration. The compound-treated plate was sealed to protect from light and kept in a hood for 10 min. Real-time fluorescence was measured by a FlexStation 3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices) instrument using the prepared α,β-methylene ATP solution at excitation wavelength of 485 nm and emission wavelength of 525 nm. For the P2X2, no additional compounds were added. Real-time fluorescence was directly measured using ATP solutions of different concentrations.

Expression and purification of the human P2X2, P2X3, and P2X2/3 receptor

Human P2X2 (UniProt: Q9UBL9; residues 1 to 471) and P2X3 (UniProt: P56373; residues 1 to 364) (with enhanced expression level and stability) were cloned into the pEG BacMam vector for coexpression, respectively. The P2X2 construct included an N-terminal 3× FLAG tag and a 15–amino acid glycine-serine (GS) linker followed by an MBP tag. The P2X3 construct included an N-terminal Twin-StrepII tag and a 15–amino acid GS linker followed by an MBP tag. The MBP tag and P2X constructs are connected via the HRV3C protease cleavage site (LEVLFQGP). When expressing P2X2 or P2X3 alone, no additional FLAG tag or Strep tag is added to the construct.

For P2X2/3 heteromer protein expression, P2X2-EM and P2X3-EM were heterologously expressed in HEK293S cells. When cell density reached 1.0 to 1.5 × 106 cells/ml, the cells were cotransfected with plasmids at mass ratio 1:2 for P2X2-EM and P2X3-EM. For 1-liter HEK293S cell culture, the plasmids (~1 mg) were premixed with linear PEIs (MKbio) in 50-ml fresh medium for 15 to 30 min. The mixture was then added into cell culture followed by 15-min incubation. After 24-hour incubation at 37°C, 10 mM sodium butyrate was added to induce the protein expression at 30°C. Cells were harvested after 48 hours and then flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until needed. For P2X2 or P2X3 protein expression, P2X2-EM or P2X3-EM was individually expressed in HEK293F cells. When cell density reached 1.0 to 1.5 × 106 cells/ml, the cells were transfected with plasmids for P2X2-EM or P2X3-EM.

For P2X2/3 heteromer protein purification, 4-liter cell pellets were resuspended in 200 ml of hypotonic buffer [25 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM tris(2-carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), and 10% glycerol] supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Selleck) for 30 min with gentle agitation. Crude cell membranes were collected by ultracentrifugation at 32,000 rpm for 35 min. The membranes were then resuspended and solubilized in buffer containing 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, and 0.5% lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol:cholesteryl hemisuccinate (LMNG:CHS) (10:1, w/w) for 2 to 2.5 hours at 4°C. After centrifugation at 32,000 rpm for 45 min, the supernatant was incubated with Anti-FLAG Affinity Resin at 4°C for 1 hour. This was then washed with washing steps in 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 100 μM TCEP, 10% glycerol, 0.1% LMNG, and 0.01% CHS for 20 column volumes. The protein was eluted with FLAG peptide in wash buffer and then incubated with Strep-Tactin beads at 4°C for 2 hours. This was then washed with washing steps in 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 100 μM TCEP, 10% glycerol, 0.03% LMNG, and 0.003% CHS for 10 column volumes. The final protein was eluted with desthiobiotin in wash buffer and subsequently concentrated by a 100-kDa concentrator (Millipore) before being injected onto a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 100 μM TCEP, 0.003% LMNG, 0.001% glyco-diosgenin (GDN), and 0.0001% CHS. The peak fractions were pooled, concentrated to 5 to 6 mg/ml using a 100-kDa molecular weight cut-off (MWCO) centrifugal device (Millipore) before cryo-EM sample preparation. The P2X2/3-gefapixant samples were expressed and purified using a similar protocol, except that 50 μM gefapixant was added throughout the purification process and before cryo-EM sample preparation.

For P2X2 or P2X3 protein purification, 500-ml cell pellets were resuspended in 25 ml of hypotonic buffer (25 mM Hepes, pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM TCEP, and 10% glycerol) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Selleck) for 30 min with gentle agitation. Crude cell membranes were collected by ultracentrifugation at 32,000 rpm for 35 min. The membranes were then resuspended and solubilized in buffer containing 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM CaCl2, 2 mM TCEP, 10% glycerol, and 0.5% LMNG:CHS (10:1, w/w) for 2 to 2.5 hours at 4°C. After centrifugation at 32,000 rpm for 45 min, the supernatant was incubated with amylose resin (NEB) at 4°C for 2 hours. This was then washed with washing steps in 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 100 μM TCEP, and 10% glycerol supplemented with different concentrations of detergents: first 0.1% LMNG, 0.01% CHS, then 0.03% LMNG and 0.003% CHS for 10 column volumes each. The protein was eluted with 40 mM maltose in wash buffer and subsequently concentrated by a 100-kDa concentrator (Millipore) before being injected onto a Superose 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated with 20 mM Hepes (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 100 μM TCEP, 0.003% LMNG, 0.001% GDN, and 0.0001% CHS. The peak fractions were pooled, concentrated to 10 mg/ml using a 100-kDa MWCO centrifugal device (Millipore) before cryo-EM sample preparation. For the P2X2-ATPopen samples, to capture the open state while minimizing desensitization, 100 μM ATP was added 30 s before grid blotting and vitrification. For the P2X3-camlipixant samples, 50 μM camlipixant was added throughout the purification process and before cryo-EM sample preparation.

Gel destaining and in gel digestion

Protein bands were excised from the Coomassie stained gel, cut into small cubes (~1 × 1 mm3) and destained in 150 μl of destaining solution (10% ethanol and 2% phosphoric acid) for 30 min at 37°C and 550 rpm in Thermomixer (Comfort, Eppendorf). This procedure was repeated two to three times until the gel pieces and destaining solution no longer displayed a blue color. Thereupon, destaining solution was removed, and the gel pieces were dried at 30°C in a SpeedVac.

The reduction with dithiothreitol and alkylation of proteins have been done. Acetonitrile (ACN) (80%) was added to dry the gel pieces. After 15-min dehydration in the SpeedVac, the dried gel pieces were completely immersed in digestion solution (~200 μl). The digestion solution consisted of sequencing-grade modified porcine trypsin (Promega, Madison, USA), which was diluted in 40 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 8.6) to a concentration of 12.5 ng/μl. The protein digestion was performed overnight at 37°C with tempered shaker (550 rpm; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After protein digestion, the peptides were extracted from the gel pieces. The supernatant was transferred to autosampler vials (SureSTART, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The extracted peptides were dried using a SpeedVac at room temperature and stored at 4°C before MS analysis.

LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis

The mass spectrometry analysis was performed by the Core Facility for Biomedical Science of Nanchang University. Dried peptides were resuspended in buffer A (0.1% formic acid in mass grade water; Thermo Fischer Scientific, GmbH, Germany) and sonicated for 10 min in an ultrasonic bath (SB-5200DT, SCIENT, China). The liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization tandem mass spectrometry (LC-ESI-MS/MS) system consisted of a nanogradient ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) pump (Vanquish Neo, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) interfaced to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For LC, an Acclaim PepMap C18 trap column (100 Å, 5 μm, 75 μm by 20 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was coupled to a Syncronis C18 separation column (75 μm by 250 mm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The nanospray source was a stainless-steel tip with nano-bore emitters (catalog no. ES542, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China). Xcalibur (Version 4.7) was used for the software-based instrument control of the mass spectrometer.

For the ultra performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) method, the flow rate was 0.3 μl/min. A 65-min gradient was used with 0 to 3 min: 2 to 5% buffer B (80% ACN, UPLC/MS, Thermo Fischer Scientific, USA); 3 to 48 min: 5 to 25% buffer B; 48 to 56 min: 25 to 35% buffer B; and 57 to 65 min: 95% buffer B. The analytical column oven was set to 60°C.

Samples were separated using a nanoElute LC system with the gradient as described above. The MS data were acquired using the DIA method in auto mode. The isolation window was set to 5 mass/charge ratio (m/z) and windows overlap is 0 m/z. The capillary voltage was set to 1700 V. The FAIMS CV was −45 V. The MS and MS/MS mass ranges were acquired from 400 to 900 m/z resolution. The HCD collision energy type was set to normalized with energy of 30%. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set to custom mode with 800% of normalized AGC target. The resolution of master scan was set to 60,000 with 100 events scans with 30,000 resolutions. The maximum injection time of master scan was on custom mode with 100 ms. The maximum injection time of event scan was set to auto.

Protein identification and label-free quantification

MaxQuant (version 2.6.7.0) with Andromeda search engine was used for protein identification and label-free quantification (LFQ) (53–55). The protein identification was against the complete proteome database of human. The mass tolerance for calibrated precursor ions was set to 4.5 ppm; the mass tolerance for fragment ions was set to 0.6 Da. Only tryptic peptides with up to two missed cleavages were accepted. The oxidation of methionine, acetylation on protein N terminus, and propionamide on cysteine were admitted as a variable peptide modification. The false discovery rate was 0.01 for protein. For protein quantification, the “Label free quantification (Max LFQ)” function in MaxQuant was used (53). This method is based on the “delayed normalization” strategy, which, after the identification of peptides, determines the normalization coefficients for each LC-MS/MS run.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

Purified protein samples (3 to 4 μl) were applied to glow-discharged 300-mesh R1.2/1.3 UltrAuFoil grids (Quantifoil) or holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Au R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh). The grids were plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 10-s wait period, −2 blot force, 3-s blotting time, 100% humidity, and 4°C. Vitrified grids were clipped with Autogrid carriers (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Images were collected on a Titan Krios electron microscope (FEI) equipped with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) and operating with EPU data collection software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Summary of detailed data collection was shown in table S1.

Image processing

Images of all datasets were imported into cryoSPARC v4.1.1 (56, 57). First, the P2X2/3 heteromer dataset was processed. Cryo-EM movies (n = 6127 + 5743) were motion-corrected using MotionCor2 (58). Contrast transfer functions (CTFs) were calculated using the patch CTF estimation module. The initial particles were picked by Blob picker. The picked particles were extracted and classified with two-dimensional (2D) classification, and the best 2D classes were selected as the template. Then, the particles were picked by template picker and extracted with a box size of 360 pixels (binned by 2) and classified with 2D classification and heterogeneous refinement. The sorted particles were subjected to heterogenous refinement using initial models generated by ab initio reconstruction, yielding five distinct classes. Through careful density comparison, there is no clear density difference among the subunits in class 1, whereas class 2 and class 3 have clear differences among the subunits. The final particle sets were re-extracted with original box size and further applied for final nonuniform refinement and local refinement. For class 1 particle sets, additional nonuniform refinement and local refinement with C3 symmetry were applied.

Next, we processed the P2X2/3-gefapixant dataset (5746 movies) using the same pipeline used to process the P2X2/3 heteromer dataset, except that the template for the P2X2/3 heteromer was used for template picking. Heterogenous refinement identified the same five classes, but we can only focus on one class to obtain a clean particle stack for subsequent processing.

Then, for the P2X2-apo dataset, a total of 5109 collected movies underwent motion correction and CTF estimation using MotionCor2 and patch CTF estimation module in cryoSPARC. Particles were picked by the blob and template picker using good 2D classes as templates. After several rounds of 2D classifications, 1,240,299 particles were selected and subjected to 3D reconstruction using ab initio refinement (n = 5) followed by heterogeneous refinement. The final particle sets were re-extracted with original box size and further applied for final nonuniform refinement and local refinement with C3 symmetry. For the P2X2-ATPopen dataset, the processing procedures used were the same as for P2X2-apo, except that the template for the P2X2-apo was used for template picking.

Last, for the P2X3-camlipixant dataset, a total of 4,953 collected movies underwent motion correction and CTF estimation using MotionCor2 and patch CTF estimation module in cryoSPARC. Particles were picked by the blob and template picker using good 2D classes as templates. After several rounds of 2D classifications, 491,690 particles were selected and subjected to 3D reconstruction using ab initio refinement (n = 4) followed by heterogeneous refinement. The final particle sets were re-extracted with original box size and further applied for final nonuniform refinement and local refinement with C3 symmetry.

Model building

All maps were sharpened using the B-factor automatically calculated in cryoSPARC. For the P2X2/3 heteromer, an AlphaFold model of the human P2X2 from AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (59) (UniProt accession code Q9UBL9) and the structure of the human P2X3-closed (desensitized) state (PDB accession code 5SVL) were used to generate initial templates for model building. For the gefapixant-bound P2X2/3, our model of the P2X2/3 heteromer in this study (PDB accession code 9UX2) was used. For the P2X2 homomer, the AlphaFold model of the human P2X2 was used to generate initial templates for model building. For the P2X3 homomer, the structure of the human P2X3-apo (PDB accession code 5SVJ) was used to generate initial templates for model building. All models were manually adjusted in Coot 0.9.8.1 (60, 61). The models were further refined by several rounds of real-space refinement in Phenix and were validated using MolProbity tool in Phenix (62). The final refinement statistics are provided in table S1. All structure figures were prepared in University of California San Francisco ChimeraX (63).

MD simulations

MD simulations were performed using the Desmond module in the Schrödinger suite (Schrödinger LLC, New York, USA) to study the dynamic behavior of the P2X3 receptor-ligand complex. The initial P2X2/3 (1:2) receptor structure was preprocessed using Maestro to correct the structural integrity, and the “Protonate 3D” tool was used to adjust the protonation states of ionizable residues to physiological pH 7.4. Hydrogen atoms were added, and the hydrogen-bond network was optimized to eliminate steric conflicts and ensure that the starting conformation was physiologically relevant. The preprocessed structure was converted into a .cms file (containing topological and coordinate information) compatible with Desmond using the prepwizard utility with default parameters. Each protein structure was inserted into a pre-equilibrated hydrated 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) bilayer membrane. The distance from the farthest vertex of the protein to the edge of the simulation orthogonal box was 30 Å in all axial directions, and NaCl (0.150 M) was added. The resulting lipid-buffered system contained ~479,000 atoms, consisting of 584 POPC lipids and ~123,500 water molecules. We used the ff19sb force field to model the protein and the lipid21 force field to model the POPC lipids. The protocol consisted of five consecutive steps:

1) Energy minimization: The steepest-descent method (up to 1000 steps) was applied to eliminate steric conflicts and ensure the stability of the initial structure.

2) NVT equilibration: A 100-ps simulation was performed at a constant volume and temperature (298 K) using the Nose-Hoover thermostat to equilibrate the molecular thermal motion.

3) NPT equilibration: A 100-ps simulation was performed at a constant pressure (1 atm) and temperature (298 K) using the Parrinello-Rahman barostat to adjust the system density to physiological conditions.

4) Production simulation: A 100-ns NPT simulation was performed with a time step of 2 fs, and the trajectory was saved every 10 ps for subsequent analysis.

5) Trajectory analysis: After the simulation, analysis was performed using the Desmond trajectory analysis and Maestro visualization tools. Key metrics included the RMSD of the protein backbone to evaluate structural stability, the root mean square fluctuation of Cα atoms to identify flexible regions, and ligand-protein interactions to characterize the dynamic changes in the binding mode.
