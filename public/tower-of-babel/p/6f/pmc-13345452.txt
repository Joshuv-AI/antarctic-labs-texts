Professional Use and Acceptance of Complementary and Alternative Medicine Among Nurses: A Cross‐Sectional Study From a Developing Country
Authors: Rasha S Abu Zaitoun, Israa R Sakhleh, Nizar B Said, Loai Zabin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe purpose of this study was to investigate Palestinian nurses' knowledge, beliefs, attitudes and practices towards complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) as well as the factors that influence these behaviours.DesignA descriptive cross‐sectional design was used.MethodsA questionnaire was distributed to nurses at healthcare institutions throughout Palestine using social media. Information on knowledge, beliefs, attitudes and practices of CAM was collected, as well as demographic data. SPSS version 20 was used for analysis.ResultsA total of 202 subjects (female, 112; male, 90) participated in the study. The mean knowledge, belief, attitude and practice of CAM scores were 61 out of 80 (75%), 25 out of 43 (58%), 33 out of 54 (61%) and 15 out of 40 (38%), respectively. Knowledge was significantly negatively associated with age and work experience (p < 0.05). Males and lower‐income nurses reported stronger beliefs in CAM than other groups (p < 0.05). Attitudes towards CAM were statistically significantly affected by gender, age and chronic disease status (p < 0.05). Regression analyses showed that work experience was a predictor of CAM knowledge. Gender, place of graduation and income were found to be predictors of CAM beliefs. Background

Complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) are health care practices that may be considered part of a country's traditional beliefs or culture but are not always part of the dominant health care system (Wourld Health Organization 2020). A closely related practice, holistic nursing care, entails nursing to protect, promote, and improve the patient's body, mind, emotions, spirituality, religion and personal relationships from illness and respecting social and personal preferences (Ambushe et al. 2023). CAM plays a critical role in 21st‐century health care and self‐care (Dehghan et al. 2022). The nursing profession has been a particularly strong advocate of CAM worldwide and has used CAM to introduce numerous holistic treatments in everyday nursing practice (Balouchi et al. 2018).

The United States National Center for Complementary and Alternative Medicine (NCCAM) defined CAM as a diversity of practices, products, and medical approaches incorporated with or replacing conventional medicine (National Academies of Sciences Engineering and Medicine 2005). CAM is an umbrella that can include natural products, such as vitamins, herbs, and dietary supplements, as well as mind and body practices such as acupuncture, breathing exercises, guided imagination, yoga and meditation, and other Chinese medicine practices (National Centar For Complementary And Inegrative Health 2018). Over the years, CAM has become widely used. For example, the WHO Global Report on Traditional and Complementary Medicine in 2019 noted that 88% of member states use traditional and complementary medicine, while 50% have had a national policy on traditional and complementary medicine since 2018 (World Health Organization 2019).

Previous research indicates that the use of CAM by nurses for patients is consistent with their perceptions of its potential advantages and is related to their professional experience and knowledge (Balouchi et al. 2018; Brewer et al. 2019; Hall et al. 2018). Factors that may influence further use may include awareness of health care providers regarding CAM and national policies and regulations. Providing instruction in nursing school regarding CAM appears to foster positive attitudes and interest in CAM (Makarem et al. 2022).

The use of CAM has been studied in different patient populations. Several studies, for example, examined CAM in a population with diabetes (Rakel and Minichiello 2022), the study revealed that 51.9% of their participants used CAM and 71.7% were satisfied with CAM effects. Cardiovascular diseases (Salah et al. 2020), as more than 45% of participants reported that they used CAM for their heart problems. In haemodialysis (Zyoud et al. 2016), more than 60% of haemodialysis patients used at least one type of CAM. Related to cancer (Ben‐Arye et al. 2015), about 63% of cancer patients used CAM to relieve cancer‐related outcomes. Additionally, during pregnancy (Ali‐Shtayeh et al. 2015; Salah et al. 2020), about 40% of participated women used CAM during pregnancy and the majority perceive that CAM is safe and beneficial for their health.

Herbal remedies have also been the subject of research in Palestine (Ali‐Shtayeh et al. 2015; Zyoud et al. 2014). According to these studies, Palestinians are interested in using CAM, and some CAM modalities used in Palestine are prevalent in other parts of the world, such as honey, prayer, and herbal therapy (Quzmar et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2022) while others are unique to the region, such as Al Hujama, blood biting and moxa (Zhang et al. 2022).

Safe use of CAM modalities has been discussed in various studies. For instance, Oyedepo, Temitope A Palai, Santwana in their study (Oyedepo and Palai 2021) emphasised the importance of having competent practitioners to supervise the herbal treatments to avoid toxicity and interactions. Similarly, the WHO established guidelines to enhance the safe administration, standardise, regulate, and control the quality of prescribing and administration (Wang et al. 2023).

In their roles in the health care system, nurses have played a crucial part in using and disseminating knowledge of CAM for many purposes, for example, to relieve pain, allergies, heartburn, osteoarthritis (Dimitrelis et al. 2017), and joint pain (Wang et al. 2023). In recent years, nurses have had increased access and exposure via technology to other cultures and communities and incorporated global CAM into their practice (Sibiya et al. 2017). Recent studies indicate that nurses tend to incorporate many CAM modalities in their practice, including prayers, herbal medicine, vitamins and minerals (Balouchi et al. 2018; Nejatian et al. 2018) diet regimen (Dimitrelis et al. 2017), and music therapy (Moeini et al. 2021).

Despite being an essential part of this health continuum, however, no study has been conducted in Palestine that examines nurses' knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, and practice of CAM. In an era of patient‐centred care, nurses can provide patients with advice and various treatment alternatives, including CAM therapies. In addition, nurses may be able to inform patients about the evidence supporting CAM therapies and the likelihood of side effects and contraindications. A greater understanding of the scope of Palestinian nurses' knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, and practices towards CAM is essential for increasing acceptance, developing educational efforts, and integrating these therapies into the dominant health care system. Therefore, this study investigates Palestinians' knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and practices towards complementary and alternative medicine (CAM), as well as the factors that influence these behaviours.

Methods

Study Design

A descriptive cross‐sectional study was conducted among nurses in different clinical areas of nursing in governmental, private, and teaching hospitals in Palestine. In addition, this study adheres to the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Instruments and Measures

Data were collected over 2 months using an investigator‐designed questionnaire based on previous studies in the CAM field (Al‐Omari et al. 2013; Alzahrani et al. 2016; Hall et al. 2017; Samara et al. 2019). The questionnaire in the native Arabic language was distributed to nurses in an electronic format and self‐administered to reduce face‐to‐face contact and comply with physical distance restrictions due to the COVID‐19 pandemic in Palestine. The survey was divided into two sections.

The first section requested demographic data, including age, gender, job title, marital status, academic level, country of graduation, specialty area, work experience and presence of chronic disease for the participant and any family member. The second section assessed nurses' knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, and practices of CAM as follows:

Knowledge regarding 20 types of CAM, the participants were asked to rate their knowledge approximately 20 types of CAM with potential responses including I have full knowledge (3), I have superficial knowledge (2), I have no knowledge (1), and not interested (0) and were scored 0–60 (higher indicating greater knowledge).

Knowledge regarding 20 types of CAM, the participants were asked to rate their knowledge approximately 20 types of CAM with potential responses including I have full knowledge (3), I have superficial knowledge (2), I have no knowledge (1), and not interested (0) and were scored 0–60 (higher indicating greater knowledge).

Beliefs about CAM included queries about signs and symptoms that CAM can relieve and common characteristics of CAM. Responses were collected in the form of a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to no opinion. The maximum score was 43 (higher indicating greater belief in CAM).

Beliefs about CAM included queries about signs and symptoms that CAM can relieve and common characteristics of CAM. Responses were collected in the form of a 4‐point Likert scale ranging from strongly agree to no opinion. The maximum score was 43 (higher indicating greater belief in CAM).

The attitude of nurses towards CAM reflected the nurses' integration of CAM into their personal and professional lives. This dimension was also calculated as the percentage of the total answer of nurses for 10 questions answered by Yes (1) and No (0) and 11 questions with answers ranging from strongly agree (4) to no (0) opinion. Thus, the maximum score of the attitude dimension was 54 (higher scores indicated a more positive attitude towards integrating CAM).

Sample and Setting

Nurses from health care institutions and nursing departments throughout Palestine were invited to participate in this study via social media such as Facebook, WhatsApp, and Instagram. A convenience and snowball sampling method was applied to reach the needed sample size. The sample size was calculated using the Raosoft calculator with a population of 600 nurses, marginal error of 5%, a confidence interval of 95%, and a response rate of 50%. Ten individuals were deemed ineligible due to incomplete and non‐professionally completed forms. The response rate was 86%.

The web‐based questionnaire was the most suitable way to reach nurses because of the nature of their working environment in terms of shifts and their inability to reach all of them in a short period of time, in addition to the type of service they provided that necessitated minimal disturbance, and to minimise contact during the spread of COVID‐19. On the other hand, to guarantee that the forms are filled out by nurses, the form's introduction explicitly states that only nurses are eligible to participate in the study, and they were requested to provide their professional status.

Ethical Consideration

Approval of this study was sought and obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An‐Najah National University. Participants were informed that their participation was voluntary. All data collected was kept confidential and did not require disclosing personal details, such as participant name or clinical affiliation.

The study utilised a validated questionnaire that was adapted from previously published studies in the field of CAM (Akan et al. 2012; Chu and Wallis 2007; Hall et al. 2018; Zyoud et al. 2016). Permission to use the questionnaire was obtained from the corresponding authors when necessary. The study was conducted in compliance with the norm ‘Declaration of Helsinki’.

Validity and Reliability

To determine the completeness, logical sequence of the statements and appropriateness of questions, three experts in CAM discussed content validity and potential questions. Before starting the study, the prospective questionnaire was tested to evaluate the time needed to complete the questionnaire, clarity and simplicity. After receiving feedback, the authors incorporated additional revisions based on this feedback. Content validity was assessed by a panel of experts, and the instrument was pilot tested among 20 nurses to confirm clarity, internal consistency, and time needed to complete the questionnaire. The pilot study revealed good internal consistency for knowledge scale (α = 0.805), for attitude (α = 0.838), and for beliefs (α = 0.753).

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM Statistical Package for Social Science programme version 20 (SPSS). Data are expressed as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as the mean, median, standard deviation (SD) and interquartile range for continuous variables. The level of statistical significance of our study was set at p < 0.05 and 95% confidence interval, measured by both the Kruskal–Wallis and Mann–Whitney U tests. Using multiple linear regression analysis, the relationship between sociodemographic characteristics and knowledge, attitude, practice, and beliefs of CAM was investigated.

Nurses' Knowledge of CAM

The mean knowledge score among participants was 61 out of 80 (SD = 60.88) or 75 out of 100 (SD = 76.10). When nurses were asked if they knew the difference between CAM and conventional medicine, 63.9% of the respondents reported knowing the difference. Approximately half of the nurses had a superficial level of knowledge about various types of CAM. While nurses were having full knowledge about the diet regimen (78.7%, n = 159), using the Holly Book (77.7%, n = 157), and walking (67.3%, n = 136). On the other hand, more than half of the respondents (55.4%, n = 112) lacked knowledge about hypnosis.

The results of the Spearman correlation indicated a significant negative association between age and CAM knowledge (rs = −0.193, p < 0.001). In addition, a Kruskal–Wallis H test showed a statistically significant difference in CAM knowledge between the different work experiences: χ 2(4) = 9.655, p = 0.047. Less than 1 year of work experience had a mean rank knowledge score of 119.1; 1–3 years had 111.69; 4–6 years had 97.74; 4–6 years of work experience had 103.06; and 10 or more years had 75.54.

Nurses' Beliefs About CAM

The mean belief score was 25 of 43 (58 out of 100) (SD = 5.34). A Mann–Whitney U test of belief in CAM demonstrated a significantly higher mean rank among males than females (U = 4079.500, p = 0.020) and a higher mean rank among those who graduated from Palestinian institutions (U = 1377.500, p = 0.010). A Kruskal–Wallis H test showed that there was a statistically significant difference in beliefs about CAM between the different income groups, χ 2(3) = 8.485, p = 0.037, with a mean rank belief score of 108.17 for less than 500 JOD (low income), 103.37 for middle income of 500–1000 JOD, and for high income the mean rank was 96.71 for 1000–2000 JOD, and 34.5 for income of 2000 JOD and more.

Nurses' Attitudes Towards CAM

The mean attitude score was 33 out of 54 (61 out of 100); (SD = 5.95). The Mann–Whitney U test showed that attitudes towards CAM were significantly higher among men than among women, with mean ranks of 93.85 and 111.20, respectively (U = 4183, p = 0.038). The results of the Spearman correlation indicated a significant negative association between age and attitude towards CAM (rs = −0.181, p < 0.001), indicating more positive attitudes among younger respondents.

Nurses' Practice of CAM

The mean practice score was 15 out of 40 (37.5%) (SD = 7.32). The Mann–Whitney U test showed that the attitude towards CAM was significantly higher among nurses who had a chronic condition (U = 374, p = 0.013) (see Table 1). The results revealed that 78.7% (n = 159) of nurses had used CAM for themselves, and more than 50% had used several CAM methods. The CAM most practiced by the nurses was prayer (71.8%), reading the Holly Quran (69.3%), and walking, with a 64.4% response rate. However, acupuncture (1.5%, n = 3) and hypnosis (1%, n = 2) were rarely practiced CAM (see Table 2).

Most participants (57.8%, n = 116) agreed or strongly agreed that it is their responsibility to ask patients if they used any CAM during their treatment journey from the time of admission until discharge. However, only 39.1% (n = 79) agreed or strongly agreed that it was their duty to educate patients and their families about the interaction of CAM with conventional therapy. Nearly one‐third (30.7%, n = 62) believed that CAM should never be part of their ordinary practices. Regression Analysis

Multiple regression analysis (Table 4) was conducted to examine the relationships among the demographic variables and CAM. The findings indicated that age and work experience significantly predicted knowledge of CAM. These predictors explained 5% of the variance in knowledge of CAM knowledge variance [R 2 = 0.05, F (2, 200) = 8.232, p < 0.05]. The analysis found that gender, graduation place, and income predicted 11% of the variance in CAM beliefs [R 2 = 0.11, F (3, 198) = 8.05, p < 0.05]. Age and gender were predictors that explained 5% of the variance in attitudes towards CAM [R 2 = 0.05, F (2, 199) = 4.48, p < 0.05]. Finally, the presence of chronic disease was a predictor that significantly explained 2% of the variance in CAM practice variance [R 2 = 0.02, F (1, 200) = 4.37, p < 0.05].

Discussion

The study found levels of knowledge about Complementary and Alternative Medicine (CAM) among nurses to be varied. More than half of the nurses in this study reported that they could differentiate between CAM and conventional medicine. However, most reported that they did not have sufficient information on CAM, a finding similar to previous studies (Shorofi and Arbon 2017). However, compared to Cypriot studies, most nurses had some knowledge of CAM, with males being more knowledgeable and having a more positive attitude than females (Aveni et al. 2016). Nurses may have acquired formal education or training in CAM as a component of their nursing course of study. However, CAM education is not consistently integrated into nursing education, resulting in some nurses having a limited understanding or exposure to these practices. In addition, nurses in the current study do not use such methods within their clinical practice. This may reflect that our results show insufficiency in information related to CAM.

The findings regarding the types of CAM utilised were reflective of the religious and cultural environment of Palestine. The study found that the most CAM used by nurses was praying (71.8%, n = 145) and reading the Quran (69.3%, n = 140), and for patient recommendations, the Quran was 2nd and prayer was 10th. This might be because of the religious culture among Palestinian people. Since the vast majority of Palestinian people are Muslims, they were born in a culture that believes in the statements mentioned in the Quran. The Quran says that praying and reading the Quran is the healing of the soul. Muslims believe that even if physical issues are present, they can use the Quran and pray to ask God to relieve the pain and help them heal (Samara et al. 2019). However that our study reflected national culture but the spiritual practices as part of CAM are also found in other countries and other cultures. A systematic review supported our results that the religious and spiritual practices such as prayers, yoga, mindfulness and meditation demonstrated direct and indirect effects on insomnia, for example, and relevant mental problems (de Diego‐Cordero et al. 2022).

It's crucial to remember that even though a lot of CAM techniques are thought to be safe, vulnerable groups may be at danger from them. Herbal treatments, for instance, are discouraged for cancer patients who are neutropenic because they may negatively interact with chemotherapy. The immune systems of these individuals are frequently weakened, and using unapproved herbal supplements can result in infections or lessen the efficacy of traditional therapies (Chan et al. 2023).

This finding reveals a paradox inherent in investigating CAM in many parts of the world—that a modality regarded as ‘alternative’ in terms of the dominant, usually Westernised health care system may be at the heart of the most traditional cultures, in this case Palestine. In addition, traditional Islamic medicine draws on a wealth of knowledge from a long tradition of healing that shares some ground with CAM. For example, Tibb Al‐Nabawi, or the health and healing teachings of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him), are an integral part of Islamic tradition. Honey, dates, black seed ( Nigella sativa ), and cupping (Hujama) are some of the natural therapies that are suggested herein. Therefore, we notice that nearly two thirds of the participated nurses are likely to use CAM related to their religion. We believe that practicing or engaging in Islamic therapies should not require one to be a Muslim; therefore, if such practices are beneficial, then any interested person can use such therapies.

This study indicates that being a newly graduated nurse with less than 1 year of work experience and expectedly less income tends to motivate nurses to seek information about CAM. Previous research indicates that nurses may be willing to obtain education and training in CAM if available. (Siedlecki 2021). The literature also revealed that the more nurses use CAM, the more interested they are in further expanding their knowledge and vice versa (Hall et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2022). This presents an opportunity for both clinical and academic nursing education. Many nurses are looking to increase their expertise in CAM in order to better caring for their patients, and there has been a recent uptick in interest in this field as a whole. In addition, patients increasingly seeking out CAM treatments for a range of health issues; as a result, nurses may be asked about or asked for information on CAM in the course of their clinical practice.

There are many resources for information about CAM, including websites and social networks, family and friends (Sibiya et al. 2017), and nursing and medical studies (Nam et al. 2024). In this study, websites and social networks were the most commonly used. In general, staying up‐to‐date on the latest developments in CAM is essential for nurses. They may learn how to safely and effectively integrate evidence‐based CAM treatments into their practice via continuing education programmes, seminars, conferences, and online resources.

Another important point emerging from the results is that nurses in our study have poor beliefs and attitudes about CAM. These findings are in contrast to those of Scottish and Iranian nurses (Buchan et al. 2012; Dehghan et al. 2022), whose beliefs and attitudes are more favourable. However, these findings may be representative of other studies conducted differently and warrant further investigations in future studies to understand the reasons behind such beliefs and attitudes. Generally, several factors, such as nurses' exposure to CAM modalities, cultural and/or family background, personal experiences, education, and training, might influence their opinions and attitudes about CAM. Some nurses may have favourable opinions about CAM and work to include it as part of their practice, while others may be sceptical or even opposed to this approach (Hall et al. 2018). This was consistent with the findings of many studies outside our country, where nurses thought they were responsible for disseminating information about CAM (Dimitrelis et al. 2017).

Implications for Research

The findings of this study have several implications for CAM in Palestine. CAM can and does play an important role in nursing practice in many parts of the world, including Palestine. Additional understanding of each component—nurses' awareness of knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and practices towards CAM—is vital to supporting nurses in making informed decisions regarding patient care. The findings of this study demonstrate the importance of conducting more research to understand nursing behaviour towards CAM and fostering nursing knowledge and practice of standardised and holistic patient care.

Implications for Education

The findings regarding knowledge of CAM reflect a lack of specialised training programmes and Palestinian nursing programmes. These barriers are compounded by the rapid flow through social networks, creating poorly trained nurses encountered by patients with many questions (Sibiya et al. 2017). It is imperative that CAM modalities be more thoroughly taught to nursing students at the undergraduate and graduate levels. The educational curriculum may encompass the fundamental principles, concepts, and proficiencies associated with various CAM modalities, placing particular emphasis on the safety, efficacy, and quality of CAM treatments. The education would also emphasise the significance of cultural competence and sensitivity when administering CAM therapies to a varied group of patients, given that each individual's attitude on health and healthcare decisions is influenced by their unique cultural background.

Implications for Practice

Additionally, the findings from this study can provide insight into the potential application of CAM in bedside nursing. The results provide information as to which groups of nurses are already most likely to use CAM. Additionally, nurses have a vital role in the community. It is possible that CAM may play a role in nurses' self‐care as well as their care of their families and immediate circle.

Implications for Policy

Nurses' ambiguity and contradictory behaviour towards CAM may be a consequence of the absence of agreed‐upon definitions adopted by any official entity and the lack of ongoing regulations and policies towards their use. This fragile environment may force patients to hide information about CAM use and inversely affect the quality and safety of care. The findings of this study suggest that efforts to clarify the role of nurses in regard to the dissemination of CAM at the bedside can be useful in supporting future plans for education and practice.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is the first known investigation in Palestine to report nurses' knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and practices towards CAM and has several strengths and limitations. The study demonstrates the benefits and weaknesses inherent in the cross‐sectional design, as it yields new knowledge regarding the relationships among several of the variables examined at a given moment in time and adds to the body of knowledge about the importance of integrating CAM and related aspects into the nursing school curriculum.

The results of this study can direct hospitals to give insight into the use of CAM by their patients, nurses, or other health care providers, so hospital administrators, policy makers and educators may add this topic to the assessment sheets, health education topics, and policies to monitor, control, and promote awareness of CAM usage. However, causation cannot be discerned from this information.

Another strength is that the study has a robust sample size. Nevertheless, the findings would be more informative and generalizable if the survey had been distributed across a more diverse geographic area, expanded the sample size, and included more hospitals. This study used a survey with a convenience sample that was distributed throughout the researchers' networks.

Another limitation of this study is the likelihood that participants will respond in a perceived socially acceptable manner. Finally, because this study relied on self‐reported data, participants likely responded positively to attitude and practice questions based on what they thought was expected of them (Van de Mortel 2008).

Conclusions

This study expands previous research efforts to measure nurses' knowledge, beliefs, attitudes, and practices towards using CAM. Findings from this investigation can be used by nursing staff, administrators, and educators to initiate strategic plans to support CAM practices in the clinical setting and integrate CAM principles into the academic nursing curriculum. Additional research is needed to study the impact of nurses' CAM therapies and their impact on the plan of patient care and their overall well‐being.
