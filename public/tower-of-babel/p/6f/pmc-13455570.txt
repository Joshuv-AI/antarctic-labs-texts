Lipocalin-2 perpetuates postoperative and post-infectious neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behavior
Authors: Shany E Yang, Jennifer Meng, Colin Johnston, Swapnil Gavade, Will Schwieterman, Holland E Hubert, Michael W Newstead, Benjamin H Singer, Joanna L Spencer-Segal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipocalin-2 (LCN2) is a pleiotropic cytokine with well-established roles in immune defense, iron metabolism, and inflammation that has been used as a biomarker for kidney injury, cancer, and infection. Recent work has also suggested a role for LCN2 in brain function and behavior, suggesting that LCN2 excess could be a driver of neuropsychiatric complications of infectious and inflammatory conditions. Here, we show that LCN2 levels remain elevated in both the brain and circulation for weeks after an acute infection or simple laparotomy in male and female mice. We used an inducible knockout mouse model to decrease lcn2 expression after recovery from surgery and infection and found that higher brain lcn2 RNA levels predicted persistent neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behavior in survivors. Exogenous LCN2 administration increased in vivo activity of hippocampal CA1 pyramidal cells and increased anxiety-like behavior in male mice. Together, these findings reveal a novel role for LCN2 as a neuroinflammatory mediator of long-term neuropsychiatric dysfunction in infection survivors.Keywords: Sepsis, Post-sepsis syndrome, Infection, Lipocalin-2, Neuroinflammation, Anxiety-like behavior, Laparotomy, Cecal ligation and puncture

Introduction

Acute infections from diverse sources are associated with significant post-infectious morbidity and mortality, although the underlying mechanisms remain largely unknown (Choutka et al., 2022; Prescott and Angus, 2018a). Severe infection can lead to a dysregulated immune response and organ dysfunction—a condition known as sepsis, which affects approximately 50 million individuals globally each year (Rudd et al., 2020). Survivors of sepsis often experience prolonged neuropsychiatric symptoms, including anxiety, depression, and cognitive impairment (Prescott and Angus, 2018b; Semmler et al., 2013). The public health burden of sepsis survivorship means there is an urgent need to understand the mechanisms of this post-sepsis syndrome and to develop targeted treatments.

Rodent infection survivors exhibit behavioral changes reminiscent of human post-sepsis and post-infectious syndromes, including increased anxiety-like behavior, impaired extinction of conditioned fear, and impairments on other hippocampal-dependent cognitive tasks (Anderson et al., 2015; Calsavara et al., 2013; Denstaedt et al., 2020; Hill et al., 2024; Singer et al., 2016; Spencer-Segal et al., 2020). Use of these rodent models has pointed to persistent neuroinflammation as a key potential driver of these post-infectious behaviors. In our previous work, we showed upregulation of pro-inflammatory genes in the hippocampus of male and female murine infection survivors, with lipocalin-2 (lcn2) as the most highly upregulated target (Hill et al., 2024).

LCN2 is a pleiotropic cytokine with previously identified roles in kidney disease, cancer, infection, and neuroinflammation (Flo et al., 2004; Goetz et al., 2002; Gomez-Chou et al., 2017; Kang et al., 2018; Olson et al., 2021; Viau et al., 2010). It is part of the acute phase response, with LCN2 levels being acutely upregulated in blood, brain, and liver following turpentine administration, lipopolysaccharide (LPS) endotoxin injection, and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) (Kang et al., 2018; Liu and Nilsen-Hamilton, 1995; Srinivasan et al., 2012a; Sultan et al., 2012; Vazquez et al., 2015). It binds bacterial and mammalian siderophores and exerts antimicrobial properties in part via its iron-sequestering capability (Flo et al., 2004; Goetz et al., 2002; Srinivasan et al., 2012a). Prior work suggested that LCN2 can exert a pro- or anti-inflammatory role in the brain depending on the context. In some cases, lcn2-null mice showed exacerbated neuroinflammation, increased mortality, and increased levels of serum transaminases and lactate dehydrogenase after administration of 2, 5, and 20 mg/kg of LPS, which suggests an important anti-inflammatory role for LCN2 even in the absence of a live bacterial infection (Kang et al., 2018; Srinivasan et al., 2012b). However, in other studies, lcn2 knockout either had no effect on neuroinflammation when a lower dose of LPS was used or mitigated glial activation and neuroinflammation after a 5 mg/kg LPS challenge, which is more consistent with a pro-inflammatory role (Jin et al., 2014a; Vichaya et al., 2019). Furthermore, various groups have shown that lcn2-null mice exhibit anxiety-like behaviors, altered hippocampal activity, and impaired dendritic arborization compared to wild-type controls (Ferreira et al., 2013; Ferreira and Marques, 2023; Mucha et al., 2011). These findings led us to hypothesize that LCN2 might be an important regulator of neuroinflammation and behavior in infection survivors.

The goal of this study was to determine the role of LCN2 in neuroinflammatory gene expression and anxiety-like behavior in murine infection survivors. We found that LCN2 is upregulated long after the initial recovery from multiple types of infection in male and female mice. Using a combination of inducible lcn2 knockout mice, behavioral tests, and microendoscopic calcium imaging in freely behaving mice, we demonstrated that LCN2 perpetuates neuroinflammatory gene expression in murine CLP survivors and increases anxiety-like behaviors and hippocampal pyramidal cell activity in vivo. Our findings reveal a novel pro-inflammatory role of LCN2 in infection survivors, identifying this cytokine as a novel candidate mediator of long-term neuropsychiatric symptoms after sepsis and critical illness.

Results

Lipocalin-2 is elevated in murine infection survivors and human patients who died from infectious causes

We previously found that murine CLP survivors exhibit anxiety-like and depressive-like behaviors several weeks after the resolution of an infection (Hill et al., 2024; Spencer-Segal et al., 2020). To identify candidate molecular mechanisms of such behaviors, we used bulk RNA sequencing from dorsal and ventral hippocampus to identify differentially regulated transcripts in CLP survivors compared to a sham condition (Hill et al., 2024). The gene expression changes showed upregulation of transcripts involved in innate immunity, and lcn2 was the top upregulated transcript in male and female mice 3 weeks after CLP (Fig. 1A). The raw lcn2 gene count was significantly higher in CLP mice, with a 19.8-fold increase compared to sham mice (U = 11, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1B). To assess the relevance of LCN2 in clinical settings, we examined lcn2 mRNA levels in the brains of patients who died from non-infectious causes and those who died from pulmonary, urinary, abdominal and other infections. We found that lcn2 mRNA levels were highly upregulated in the brains of patients who died from infectious causes (U = 33, p = 0.0171) (Fig. 1C). We additionally found a 9-fold upregulation of circulating LCN2 in 3-week murine CLP survivors compared to their sham counterparts (U = 0, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1D), suggesting that the persistent LCN2 upregulation after infection is systemic and not limited to the brain. Moreover, this persistent increase in circulating LCN2 was not specific to CLP, as we also saw it in survivors of other murine infection models, including abdominal cecal slurry injection (CS) (U = 12, p = 0.0057) (Fig. 1E) and Klebsiella pneumoniae lung infection (KPA) (U = 1, p = 0.0476) (Fig. 1F). In summary, LCN2 is a cytokine that is upregulated in the brain during acute infection in humans and persistently upregulated in the brain and circulation in murine infection survivors.

Establishment of an inducible lcn2 knockout mouse strain

We wanted to understand the role of persistent LCN2 upregulation in neuroinflammation and behavioral changes in infection survivors. Given that prior work demonstrated an important role for LCN2 in acute inflammation, we generated an inducible knockout by crossing lcn2loxP mice with ROSA26-Cre-ERT2 mice to enable systemic deletion of lcn2 in infection survivors without disrupting the initial innate immune response to the acute infection. Lcn2loxP-Cre-ERT2-positive and Lcn2loxP-Cre-ERT2-negative mice of both sexes underwent either sham or CLP surgery and were allowed to recover for 5 days prior to receiving intraperitoneal injections of vehicle or 75 mg/kg tamoxifen daily for 5 days to induce Cre recombination and lcn2 gene knockout (Fig. 2A). As expected, CLP mice had a lower survival rate than sham mice regardless of genotype (p = 0.0181) (Fig. 2B). In Cre-ERT2-negative mice, 2-week CLP survivors showed an increase in circulating LCN2 protein (Fig. 2C) as well as an increase in lcn2 RNA in liver (Fig. 2D) and brain (Fig. 2E) irrespective of vehicle or tamoxifen treatment (plasma: F1,36 = 7.571, p = 0.0092; liver: F1,36 = 11.94, p = 0.0014; brain: F1,36 = 11.67, p = 0.0016). There was no effect of tamoxifen treatment itself on lcn2 RNA in brain or liver or circulating LCN2 protein in Cre-ERT2-negative mice (plasma, 2C: F1,36 = 0.01904, p = 0.8910; liver, 2D: F1,36 = 0.3478, p = 0.5590; brain, 2E: F1,36 = 0.01520, p = 0.9026). This LCN2 upregulation in CLP survivors was eliminated in plasma and liver and diminished in the brains of Cre-ERT2-positive mice, demonstrating a full knockout in peripheral tissues and a partial knockout in the brain (Fig. 2F, 2G, 2H). In the plasma of Cre-ERT2-positive mice, LCN2 levels were elevated after CLP, and this increase was completely eliminated by tamoxifen treatment (main effect of surgery, F1,32 = 4.325, p = 0.0457; main effect of tamoxifen, F1,32 = 19.68, p < 0.0001; interaction between surgery and tamoxifen, F1,32 = 4.245, p = 0.0476) (Fig. 2F). Similarly, in the liver of Cre-ERT2-positive mice (Fig. 2G), CLP increased and tamoxifen decreased lcn2 RNA; the upregulation of lcn2 RNA in the liver was completely eliminated in the knockout (main effect of surgery, F1,32 = 8.668, p = 0.0060; main effect of tamoxifen, F1,32 = 210.3, p < 0.0001; interaction between surgery and tamoxifen, F1,32 = 8.658, p = 0.0060). Finally, in the brain, CLP also increased, and tamoxifen decreased, lcn2 RNA (main effect of surgery, F1,32 = 14.68, p = 0.0006; main effect of tamoxifen, F1,32 = 11.70, p = 0.0017) (Fig. 2H). While the CLP-vehicle mice showed a significant lcn2 upregulation when compared to their sham-vehicle counterparts (p = 0.0316), as expected, there was also a trend toward increased lcn2 RNA in the brain of CLP-tamoxifen mice compared with the sham-tamoxifen mice (p = 0.08), suggesting a partial knockout in the brain. Overall, we demonstrated that tamoxifen administration resulted in a full lcn2 knockout in the liver and circulation and a partial lcn2 knockout in the brains of male and female mice.

A role for brain lcn2 in persistent innate immune activation and anxiety-like behavior in survivors of surgery and CLP

Our goal was to use the inducible lcn2 knockout mouse to determine the role of brain LCN2 on anxiety-like behaviors and persistent innate immune activation in CLP survivor mice, so we performed behavioral testing in mice using the paradigm in Fig. 2A. The elevated zero maze (EZM) and open field (OF) are common tests of anxiety-like behavior, where fewer entries into the center (OF) or open areas (EZM) or less time spent in the center (OF), or open areas (EZM) can be interpreted as anxiety-like behavior. In the EZM, Cre-ERT2-negative and Cre-ERT2-positive mice displayed comparable behavioral changes (Figs. S1A-S1F), suggesting a direct effect of tamoxifen on mouse behavior. Similar results were observed in the open field (OF) (Figs. S1G-S1L).

While this effect of tamoxifen precluded interpretation of the behavior, we used tissue harvested from these mice to determine the effect of decreased lcn2 on persistent innate immune activation in the brain of CLP and sham survivors. To do this, we measured RNA levels for leucine-rich alpha-2-glycoprotein 1 (lrg1), p-selectin (selp), and S100 calcium-binding protein A8 (s100a8), as these were three of the top ten pro-inflammatory targets upregulated in the brain of 3-week CLP survivors (Fig. 1A). While the expression of these genes was highly variable in the CLP mice, we were able to confirm increases in brain selp and lrg1 RNA at the group level (Figs. S2A-S2F). Considering the heterogeneity of the lcn2 knockout in the brain (Fig. 2H) and in the CLP effect on lrg1, selp, and s100a8 RNA expression in this experiment (Figs. S2A-S2F), we used correlation analyses to directly examine the relationships between brain lcn2 RNA levels and other neuroinflammatory markers across post-surgery (sham) and post-infectious (CLP) conditions. In sham mice, brain lcn2 RNA levels were positively correlated with all three inflammatory markers: lrg1 (Spearman r = 0.3874, p = 0.0148), selp (Spearman r = 0.5172, p = 0.0007), and s100a8 (Spearman r = 0.5275, p = 0.0006) (Fig. 3A–3C). In CLP mice, brain lcn2 RNA levels were also positively correlated with all three markers: lrg1 (Spearman r = 0.7646, p < 0.0001), selp (Spearman r = 0.7162, p < 0.0001), and s100a8 (Spearman r = 0.4704, p = 0.0033) (Fig. 3D–3F). These positive associations remained when data from all mice were pooled (Figs. S2G-2E). Taken together, the finding that brain lcn2 levels predict the levels of other markers of innate inflammation in the brain, including after partial lcn2 knockout, suggests that LCN2 is an important regulator of neuroinflammation after sham and CLP.

As expected, the survival rate of CLP mice was significantly lower than that of the sham mice (χ2 = 8.886, p = 0.0029) (Fig. 4B). This tamoxifen-reduced regimen minimized the CLP-induced upregulation of circulating LCN2 (main effect of surgery, F1,43 = 13.94, p = 0.0006; main effect of genotype, F1,43 = 29.40, p < 0.0001, interaction, F1,43 = 5.733, p = 0.0212) (Fig. 4C). In the liver, tamoxifen also decreased lcn2 RNA and completely prevented the CLP-induced upregulation (main effect of surgery, F1,43 = 4.316, p = 0.0438; main effect of genotype, F1,43 = 42.8, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4D). Only a partial lcn2 knockout was observed in the brain, where tamoxifen did not completely prevent the CLP-induced upregulation of lcn2 (main effect of surgery, F1,43 = 9.603, p = 0.0034), resulting in pronounced variability in brain lcn2 RNA expression across individual mice (Fig. 4E). Overall, we developed a tamoxifen regimen that induced a complete lcn2 knockout in the liver and a partial knockout in the brain, while having no adverse effects on mouse behavior.

Tests for anxiety-like behavior 2 weeks after CLP or sham in mice treated in this new experimental paradigm showed high variability with no significant changes at the group level (Fig. 5A–5F). Given the heterogeneity of the lcn2 brain knockout, we turned to a linear regression model to determine whether brain lcn2 RNA levels predicted behavior at the level of individual mice to leverage these individual differences. Higher brain lcn2 RNA (log2 fold change) predicted increased anxiety-like behaviors across multiple measures (Fig. 5G). In the EZM, higher lcn2 levels reduced entries in the open areas (Model Estimate = −1.35, p = 0.011) and time spent in the open areas (Model Estimate = −5.37, p = 0.0431). In other words, for every log2 fold increase in brain lcn2 RNA levels, we observed a 5.37-second decrease in the time spent in the open areas and a 1.35-second decrease in the number of open area entries. In the OF, higher lcn2 levels reduced the time spent in the center such that every log2 fold-change increase0020in brain lcn2 RNA levels corresponded to a 6.52-second decrease in the time spent in the center (Model Estimate = −6.52, p = 0.0483). We did not observe any effects of brain lcn2 RNA levels on OF Center Entries (Model Estimate = −0.84, p = 0.23). In contrast, lcn2 did not significantly predict locomotor behavior (OF Total Distance Traveled: Model Estimate = −153.84, p = 0.179; EZM Total Distance Traveled: Model Estimate = −32.20, p = 0.249), suggesting a specific effect of brain lcn2 levels on anxiety-like behaviors. When analyzing the CLP group only, we also saw that higher brain lcn2 RNA predicted greater anxiety-like behavior in the EZM (Time Spent in Open Area: Model Estimate = −12.49, p = 0.0203; Open Area Entries: Model Estimate = −2.05, p = 0.0323) and the OF (Time Spent in Center: Model Estimate = −15.24, p = 0.0321), with an even greater effect size (Fig. 5H).

As seen in the previous experiment, our findings once again showed that brain lcn2 RNA levels in CLP mice of both genotypes were positively correlated with other markers of innate immune activation, including brain lrg1 (Spearman r = 0.6429, p = 0.0040) and selp (Spearman r = 0.5377, p = 0.0214), though not with s100a8 in this sample (Spearman r = −0.1620, p = 0.5207) (Fig. 5I–5K). In summary, our results suggest that LCN2 contributes to persistent neuroinflammation and drives anxiety-like behaviors in survivors of sham and CLP surgeries.

Because we observed the same relationship between lcn2 and behavior and inflammatory markers in sham and CLP mice, this raised the question of whether LCN2 regulates these phenotypes at baseline, or whether laparotomy (the sham intervention) also creates a persistent LCN2-driven inflammatory state. To investigate this, we examined the effect of a simple laparotomy, equivalent to our sham group, on the same anxiety-like behaviors and inflammatory markers in the brain, at the same post-surgical time point as the prior experiments (Fig. 6A). We found that circulating LCN2 levels are persistently elevated 3 weeks after laparotomies (F1,28 = 6.461, p = 0.0168) (Fig. 6B). A significant main effect of sex was observed, with female mice exhibiting lower LCN2 levels than males (F1,28 = 15.47, p = 0.0005). Laparotomy did not significantly affect behavior in the EZM, though we did find that there was a main effect of sex with increased exploration in females (total distance traveled, F1,28 = 4.594, p = 0.0408; time spent in the open areas, F1,28 = 5.232, p = 0.0299; open area entries, F1,28 = 10.03, p = 0.0037) (Fig. 6C–6E). In the OF, laparotomy reduced locomotion (t30 = 2.088, p = 0.0454) and increased anxiety-like behavior, as indicated by fewer center entries (t30 = 2.206, p = 0.0352), compared to unoperated controls (Fig. 6F–6H). The decrease in total distance traveled in the OF suggests a reduction in exploratory drive, or increased anxiety-like behavior, in the recovered laparotomy mice. Similarly to what we observed in the circulation, lcn2 RNA levels were significantly increased in the brain 3 weeks after laparotomy compared to the unoperated control (t30 = 2.992, p = 0.0055) (Fig. 6I); this was also the case for lrg1 (t30 = 2.062, p = 0.0479) and s100a8 (t30 = 1.956, p = 0.0598), but not selp (Fig. 6J–6L). Finally, correlational analyses showed that brain lcn2 RNA levels were positively correlated with lrg1 and s100a8 RNA levels, but not selp (Fig. 6M–6O). Overall, our data demonstrate that laparotomy, commonly used as a sham control in CLP studies, induced persistent changes to exploratory and anxiety-like behavior and increased systemic LCN2 and brain lcn2 relative to an unoperated control. Excess LCN2 increases dorsal CA1 neuronal activity in vivo

One mechanism through which LCN2 could modulate anxiety-like behavior is by altering neural activity in brain regions controlling this behavior. The hippocampus is a crucial limbic region for the control of stress-related behaviors including anxiety-like behavior (Ghasemi et al., 2022; McEwen et al., 2015). Here, we focused on the dorsal CA1 (dCA1) region due to its established role in hippocampal function and sensitivity to factors such as neuroinflammation and exploratory behavior (Gavade et al., 2022; Nairuz et al., 2024; Ugolini et al., 2018; Zeng et al., 2024). Prior studies have also shown that LCN2 alters neuronal activity and synaptic function within the CA1 (Ferreira et al., 2013; Mucha et al., 2011; Olson et al., 2021) making the dCA1 an ideal target for our studies. To investigate whether LCN2 affects hippocampal activity, we produced recombinant mouse LCN2 (rLCN2) protein, which had the expected biological activity to enhance pro-inflammatory cytokine production from bone-marrow-derived macrophages (Fig. S4A-S4B), in line with previous reports and our own data highlighting the pro-inflammatory effect of LCN2 (Olson et al., 2021; Xiang et al., 2022). While our data above suggest an important role for brain-derived LCN2 in anxiety-like behavior, prior work does suggest that circulating LCN2 can cross the blood–brain barrier and act on specific brain regions (Mosialou et al., 2017; Petropoulou et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2024). For this experiment, we used peripheral injections of rLCN2 to test the hypothesis that LCN2 excess would alter hippocampal activity. The optimal dosage was determined through pilot studies to replicate the circulating levels of LCN2 we observed in CLP survivors. First, we measured LCN2 levels in plasma samples 1 h after 0.5 mg/kg rLCN2 injections to evaluate the efficacy of our administration to increase circulating LCN2. As anticipated, circulating LCN2 protein levels were significantly increased in rLCN2-injected mice compared to the vehicle-injected controls (40.6-fold increase; t16 = 4.390, p = 0.0005) and returned to baseline within 24 h (t16 = 1.682, p = 0.1119) (Fig. 7A). To ensure that any observed effects were not attributable to high endotoxin concentrations, we measured endotoxin levels in the rLCN2 preparation (Fig. S5). The administered endotoxin dose was negligible (0.0000032 mg/kg per mouse), more than 10,000-fold below doses used for induction of acute or chronic inflammation in mice (Biesmans et al., 2013; Catorce and Gevorkian, 2016; Copeland et al., 2005; Engler-Chiurazzi et al., 2023; Leon et al., 1999; Y. Yang et al., 2022).

To determine the effect of rLCN2 on hippocampal activity, we administered 0.5 mg/kg of rLCN2 via subcutaneous injection and measured the activity of CA1 pyramidal neurons in the dorsal hippocampus using in vivo microendoscopic calcium imaging in male mice while they were exploring a small open arena (Fig. 7B). A histological analysis of brain sections showed successful viral targeting and lens implant into the dCA1 (Fig. S6A-S6B). Multi-session cell registration was performed to identify the same cells over our 4-day experiment. Acute injection of rLCN2 increased the number of active neurons in the dCA1 (Fig. 7C). Single-neuron calcium traces across 4 days showed that rLCN2 treatment increased the frequency of calcium transients (Fig. 7D) and increased the average calcium amplitude compared to saline treatment (Fig. 7E). A linear regression model showed that rLCN2 treatment led to a 5.15% increase in the average calcium amplitude of CA1 pyramidal neurons (slope = 0.6530, p = 0.041) (Fig. 7F). There were no apparent differences in total distance traveled or velocity between treatments (Fig. 7G–7H). This finding suggests that acute peripheral injection of rLCN2 increases dCA1 neuronal activity.

Chronic recombinant LCN2 administration influences anxiety-like behavior in males

The data above show that LCN2 excess is sufficient to increase hippocampal CA1 pyramidal cell activity in male mice. To establish whether chronic LCN2 excess alters anxiety-like behavior, male mice received 2 daily subcutaneous injections of either vehicle or 0.5 mg/kg rLCN2 for 10 days and underwent behavioral testing in the OF and EZM to assess locomotion and anxiety-like behaviors (Fig. 8A). In the OF, rLCN2 treatment had no effects on locomotion but induced anxiety-like behaviors as indicated by a significant decrease in the time spent in the center compared to vehicle-injected mice (t16 = 2.223, p = 0.0409), and a slight decrease in the number of entries in the center, though this was not statistically significant (t16 = 1.438, p = 0.1696) (Fig. 8B–8D). There was no effect of LCN2 administration on behavior in the EZM (Fig. 8E–8G). Thus, not only did acute peripheral injection of rLCN2 increase hippocampal neuron activity, but chronic peripheral rLCN2 administration was also sufficient to cause anxiety-like behavior in the OF in male mice.

Brain endothelium is a major source of excess LCN2

Given our data suggesting a role for LCN2 in driving neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behaviors after infection, we sought to identify the primary source of brain-derived lcn2 mRNA by performing RNAscope on hippocampal tissues 3 weeks after sham and CLP surgeries. Lcn2 mRNA colocalized with tek+ endothelial cells (t26 = 4.071, p = 0.0004), but not CA1 neurons. Minimal colocalization was observed in sham controls, which suggests that brain endothelium is one of the sources of excess lcn2 mRNA 3 weeks after CLP (Fig. 9A-9B). Additionally, further analyses using publicly available endothelial-specific translating ribosome affinity purification (EC-TRAP) data from (Cleuren et al., 2019), showed acute upregulation of lcn2 after LPS injection in endothelial cells in the brain and other tissues (Fig. 9C). Taken together, these data suggest that brain endothelium is a major source of persistent LCN2 upregulation following acute infection and recovery.

Discussion

Here, we identify LCN2 as a key molecular mediator of post-infectious and post-surgical neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behavior using a combination of lcn2 knockout, molecular and behavioral assays, and in vivo calcium imaging. We found that LCN2, usually considered an acute-phase protein, remained systemically upregulated several weeks after recovery from multiple types of acute infection in male and female mice. Furthermore, we demonstrated that in survivors of infection or sham surgery, higher brain lcn2 RNA predicted higher levels of other neuroinflammatory markers and anxiety-like behavior. Finally, we found that administration of exogenous LCN2 was sufficient to increase dorsal hippocampal pyramidal cell activity and induce anxiety-like behavior in male mice in the OF. The results suggest that LCN2 perpetuates neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behavior in survivors of surgery and infection weeks after the surgical or infectious insult.

Prior work from our group has shown that CLP survivors display ongoing behavioral despair and anxiety-like behaviors despite showing signs of complete physiological recovery (Hill et al., 2024; Spencer-Segal et al., 2020). These behavioral changes were coupled with persistent neuroinflammation, as bulk RNAseq data from murine hippocampus indicated that 3-week CLP survivors showed a pronounced upregulation of lcn2 along with more than 100 inflammation-related transcripts (Hill et al., 2024). We found here that lcn2 RNA levels in the brain were tightly linked to the expression of other inflammatory markers and to anxiety-like behavior. In particular, our finding that knocking out lcn2 after the resolution of the postoperative and post-CLP acute inflammatory phase also lessened neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behaviors in survivors at the individual animal level provides a direct mechanistic link between LCN2 and these outcomes. The finding further suggests that LCN2 could be a therapeutic target to alleviate the highly prevalent mood dysfunction in infection survivors (Jackson et al., 2014; Prescott and Angus, 2018a, 2018b).

While other studies showed that LCN2 is acutely upregulated after an inflammatory insult (Kang et al., 2018; Srinivasan et al., 2012b; Vazquez et al., 2015), we found that LCN2 upregulation persists up to 3 weeks later. This contrasts with other pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are largely absent from the circulation within 48 h after CLP (Walley et al., 1996). We also found that the persistent upregulation of LCN2 was systemic, as it was seen in the liver, brain, and circulation of CLP survivors compared with sham-operated mice, and that this was generalizable to other infection models. While LCN2 is classically secreted by neutrophils, prior studies showed that excess LCN2 can be produced by neurons, for example, after stress (Mucha et al., 2011), tibial fracture (Xiang et al., 2022), and focal ischemia (Xing et al., 2014a). Additional studies showed that LCN2 is expressed in both glial cells (astrocytes and microglia) and endothelial cells following ischemia and reperfusion injury and LPS injection (Jin et al., 2014a, 2014b; Olson et al., 2021). Here, we found that endothelial cells exhibit increased lcn2 mRNA expression during acute inflammation, and that in CLP survivors, brain endothelial cells, but not neurons, show elevated levels of lcn2 mRNA compared to the sham condition. This clearly highlights endothelial cells as an important source of excess LCN2, though we cannot yet conclude that endothelial cells are the primary source of systemic excess LCN2 in infection survivors. Endothelial cell dysfunction is a key component of the pathogenesis of organ dysfunction in human sepsis (Ince et al., 2016; Joffre et al., 2020; Vincent et al., 2021). The importance of this endothelial dysfunction in chronic LCN2 excess and as a driver of ongoing neuroinflammation and behavioral changes in infection survivors is a crucial topic for future study. Based on the current data, we cannot exclude significant roles for other cell types, including astrocytes, microglia, and immune cells. Future studies should address the function of endothelium-derived LCN2 in infection survivors using endothelial-specific knockout or overexpression approaches, including the mechanisms by which LCN2 drives neuroinflammation. Previous research indicates that LCN2 can act on neurons and glial cells, which can be mediated through its receptor, 24p3R. For instance, LCN2 signaling through 24p3R in the prelimbic cortex, has been shown to promote anxiety-like behavior (Yan et al., 2024). Moreover, it has been shown that neuron-derived LCN2 can act on glial cells to promote the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and growth factors (Xing et al., 2014b). Therefore, it is likely that LCN2 exerts its effects through complex interactions with multiple cell types and receptor-mediated signaling cascades.

Interestingly, we saw the same association between lcn2 RNA and neuroinflammation and behavior in sham-operated and CLP mice. There are several possible explanations for this. On one hand, the sham laparotomy with cecal manipulation is still a major abdominal surgery that acutely causes upregulation of inflammatory genes in blood cells, increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and microglial activation in the brain (Ko et al., 2015; J. Yang et al., 2022). Indeed, we found decreased exploratory behavior, systemic LCN2 upregulation, and neuroinflammatory gene induction in the brain 3 weeks after a laparotomy compared to an unoperated control, supporting the idea that our sham condition itself induces a lasting inflammatory response, though to a lesser extent than CLP. Nevertheless, LCN2 could also be a basal regulator of neuroinflammation irrespective of any intervention. Indeed, we found that brain lcn2 RNA levels positively correlated with other inflammatory markers in laparotomy and unoperated control mice. In support of this evidence that LCN2 may regulate the basal neuroinflammatory state in the brain, a recent study found that whole-body lcn2 knockout reduced mortality rate and neuroinflammation in galactosylceramidase-deficient mice irrespective of any additional inflammatory or infectious intervention (Favret et al., 2024). Taken together, these findings suggest that while CLP induces a more robust inflammatory and behavioral phenotype compared to sham, laparotomy alone produces persistent neuroinflammation and behavioral changes that overlap with those seen in infection survivors. Therefore, our findings may not indicate an infection-specific mechanism; LCN2 may be an important driver of changes to brain function and behavior in a variety of inflammatory and infectious conditions. Future work should also clarify the importance of different cellular sources of LCN2 in setting basal levels of neuroimmune activation versus its role during and after a systemic challenge.

A strength of our approach was the use of an inducible knockout of lcn2 to induce Cre recombination after recovery from surgery and infection. This allowed us to avoid the effects of developmental lcn2 knockout on anxiety-like behaviors (Ferreira et al., 2013), neural stem cell proliferation (Ferreira et al., 2018), and apoptosis (Wu et al., 2021). It also allowed us to specifically address the role of persistent LCN2 excess in the survivor phenotype by avoiding any LCN2 manipulation during the initial recovery. Thus, we were able to separate the effects of LCN2 in operative and infection survivors from its role in the acute immune response. While this strategy decreased LCN2 levels and completely abrogated the increase in peripheral LCN2 in CLP survivors, we only saw a partial knockout of lcn2 in the brain. Decreased efficacy of tamoxifen-induced Cre recombination in the brain has been described in inducible knockout models (Ilchuk et al., 2022), which could be attributed to cell- or tissue-specific expression of the Rosa26 locus, Cre-ERT2 activity, or partial tamoxifen delivery due to the blood–brain barrier. The heterogeneity of lcn2 knockout in the brain explains why its effects on neuroinflammation and behavior were seen in the analysis of individual animal-level data but not at the group level.

Our finding of a close relationship between brain lcn2 levels and neuroinflammation and behavior suggests the central importance of brain-derived LCN2 in these outcomes, but we cannot rule out an important role for systemic LCN2. Indeed, LCN2 is known to cross the blood–brain barrier (Mosialou et al., 2017; Petropoulou et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2024), and in our experiments, exogenous administration of LCN2 was sufficient to increase anxiety-like behavior in the OF. These behavioral findings are consistent with studies that reported anxiety-like behaviors following either a single intracerebroventricular injection of rLCN2 or two weeks of daily rLCN2 intraperitoneal injections (Chen et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2024). We did, however, observe different effects of rLCN2 in the OF and the EZM, which likely reflect the distinct facets of anxiety-like behavior associated with these paradigms (Snyder et al., 2021). The OF is sensitive to novelty-induced avoidance and changes in exploratory drive, while the EZM is designed to assess approach-avoidance conflict under height-related threat. This pattern of behavioral changes in the OF but not the EZM was also observed following laparotomy. In addition to the behavioral phenotypes observed, our findings showed that acute administration of rLCN2 increased the number of active neurons and the average calcium amplitude in the dorsal CA1 region in vivo. Those results are broadly consistent with prior work showing that LCN2 regulates hippocampal activity (Ferreira et al., 2013; Mucha et al., 2011). Our imaging experiment focused specifically on the dorsal CA1 region for several reasons. First, prior studies have demonstrated that LCN2 deficiency alters hippocampal neuronal activity and synaptic function (Ferreira et al., 2013; Mucha et al., 2011; Olson et al., 2021) with evident effects in the CA1. Moreover, compared to other subfields such as the dentate gyrus and the CA3, the dCA1 is especially sensitive to neuroinflammation (Nairuz et al., 2024; Ugolini et al., 2018) and plays a critical role in exploratory behavior (Gavade et al., 2022; Zeng et al., 2024). These characteristics make the dCA1 an ideal target for investigating the effects of LCN2 on hippocampal activity. While our imaging results are based on two animals, we imaged hundreds of neurons and saw a consistent effect of LCN2 administration on hippocampal activity with repeated administration in a counterbalanced design. It is also important to note that Mucha et al. (2011) reported increased neuronal excitability in lcn2-null mice, contrasting with our observation that elevated LCN2 enhances neuronal activity, suggesting that the effects of LCN2 on hippocampal activity are not uniform and, as with its effects on neuroinflammation, likely context-dependent. While our current study demonstrates associations between brain lcn2 RNA, neuroinflammation, and anxiety-like behavior, the knockout model was not limited to the brain, and we also found effects of peripherally administered LCN2 on behavior and hippocampal activity. Therefore, distinguishing the specific contributions of centrally versus peripherally derived LCN2 on these endpoints remains an important future goal.

The positive relationship between lcn2 levels and anxiety-like behavior seen here appears to conflict with studies reporting increased anxiety-like behavior and LPS-induced sickness behavior in the lcn2 knockout (Ferreira et al., 2013; Kang et al., 2018). This could be attributed to a role of LCN2 in neurodevelopment. It is also possible that the role of LCN2 in neuroinflammation, and therefore behavior, differs in the acute versus chronic inflammatory setting (Cherry et al., 2014). Based on both our findings and prior work, we propose a model in which LCN2 serves a protective, anti-inflammatory role during the acute phase of infection with inflammation by preventing bacterial proliferation and dampening excessive immune activation. However, when LCN2 is chronically elevated, such as after a surgical or infectious insult, its function shifts, contributing to ongoing neuroinflammation and promoting maladaptive behaviors. This dual role has important implications for therapeutic interventions targeting LCN2, as the timing and context of treatment will be critical in determining efficacy and safety.

Our study has a few additional limitations. We initially saw an independent effect of tamoxifen on locomotion and exploratory behavior, which has been seen previously (Li et al., 2020) and which limited the interpretability of our behavioral data from the first knockout experiment. We controlled for this in the second experiment by decreasing the tamoxifen dose, prolonging the time between treatment and behavior, and administering tamoxifen to all Cre-ERT2+ and Cre-ERT2− mice. However, we still cannot rule out an effect of tamoxifen on outcomes. The additional stress of multiple injections plus tamoxifen may also explain why we did not consistently see group differences between sham and CLP mice on behavior as we have in the past using this infection paradigm (Hill et al., 2024; Spencer-Segal et al., 2020). This lack of difference in anxiety-like behavior between sham and CLP groups could also be explained by the effect of laparotomy itself on behavior (Fig. 6), and by single housing in the current experiments, which can itself increase anxiety-like behavior and influence the response to an infectious or inflammatory challenge (Al Omran et al., 2022; Hamilton et al., 2022; Magalhães et al., 2024). Additionally, based on our findings, we cannot definitively conclude whether LCN2 acts directly in the brain parenchyma on neurons, microglia or other brain cell types. Even brain endothelium-derived LCN2 may be secreted apically into blood vessels or basally into the brain parenchyma. Clarification of this mechanism is an important area for future study. We also cannot definitively conclude that peripheral LCN2 injections alter hippocampal activity and behavior via the activation of neuroinflammatory pathways. Although our findings demonstrate associations between LCN2 expression, hippocampal activity, and anxiety-like behaviors, we did not directly establish a mechanistic link between the LCN2-induced changes in hippocampal activity and behavior, though this is plausible. Finally, since the exogenous administration studies were only performed in males, we cannot be sure that the same effects would occur in females. While we did not find any sex differences in our experiments with lcn2 knockout, LCN2 can act in a sex-specific manner in metabolic settings and can affect estrogen receptor signaling in female mice (Chella Krishnan et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2012; Miao et al., 2024).

In summary, we found that LCN2 is produced in excess by brain endothelial cells in infection survivors. Using male and female inducible lcn2 knockout mice, we provided evidence that brain lcn2 perpetuates neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behaviors after recovery from laparotomy and polymicrobial abdominal infection. Exogenous LCN2 administration was sufficient to increase hippocampal pyramidal cell activity in vivo and induce anxiety-like behavior in male mice. These findings suggest that LCN2 is an important and potentially targetable molecular mediator of post-surgical and post-infectious neuroinflammation and anxiety-like behavior.

From a clinical perspective, our results raise the possibility that plasma LCN2 could serve as a biomarker to predict the risk and severity of neuropsychiatric symptoms and neuroinflammation following sepsis or major surgery. Additionally, our findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting LCN2 as a strategy to mitigate physical and neuropsychiatric sequelae in sepsis survivors. Further studies in patient cohorts will be essential to assess the utility of plasma LCN2 as a predictive marker and to determine the efficacy of LCN2-based interventions in reducing brain dysfunction after critical illness.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our study primarily examined both male and female mice, and similar results were observed across sexes. Any sex differences were indicated in the figures or described in the text. All recombinant LCN2 experiments were performed in male mice only due to limited resource availability.

Experimental animals

Experiments were performed using either 10–12-week-old male and female C57BL/6J mice (JAX:000664) obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (Jax; Bar Harbor, ME) or Lcn2loxP x CreERT2 weanlings generated in-house. The following mouse strains were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory: (1) B6.129-Gt(ROSA)26Sortm1(cre/ERT2)Tyj/J (JAX:008463) and (2) Lcn2tm1.1Bara/J (JAX:031034) and were bred in-house to generate the experimental cohorts used in this study. Specifically, lcn2fl/fl mice were crossed with Cre-ERT2 mice, and progeny with the floxed lcn2 allele that were heterozygous for Cre were intercrossed to maintain the colony and produce the desired genotypes: lcn2fl/fl/ERT2-Cre+/+, lcn2fl/fl/ERT2-Cre+/− and lcn2fl/fl/ERT2-Cre−/−. Tail samples from the progeny were sent to and genotyped by Transnetyx via polymerase chain reaction (PCR). Ten to twelve-week-old male and female C57BL/6J mice and mice expressing both Cre-ERT2 and lcn2loxP transgenes between the ages of 3 and 12 months old (sample sizes are described in the corresponding figure legends) were either group-housed (C57BL/6J mice) or single-housed (transgenic mice) on a 14:10 light/dark cycle with food and water provided ad libitum (5L0D – PicoLab Laboratory Rodent Diet; Purina Lab Diet). For experiments involving transgenic mice, lcn2fl/fl/ERT2-Cre−/− littermates were used as negative controls. All experiments were approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol No. PRO00011000) and were conducted in accordance with the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Sham and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)

Day 0 marked the day of surgeries. On day 0, all mice underwent either sham or cecal ligation and puncture surgery. Mice were anesthetized using 3–4% isoflurane in oxygen (induction; VetOne) and the surgical site was shaved using an electric razor. Eye lubricant was applied to each eye, mice were placed in a nose cone, and isoflurane levels were reduced to 1–2% (maintenance). The surgical site was disinfected with betadine (Henry Schein, Inc.) and 70% EtOH using sterile cotton swabs. A toe pinch response was used to assess the animal’s level of consciousness and nociception. In the absence of a response, 60 μL of bupivacaine (0.15 mg) was injected subcutaneously at the surgical site using an insulin syringe. A 1-cm midline laparotomy was performed under aseptic conditions. For CLP, the cecum was tightly ligated 5 mm from the end using a non-dissolvable silk loop and punctured through-and-through with a 19-gauge needle. For sham laparotomy, the cecum was removed and placed back into the peritoneal cavity. The peritoneum was then closed using dissolvable 4–0 sutures (Ethicon), and the skin incision was closed with 2–3 surgical clips. Fluids (1 mL of 0.9% saline; Hospira, Inc.) and antibiotics (0.5 mg of imipenem-cilastatin; Fresenius Kabi) were administered via subcutaneous injections. Mice were placed in a clean cage on a heating pad until they were bright, alert, and responsive.

For the unoperated and laparotomy groups, 16 control mice were exposed to 3–4% isoflurane in oxygen until loss of the toe-pinch reflex, and 16 experimental mice underwent a laparotomy as described previously. The University of Michigan policy for humane endpoints was followed. If mice appeared too sick (temperature or body weight never recovered) or in pain, they were euthanized using carbon dioxide. Post-operative care was performed daily for 10 days.

Cecal slurry (CS)

The experimental procedure was adapted from the study by (Bongers et al., 2024), with modifications to align with the objectives of the present research. Briefly, mice received intraperitoneal injections of either 0.9% saline or 18 μL of cecal slurry per gram of body weight. Six hours post-injection, all mice were administered an intraperitoneal injection of an antibiotic mixture consisting of 25 mg/kg metronidazole and 75 mg/kg ceftriaxone in sterile saline. Antibiotic treatment continued with twice-daily intraperitoneal injections, along with subcutaneous administration of warmed 0.9% saline, for four consecutive days. On day 13 following CS induction, blood was collected via intracardiac puncture, and mice were perfused with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Plasma samples were subsequently analyzed to assess circulating LCN2 levels.

Klebsiella pneumoniae (KPA)

The experimental procedure involving Klebsiella pneumoniae was conducted exactly as outlined in (Denstaedt et al., 2020). Two weeks after intranasal KPA administration, blood was collected, and serum samples were used to assess LCN2 levels in the circulation.

In vivo drug and protein administration

Tamoxifen (Thermo Scientific Chemicals) was dissolved in corn oil (Thermo Scientific Chemicals) at a concentration of 15 mg/mL by stirring until the powder was dissolved at 55°C. The mixture was foil-wrapped and placed in the 4°C fridge when not in use. Tamoxifen (75 mg/kg) or corn oil (vehicle) was administered daily for either 3 or 5 consecutive days via intraperitoneal injections.

Recombinant mouse LCN2 (apo form, iron-free) was expressed and isolated from Rosetta2 DE3 competent cells by the University of Michigan Center for Structural Biology. Endotoxin levels were assessed and quantified using the Pierce Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To ensure measurements within the linear range of the assay, the rLCN2 protein stock (10 mg/mL) was serially diluted. The recombinant protein was diluted in 0.9% saline the morning of injections and administered subcutaneously twice daily for 10 days at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg. Subcutaneous injections were chosen to minimize animal stress, slow down absorption, and avoid potential peritoneal inflammation.

Behavioral tests

Anxiety-like behavior was assessed in the elevated zero maze by measuring exploration in a brightly lit, ~200 lx, circular apparatus comprised of two opposing quadrants enclosed by 16 cm tall opaque walls and two unenclosed opposing quadrants (inner diameter: 50 cm, outer diameter: 62 cm, 52 cm off the ground, width of the track: 12 cm). At the beginning of each trial, the mice were placed in the open area facing the closed area and were allowed to explore the apparatus for 5 min. The total distance traveled, time spent in the open areas, and the number of entries into the open areas were used to assess locomotion and anxiety-like behavior.

The open field was also used to assess anxiety-like behavior by measuring exploration in a brightly lit, ~200 lx, open square arena (72 cm × 72 cm × 36 cm). The mice were placed in the center of the open field and allowed 10 min to explore the arena. The total distance traveled, time spent in the center, and the number of entries into the center were used to assess locomotion and anxiety-like behavior.

For both the elevated zero maze and open field, mice were allowed to acclimate to the behavioral room 1 h prior to the start of testing. Each cage was given a water bottle. Animal behavior was recorded with an overhead camera and analyzed with EthoVision XT Version 15.0 (Noldus et al., 2001).

RNAscope

Fresh-frozen brains were sectioned coronally at 10-μm thickness onto slides using a cryostat and stored at −80°C until use. Commercially available RNAscope kits (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Inc.) were used for fluorescence in situ hybridization detection of mRNAs as described in (Wang et al., 2012) for lcn2, tek (endothelial cell marker), and rbfox3 (neuronal marker). Lcn2 was assigned the C1 probe for the FITC fluorophore, tek (endothelial marker) was assigned C2 probe for the Cy3 fluorophore, and rbfox3 (neuronal marker) was assigned the C3 probe for the Cy5 fluorophore. Fluorophores (Akoya Biosciences) were chosen to have minimal overlap in fluorescent spectra. Tissues were also co-labelled with DAPI to label nuclei.

Tissue harvest

Mice were given 5 mg of nembutal (pentobarbital sodium solution) via intraperitoneal injections. A toe pinch response was used to assess the animal’s level of consciousness and nociception. In the absence of a response, mice were placed on a Styrofoam stage, and their paws were pinned to the stage using needles. The chest cavity was exposed, and blood was collected via intracardiac puncture by placing a 26-gauge needle in the left ventricle and slowly drawing out the blood. For plasma, the blood was transferred to EDTA-coated microvettes (Sarstedt, Inc.) and placed on ice. For serum, it was transferred to regular 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes and left at room temperature for 1 h to allow for blood clot formation. Trans-cardiac perfusions were performed using 50 mL of ice-cold 1X PBS composed of 13 mM NaCl, 0.27 mM KCl, 1 mM Na2HPO4, and 0.18 mM KH2PO4. Liver, spleen, heart, and brain tissues were collected and stored in RNA-later solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 4°C for 24 h. The tissue samples were subsequently frozen at −80°C until further use. Blood tubes were centrifuged at 1500× g for 10 min at 4°C, and plasma and serum samples were aliquoted and placed in the −80°C freezer until further use.

Human tissue processing and RNA sequencing

Human samples used in this study were analyzed as described previously (Bustamante et al., 2020) (accession number GSE135838) and the cohort characteristics are provided below. Briefly, RNA was isolated from the parietal cortex gray matter of 24 subjects (12 sepsis, 12 nonsepsis). In the sepsis group, the mean age was 91.2 ± 5.6 years (4 males, 8 females), and in the nonsepsis group, 88.7 ± 9.4 years (8 males, 4 females). Age, Braak and CERAD scores, dementia diagnosis, and RNA integrity were comparable between the sepsis and nonsepsis groups. In the sepsis group, sepsis sources included pulmonary (n = 7), urinary (n = 2), abdominal (n = 2), and unknown (n = 1). Causes of death in the nonsepsis group included cardiac (n = 5), hemorrhage (n = 4), respiratory failure (n = 1), and comfort care (n = 2).

In vitro assay

Bone-marrow-derived macrophages were isolated and, after one week in culture, were treated in technical duplicates with either vehicle (1 × PBS) or recombinant LCN2 at 0.05 μg/mL, 0.5 μg/mL, 2.5 μg/mL, or 5 μg/mL. Six hours later, protein levels of TNFα and IL-6 in the culture medium were quantified by ELISA.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from liver and hemibrain tissue using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The concentration and purity of RNA were determined by absorbance at 260/280 nm and 260/230 using a Nanodrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The RNA was purified using lithium chloride precipitation (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 1 μg of DNase-treated RNA was used to generate complementary DNA (cDNA) using reagents from a GoTaq 2-Step RT-qPCR kit (Promega). The cDNA was diluted 1:10 with nuclease-free water, and 30 ng of cDNA was used for qPCR reactions, which were run on either a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad) or a ViiA 7 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with GoTaq® qPCR Master Mix containing BRYT Green® Dye (Promega). β-actin was used as a housekeeping gene to normalize the results from gene-specific reactions. Primers used for qPCR were obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies. The primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Plasma and serum LCN2 levels were quantified according to the manufacturer’s protocol using a mouse lipocalin-2 ELISA kit (RayBiotech).

In vivo microendoscopic calcium imaging and data processing

The neuronal activity of dorsal CA1 pyramidal cells was measured in freely behaving mice using the nVista 3.0 miniature endoscope (Inscopix) following the procedures described in Resendez et al. (2016) and Gavade et al. (2022). Briefly, adult male mice underwent stereotactic surgeries and injection of AAV5-CamKIIa-GCamp6f (Addgene; 300 nL diluted 1:5 in artificial CSF. AP: −2.05, ML: 1.75 from bregma; DZ: −1.3 from skull) in the right hemisphere. Three weeks later, the mice underwent implantation of a 4 × 1 mm GRIN lens (AP: −1.95, ML: 1.6 from bregma, DZ −1.55 from skull) over the dorsal CA1. The mice were habituated to a square 40 cm × 40 cm arena with the miniscope on for 3 days prior to the start of the experiment. On recording days, they were injected with either 0.9% saline or 0.5 mg/kg rLCN2 subcutaneously (counterbalanced between animals) and were allowed 1 h to recover prior to exploring the arena for 10 min. Behavior was recorded and analyzed using Ethovision XT (version 15) and synchronized with calcium imaging using TTL pulses with a Noldus I/O Box. Each mouse underwent 4 different recording sessions with 2 days of rest in between, allowing for recovery. Following completion of behavioral testing, mice were perfused transcardially with 1X ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed overnight and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose following the careful removal of the GRIN lens. Brains were sectioned coronally at 30 μm using a sliding microtome (Leica). Sections containing dorsal CA1 were mounted and coverslipped with Vectashield mounting medium with DAPI (Vector Laboratories). Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus microscope equipped with a DP23M camera and 1.25× intermediate magnifier, operated through cellSens Standard software, to verify GCaMP6f expression and GRIN lens placement.

CaImAn, an open-source tool for scalable calcium imaging data analysis, was used to perform background subtraction, cell identification, and Δf/F trace extraction on collected calcium imaging data (Giovannucci et al., 2019; Zhou et al., 2018). The imaging sessions spanned multiple days (2 days for rLCN2 and 2 days for 0.9% saline for each animal, counterbalanced in order), capturing dynamic activity patterns across neuronal populations for different treatment groups. For multisession registration, we leveraged the spatial footprints of neurons obtained from individual sessions, which allowed us to reliably track the same neuronal populations over time. By aligning neurons across sessions, we obtained a longitudinal dataset suitable for analyzing consistent neuronal activity patterns for different treatment groups.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA), RStudio, and Python. Statistical analyses were performed using independent two-tailed t-tests, Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data sets, one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey-Kramer post-test as specified in the figure legends. Spearman correlations were used to examine the relationship between brain RNA levels of lcn2, lrg1, selp, and s100a8 when these data sets were not normally distributed. A log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test was used to compare the survival distributions of sham and CLP groups. The regression models used to determine the relationship between brain lcn2, and behavior were specified as follows: Yi (EZM/OF Behavior) = β0 + β1·Surgeryi + β2·Genotypei + β3·Sexi + β4·Lcn2 log2 Brain + ∊i (all groups) and Yi (EZM/OF Behavior) = β0 + β1·Genotypei + β2·Sexi + β3·Lcn2 log2 Brain +∊i (CLP group only). Another linear regression model was fitted to evaluate the relationship between the average calcium amplitude (dependent variable), treatment (vehicle and rLCN2; independent variables), and animals (fixed effect) using R. When appropriate, normality (Shapiro-Wilk; α = 0.05), log2 transformation, and ROUT (outlier test) were performed to ensure normal distributions of data sets. For all analyses, a p-value less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. In all figure legends, n represents the number of biologically independent animals or technical replicates.
