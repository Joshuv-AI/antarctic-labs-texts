Pathogenic and genetic landscape of Still’s disease across ages, with new insights into age-related IL-18 patterns
Authors: Greta Rogani, Haeja Kessler, Bella Mehta, Elizabeth Baker, Helen Leavis, Sebastiaan Vastert, Grant Schulert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStill’s disease (SD) is now widely recognized as a single disease spectrum encompassing both childhood- and adult-onset forms. While most comparative studies so far have focused on clinical manifestations and treatment responses, aspects related to the pathophysiology of the disease including molecular mechanisms and genetic predisposition have remained less deeply explored.In this review, we provide an integrated overview of current knowledge on the pathogenic pathways driving SD. We summarize evidence supporting their contribution across the age spectrum from children to adults, and discuss age-related trends in IL-18 and S100 proteins, recognized key-mediators of systemic inflammation.To complement the existing literature, we present novel data on IL-18 measurements collected in the context of cohort studies and clinical practice in three tertiary centers involved in SD care across the ages, showing a modest but significant age-related decline in patient IL-18 levels across sJIA and AOSD cohorts.Finally, we emphasize that both sJIA and AOSD share convergent genetic associations within the HLA class II region, although specific variants may exert different functional effects. In particular, HLA-DRB1*15 has been linked to disease susceptibility in the adult spectrum, whereas in children this specific HLA background appears to predispose to lung involvement and features of hypersensitivity.Altogether, the data presented collectively support the view that SD represents a single acquired and complex autoinflammatory disease in which both pediatric and adult forms share core pathogenic mechanisms as well as genetic associations within the HLA class II region. Nevertheless, we observed some differences between children and adults that remain difficult to interpret, as it remains unclear whether they reflect true biological variation or result from differences in study design or methodology. Further comparative, cross-cohort and longitudinal studies will be needed to resolve these uncertainties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13075-026-03808-8.Keywords: Still’s Disease, Systemic Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis, Adult-Onset Still’s Disease, IL-18, s100 proteins, HLA class II, Autoinflammation, Genetic susceptibility

Introduction

Still’s disease (SD) is increasingly regarded as a unified disease entity manifesting across the age spectrum, from childhood to adulthood. It was first described in 1897 by George Frederick Still as a pediatric condition characterized by destructive arthritis, fever, and an evanescent rash, distinct from other rheumatic diseases of the time. In 1971, Eric Bywaters reported the same clinical phenotype in young adults and introduced the term Adult-Onset Still’s Disease (AOSD).

Despite their clear clinical overlap from the outset, differences in clinical practice and age-based classification criteria led to their separation within pediatric and adult medicine. These entities became designated as systemic Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (sJIA) in children and AOSD in adults, and continue to be addressed within distinct nomenclature and therapeutic frameworks [1]. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence has progressively supported the concept that sJIA and AOSD represent age-dependent manifestations of the same underlying disease process.

Although currently classified under the umbrella of Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA), systemic JIA (sJIA) represents a distinct clinical entity characterized by prominent systemic inflammation rather than primary joint disease. The International League of Associations for Rheumatology (ILAR) classification, introduced in 1997, formally included sJIA among the seven JIA subtypes [2], yet its phenotype, marked by quotidian fever, evanescent rash, lymphadenopathy, hepato-splenomegaly, and serositis, clearly sets it apart from the other categories. sJIA accounts for approximately 10–20% of all JIA cases in Europe and North America, and for 30–50% in India and Japan. Reported incidence rates range from 0.4 to 0.8 cases per 100,000 children per year in low-incidence regions to as high as 1.5–2 cases per 100,000 children per year in areas with higher prevalence [3]. Importantly, arthritis may be absent at onset, a feature that prompted the Pediatric Rheumatology International Trials Organization (PRINTO) to revise the classification criteria and remove the mandatory requirement of arthritis, thereby improving diagnostic sensitivity and allowing timely therapeutic intervention. The complete PRINTO classification criteria for sJIA are shown in Fig. 1 [4].

Currently widely adopted classification criteria for sJIA, ILAR [2], PRINTO [4], and AOSD, Yamaguchi [5]

In contrast, AOSD was initially described in young adults (age 16–35) but can occur in older adults and the elderly population [6, 7]. The incidence of AOSD is lower than that of sJIA. In Europe, the estimated annual incidence of AOSD ranges from 0.1 to 0.4 cases per 100,000 people [8]. In Japan, a retrospective survey reported incidence rates of 0.22 and 0.34 cases per 100,000 for men and women, respectively [9], but this data is likely outdated given the advancement in diagnosis over the past few decades [6]. The Yamaguchi classification criteria were first described in 1992. The classification criteria are composed of major and minor criteria as shown in Fig. 1 [5].

These differences in classification based on arbitrary age cutoffs have perpetuated the notion that sJIA and AOSD are distinct conditions. However, they are remarkably similar clinically. A multicenter Spanish study with 326 patients diagnosed with either sJIA or AOSD reported mostly similar clinical manifestations, with sJIA having a higher reported frequency of rash while those with AOSD a higher frequency of serositis, morning stiffness, arthralgia, odynophagia, and lymphadenopathy [10]. Similarly, a meta-analysis by De Matteis et al. found only sore throat and myalgia to be more frequently reported in AOSD versus sJIA, while noting these symptoms could be hard for young children to report [1]. AOSD and sJIA resemble each other with lab manifestations including elevated Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), hyperferritinemia, and elevated inflammatory cytokines. Both populations are also at risk for severe complications such as Macrophage Activation Syndrome (MAS) and Lung Disease (LD). Finally, both conditions also show remarkable treatment responses to IL-1 and IL-6 inhibition [1].

As clinical manifestations, laboratory abnormalities, and treatment responses increasingly support the view that AOSD and sJIA represent the same disease, experts involved in a recent series of consensus meetings, developing an international treatment guideline for SD, proposed adopting the term “Still’s disease” to describe and unify the pediatric and adult-onset forms [11]. In light of this proposal, we sought to further examine the immunopathogenic and genetic studies in this disease spectrum across the ages. Our objectives were to highlight areas of similarity and difference, as well as consider the future research agenda into SD. For a comprehensive examination of clinical features in SD across the ages we refer to several recent reports that are summarized in Table 1 [7, 10, 12–15].

Immunopathogenesis

The initial clinical manifestations of SD reflect an autoinflammatory phenotype, and a general overview of the underlying immunological processes, as currently understood, is presented in Fig. 2. As further detailed below, individuals may have a genetic predisposition towards this phenotype and (largely unknown) triggers may induce a robust and sustained pro-inflammatory response [16]. This pro-inflammatory response is thought to be driven by pathogen associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) which may include environmental, chemical, or infectious etiologies [6, 16]. PAMPs/DAMPs transmit signals via Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs) such as TLR-4 and TLR-7, which contribute to activation of other pattern recognition receptors including the NOD-like receptor family CARD domain-containing protein 4 (NLRC4) and NOD-like receptor family Pyrin domain–containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasomes promoting downstream activation of monocytes/macrophages. Inflammasome activation triggers caspase-1 leading to the overproduction of active IL-1β and IL-18 which is a central step in the pathogenesis of SD [17, 18].

Proposed model of immunopathogenesis and disease progression in SD. The figure summarizes key innate and adaptive immune mechanisms involved in SD. In genetically predisposed individuals, PAMPs/DAMPs activate macrophages and monocytes through PRRs, such as TLR-4, TLR-9, and RAGE, leading to NLRP3 inflammasome activation and the release of IL-1β and IL-18. These cytokines amplify inflammation, induce S100 protein secretion, ferritin release, and neutrophil activation with NET formation, characterizing the febrile, innate-driven phase of the disease. Persistent cytokine stimulation promotes activation of CD4⁺ T cells (Th1, Th17) and a shift toward adaptive immunity, contributing to chronic arthritis in later stages. This evolution reflects the biphasic model of SD, in which an early autoinflammatory phase transitions into a secondary adaptive immune phase [19]

During inflammation, neutrophils are recruited and activated via interactions with PAMPs, DAMPs, and various Pattern Recognition Receptors (PRRs). Studies in sJIA report neutrophil activation in patients with active and clinically inactive disease [20, 21]. Similarly, studies in AOSD show an increased neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and transcriptomic studies show enrichment of signatures for neutrophil activation and degranulation [22, 23]. The activated neutrophils communicate with macrophages, and demonstrate enhanced formation of Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) [17]. There are numerous components to NETs such as the S100 proteins including S100A8/A9 (calprotectin, MRP8/14) and S100A12. These are calcium-binding proteins which are released from myeloid cells, and function as DAMPs to transmit signals via TLR-4 and Receptor for Advanced Glycation End-products (RAGE), respectively, leading to expression of proinflammatory cytokine genes [24, 25]. Importantly, IL-18 can induce NET formation by enhancing calcium influx into neutrophils [24], serving as a pro-inflammatory feedback loop (Fig. 2). Studies in AOSD have investigated the pathogenic role of NETs by quantifying cell-free DNA and NET-DNA complexes, both of which are increased in patients compared to healthy controls [26, 27]. NET-DNA complex studies have not been reported in sJIA; however, higher histone levels as a proxy for NETosis have been evaluated and higher levels correlated to disease activity and clinical manifestations [28]. Notably, several studies have shown profound elevations in S100 protein levels in patients with both sJIA and AOSD (see below) [25, 29–31].

Overproduction of inflammatory cytokines, most notably the IL-1 family members IL-1β and IL-18, are likely pivotal in the pathogenesis of SD through activation of both the innate and adaptive immune responses. IL-18 in particular was initially named “IFN-γ inducing factor” and leads to activation and differentiation of various T-cell populations. IL-18 in conjunction with IL-12 and/or IL-15 can induce IFN-γ production via NK cells, CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells [18, 32]. Notably, one of the severe complications of SD is MAS which is an IFN-γ driven process [17]. Recent studies have shown significantly elevated levels of IL-18 in sJIA patients who develop MAS compared to those who do not develop MAS, and one paper in AOSD found patients who developed MAS had higher IL-18/IL-6 and IL-18/CRP ratios [33, 34]. Both IL-18 and IL-1β induce synthesis of other cytokines and chemokines including IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ, and TNF. IL-1β also increases expression of adhesion molecules on endothelial cells to promote the migration of neutrophils and leukocytes; and IL-18 and IL-1β lead to further activation of macrophages, neutrophils, and S100 proteins [16, 18]. Notably, medications have been developed to target the IL-1 pathway including anakinra which binds to the IL-1 receptor, canakinumab which blocks IL-1β, and rilonacept which is a soluble decoy receptor [35], and the use of IL-1 inhibitor monotherapy is one of the recommended first line agents for treatment of sJIA and AOSD [11, 36, 37]. IL-6 is a central driver of the acute phase response, and is implicated in development of symptoms such as arthritis, fever, and skin rash. Blocking the IL-6 receptor with medication such as tocilizumab leads to decreased systemic inflammation and improves arthritis [17, 38]. Distinct clinical phenotypes have been proposed in SD where those with systemic disease have relatively higher levels of IL-18 and IL-1β, while those with arthritis driven disease have elevated IL-6 and TNF-α [17].

SD is also characterized by profoundly elevated ferritin (particularly in MAS). Ferritin is an indicator of macrophage activation by inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β, IL-6, and IFN-γ. Ferritin has been shown to activate Nuclear Factor-κB (NF-κB) leading to further pro-inflammatory cytokine production including IL-1β [6, 16]. Studies in JIA have shown ferritin levels to be highest among patients with sJIA compared to other juvenile arthritis subtypes, and higher ferritin levels have been shown to have a positive correlation with disease activity using JADAS-10 [39, 40]. It has also been proposed to use ferritin to ESR ratio to assess for MAS versus active sJIA, and to use ferritin to platelet ratio to predict who will be prone to MAS [41, 42]. Similarly, in AOSD, ferritin levels are significantly elevated compared to non-AOSD controls including those with other rheumatic diseases [43, 44]. In addition to total ferritin levels, glycosylated ferritin has emerged as a clinically relevant marker in AOSD. Glycosylated ferritin is markedly reduced during active disease, and this pattern is particularly useful in distinguishing AOSD from other hyperferritinemic conditions [43].

Taken together, these observations illustrate how both sJIA and AOSD are initiated by a profoundly dysregulated innate immune response, in which IL-1β, IL-18, S100 proteins and activated myeloid cells serve as early drivers of inflammation. The biphasic model in sJIA proposes that IL-1 production favors the production of pathogenic T cell responses (i.e. Th1, Th17) leading to Treg inhibition and T-cell driven synovitis [19, 45, 46]. Similarly, one study analyzed flow cytometry data in untreated AOSD patients and showed a significant elevation in Th17 cells in patients compared to healthy controls [47]. A second study in AOSD found a significant decrease in CD4+CD25high Treg cells in patients with chronic arthritis compared to patients presenting with a monocyclic systemic course [48]. The model of an autoinflammatory phase preceding and conditioning downstream adaptive responses was proposed more than a decade ago and continues to provide a useful interpretive framework for understanding the broader biological determinants of SD.

Elevation of IL-18 and S100 proteins across the ages: a comprehensive literature review and novel evidence on IL-18 dynamics

To better understand the relationship between age and the distinctive IL-18 and S100 protein levels in patients with SD, we performed a comprehensive literature review and visualized this data using forest plots, and then complemented it with results from our own patient cohorts. As sample time points, disease activity, and other patient characteristics vary across each cohort and contribute to heterogeneity of the data, all of which can influence IL-18 and S100 protein levels, the plots are mainly to be used as descriptive visual summaries, rather than a formal meta-analysis. Starting with published data, papers had different methods of reporting levels using median or mean. We found 9 papers in AOSD [49–57] and 11 papers in sJIA [20, 49, 57–65] that looked at IL-18 levels. As authors differed in reporting interquartile range versus overall range, only median values are shown, while mean IL-18 is shown with standard deviation (Fig. 3). If the standard deviation was not provided by the study, it was calculated using the reported confidence interval. While difficult to directly compare studies, some studies in sJIA patients show higher IL-18 levels compared to AOSD, although this is not universally true. Additionally, patients with active disease, whether in AOSD or sJIA, typically had higher levels of IL-18 compared to those with inactive disease.

There was specifically limited data on S100A12 levels in adults, as only one paper was found on AOSD [75]. This study analyzed 37 subjects with the mean value being 547.9ng/mL, which was significantly lower than that found in papers studying sJIA patients [20, 31, 69, 70, 76]. However, limited data makes it hard to compare the two. Additionally, measurements of IL-18 and S100A8/A9 vary based on assay and are not standardized across institutions, which may lead to difficulties interpreting data across cohorts.

As noted, while IL-18 levels are markedly elevated in patients across the SD age spectrum, meaningful comparisons between published studies remain difficult due to differences in study design, patient populations, and assay methodologies. To examine this further, we analyzed IL-18 levels from children with sJIA and adults with AOSD at Cincinnati Children’s Hospital, Hospital for Special Surgery in New York, and University Medical Center (UMC) of Utrecht. Data collection from Cincinnati Children’s was approved by IRB (CCHMC#1028–2409) and informed consent was obtained from all patients and their parents/guardians. The Utrecht ethical consent was covered under protocols 16–178, 16–474, and 25–0401. Data collection for the Hospital for Special Surgery was approved by IRB - The Established Still’s Disease Cohort (#2022 − 1072). If a patient had one sample collection, this was included in both the minimum and maximum figure (Figs. 5 and 6, respectively). A total of 172 levels of IL-18 were included in the analysis. Notably, information regarding disease activity, treatment history and response, recurrence of MAS, disease duration, and additional parameters were not taken into account given heterogeneity of data from each institution, all of which may influence IL-18 levels. Genetic landscape of SD

The specific cytokine-driven (auto-)inflammatory state of early SD raises the question of whether shared genetic determinants contribute to disease onset and its variable severity across ages. To address this, the genetic basis of SD has been investigated in both pediatric and adult forms, yet the quantity and quality of available evidence differ substantially between sJIA and AOSD.

The most robust and comprehensive evidence stems from a genome-wide association-study (GWAS) of the largest sJIA population assembled including 902 patients and 8010 controls from nine countries and three continents (collected under the International Childhood Arthritis Genetics Consortium, INCHARGE). The primary genetic contributor to disease risk was identified in the HLA class II locus (Table 2), implicating a role of the adaptive immune mechanisms in sJIA for the first time [77]. Further MHC region study identified that the peak-SNP was located nearest to HLA-DRA gene and was in strong linkage disequilibrium (LD) with the HLA allele, HLA-DRB1*11. Other sJIA-associated alleles and haplotypes are listed in Table 3. Finally, SNPs and alleles were reported to be co-inherited on a sJIA-associated 243 kilobase (kb) haplotype including HLA-DRA, the HLA-DRB gene cluster, HLA-DQA1, and HLA-DQB1 [78]. A second significant genetic contribution to sJIA consists of a cluster of 14 SNPs located upstream of LINC01777, a long intergenic non-coding RNA region on chromosome 1 (Table 2). An additional 20 loci were highly suggestive, though not reaching genome-wide significance [77]. Importantly, the GWAS study demonstrated also that the genetic signature of sJIA remarkably differs from those of rheumatoid factor (RF)-positive polyarticular JIA or the combination of oligoarticular and RF-negative polyarticular JIA, further supporting the biological distinction of sJIA [77].

Before this large and definitive study, genetic investigations had relied mainly on small case–control candidate gene studies, often limited by biased locus selection and insufficient statistical power. These identified associations with more than 20 SNPs across 11 loci (IL1 family, IL1R family, IL10 family, IL6, MIF, TPSN, CCR5, SLC26A2, MVK, TNFRSF1A, for further details see Supplementary Table 2, Additional File 1), most of which lacked a consistent replication [81, 82]. Indeed, nearly all of these genetic associations have at least one study that found no significant link to sJIA [82].

To systematically evaluate these findings, a regional association study of the 11 reported loci was conducted within the INCHARGE sJIA case–control collection. This analysis was similarly unable to validate earlier candidate SNPs but instead uncovered three variants in the IL1RN promoter region associated with sJIA risk, that correlated with reduced IL1RN expression and serum IL1Ra protein level, linking low gene, and related protein, expression levels to sJIA risk [81]. Within the same locus, the study also identified SNPs that, in a smaller cohort of 38 U.S. patients, correlated with clinical response to anakinra, suggesting their potential as genetic biomarkers of treatment response. However, this observation was limited to a single cohort and lacked external validation. Although the association was subsequently confirmed in an Italian cohort [83], independent studies in German [84] and Dutch [85] populations failed to replicate it, suggesting this association may not apply across any ancestries. By contrast, the genetic architecture of AOSD remains less clearly defined. The only GWAS for AOSD was relatively small and performed in a single ancestry cohort of 264 Chinese AOSD patients and 2420 controls [79]. This identified genome-wide significant associations in both HLA class II and HLA class I (Table 2), with a suggestive signal also detected at VEGFC. Interestingly, the product of VEGF was previously described to correlate to disease activity in AOSD patients [86].

Subsequently, using the same Chinese cohort, an association study specifically focused on the MHC region demonstrated that the most significant signal mapped to HLA class II, with the strongest effects arising from HLA-DRB1 and HLA-DQA1 [80]. The top SNP (rs1136759, p = 2.52 × 10⁻²²) was a missense variant of HLA-DRB1. Furthermore, three MHC class II alleles were identified to have GWS associations: HLA-DRB1*15:01, HLA-DQB1*06:02, and HLA-DQA1*01:02 (Table 3). Similar findings were observed for HLA-DRB1*15:01 in three independent candidate-gene studies (two Japanese, n = 30 and n = 96 cases [87, 88]; one Korean, n = 47 cases [89], although their limited sample sizes likely precluded statistically significant associations after multiple-testing correction. Notably, these alleles differ from those identified in sJIA, indicating that the genetic background of AOSD is at least partially distinct. Even more interestingly, the only allele described in both conditions, HLA-DRB1*15:01, shows diverse clinical correlations: it confers susceptibility to AOSD, whereas in sJIA it has been linked instead to an increased risk of developing lung disease, rather than to disease onset itself (see below). Beyond this Chinese GWAS, numerous small studies reported associations between AOSD and variants in HLA genes. A comprehensive overview of all the HLA alleles which were identified to be associated with AOSD is reported in Supplementary Table 3, Additional File 1. However, each of these investigations was conducted in relatively small cohorts and generally lacked external or functional validation. Outside the HLA region, additional genes have been implicated as risk factors in AOSD, including polymorphisms in IL-18 and other immune-related genes, as well as rare variants in genes responsible for monogenic Auto-Inflammatory Diseases, mAIDs (respectively in Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Table 5, Additional File 1). Although most of these observations arise from small cohorts and lack external replication or functional validation, they underscore the possibility that variants in innate immune sensors and pro-inflammatory pathways contribute to the SD spectrum. Interestingly, some of these findings parallel those observed in sJIA. For example, although the specific MIF variants identified differ between sJIA [90, 91] and AOSD [92], they converge functionally, as several studies showed that these alleles correlate with increased MIF levels, which were reported to be elevated in SD patients [90, 92]. Polymorphisms in MVK and TNFRSF1A have also been described in both sJIA [93] and AOSD [94–97], although they differ in nature and their functional significance remains uncertain.

Beside these common variants, there may exist rare, monogenic conditions that fit within the SD spectrum. The best described example of this is the biallelic loss-of-function mutation in Laccase domain-containing 1 (LACC1). Thirteen children from five consanguineous Saudi families were described with a recessively inherited disorder fulfilling ILAR classification criteria for SJIA, which segregated with homozygous LACC1 p.C284R [98]. This gene has also been implicated in inflammatory bowel disease with severe arthropathy and in non-systemic polyarticular JIA. LACC1 is highly expressed in inflammatory macrophages, where it regulates metabolism and immune function. Its loss promotes excessive inflammatory signaling, linking metabolic dysregulation to sJIA pathophysiology [82, 99]. Despite the strong phenotypic overlap, they presented an atypical disease course for SD, marked by severe polyarthritis, unresponsive to glucocorticoids, methotrexate, TNF inhibitors, or tocilizumab (no data available on IL-1 blockade) and frequent auto-antibody (such as anti-nuclear antibody, ANA) positivity. Notably, none developed MAS. Additionally, cytokine profiling was unavailable, leaving uncertain whether these patients exhibited the elevated IL-18 levels, hallmark of SD.

Beyond genetic determinants of SD susceptibility, several studies have investigated variants linked to its severe complications, particularly MAS. Schulert and Cron [100] summarized variants in genes involved in primary Hemophagocytic Lympho-Histiocytosis (HLH) PRF1, UNC13D, LYST, STXBP2, while the INCHARGE GWAS [101] showed enrichment of rare STXBP2 and UNC13D variants in sJIA, with UNC13D specifically associated with MAS. Together, these findings suggest that heterozygous HLH variants may increase MAS susceptibility and contribute to overall SD risk. In contrast, the genetic underpinnings of SD-associated lung disease (SD-LD) remain the subject of intense discussion. Several reports have linked HLA-DRB1*15 haplotypes to both SD-LD and hypersensitivity-like reactions (odds ratios > 50) [82, 102]. Intriguingly, this same haplotype has also been associated with AOSD susceptibility, as described above. Notably, carriage of this allele alone does not inevitably lead to SD-LD, implying that additional genetic, environmental, or disease-related factors modulate risk. Although still exploratory, these findings highlight emerging directions for future studies aimed at disentangling the genetic contribution to SD-related complications.

Beyond the genetic features identified in SD, monogenic autoinflammatory and immune dysregulation disorders that phenotypically overlap with SD, MAS, and LD have provided important clues to the mechanisms driving hyperinflammation in these conditions (see Supplementary Table 6, Additional File 1). Moreover, the presence of rare undefined variants in genes associated with periodic fever syndromes in a subset of patients with AOSD or sJIA further supports a shared genetic and pathogenic continuum between SD and monogenic inflammatory disorders. Notably, the degree of overlap between polygenic and monogenic forms can be so substantial that some cases initially classified as SD were later redefined as mAIDs (see Supplementary Table 7, Additional File 1).

To conclude, while no overlapping SNPs have emerged across the pediatric and adult forms, both show peak associations within HLA class I and II regions, underscoring the convergent role of adaptive immunity across ages. Interestingly, HLA-DRB1*15:01 is linked to SD-lung disease in children but only to SD itself in adults, a discrepancy that warrants further study. Additional well-powered and cross-ancestry studies, particularly in adult SD, are needed to clarify the genetic basis of the condition.

Discussion and conclusion remarks

A robust body of evidence has consolidated the view that sJIA and AOSD should be regarded as a single disease entity manifesting at different life stages, which is best termed “Still’s Disease”. This conclusion stems from converging data on clinical features, treatment responses, immunopathogenesis, gene-expression profiles, and complications [1, 7, 10, 12–16]. Most pathogenic mechanisms described to date overlap substantially between pediatric and adult forms, reinforcing the concept of a shared autoinflammatory core early in the disease, with a transition to a more chronic phase with auto-immune features in a subset of patients. This seems to occur across the age spectrum.

However, when focusing on circulating biomarkers such as IL-18 and S100 proteins, some quantitative differences emerged between reported values in children and adults. Given the heterogeneity in study design, inclusion criteria, disease activity, and variability in assays at sampling, it remains difficult to determine whether these discrepancies reflect biological differences or methodological variation. Interestingly, our multicenter data on IL-18 levels across the ages revealed a modest age-related decline in IL-18 levels, yet the cohorts analyzed were heterogeneous and did not take disease activity into account. Coordinated efforts to minimize bias and harmonize study protocols will be essential to clarify the biological significance of these findings.

Nevertheless, IL-18 remains a biologically pivotal cytokine and one of the most informative biomarkers of SD diagnosis and activity across ages. In both pediatric and adult cohorts, IL-18 concentrations frequently exceed those observed in other inflammatory or infectious conditions [49–53, 56, 59, 60, 65], supporting its role in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with fever of unknown origin and hyperferritinemia. Elevated IL-18 levels help differentiate SD from sepsis, secondary HLH, malignancy-associated hyperinflammatory states, other systemic autoinflammatory diseases, and, in children, from Kawasaki disease and other febrile syndromes [50–54, 56, 59–61, 65, 103]. In addition, IL-18 correlates with disease activity and may precede the development of MAS, particularly when interpreted alongside ferritin, IL-6, or IFN-γ [33, 34, 58, 63, 64]. Importantly, IL-18 should not be viewed as a standalone diagnostic criterion, but rather as a biomarker whose greatest clinical value lies in its integration within a multimarker framework. Its measurement is still challenged by technical and practical constraints: assays are not uniformly available across centers, and reported concentrations can vary substantially depending on the platform employed. These limitations currently hamper its clinical utility and highlight the urgent need for assay harmonization and standardization if IL-18 is to be reliably implemented in clinical practice.

At the genetic level, although no non-HLA SNPs have been consistently shared between pediatric and adult cohorts, both show peak associations within the HLA class I and II regions, underscoring a convergent role of adaptive immunity across ages. The lack of overlapping SNPs may, at least in part, reflect differences in cohort composition across studies, including distinct ethnic backgrounds and underlying HLA allele frequencies. Of particular interest, HLA-DRB1*15:01, strongly linked to SD-LD in children, has also been reported in adults but as a risk allele for SD itself instead, a discrepancy that merits further investigation. Enrichment of rare variants in genes related to immune regulation has been observed in both groups; while not individually pathogenic, such variants may shape disease susceptibility or specific complications within a polygenic framework. Moreover, variants in genes implicated in familial HLH are enriched in SD, suggesting that these alterations may not only predispose to MAS but also participate in the disease process itself.

Taken together, current literature depicts SD as a complex, age-spanning condition in which shared molecular foundations coexist with quantitative or context-dependent differences. Such genetic heterogeneity, interacting with environmental exposures and age-related immune maturation, likely contributes to the variable clinical expression of SD across the lifespan.
