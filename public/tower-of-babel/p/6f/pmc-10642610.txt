The nature and extent of outdoor alcohol marketing in Wellington, New Zealand: a longitudinal audit and spatio-temporal analysis of outdoor alcohol marketing
Authors: Tim Chambers, Timothy Royds, Amanda C Jones
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimexposure to alcohol marketing is associated with increased consumption. We aimed to (i) measure the nature and extent of outdoor alcohol marketing within a high-density urban neighbourhood and (ii) examine temporal and spatial trends in alcohol marketing.Methodsthis study used a longitudinal design to monitor paid advertising in public spaces over two 10-week periods in Wellington, New Zealand (Nov-Jan 2020–2021, Nov-Jan 2021–2022). The data were collected on-foot following an established route once a week using a phone camera, which also recorded gps data of ad locations. Temporal and spatial trends in alcohol ad prevalence were assessed.Resultsover the study period, 13% (n = 1619) of all ads (n = 12,472) were for alcohol. Alcohol ads were predominately for spirits (29%), ready-to-drink (27%) and beer (23%). Almost half of all alcohol ads (49%) did not contain a responsible consumption message, while those with a message were de-emphasized relative to promotional features. A temporal trend was observed in 2020, whereby alcohol marketing decreased over the summer, but this trend was not reflected in 2021. Alcohol ads were more likely than non-alcohol ads to be placed in premium positions on roads of high pedestrian and motor vehicle traffic.Conclusionalcohol marketing is common in urban centres. Local and central government policy could substantially reduce the levels of alcohol marketing exposure via outdoor marketing.

Background

Alcohol is a drug and a causal factor in more than 200 disease and injury conditions (World Health Organization 2019a). The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates the harmful use of alcohol to be responsible for 5.1% of the global burden of disease, and states it is the leading risk factor for premature mortality and disability among those aged 15–49 years (World Health Organization 2019a). Accordingly, reducing alcohol consumption remains a worldwide public health priority.

Alcohol marketing is considered a causal risk factor for alcohol consumption (Sargent and Babor 2020). According to WHO, enforcing restrictions on alcohol marketing is one of the five most cost-effective interventions to reduce alcohol-related harm (World Health Organization 2019b). In New Zealand (NZ), multiple Government-initiated reports have concluded legislative restrictions on alcohol marketing are required to protect public health (New Zealand Law Commission 2010, Ministry of Health 2014, Government Inquiry into Mental Health and Addiction 2018). Despite these recommendations, alcohol marketing regulation currently relies on advertising industry self-regulation through the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) codes (Advertising Standards Authority 2021). The ASA codes are under constant criticism for failing to protect vulnerable populations (Jackson et al. 2021), which is consistent with extensive international evidence demonstrating the flaws in self-regulatory approaches (Noel et al. 2017). One exposure assessment estimated children in Wellington, NZ are exposed to over 12 alcohol advertisements per day across all media and settings (Chambers et al. 2018, Chambers et al. 2019), which was disproportionately borne by Māori children (the indigenous population in NZ).

To maintain and expand their markets, alcohol companies must encourage drinkers to switch brands, increase their consumption or persuade abstainers to begin drinking, through promotion of their products—which makes children and young people ideal targets for alcohol marketing. In fact, 50% of those with alcohol use disorders have begun drinking before age 20 (Rapsey et al. 2019), while the alcohol industry is reliant on heavy drinking sessions and heavy drinkers for the majority of its profits (Casswell et al. 2016). In 2010, it was estimated the alcohol industry spent over $70 million annually on marketing in NZ (New Zealand Law Commission 2010).

A number of systematic reviews have demonstrated that exposure to alcohol marketing is associated with early initiation of alcohol use, increased level of consumption among current drinkers and engagement in binge and hazardous drinking patterns among adolescents (Anderson et al. 2009, Jernigan et al. 2017, Finan et al. 2020). Young people and children are easily influenced by marketing messages: they lack the cognitive capacities required to differentiate between the reality portrayed in advertisements from real-life experiences (Meier 2011, Gevorgyan and Manucharova 2012) and are preoccupied with their personal image in a way that makes them highly likely to identify with and model what they see in their everyday environments (Giles and Maltby 2004, Jernigan et al. 2017).

The marketing of alcoholic beverages has become ubiquitous in urban centres throughout the world. Outdoor alcohol advertising is of particular concern due to the fact billboards, street posters and advertisements in bus shelters cannot be age-gated and their presence normalizes alcohol as part of everyday life. Among heavy-drinkers aged 16–19 in Australia, 82% recall seeing alcohol advertising on outdoor billboards and posters within the previous year (Aiken et al. 2018). Likewise, in NZ, children see an estimated 1.8 alcohol ads per day via outdoor marketing (15% of all alcohol marketing exposures) (Chambers et al. 2018). Unlike other forms of media such as internet, television or radio, it is almost impossible to avoid, as it cannot simply be ‘switched off’ by consumers nor can it be adequately targeted to adult populations. Children’s repeated exposure to alcohol promotions may endorse the formation of positive attitudes towards alcohol even if they cannot consciously recall the advertisement (Unger et al. 2003).

Studies in San Francisco, Sydney, Los Angeles and Chicago have demonstrated that between 7% and 22% of all outdoor marketing is for alcohol (Altman et al. 1991, Hackbarth et al. 2001, Kelly et al. 2008, Scott et al. 2008). Other studies focusing exclusively on public transport infrastructure have found alcohol advertising comprises between 2% and 12% of all advertising in these locations (Lucan et al. 2017, Parnell et al. 2019, Robertson et al. 2022). A longitudinal study has also demonstrated that exposure to outdoor alcohol marketing at ages 11–12 is associated with intentions to use alcohol at ages 13–14 (Pasch et al. 2007). To our knowledge, there is no other research internationally or within NZ, which focuses on all outdoor alcohol advertising in urban centres, especially longitudinal studies with serial data collection capturing changes to the advertisements over time. The aim of the present study is to describe the nature and extent of marketing via street posters, billboards and bus-shelters within paid advertising spaces throughout a foot traffic-heavy Wellington City central business district (CBD) route over two ten-week periods in the summers of 2020–2021 and 2021–2022. Specifically, it aims to quantify the prevalence and spatio-temporal trends of alcohol ads compared to non-alcohol ads.

Methods

Scope

This study used a longitudinal design to monitor paid marketing weekly in public spaces over two 10-weekly periods within a high-density urban centre in Wellington, NZ (Nov-Jan 2020–2021, Nov-Jan 2021–2022). The time period selected was influenced by the availability of research staff over the summer break. Wellington is the capital city of NZ with a 2018 resident population of approximately 200 000 people and another 300 000 in the wider surrounding region (Statistics New Zealand 2018). The geographic scope was concentrated on the inner-city suburb of Te Aro, which encompasses a portion of the central business district (CBD), a large proportion of the city’s entertainment district and contains primarily multi-unit dwellings (see Fig. 1). The selected study area accounts for ~50% of all pedestrian foot traffic into the city and includes one of Wellington’s busiest pedestrian streets (Cuba Street) (Greater Wellington Regional Council 2017, 2019).

Study area—Wellington, New Zealand.

Paid street advertising was defined as including the following modes: street posters (sizes larger than A4), street furniture (both static and dynamic forms), bus stops (both static and dynamic forms) and billboards (both static and dynamic forms). Static forms of marketing were typically a paper-based poster, while dynamic forms of marketing were usually a screen. Following the initial inventory, poster columns and street posters of an A4 size or smaller were excluded since the condition of these advertisements suggested that they were not regularly replaced. Also excluded was all alcohol advertising that was located on licenced premises (e.g. posters outside of a supermarket that advertise products sold within the store, or a sign outside a bar advertising their price of drinks). On-site marketing was deemed out of scope as it would have substantially increased data collection burden.

Data collection and coding

Data collection was completed by a single researcher over two ten-week periods in November–January 2020/2021 and 2021–2022 (summer in NZ). The data were collected on-foot following an established route once a week on a weekday. The default camera app on the researcher’s phone (OnePlus5T) was used for photographing each advertisement that was visible during a data collection cycle. The resulting photos also retained GPS data on the precise location of the advertisement sites. An ‘ad site’ was classified as an advertisement or a group of advertisements at the same geographical location using the same mode of advertisement (for example, six posters side-by-side would be one site). Each site was assigned a number between 1 and 194 (total of 194 sites). Within a site, each different advertisement (or potential space for advertisement) was assigned a letter, in the order that the advertisements were observed defined as ‘ad unit’. Each ad unit was coded using the following variables: date, ad site, ad unit, a description of the content of the advertisement (often using the ad’s slogan), alcohol category, ad type (see Supplementary Table 1 for coding schedule). Non-alcohol ads were further categorized (see Supplementary Table 2 for categorizations). Advertisements that pertained to alcohol were further examined for the presence/absence of responsibility messaging. There is no direct requirement to include social responsibility messages in alcohol advertisements, but these typically fall within the ASA codes under Principle 1 that states advertising must include a high standard of social responsibility to consumers and society. We also made observations on the quality of these messages through field observations and a subsample of 20 messages to determine the prominence of the messaging.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in Stata v17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Analyses were conducted by ad sites, ad units and total observations. We first examined characteristics related to the marketing medium (e.g. billboard) by ad sites, locations and observations, by alcohol and non-alcohol ads using Chi2 tests. We tested differences between 2021 and 2022 in the proportion of alcohol ads of total ads, alcohol product types as a proportion of all alcohol ads and difference responsibility messages as a proportion of all alcohol ads using Chi2 tests. We tested for temporal trends in the proportion of alcohol advertisements per week over the study period using linear regression. We used ArcGIS Pro version 2.7.1 optimized hot spot analysis tool to visually examine clustering by ad type (alcohol vs non-alcohol) across the study area. The optimized hot spot analysis method determines whether there are hot spots of ad units promoting alcohol surrounded by other ad units promoting alcohol or vice versa.

Results

Data were collected for 13 753 observations, from 738 ad units from 194 ad sites over the 20-week observation period. Exclusions led to 1281 observations being removed, leaving 12 472 observations from 738 ad units and 194 ad sites in the analytic dataset (see Fig. 2). Ad sites had a median of two ad units per site (interquartile range 1–6). Eleven ad sites were available in only one of the two observation years (e.g. either 2020–2021 or 2021–2020, but not both), which was the main cause for missing longitudinal data.

Ad types

Table 1 outlines the prevalence of non-alcohol and alcohol ads by ad type. In total, alcohol ads contributed 13% of all outdoor marketing over the study period. Alcohol ads were more likely to be marketed through street posters than non-alcohol ads (P = <0.001, Cramer’s V 0.16). In contrast, non-alcohol ads were more likely to be marketed through bus shelters and street furniture than alcohol ads. While most ad types detected a statistically significant difference between alcohol and non-alcohol ads, Cramer’s V was low indicating a weak association.

Alcohol products

Spirits were the most heavily marketed alcohol type over the study period contributing 29.2% of all alcohol ads, closely followed by (Table 2) Ready-to-drink (RTD) (27.1%). Beer ranked third overall with 22.9% of all alcohol ads, while other alcohol products contributed 15% and wine was the least marketed product with 6%. The other product category included marketing for multiple products (e.g. an ad of Smirnoff RTD also contained an image of the spirits product) and cider. The only statistically significant changes in alcohol product marketing between 2020 and 2021 were from a decrease in RTD marketing (30%–24%, P 0.018, Cramer’s V 0.06) and an increase in the other category (11%–18%, P < 0.001, Cramer’s V 0.10). Given RTDs are more likely to be marketed with other products (e.g. Smirnoff RTDs and spirits), it is likely these trends reflect a change to multi-product marketing rather than a decrease in RTD marketing.

Responsibility message

Just over half (51%) of alcohol ads had an associated responsibility message (Table 2). The most common message was a link to the alcohol industry corporate responsibility organization Cheers (on 39.5% of all alcohol ads). Other messages included to drink or enjoy responsibly (21.3%). A subsample of 20 ads showed that responsibility messages were often visibly de-emphasized in advertisements (see Supplementary Figure 1). Overall, there was an increase in the proportion of alcohol ads that displayed any responsibility message from 45% to 57% (P < 0.011, Cramer’s V 0.13). The change was largely attributable to a higher proportion of ads containing reference to the corporate responsibility organization Cheers (26%–53%, P < 0.001, Cramer’s V 0.27). All other messages saw statistically significant decreases from 2020 to 2021, with ‘please don’t drink and drive’ and ‘18+’ completely absent in 2021.

Temporal trends in alcohol ads

Figure 3 displays the temporal trends in alcohol marketing as a proportion of all outdoor alcohol marketing across the summers of 2020 (red) and 2021 (blue). Alcohol ads were present in each week during the observation period, while the overall prevalence did not drop below 8% in any given week. In 2020, the proportion of alcohol adverts was negatively associated with week (Coefficient − 0.012, 95%CI −0.018, −0.007, standard error 0.002, P < 0.001), thus suggesting the proportion of adverts declined over the observation period. In contrast, In 2021, there was no association between week and proportion of alcohol adverts (Coefficient 0.003, 95%CI −0.002, 0.008, standard error 0.002, P 0.192). Alcohol ads contributed a slightly higher proportion of all ads in 2021 (14%) than in 2020 (12%) (P 0.023, Cramer’s V 0.02).

Discussion

This paper aimed to assess the nature and extent of outdoor alcohol marketing in the Wellington CBD. We found alcohol marketing contributed around 13% of all outdoor marketing and was predominately marketed through street posters. Alcohol ads were also more likely than non-alcohol ads to be positioned in ‘premium’ marketing sites such as those with heavy pedestrian and motor vehicle traffic. Almost half of all alcohol ads omitted any responsibility messaging, while responsibility messages that were present contained weak messaging and were visually de-emphasized.

The prevalence of outdoor alcohol ads in our study was 13%, which is consistent with levels observed in San Francisco (17%), Los Angeles (8%) and Southern Louisiana (5%) (Altman et al. 1991, Scott et al. 2008). Consistent with one previous study (Scott et al. 2008), alcohol ads in our study were over-represented in billboards and large street posters compared to non-alcohol ads, while being under-represented at bus shelters and street furniture. Alcohol companies typically possess large marketing budgets, which may enable them to occupy premium marketing media (e.g. billboards) (New Zealand Law Commission 2010). Likewise, while alcohol ads were a minority of the total ad volume (13%), they were more likely to be placed in premium locations than non-alcohol ads. For example, Vivian Street facilitates ~1200 vehicles per hour, compared to north Kent Terrace (~650 per hour) (Greater Wellington Regional Council 2017). Likewise, Cuba Street has the third highest pedestrian volumes of any street in the study area (~1200 per hour) (Greater Wellington Regional Council 2017). Interesting, one premium spot at the corner of Taranaki street was not occupied by alcohol marketing, which could be attributed to the ASA codes that prohibit alcohol marketing within a 300-metre sightline of the main entrance of schools (Advertising Standards Authority 2021).

Our results provide the first audit of outdoor alcohol marketing in NZ and add to the relatively limited number of observational studies (Altman et al. 1991, Scott et al. 2008). Furthermore, the results provide further evidence of alcohol’s presence in urban environments, which is corroborated by other NZ evidences that suggest children see 1.8 alcohol ads per day in outdoor environments (Chambers et al. 2018) and Australia where 82% of drinkers aged 16–19 in Australia recalled seeing outdoor alcohol advertising within the previous year (Aiken et al. 2018). Children’s exposure to alcohol marketing is particularly problematic as experimental (Zerhouni et al. 2016, Zerhouni et al. 2019) and epidemiological evidence (Anderson et al. 2009, Jernigan et al. 2017) suggests alcohol marketing leads to early onset drinking and more hazardous drinking.

Alcohol marketing was persistent throughout the 10-week period in both observation years. It is unclear why we observed a linear decrease over time in the proportion of alcohol advertisements in 2020, while during the same period in 2021 advertising remained constant. Overall, alcohol ads contributed a higher proportion of all ads prior to the Christmas break. It is unclear why alcohol ads were less prevalent during the Christmas break but one explanation could be that alcohol consumption around Christmas is at its highest (Foster et al. 2015, Knudsen and Skogen 2015); thus, there is a diminishing rate of return for each marketing dollar spent during the holiday period (Gallet 2007).

Our analysis found that 49% of all alcohol ads did not have an associated responsibility message. While not a specific requirement of the ASA codes, failing to apply any message appears to undermine Principle 1 Social Responsibility of Marketers. When responsibility messages were included in ads, they were often strategically vague, had an underlying pro-drinking theme (‘enjoy/drink responsibly’ ‘Cheers’) and were visually de-emphasized (see Supplementary Figure 1). While there was an increase in the proportion of alcohol ads with any responsibility message from 2020 to 2021, this increase was entirely attributable to the Cheers.co.nz being present on more ads. The increase in “Cheers” responsibility messages came at the expense of those messages about moderate drinking or drink drinking. Responsibility messages in this study were consistent with multiple reviews that conclude industry-developed responsibility messages are ambiguous and de-emphasized (Barry and Goodson 2010, Knai et al. 2015).

Strengths and limitations

One strength of this study was the longitudinal design of data collection over two 10-week periods over two years. While we did not observe clear temporal trends across years, the findings highlight the persistent presence of alcohol ads throughout the summer. Spatial data on ad sites also enabled analysis of spatial variation in alcohol ads over the study period, in particular, the identification of premium locations.

The study also had a number of limitations related to the inclusion/exclusion criteria. First, we excluded all alcohol ads that were less than A4 in size, which led to the exclusion of a large amount of advertising. Experimental studies have shown that when small-format ads are displayed multiple times, they can take up almost as much space as billboards but at an eye-level location for pedestrians and generate equivalent impact as traditional print media. Second, we excluded all marketing associated with licenced alcohol outlet shopfronts (Higgins et al. 2014). One study in NZ found children had three-times higher rates of exposure to alcohol marketing via shopfronts of licenced outlets than other outdoor marketing (e.g. Billboards) (Chambers et al. 2018). Third, data collection was only conducted by one researcher. Lastly, we only collected data during the summer months over two years, so the results may not be generalizable to the entire year. However, it is unclear what impact seasonality has on alcohol marketing expenditure, with one study of data from 1997, suggesting alcohol tend to increase around summer and the holiday period (Snyder et al. 2000). For example, while demand/alcohol sales increase during the summer months, this may be a natural increase in consumption with warmer weather and holidays rather than an associated increase in alcohol marketing expenditure (Foster et al. 2015, Knudsen and Skogen 2015).

Policy options

The prevalence of outdoor alcohol marketing strengthens recommendations for additional legislative restriction on alcohol marketing from multiple NZ Government-initiated reports (New Zealand Law Commission 2010, Ministry of Health 2014, Government Inquiry into Mental Health and Addiction 2018) and the World Health Organization. At a national-level, comprehensive legislative alcohol marketing restrictions could capture outdoor alcohol marketing as it does in countries like Lithuania, Finland and France (Arthur 2018, Gallopel-Morvan et al. 2017; Marjatta 2017). Local governments can also impact the levels of outdoor alcohol marketing through bylaws. For example, the São Paulo City Council (Brazil) removed over 15 000 billboards and 300 000 over-sized shop front signs in a single year (Mahdawi 2015). Likewise, Grenoble (France) became the first European city to ban outdoor marketing, replacing 326 billboards with trees and areas for public expression (Hopkins 2014). Many local governments internationally have also banned alcohol marketing on public transport facilities (Davey 2016, New York Times 2017, Tupou 2018). Based on our results, bans on public transport infrastructure would have a smaller impact on total alcohol ads compared to other outdoor marketing in Wellington. Although, our current study did not include marketing associated with public transport vehicles (e.g. buses), which has been a major source of outdoor alcohol marketing in other jurisdictions (Lucan et al. 2017, Parnell et al. 2019, Robertson et al. 2022).

Conclusion

Previous research has established exposure to alcohol marketing as a causal factor in alcohol consumption, with young people and children particularly vulnerable to effects of alcohol marketing. Our results build on this existing literature by showing outdoor alcohol marketing is prevalent, is relatively constant over time and is clustered in areas of high pedestrian and vehicle traffic. The supporting literature and results from this current study show that legislative restrictions on outdoor alcohol marketing would lead to a substantial reduction of alcohol advertising in dense urban areas of NZ.
