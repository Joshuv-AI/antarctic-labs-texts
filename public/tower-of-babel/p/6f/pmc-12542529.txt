What is the experience of healthcare professionals managing Achilles tendon ruptures in the National Health Service (NHS)?—a qualitative interview study in the United Kingdom
Authors: Samuel Briggs-Price, Thomas Yates, Jitendra Mangwani, Maneesh Bhatia, Seth O’Neill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo explore National Health Service (NHS) healthcare professionals’ experiences of delivering rehabilitation for patients with Achilles tendon rupture (ATR).DesignQualitative study using semistructured interviews and reflexive thematic analysis.SettingNHS hospital sites in the United Kingdom, providing non-surgical ATR management using the Leicester Achilles Management Protocol (LAMP).ParticipantsTwenty-one NHS healthcare professionals, including physiotherapists and orthopaedic consultants with a mean of 10.8 years of clinical experience, purposively sampled based on profession and experience managing ATR.ResultsThree main themes were identified: (1) clinical training on Achilles tendon ruptures, (2) rehabilitation following ATR and (3) the NHS service delivering rehabilitation. Participants reported limited training on ATR, leading to reduced confidence and slower rehabilitation progressions. Fear of re-injury influenced clinical decision-making, while discharge decisions were goal-oriented but constrained by service limitations.ConclusionsThis study highlights limited clinical training, inconsistent rehabilitation practices and NHS service constraints that collectively slow rehabilitation progress and affect return to sport following ATR. Enhanced training, service development and greater standardisation of ATR rehabilitation pathways are recommended to improve patient outcomes.Keywords: Musculoskeletal disorders, Foot & ankle, Health Services

Introduction

Achilles tendon rupture (ATR) is the most common lower limb tendon rupture with an incidence of 5–13 per 10 000 in the United Kingdom (UK).1,3 ATR incidence is increasing, resulting in a significant economic burden.4 5 In the UK, ATR is primarily managed non-surgically as it offers a cost-effective alternative to surgical approaches.6 7

Non-surgical management protocols are varied and despite short-term differences between immobilisation strategies, different approaches achieve similar outcomes post-ATR.6 Following the initial bracing or cast protocol, approximately 82% of individuals are referred to physiotherapy for guided rehabilitation.8 With no standardised rehabilitation approach, clinical practices vary, likely impacting clinical outcomes. Rehabilitation delivery may be significantly influenced by healthcare professional experience and beliefs around rehabilitation alongside the healthcare setting and infrastructure. Previous investigations have surveyed or interviewed orthopaedic consultants to improve understanding of initial management strategies.8,11 These studies do not include the important contribution of other healthcare professionals. This is particularly important in the UK where many National Health Service (NHS) ATR clinics are led by physiotherapists and most patients’ rehabilitation is managed in physiotherapy outpatient departments.12 13

Healthcare professionals have been engaged in qualitative investigations to evaluate patient decision aids.14 However, there have been no qualitative investigations into rehabilitation practices following ATR in the NHS. Therefore, there is a lack of understanding of the multidisciplinary approach to delivering rehabilitation. Healthcare professionals clinical decision making regarding the start, progression, content and discharge during rehabilitation is unknown. Understanding rehabilitation practices may provide an insight into the long-term deficits post-ATR.6 Understanding the lived experience of healthcare professionals’ management of patients would inform future strategies for improving and standardising patient care. This study aimed to understand the experience of healthcare professionals managing ATR rehabilitation in the NHS.

Methods

In person, one-on-one semistructured interviews were conducted with healthcare professionals currently practising in the NHS. Individuals were recruited from hospital sites in an NHS trust routinely using a single management approach following ATR.12 Interviews were completed between July and October 2023. Interviews were directed by a topic guide developed by the authorship team and stakeholders who had experienced ATR. Interviews were recorded using a digital voice recorder and transcribed verbatim. The topic guide is available in online supplemental material. All interviews were completed by the lead author (SB-P) only, a male physiotherapist completing interviews as part of a PhD studentship. The lead author acknowledges their positive preconceptions about exercise-based rehabilitation, and their experience within the NHS has shaped an awareness of resource constraints and limited access to rehabilitation. The lead author has completed interview data collection and analysis previously. He had not previously worked as a physiotherapist at the recruitment sites. We adhered to the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) guideline for reporting of qualitative research.15

Participants

Purposive sampling was used to ensure a representative distribution of professions, seniority levels and years of clinical experience. Participants were invited to schedule an interview by the lead author (SB-P) at departmental training events. Interviews were completed in a suitable environment: a quiet room at the participants' hospital site. Participants were informed of the study aims prior to completing the interview and were aware of the interviewer’s profession. The recruitment target of 20 participants was informed by previous qualitative studies investigating ATR.14 16 17 The sample size allowed recruitment to represent the range of professions supporting the management and rehabilitation of individuals following ATR. Healthcare professionals were eligible if they had specialised in musculoskeletal practice or were practising clinically in a musculoskeletal department at the time of interview. The participants did not read the transcripts at any time and did not provide feedback on the results.

Data analysis

Transcriptions were analysed using NVivo software V.12, (QSR International, Doncaster, Australia). Reflexive thematic analysis described by Braun and Clarke18 was used for analysis. The six stages of thematic analysis were used as an analysis framework; familiarising yourself with the data, generating initial codes, generating themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes and producing the report.18 A reflexive diary was completed by the lead author (SB-P).

A prospective interim analysis was completed following the recruitment of 15 participants to assess for data saturation. Transcripts were assessed for data saturation by two authors (SB-P, SO). If data saturation was achieved prior to 20 interviews, recruitment would be stopped. If data saturation was not achieved in the initial 20 interviews, recruitment would continue to a pragmatic limitation of 30 participants.

Authors (SB-P, SO) independently generated codes from the initial three interview transcripts. These codes were compared for accuracy and consistency and a coding agreement was set for the remaining interview transcripts. Coding was then completed for the remaining interviews and codes were grouped to develop themes by the lead author (SB-P). The development of themes and relationship between themes were reviewed by all authors.

The participants were grouped into profession and seniority. Years of clinical experience are reported as means with SD for each group.

Results

A total of 21 participants completed interviews. All participants who volunteered successfully completed the interview. An additional interview with an orthopaedic consultant was completed to improve the depth of data in that profession. The mean interview duration was 43 min. Participants’ years of clinical experience were rounded to the nearest year and ranged from 1 to 30 years with a mean of 10.8 years. Theme 1—linical training on Achilles tendon ruptures

Participants reported that they have received little to no training on the pathology and rehabilitation of ATR. The lack of training was driven by a lack of clinical exposure to patients who had experienced ATR. Therefore, training was seen as unjustifiable in comparison to conditions seen more frequently by the service. The absence of training resulted in a lack of awareness of current best practice and impacted the confidence in managing ATR in the clinical setting. Consequently, healthcare professionals reported an overly cautious and more conservative rehabilitation approach due to fear of re-rupturing the Achilles during rehabilitation.

Participant 6—I don't think you could justify giving me loads of training on it because I don't see that many.

Participant 10—because I've not seen any I can get away with not knowing, but if someone came in without spending a little bit of time preparing, I probably would feel out my depth.

Participant 2—I personally have a relatively poor understanding of contemporary research surrounding Achilles ruptures… I think we're quite conservative with it, because I'm trying to avoid re rupture.

When reflecting on their knowledge of ATR management, participants felt they would value training on the topic. Participants identified that training would improve clinical competence and enhance consistency in ATR management across the service. It was reported by junior physiotherapists that it was realistic to deliver training in the current NHS service, and available time was not a barrier to delivering training.

However, this insight was not shared across the participants. More senior physiotherapists recognised the barrier of service waiting lists and the requirement of repeated training for junior physiotherapists who move across musculoskeletal services. Senior physiotherapists also recognised that the insufficient knowledge in managing ATR may lead to lower clinical standards which are identified when patient management is shared across the service.

Participant 4—I think with more training, everyone would be happier. I think we manage. But if everyone was to rotate, I'd probably say that a few questions or a few problems might start popping up.

Participant 15—Our waiting list is already really long. We haven't got the capacity to take that many people out of patient care for that period of time (for ATR training).

Theme 2—rehabilitation following Achilles tendon ruptures

Subtheme—starting rehabilitation of Achilles tendon ruptures

A prominent theme identified was the inconsistency and perceived delay in initiating rehabilitation. Delays were seen to reflect a culture in the service which influenced clinical decision making. Healthcare professionals highlighted that the first appointment timeframe for seeing individuals in the outpatient environment varied widely, with some patients entering rehabilitation as early as 8 weeks postinjury, while others were not seen until a year.

Participant 1—Typically we'd see people … probably about three months post removal of boot, but recently, we've had some people who have been in a year down the line because they haven't initially received their physio. I think sometimes people will just slip through because maybe they missed an appointment and just been discharged because of that, sometimes it’s social circumstances.

Participant 7—we are currently letting it heal and then starting the rehab, until after the 8-week mark. But I would like to move on and start the rehab whilst they're in the boot.

Physiotherapists expressed frustrations with delays to rehabilitation and the duration of immobilisation. It was stated that these delays are a consequence of historic approaches that did not prioritise early rehabilitation. This revealed a conflict between the clinical actions being taken and the belief that movement promotes recovery.

Participant 4—So I'd say it could start a lot earlier, but we need a sort of cultural change. The vibe you'll get is very conservative. Frustratingly so at some point.

Participant 15—we know that movement promotes repair, it promotes recovery, so why do we stick them in a boot for 12 weeks.

Subtheme—clinical decision making for rehabilitation progressions

Healthcare professionals displayed varying confidence in both the content and expected milestones of rehabilitation. This uncertainty was attributed to a lack of knowledge and led to more conservative exercise prescription and slower rehabilitation timeframes. Healthcare professionals doubted themselves when justifying rehabilitation decisions. Subsequently, exercise prescription was not informed by patient capability but rather the healthcare professional’s anxieties and lack of clarity regarding when and how to implement exercises.

Participant 2—I'm not hugely aware of certain milestones and what would be most beneficial. I think it'd be quite rare that I would get them doing unilateral reps. And that’s not necessarily because they can't, it’s more because I'm personally a bit fearful of doing it.

Participant 15—I'm not a huge fan of dropping over the edge of a step. I don't know why. I haven't got a reason, I've only tried it once.

Fear of re-injury was a dominant factor influencing rehabilitation decisions. Participants described being hesitant to progress rehabilitation despite their patient’s motivation to progress. Healthcare professional’s caution regarding re-rupture was reported to cause inconsistencies in the education and rehabilitation provided to patients.

Participant 13—I worry about progressing them too soon or doing too much too soon and it re-rupturing.

Participant 2—I'd say it’s actually more us holding them back because of our beliefs, and concerns, most people are really keen to press on in terms of running and return to sport, its actually us saying ‘not yet’. But again, it’s not always based on any meaningful data, it’s just based on our fear of re-rupture

Participant 5—a lot of people say to me, well, could I go swimming? or, could I do cycling? And I think, well, I don't know. I don't know. But I don't want to say yes, and then that person doesn't recover very well.

Fear of re-rupture not only shaped exercise progression but also influenced decisions around the use of outcome measures, with participants delaying objective testing due to anxieties about re-rupture. Participants reported that patient-reported outcomes or validated measures were rarely used, leaving clinical decisions dependent on subjective reports. The absence of routine objective data collection further exacerbated uncertainty about rehabilitation progression.

Participant 5—(Strength testing) I'm a bit dubious about doing that until about a year. Don’t know if I do it at six months, whether I’d be brave enough to do it then.

Participant 7—We don't use any patient reported outcome measures. So it’s all just based on what the patient says, rather than gaining any objective data.

Subtheme—discharge decision making for Achilles tendon ruptures

Discharge decision making was described as a dynamic and multifactorial process which was informed by patient goals, motivation and functional progress. Healthcare professionals consistently reported that goal-oriented discharge was the ideal aim. However, this was often mediated by clinical perspective regarding the achievability of patient goals. Discharge was often a negotiation between goals and realism with what the patient hoped to achieve and what the healthcare professionals viewed as feasible. Patient motivation was a key factor in whether rehabilitation continued towards previous activity levels. Participants highlighted how motivation could either push patients towards ambitious goals or lead to early discharge. Healthcare professionals described discharging patients early when rehabilitation improvements appeared to plateau.

Participant 3—I don't reach the goals with everybody. Some people just get stuck, like where they're just not progressing in terms of their strength and just kind of plateaued… some of those just disappear. Just go missing in action.

Discharge decisions were informed by daily function and returning to work, leading to a marginalisation of return to sport rehabilitation. Despite early patient aspirations to resume sport, goals were reported to change across the rehabilitation process. Returning to sport was also not seen as a primary focus for healthcare professionals. This may drive the change in rehabilitation priorities identified in patients. Healthcare professionals also viewed the absence of return to sport rehabilitation as a consequence of patient attendance or service coordination. Consequently, healthcare professionals lacked experience in the return to sport phase of rehabilitation.

Participant 3—I don't take them through right to the end of a return sport program. We haven't got that much interest in that. Or sometimes I find that they'll come in and I'll say, I want to play football again, but then four months later they're like no, I'm just happy running.

Participant 2—I might consider a gradual return to running slow speeds. And to be honest, realistically, that’s probably the point at which we see them until anyway, just because at that point, they tend to fall out the system. So I haven’t yet seen one before, return through sport.

Theme 3—the NHS service delivering Rehabilitation

Subtheme—facility limitations: equipment, infrastructure and access barriers

Infrastructure and equipment limitations were described as a fundamental constraint on the delivery of rehabilitation, with implications for both patient outcomes and healthcare professionals’ motivation. There was a consistent sense of inertia despite the awareness of a clinical need. Healthcare professionals expressed repeated frustration at the mismatch between patient rehabilitation needs and the capacity of the service to meet them. This was seen as limiting rehabilitation potential and as demotivating to staff. Efforts to secure essential equipment or improve rehabilitation infrastructure were met with barriers and perceived organisational apathy. These barriers were recognised across professions and seniority levels.

Participant 5—I've talked about before, contacting (the) head of orthopaedics and saying, can we have some more equipment please, because we are rehabbing your patients with hands behind our backs.

Participant 15—I've been trying to get equipment for two years, and none of its come. It’s been very difficult to get equipment. We've had money to spend on equipment … and then nothing’s turned up.

Participant 19—we were promised loads of money to build (rehabilitation facilities), we ended up with a couple of telephones in an office. So, I don't think it'll ever happen. It’s very pessimistic to say.

An area of concern was the limited capacity for strength-based rehabilitation. Healthcare professionals repeatedly identified this as a shortfall in NHS services, where traditional focus on mobility and flexibility was not matched by investment in equipment necessary for strengthening or assessing strength.

Participant 8—Strength work is where we sometimes let ourselves down in the NHS. Because we're kind of really good at mobility and flexibility, but the physios don't have the equipment and the facilities to be able to load patients.

Technological limitations also impaired service quality, particularly around outcome measurement and progressing rehabilitation. Participants expressed a strong desire to integrate objective measures into practice to guide clinical reasoning.

Participant 16—There’s no simple way of tying an outcome measure to a patient without a lot of faff… we don't collect any outcome measures … the computer system doesn't allow it.

Participant 9—Something like that (strength assessment equipment) would be a game changer in terms of getting people back. I think you'd have a greater understanding of where to progress because you've got the figures that back your clinical judgement up.

When the facilities or equipment were available for rehabilitation, the healthcare professionals did not have access to the space due to competing demands with other services. The requirement of protected space was highlighted and additional space was not seen as sufficient.

Participant 13—We've got the gym, but quite a lot of the time pulmonary rehab and cardiac rehab are in there so we can't use it. So we could do with, not more space, more time in the space.

Subtheme—service appointment availability

Healthcare professionals described service constraints that limited their ability to deliver timely rehabilitation, emphasising scheduling pressures and reduced patient contact time. Multiple healthcare professionals reported the inability to offer frequent follow-up sessions as a barrier to more effective care. The lack of availability meant that rehabilitation programmes were delayed or the continuity of care was affected as patients were transferred between practitioners. Healthcare professionals were frustrated by the quality of care they were delivering as a consequence of these limitations.

Participant 11—We just don't have enough space in our diaries to be seeing patients to be progressing them… I don't want to wait till six weeks before I see him, that’s a long gap.

Participant 3—Length of time between follow ups, that’s not great at the moment. So that’s about a month for me. Like three months ago it was seven weeks for a follow up, which was terrible.

Due to their responsibility of care, healthcare professionals worked to manage these limitations, using service development, non-clinical or personal time to maintain standards of care. The inability for the service to meet optimal care was recognised to be consistent across musculoskeletal conditions.

Participant 11—So for him, I'm seeing him in my management time, because there’s no slots. If I don't see him, then he waits (6 weeks).

Participant 15—(Waiting times) that’s not just Achilles, that’s everyone. If we saw people weekly, it'd be great, but that’s a dream. Not feasible.

Discussion

This is the first qualitative study to investigate the experiences of multidisciplinary teams managing ATR within the NHS. Previous studies have been isolated to orthopaedic consultants.8 International studies have completed comparable qualitative investigations with individuals post-ATR,16 17 although as these represent alternate healthcare systems, they lack transferability to the NHS. Additionally, previous studies have consisted of surgically managed cohorts which do not reflect the predominantly non-operative approach seen in the local NHS population.1 12 13

Clinical training on Achilles tendon ruptures

One of the themes identified was the impact of clinical training on ATR. Insufficient training was associated with a lack of confidence in the clinical management of ATR. Previous studies have identified that healthcare professionals who were insecure in their ATR knowledge caused patients to have greater hesitancy during rehabilitation.17 To guide rehabilitation progression, reduce the fear of re-injury and achieve satisfactory recovery, trust and rapport with healthcare professionals are seen as essential in this patient group.16 17 Although participants reported that they lacked the appropriate knowledge to support individuals post-ATR. Specific training was desired by healthcare professionals, although due to a low clinical exposure it was not prioritised within broader training programmes. This created a self-perpetuating cycle where ATR cases were rarely seen, therefore the condition was not considered a training priority, which in turn left healthcare professionals underprepared and hesitant when cases did arise. To increase clinical exposure, future research should explore the feasibility of a dedicated Achilles service. A specialised service may provide an opportunity for healthcare professionals to be supported and achieve appropriate levels of training.

Rehabilitation following Achilles tendon ruptures

Previous qualitative studies have focused on the process of returning to sports following ATR.16 17 This study identified that returning to sport was neither prioritised nor frequently achieved. Barriers to returning to sport included the duration of rehabilitation required, absence of healthcare professionals’ training, access to facilities and fluctuating patient motivation. The fluctuating motivation of patients may be reflective of the lack of confidence and experience in the participants at returning ATR patients to sports. Consequently, if healthcare professionals are not confident in delivering return to sport rehabilitation, patients may become demotivated and accept alternative activities ultimately not returning to sport. This may explain the reduced motivation and attendance of this patient group reported in the later stages of rehabilitation.

It is also important to consider that the healthcare professionals interviewed for this study were employed within the NHS and were therefore treating a population distinct from that seen in professional sporting contexts. NHS clinicians do not routinely encounter elite athletes but more commonly see members of the general population who are older and engaged in recreational sporting activity.1 3 This may explain why returning to sport was rarely prioritised in this study. For some clinicians, the risks, time commitment and physical demands associated with a full sporting return were perceived as disproportionate to the benefits.

The NHS service delivering rehabilitation

The service is also likely to be a significant factor in returning to sport, as there is limited appointment availability, long waiting lists, delayed referrals and a lack of access to appropriate rehabilitation facilities. These factors were seen to disincentivise rehabilitation, potentially reinforcing a service model that deprioritises return to pre-ATR activity levels. Long-term functional deficits post-ATR are reported to be associated with developing additional pain sites.17 Further research is needed to understand the long-term health implications of not returning to previous levels of activity post-ATR. The burden of limited appointment availability frequently fell on individual clinicians, who described using non-clinical or personal time to fill the care gap. While this is evidence of staff dedication to a duty of care, it also raises concerns about workforce sustainability. Lack of appointment availability was not specific to ATR but appeared as a systemic issue affecting all musculoskeletal conditions.

Limitations

This study successfully recruited participants across multiple hospital sites within an NHS trust. This NHS trust primarily uses a single established controlled mobilisation protocol post-ATR.12 Therefore, these findings may not be transferable to NHS hospitals trust which use alternate staff training programmes or post-ATR controlled immobilisation protocols. Around 26% of ATRs are managed surgically across the UK,19 and experiences of healthcare professionals managing post-surgery rehabilitation may differ. In addition, the experiences of healthcare professionals’ managing ATR rehabilitation outside the NHS are not captured in this study. However, as patient-reported outcomes are comparable across the UK,6 it is possible that the themes identified here may resonate across wider NHS services.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the experiences of multidisciplinary healthcare professionals managing ATR within the NHS. Three main themes were identified: (1) clinical training on Achilles tendon ruptures, (2) rehabilitation following Achilles tendon ruptures, (3) the NHS service delivering rehabilitation. The findings highlight significant challenges related to training and service provision. There is a need for targeted training initiatives and development of rehabilitation services in the management of ATR.
