Barium titanate-based perovskites for multifunctional applications: comprehensive computational and experimental insights into electronic, thermoelectric, phonon dispersion and optical characteristics
Authors: Muhammad Tauseef Qureshi, Ghazala Yunus, Abdul Moiz Mohammed, A. Wahab M. A. Hussein, Hayat Ullah, Surriya Bibi, Murtaza Saleem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Barium titanate is a renowned ferroelectric material with a perovskite structure. This comprehensive study explores various properties of these perovskites for widespread applications. Density functional theory calculations were performed to predict the influence of Mg on different parameters. Uniform thin films of these materials were grown in a phase-pure form for experimental investigations. Crystallographic analysis confirmed a cubic phase perovskite structure in these compositions. The morphological analysis of the pure composition demonstrated aggregated, randomly scattered grains, which were strategically altered upon Mg doping. Distinct orbital contributions of the Ba-p, O-p, Ti-d, and Mg-s states were depicted in the Mg-doped compositions. Introduction of Mg effectively tuned the thermoelectric response of the BaTiO3 matrix, modifying the figure of merit and Seebeck coefficient. The composition doped with the highest maximum Mg content presented a refractive index and epsilon value of ≈3.35 and ≈8.5, respectively, in the experimental evaluation of optical parameters. The optical bandgap of the BaTiO3 lattice markedly reduced from 2.3 eV to 1.71 eV after Mg doping; these enhanced parameters make it a promising material for future optoelectronics.

Introduction

The rapid growth of information technology in materials research entails an innovative platform for designing novel functional materials with unfailing energy footprints in optoelectronics.1,2 Metal-oxide thin films and chemically fabricated photonic nanostructures are expected to satisfy these predictable demands, offering breakthroughs for divergent electro-optical applications.3,4 Functional optical devices with fast light-interacting response have been the focus of research in the past few years.5,6 Continuous efforts have been made to explore compatible materials with improved physicochemical properties and doping effects.7,8 Recent advances in the fabrication of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) have established them as important ceramic materials with attractive features for future electronic applications.9,10 The incorporation of TMDs into two-dimensional (2D) thin-layered materials have paved the way for innovative frameworks, enabling rapid advances in industrial research.11,12 As a result of these advancements, researchers have also turned their focus on perovskite oxides due to their useful properties for energy and electronic applications.

Barium titanate (BaTiO3) is a ferroelectric semiconductor having a perovskite structure with a band gap of 3.2 eV.13,14 The exceptional chemical endurance, structural flexibility, and outstanding electro-optical and ultrafast dielectric response of BaTiO3 make it a tunable material for future research to reconfigure modern electronics and circuit architectures.15,16 A structural modification in BaTiO3 crystals offers an irreplaceable challenge to engineer unique heterostructures for broad-range optoelectronics. Doping is a critical approach that deeply modifies the intrinsic features of materials, boosting their thermoelectric and optical responses for versatile optoelectronic devices.7 The electronic behavior of BaTiO3 can also be modified by doping and changing its composition. By incorporating appropriate dopants, the band gap, charge transport, and conductivity can be tuned, which broadens its applications from traditional capacitors to advanced optoelectronic and functional devices. For example, doping with elements like La, N, or Eu can change the band gap, improve electrical conductivity, and affect the material's grain structure, which lead to better performance in practical applications.17,18 The multi-responsive nature of BaTiO3, including superconductivity, piezoelectricity, high dielectric properties, and structural tunability, makes it a promising functional material for energy harvesting, sensing, photonic, solar cell, capacitor, and photocatalysis applications.19–22 In photocatalysis, elemental doping with La or Ag has been shown to significantly improve photocatalytic activity by increasing the ability to absorb visible light and by decreasing the rate of electron–hole recombination, leading to improved catalytic efficiency.17,20 For example, the introduction of Ca into BaTiO3 has been shown to boost electrical conductivity by raising the charge carrier density.23 Similarly, in thermoelectrics, dopants such as Ca, rare-earths, and Mg enhance carrier density, reduce thermal conductivity, and improve the Seebeck coefficient,23,24 which makes BaTiO3 a versatile material for optoelectronic, photocatalytic, and thermoelectric applications. Thus, doping with rare-earth elements in perovskites can lower thermal conductivity by enhancing phonon scattering and can also optimize the Seebeck coefficient through band structure modification; however, its thermoelectric performance suffers due to low electrical conductivity.25 To solve this issue, suitable elemental doping has been applied to adjust its transport characteristics. Magnesium (Mg) has been a versatile dopant in materials production due to its fascinating structural stability, charming electrical conductivity, and delightful optical behavior.24 It acts as an adaptable acceptor in semiconducting, optical, and ceramic materials, which facilitate the carrier's concentration and hole conduction with modified electrochemical features upon doping. Moreover, Mg is commonly used to improve the thermoelectric performance of materials. In Mg-doped ZnO and Mg2Sn0.75 Ge0.25, it enhances electrical conductivity and reduces thermal conductivity, which leads to better efficiency.23–25

BaTiO3 compound has a perovskite (ABO3) structure, with Ba2+ and Ti4+ cations on the A and B sites, respectively. In principle, other metal ions can substitute both cations to obtain the required characteristics for various electromechanical, electro-optic, and dielectric applications. Generally, doping strategies are separated into A- and B-site species within perovskite structures. These structures can accept ions of different sizes, and numerous dopants can be placed in the lattice to modify the Curie temperature, tune the sequence and nature of the phase transitions, and alter the piezoelectric and electrical properties. It is generally assumed that Mg dopant can occupy either A or B sites as an isovalent or aliovalent dopant, respectively. The ionic radius of Mg2+ in a 6-fold coordination is smaller (0.065 nm), and these ions can occupy the Ti4+ (0.061 nm) at the B-site in titanate structures, generating a charged defect. After the Ti4+ ions are replaced by Mg2+, negatively charged defects will be formed, and a corresponding number of positively charged oxygen vacancies will be required to satisfy the charge neutrality and site balance conditions. In contrast, the ionic radius of Mg2+ in 12-fold coordination is 0.120 nm, and it can also substitute at the A-site with Ba2+ (0.161 nm), generating a neutral defect. A first-principles-based atomistic model investigation of Mg incorporation into BaTiO3 observed that Ba replacement by Mg at the A-site generates local electric dipoles due to Mg off-centering along the (001) direction, which causes an increase in the Curie temperature and a decrease in the cell volume. An inverse dependence was observed for the B-site doped compositions, where the defect dipoles decreased the Curie temperature and expanded the volume.26

Density functional theory (DFT) predicts the influence of doping on the structural intrinsic defects, optical absorption, and magnetic performance in crucial optical and spintronic devices.27,28 Wein2K guides experimental determinations in material design with electronic structure and bandgap calculations to simulate and evaluate the properties at the quantum level.29,30 Here, we report the electronic, thermoelectric, and optical response of Mg-containing barium titanate perovskites using computational and experimental approaches. Mg has played an important role in optoelectronic applications due to its tremendous properties. Different parameters of barium titanate are examined, and the effect of Mg content is investigated for advanced applications. The comparative study using DFT and experimental investigations gives a practical insight into the use of these compositions as future optoelectronic materials.

Materials and methods

Computational method

In the present work, DFT is used to study the effect of Mg doping on the electronic and optical properties of BaTiO3. It helps evaluate changes in key electronic features, such as band structure and density of states, which are important for understanding conductivity, bandgap variation, and optical response. These parameters are essential for investigating thermoelectric and optoelectronic performance. Recent studies also show that DFT is a reliable tool for investigating such changes in advanced functional materials,9,14,17 especially perovskite oxides. The widely utilized Wein2K code with DFT theory was implemented for the theoretical examination of the thermoelectric and optical response of pure and Mg-doped BaTiO3 films. The calculations were performed by using the full potential linearized augmented plane wave (FP-LAPW) approach for solving the Kohn–Sham equations. The exchange–correlation potential was treated within the framework of the Tran–Blaha modified Becke–Johnson (TB-mBJ) approximation. The Mg doping levels (5.55%, 8.33%, and 12.5%) were chosen based on known solubility limits and previous studies using similar amounts of elements, like Sr in BaTiO3.13 These doping levels help examine changes in structure and properties without affecting stability. A cubic phase BaTiO3 structural file with a lattice constant (4.036 Å) was selected, having a space group (221-Pm3m). Different BaTiO3 supercell conformations were implemented for computational evaluations of various properties. Briefly, in the BaTiO3 crystal, the Ti, O, and Ba are positioned at the body-centered, face-centered, and the corner of a cube, respectively. The Mg atoms at three different concentrations (5.55%, 8.33%, and 12.5%) by generating the supercell configurations (3 × 3 × 2), (3 × 2 × 2), and (2 × 2 × 2), respectively, were integrated into the host BaTiO3 at the site of Ba. The orbital configurations with maximally contributed Mg-s, Ba-p, Ti-d, and O-p were precisely created. The cut-off k-point and energy value on the basis of Monkhorst–Pack were adjusted to 1000 and −6.0 Ry, respectively. The Gmax, Imax, and Kmax were maintained at 10, 12, and 8, respectively. The convergence values for charge (10−2 C) and energy (10−4 eV) were taken for self-consistency observations. The thermoelectric calculations were performed by using the BoltzTraP approximation. The constant scattering relaxation time (τ) was assumed to be 10−14 s. The phonon calculations were executed by the finite-displacement method based on forces obtained from Phonopy under the WIEN2k framework. The force constants were extracted from a supercell [2 × 2 × 2], and the phonon dispersion relations were subsequently constructed from the calculated dynamical matrices.

Experimental methods

The undoped and Mg-doped BaTiO3 film compositions (5.55%-Mg, 8.33%-Mg, and 12.5%-Mg) were produced on clean glass substrates with a sol–gel-based chemical spin method. The precursor salts, including titanium butoxide (Ti [O (CH2)3CH3]4), barium carbonate (BaCO3), and magnesium nitrate hexahydrate (Mg (NO3) 2·6H2O), with 99.9% purity, were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. The glass substrates were ultrasonically cleaned before deposition to ensure proper adhesion and a uniform surface. The dopant (Mg) salt and host salts were carefully dissolved with the required stoichiometric amounts in ethanol under constant stirring. Then, acetic acid was added as a chelating as well as stabilizing agent. The solution mixtures were heated with continuous stirring at 100 °C until they were transformed into transparent gels. Further, the thick solutions were dropped on the surface of the substrate for coating at 1000 rpm for 45 seconds and then were exposed for baking at 100 °C for 10 minutes to remove the impurity solvent. After the coating process, the prepared films were aged for 24 hours and thermally annealed for 2 hours at 600 °C to improve crystallinity.

After successful synthesis, all Mg-doped BaTiO3 film compositions were subjected to various characterization techniques. The crystal structural details were obtained with a diffractometer (Bruker D-2 Phaser). The composition and shape of prepared films were analyzed with EDX using an Oxford INCA X′ detector and FESEM imaging with FEI Nova NanoSEM, respectively. In addition, the optical responsive parameters were estimated with Alpha SE ellipsometry.

Morphology and elemental composition

The morphological analysis of all undoped and Mg-doped BaTiO3 films was verified by FESEM high-resolution imaging with an LTD detector operated at 10 kV, as presented in Fig. 2(a–d). The pure sample indicated aggregated randomly scattered grains (Fig. 2(a)). However, the BaTiO3 film morphology prominently transformed to a distinguishable large granular shape with low aggregation upon Mg doping. Additionally, the excessive Mg content (12.5%) led to the formation of a non-uniformly distributed mixed spherical-like compact film geometry (Fig. 2(d)). The undoped film shows a smooth surface with evenly distributed and well-separated grains. After Mg is added, the grains become more compact and begin to cluster. This change occurs due to the effect of Mg on surface energy and atomic movement during film growth, which modifies the process of grain formation and growth. Consequently, the film becomes denser and less porous. Further, the undoped composition showed characteristic elemental peaks in the elemental analysis corresponding to Ba, Ti, and O, suggesting the phase purity of BaTiO3, as plotted in Fig. 3(a). Moreover, a vibrant peak related to Mg contents appeared in all Mg-doped compositions. The intensity and broadness of the Mg peak were enhanced with an increase in the doping amount of Mg content. The EDX spectra of all compositions are elaborated in Fig. 3(a–d).

Electronic properties

The nature of the electronic structure and bandgap has been estimated using DFT theory with the Wein2k code. The curves of band structures for Mg-doped and undoped BaTiO3 films are shown in Fig. 4(a–d). The solid red line indicates the region of the Fermi level between the conduction and valence bands at 0 eV. The simulated band gap of pure BaTiO3 films was estimated at 2.57 eV (Fig. 4(a)). A similar value of band gap was found in previously doped BaTiO3 films.32,33 However, the energy gap was linearly reduced by adding Mg contents into the host BaTiO3 films upon doping. Additionally, the Mg-doped compositions deliberated the additional highly overlapped orbital states with Mg contents, as depicted in Fig. 4(b–d). The band gap values are 2.54 eV, 2.52 eV, and 2.48 eV for 5.55%-Mg, 8.33%-Mg, and 12.5%-Mg, respectively. The diminished energy gap upon Mg doping is attributed to the participation of new states modifying electronic interactions within the BaTiO3 lattice.

Band structure plots of the (a) BaTiO3, (b) 5.55%-Mg, (c) 8.33%-Mg and (d) 12.5%-Mg doped compositions.

Further, the band gap evaluation of electronic energy states was carried out by the density of states analysis. The curves of partial density of states (PDOS) are plotted for both Mg-doped and undoped BaTiO3 films in Fig. 5(a–d). A prominent contraction in the band gap is visible in the spectra upon Mg doping. The pure BaTiO3 films exhibited a superior contribution of O-p orbital in the valence band with minimal contribution of Ti-d and Ba-p orbitals. However, Ti-d states were found to maximally contribute to the conduction band of BaTiO3. When Mg contents were added to the host structure, an additional peak associated with Mg-s orbital appeared, showing great involvement in the conduction bands of doped BaTiO3. The intensity of the Mg-s peak increased with an increase in Mg contents, and the energy gap lifted towards the Ef value. The decrease in the energy gap in Mg-doped films was further clarified by the analysis of the total density of states. In the highly doped sample (Fig. 5(d)), the overall contribution of Mg s orbitals is reduced. This is because Mg s states are located at higher energy positions and exhibit weak hybridization with Ti d orbitals. Therefore, the conduction band is mainly formed by Ti d states, while Mg affects it indirectly through structural or charge interactions. When Mg is doped at the Ba site, the electronic band structure and density of states (DOS) show a clear shift of the localized states closer to the Fermi level. This shift leads to a smaller band gap compared to that in the undoped material. The reduced band gap indicates higher carrier concentration and better electrical transport, which are beneficial for optoelectronic performance. Fig. 6(a–d) elaborates the TDOS plots of undoped films and Mg-doped BaTiO3 films in the range of −4.0–5 eV. The expected shrinking in the gap toward the Fermi position after the integration of Mg contents was deduced from the spectra. The results supported the appropriateness of BaTiO3 as an excellent choice for optoelectronics.

Fig. 7(a) highlights Mg doping effect on the electrical conductivity (σ) of pure BaTiO3 composition at three different levels (5.55%, 8.33%, and 12.5%) over a wide temperature range. The conductivity of all BaTiO3 compositions was observed to increase with temperature rise. It might be because high temperature led to an increase in the charge mobility of electrons, ultimately improving the conductivity of materials. The σ value of pure BaTiO3 composition decreases upon Mg doping up to 8.33% concentration. However, for excessive doping (12.5%), the σ value strategically increases with higher dopant contents. The higher contents introduced more charges, deteriorating the structural lattice of BaTiO3 and promoting its electrical conductivity response, due to free charge carriers. This explains the nonlinear but justified trend. Similar observations have been found in previous Sr-doped BaTiO3 thin films.13

The thermal conductivity for all Mg-doped BaTiO3 compositions is demonstrated in Fig. 7(b). A progressive climbing trend is indicated for all the BaTiO3 films over a selected temperature range. Moreover, the pure composition showed superior thermal conductivity among all compositions. However, adding Mg contents into the BaTiO3 host led to increased conductivity upon Mg doping. The increase in thermal conductivity response on doping is attributed to the densified charge concentration, modifying lattice disorder and slowing photonic scattering in the BaTiO3 lattice.33 The maximum conductivities were obtained as 12.0, 6.0, 5.0, and 4.0 W mK−1 for pure, 12.5%, 8.33%, and 5.55% doped compositions, respectively. In this study, the total thermal conductivity was calculated using BoltzTraP and included mainly the electronic part. The lattice thermal conductivity was not directly calculated but was estimated through the Slack model from DFT results.9

The Seebeck constants for doped and undoped BaTiO3 are plotted in Fig. 7 © at different temperatures. All the films attained the maximum of Seebeck values in the lower region (100–200 °C). The values of Seebeck were sharply increased at the initial temperature region, which afterward became constant at the highest temperature range (600–800 °C) for all the BaTiO3 films. However, the Seebeck values increased after Mg doping up to 8.33% composition, which then reduced for the extreme doping concentration (12.5%). The dopant concentration may cause structural flexibility changes and alter light interactions, resulting in a lowered Seebeck coefficient of the BaTiO3 films.13,33 The Seebeck trend indicated a successful modification of the BaTiO3 lattice upon Mg doping for high-performance thermoelectric devices.

Fig. 7(d) explains the figure of merit (ZT) for all doped and undoped BaTiO3 compositions. The figure of merit of pure BaTiO3 was found to increase upon Mg doping till the concentration of the contents reached 8.33%, which shows better thermoelectric performance. The maximally (12.5%) doped composition showed the lowest figure of merit among all the BaTiO3 films, which might be due to observations that high Mg contents may alter the host characteristics by modulating its structural defects, indicating an appropriate working dopant concentration. This is also attributed to the lower Seebeck coefficient and higher thermal conductivity. The Sr-doped BaTiO3 films also exhibited a similar trend of figure of merit.13 This indicates that optimal performance is attained by moderate doping. The alteration in thermoelectric parameters indicated the enormous ability of the BaTiO3 films for heat-conversion devices.

The power factor (PF) depends on electrical conductivity (σ) and the square of the Seebeck coefficient (S), which can be expressed using the relation PF = σS2.13 Mg doping changes both parameters, as depicted in Fig. 7(a) and (c). Electrical conductivity reduces up to 8.33% Mg due to the presence of fewer free carriers, but increases at 12.5% as more carriers are introduced by lattice disturbance. The Seebeck coefficient increases up to 8.33%, and then decreases at 12.5% due to structural changes. As a result, PF enhances with moderate Mg doping but decreases at higher doping contents.

Fig. 8(a–d) illustrates the experimentally measured thermoelectric properties of pure and Mg-doped BaTiO3 in the temperature range of 300–500 K. As shown in Fig. 8(a), the electrical conductivity of all samples increases with temperature, which is a typical behavior of semiconducting materials. Among them, the 12.5% Mg-doped sample exhibits the highest electrical conductivity due to the introduction of extra charge carriers from Mg substitution, which enhances carrier density as well as charge transport. This increasing trend is also observed in theoretical measurements. Fig. 8(b) shows the thermal conductivity, which decreases with increasing temperature for all compositions. The pure BaTiO3 sample exhibits the highest thermal conductivity, while the 12.5% Mg-doped sample shows the lowest. This reduction can be attributed to increased phonon scattering caused by lattice imperfections and mass fluctuations induced by Mg host atoms.

Fig. 8(c) presents the Seebeck coefficients of pure and Mg-doped BaTiO3 samples, where all compositions show positive values, which confirms their p-type conduction nature. The Seebeck coefficient increases with temperature and reaches its maximum value for the 12.5% Mg-doped sample due to an optimized carrier density and the energy filtering effect. As shown in Fig. 8(d), the figure of merit (ZT) increases with temperature for all samples, with the highest value observed for the 12.5% Mg-doped composition. The improvement in ZT arises from the combined effect of enhanced electrical conductivity, reduced thermal conductivity, and an increased Seebeck coefficient.36 Overall, Mg doping effectively improves the thermoelectric performance of BaTiO3, making the 12.5% Mg-doped sample the most efficient among all studied compositions.

The transport properties depend on carrier density, mobility, as well as scattering. As the temperature increases, the conductivity increases because of carrier activation. Moderate Mg doping creates defects that trap carriers and increase the Seebeck coefficient, while higher doping increases carrier concentrations and disorder, which improves conductivity and lowers the Seebeck coefficient. This balance explains the changes in PF and ZT. Thermoelectric transport properties evaluated within the semi-classical Boltzmann framework exhibited stable temperature-dependent electrical conductivity and finite Seebeck coefficients over a wide temperature range, indicating transport robustness in the layered rare-earth nickelates.37,38

Heat in crystalline materials is transported by phonons from lattice vibrations, and the lattice thermal conductivity critically determines thermoelectric performance. Understanding a high ZT value hinges on a low thermal conductivity and a high power factor. These parameters are usually coupled together and varied by carrier mobility and concentration. Tailoring lattice vibrations at the local scale is vital to decouple electrical-thermal performance. The point defects, such as vacancies with associated strain fields, enhance phonon scattering and weaken bond stiffness. Moreover, excess Mg vacancies are believed to be detrimental to the performance of n-type materials. It is critical to reconcile this competing effect and alter the vacancy concentration to an optimal range in order to balance thermal and electrical transport behavior. Generally, defect-induced internal strain usually modifies the phonon frequency, which in turn causes lattice softening. Consequently, the lattice softening induced by internal strain leads to a reduced thermal conductivity. Further, the thermal conductivity, Seebeck coefficient, and electrical conductivity are interconnected through features of the band structure and carrier concentration. Particularly, the increase in electrical conductivity of the material is typically accompanied by a rise in electronic thermal conductivity and a decrease in the Seebeck coefficient. Furthermore, the possibility of an increase in the figure of merit parameter is observed through the dominant carriers scattering at the ionized impurities.41

Fig. 10 shows the phonon dispersion curves for pure and Mg-doped BaTiO3. All the modes appear in the positive frequency region, which indicates that the structures are dynamically stable. The pure BaTiO3 sample exhibits fewer phonon branches. On the other hand, the number and complexity of these phonon branches increase with higher Mg doping. This can be attributed to the introduction of Mg, which modifies the lattice mass and bonding environment, and introduces new vibrational interactions that result in an increase in phonon modes. The dense curves of phonon dispersion are an integral characteristic of modeling Mg-doped BaTiO3 through the supercell approach. Mg dopants decrease the crystal symmetry and increase the number of atoms, subsequently increasing the number of phonon branches. The branches approaching zero frequency near the Γ-point are related to the acoustic phonon modes, which are projected to approach zero frequency due to the translational invariance of the crystal lattice. No negative modes were detected throughout the Brillouin zone, indicating that the structures are dynamically stable. Mg incorporation into the perovskite systems induces structural defects and lattice distortions. Localized stress regions form within the crystal lattice due to these distortions, resulting in an overall increase in microstrain, as demonstrated by the broadening of the X-ray diffraction patterns. The replacement of Ba2+ ions (1.44 Å) with smaller Mg2+ (0.72 Å) ions potentially alters the lattice structure, generating defect centers and dislocations that contribute to the observed lattice strain variations. This nature of hybrid bonding contributes to the dielectric properties of the material, which are sensitive to charge polarization and lattice distortion effects. In addition, grain boundaries can act as barriers that impact the polarization behavior and the material's electrical conductivity. Larger grain sizes tend to enhance dielectric permittivity by reducing the influence of grain boundaries, which act as charge-carrier scattering centers. Changes in charge localization and bonding strength affect dielectric relaxation mechanisms. Further, the ionic substitution in perovskite oxides affects charge transport and polarization behavior. As temperature increases, the thermal activation of charge carriers becomes more prominent, resulting in greater ionic mobility and improved conduction pathways. The thermally activated behavior indicates that charge transport occurs both within grains and across grain boundaries. At lower temperatures, the relaxation time is longer due to the presence of immobile defect states and localized charges, whereas at higher temperatures, thermally activated carriers significantly reduce the relaxation time.42

Phonon band curves presenting frequency (THz) at the y-axis of the (a) pure BaTiO3, (b) 5.55% Mg-doped, (c) 8.33% Mg-doped, and (d) 12.5% Mg-doped compositions.

The increase in phonon branch density with Mg concentration is attributed to the fact that, as the Mg content increases, the presence of Mg alters the strain distribution and atomic arrangement, which may favor the formation of larger crystallites. This effect aligns with the general understanding that compositional variations, particularly through ionic substitution, play a crucial role in defining the microstructural and structural characteristics of perovskite ceramics. Mg incorporation introduces additional local vibrational interactions due to differences in ionic radius and bonding environment compared with the substituted host atom. These changes lead to the appearance of new hybridized or localized vibrational modes, further increasing the complexity of the phonon dispersion. Moreover, these lattice modifications influence the growth and nucleation mechanisms of crystallites, stimulating an increase in the average crystallite size with higher Mg content. Furthermore, the introduction of Mg2+ may lead to local strain relaxation within the perovskite lattice, influencing the spatial distribution of charge carriers. These structural modifications could have implications for the polarization behavior and electrical conductivity of the material, making it a significant factor in optimizing the functional performance of perovskites for advanced applications.42

Fig. 13(a–d) highlights the Tauc plots of undoped and Mg-doped BaTiO3 compositions. Optical gaps of 1.71 eV, 1.77 eV, 1.99 eV, and 2.30 eV were observed for 12.5%-Mg, 8.33%-Mg, 5.55%-Mg and pure BaTiO3, respectively. The Tauc plot observations deduced a momentous reduction in the optical bandgap of the BaTiO3 lattice upon Mg doping. A similar response has been observed in previously doped BaTiO3.13 The above findings further illustrated the efficient refining of BaTiO3 optical characteristics with Mg content addition, demonstrating the role of the overlapping of newly created localized energy states in BaTiO3. The shrinking of the bandgap with dopant content provides an optimized approach for advanced optoelectronics using doped materials.50,51 The incorporation of Mg2+ potentially modifies the local polarization environment. This effect can be attributed to the Ba2+ ions being replaced by Mg2+, which leads to changes in defect chemistry, lattice strain, and polarization stability. The presence of multiple crystallographic orientations within each grain can influence the polarization response, affecting the electronic properties. In addition, the formation of larger grains with increasing Mg content could be attributed to enhanced surface energy effects. The observed increase in grain size with Mg content suggests probable improvements in dielectric response due to reduced grain boundary density. The decrease in grain boundary energy upon Mg doping may cause a lowered activation energy for grain coalescence, supporting the formation of larger crystalline domains. This discrepancy between grain and crystallite sizes has significant implications for the dielectric behavior of the material. The dielectric relaxation process is influenced by ionic displacement and potential space charge effects. Additionally, the presence of oxygen vacancies, induced by Mg substitution, may influence the overall dielectric response and affect charge-compensation mechanisms.42 Hence, the lower value of experimentally measured optical band gaps (1.71–2.30 eV) than the calculated electronic band gaps (2.48–2.57 eV) might be due to the fact that the realistic material may contain microstructural imperfections, defect-induced localized impurity states, and grain boundary effects. Second, the oxygen vacancies are among the most common defects in oxide perovskites and are known to generate defect-induced donor states near the conduction band, which might have contributed to the apparent narrowing of the optical band gap.

Conclusion

This study explores the properties of BaTiO3 films upon Mg doping for specific optoelectronic applications. The BaTiO3 and Mg-doped thin films have been conventionally prepared with a chemical sol–gel method. Experimental results confirmed a cubic phase structure through XRD analysis for all prepared BaTiO3 compositions. The morphological analysis (SEM) of the pure composition indicated an aggregated randomly scattered flaky-shaped structure that strategically changed after Mg doping. The phase purity was analyzed by the EDX pattern with expected peaks of Ba, O, Ti, and Mg elements. On the other hand, DFT calculations provided simulated results of the electronic structure, where Ba-p, O-p, Ti-d and Mg-s orbitals showed prominent contributions in all Mg-doped structures. Additionally, the Mg contents integration effectively tuned the thermoelectric response, modifying the figure of merit and Seebeck coefficient of the BaTiO3 film matrix. The Mg-doped (12.5%) composition illustrated the refractive index and epsilon values as ≈3.35 and ≈8.5, respectively, in the experimental evaluation of optical parameters. Further, the optical bandgap of the BaTiO3 lattice was markedly reduced to 1.71 eV upon Mg doping. The consistency between DFT-based results and experimental data confirms that Mg doping effectively tunes the structural and optical properties of BaTiO3, which makes it a promising candidate for future optoelectronic applications.
