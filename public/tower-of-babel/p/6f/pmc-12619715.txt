Impact of inflammatory cytokine polymorphisms on the progression of myelodysplastic syndromes
Authors: Nanami Gotoh, Tsatsralgerel Munkh-Erdene, Ai Hayashi, Tetsuhiro Kasamatsu, Miri Kitamura, Asumi Kojima, Takafumi Okawa, Tsukasa Oda, Eiji Miyauchi, Nobuo Sasaki, Ikuko Matsumura, Akira Matsumoto, Hisashi Takei, Nobuhiko Kobayashi, Yuri Miyazawa, Yoshiyuki Ogawa, Hiroshi Handa, Takayuki Saitoh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic inflammation is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to tumorigenesis, promoting genomic instability, immune evasion, and malignant transformation. Inflammatory cytokines are particularly involved in the pathogenesis and progression of myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS). This study examined the impact of three cytokine polymorphisms—IFNG + 874 A/T, TNFA − 857 C/T, and IL1B -31 C/T—on MDS susceptibility and clinical characteristics. A total of 105 patients with MDS and 117 healthy Japanese controls were analyzed. High-expression TNFA − 857 C/T was significantly associated with adverse risk categories by the revised International Prognostic Scoring System (high or very high: non-CC vs. CC = 36.4% vs. 7.2%, p < 0.001) and an increased likelihood of transformation to leukemia (non-CC vs. CC = 30.3% vs. 11.6%, p = 0.02). Similarly, IFNG + 874 A/T correlated with a higher risk of leukemic progression (non-AA vs. AA = 33.3% vs. 14.3%, p = 0.04). Moreover, high-expression genotypes of TNFA and IL1B were linked to increased chromosomal abnormalities. In conclusion, our results indicate that high-expression cytokine polymorphisms may contribute to more aggressive forms of MDS. Introduction

Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) are a heterogeneous group of clonal hematopoietic disorders characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis, peripheral blood cytopenia, and increased risk of progression to acute myeloid leukemia (AML). MDS primarily affects older adults and arises from genetic and epigenetic alterations that disrupt normal bone marrow function. The pathogenesis of MDS involves complex interactions between hematopoietic stem cells and the bone marrow microenvironment, including immune dysregulation and chronic inflammation. Recent studies suggest that inflammatory cytokines play a crucial role in disease progression by promoting the apoptosis of hematopoietic progenitor cells and altering the bone marrow niche [1, 2].

Inflammation plays a complex role in carcinogenesis, exerting both protective and harmful effects. Acute inflammation helps eliminate tumours, whereas chronic inflammation promotes tumour development [3]. This study focused on three key inflammatory cytokines—interferon (IFN)-γ, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and interleukin (IL)−1β—which also exhibit dual roles in tumours.

These cytokines can enhance cancer immune surveillance while also fostering a tumour-supportive microenvironment. For instance, IFN-γ induces over 200 genes, many of which are involved in helping tumour cells evade the immune system, including molecules such as PD-L1, PD-L2, and CTLA-4 [4]. Moreover, TNF-α contributes to cancer progression by increasing the survival and proliferation of malignant cells, supporting angiogenesis, and facilitating immune evasion through the upregulation of immunosuppressive molecules such as PD-L1 and TIM-3 on both tumour-infiltrating leukocytes and cancer cells [5]. Similarly, IL-1β within the tumour microenvironment can drive carcinogenesis, tumour growth, and metastasis through mechanisms such as sustained inflammation, activation of endothelial cells, promotion of tumour angiogenesis, and induction of immunosuppressive cells [6].

These findings highlight the intricate and paradoxical roles of inflammatory cytokines in tumour biology and suggest that chronic inflammation may contribute to the pathogenesis of hematological malignancies. Given the established link between inflammation and tumours, investigating its role in MDS is crucial for understanding the disease progression.

Chronic inflammation is increasingly recognised as a key driver of tumorigenesis, contributing to genetic instability, immune evasion, and malignant transformation. Notably, inflammatory cytokines play pivotal roles in the pathogenesis and progression of MDS. Elevated levels of proinflammatory mediators, including IL-6 and TNF-α, are commonly observed in patients with MDS and are associated with increased apoptosis of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, leading to ineffective hematopoiesis [2]. Moreover, chronic inflammation fosters a bone marrow microenvironment that promotes the clonal expansion of malignant cells, facilitating the transition from MDS to AML, particularly in patients with TP53 mutations [7]. Importantly, a high systemic inflammatory cytokine burden correlates with poor prognosis, highlighting its potential as a biomarker for disease progression. Therefore, genetic variations in cytokine-related pathways have been investigated to elucidate their contribution to MDS susceptibility and progression.

As previously discussed, inflammatory cytokines play crucial roles in the pathogenesis of MDS. Therefore, we hypothesised that functional cytokine polymorphisms influence susceptibility to and severity of MDS. We have previously investigated the association between cytokines and hematologic malignancies and reported the impact of cytokine polymorphisms on clinical features and prognosis [8–10]. The cytokines examined in this study—IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-1β—are major inflammatory mediators. However, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have reported the impact of these polymorphisms in Japanese patients with MDS. Here, we focused on three polymorphisms, IFNG + 874 A/T, TNFA − 857 C/T, and IL1B −31 C/T, which are located within transcription factor binding sites or promoter regions and are known to influence cytokine expression level [11–14]. These loci have been extensively investigated because of their functional relevance to inflammatory and immune responses. Therefore, we investigated their effects on susceptibility to and clinical features of MDS.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study included 105 patients with MDS and 117 healthy Japanese controls. The patients were diagnosed with MDS according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification at Gunma University Hospital or Saiseikai Maebashi Hospital. The observation period was from January 2010 to July 2019, with a median duration of 47.0 months. Some cases in which the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) failed were excluded from the analysis.

Patients were reclassified into the following seven subtypes based on the 2016 WHO classification system: MDS with single-lineage dysplasia (MDS-SLD), MDS with multilineage dysplasia (MDS-MLD), MDS with ring sideroblasts (MDS-RS), MDS with excess blasts-1 (MDS-EB-1), MDS with excess blasts-2 (MDS-EB-2), MDS-unclassified (MDS-U), and MDS associated with isolated del(5q) (MDS-5q-). Prognosis was assessed using both the International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) and its revised version (IPSS-R). Karyotype classification was re-evaluated according to the IPSS-R criteria.

All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gunma University (#770).

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was extracted from the peripheral blood samples collected from each participant using a QIAamp DNA Blood Kit (Qiagen Sciences, Germantown, MD, USA). Three nucleotide polymorphisms were analyzed: IFNG + 874 A/T, TNFA−857 C/T, and IL1B−31 C/T.

Genotyping of IFNG + 874 A/T was performed using allele-specific PCR with the primers listed in Supplemental Table 1. The PCR reaction was carried out in a total volume of 20 µL, containing 1 µL of genomic DNA, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 0.5 U of TaKaRa Ex Taq HS DNA polymerase (TaKaRa Bio, Japan), and 0.5 µM of each primer.

Genotyping of TNFA−857 C/T and IL1B−31 C/T was performed using the PCR-restriction fragment length polymorphism method. The PCR reaction was performed in a total volume of 20 µL, containing 1 µL of genomic DNA, 0.2 mM dNTPs, 0.5 U of TaKaRa Ex Taq HS DNA polymerase, and 0.5 µM of each primer, as listed in Supplemental Table 1. The resulting PCR products were digested with the appropriate restriction enzymes (New England BioLabs, Tokyo, Japan), as specified in Supplemental Table 1.

Statistical analysis

Several statistical methods were used for data analysis. Genotype frequencies between groups were compared using the chi-square test, while clinical features were assessed using both Student’s t-test and the chi-square test. Overall survival (OS) was evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test.

All statistical analysis were conducted using EZR and SPSS software (version 28, IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA) with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan) is a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) [15].

Results

Study population

A total of 105 patients with MDS were included in this study (71 males and 34 females; age range, 18–86 years; median age: 66.5 years; Table 1). According to the WHO classification, the majority of cases were classified as MDS-SLD (n = 35), followed by MDS-MLD (n = 22), MDS-EB-1 (n = 14), MDS-EB-2 (n = 13), MDS-U (n = 13), MDS-RS-SLD (n = 6), and MDS-5q- (n = 2).

Based on the IPSS, most patients had an int-1 score, followed by low, int-2, and high scores. Similarly, according to the IPSS-R, the most common risk category was low, followed by the intermediate, very low, high, and very high-risk categories.

More than one-quarter of patients did not receive any treatment. Four patients had undergone stem cell transplantation.

Comparison of genotype frequencies

We analyzed three cytokine polymorphisms: IFNG + 874T/A, TNFA − 857 C/T, and IL1B −31 C/T. As a preliminary step, we compared the allele frequencies in our control group with those reported for the Japanese population in the 38KJPN dataset (https://www.megabank.tohoku.ac.jp/english). No significant differences were found, indicating that our control group is representative of the general Japanese population (Supplemental Table 2).

We then compared genotype and allele frequencies between patients with MDS and the control group (Table 2). Chi-square tests were performed under codominant, dominant, and recessive genetic models. However, no significant associations were observed between any of the three polymorphisms and susceptibility to MDS.

Comparison of clinical features

Table 3. summarizes the clinical features of patients with MDS according to IFNG, TNFA, and IL1B polymorphisms. Patients with IFNG + 874 A/T non-AA genotype, which is associated high expression level, demonstrated a significantly higher frequency of history of tumour and transformation into leukemia compared to those with the AA genotype (history of tumour: AA vs. non-AA = 3.6% vs. 19.0%, p = 0.01, odds ratio [OR] = 0.16, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.03–0.78; transformation into leukemia: AA vs. non-AA = 14.3% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.04, OR = 0.33, 95% CI = 0.11–0.995). Patients with the TNFA−857 C/T non-CC genotype, which is associated with high expression levels, had a significantly higher frequency of chromosome 7-related abnormalities compared to those with the CC genotype (CC vs. non-CC = 5.8% vs. 21.2%, p = 0.02, OR = 0.23, 95% CI = 0.06–0.84) (Fig. 1). Additionally, patients with the non-CC genotype exhibited a significantly higher frequency of adverse risk scores according to both the IPSS and IPSS-R compared to those with the CC genotype (IPSS int-2 or high: CC vs. non-CC = 13.0% vs. 36.4%, p = 0.002, OR = 0.23, 95% CI = 0.09–0.62; IPSS-R high or very high: CC vs. non-CC = 7.2% vs. 36.4%, p < 0.001, OR = 0.14, 95% CI = 0.04–0.44). Furthermore, the MDS-EB subtype was observed more frequently in patients with the non-CC genotype than in those with the CC genotype (CC vs. non-CC = 17.4% vs. 39.4%, p = 0.02, OR = 0.32, 95% CI = 0.13–0.83). This polymorphism was also associated with treatment selection. Patients with the non-CC genotype were more likely to receive chemotherapy and were less likely to receive treatment with erythropoietin or granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (EPO/G-CSF) (chemotherapy: CC vs. non-CC = 2.9% vs. 15.2%, p = 0.02, OR = 0.17, 95% CI = 0.03–0.91; EPO/G-CSF: CC vs. non-CC = 13.0% vs. 0.0%, p = 0.03, OR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.56–0.75). Finally, patients with this genotype were significantly more likely to progress to leukemia (CC vs. non-CC = 11.6% vs. 30.3%, p = 0.02, OR = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.11–0.86).

IL1B−31 C/T was associated with chromosomal aberrations (Fig. 1). Patients with the non-CC genotype, which is associated with high expression levels, had a significantly higher frequency of chromosome 7-related abnormalities compared to those with the CC genotype (CC vs. non-CC = 0.0% vs. 16.4%, p = 0.01, OR = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.52–0.72). Moreover, patients with the non-CC genotype had a higher frequency of trisomy 8 (CC vs. non-CC = 0.0% vs. 10.4%, p = 0.045; OR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.53–0.73).

Further, we analyzed the association between the combined effects of these three polymorphisms and the clinical features (Table 4). Patients were divided into two groups: the high-expression group, which included patients with two or three high-expression genotypes, and the low-expression group, comprising patients with two or three low-expression genotypes. Notably, the high-expression group had a significantly higher frequency of chromosome 7-related abnormalities than did the low-expression group (high-expression group vs. low-expression group = 29.0% vs. 2.9%, p < 0.001, OR = 13.9, 95% CI = 2.79–69.6). Furthermore, the high-expression group had a significantly higher frequency of adverse risk scores according to both the IPSS and IPSS-R compared to the low-expression group (IPSS int-2 or high: high-expression group vs. low-expression group = 38.7% vs. 15.1%, p = 0.004, OR = 4.21, 95% CI = 1.54–11.5; IPSS-R high or very high: high-expression group vs. low-expression group = 32.3% vs. 7.2%, p = 0.001, OR = 6.00, 95% CI = 1.84–19.6). Moreover, the high-expression group demonstrated a significantly higher frequency of prior radiation therapy (high-expression group vs. low-expression group = 16.1% vs. 2.9%, p = 0.02, OR = 6.35, 95% CI = 1.16–34.8). Thus, highly expressed cytokines are more commonly found in the progressive stages of MDS.

Comparison of OS and progression-free survival (PFS)

We evaluated the impact of IFNG, TNFA, and IL1B polymorphisms on OS and PFS in patients with MDS (Fig. 2). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests were performed to compare survival outcomes among the different genotype groups.

Comparison of OS and PFS in IFNG, TNFA and IL1B polymorphisms in patients with MDS

Although no statistically significant differences in OS or PFS were observed among the IFNG + 874 A/T genotypes (p = 0.33 and p = 0.15, respectively), the survival curves showed a trend toward poorer outcomes in patients with the non-AA genotype (high-expression genotype). A similar pattern was observed for TNFA−857 C/T, in which patients carrying the non-CC genotype (high-expression genotype) tended to have shorter OS and PFS than those carrying the CC genotype (low-expression genotype) (OS: p = 0.25; PFS: p = 0.14). However, IL1B−31 C/T showed no significant association with OS or PFS (OS, p = 0.83; PFS, p = 0.90).

To further assess the independent impact of these polymorphisms on survival outcomes, multivariate analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazards model, adjusting for relevant clinical variables. However, none of these polymorphisms were statistically significant predictors of OS (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated an association between cytokine polymorphisms and clinical features of patients with MDS. In particular, patients carrying the high-expression TNFA−857 C/T genotype were more frequently classified into adverse risk categories according to the IPSS and IPSS-R and had a higher likelihood of progression to leukemia. Notably, this polymorphism influences treatment selection. In addition, the highly expressed TNFA−857 C/T and IL1B−31 C/T genotypes were associated with a higher frequency of chromosomal aberrations. Furthermore, IFNG + 874 A/T was associated with an increased risk of leukemic transformation. These findings suggest that highly expressed cytokine polymorphisms are associated with more aggressive subtypes of MDS.

To ensure the validity of our comparisons, we first assessed the suitability of our control population by comparing allele frequencies of the cytokine polymorphisms with those in a representative Japanese population from the 38KJPN dataset. No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups, confirming that our control population is representative of the general Japanese population.

Building on this foundation, our results further indicate that high-expression cytokine polymorphisms are associated with MDS severity. Recent studies have reported that elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, serve as predictors of more aggressive MDS [2] and are associated with poor prognosis [16], suggesting that high cytokine levels may contribute to disease progression. Consistent with these findings, our results showed a significantly higher frequency of leukemic transformation in patients with high levels of TNFA and IFNG polymorphisms. Moreover, Rodriguez-Meira et al. demonstrated that chronic inflammation drives the progression from MDS to AML. Chronic inflammation induced by polyI: C or lipopolysaccharides in vivo suppresses the proliferation of TP53-wildtype hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) while selectively promoting the expansion of TP53-mutant HSPCs [7]. These findings were consistent with our results.

Furthermore, we observed that patients carrying the high-expression TNF − 857 C/T and IL1B−31 C/T genotypes exhibited a higher frequency of chromosomal aberrations, suggesting a potential link between proinflammatory cytokine activity and cytogenetic abnormalities in MDS. Persistent inflammatory signalling induces oxidative stress, generates reactive oxygen species, and impairs DNA repair mechanisms, all of which can lead to chromosomal instability [17, 18]. In vivo studies have further demonstrated that chronic inflammation induced by poly I: C is associated with numerical abnormalities in whole or partial chromosomes [7]. These findings support the hypothesis that a proinflammatory microenvironment promotes genomic instability in MDS, contributing to disease progression and the emergence of more aggressive subtypes.

Several studies have investigated the role of cytokine polymorphisms in MDS, primarily focusing on TNF-α, IL-6, and TGF-β. Previous research has demonstrated that high-expression TNF-α polymorphisms, particularly TNFA−308 G/A, are associated with lower hemoglobin levels, severe cytopenias, and increased transfusion dependency, suggesting a role in disease severity [19–21]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically examined the impact of IFNG and IL1B polymorphisms on MDS or the association between TNFA−857 C/T and leukemic transformation.

In contrast, our study provides novel insights by demonstrating that high-expression TNFA−857 C/T and IFNG + 874 A/T polymorphisms are significantly associated with leukemic transformation and adverse risk classification in IPSS and IPSS-R, suggesting their role in a more aggressive MDS phenotype. Additionally, TNFA−857 C/T and IL1B−31 C/T have been linked to increased chromosomal aberrations, indicating chronic inflammation and genomic instability. These findings expand on previous research by identifying new cytokine polymorphisms associated with MDS progression and chromosomal alterations, supporting the role of the proinflammatory state in MDS severity and leukemic transformation.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and the design was retrospective. Larger prospective studies are required to confirm our findings. Further validation in a cohort with a more balanced distribution of disease subtypes is also necessary. Second, our analysis was limited to IFNG + 874 A/T, TNFA−857 C/T, and IL1B−31 C/T; other potentially functional polymorphisms were not assessed. Further investigations are necessary to examine additional relevant genetic variations. Third, this study was conducted exclusively in Japanese patients, which may restrict the generalizability of the results. Future studies should include diverse ethnic populations to evaluate the broader applicability of these findings. Given these limitations, caution should be exercised when interpreting the conclusions of this study.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that inflammatory cytokine polymorphisms are variably associated with the clinical course of MDS, with the TNFA−857 C/T variant showing the most consistent and robust relationship with disease severity and progression. In contrast, associations for IFNG + 874 A/T and IL1B −31 C/T were limited, with IFNG showing a link mainly to prior radiotherapy and IL1B to certain cytogenetic abnormalities. Although no statistically significant associations with OS and PFS were observed in this cohort, the trend toward poorer prognosis in patients carrying high-expression TNFA genotypes suggests its potential utility as a prognostic biomarker. These observations provide further insight into the interplay between chronic inflammation and leukemogenesis and highlight the value of incorporating TNFA polymorphism analysis into future prognostic stratification models for MDS.
