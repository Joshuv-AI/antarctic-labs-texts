AI-driven and advanced sensing technologies for subsurface oil exploration
Authors: Alhamrah Mohannad Firas Ali, Alzharawi Shamil Kamil Khanjar, Aleksandr Aleksandrovich Azeev, Paschal Chimeremeze Chiadighikaobi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySubsurface oil exploration is becoming increasingly challenging due to the complexity of geological formations, growing exploration depths, and the limitations of conventional interpretation methods. A thorough analysis of current developments in artificial intelligence and sophisticated sensing technologies for subsurface oil prospecting is presented. With special emphasis on their uses, advantages, and implementation difficulties, this paper assesses advancements in seismic imaging, electromagnetic surveys, remote sensing, machine learning, and integrated data interpretation frameworks. The results show that while advanced sensing technologies offer higher-resolution subsurface information and lower exploration uncertainty, artificial intelligence greatly increases exploration efficiency by enhancing data processing, pattern recognition, reservoir characterization, and decision-making. Nonetheless, there are lots of issues with data quality, model dependability, scalability, and field deployment. The review offers a thorough reference for researchers and industry practitioners looking to increase exploration accuracy, efficiency, and sustainability. It also emphasizes future research possibilities for intelligent, integrated exploration systems.Keywords: oil exploration, subsoil, deep drilling, technologies in exploration, energy sustainability

Introduction

The formation of oil and gas is a geological process that unfolds over extended periods of our planet’s history.1 The exploration of subsurface hydrocarbon resources remains a fundamental component of global energy strategy, as it directly affects geopolitical stability, economic growth, and energy security. Although there is increasing momentum toward low-carbon energy transitions, crude oil and natural gas still account for a substantial share of the global energy supply, necessitating continued advancement in exploration and production technologies. Optimization of drilling operations remains essential because a large proportion of easily accessible terrestrial hydrocarbon reservoirs have already been.2 In this context, progress in seismology has been closely linked to advancements in instrument-based ground motion measurements.3 The incorporation of machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) into hydrocarbon exploration workflows represents a significant paradigm shift in the field. Recent studies highlight the rapid adoption of both supervised and unsupervised learning techniques for seismic data processing, facies identification, and reservoir property estimation. AI-based approaches improve interpretative accuracy, minimize subjective bias, and enable efficient processing of large, complex, and multi-modal datasets that would otherwise be computationally challenging to analyze using traditional methods.4

In addition to geophysical techniques, geochemical and remote sensing methods have evolved into effective tools for both regional reconnaissance and detailed exploration. Remote sensing technologies, particularly when combined with hyperspectral imaging and geospatial AI (GeoAI), can detect subtle surface and near-surface anomalies linked to hydrocarbon micro-seepage. These approaches complement conventional subsurface methods by supporting early-stage prospect identification while minimizing environmental disturbance.5 Hydrocarbon microseepage analysis, together with ML-based geochemical classification, further enhances exploration capabilities by enabling rapid, objective identification of source rock characteristics and potential hydrocarbon migration pathways from complex geochemical datasets.6 Oil and gas drilling operations face numerous technical challenges, including extreme drilling depths, steeply inclined formations, narrow pressure margins, thick stratigraphic layers, complex multi-pressure regimes, high temperatures, elevated pressures, corrosive environments, and overall drilling complexity. As a result, operational accidents remain relatively common in such demanding conditions.7

Furthermore, the upstream petroleum industry generates vast volumes of data characterized by multiple sources, formats, and structures, posing significant challenges for effective data integration and intelligent application.8 To address these geological and operational challenges, research into key enabling technologies-such as digital twin (DT) well construction, ultra-high-temperature wellbore fluids, automated and intelligent drilling systems, and real-time pre-exploration during drilling-is essential. Advancements in these areas can enhance the safety, efficiency, and reliability of deep and ultra-deep well drilling while supporting the continuous improvement of established technologies.7

Despite technological progress, subsurface oil exploration remains highly uncertain due to the complexity and heterogeneity of geological formations. Deep reservoirs often exhibit strong structural variability, fractured zones, and unpredictable fluid distribution, making accurate characterization difficult even with modern geophysical tools. Studies on deep reservoir exploration highlight that different lithologies and fracture systems require specific imaging and modeling approaches, which limit the effectiveness of standard exploration workflows.9 Another major challenge is the limited resolution of geophysical data and the presence of noise and acquisition artifacts, which can lead to the misinterpretation of seismic attributes and reservoir properties. Even advanced inversion and attribute-analysis methods may produce ambiguous results when the available data are incomplete or affected by environmental conditions.10

Data heterogeneity also creates difficulties for modern exploration systems. Different surveys may use different acquisition parameters, scales, and processing techniques, making it difficult to combine datasets into a consistent geological model. This problem becomes more significant when machine-learning methods are used, since model performance depends strongly on the quality and representativeness of the training data.11 Operational constraints further complicate exploration activities. High acquisition costs, limited accessibility of offshore and deep reservoirs, and safety requirements restrict the amount of data that can be collected, which increases uncertainty during decision-making. Monitoring studies show that maintaining safe and reliable subsurface operations often requires additional sensing systems that increase overall project cost.12 These limitations indicate that conventional exploration methods alone are insufficient for modern hydrocarbon discovery, especially in frontier basins and deep reservoirs where uncertainty is high. The growing complexity of hydrocarbon reservoirs and the economic risks associated with unsuccessful drilling have created a strong need for more accurate and automated exploration technologies. AI, ML, and advanced sensing systems are increasingly used to improve interpretation accuracy, reduce human bias, and enable real-time decision-making. AI applications in petroleum engineering have helped to develop data-driven models that can significantly enhance reservoir prediction, seismic interpretation, and drilling planning when sufficient data are available.13,14

Machine-learning techniques are particularly useful for handling large datasets produced by modern sensing systems, including seismic cubes, well logs, and satellite observations. These methods can identify hidden patterns that are difficult to detect using conventional analysis, but their reliability depends on proper training, validation, and uncertainty assessment. Current research also highlights the importance of integrating multiple technologies within a unified exploration workflow. Combining sensing data with machine-learning models and physics-based simulation improves prediction accuracy and allows more reliable evaluation of complex reservoirs. Such integrated approaches are increasingly considered essential for future exploration systems.15 In addition to AI, advanced sensing technologies such as high-resolution seismic imaging, electromagnetic surveys, and real-time monitoring tools provide more detailed information about subsurface conditions. These technologies enable better mapping of reservoir boundaries, fault systems, and fluid distribution, thereby reducing exploration risk.16 Therefore, the transition toward AI-driven and multi-sensor exploration platforms is widely regarded as a necessary step to meet the technical and economic challenges of modern oil exploration.

Even though research in this field is expanding quickly, the review studies that are currently available are still dispersed and frequently concentrate on general petroleum engineering applications rather than particularly addressing the integration of sophisticated sensing technologies and AI-driven interpretation for subsurface oil exploration. Relatively few studies offer a thorough evaluation of the interactions between sensing systems, ML methods, and intelligent analytical frameworks within contemporary exploration workflows, although many review individual technologies separately. Furthermore, it is challenging to draw broad conclusions about the performance and usability of technologies due to disparities in datasets, evaluation techniques, and geological contexts.

This paper offers a thorough analysis of current developments in AI-driven and sophisticated sensing technologies for subsurface oil prospecting in order to overcome these constraints. The review critically looks at new exploration technologies, assesses the benefits and drawbacks of using AI and ML to interpret exploration data under different geological conditions, and highlights current implementation, uncertainty, cost, and scalability issues. The study further highlights future research directions for the development of intelligent and integrated exploration systems, thereby providing a comprehensive and structured synthesis of the technologies shaping the future of subsurface oil exploration. Only technologies directly associated with subsurface exploration are covered in this review. Economic research, policy analysis, and production optimization were excluded unless they dealt with sensing or AI-based subsurface characterization. The review is limited to studies published between 2021 and the present. Direct numerical comparison was avoided because various datasets and metrics were used in the investigations. The framework provides a structured procedure for identifying, screening, and synthesizing scientific literature using predefined criteria, thereby reducing bias and improving the reliability of review conclusions. Results

Seismic imaging

Seismic imaging techniques are essential for interpreting subsurface geological structures by analyzing how seismic waves propagate and reflect. However, traditional methods often struggle to achieve high resolution due to theoretical limitations and computational costs.17 The accuracy of seismic images depends on the fidelity of the image, which is affected primarily by errors in velocity models used during imaging. To maintain high fidelity, practical evaluation standards should be established during the creation and interpretation of seismic images.18 In survey design, the goal is to select acquisition parameters that allow the collection of the most cost-effective data while meeting required image quality standards.19 Distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) technology is well established for vertical seismic profiling as a receiver array for active-source studies. Yet, its application in surface array studies for reflection seismology and mineral exploration remains limited due to fiber-optic cable sensitivity and cost constraints.20 Compressive sensing (CS) provides an alternative to standard Shannon sampling by capturing equivalent information from fewer measurements. By employing randomized sampling patterns and sparse data representation, CS enables affordable seismic acquisition, improving near-surface mapping and noise suppression. However, the near-surface traps most source energy, resulting in data with high-wavenumber content and amplitudes spanning several orders of magnitude.21

Applications of seismic imaging

ML and deep learning (DL) algorithms can analyze seismic data to identify subtle patterns indicating hydrocarbons or other geological formations. AI systems can assist geoscientists and engineers in selecting prospective drilling sites and optimizing exploration strategies. Additionally, AI can detect anomalies in seismic data, such as faults, fractures, or fluid accumulations, which may signal potential hydrocarbon reservoirs.22 Beyond pattern recognition and anomaly detection, AI enables the fusion of heterogeneous subsurface datasets, including well logs, core measurements, seismic information, and remotely sensed imagery, allowing ML models to uncover complex geological relationships, improve reservoir characterization, reduce interpretation uncertainty, and provide a more comprehensive assessment of subsurface geology and resource potential.23

Advantages of seismic imaging

Seismic imaging offers several advantages over other geophysical methods due to its ability to increase exploration accuracy, reduce costs, and improve decision-making.24 Seismic imaging produces detailed structural images with high spatial resolution, making it suitable for detecting faults, fractures, and stratigraphic features. Advanced migration and inversion techniques, such as reverse-time migration and least-squares imaging, improve accuracy even in complex geological settings.25

The method has strong signal-to-noise improvement capability through advanced acquisition and processing techniques such as CS, stacking, and reconstruction, which enhance imaging quality even when data sampling is irregular.26 Seismic imaging is highly versatile and can be applied in both shallow and deep subsurface investigations, making it useful in petroleum exploration, mineral exploration, and engineering site characterization.27 Seismic imaging can also resolve small-scale geological features when diffraction and full-waveform techniques are used, allowing the identification of fractures, wedges, and discontinuities that are not visible with conventional methods.28

Finally, modern seismic imaging supports integration with well data, borehole surveys, and other geophysical datasets, which improves interpretation accuracy and reduces uncertainty.29

Applications of seismic imaging

Seismic imaging is widely applied across different fields of geoscience and engineering. The most common application is hydrocarbon exploration, where seismic images are used to identify reservoirs, traps, and structural features before drilling. High-resolution depth imaging helps locate complex targets such as subsalt structures and steeply dipping formations.25

In mineral exploration, seismic imaging is used to map ore bodies, faults, and deep geological structures that cannot be detected using shallow geophysical methods. Pre-stack depth migration has shown improved results for irregular and complex targets.27 The technique is also used in geothermal exploration, carbon capture monitoring, and environmental studies, where subsurface imaging helps track fluid movement and reservoir changes.30

Challenges and limitations of seismic imaging

Despite its advantages, seismic imaging has several limitations. Large data volumes and long processing times further increase the difficulty, especially for regional or deep exploration surveys.31

Furthermore, complex geological structures such as salt bodies, steep faults, and anisotropic media remain difficult to image accurately using conventional methods. The technique often requires large acquisition systems and active sources, which may not be practical in urban areas, offshore environments, or restricted locations. Another difficulty is the need for an accurate velocity model. Incorrect velocity estimation leads to migration errors and distorted images, making iterative processing necessary.32 The technique often requires large acquisition systems and active sources, which may not be practical in urban areas, offshore environments, or restricted locations. It is also sensitive to initial conditions, particularly in full-waveform inversion, where poor starting models may cause convergence to incorrect solutions.33

Future research directions in seismic imaging

Several research directions have been aimed at improving seismic imaging performance. One important direction is the development of full-waveform inversion and machine-learning-based imaging, which can produce higher-resolution subsurface models than traditional migration methods.17 Researchers are also focusing on uncertainty quantification and probabilistic imaging, which helps evaluate the reliability of seismic interpretations.34

Improving computational efficiency through parallel computing, neural operators, and compressed sensing is another key research goal, especially for large-scale 3D imaging.26 Finally, future work aims to integrate seismic imaging with other datasets such as well logs, electromagnetic surveys, and borehole data to produce more accurate and reliable subsurface models.29

Electro-magnetic sensing

Electro-Magnetic (EM) sensing involves generating an electromagnetic field from a controlled source and recording the subsurface response to infer conductivity variations. The controlled-source electromagnetic (CSEM) method is widely used for deep and offshore exploration because artificial transmitters offer controlled signal characteristics.35 In addition, natural-source measures such as magneto-tellurics (MT) use Earth’s ambient magnetic field to explore deeper structures, while time-domain electromagnetics (TEM) measures transient decay for subsurface resistivity. Modern advances include high-performance inversion, 3D modeling, and ML-assisted imaging to improve ambiguity resolution and depth sensitivity.36

Applications of electromagnetic sensing

The CSEM method has been widely applied in monitoring fluid distributions and identifying target zones in carbon storage and EOR. EM methods are also increasingly used to monitor CO2 plume evolution in storage formations because resistivity changes track fluid saturation. CSEM surveys have been adapted to multiple resource objectives beyond hydrocarbons, including geothermal reservoirs and lithium/brine targets.35 TEM and MT techniques map conductivity contrasts related to aquifers and contaminant plumes in soil and shallow subsurface. Emerging studies review EM imaging together with DL and numerical modeling, demonstrating applications in multi-scale subsurface characterization.36

Advantages of electro-magnetic sensing

EM methods directly image conductivity variations, allowing the discrimination of fluids and lithological contrasts that many other methods cannot resolve. EM results are often combined with seismic, resistivity, or borehole data to reduce ambiguity and improve the confidence of subsurface models.36

Surface and marine CSEM/MT surveys can cover large areas without invasive drilling, making EM approaches suitable for initial reconnaissance followed by detailed investigations.35

Limitations of electromagnetic sensing

EM methods generally have lower spatial resolution compared with high-frequency seismic imaging due to diffusive wavefields, especially in CSEM/TEM scenarios.36 Different subsurface conductivity distributions may produce similar EM responses, requiring additional constraints for unique imaging. Inverse modeling of EM data is computationally demanding because the physics are nonlinear and require iterative solvers and large forward modeling. Simulation requires good prior knowledge of background conductivity, which is often unavailable and must be estimated. Integrating EM with seismic or resistivity data improves reliability but adds complexity to data fusion workflows.36 Anthropogenic EM noise (e.g., power lines, infrastructure) can contaminate signals, complicating interpretation. Marine and onshore EM acquisition may face logistical issues related to instrumentation, deployment, and environmental conditions.37

Future research on electromagnetic sensing

Future applications include real-time EM monitoring of reservoirs and fluid movement, particularly for CCUS and geothermal projects.35 Emerging work emphasizes using DL to assist inversion and improve imaging resolution and speed. Combining EM with seismic, gravitational, and resistivity data promises to reduce ambiguity and improve model fidelity.36 New methods aim to enhance 3D CSEM modeling and tackle anisotropy and structural complexity.38

Electrical resistivity tomography (ERT)

Electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) involves deploying multiple electrodes on the ground surface or in boreholes, applying a controlled electric current through a pair of electrodes, and measuring potential differences at other electrode pairs. Apparent resistivity data are inverted using numerical algorithms (e.g., least squares) to produce subsurface resistivity models (2D/3D) that reveal geological and hydrogeological features. Because resistivity is sensitive to fluid saturation, porosity, and lithology, ERT can delineate aquifer zones, faults, fracture networks, and buried objects.39

Advanced ERT configurations, with high electrode counts, have been emphasized, and combined interpretation with ground penetrating radar (GPR) or UAV topographic data has been used to improve image resolution and minimize ambiguity.40

Applications of electrical resistivity tomography (ERT)

Geotechnical and geoenvironmental Engineering: ERT is increasingly applied to investigate pavement moisture variation, slope stability, landfill leachate movement, and moisture accumulation in infrastructure materials.39 ERT surveys have been used to characterize landslide internal structures and sliding surfaces by imaging resistivity contrasts within soil and rock masses.41

Combined ERT and GPR approaches have been deployed to detect structural singularities and anomalies in historic foundations and earthen engineering works.40

Abandoned Underground Structures and Cavities: ERT has been employed to identify water-filled tunnels and voids beneath planned expressway alignments, improving safety in infrastructure planning.41

Site Investigation for Construction: Case studies in Nigeria demonstrate ERT for subsurface profiling ahead of heavy structure construction, revealing layer sequences such as lateritic topsoil, sands, and sandstones.42

Advantages of electrical resistivity tomography (ERT)

High sensitivity to lithological and fluid contrasts: ERT directly images variations in resistivity related to pore fluid content, clay zones, and rock type differences. Surveys can also be conducted on the ground surface, along infrastructure corridors, or in environmentally sensitive areas without excavation.39 Advanced multi-electrode systems yield detailed cross-sections of the subsurface useful for engineering, environmental, and hazard studies. ERT data can be combined with GPR and remote sensing to constrain interpretation, especially in complex geological settings.40

Limitations of electrical resistivity tomography (ERT)

Resolution decreases with depth: Near-surface surveys provide high resolution, but deeper imaging often loses detail due to limited current penetration and algorithm stability constraints. Poor contact in dry or rocky terrain can degrade data quality, leading to noisy measurements. Similar resistivity distributions can also arise from multiple subsurface conditions, necessitating additional data (e.g., boreholes or other geophysical surveys) to constrain models.40

High-resolution 3D inversion and time-lapse imaging require significant computational resources and expertise.42 Electrode spacing, array type (Wenner, Schlumberger, dipole-dipole), and survey geometry must be chosen carefully to balance resolution with depth of investigation. ERT inversion is ill-posed and requires regularization and parameter tuning, which can be subjective and influenced by noise and terrain variations. In addition, linking resistivity contrasts to specific geological features demands multidisciplinary knowledge in geology, hydrogeology, and geophysics.40 Combining ERT with complementary surveys (e.g., GPR, seismic, remote sensing) enhances interpretation but adds complexity in data fusion.42

Future research on electrical resistivity tomography (ERT)

Improved joint inversion: Research is focusing on combining ERT with other datasets, such as GPR and seismic, to reduce ambiguity and improve subsurface models.42 Developing robust time-lapse techniques for monitoring dynamic processes (e.g., moisture migration, saturation changes) is an active area of research. Continued progress in multi-electrode arrays and inversion algorithms aims to enable faster, finer-scale 3D imaging for engineering and hazard studies.42

Ground penetrating radar (GPR)

GPR is a non-destructive electromagnetic sensing technique that uses high-frequency radio waves to image subsurface structures. The method is widely applied in geotechnical engineering, archaeology, infrastructure assessment, and environmental investigations because it provides high-resolution information about shallow subsurface conditions without excavation.43

Advantages of GPR

Several studies highlight the strengths of GPR as a subsurface investigation tool. GPR is non-destructive, fast, and capable of producing high-resolution images of shallow subsurface layers, making it suitable for engineering and geological surveys.44 It is also portable and requires less site preparation compared with seismic methods, allowing rapid field deployment.45 According to,46 GPR provides accurate detection of reinforcement bars, moisture ingress, and structural defects in bridges and road decks, improving maintenance planning. In pavement and concrete evaluation, GPR can determine layer thickness, detect voids, and identify reinforcement without damaging the structure.47 GPR also offers high-precision shallow subsurface imaging and strong adaptability to different engineering environments.48

Applications of GPR

GPR has been applied in many fields due to its ability to detect changes in the electromagnetic properties of materials. In civil engineering, it is used for pavement evaluation, concrete inspection, and structural health monitoring.47 Bridge and road inspection studies show that GPR can identify internal deterioration and hidden defects without interrupting traffic.48 In geological and environmental studies, GPR is used to map soil layers, groundwater conditions, and buried objects.43 There is also a growing use of GPR in automated defect detection and subsurface characterization using AI techniques.49

Limitations of GPR

Despite its advantages, several limitations affect GPR performance. Signal penetration is strongly influenced by soil conductivity, with clay and wet soils reducing the depth of investigation.46 High-conductivity materials and heterogeneous ground conditions can cause signal scattering and noise, reducing accuracy. Pavement evaluation also revealed difficulties in interpreting signals and limitations in accuracy under complex field conditions.47 Equipment cost and the need for multiple antenna frequencies can make surveys expensive. Another limitation is the trade-off between penetration depth and resolution, where deeper penetration reduces image detail.46 GPR surveys require technical expertise in both data acquisition and interpretation. Radargram interpretation is not intuitive and requires trained specialists to avoid errors (GPR overview). Complex subsurface conditions can produce irregular signals that are difficult to analyze using conventional methods. It is also emphasized that heterogeneous soil conditions introduce clutter and noise that complicate inversion and imaging processes.50 Field surveys may also require advanced processing software and calibration to obtain reliable. Because of these factors, GPR surveys often require experienced operators and post-processing specialists.48

Future research directions

Studies show that future research focuses on improving the accuracy, automation, and depth capability of GPR. AI and DL are being developed to automate interpretation and reduce human error.49 Studies on pavement and infrastructure monitoring suggest integrating GPR with other sensing methods to improve reliability.47 The study49 also recommends developing multi-frequency antennas and advanced signal processing to overcome penetration and resolution limits. Future work is expected to expand the use of GPR in smart infrastructure monitoring, robotics, and large-scale automated surveys.45

Carbon capture and storage (CCS)

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a technology used to reduce carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions by capturing CO2 from industrial sources, transporting it, and storing it in deep geological formations such as saline aquifers, depleted oil and gas reservoirs, and coal seams. Because CCS relies on safe subsurface storage, geophysical monitoring, reservoir characterization, and remote sensing techniques are required to ensure long-term containment and stability of injected CO2.51

Advantages of CCS

Several studies identify CCS as one of the most effective technologies for reducing greenhouse gas emissions from large industrial sources. CCS allows a significant reduction of CO2 emissions while enabling continued operation of fossil-fuel-based power plants and heavy industries.52 Geological formations such as deep saline aquifers and depleted reservoirs can store large volumes of CO2 for long periods, making CCS suitable for large-scale climate mitigation.53 CCS can be integrated with existing oil and gas infrastructure, reducing development cost and improving feasibility.18 Monitoring technologies such as seismic, gravity, and geochemical sensing can track CO2 plume migration, improving storage safety.54 CCS is also considered essential for achieving global carbon-neutrality targets, especially in hard-to-decarbonize sectors such as cement, steel, and petrochemical industries.55

Applications of CCS

CCS has wide applications in energy, environmental engineering, and subsurface investigations. It is widely used in power plants, cement production, and oil and gas industries to capture CO2 before release into the atmosphere.53 In petroleum engineering, injected CO2 is used for EOR while simultaneously storing the gas underground.18 Geological storage studies use CCS to evaluate reservoir properties, sealing capacity, and fault stability to ensure safe long-term storage.52 CCS is also being explored for negative-emission technologies and environmental protection strategies.55

It has been demonstrated that AI has significantly improved the accuracy of subsurface characterization, lithology identification, fracture detection, and CO2 storage evaluation, particularly in complex reservoirs where conventional geophysical interpretation remains uncertain. For example,56 developed an intelligent characterization framework for fractured reservoirs to secure CO2 storage by identifying critical natural fracture pathways that may control leakage risk. Their results showed that ML-based fracture detection can significantly enhance the reliability of reservoir integrity assessment, which is essential for safe CCS deployment in fractured formations.

Similarly,57 introduced a large-scale, high-quality dataset for lithology identification designed to support DL applications in subsurface interpretation. The study demonstrated that training convolutional neural networks on large, well-labeled geological datasets substantially improves lithological classification accuracy, thereby reducing interpretation uncertainty in seismic and well-log analysis. This finding highlights the importance of data quality and dataset scale in achieving reliable AI-based subsurface prediction.

The study58 re-evaluated the CO2 storage capacity of depleted fractured-vuggy carbonate reservoirs using advanced reservoir modeling and intelligent analysis methods. Their results indicated that traditional estimation techniques may significantly underestimate storage capacity when fracture networks and vuggy structures are not properly characterized, emphasizing the need for intelligent reservoir modeling approaches that integrate geological, seismic, and petrophysical data.

In the context of lithology recognition,59 proposed an intelligent lithology identification system based on digital core images and convolutional neural networks. The model achieved high classification accuracy for continental shale formations, demonstrating that DL can extract complex textural features that are difficult to identify using conventional manual interpretation. This approach provides a reliable tool for improving subsurface imaging resolution and reducing uncertainty in unconventional reservoirs.

Beyond storage capacity estimation,56 investigated underground storage performance and economic prediction for catalyzed green methanol systems using intelligent evaluation models. Their work showed that AI-assisted reservoir performance prediction can improve both technical feasibility assessment and economic decision-making, which is critical for large-scale subsurface energy storage and CO2 utilization projects.

Limitations of CCS

Despite its potential, CCS faces several technical and economic limitations. The high cost of capture, transport, and storage remains one of the major barriers to large-scale deployment. Even in demonstration projects, cost projections often underestimate real subsurface challenges (e.g., re-engineering, monitoring requirements), leading to overruns and canceled investments.60 Long-term storage safety must be ensured, as leakage from wells or faults may reduce effectiveness and pose environmental risks.52 Suitable geological formations are not available in all regions, limiting the applicability of CCS projects.18

Monitoring CO2 movement underground requires advanced geophysical techniques, increasing operational complexity.61 Wellbore integrity, cement degradation, and chemical reactions in reservoirs can affect long-term storage stability.62 One of the core barriers for CCS deployment is injectivity limits: CO2 can only be injected into a geological formation at rates that avoid exceeding fracture pressure, avoiding caprock failure or unintended migration pathways. These limits depend on reservoir permeability, wellbore design, thermal stress, and local pressure regimes, which vary widely by site and complicate scaled deployment.63 Accurate subsurface characterization requires seismic, electromagnetic, and reservoir modeling before CO2 injection.18

Predicting CO2 flow in porous rocks is difficult because of uncertainties in permeability, pressure, and geochemical reactions. Long-term containment depends on managing pressure buildup, fault activation, and chemical interactions that affect porosity and injectivity for geo–mechanical and geochemical stability.52 Long-term leakage risk–whether through caprock, faults, or legacy wells–continues to be a serious concern and must be evaluated on a site-by-site basis using techniques such as seismic monitoring, fluid sampling, and advanced technologies such as 4D seismic monitoring and satellite imaging to allow for real-time tracking of CO2 plume migration and early detection of leaks.54,64 Geophysical monitoring generates complex datasets that require advanced inversion and simulation methods.61 There is a need for computational modeling and numerical simulation to evaluate storage performance and risk.18 According to,60 CCS faces technological, economic, and strategic limitations. Many projects, including Petra Nova, In Salah, and Gorgon, underperform due to engineering challenges and regulatory oversights. Even “successful” projects often fail to meet rigorous climate mitigation standards, suggesting CCS may be ill-suited as a core climate solution under current timelines.

Regulatory frameworks

Without mandatory baseline datasets and robust post-closure monitoring frameworks, detecting and attributing leakage to a specific injection operation is difficult, creating potential liability and environmental risk. In many countries, permitting relies on modeled compliance rather than empirical validation, and ongoing responsibility for long-term storage can remain unclear, deterring investment.60 Regulatory gaps are often exacerbated in developing regions, where institutional capacity to manage complex subsurface projects such as CCS is limited.65

Future research directions

CCS research is moving toward improved monitoring, modeling, and cost reduction. AI and machine-learning models are being developed to simulate CO2 plume migration and optimize storage site selection digital-twin and real-time monitoring systems are being explored to improve decision-making during CO2 injection.66 Advanced geophysical monitoring techniques such as seismic, gravity, and geochemical sensing are being developed for better leak detection.61 Future work also focuses on improving well integrity, cement durability, and reservoir sealing mechanisms.62 Integration of CCS with renewable energy and negative-emission technologies for large-scale climate mitigation is recommended.53

Remote sensing technology

Remote sensing is a non-contact geophysical and geospatial technology that acquires information about the Earth’s surface and near-surface using satellites, aerial sensors, radar, LiDAR, and unmanned aerial vehicles. It has become an important tool in geotechnical investigation, infrastructure monitoring, environmental assessment, and subsurface studies due to its ability to collect large-scale data efficiently and repeatedly. Remote sensing provides high-quality spatial information that supports decision-making in engineering, environmental, and geological applications.67,68,69

Advantages of remote sensing

Remote sensing offers several advantages compared with conventional field-based investigation methods. One major benefit is its ability to cover large and inaccessible areas quickly while maintaining acceptable accuracy. Satellite- and UAV-based sensors allow continuous monitoring of infrastructure, terrain deformation, and environmental changes without direct contact with the ground.68

Another advantage is the capability to obtain multi-temporal and multi-spectral data, which improves the detection of changes in soil conditions, land stability, and structural behavior. Integration with AI and ML further enhances data processing efficiency and interpretation accuracy.69

Remote sensing is also considered cost-effective for large-scale investigations because a single dataset can be used for multiple analyses, including land classification, deformation monitoring, and environmental evaluation.70 Additionally, remote sensing techniques can operate in hazardous or restricted environments, reducing risks to personnel during site investigation and monitoring. Finally, the combination of different sensors such as GNSS, SAR, LiDAR, and optical imagery improves reliability because the strengths of one method can compensate for the limitations of another.68

Applications of remote sensing

Remote sensing has been widely applied in geotechnical engineering, environmental monitoring, and subsurface investigation. In infrastructure engineering, it is used to monitor bridges, dams, roads, railways, and buildings for deformation, settlement, and structural damage.68

Environmental and resource management also rely on remote sensing for monitoring vegetation, water bodies, climate change, and urban development, making it a key technology for sustainable planning.69 In civil engineering investigations, electromagnetic-based remote sensing techniques are used to measure distance, displacement, and structural movement for health monitoring of engineering structures.71 Furthermore, remote sensing is increasingly used in disaster management for rapid assessment after earthquakes, floods, and landslides because it can provide near-real-time information over large areas.68 In geological and geotechnical studies, remote sensing supports landslide detection, soil erosion monitoring, terrain mapping, and land stability assessment, providing essential data for site selection and hazard analysis.70,72

Limitations of remote sensing

Despite its advantages, remote sensing has several limitations that restrict its effectiveness in subsurface and geotechnical investigations. One major limitation is the restricted depth penetration, as most remote sensing techniques primarily capture surface or near-surface information, making it difficult to directly detect deep underground features. Spatial and temporal resolution constraints can also reduce accuracy, especially when small defects or localized subsurface anomalies must be identified. In addition, remote sensing data often require ground verification (ground-truthing) to confirm results, increasing the time and effort required for accurate investigation.73 Atmospheric conditions such as clouds, haze, and dust may distort satellite images and reduce data quality, which affects interpretation reliability. Another limitation is the high cost of high-resolution sensors, data processing software, and skilled personnel required for analysis, which can limit its use in developing regions.67 The use of remote sensing in subsurface and structural investigation involves several technical difficulties. Data interpretation is complex because similar materials may produce similar spectral signatures, leading to the misclassification of geological or structural features.73 Another difficulty is the need for advanced algorithms and large datasets to process high-resolution imagery, especially when ML or DL methods are used.69 Multi-sensor integration can improve accuracy but also increases computational complexity and requires specialized expertise in geospatial analysis. Furthermore, the limited temporal resolution of some satellite systems means that rapid changes in ground conditions may not be captured at the required time interval.68 Finally, the lack of standardized processing methods across different sensors and datasets makes comparison of results difficult in research and engineering practice.18

Future research of remote sensing

Future research in remote sensing is focused on improving spatial resolution, depth detection capability, and automated interpretation. The integration of AI and DL is expected to significantly enhance feature extraction and anomaly detection in complex datasets.69 Researchers are also developing multi-sensor fusion techniques that combine SAR, LiDAR, optical, and GNSS data to overcome the limitations of individual methods and improve accuracy in infrastructure and subsurface monitoring.68 Another important direction is the development of high-resolution and high-frequency satellite systems that can provide more detailed and timely data for engineering applications.18 Future studies are also expected to focus on improving subsurface detection capability by integrating remote sensing with other geophysical methods such as GPR, seismic, and electromagnetic techniques.70 Finally, the use of cloud computing and big-data platforms will allow faster processing of large remote sensing datasets, making real-time monitoring more feasible in engineering and environmental applications.69

As a result, state-of-the-art ML techniques have increasingly been adopted to enhance reservoir characterization, fluid identification, facies classification, and production forecasting.

Discussion

Cost considerations

Cost was identified as a major limiting factor across many of the reviewed studies. Due to the requirement for dense receiver layouts, regulated sources, and computationally demanding migration and inversion techniques, high-resolution seismic imaging was often linked to expensive collection and processing costs.25,26 Studies on electromagnetic sensing also reported high costs, particularly for controlled-source EM surveys in offshore or deep exploration environments where specialized transmitters and receivers are required.36,37 For shallow examinations, ERT and GPR were typically considered to be more cost-effective; nevertheless, several studies found that costs rise when extended survey profiles or high-resolution 3D images are needed.40,49 Although high-resolution satellite imagery, LiDAR, and sophisticated processing software may still demand substantial financial resources, remote sensing research has highlighted that huge areas can be examined at comparatively minimal field cost.68,69 Cost has frequently been cited as one of the primary implementation obstacles in CCS applications since monitoring must continue throughout injection and even after storage operations, raising project costs overall.51,55 Therefore, the analyzed studies indicate that the choice of a single sensing method or the integration of many techniques is frequently determined by economic considerations.

Scalability and applicability to large-scale investigations

The reviewed literature shows clear differences in scalability among sensing technologies. Seismic imaging was frequently described as suitable for regional-scale exploration due to its ability to provide deep penetration and continuous structural information, although several studies emphasized that large survey size leads to increased data volume and processing time.26 Electromagnetic methods, particularly magnetotellurics, were also reported to be effective for large-scale investigations, but the analyzed papers noted that spatial resolution decreases with depth.37

In contrast, most ERT and GPR studies focused on site-scale investigations, including engineering surveys, groundwater mapping, and infrastructure assessment, because these methods require dense electrode or antenna spacing that limits their efficiency for large areas.40,42,48 Remote sensing studies showed the highest scalability, as satellite and UAV systems allow repeated coverage of extensive regions; however, several authors emphasized that remote sensing alone cannot provide detailed subsurface information and must be combined with ground-based methods.67,69 In CCS-related studies, scalability was often constrained by the availability of suitable geological formations and by the requirement for long-term monitoring systems, rather than by the sensing technology itself.18,51

Uncertainty and non-uniqueness of interpretation

Uncertainty was a recurring theme in nearly all of the reviewed studies. Seismic imaging papers reported that errors in velocity models may lead to incorrect structural interpretation, especially in complex geological settings such as salt bodies or fractured reservoirs.25,32 Electromagnetic and resistivity studies similarly noted that different conductivity distributions can produce similar responses, requiring additional constraints to obtain reliable subsurface models.36,39 GPR studies highlighted that signal attenuation and scattering in conductive or heterogeneous soils can reduce interpretation accuracy, making data processing and calibration essential.48,50 Remote sensing investigations also reported uncertainty related to spectral similarity between different materials, which often requires ground verification to confirm interpretations.67 In CCS monitoring, uncertainty was considered particularly critical because incorrect prediction of CO2 migration may lead to leakage risks. Several studies emphasized the need for the integration of seismic, electromagnetic, and reservoir simulation data to improve confidence in storage models.51,53,60 Overall, the reviewed literature consistently recommends joint inversion and multi-sensor integration as effective approaches for reducing uncertainty, although these methods increase computational complexity.18,36

Field deployment constraints

Field deployment limitations were reported in many of the analyzed studies. Seismic surveys were often described as requiring large equipment, controlled sources, and extensive receiver arrays, which may not be practical in urban areas, offshore environments, or environmentally sensitive locations.26 Electromagnetic surveys were reported to be sensitive to cultural noise from infrastructure such as power lines and pipelines, which can contaminate signals and complicate interpretation.37 ERT studies frequently mentioned difficulties related to electrode contact, particularly in dry or rocky terrain, while GPR studies reported limited penetration depth in clay-rich or water-saturated soils.40,48 Remote sensing studies indicated fewer field constraints but noted that atmospheric conditions, sensor resolution, and data availability may affect reliability.67 In CCS monitoring, deployment challenges were reported to be more complex because monitoring systems must operate continuously over long periods and under harsh subsurface conditions, requiring reliable instrumentation and maintenance.18,60

Regulatory and safety requirements

Several reviewed studies emphasized that regulatory requirements strongly influence subsurface investigations, particularly in CCS projects. Long-term CO2 storage requires continuous monitoring, risk assessment, and verification to comply with environmental regulations.51 Seismic and electromagnetic surveys may also require permits due to environmental impact, land-access restrictions, or safety concerns related to active sources.18

Remote sensing studies reported fewer regulatory limitations, although some authors noted that airspace restrictions, data access regulations, and privacy concerns may affect the use of UAV and satellite systems.67 Overall, the literature indicates that regulatory requirements often determine monitoring frequency, data quality standards, and acceptable survey methods.54

Monitoring difficulty and data complexity

The reviewed studies consistently reported increasing monitoring difficulty due to the large volume and complexity of geophysical data. Advanced seismic, EM, and ERT imaging require iterative inversion, numerical modeling, and high computational resources.34,42 GPR and remote sensing studies also noted that high-resolution datasets require advanced processing software and often benefit from machine-learning-based interpretation.68 In CCS monitoring, the difficulty is greater because multiple sensing techniques must be integrated to track plume migration, pressure changes, and reservoir stability over long periods.53,60 Several studies therefore recommend the use of AI, high-performance computing, and joint inversion to improve interpretation reliability while managing large datasets.36,65

Limitations of current subsurface imaging and monitoring technologies

The reviewed studies consistently report that, despite significant progress in subsurface imaging and monitoring, current technologies still present important limitations related to sensor cost, data availability, uncertainty in deep reservoirs, reliability of AI methods, and risks associated with CCS. Evaluation of the included literature shows that these limitations affect both the accuracy of subsurface characterization and the feasibility of large-scale deployment, particularly in complex geological environments.

Expensive sensors and acquisition systems

Many of the reviewed studies identify the high cost of sensing equipment as a major limitation, especially for seismic and electromagnetic surveys. High-resolution seismic imaging requires controlled sources, dense receiver arrays, and advanced processing algorithms, all of which increase acquisition and operational expenses.25,26 CSEM surveys also require specialized transmitters, receivers, and inversion workflows, making them costly for offshore and deep exploration applications.35,36

Although ERT and GPR are generally less expensive, several studies note that costs increase significantly when high-density electrode configurations, multi-frequency antennas, or 3-D imaging are required.39,48 Remote sensing can reduce field costs, but high-resolution satellite imagery, LiDAR, and advanced processing software remain expensive and may limit use in developing regions.67,68

In CCS projects, cost is even more critical because monitoring systems must operate over long periods, increasing the overall expense of storage operations.51,54 The reviewed studies, therefore, indicate that economic constraints often restrict the use of high-resolution or multi-method monitoring systems.

Limited data availability and coverage

Several reviewed papers emphasize that subsurface imaging is often constrained by limited data availability, particularly in areas where access is restricted or survey acquisition is difficult. Seismic surveys may be limited by environmental conditions, land access, or logistical constraints, leading to incomplete coverage and reduced image quality.26 Electromagnetic and resistivity surveys may also be affected by terrain conditions, poor electrode contact, or cultural noise, which reduces data reliability.37,39 Remote sensing studies report that satellite data may have limited spatial or temporal resolution, making it difficult to detect small or rapidly changing features.67 GPR investigations are often limited by soil conductivity and signal attenuation, which restrict the depth of penetration and reduce the amount of usable data.48 In CCS monitoring, limited data coverage is particularly problematic because accurate prediction of CO2 plume migration requires continuous and long-term observations. Several studies note that incomplete monitoring data increases uncertainty in reservoir models and may reduce confidence in storage safety.53,60

Uncertainty in deep reservoir characterization

Uncertainty in deep subsurface conditions is a major limitation reported across the reviewed studies. Seismic imaging may produce inaccurate results when velocity models are uncertain, especially in complex geological settings such as salt bodies or fractured reservoirs.25,32 Electromagnetic methods also suffer from non-uniqueness, since different conductivity distributions can produce similar responses, making interpretation difficult without additional constraints.36 ERT and GPR are particularly affected by depth limitations, as resolution decreases with increasing depth, reducing their effectiveness for deep reservoir studies.39,48 Remote sensing techniques mainly provide surface or near-surface information and therefore cannot directly resolve deep geological structures.67

In CCS applications, uncertainty in deep reservoir properties is considered a critical risk because incorrect estimation of permeability, pressure, or sealing capacity may lead to leakage or storage failure.18,51 The reviewed literature consistently recommends combining multiple geophysical methods and numerical simulations to reduce these uncertainties.

Reliability and limitations of artificial intelligence methods

There is an increasing use of ML and DL in subsurface imaging, but the reviewed literature also highlights limitations in the reliability of these methods. AI models depend strongly on training data, and insufficient or biased datasets may lead to inaccurate predictions.36 Several studies report that ML algorithms may perform well on synthetic or controlled datasets but show reduced accuracy when applied to real field data with noise and heterogeneity.68

In seismic imaging, AI-based interpretation can accelerate processing but may introduce errors if the model does not correctly represent the geological conditions.26 Similar concerns are reported in remote sensing and GPR studies, where automated classification may misidentify features without proper validation.67

In CCS monitoring, AI and numerical simulation are increasingly used to predict CO2 plume migration, but several studies emphasize that these models must be validated with field data to ensure reliability.60,65 The reviewed literature, therefore, suggests that AI should be used as a supporting tool rather than a replacement for physical modeling and expert interpretation.

Risks associated with carbon capture and storage (CCS)

The reviewed studies identify CCS as a promising technology for emission reduction, but they also emphasize several risks related to long-term storage. One major concern is the possibility of CO2 leakage through faults, fractures, or abandoned wells, which may reduce storage effectiveness and create environmental hazards.51 Chemical reactions between CO2, reservoir rocks, and well materials may also affect sealing integrity over time.61

Monitoring CCS reservoirs is technically challenging because plume migration must be tracked over long periods using multiple sensing methods, including seismic, electromagnetic, and geochemical techniques.53 Several studies note that long-term monitoring increases project cost and requires strict regulatory compliance.54

In addition, not all regions have suitable geological formations for safe storage, which limits the global applicability of CCS.18 The reviewed literature, therefore, concludes that although CCS is essential for climate mitigation, its implementation requires careful site selection, continuous monitoring, and integration of multiple geophysical methods to minimize risk.

Evolution of subsurface technology

The development of advanced subsoil technologies has progressed from isolated geophysical methods to integrated; intelligent systems capable of predictive subsurface characterization. Before the 2000s, exploration relied on classical techniques such as seismic, electrical resistivity, gravity, and magnetic surveys, which were applied independently and required manual interpretation, often resulting in high uncertainty.87,88

Between 2000 and 2015, the field shifted toward multi-method integration, with joint inversion and data fusion improving subsurface imaging by combining complementary datasets.89,90,91,92 From 2015 to 2020, advances in high-resolution imaging and remote sensing, including full waveform inversion and DAS, enabled more detailed and large-scale subsurface mapping.93 Since 2020, AI and ML have transformed subsurface exploration by enabling automated interpretation and predictive modeling of complex geological features. More recently, intelligent integrated systems incorporating physics-informed ML (PIML) and DTs have emerged, improving real-time analysis and decision-making.55,94 This evolution highlights a clear transition toward more accurate, efficient, and predictive subsurface characterization and is visualized in Figure 2.

Physics-informed machine learning (PIML)

PIML-where ML models incorporate governing physical laws (e.g., fluid flow and mass balance PDEs)-is rapidly advancing reservoir modeling and prediction. Techniques such as physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) or physics-informed neural operators can significantly accelerate simulation and history-matching tasks while respecting reservoir physics. The study95 has presented a PINN with domain decomposition to handle sparse production data and large-scale reservoir systems, improving simulation accuracy even with limited real data. This approach vastly outperforms traditional data-driven models by embedding the physical constraints directly into the learning process. PIML’s broader role in subsurface energy systems, including reservoir simulation, seismic interpolation, and hydrocarbon production forecasting, underscores its potential to transform subsurface oil applications by enhancing generalization, interpretability, and physical consistency compared to purely data-driven models.

Subsurface applications of PIML

Convolutional recurrent PIML surrogate models have been developed that learn reservoir state evolution (pressure, saturation) from initial states and well controls by minimizing residuals of governing state equations. The ConvLSTM is linked to the state space equations, enabling the incorporation of a discrete-time sequence of well control. The model requires initial state conditions and a sequence of well controls as inputs, and predicts the state variables of the system, such as pressure, as outputs. By minimizing the residuals of reservoir flow state-space equations, the network is trained without the need for labeled data. The model is designed to serve as a surrogate model for predicting future reservoir states based on the initial reservoir state and input engineering controls. Boundary conditions are enforced in the state-space equations, so no additional loss term is needed.14 The study96 revealed that managing subsurface reservoir pressures is critical for CO2 sequestration and wastewater injection but is challenged by heterogeneity and uncertainty. Using PIML with the DPFEHM framework, convolutional neural networks were used to predict extraction rates, enabling near real-time, accurate simulations. The approach achieves robust uncertainty quantification and operates 400,000 times faster than traditional models.

Differentiable multiphase flow models combined with CNNs directly embed the physics solver into a trainable model using automatic differentiation, enabling gradients for optimization. Such frameworks enable surrogate models that capture reservoir behavior with far fewer training simulations than conventional data-driven models.97 A PIML framework using convolutional neural networks provides an accurate, differentiable surrogate model, enabling fast optimization of thermal recovery for Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) management, which is challenged by reservoir heterogeneity and coupled multi-physics. Sensitivity analyses show injection rate dominates performance, achieving near real-time management with 4,564× speedup over conventional simulators.98

Benefits of PIML in subsurface applications

Physics constraints drastically reduce the amount of data needed, enabling high-fidelity modeling with sparse field measurements.99 By embedding conservation laws, PIML ensures predictions respect mass, momentum, and energy balances, avoiding unphysical results.15 Surrogate models can drastically shorten simulation time compared to full reservoir simulators, making them suitable for fast decision-making and optimization loops.100 PIML can estimate reservoir parameters (e.g., permeability, storage) from limited observations, providing useful tools for characterization and uncertainty quantification.15 Opportunities to make use of PIML exist in the ability to: develop high-fidelity PIML surrogates for coupled reservoir flow, geomechanics, and thermal processes, integrate PIML into real-time decision analytics for field optimization (e.g., EOR strategy evaluation), and combine explainable AI techniques with physics constraints to improve trust and operational adoption.

Digital twins and real-time monitoring of reservoirs

DT technology has surfaced as a revolutionary idea that could have a big impact on how industrial systems and contemporary society grow in the future. A DT is a complete system-of-systems that replicates the structure and behavior of a physical entity in a virtual environment, in contrast to traditional computer-based modeling and simulation techniques. It entails the meticulous digital replication of a physical system’s parts, operations, procedures, and control systems. In order to guarantee synchronization of inputs, states, and responses, the physical asset and its digital representation function concurrently, constantly exchanging data via real-time communication networks. The digital/virtual counterpart of the real-world system can offer smooth monitoring, analysis, evaluation, and forecasting by integrating the Internet of Things (IoT), AI, 3D models, next-generation mobile communications (5G/6G), augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), distributed computing, Transfer Learning (TL), and electronic sensors.101

Applications in subsoil exploration

In order to mimic, replicate, and forecast subsurface behavior, DTs are dynamic digital copies of physical systems that continuously consume real-time data. Reservoir DTs in the oil and gas industry can combine continuously collected sensor streams (pressure, flow, and injection data) with physics-based reservoir simulation to provide real-time forecasting, anomaly forecasting, and optimization. Using sensor data streams, DTs generate real-time model updates that are crucial for reservoir management’s virtual scenario testing and ongoing monitoring.102

DT can bridge the gaps between seemingly distinct fields such as geophysics and reservoir engineering, by integrating diverse monitoring data, e.g., geophysical well observations and imaged seismic data. In addition, the recent advancements in generative AI also facilitate DT with principled uncertainty quantification. Through recursive training and inference, DT utilizes simulated current state samples, e.g., CO2 saturation, paired with corresponding geophysical field observations to train its neural networks and enable posterior sampling upon receiving new field data. However, it lacks decision-making and control capabilities, which are necessary for full DT functionality.66

Building real-time data pipelines from downhole sensors, surface facilities, and well instrumentation into DT platforms, implementing live calibration and uncertainty quantification modules that adjust the twin as conditions evolve, and extending DTs to integrate reservoir performance with surface facilities (Integrated Asset Models), will provide a clear path for further applications of DT in the future.

Integrated sensing and data assimilation for subsurface fields

Uncertainties in model parameters, initial and boundary conditions, atmospheric forcings, and biophysical processes all have an impact on simulations using these models. Uncertainty can be measured and decreased by data assimilation (DA). For single-compartment models, this has been thoroughly validated; however, integrated versions with numerous compartments have received significantly less testing.103 DA enhances the initial estimates for hydrological and atmospheric modeling by providing significant opportunities for uncertainty identification.104

An inventive smart-liner system that combines distributed fiber optic sensors with polymer composite liners to improve the efficiency and safety of oil and gas transportation and storage systems. The technology accurately depicts the state of the infrastructure by continuously collecting data on everything from the glue curing process to mechanical deformations and damage over the course of the infrastructure’s lifetime. The viability of the smart-liner is confirmed by experimental testing carried out on a steel substrate, which also demonstrates the efficacy of the developed data analysis and visualization tools.105 Robust sensor fusion algorithms that harmonize various subsurface measurements (logs, seismic, fiber optics), employ ensemble and Bayesian techniques to control model uncertainty and dynamically update reservoir state estimates, and use sensing data to calibrate and validate DT and PIML frameworks for high-confidence operational insights would all be advantageous.105

Conclusion

This review looked at recent developments in advanced and AI-based sensing technologies for subsurface oil exploration, including how these technologies improve subsurface characterization, how AI improves interpretation, what limitations still exist under complex geological conditions, and what obstacles affect real-field application. Seismic imaging, electromagnetic sensing, ERT, GPR, remote sensing, and AI-based analysis were among the exploration-stage techniques covered in the examined literature. In addition to addressing industry demands such as lowering drilling risk, cutting costs, enhancing decision reliability, and guaranteeing safe monitoring for applications such as CCS, these technologies seek to address important scientific issues such as precise imaging of complex structures, prediction of reservoir properties, and reduction of uncertainty in deep formations.

The results demonstrate that no single technique can adequately characterize complicated subsurface formations, and each methodology has advantages and disadvantages. Although seismic techniques offer excellent structural resolution, they are costly to acquire and require precise velocity models. Although they offer less resolution and ambiguity in interpretation, electromagnetic and resistivity techniques are sensitive to lithology and fluids. Deep reservoirs cannot be directly imaged by remote sensing, while shallow techniques like GPR are depth-limited. AI increases the effectiveness of interpretation, yet it heavily relies on data validation and quality. Due to these restrictions, multi-sensor integration and hybrid modeling techniques are being used more frequently.

High survey costs, sparse data coverage, deep reservoir imaging uncertainty, and unreliability in AI forecasts are just a few of the problems that still need to be overcome. Long-term, high-accuracy sensing devices are also needed for CCS monitoring, although they are still being developed. In order to create more accurate subsurface models while preserving operational safety and economic viability in actual field conditions, current research should consistently concentrate on integrating multi-sensor acquisition, numerical modeling, and AI within unified exploration frameworks. ML, joint inversion, distributed sensing, and real-time monitoring are promising avenues; nevertheless, uncertainty quantification, big datasets, and economical field validation require more study. Future research will therefore rely on clever, integrated procedures that connect scientific modeling to real-world business needs.

Recommendations

In order to lower uncertainty in complex reservoirs, future subsurface exploration research should prioritize the integration of several geophysical and computational techniques. Combining seismic, electromagnetic, resistivity, and remote sensing data with AI-based interpretation can increase accuracy and dependability because single-technique approaches are frequently insufficient, especially in heterogeneous and deep formations. In challenging geological circumstances, multi-source data fusion lowers ambiguity and improves subsurface structure characterization. To improve forecast capabilities in fractured reservoirs, deep formations, and CCS applications-where traditional approaches are still uncertain-developments in PIML, DT technologies, and linked inversion models are also necessary. Additionally, to guarantee that machine-learning models retain reliability when applied to actual field data rather than controlled datasets, standardized datasets, robust validation processes, and repeatable workflows are required. In order to support continuous subsurface monitoring while reducing the cost of deep and offshore exploration, future work should also concentrate on scalable sensors, fiber-optic monitoring, satellite observation, and automated inversion techniques. To ensure practical implementation, there should be greater cooperation between academia, industry, and regulatory bodies.

Limitations of the study

This study highlights key subsurface characterization limitations (2021–2025). Methodologically, extreme dataset heterogeneity prevents statistical meta-analysis, restricting findings to qualitative synthesis. With respect to Geophysics, single-sensing systems face significant limitations: GPR and remote sensing have trouble with depth penetration and atmospheric interference; electromagnetic and electrical resistivity methods lose spatial resolution at depth and are vulnerable to anthropogenic or fluid ambiguity; seismic imaging is non-unique and has high computational costs. Traditional AI models (ANN, RF, KNN, SVM) lack physical consistency, run the danger of overfitting on short datasets, and function as a “black box.” As a result, multi-sensor data integration and PIML are highly favored.
