Monitoring of dual-drug combination therapy in pediatric epilepsy patients: a machine learning model for simultaneous VPA-LEV concentration-dose prediction
Authors: Yue-Wen Chen, Si Chen, Yi-Wei Xie, He Zou, Xin Rao, Jian-Wen Xu, Wei Wu, Zhou-Jie Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo better meet the therapeutic demand in pediatric epilepsy patients (PEPs) receiving combined valproic acid (VPA) and levetiracetam (LEV) (therapeutic ranges: VPA 50–100 μg/mL; LEV 6–20 μg/mL.), We developed a dual-predictive machine learning (ML) model that integrates concentration monitoring with dose recommendation capabilities, serving as an adjunct tool for therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM).MethodsA retrospectively collected dataset comprising 497 paired concentration samples from 402 PEPs was used to train (i) classification models that predict adequacy of VPA and LEV concentrations and (ii) regression models that recommend individualised daily doses. Model explainability was interrogated with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis to delineate concentration-dose covariates.ResultsAmong nine nonlinear ML algorithms, the Extra-Trees classifier achieved optimal performance for concentration adequacy prediction, delivering test-set accuracies and AUCs of 0.74 and 0.75 for VPA, and 0.75 and 0.75 for LEV, respectively. SHAP analysis elucidated body weight-normalized daily dose, blood urea nitrogen/creatinine (BUN/CREA) ratio, and platelet count (PLT) were identified as shared critical covariates. Serum uric acid (UA) exhibited LEV-specific positive regulation (SHAP rank 2, contribution 16.2%). For dose recommendation, an XGBoost multi-output regressor yielded test-set R2 values of 0.60 for both VPA and LEV daily dose. Body weight dominated dose predictions (VPA 70.2%; LEV 62.8%), followed by respective trough concentrations (VPA 7.1%; LEV 14.7%).ConclusionThis dual-prediction model enables simultaneous concentration monitoring and dose recommendation of VPA and LEV, offering a data-driven decision-support tool for personalised therapy in PEPs.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08063-8.

Introduction

Epilepsy, one of the most prevalent chronic neurological disorders in children, is characterized by recurrent seizures that induce progressive neurological deficits and is strongly associated with cognitive impairment and psychiatric comorbidities, significantly compromising long-term outcomes [1]. Although novel antiseizure medications (ASMs) have continued to emerge, approximately 30% of patients remain refractory to monotherapy, necessitating polytherapy for seizure control [2]. Valproic acid (VPA) and levetiracetam (LEV) have become one of the principal combination regimens for the long-term maintenance treatment of refractory pediatric epilepsy, owing to their complementary mechanisms of action (VPA enhances GABAergic transmission, while LEV modulates synaptic vesicle glycoprotein 2A [SV2A]) and minimal pharmacokinetic interactions [3, 4]. However, their different metabolic pathways greatly increase the difficulty of concentration monitoring and dose recommendation: VPA undergoes uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase (UGT)-mediated glucuronidation as its primary elimination route, whereas LEV is predominantly cleared renally (66% excreted unchanged), with 24% hydrolyzed in blood to inactive metabolites via non-hepatic pathways, rendering its exposure highly sensitive to fluctuations in creatinine clearance [5, 6].

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) remains pivotal for optimizing clinical decisions and managing PEPs, especially those with drug-resistant epilepsy [7]. Key TDM considerations for VPA and LEV are as follows: (1) VPA has a narrow therapeutic range (50–100 μg/mL) and large pharmacokinetic variability [8]. Concentrations < 50 μg/mL increase the risk of seizures breakthrough, wheres levels > 100 μg/mL significantly raise the incidence of hepatotoxicity and thrombocytopenia [9]. (2) Neuropsychiatric adverse events (e.g., aggression and irritability) are relatively common with LEV [10]. Furthermore, the definition of a therapeutic reference range for LEV remains contentious, particularly in pediatric epilepsy patients (PEPs). Although international guidelines such as those from the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) have proposed ranges of 12–46 μg/mL, these are primarily based on adult data [11]. Emerging evidence suggests that for children, a lower range of 6–20 μg/mL may be more pharmacologically justified and clinically relevant. This range is supported by population pharmacokinetic modeling which indicates that a pediatric maintenance dose of 40 mg/kg/day reliably achieves trough concentrations within this window [12], accounting for age-related changes in clearance [13]. This range is also consistent with real-world TDM observations in specific populations, where a significant majority of pediatric patients exhibit concentrations below 20 μg/mL [14], and special caution is required to avoid LEV accumulation in PEPs with immature renal function.

Although traditional TDM can provide single-point plasma concentration feedback, it suffers from several limitations: (1) it relies on centralized laboratory platforms (e.g., LC-MS/MS, chromatography) that are expensive and complex, require long turnaround times, resulting in high testing costs and delayed reporting; (2) it necessitates invasive venipuncture; and (3) empirical dose adjustments are inherently lagging [15]. Consequently, the modern TDM concept should extend beyond merely quantifying drug concentrations and should instead deeply integrate monitoring results with dose optimization to achieve data-driven precision dosing.

In recent years, machine learning (ML) has demonstrated unique advantages in TDM: by integrating multidimensional clinical data it captures non-linear relationships and outperforms traditional physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) or population pharmacokinetic (PopPK) approaches in early prediction, complex-relationship modelling and data-utilisation efficiency [16, 17]. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) visual analytics further bridge the gap between covariate interpretability and predictive accuracy [18, 19]. Although ML-based VPA concentration prediction has shown promise, shortcomings such as suboptimal model fit and limited direct clinical utility persist [20, 21]. Our previous ensemble model that combined Gradient Boosting Regression Trees (GBRT), Random Forest (RF), and Support Vector Regression (SVR) improved the prediction of VPA trough concentrations to R2 = 0.50, representing clear progress in pediatric plasma concentration monitoring; however, concomitant-drug covariates were still omitted and no dose-adjustment protocol was provided [22]. Recent classification models for VPA concentration have revealed inadequate specificity: accuracy for identifying subtherapeutic range samples (<50 μg/mL) is only 0.21–0.50 [20], risking a “false-safe” misclassification in which under-treated patients are deemed therapeutic, leading to unnecessary up-titration and potential toxicity. Moreover, few ML-based monitoring studies for LEV have been reported; existing work remains confined to single-drug concentration or dose prediction, and simultaneous monitoring in polytherapy scenarios has yet to be explored.

Therefore, this study addresses these challenges by focusing on PEP receiving combined VPA and LEV therapy, proposing an innovative multi-task ML model as a clinical decision support tool. By integrating trough concentrations of VPA and LEV, demographic characteristics, laboratory parameters, and concomitant medications, the research aims to achieve two primary objectives: (1) developing high-accuracy blood concentration classification models to overcome limitations of conventional TDM detection; (2) establishing multi-output regression models for simultaneous prediction of personalized dosing regimens for both ASMs. This study further incorporates SHAP visual analytics to elucidate mechanisms of key features and evaluates clinical translation potential through prospective external validation, providing both theoretical foundation and technical support for precision management of polypharmacy in PEPs.

Methods

Patients and data

A retrospective study was conducted on PEPs receiving concurrent oral VPA and LEV therapy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University from January 2018 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosed with epilepsy; (2) age range 1–18 years; (3) oral VPA and LEV combination therapy; (4) documented trough concentrations for both drugs sampled within 30 minutes before next scheduled dose under steady-state conditions. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients with incomplete clinical records (more than 15% missing); (2) concomitant use of carbamazepine, phenobarbital, or carbapenem antibiotics; (3) patients with other severe systemic disorders. Clinical data were extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record information system (EIS), encompassing VPA medication information (trough concentration, dose form and daily doses), LEV medication information (trough concentration and daily doses), concomitant medication [oxcarbazepine (OXC), lamotrigine (LTG), clonazepam (CZP), perampanel (PER), topiramate (TPM)], personal information (gender, age, body weight), and laboratory data [alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), urea (UREA), creatinine (CREA), uric acid (UA), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLO), hemoglobin (HGB), erythrocyte count (RBC), platelet count (PLT), neutrophil count (NEUT)].

Data processing

A total of 502 samples were initially included, followed by data formatting standardization and assignment of values to categorical variables. We implemented sample/variable exclusion based on missingness thresholds (>15% missingness rate row-wise or column-wise). Median imputation was applied to residual missing values in retained variables, thus generating a primary dataset (497 × 25).

Based on different modeling objectives, we implemented a systematic data preprocessing pipeline for feature screening (Supplementary Table S1). Initially, Spearman correlation analysis and multicollinearity diagnostics were conducted for all variables. For variables demonstrating strong linear correlation (|r| >0.6) or high multicollinearity (Variance Inflation Factor, VIF > 10), an integrated approach combining variable transformation and iterative elimination was employed, with comprehensive consideration of clinical relevance throughout the screening process. This led to the preliminary identification of key covariates. Finally, ML methods were applied to further refine the selection of significant variables, resulting in the formation of three modeling datasets: (1) A single-target feature set (460 × 8) for VPA trough concentration prediction (Supplementary Table S2); (2) A single-target feature set (460 × 9) for LEV trough concentration prediction (Supplementary Table S3); (3) A shared feature set (497 × 10) with dual-target variables (VPA and LEV daily doses) (Supplementary Table S4). All datasets were randomly partitioned into training and testing sets at an 8:2 ratio, with a fixed random seed assigned to ensure experimental reproducibility. Min-max normalization parameters derived exclusively from training sets were applied to corresponding test sets, implementing strict data leakage prevention measures throughout preprocessing. Some patients had multiple trough concentration measurements. Since the intervals between measurements all exceeded four weeks, these were treated as independent samples in the analysis. In addition, we prospectively collected two independent external validation datasets: an in-hospital dataset and an external hospital dataset. Both datasets included comprehensive clinical parameters of PEPs. These two datasets were subsequently split and processed according to the validation objectives. The analysis results of the external validation set have been included in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Tables S5, S6, and S7).

Strategy for handling repeated measures data

This study included drug concentration data from multiple measurements in some patients. To properly address the non-independence arising from such repeated measures data, we employed a linear mixed-effects models (LMEM) for sensitivity analysis and statistical validation. By incorporating “Patient ID” as a random effect, the model effectively adjusted for correlations among multiple measurements from the same individual, thereby obtaining more robust estimates of fixed effects. This analysis aims to provide interpretable and statistically sound supplementary evidence from a traditional statistical perspective for the key features identified by the ML model.

Determination of VPA and LEV trough concentrations

Plasma trough concentrations of VPA and LEV were quantified using the Viva-ProE automated biochemical analyzer (Siemens Medical Diagnostic Products Ltd.). Blood plasma samples were collected 30 minutes prior to the next scheduled dose after confirming steady-state drug concentrations. Subsequently, the plasma samples were pre-processed and the supernatant was collected. Quantitative analysis was performed via enzyme-multiplied immunoassay technique (EMIT), with validated linear ranges of 10–150 μg/mL for VPA and 3–100 μg/mL for LEV. External validation cohort samples adhered to identical analytical criteria, including pre-dose sampling protocols and EMIT-based quantification.

Machine learning model process

Algorithm selection

For classification modeling, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of the linear relationships between VPA and LEV trough concentrations and covariates, revealing a non-significant linear association with low correlation coefficients between these variables (Supplementary Figure S1). Based on these findings, we employed multiple nonlinear ML classifiers [Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Decision Tree (DT), Extra Trees (ET), Gradient Boosting Classifier (GBC), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Support Vector Machine (SVM)] for trough concentration modeling.

For regression modeling, XGBoost was a gradient boosted tree model for single objective regression, which excels in handling nonlinear relationships and feature interactions. The Multi-Output Regressor, implemented through scikit-learn’s meta-estimator framework, independently trains regression models for each target variable, enabling parallel prediction of multiple targets. Our integrated XGBoost multi-output regressor architecture achieves optimal balance between modeling flexibility and computational efficiency in multi-output regression tasks. This hybrid approach preserves XGBoost’s capacity for modeling complex nonlinear dependencies while addressing target heterogeneity through independent modeling mechanisms, establishing robust technical foundations for personalized dose regimen development. Software versions: XGBoost version 2.0.3; LightGBM version 4.6.0; SHAP version 0.47.0; scikit-learn version 1.5.1; python version 3.12.7.

Construction of the VPA-LEV trough concentrations classification models

To enable rapid and accurate identification of whether VPA and LEV trough concentrations meet therapeutic ranges for timely monitoring of combination therapy in PEPs, binary classification prediction model datasets were established by defining VPA trough concentrations (50–100 μg/mL vs. <50 μg/mL) and LEV trough concentrations (6–20 μg/mL vs. <6 μg/mL) as target variables. Feature importance ranking was performed using the LightGBM classifier, and key features were selected by evaluating the mean AUC values of feature combinations under cross-validation. Subsequently, the most critical features with the highest AUC values were retained for model training. The selected features and target variables were then separated for subsequent model training and optimization.

In order to construct and screen for superior monitoring models, a variety of ML classifiers, including ANN, DT, ET, GBC, KNN, LightGBM, RF, SVM, and XGBoost, were employed in the training process. For each model, distinct hyperparameter sets were defined, and GridSearchCV was employed for grid search to identify optimal parameter configurations. All models were evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation to assess their performance. Based on these evaluations, models demonstrating superior performance in both VPA and LEV trough concentration predictions were selected as optimal candidates and subjected to in-depth visualization analysis. Finally, the predictive performance of the optimal models was prospectively validated using an independent external dataset.

Construction of the VPA-LEV dose recommendation regression models

To identify shared feature combinations for VPA and LEV, spearman correlation coefficient matrices were first computed among features. Features exhibiting strong linear correlations (|r| >0.6) with already selected features were excluded. Based on the multicollinearity assessment results, variables with a VIF greater than 10, specifically ALB/GLO, UA and PLT, were retained due to their clinical importance and the relative insensitivity of ML models to multicollinearity (Supplementary Table S8). RF was then applied to model the VPA and LEV datasets separately, and feature importance rankings were extracted to select the top 10 features for each drug. This approach ensured that the selected features not only demonstrated high importance but also minimized potential multicollinearity impacts on the models. For the 10 candidate features, exhaustive computation of all possible feature combinations was performed, with each combination evaluated using RF under 5-fold cross-validation. Mean R2 scores were calculated, ranked, and recorded to identify the optimal feature combinations for VPA and LEV, which were subsequently merged into a shared feature combination.

The shared feature combination was normalized via min-max scaling and randomly partitioned into training and test sets at an 8:2 ratio. Based on the XGBoost framework, a Multi-Output Regressor wrapper was employed to develop a multi-target regression model, enabling simultaneous prediction of VPA and LEV daily dose. Following model training and hyperparameter tuning, 5-fold cross-validation was conducted. Finally, the model’s predictive performance was prospectively validated using an independent external dataset. Models interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is grounded in Shapley value theory from game theory, which quantifies the marginal contribution of each feature to model predictions across diverse feature combinations, thereby enhancing the interpretability of ML models. This method employs visualization tools such as SHAP-based feature importance, dependency plots, and force plots, which intuitively reveal global feature influences and individual prediction logic, thereby demystifying model “black-box” behavior. Implemented via the Python SHAP library (version 0.47.0), this study enhances model transparency and decision reliability through equitable attribution of feature contributions.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. Categorical variables are presented as n (%), and differences in distribution between groups were evaluated using the chi-square test. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For normally distributed data, intergroup comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test, while non-normally distributed data were analyzed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Correlations between variables were quantified using spearman correlation coefficient. A value of p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Basic information of the dataset

This study enrolled clinical data from 402 PEPs, assembling a comprehensive dataset comprising 497 paired VPA and LEV trough concentration records (497 × 25). Analysis of the dataset revealed that nearly one-third of the trough concentration measurements fell outside the therapeutic range. Specifically, VPA and LEV trough concentrations below the therapeutic range (insufficient treatment) accounted for 26.4% and 27.4%, respectively, while those exceeding the therapeutic range constituted 4.8% and 3.6% (Supplementary Table S4). We postulate that this phenomenon might stem from clinicians’ excessively cautious adjustment of PEPs doses, as well as the possibility that relying solely on drug labels, practice guidelines, or clinical experience may not adequately meet the requirements for effective treatment. The comprehensive comparisons presented in Supplementary Tables S5–S7 confirm that our external validation cohorts are broadly representative of the original patient population used for model development, while also illuminating specific, manageable distribution shifts.

Guided by this concentration landscape, the binary classification outcomes were defined as follows: an insufficient-treatment group (VPA < 50 μg/mL and LEV < 6 μg/mL) and an adequate-treatment group (VPA 50–100 μg/mL and LEV 6–20 μg/mL). Optimal feature subsets were identified through LightGBM-based importance ranking coupled with forward feature selection and nested cross-validation (Fig. 2A–B). The VPA-LEV dose-recommendation regression model

PEPs assigned to the insufficient-treatment group by the classification models will undergo subsequent dose recommendation guided by the regression model. Systematic feature engineering was employed to optimise the inputs of the regression model. Initially, the top 10 key features for VPA and LEV were identified using the RF algorithm (Supplementary Figure S3A-B). Subsequently, an exhaustive method was applied to candidate features, with 5-fold cross-validation employed to determine the optimal subsets (Supplementary Figure S3C-D). Notably, significant feature overlap was observed between these two optimal subsets, ultimately yielding eight shared features across both medications: body weight, LTG, ALB/GLO, UA, ALP, PLT, VPA trough concentration, and LEV trough concentration.

In this study, we employed a multi-output regression framework powered by XGBoost to predict VPA and LEV doses concurrently. Although separate prediction models were generated, this integrated approach streamlined the modeling process and ensured a consistent analytical pipeline for both drugs. The model achieved satisfactory predictive performance on the test set (VPA R2 = 0.60; LEV R2 = 0.60), and cross-validation demonstrated robust consistency (VPA R2 = 0.54 ± 0.07; LEV R2 = 0.64 ± 0.07). Linear mixed-effects model (LMEM) and intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)

To validate the robustness of the relationship between the features selected by the XGBoost multi-output regression model and the target variables (VPA and LEV daily doses), we additionally constructed a LMEM for sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table S9, and Figures S4–S5). ICC analysis indicated that both VPA and LEV daily dose data exhibit within-individual correlation (ICC values of 0.40 and 0.60, respectively), suggesting that the initial modeling assumption of treating the multiple trough concentration data from different patients as independent may have been affected. Despite the clustered structure of the data, the key fixed effects identified by the LMEM were consistent with the ML model results in terms of direction of effect and statistical significance, further supporting the reliability of the main conclusions.

Discussion

This study pioneers a ML model for simultaneous VPA-LEV concentration–dose prediction in PEPs receiving combination therapy, which may assist clinical decision-making by reducing reliance on empirical dose titration. By transforming traditional TDM from isolated concentration feedback into a dynamic concentration-guided dose-optimization system, we establish a data-driven strategy for clinical personalized medication (Fig. 7), underscoring its substantial clinical utility in antiseizure-medication combination therapy for PEPs. Due to the inherent uncertainty in ET classification predictions, we implemented the following measures to mitigate the risk that this uncertainty might propagate to subsequent regression models: For samples predicted below the therapeutic window, validation was conducted through seizure control status and TDM when necessary. For samples predicted within the therapeutic window, additional indicators such as BUN/CREA, PLT, and UA were used to assess potential concentrations exceeding the therapeutic window, with TDM initiated for confirmation when warranted. This design effectively addresses the issue of excluding samples with high therapeutic window concentrations (potential toxicity) from the model training data, thereby enhancing the model’s generalization capability and safety assessment accuracy. To address the clinical monitoring demands of VPA-LEV combination therapy, we innovatively deployed SMOTE to resolve class imbalance in trough concentration classification modelling [23]. Comparative evaluation across nine machine learning algorithms revealed that the ET classifier achieved superior performance for the VPA and LEV combination, exhibiting the highest specificity for identifying samples within the subtherapeutic range [20]. For dose-recommendation regression modeling, we pioneered the integration of XGBoost with a Multi-Output Regressor [24, 25], harnessing the core strengths of multi-target regression frameworks. This hybrid approach retains robust capacity for modeling complex nonlinear relationships while, via parameter-sharing layers and an independent output layer, it systematically balance heterogeneous pharmacokinetic parameter requirements. The approach establishes a solid technical foundation for personalized dosing and effectively mitigates the pharmacodynamic heterogeneity in combined antiepileptic therapy.

To quantitatively contextualize the performance of our model, we compared its predictive outcomes against established pharmacometric benchmarks from published PopPK and Bayesian forecasting studies. Our classifier demonstrated test-set accuracies and AUCs of 0.74/0.75 for VPA and 0.75/0.75 for LEV in predicting target concentration attainment. These results align with those from PopPK models incorporating Bayesian inference, which typically achieve 60–80% accuracy or report 60–75% of predictions within ±20–30% of observed concentrations under therapeutic drug monitoring scenarios [26, 27]. Similarly, our XGBoost-based dose recommender (test-set R2 = 0.60 for both drugs; >65% of predictions within ±30% of actual dose) performed comparably to PopPK-informed dosing simulations in pediatric populations [28, 29]. While direct head-to-head comparison with maximum a posteriori Bayesian estimation was not feasible on the same dataset, our model’s performance suggests that it offers a complementary, mechanism-agnostic alternative for predicting exposure and dosing—particularly valuable in clinical settings where PopPK models are unavailable or insufficient to capture complex patient heterogeneity.

SHAP analysis elucidated the contribution of core features to the model’s predictions, offering interpretable and data-driven insights to support clinical decision-making. However, while the associations identified may be clinically informative, they should not be construed as definitive evidence of direct biological causality. In classification models, the body weight-normalized daily dose parameter demonstrated pivotal regulatory effects on VPA and LEV trough concentrations (Fig. 3). VPA/Weight (mg/kg/d, 27.4%) and LEV/Weight (mg/kg/d, 36.9%) emerged as the most influential contributors, both exhibiting positive correlations with their respective trough concentrations. Body weight serves as a key covariate for LEV clearance (CL) and volume of distribution (Vd) [29], with children demonstrating 37% higher weight-normalized CL than adults, necessitating dose adjustment based on body weight to maintain therapeutic trough concentrations [27]. This pharmacodynamic principle aligns with the SHAP analysis results showing the highest contribution (36.9%) of LEV/Weight.

Notably, a Pop Pk study revealed an 18.8% reduction in LEV clearance during VPA co-administration, potentially mediated by VPA-induced inhibition of unidentified LEV metabolic pathways [30]. This model-derived association, potentially reflecting a drug interaction, was quantified through SHAP analysis (Fig. 3D), demonstrating that VPA/Weight contributed 11.2% (ranked third) to LEV trough concentration, with SHAP dependence plots exhibiting a “V”-shaped nonlinear relationship. Threshold analysis based on VPA prescribing information recommendations (20–35 mg/kg/d) revealed that when VPA/Weight reached 20 mg/kg/d, the target attainment probability of LEV trough concentrations (therapeutic range: 6–20 μg/mL) significantly decreased, indicating subtherapeutic concentration risks. Conversely, at VPA/Weight > 20 mg/kg/d, LEV trough concentration attainment probability showed dose-dependent elevation, correlating with enhanced VPA-mediated metabolic inhibition [30, 31]. Clinical monitoring recommendation: For patients receiving VPA/Weight < 20 mg/kg/d, LEV dose escalation should be considered to maintain antiepileptic efficacy. Interestingly, LEV/Weight emerged as the second most significant factor (21.3%) contributing to the variability of VPA trough concentrations (therapeutic range: 50–100 μg/mL), which, to some extent, reflects the degree to which LEV is influenced by VPA. Although the prescribing guidelines recommend LEV dosing at 20–60 mg/kg/d, threshold analysis revealed a dose-dependent increase in the probability of achieving target trough concentrations when LEV/Weight weight fell within 30–60 mg/kg/d and VPA doses were maintained at 20–35 mg/kg/d, suggesting a potential synergistic interaction between the two agents. The synergistic underdosing effects of combination therapy may exponentially elevate seizure recurrence risks, underscoring the necessity for model-guided dose optimization. SHAP analysis provides data-driven justification for this precision dosing strategy, enabling quantitative assessment of drug-drug and dose-concentration relationships in combinatorial antiepileptic therapy.

The BUN/CREA ratio, a crucial clinical indicator for assessing renal function and nitrogen metabolic homeostasis [32], exhibits dynamic alterations influenced by multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. Hepatic dysfunction, as the urea cycle’s central organ, significantly reduces urea synthesis efficiency, leading to abnormally decreased BUN/CREA ratios [33]. Nutritional status further modulates this ratio, where protein malnutrition diminishes urea precursor availability, thereby lowering BUN/CREA levels [32]. Regarding VPA metabolism, this hepatically cleared drug carries dose-dependent hepatotoxicity risks. Notably, SHAP analysis revealed that reduced BUN/CREA ratios in patients with hepatic impairment may reflect compromised urea synthesis capacity alongside potential VPA accumulation due to impaired metabolic clearance. In PEPs, protein malnutrition (ALB < 35 g/L) exacerbates VPA hepatotoxicity through increased free drug concentrations [34], a pathophysiological process consistent with the BUN/CREA hypometabolic pattern identified in SHAP (Fig. 3C). From renal clearance perspectives, high VPA exposure induces dose-dependent renal tubular injury [35, 36]. Renal dysfunction simultaneously reduces tubular urea reabsorption and decreases creatinine clearance, resulting in non-linear BUN/CREA ratio reduction through dual-pathway interactions [37]. Importantly, impaired renal elimination also affects LEV pharmacokinetics, correlating strongly with the “reduced BUN/CREA-elevated LEV trough concentration” association pattern demonstrated in SHAP dependency plots (Fig. 3D). These collective findings position BUN/CREA ratio as a potential auxiliary biomarker for therapeutic drug monitoring of both VPA and LEV trough concentrations.

The kidneys play a predominant role in UA excretion, with serum UA levels reflecting both glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and tubular secretory capacity [38]. As the second most significant covariate (16.2%) in predicting LEV trough concentration, elevated UA levels strongly correlate with increased probability of attaining therapeutic LEV concentration. This association arises as elevated UA levels reflect decreased GFR and impaired tubular secretion, both of which impair LEV elimination. Conversely, lower UA levels may indicate a risk of LEVconcentrations falling within the subtherapeutic range (Fig. 3D). Notably, UA demonstrated weaker predictive value for VPA concentration (excluded from final model), consistent with VPA’s primary hepatic metabolism and lack of direct involvement in uric acid metabolic pathways. These findings establish UA as an independent predictor for LEV therapeutic monitoring, though clinical interpretation requires integration with BUN/CREA parameters.

SHAP dependency plot analysis revealed negative correlations between trough concentrations of VPA/LEV and PLT (Fig. 3C–D). This observation aligns with established mechanistic evidence: VPA has been demonstrated to induce dose-dependent thrombocytopenia through inhibition of platelet production, with this hematologic effect positively correlating with hepatotoxicity [39]. Conversely, LEV shows no clinically significant platelet suppression according to current pharmacovigilance data [40]. This mechanistic divergence suggests that VPA may elevate hemorrhagic risk through specific pharmacological pathways, whereas LEV potentially offers superior hematologic safety, a critical determinant requiring prioritization in clinical dose optimization. Based on these findings, we strongly recommend comprehensive evaluation of platelet parameters and associated bleeding risks prior to: (1) Long-term VPA-LEV combination therapy; (2) Surgical interventions requiring anticoagulation management [40].

In the regression model, body weight was confirmed to be the most important covariate of VPA (70.2%) and LEV (62.8%) dose, showing a significant positive correlation with the required treatment dose when body weight was < 60 kg (Fig. 5A–B). When body weight was ≥ 60 kg, the impact on dose prediction tended to stabilize, which may be attributed to pediatric pharmacokinetic parameters (such as liver enzyme activity and glomerular filtration rate) gradually approaching adult levels, thereby reducing interindividual variability. The trough concentrations of VPA and LEV ranked second (7.1% and 14.7%, respectively), reflecting clinical “concentration feedback adjustment” logic (dose escalation is required when subtherapeutic trough levels are observed). Notably, the impact of LEV concentration (ranked eighth) on VPA dosing was negligible, with SHAP values ranging from −10 to 5 (Fig. 5A), whereas VPA concentration (also ranked eighth) exhibited a marginally greater influence on LEV dosing, with SHAP values spanning −20 to 50 (Fig. 5B), consistent with prior analyses.

LTG (ranked third) exhibited a strong influence on LEV dosing, with higher LTG doses correlating with increased LEV requirements (Fig. 6B). Clinical observations report elevated LEV doses in some patients during LTG co-administration [41], despite lacking direct pharmacokinetic interactions [42]. Notably, LTG’s glucuronidation metabolism shows no significant enzyme induction/inhibition effects. While VPA-LTG combinations require dose adjustments due to UGT inhibition by VPA [43], LTG (ranked seventh) minimally impacts VPA dosing (Fig. 6A). The observed need for LEV dose adjustments in triple therapy (VPA+LEV+LTG) may arise from individual variability or disease complexity, though underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Although the use of repeated measurements with intervals exceeding four weeks as independent samples in ML modeling may introduce certain biases, sensitivity analyses based on LMEM demonstrated that the effects of key predictors remained robust (Supplementary Table S9). The model also confirmed the presence of significant inter-individual variability, with LEV exhibiting stronger individual dependency compared to VPA (ICC: 0.60 vs. 0.40). These results suggest that for LEV treatment, more proactive and timely individual dose adjustments are necessary after the initial population model-based dosing regimen.

The regression model developed in this study is designed to serve as a decision-support tool rather than a standalone clinical solution. Its outputs must be interpreted by clinicians within a broader context that includes seizure control, adverse reactions, and serial laboratory examinations. To mitigate the risks of misestimation, we implemented multiple strategies: first, the classification model acts as a filter to prioritize the identification of cases requiring intervention; second, features strongly associated with risks—unveiled by SHAP analysis, such as low PLT indicating potential VPA toxicity or abnormal BUN/CREA/UA suggesting hepatic/renal dysfunction—provide critical early-warning signals to clinicians; lastly, prospective validation and explicitly stated model limitations (e.g., the omission of pharmacogenomic data) establish clear safety boundaries for the model’s application. Future integration with electronic health record systems to enable real-time monitoring of these risk markers could further enhance the safety of this framework.

Limitations

Although the dual-prediction ML model developed in this study addresses an unmet clinical need in PEPs receiving VPA-LEV combination therapy, several limitations remain: (1) the retrospective design resulted in missing unstructured clinical data, such as detailed seizure severity, adverse effects, and prior treatment history, while the absence of these variables had a limited impact on the concentration classification models (as they rely on pharmacokinetic determinants), it noticeably constrained the dose regression models’ ability to personalize therapeutic targets and avoid previously intolerated doses; (2) pharmacogenomic variants were not included as covariates (e.g., the impact of UGT polymorphisms on VPA metabolism or the regulatory effects of SV2A genetic variants on LEV sensitivity), which may compromise predictive accuracy in PEPs with metabolic phenotypes; (3) during feature selection, the inherent incompleteness of retrospective data precluded the inclusion of seizure types, electroencephalogram data and other disease-specific indicators, potentially omitting key predictive factors; (4) the current cross-sectional dataset cannot capture time-dependent pharmacokinetic shifts driven by nonlinear BMI trajectories and maturation-related changes in hepatic and renal function during childhood development; (5) although SHAP analysis enhanced clinical interpretability, the biological mechanisms underlying complex feature interactions remain insufficiently elucidated and warrant further investigation.

Furthermore, due to the constraints of our retrospective data extraction from electronic medical records, clinically significant variables such as standardized seizure severity scores, detailed adverse effect profiles, and comprehensive prior treatment history were not consistently available for model inclusion. While this limits the model’s ability to personalize the therapeutic target concentration based on disease severity or avoid previously ineffective/intolerable doses, it is important to emphasize that the model is designed as a pharmacokinetic decision-support tool. Its outputs regarding concentration status and dose requirements must be integrated with the treating clinician’s holistic assessment of the patient’s individual clinical picture.

Conclusion

This study has addresses the critical challenge of personalized medication in PEPs receiving VPA and LEV combination therapy. We pioneer a ML framework termed “concentration-dose synergy prediction”, which integrates multidimensional clinical data to establish a decision support system encompassing concentration alerting and dose optimization, serving as an adjunct tool for TDM. This approach provides a reliable, proof-of-concept feasibility solution for precision antiepileptic therapy. SHAP analysis elucidates critical drivers of the predictive model, offering mechanistic insights to guide TDM and dose adjustments. The study transcends the limitations of conventional single-dimensional TDM feedback loops, with prospective external validation demonstrating robust clinical generalizability of the model.
