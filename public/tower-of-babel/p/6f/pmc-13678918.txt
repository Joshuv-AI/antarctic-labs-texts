Assessing medication safety culture in Norwegian pediatric wards: a first nationwide survey
Authors: Lavin Kadir, M. Einen, J. C. Bergman, H. U. Irgens, G-T. L. Sørland, A. G. Granas, A. Mulac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsHealth information technologies are widely used in hospitals to reduce the risk of medication errors. However, evidence on their impact on medication management and patient safety in pediatric settings remains limited. The aim of this study was to assess safety culture in medication management and medication error reporting in pediatric wards and to identify areas for improvement.MethodA survey was developed and presented on the annual meeting of Medicines for Children Network, Norway, attended by 80 physicians, pharmacists, and nurses. Following adjustments, the questionnaire was piloted with two physicians, three pharmacists, and three nurses. The survey was then distributed by e-mail to all pediatric wards in Norway. We used the SEIPS (Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety) model to guide the analysis.ResultsIn total 102 nurses, 24 physicians and seven pharmacists responded, representing 19 out of the 22 (86%) pediatric wards. Access to health information technology adapted to pediatrics, including electronic health records, electronic medication administration records, and clinical decision support systems, varied across hospitals and across stages of the medication management process. Respondents generally agreed that a well-established reporting culture promotes learning. A majority also expressed a need for greater involvement in the review of reported medication errors, and more extensive training regarding patient safety and reporting culture.ConclusionHospital staff demonstrated generally positive awareness of pediatric patient safety. Medication management seemed to be affected by the ongoing implementation of new health information technology, suggesting opportunities for better health information technologies adapted to pediatric wards. Persistent barriers to medication error reporting may limit opportunities for learning and improvement.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12887-026-07365-6.

Introduction

Medication errors pose a significant risk in pediatric care due to the complexity of the medication management process, including weight-based dosing, frequent dose calculations, and age-related variations in pharmacotherapy. These factors highlight the need for targeted safety measures [1–3]. While global initiatives promote standardization, pediatric medication management also requires tailored and individualized medical treatment, which further increases complexity and the risk of medication errors [4, 5]. Understanding the causes to these errors is therefore essential for developing and implementing effective risk-reduction measures [6]. Improving patient safety requires a multifaceted approach including comprehensive staff training, awareness-raising initiatives, and the integration of appropriate health information technologies [7–9].

To reduce medication errors, the use of health information technologies has expanded, including electronic Medication Administration Record (eMAR), Computerized Prescriber Order Entry (CPOE), and Clinical Decision Support Systems (CDSS) with alarms and alerts [10–12]. Furthermore, the implementation of verification steps, such as the Barcode Medication Administration (BCMA), has become a widely adopted strategy for enhancing patient safety [13, 14]. In recent years, artificial intelligence has also been integrated into CDSS to support healthcare professionals in making informed clinical decisions [15, 16]. A meta-analysis investigating interventions aimed at reducing medication errors in pediatric hospitals reported inconsistent results, and the overall impact on patient harm was limited [17]. However, BCMA has been shown to substantially reduce medication errors, leading to its widespread implementation globally [17, 18]. Even so, previous studies have reported challenges related to the proper integration of health information technology into hospital workflows, including the use of workarounds due to limited system adaption [19, 20].

In Norway, several measures have been implemented to strengthen the pediatric patient safety, including the establishment of the Medicines for Children Network (MCN) in 2009 [21]. Although national studies on patient safety culture have been conducted, research specifically focusing on pediatric settings remains limited. In 2019, Waaseth et al. identified key barriers to medication error reporting and examined perceptions of safety culture within a Norwegian hospital, including factors such as time constraints, a complicated reporting system, and insufficient training[22].

Variation in the choice of health information technology across the four regional health authorities in Norway further complicates national standardization of medication management. At the same time, little is known about how these technologies are experienced by healthcare professionals and how they influence the medication management process and pediatric patient safety [3, 23]. International literature highlights limited evaluation of health information technology in relation to human risk factors [24]. Thus, there is a need to investigate the current safety culture in pediatric medication management, including how health information technology affects medication-related practices. The aim of this study was therefore to assess safety culture related to the medication management process in pediatric wards, including medication error reporting, and to identify areas for improvement.

Methods

Nationwide study collecting qualitative and quantitative data from pediatric wards, allowing exploration of practices and variations across wards.

Aim

To assess safety culture in medication management and medication error reporting in pediatric wards and to identify areas for improvement.

Questionnaire

We developed a questionnaire adapted to the three professions: nurses, physicians, and pharmacists. The first draft was developed over several months through an iterative process, including multiple meetings and discussions within the multidisciplinary research team. Based on the study aims and relevant literature, we identified key domains, generated profession-specific items, and refined the wording and structure of the questionnaire through repeated rounds of review and consensus-building. The questionnaire was further developed through several phases:

The first draft of the questionnaire was tested at the MCN’s annual gathering. Feedback on the content and readability was collected during a session dedicated to the questionnaire, which involved 13 groups organized by profession: 5 groups of nurses, 4 groups of pharmacists, and 4 groups of physicians. Each group consisted of a maximum of 6 respondents and was given a total of 1 h to review the questionnaire and provide written feedback.

The second draft of the questionnaire was subsequently refined by 3 nurses, 3 pharmacists, and 2 physicians, all recruited through MCN. Feedback provided in both phases regarding question relevance and validity was implemented in the questionnaire.

The questionnaire covered 5 main themes: (1) The prescribing process (2), Proper medication management (3), Double checking and signing (4), Occurrence of medication errors, and (5) Handling and prevention of medication errors. In total, the questionnaire comprised 55 questions, with 33 being common to all professions, whilst the remaining questions were specific to the respondents’ profession and tasks. Further, the questionnaire featured both free text and closed-ended questions. Close-ended questions were used to identify trends and gaps in practice, while free-text responses allowed respondents to describe their experiences with medication management in more detail. These responses were later analyzed qualitatively.

Setting and data collection

The survey was conducted among healthcare professionals providing care for pediatric inpatients (0–18 years) across the 22 hospitals in Norway that have pediatric wards. While these wards employ both medical and nursing personnel, pharmacists are less commonly employed.

The hospitals were at varying stages of implementing health information technologies. Two different vendors of electronic health record (EHR) systems and three different vendors of eMAR systems were used across the four regional health authorities [25].

An online link to the survey (Supplementary) was distributed nationwide via email to MCN members and other relevant staff working in all the pediatric wards who were not members of the MCN. The link remained open for responses for 3 weeks, between November and December of 2024.

Data analysis

All close-ended data was analyzed using descriptive statistics (percentages and frequencies) with IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences), version 30, while the free text data, consisting of total of 9,680 words, were analyzed and categorized with the support of the SEIPS (Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety) model, a framework fitted to explore how employees’ perceptions can be understood in the healthcare system [26].

The SEIPS model was used to identify elements influencing the medication management process and safety culture. The analysis involved categorizing the text data according to the five SEIPS elements of the work system which include: tools and technological factors, organizational factors, profession related factors, task related factors and environmental factors. This approach enabled the identification of recurring themes and patterns, and the synthesis of respondents’ perspectives into coherent themes relevant to the research aim [27]. The analysis was conducted through iterative readings of the responses followed by categorization. For free text answers, the text was reviewed in the research team (LK, AGG & AM), until consensus was met.

Ethics

Based on the national regulation guidelines from Norwegian Agency for Shared Services in Education and Research (SIKT), no additional approvals were necessary, as the questionnaire was anonymous.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Not applicable.

Results

A total of 133 healthcare professionals responded, including 102 nurses, 24 physicians, and seven pharmacists. We received responses from 19 (86%) of the 22 pediatric wards in Norway, covering all four health regions.

When asked about the frequency of medication errors during the medication management process, respondents most commonly reported “less than monthly” for prescribing (54.9%), preparation (60.2%), and administration (71.4%). Most respondents also reported that they were encouraged to report medication errors (89.4%) and most expressed confidence in reporting errors themselves (95.7%).

In contrast, near miss incidents were reported to occur more frequently. Respondents most commonly indicated that near miss incidents were detected on a weekly (45.0%) or daily (35.1%) basis. Despite this, 82.3% answered that they did not report a near miss when detected. In addition, 71.5% reported that they did not have access to medication error reports or were unsure whether such access was available.

Free text responses analyzed using the SEIPS framework

Analysis of the free-text responses using the SEIPS model identified statements related to all five elements of the framework across the questionnaire themes, emphasizing tools and technological factors, followed by organizational factors.

Tools and technological factors were mostly related to lack of health information technology such as BCMA. Respondents reported that inadequate adaptation to the needs of pediatric wards contributed to frustration among staff. BCMA has only been integrated into one of four health regions and was described as a natural part of the workflow in this region.

Organizational factors addressed limitations in learning, inclusiveness, and follow-up. Most of the respondents requested enhanced learning on medication errors and better management of medication safety activities. When any new health information technology was implemented, the respondents reported that insufficient time was allocated for proper training at the wards. This was of particular concern for newly employed nurses, especially in wards already experiencing time constraints. Moreover, the notion of fostering a safe and supportive safety culture and improving staffing levels were recurrent themes throughout the questionnaire. The respondents generally stated that reporting errors in the reporting systems was “unproblematic”. Mainly, this was referred to as a result of improved reporting systems. However, when reporting errors, the way the issue was addressed and handled by management was considered important. Many respondents expressed the belief that more frequent evaluations of reported errors could foster a conducive environment for learning and encourage increased reporting. Notably, near miss incidents were regarded as an indication of effective procedures for identifying errors through double checking. Some voiced their intention to address the issue directly with the individual responsible, while others perceived no educational value in reporting near miss incidents.

Profession related factors were concerning ongoing learning. From the nurse’s perspective, there was a concern related to how physicians utilized the EHR systems, specifically documentation in the eMAR system. Physicians and nurses both expressed a need for more training in utilizing electronic record charts, especially to better each other’s documentation practices. Following, several nurses suggested increased use of double checking, particularly of prescribers, as this was not a standard practice.

Task related factors pointed to practical solutions in an otherwise busy work schedule. Respondents suggested that increased cooperation between hospital wards and hospital pharmacies, as well as better coordination of medication preparations, could facilitate daily tasks.

Environmental factors referred to both time and space in the hospital. In the prescribing process, the physicians consistently reported time pressure, and a lack of tranquility when prescribing. Larger medicine rooms were desired for the nurses, as well as less disruption during both prescribing and preparation.

Discussion

This nationwide study provides new insights into medication safety culture within Norwegian pediatric wards. Although most respondents felt encouraged to report medication errors and expressed confidence in doing so, substantial barriers to reporting persist. These barriers appeared to be rooted in organizational factors (insufficient training, limited follow-up, and lack of learning from reports) as well as environmental factors (workflow constraints such as time, space, and staffing). Additionally, challenges with the implementation of health information technologies create frustration and workarounds.

Despite respondents’ generally positive attitudes toward reporting medication errors, including describing incident reporting systems as user-friendly, the qualitative analyses revealed that emotional discomfort, including fear of negative reactions from colleagues, continues to influence reporting behavior. This discrepancy suggests that self‑reported confidence does not necessarily translate into consistent reporting practice. Similar discrepancies have been described in previous literature, where fear of blame, lack of feedback, and unclear definitions of reportable events reduce the likelihood of reporting medication errors or near miss incidents [28].

Across professions, many respondents expressed concern about the limited review and follow‑up of reported medication errors. A lack of systematic learning processes appeared demotivating, and several respondents linked this gap to insufficient leadership support or unclear organizational responsibilities. These factors influence the perceptions of patient safety and safety related behavior [29].

We raise the argument of the absence of a national, harmonized system for medication error reporting as a barrier. Learning from reported medication errors is one of the most valuable outcomes of reporting systems [30], yet fears of repercussions may hinder effective use of these systems [31]. Although regional systems exist, the lack of common structures for learning across institutions hinders broader improvements in safety culture. Respondents expressed a desire for greater involvement in the analysis of reported incidents and for more inclusive learning arenas at the ward level. This is crucial for motivating professionals and reinforcing their ethical obligation to report errors [28].

Near miss reporting was notably low, with 82.3% of respondents stated that they rarely or never reported near miss incidents. These findings suggest that promoting positive attitudes towards reporting alone is not sufficient. A further explanation may be that reporting practices and system design do not clearly define near miss incidents as reportable events. Because reporting systems typically do not distinguish between incidents that reached the patient and those that did not, near miss incidents may be perceived as unnecessary to report. Additionally, some viewed near miss incidents as evidence that existing preventive measures, particularly double checks, were functioning as intended[32–34]. Workflow constraints and time pressure were also frequently described as barriers to reporting. Under such conditions, informal mechanisms, such as verbal communication, were often perceived as more practical, particularly when submitting a formal report was experienced as adding to an already high workload. Similar patterns have been documented in previous studies, especially among nurses [35]. Differences between the pediatric wards across the study hospitals in how near miss incidents were understood and managed indicate variation in the local emphasis on near miss reporting and learning from such events. This is consistent with literature demonstrating a lack of agreement regarding both the definition of “near miss incidents” and how such events should be used for learning [36]. Despite this lack of agreement and implementation, there is some evidence to indicate that near miss reporting produces a small but measurable reduction in medication errors [37, 38]. This underscores the importance of both preventive measures to reduce harmful medication errors and a sense of shared responsibility within the workforce are important [32]. Contributing factors to near miss reporting includes organizational, human, and technical elements, while insufficient feedback, unclear reporting protocols, and the absence of patient harm may reduce reporting rates. These factors reinforce the need for clear leadership to support near miss reporting [39].

A recurrent theme was frustration with health information technologies that were poorly adapted to pediatric workflows. The digitalization of clinical workflows can lead to alert fatigue, where frequent system notifications cause staff to become desensitized and overlook important alerts [40]. Several respondents reported insufficient training, especially for newly employed staff, and prior to system implementation. Systems that increase workload or disrupt existing workflows often lead to workarounds, allowing errors to go unnoticed and unreported [24]. These unintended effects link technological factors to gaps in both reporting rates and organizational learning. User-friendly design is crucial for enabling staff to integrate health information technologies into daily practice. Our findings align with other reports of challenges in EHR implementation within the Norwegian healthcare system [41, 42]. Successful utilization of any health information technology requires cultural change and coordinated efforts across multiple levels of the system, including hospital leadership, national policymakers, and system developers [43].

Even in hospitals where medication alerts and BCMA were implemented, adherence remained limited due to technological challenges such as poorly adapted pediatric eMAR functions and missing barcode scanners. Staff in these settings were nevertheless more satisfied with digital workflows overall, suggesting benefits when health information technology is well designed and integrated. These findings emphasize the necessity of maintaining continuous investment in well-established digital safety measures, as described by the World Health Organization (WHO) [44]. Mulac et al. found that deviations from BCMA use often stem from unclear or poorly described policies and suboptimal eMAR functionality, in concordance with our findings [45]. This is particularly important because the value of BCMA in pediatrics remains uncertain: many pediatric doses are drawn from multidose vials or liquid preparations without individual barcodes, limiting the applicability of scanning technologies and contributing to workarounds despite staff generally appreciating well‑functioning health information technology [46]. The findings suggest that the MCN may have an important role as a national advocate for better pediatric adaptation of scannable pediatric medication.

Double checking emerged as a frequently mentioned intervention for preventing medication errors; however, respondents’ descriptions reflected significant variation in how double checking is practiced, corresponding to earlier studies [47–49]. Although many respondents in our study believed double checks enhanced patient safety, the evidence from systematic reviews of the impact of double checks to reduce medication errors remains inconclusive [50]. In contrast to several other countries, prescriptions from hospital physicians are not routinely checked by a ward pharmacist in Norway. This differs from the community pharmacies, where pharmacists by law must check all prescriptions [51, 52]. There is a need for further studies on the impact of double checking prescriptions and dispensing, including emerging technologies that can reduce medication errors within hospital settings.

Additionally, there appears to be a lack of comprehensive understanding among system developers, who may focus on the technical solution rather than the broader clinical workflow. Alignment between IT and clinical practice is essential, both before and after implementation, underscoring the need for improved competence development when introducing new health information technology [53].

Overall, the SEIPS analysis suggested that pediatric medication safety and incident reporting are shaped by dynamic interaction across all five work system elements: technology, organization, personnel, tasks, and environment. These factors jointly influence medication management processes as well as healthcare professionals’ capacity to report and learn from medication errors.

Safety culture emerged both as an outcome of reporting practices and as a contextual condition influencing whether reporting is perceived as meaningful and supportive of practice improvement.

These findings indicate that strengthening medication safety and reporting practices in pediatric care requires coordinated system-level interventions. Such interventions should target health information technology (including enhanced pediatric adaptation and integration), strengthened organizational learning (e.g. clear policies and leadership engagement), streamlining clinical workflows with embedded reporting tasks, and work environments that support adequate time, space, resources, and psychological safety for open discussion of errors and near miss incidents.

Finally, our findings suggest that although Norway benefits from the MCN, which promotes standardized pediatric medication practices, variation in health information technology, system integration, and local organizational priorities still limits progress toward a unified national safety culture [20]. Fragmentation in health information technology infrastructure remains a key challenge and may undermine the potential for shared learning and standardized workflows [54]. Strengthening collaboration between the MCN and health information technology developers may further support national efforts to improve pediatric patient safety.

Implications

The study achieved a substantial response rate and comprehensive national coverage. Consequently, it contributes to a better understanding of pediatric medication management and provides a foundation for future national research aimed at improving patient safety culture. Furthermore, the results can be used to design more user-friendly eMAR, including systems for medication error reporting, by addressing the concerns and wishes of healthcare professionals in pediatric wards.

Our findings indicate an intention–behavior gap in pediatric wards: although staff express a strong willingness to report errors, near miss incidents are reported less frequently. This suggests an opportunity to improve system design and reporting practices so that near miss incidents are more clearly defined and actively encouraged as reportable events.

Moreover, these results support initiatives for better reporting cultures by working more actively on an inclusive reporting culture that both contributes to learning from reported errors, confidence in reporting errors and creating room for discussion about how practices can be improved to reduce medication errors.

Strength and limitations

A key strength of this study is its national scope within a pediatric setting, with over 100 participants from across Norway, providing a broad overview of medication management and patient safety culture, hence supporting generalizability. Further on, the questionnaire was developed and reviewed in collaboration with interprofessional MCN members working in pediatric medication management, and feedback from testing and pilot phases strengthened its reliability and validity. Even so, these strengths must be interpreted in light of certain methodological limitations, especially considering the study is specific to Norwegian pediatric ward. Further research is needed to assess the applicability of these findings and interventions in wider pediatric healthcare contexts.

A questionnaire provides limited in-depth understanding compared to, for example, interviews, where respondents have greater scope to elaborate and receive follow-up questions. This will in return affect any misunderstandings or incomplete answers. In addition, the questionnaire does not fully reflect the implementation of procedures due to local adaptations. Even though we addressed these issues by incorporating free text questions, thereby enabling respondents to provide more comprehensive and detailed responses, this skewness must be taken into account in further mapping and patient safety work. It is equally important to point out that this questionnaire is a cross-sectional study, limiting insight into the ongoing digitalization in hospitals. Furthermore, the sample in this study is limited to employees in pediatric wards and most responses were from nurses, making it challenging to apply the results to other health professional groups. A survey among employees from a larger range of wards would provide a more nuanced picture of today’s hospitals. Also, although the questionnaire was anonymous, responses may have been influenced by socially desirable bias, with participants potentially reporting practices they may perceive as expected rather than their actual experience or opinions. Moreover, as with all self-reported data, the results are subject to interpretation bias, as respondents’ perceptions and understandings may differ from actual practice. Finally, a selection of answers to questions has been excluded from the study in light of possible differences in item comprehension among respondents.

Conclusion

This study highlights key aspects of the safety culture regarding medication management in pediatrics wards. The substantial variation in implemented health information technology across different health regions points to the need for clear national strategies. Additionally, the study identified barriers to reporting medication errors, such as limited feedback and learning following reported incidents, insufficient training in reporting practices and safety culture, and differing perceptions of the importance of near miss incidents in medication safety work. These factors are all connected and point to underlying organizational challenges which emphasize the potential benefits for national standardization across pediatric wards. However, these findings are specific to the study Norwegian pediatric wards, and further research is needed to explore effective approaches to improving medication safety across diverse pediatric settings.
