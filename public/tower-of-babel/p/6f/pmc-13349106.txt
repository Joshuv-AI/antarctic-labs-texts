Static‐Guided Endodontics for Complex Intracanal Obstructions and Zirconia Post Removal: Report on Three Cases
Authors: Xinxuan Wang, Xuguang Li, Baicheng Yi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA standardized static‐guided framework integrating CBCT and intraoral scanning provides predictable, minimally invasive management for diverse intracanal obstructions. This unified digital workflow ensures precise root canal localisation in severe obliteration and conservative retrieval of fractured fiber or ultra‐hard posterior zirconia posts without iatrogenic complications.Keywords: digital workflow, post‐endodontic restoration, pulp canal obliteration, static‐guided endodontics, zirconia post removal

Introduction

Managing complex intracanal obstructions, including pulp canal obliteration (PCO) and intracanal post removal, remains a major challenge in endodontics. Calcified canals associated with trauma, aging, or restorative procedures often make canal localization difficult, whereas intracanal posts, particularly zirconia posts, complicate retreatment because of their high hardness and increased risk of canal deviation, perforation, excessive dentin removal, and instrument fracture [1, 2]. Although dental operating microscopes and ultrasonic instruments have improved treatment precision, conventional freehand procedures remain technique‐sensitive and highly dependent on operator experience [3].

Static‐guided endodontics has emerged as a predictable and minimally invasive alternative. By integrating cone‐beam computed tomography (CBCT) with intraoral scanning (IOS), three‐dimensional printed guides can direct access preparation along a pre‐planned trajectory, improving accuracy while reducing dentin loss and iatrogenic complications [4, 5, 6]. The technique may also reduce dependence on operator experience and improve treatment safety [7].

Despite its increasing clinical application, clear guidance regarding case selection, guide design, operative procedures, and outcome evaluation remains limited. Most published reports focus on single indications, such as calcified canals or fiber post removal, and a standardized workflow applicable to different types of intracanal obstructions has not yet been established [8, 9]. Furthermore, although guided removal of fiber posts has been widely reported, evidence regarding zirconia post removal remains scarce because of the material's extreme hardness and technical difficulty [10].

Therefore, this case series presents a standardized digital workflow for static‐guided endodontics and demonstrates its application in three complex cases, including two severely calcified canals, one fiber post removal, and one posterior zirconia post removal. The report highlights the feasibility, predictability, and broader clinical applicability of this unified approach.

Case Presentations

The case report follows the PRICE 2020 reporting guidelines [11].

Case 1: Static‐Guided Treatment of a Severely Calcified Maxillary Central Incisor

A 36‐year‐old woman presented with discoloration of tooth #21 following trauma 10 years previously. Clinical examination showed mild percussion sensitivity and negative pulp vitality tests. Periapical radiography and CBCT revealed complete pulp canal obliteration and a small periapical radiolucency (Figures 1 and 2). The diagnosis was chronic apical periodontitis.

Preoperative examination of case 1. (A) Preoperative intraoral photography. (B) Preoperative periapical X‐ray. Preliminary diagnosis: 1. #21 Chronic apical periodontitis. Treatment plan: #21 root canal treatment. Given the preoperative periapical radiographic findings of pulp canal obliteration and unclear visualization of the middle and apical portions of the root canal, a digital guide‐assisted access cavity preparation was proposed to reduce the risk of procedural complications. After obtaining informed consent, a static guided endodontic approach was used to establish the access path for tooth #21, followed by root canal treatment and subsequent coronal restoration.

Case 1 preoperative CBCT images. (A) Axial view. (B) Sagittal view. (C) Coronal view. Radiographs reveal pulp chamber and root canal obliteration due to calcification in tooth #21.

CBCT and intraoral scan data were merged in 3Shape Implant Studio to design a static guide for minimally invasive access preparation (Figures 3 and 4). Under rubber dam isolation and microscopic guidance, guided access was performed using a long‐shank diamond bur. The canal was negotiated with #10 C‐files and #15 K‐files, prepared with Ni–Ti rotary instruments, and obturated using bioceramic sealer and warm gutta‐percha. Follow‐up radiographs at 3 months and 2 years demonstrated progressive healing of the periapical lesion without symptoms or complications (Figure 5).

Case 2: Static‐Guided Removal of a Fiber Post

A patient was referred for retreatment of tooth #22 after crown removal because of discomfort during biting. Clinical and radiographic examination revealed a fractured residual crown and a suspected fiber post in the coronal root canal, with inadequate coronal sealing of the previous restoration (Figure 6). Root canal retreatment and guided fiber post removal were planned.

Clinical examination at the initial visit for case 2. (A–C) Intraoral photographs taken at the initial visit. (D) Periapical radiograph taken at the initial visit. Preliminary diagnosis: #22 residual crown. Treatment plan: Removal of a #22‐fiber post under the guidance of a static 3D guide. Root canal retreatment and post‐crown restoration.

CBCT and intraoral scan data were combined to design a guide aligned with the long axis of the fiber post (Figure 7). Under static‐guide and microscopic assistance, the fiber post was conservatively removed using a diamond bur. Root canal retreatment was subsequently completed with rotary instrumentation and obturation (Figure 8). At the 1‐year follow‐up, the tooth remained asymptomatic with satisfactory radiographic healing (Figure 9).

Case 3: Static‐Guided Removal of a Posterior Zirconia Post

A patient presented with pain on biting associated with tooth #46 after previous endodontic treatment performed elsewhere. Clinical and radiographic examination demonstrated inadequate root canal filling, a periapical radiolucency, and a high‐density structure within the pulp chamber and root canals, suspected to be a zirconia post (Figure 10). Because the pulp chamber floor and canal orifices could not be identified clinically, guided zirconia post removal was planned.

Preoperative clinical and radiographic examination of case 3. (A) Intraoral photograph showing that the restoration on tooth #46 had been removed. (B) Occlusal view showing the temporary filling. (C) Preoperative periapical radiograph. (D) Preoperative CBCT image showing high‐density shadows within the pulp chamber and root canals, as well as a periapical radiolucent lesion. (E) Microscopic view after removal of the temporary filling, with the pulp chamber floor and canal orifices not identifiable.

CBCT and intraoral scan data were used to design a guide corresponding to the position and angulation of the zirconia post (Figure 11). Under microscopic guidance, the zirconia post was removed using diamond burs with continuous saline irrigation to minimize overheating. Root canal retreatment and obturation were then completed successfully (Figure 12). Three‐month follow‐up demonstrated symptom resolution and reduction of the periapical lesion (Figure 13).

Discussion

To facilitate the correct application of static guided endodontics by less experienced clinicians and to promote standardized data collection for future high‐quality clinical research, it is essential to establish a clear and reproducible treatment protocol. At present, most published in vivo reports focus on individual cases or single indications, with limited guidance regarding comprehensive clinical workflows. Based on the published papers, we summarized a standardized digital workflow that can be applied across different types of intracanal obstructions (Figure 14). This protocol covers case selection, preoperative imaging acquisition, virtual planning and guide design, guide fabrication, guided clinical execution, and postoperative evaluation. Such a standardized approach not only improves clinical predictability and reduces technique sensitivity but also provides a consistent framework for outcome assessment and future comparative studies, including randomized clinical trials.

Indications and contraindications: Static‐guided endodontic treatment allows for precise localisation of the root canal orifice, minimizes damage to dental tissues, and reduces the incidence of iatrogenic complications. Its primary indication is pulp chamber or root canal calcification/obliteration, which refers to calcification of the pulp chamber and root canals caused by trauma, orthodontic treatment, age‐related changes, or other irritating factors [12, 13]. Additionally, it is suitable for the removal of root canal posts and the treatment of teeth with anatomical abnormalities, such as dens invaginatus [14, 15].Excessively long or severely curved root canals constitute relative contraindications. These situations may make it difficult to find a guide drill that matches them, and as the preparation path for static guides is typically a straight approach, their clinical application is limited in cases of markedly curved root canals [16]. Other relative contraindications include limited mouth opening, especially in posterior regions where guide placement and drill access may be restricted. Insufficient remaining tooth structure, which compromises the stability and accuracy of guide positioning, and poor periodontal support or severe tooth mobility may reduce the precision of guided access due to inadequate tooth stability.

Data acquisition: All static guide technologies rely on CBCT to visualize root canal anatomy, as well as the tooth structure and surrounding anatomical structures. Intraoral data is typically acquired using an intraoral scanner to capture the surfaces of the upper and lower dentition and soft tissues, which are then saved as STL files [5].

Guide design and production: Static guide‐based endodontic treatment relies on 3D‐printed guides to precisely guide the drill. The accuracy of this treatment is influenced by various factors, including the material of the guide, the presence of windows, the height of the sleeve, the drill speed, and the penetration depth. The material of the guide directly affects precision, stability, sterilizability, and cost. Currently, common options include resin guides, metal guides, and guides with or without embedded metal sleeves. Among these, resin guides combined with metal sleeves can achieve higher angular and linear precision [17]. Window openings in the guide facilitate preoperative and intraoperative observation of the guide's fit with the tooth [18]. The core of guidance precision lies in the constrained length of the drill within the sleeve. Studies indicate that the apical deviation for a 4 mm metal sleeve is significantly greater than that for 6 mm and 8 mm sleeves, while the difference between 6 mm and 8 mm is not significant. This suggests that a sleeve height of ≥ 6 mm yields better apical accuracy [17]. The diameter and length of the drill should be selected based on tooth location, mouth opening, and the degree of tooth calcification. In vitro studies show that the longer the drilling distance, the greater the apical deviation; therefore, while ensuring precise guidance, the drilling depth should be minimized as much as possible [19].

Clinical execution: Prior to the procedure, the guide should be tried in the mouth to ensure accurate positioning and stability, and the procedure should be performed under rubber dam isolation [20]. If necessary, a thin layer of enamel may be removed first to create a flat platform, thereby preventing the guide drill from slipping on an inclined surface and causing a deviation in the path [16]. Subsequently, drilling should proceed stepwise through the sleeve along the designed path. In static guide‐assisted root canal therapy, the drill speed not only affects treatment efficiency but also directly influences the risk of thermal damage and instrument fracture. The literature reports that the drilling speed range for guides is approximately 350–10,000 rpm, and in cases involving the removal of fiber posts, it can even reach 40,000 rpm [19]. Selecting an appropriate speed and ensuring adequate cooling helps reduce the risk of overheating the root surface and instrument fracture. The intensity of intraoperative pain can be recorded using the VAS, Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), and Verbal Rating Scale (VRS) [9].

Postoperative evaluation and follow‐up: To provide high‐quality data for clinical research, metrics such as coronal deviation, apical deviation, and angular deviation should be collected after drill guidance using a surgical guide is completed. Current methods for obtaining these measurements primarily include indirect impressions or intraoral scanning following the placement of reference markers [9]. Although postoperative CBCT scans can also be used for evaluation, they are generally not recommended due to the increased radiation exposure to the patient.

The intensity of postoperative pain can be recorded using a pain scale. A periapical radiograph should be taken immediately postoperatively to assess the outcome of root canal treatment. Subsequently, periodic clinical and radiographic follow‐ups should be conducted to monitor periapical healing and the restoration of tooth function.

By integrating the above procedures, standardized operational and data collection guidelines can be provided to beginners, thereby standardizing the static guide‐assisted root canal treatment process and providing reliable data support for high‐quality clinical research. In this case series, one case of calcified root canals and two cases of post removal were treated in accordance with the above standards, and all cases met the criteria for success.

It is worth noting that, to our knowledge, this study represents the first clinical report of the successful removal of a zirconia post in the posterior region using a static guide, thereby further expanding the range of indications for static guide‐guided root canal treatment. Research on the use of guide files in root canal treatment has primarily focused on PCO, the removal of fiber posts, and the removal of cast metal posts [21]. Clinical reports on the removal of zirconia posts, particularly in the posterior region, remain extremely rare. This is not only because zirconia posts are used less frequently in clinical practice compared to fiber posts and metal posts, but more importantly, because the removal process itself is technically more challenging.

Compared to fiber posts and traditional metal posts, zirconia exhibits extremely high hardness, strong flexural strength, and significant brittleness, making its mechanical removal a highly challenging process [22]. Conventional drills are less efficient when cutting zirconia. Furthermore, due to its smooth surface, the drill bit is more prone to slipping during initial penetration. This is particularly true in the posterior region, where limited mouth opening and insufficient vertical access can more easily lead to drill bit deviation, root canal deviation, lateral perforation, and excessive removal of tooth structure. Furthermore, zirconia generates significant heat during cutting. Insufficient cooling may increase the risk of thermal damage to the periodontal ligament and surrounding supporting tissues [23]. Although zirconia produces relatively fewer radiographic artifacts compared to metal posts, the interfaces between the post, the adhesive, and the root canal walls may still be difficult to accurately identify on CBCT images, thereby complicating the design of the guide path.

Another significant limitation lies in the selection of burs. The burs commonly used in static‐guide‐assisted endodontic treatment today—such as the Munce Discovery bur, DSP precision drill, Komet guide drill, and pilot drills in implant guidance systems—are primarily suited for dentin removal, locating calcified root canals, or removing fiber posts [9, 24, 25, 26, 27]. However, due to their extremely high hardness, zirconia posts typically require diamond‐coated burs or special tungsten carbide burs with superior cutting capabilities. Yet, these instruments often do not integrate well with conventional guide sleeve systems, which significantly limits their clinical application and may be one of the key reasons for the scarcity of relevant clinical reports to date.

The most critical technical challenge in this case lies in the selection of the drill system. In Case 3, the distance from the occlusal surface of tooth 46 to the base of the zirconia post is approximately 10.75 mm. When combined with the approximately 2 mm height required for the guide sleeve, this means that the effective working length of the drill must be at least 12 mm, a requirement that standard drill bits often cannot meet. Furthermore, the diameter of this zirconia post, as measured on the CBCT, was approximately 1.82 mm at its thickest point and approximately 1.20 mm at its thinnest point, providing an important reference for selecting the drill bit diameter. However, due to the high radiopacity of zirconia itself, even though it produces relatively fewer artifacts compared to metal posts, it may still be affected by artifacts and image overlap from surrounding resin bonding materials in CBCT images, making it difficult to accurately determine the true boundaries of the post. Therefore, CBCT measurement results should only serve as a reference for preoperative planning. During clinical procedures, the selection of drill diameter must be dynamically adjusted based on actual microscopic observation, drill cutting feedback, and the condition of the root canal walls to determine whether the drill is truly suitable for the current case.

Considering the drill length, cutting efficiency, guidance stability, and the need for gradual removal of the post diameter, this study ultimately selected the Komet 801.M FGXL.012 and 801.M FGXL.014 drills (total length 25.6 mm, diameters 1.2 mm and 1.4 mm, respectively; diamond‐coated, round‐shaped). Specifically, the wider upper segment was first rapidly removed using the 1.4 mm drill to enhance cutting efficiency, while the narrower lower segment was then meticulously removed using the 1.2 mm drill to minimize damage to the root canal walls and improve guidance accuracy. Although larger drills can improve cutting efficiency, they may increase the risk of root canal wall damage and excessive removal of tooth structure; conversely, drills that are too small may reduce removal efficiency and prolong procedure time. Therefore, the final selection of the drill bit depends not only on imaging measurement data but also requires a comprehensive assessment based on actual clinical conditions. This staged, multi‐diameter removal strategy ensures operational efficiency while maximizing the preservation of remaining tooth structure, providing a reliable clinical approach for the removal of zirconia posts in the posterior region.

Furthermore, although zirconia is extremely hard, it is also highly brittle [28]. If excessive force is applied continuously during removal, it can easily lead to uncontrolled brittle fracture of the zirconia post, potentially causing damage to the root wall. Therefore, excessive pressure should not be applied continuously during treatment. An intermittent, gradual removal technique should be used to maintain cutting efficiency while minimizing the risk of postfracture [21]. To minimize thermal damage and prevent debris from obstructing the root canal, continuous irrigation with saline solution is required throughout the procedure to cool the area, remove debris, and maintain a clear visual field. Additionally, due to the extremely high hardness of zirconia, drill bits wear down rapidly during the procedure. Continuing to use severely worn drill bits can lead to reduced cutting efficiency, heat accumulation, and path deviation. Therefore, the degree of drill bit wear should be monitored closely during the procedure, and drill bits should be replaced promptly as needed to ensure the safety and precision of the treatment.

Given the limited occlusal space in the posterior region, the standard height of guide sleeves often restricts handpiece access and maneuverability [29]. Therefore, this study optimized the guide design by appropriately reducing the height of the guide sleeves, thereby improving operability in the posterior region while maintaining guidance accuracy. The entire treatment process was performed under microscopic guidance, and the zirconia post was gradually removed along the predefined path of the guide. Ultimately, precise removal was successfully achieved without any iatrogenic complications such as root perforation, excessive tooth structure removal, or instrument fracture, demonstrating that this protocol offers good safety and reproducibility.

Conclusion

This case series demonstrates that a standardized CBCT and intraoral scanning workflow offers a predictable, minimally invasive approach for complex intracanal obstructions, successfully extending static‐guided endodontics to ultra‐hard posterior zirconia post removal without iatrogenic complications. By reducing technique sensitivity, this unified digital framework enhances clinical reproducibility. Adopting these standardized guidelines establishes a vital methodological foundation for future randomized clinical trials to validate the long‐term efficacy of guided endodontics.
