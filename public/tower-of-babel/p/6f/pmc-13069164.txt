Episiotomy and Obstetric Anal Sphincter Injury in the Setting of Instrumental Vaginal Birth: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Meghan G. Hill, Aimee Humphries, Jessica Wilson, Amanda D. Harvey, Robin Cronin, John M. D. Thompson, Lynn C. Sadler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEpisiotomy may be performed during an instrumental vaginal birth. The evidence that the procedure is associated with less obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASI) in this setting is scarce.AimsWe undertook a retrospective cohort study to ascertain whether the performance of episiotomy was associated with fewer obstetric anal sphincter injuries in patients undergoing instrumental births over a 14‐year period from one tertiary institution.Materials and MethodsEvery vaginal birth at Te Toka Tumai Auckland from gestational age 36 weeks was included in a retrospective cohort spanning the years 2008–2021. Forceps‐assisted and Vacuum‐Extraction and Sequential Instrumental births were included. Univariable analysis was performed for the forceps, Vacuum‐extraction and Sequential Instrumental birth strata. Multivariable analysis was performed for all births using generalized linear models including maternal, neonatal and labour characteristics.ResultsBetween 2008 and 2021 there were 3714 forceps‐assisted births, 7793 Vacuum‐extraction births and 505 Sequential Instrumental births. The majority of these births were carried out with the use of a mediolateral episiotomy (89%, 66%, and 93.5% respectively) and OASI occurred in 9.5%, 5.2%, and 16.2%. Among people having an instrumental birth (all groups forceps, vacuum‐extraction and sequential instrumental births), mediolateral episiotomy was associated with lower relative risks of OASI after adjusting for type of instrument, parity and other clinical and demographic factors (RR 0.68 (0.58–0.80)).ConclusionMediolateral episiotomy was associated with a lower relative risk of OASI in patients having operative vaginal births.

Introduction

Guidelines vary regarding the performance of episiotomy in the setting of instrumental vaginal birth. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists does not recommend routine episiotomy in this circumstance [1]. The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists makes no specific recommendation on performance of either routine or restrictive use of episiotomy for instrumental vaginal birth in their Assisted Vaginal Birth Guideline [2] but recommends it in nulliparous patients in the OASI Care Bundle [3]. The Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists recommends that episiotomy be considered when a nulliparous patient is undergoing an instrumental vaginal birth, based on unpublished data from Women's Healthcare Australasia [4]. In New Zealand, the performance of episiotomy is exclusively mediolateral, as the standard of care, with midline episiotomy not being taught or considered acceptable practice. Mediolateral episiotomy is commonly performed with instrumental vaginal birth. We sought to investigate the association between episiotomy and OASI in patients giving birth with the assistance of forceps, with the assistance of vacuum extraction (either the metal ventouse or disposable cup) or with the use of sequential instruments.

Materials and Methods

A retrospective cohort study was performed which comprised vaginal births from 36 weeks' gestational age at the obstetric unit of Te Toka Tumai Auckland from 2008 to 2021. A sample size calculation was not performed as there was no way to increase the number of cases within the study timeframe. Additionally, it was estimated that there would be sufficient events within the specified timeframe to answer the study questions. The hospital maternity clinical database was utilized to ascertain the cases. Births where the occurrence of OASI or episiotomy was not recorded were excluded.

Data checking was performed for all OASI tears (single reviewer) and for missing and out‐of‐range data for extremes of head circumference (two reviewers). A random sample of births from the entire dataset (including unassisted vaginal births and instrumental vaginal births) was selected for audit to confirm the accuracy of reporting of tear data within the cohorts (Data S1, supplement for sister paper also included here). From all births over the study period, 50 s degree tears were screened, 5 of which were from instrumental births and none had their classification changed (the other 45 s degree tears from unassisted births are detailed in the Data S1). Additionally, 50 tears notated as 1st degree or intact perineum were screened, of which 6 were from instrumental births. No tears in this group were reclassified.

Instrumental births were identified from all births. Then, three strata were defined, according to mode of birth: forceps‐assisted vaginal birth, vacuum‐extraction vaginal birth, and sequential instrumental birth. Births from 36 weeks of singleton pregnancies were included in the study. Patients were excluded if they had a buttonhole tear with intact sphincter complex or if there was not OASI descriptive information available.

Statistical analysis plan: Bivariable comparisons of categorical variables were undertaken using chi‐squared tests, with relative risks and 95% confidence intervals produced from generalized linear models. Statistical comparisons of continuous variables were undertaken using Student's t‐tests. Statistical significance was assumed at p < 0.05. We included parity, previous caesarean, maternal height, Body Mass Index (BMI) category, maternal age at birth, maternal ethnicity, smoking status, augmentation of labour, induction of labour, length of second stage, health professional type: public (community or hospital midwife, hospital obstetrician, or General Practitioner) or private (obstetrician), socioeconomic deprivation quintile, gestational age by week, baby head circumference, baby birthweight and use of epidural for pain relief in our full statistical models. The assumption of linearity of continuous variables included in the models was checked using generalized additive models. We tested for an interaction between episiotomy and parity. The final model included all statistically significant variables and identified confounders of the association between episiotomy and OASI. Variables were eliminated manually in a backwards manner. Missing data points were treated as such. Data were not imputed.

Site: The Auckland Health Research Ethics Committee approved this study on 16/09/2021 (AH1140), and the Te Toka Tumai Auckland Research Office granted locality approval on 16/09/2021, study number (A + 9256). The study was reported in accordance with STROBE guidelines.

Results

During the 14‐year study period, there were 64,381 births. Missing OASI information resulted in 7 exclusions. This left 12,012 instrumental vaginal births with known OASI status, after two buttonhole tears with intact anal sphincter muscles were excluded from the analysis. Of these, there were 3714 forcep‐assistedbirths and 7793 Vacuum‐extraction births (Figure 1). There were 505 people where sequential instruments were used in a vaginal birth (Figure 1). In most, but not all cases, sequential instruments were Ventouse followed by forceps.

Discussion

Main Findings

In patients undergoing an instrumental birth (forceps, vacuum, or sequential instruments) there was an association between mediolateral episiotomy and increased OASI in our unadjusted model. However, there was a significant benefit to the performance of a mediolateral episiotomy when we adjusted for confounders. This may relate to episiotomies being performed in those who health professionals (correctly) believe to be the highest risk patients. Interestingly, our results closely approximated French data, which found similar reductions in OASI risk when an instrumental vaginal birth was performed [5, 6].

Results in the Context of Existing Literature

At first glance, our findings are in conflict with evidence from a randomized controlled trial in which patients undergoing a forceps‐assisted birth were allocated to either performance of ‘routine’ episiotomy or ‘restricted use’ of episiotomy [7]. There were no differences between groups in relation to birth outcomes for patients or their babies. However, most allocated to the ‘restricted use’ of episiotomy group underwent episiotomy, making the groups very similar in treatment exposure.

It is well established that forceps use does increase the risk of OASI [8, 9]. The increased risk is not in question, and forceps are important to safe care for patients in some circumstances. Several approaches may decrease the rate of OASI, including using vacuum extraction where feasible and substituting mediolateral episiotomy for midline episiotomy [10, 11]. While not all studies show that episiotomy is protective of OASI in the setting of forceps use [12], the balance of evidence suggests that mediolateral episiotomy specifically may decrease the risk of OASI in the setting of an instrumental vaginal birth, both with the use of forceps and vacuum extraction [5, 10, 11, 13, 14, 15].

We were aware of a randomized controlled trial where nulliparous participants were allocated to either no episiotomy or a mediolateral episiotomy for vacuum extraction during the design of our study [16]. The trial has since been published, indicating a benefit to episiotomy in the setting of instrumental birth with a vacuum‐extraction instrument in nullipara 0.47 (96% CI 0.23 to 0.97) [17]. Our findings are in line with those of this study, indicating that the risk of OASI is lower with vacuum‐extraction when compared to forceps, and lowest with vacuum‐extraction and mediolateral episiotomy when an instrumental vaginal birth is performed. While we would welcome results from an RCT regarding births assisted by forceps, we do believe that there is now sufficient evidence from multiple large, retrospective studies to indicate that a similar benefit exists to mediolateral episiotomy for all instrumental births in nulliparous patients. While there were parous patients included in our study, the number of OASI injuries in parous patients was small, and hence they were included in the model as opposed to being analysed separately. This is relevant to the interpretation of our results. It remains unclear whether there is a benefit to mediolateral episiotomy for instrumental vaginal birth in patients with a prior vaginal birth.

Clinical Implications

All obstetric providers and their patients should be able to weigh the available evidence to make informed decisions about best practice. Episiotomy is not without risk and has consequences for long‐term pelvic floor function. In our study, approximately a quarter of people having a first vaginal birth with the use of forceps or vacuum extraction had an intact perineum or a first‐degree laceration if an episiotomy was not performed. Considering the long‐term effects on continence when an OASI occurs, this trade‐off for a 100% chance of a second‐degree perineal laceration (which is incurred with a mediolateral episiotomy) will be appropriate for many people. However, in the absence of evidence that episiotomy protects against OASI in subsequent vaginal births where an instrument is used, avoidance of episiotomy may be preferable, considering half of these patients will have an intact perineum or first‐degree laceration only. As noted, the rate of OASI in this group was low in our study. We would suggest more research is needed to properly assess whether there is a benefit to mediolateral episiotomy in subsequent vaginal births where an instrument is used. Any future trials may therefore be most beneficial if they focus on the potential risks and benefits of mediolateral episiotomy for instrumental births in parous patients.

While there have long been concerns raised about the impact of the sequential instrument use on neonatal outcomes [18], the maternal injuries have not previously been fully explored. Our findings indicate maternal risk as well. There was a significant increase in the chance of OASI with the use of sequential instruments to effect birth. With Vacuum‐extraction as the reference, the relative risk for an OASI injury was 2.75. Careful consideration should be given in each clinical situation as to which approach is best, with an understanding that not all instrumental births will be successful and not all indicators of success are always able to be prescribed in guidelines. However, it is clear that sequential instrument use is associated with an increased risk for the maternal patient and does not just entail an elevated fetal risk.

Research Implications

One prominent risk factor for OASI in patients in our cohort was Indian ethnicity. This risk persisted after multivariable analysis. The reason for this is unclear, but the risk is documented in literature from Asia. One study on the use of forceps in Japan (with midline or mediolateral episiotomy performed at provider discretion) found that 29.9% and 5.7% of patients had 3rd and 4th degree tears, respectively [19]. The question of why this increased risk occurs is relevant to future avoidance of OASI injuries. The reported performance of medial episiotomy, as opposed to mediolateral episiotomy (performed in New Zealand) may be relevant. The available evidence indicates that Asian women do not have significantly shorter perineal length when compared to people of other ethnicities [20]. However, Asian people do generally have babies that are slightly smaller than those of other ethnicities that make up the majority of our patient sample. Whether or not instruments could be safely customized to fetal head size (or indeed maternal pelvis size) is unknown. It is worth noting that the forceps used on our unit are sourced from Europe and the United States. They were designed initially for use in the European pelvis. In some settings, customized forceps intended for use in Asian people are utilized [19]. There is no available research indicating whether adoption of these instruments in Asian patients residing in predominantly non‐Asian populations with use only of mediolateral (and not medial) episiotomy could improve outcomes in this group. However, we would suggest that other factors are relevant here for both forceps and vacuum extraction, noting that the fetal head circumference and weight were not statistically significantly associated with the risk of OASI (Table 2). Better understanding of the reasons for an increased OASI risk in Asian patients is a research priority.

Importantly, lower (less‐deprived) socioeconomic deprivation quintile and private obstetrician as the health professional (both highly associated with individual‐familial wealth and standard of living) were associated with a lower risk of OASI in our cohort. The reasons for this are unclear, although nutrition and subsequent tissue integrity are unexplored contributors to outcomes. This may also be relevant when looking at the risk of OASI by ethnicity, as almost all of the Indian patients in the cohort are new immigrants to New Zealand [21].

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of our study relate to the number of births included. There is a heterogeneous population included in our cohort, which provides important information on relative risks based on ethnicity, BMI and parity. Additionally, a single reviewer accessed every patient record where an OASI occurred, and a random sample of controls without OASI to ensure accuracy of the primary outcome data presented. Likewise, extremes for baby head circumference at birth were manually checked to ensure accuracy. This provides an advantage as compared to studies utilizing databases without any manual confirmation with the chart for clinical data.

Limitations included that not every chart was reviewed for checking of the existing data supplied for the study. This was not feasible considering the large number of patients within the cohort. However, we believe that our approach in checking each OASI and a random sample from controls was a reasonable approach to address concerns about the integrity of the data and any bias in identification of the cases. Additionally, this study period is considerable and there was a rise in identification of OASIs over the study period. While we suspect this is due to improved identification of OASI, the reason cannot be confidently identified due to the retrospective nature of the research. Concurrently, the caesarean birth rate increased during the study period which implies the complexity of the instrumental births at our institution may have changed during the study period, which represents another limitation of our work.

While it does appear that there was provider assessment of risk in individual patients, which modified the chance an episiotomy would be performed (as evidenced by the fact that Asian people and those of shorter stature had a higher chance of episiotomy), due to the retrospective design, reasons for performance of an episiotomy cannot be known in individual cases.

Lastly and perhaps most importantly, we were unable to assess whether mediolateral episiotomy appears to be of clinical utility in people undergoing either forceps or vacuum extraction when they have already undergone a prior vaginal birth. The number of subsequent births included in the cohort was small and so a recommendation cannot be made based on our results. Caution would be suggested in recommending the procedure in this clinical situation, as in a contemporaneous cohort of people having spontaneous births (see sister article reporting outcomes in unassisted vaginal births), episiotomy was associated with an increased risk of OASI when the birth was a subsequent vaginal birth.

In summary, people undergoing an instrumental vaginal birth with the use of forceps or vacuum‐extraction appear to have a lower chance of OASI when a mediolateral episiotomy is performed. The use of sequential instruments is associated with a significant increase in the risk of an OASI injury. We have an insufficient number of cases to provide conclusive guidance regarding episiotomy performance for instrumental vaginal birth in people who have had a prior vaginal birth.
