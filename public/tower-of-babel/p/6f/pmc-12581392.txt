Differential prognostic significance of tumor size and node in invasive lobular carcinoma of breast
Authors: Eunhye Kang, Ik Beom Shin, Ji-Jung Jung, Jinyoung Byeon, Yunhee Choi, Jong-Ho Cheun, Han-Byoel Lee, Wonshik Han, Hong-Kyu Kim, Hyeong-Gon Moon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInvasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is the second most common histological type of breast cancer. Despite its distinct clinical and pathological characteristics, ILC shares the same prognostic classification system and adjuvant treatment strategies as invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC).MethodsWe reviewed the data of the 16,365 IDC and ILC patients who were treated between 2005 and 2022 at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH). Also, we conducted a parallel analysis using the data of 247,290 IDC and ILC treated between 2010 and 2015 from the surveillance, epidemiology, and end results (SEER) database.ResultsILC patients were older at diagnosis, had larger tumors, and ILC tumors were predominantly HR-positive/HER2-negative. ILC exhibited unique prognostic implications of anatomic features. Unlike in IDC, the tumor size and nodal status showed differential prognostic impact on survival in ILC. While there was no significant survival difference for different T stages in ILC patients, the prognostic impact of nodal involvement was more pronounced for ILC in the SNUH dataset. Similar survival implication of tumor size and nodal involvement was observed for the ILC cases in the SEER dataset.ConclusionIn ILC, tumor size may not be as strong a predictor of survival as in IDC, while advanced nodal involvement carries a particularly high risk for breast cancer-specific mortality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02141-3.

Background

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women worldwide, and its incidence is steadily increasing among Korean women [1, 2]. Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is the second most common histological type of breast cancer following invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), accounting for 5–15% of all breast cancer cases [3–5]. ILC exhibits pathological and clinical characteristics that differ than those of IDC. Compared to IDC, ILC lacks E-cadherin expression and is predominantly hormone receptor (HR) positive and human epidermal growth factor2 (HER2) negative [6, 7]. Studies have shown that multifocality and bilaterality incidence is higher in ILC than in IDC [8, 9], while some have suggested possible differences in stage distribution and metastatic features between ILC and IDC [6, 10, 11]. Results on the prognostic impact of ILC compared to those of IDC are conflicting [3, 6, 12–16].

Tumor cell spread pattern is a key histological characteristic of ILC. Tumor cells in ILC exhibit a “single file” spread pattern, and gland formation is rare [17]. ILC does not show a substantial stromal response compared to IDC [18]. These unique histological features make ILC difficult to detect through lump palpation or imaging screening [19]. Furthermore, this lack of distinct mass formation in ILC leads to an increased incidence of tumor size underestimation [20] and higher resection margin positivity rates following breast-conservation surgery.

Lump formation in breast cancer, previously described as desmoplastic reaction, reflect a complex interaction within the tumor microenvironment involving myofibroblasts, collagen, secreted factors such as platelet-derived growth factor and CD36 membrane protein [21, 22]. Several decades of research have demonstrated that the dynamic shaping of the tumor microenvironment is closely associated with tumor progression and metastasis [23]. As the ILC show a distinctly different stromal response and tumor microenvironment features from the IDC, one can postulate that the clinical importance of tumor size can also carry differential implication between the ILC and IDC.

However, major breast cancer treatment guidelines propose a uniform approach for tumor size when deciding on various adjuvant treatment options for IDC and ILC [24, 25]. This is probably due to the lack of large-scaled studies that specifically investigated the linear prognostic importance of tumor size in ILC. The aim of this study is to address this issue by utilizing both the Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) database which provide detailed clinicopathologic information and the nationwide Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database of ILC.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively collected data from 16,365 consecutive female patients who underwent breast surgery for invasive breast cancer between January 2005 and December 2022 at SNUH, Korea. The study included patients diagnosed with either IDC or ILC. Patients with distant metastasis at the time of diagnosis were excluded from the study. Additionally, patients with T4 stage disease and those with unknown T stage or N stage were also excluded from the analysis.

Tumors classified as ILC had invasive lobular histologic type, and IDC had only invasive ductal carcinoma. Tumor stages were based on the 7th edition of American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) classification. Mixed invasive ductal and lobular carcinomas (IDC/ILC) were excluded from both groups to maintain clear histological distinction between the two cohorts. ILC group included all subtypes of invasive lobular carcinoma. However, specific subtype information (classic, pleomorphic, or other variants) was not consistently available in pathology reports from SNUH dataset and SEER dataset. T and N stages were defined as follows: Pathological T and N stages for patients who initially underwent surgery; higher clinical and pathological T and N stages for those who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy; and clinical N stage for those who did not undergo axillary surgery.

HR expression was considered positive if at least 1% of the tumor stained. HER2 overexpression was defined as 3 + and/or a positive fluorescent in situ hybridization test. The Institutional Review Board of SNUH approved this study’s protocol (H-2308-007-1455) and waived the informed consent requirement due to its retrospective nature and the minimal risk to the participants.

SEER data analysis

SEER*Stat version 8.4.2 was utilized to filter and collect the information of representative patients in the research (http://seer.cancer.gov/). Patients were selected from the “SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2023 Sub (2000–2021)-Linked to County Attributes—Time Dependent (1990–2022) Income/Rurality, 1969–2022 Counties, National Cancer Institute, Division of Cancer Control and Population Sciences, Surveillance Research Program”, released in April 2024, based on the November 2023 submission. We finally focused on 247,290 eligible patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2015, as breast cancer subtype information was available from 2010 onwards. The inclusion criterion was patients with microscopically confirmed primary IDC or ILC. Specifically, the breast cancer stage was based on the AJCC classification, 7th edition. Patients were excluded if they were male, had distant metastasis at diagnosis or T4 stage, had in situ cancer or unknown TNM stage, or had unknown survival time. The histological classification was based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O) codes. Cancers with histology ICD-O-3 codes 8500 and 8520 were classified as IDC and ILC, respectively (Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

The clinical and pathological characteristics of the two groups were compared using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data and the independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U-test for continuous data. Breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of death due to breast cancer. Patients who died from other causes or were alive at the end of follow-up were censored at the time of last contact. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of surgery to the date of death. Patients who were alive at the end of follow-up were censored at the time of last contact. In the SEER data, patients with a cause-specific death classification of "Dead (attributable to this cancer dx)" were considered under breast cancer-specific death, while those with a classification of alive, dead of other cause, unknown death and patients lost to follow-up were considered alive for the purpose of BCSS analysis.

Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier plots and Cox proportional hazards models. We categorized the follow-up period into early and late periods in cases where the Kaplan–Meier plots intersected, and the proportional hazards assumption was violated. To account for the potential time-varying effects of the covariates, we employed a stratified Cox proportional hazards model using a step-function approach. The analysis time was split at 60 months, creating two distinct time intervals (0–60 months and > 60 months). Subsequently, the model was stratified based on these intervals, allowing the effect of covariates to vary between the early (≤ 60 months) and late (> 60 months) follow-up periods. Cox proportional hazard analysis was used to obtain hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) in the multivariable analyses. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.3.

Results

In the SEER dataset, 11% of the patients had ILC. A greater proportion of patients with ILC were 50 years or older compared to IDC patients. Similar to the SNUH dataset, ILC tumors were characterized by a larger tumor size and higher low-grade tumor incidence (p < 0.001). Additionally, 94% of ILC patients in the SEER dataset were HR-positive and HER2-negative. In the SEER dataset, the crude proportion of node-positive cases was slightly higher in ILC than in IDC (30.7% vs. 28.4%, p < 0.001), whereas in the SNUH dataset, IDC demonstrated a higher node positivity rate than ILC.

To further investigate the relationship between tumor histology, size, and nodal involvement, we performed a stratified analysis examining lymph node positivity by T stage (Table S1). In the SNUH dataset, IDC showed significantly higher lymph node positivity rates than ILC at each T stage: T1 (18.73% vs. 12.06%), T2 (57.02% vs. 36.78%), and T3 (93.87% vs. 74.11%) (all p < 0.001). Similarly, in the SEER dataset, IDC patients had higher nodal metastasis rates than ILC patients at T1 (17.18% vs. 15.44%), T2 (46.47% vs. 40.74%), and T3 (69.12% vs. 64.52%) stages (all p < 0.001). When stratified by tumor size, IDC consistently demonstrated higher rates of nodal involvement compared to ILC across all T stages.

Comparison of treatment of IDC and ILC

The treatment patterns differed substantially between patients with IDC and those with ILC (Table 2). Due to the limited treatment details in the SEER dataset, these analyses were conducted using the SNUH cohort. Substantial differences in systemic therapy administration were observed between the two histological subtypes. Chemotherapy was administered to 63.6% and 49.1% with IDC and ILC, respectively (p < 0.001). Endocrine therapy was more prevalent in patients with ILC than in those with IDC (96.6% vs. 74.7%, p < 0.001). Given that ILC patients predominantly had HR+/HER2- subtype tumors, we performed a subgroup analysis within each receptor subtype to control for this confounding factor. When restricted to HR+/HER2- cases, IDC patients still received chemotherapy more frequently than ILC patients (50.1% vs. 46.5%, respectively, p = 0.04). The local therapy patterns also differed between two groups. Radiation therapy was similar between the IDC and ILC groups (74.6% vs. 73.0%, p = 0.297). Regarding surgical management, patients with ILC had higher mastectomy rates than those with IDC (44.7% vs. 36.1%, p < 0.001). For axillary management, axilla lymph node dissection (ALND) was more frequently performed in patients with IDC than in those with ILC. Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) was more common in ILC patients (p = 0.001). In the HR+/HER2- subtype, ILC patients still had higher mastectomy rates (45.1% vs. 34.0%, p < 0.001) and lower ALND rates (23.3% vs. 26.4%, p = 0.014), but no differences in surgical management between ILC and IDC were observed in other subtypes (Table S2).

Comparison of survival outcomes between IDC and ILC

In the SNUH dataset, the median follow-up time for the 16,365 patients was 94 months (range, 0–232 months). The median follow-up time for cases in the SEER dataset was 95 months (range, 0-143 months). In the SEER dataset, the Kaplan–Meier survival analysis with log-rank tests demonstrated significant differences in BCSS and OS between ILC and IDC groups (p < 0.001; Fig. 1C, D). After adjusting for other risk factor in the SEER dataset, patients with ILC showed a small but significantly increased risk of death (adjusted HR: 1.11, 95% CI: 1.06–1.16, p < 0.001 for BCSS and adjusted HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.07, p = 0.004 for OS, Table S4). The survival curve crossover points were 73 months for BCSS and 54 months for OS, respectively (Fig. 1C, D). Since studies have suggested that the prognostic significance of ILC histology is time-dependent, we performed an additional analysis focusing on the follow-up period of patients with ILC. For BCSS and OS, patients with ILC showed better survival than those with IDC during the first 5-years (adjusted HR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.86–0.96 for BCSS and adjusted HR = 0.94, 95% CI = 0.91–0.97 for OS) but worse outcome after the first 5-years (adjusted HR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.44–1.63 for BCSS and adjusted HR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.14–1.23 for OS).

Unique prognostic implications of the anatomical features in ILC

Since ILC tumors are predominantly HR-positive and HER2-negative, we restricted the survival analysis of ILC and IDC to the HR-positive and HER2-negative tumors in the SEER dataset. Subsequently, we grouped the patients into different anatomical stages based on the T and N stages. A proportional decrease in the 10-year BCSS was observed in patients with IDC according to the T and N stages. Across all N stages (N0-N3), the difference in 10-year BCSS between T1 and T3 was markedly smaller in patients with ILC than in those with IDC (Fig. 2). Specifically, the difference in the 10-year BCSS between the T1 and T3 groups were 14.7%, 17.9%, 15.5%, and 15.1% for N0, N1, N2 and N3, respectively, in the patients with IDC. These differences were 7.9%, 8.9%, 12.2%, and -0.2% for N0, N1, N2, and N3, respectively, in the patients with ILC. Analysis using the SEER dataset further corroborated these findings. For IDC, T2 and T3 stages showed 2.2-fold (95% CI: 2.14–2.29) and 3.4-fold (95% CI: 3.20–3.51) increased risks, respectively. N1, N2, and N3 stages had 1.7-fold (95% CI: 1.68–1.80), 2.8-fold (95% CI: 2.70–2.96), and 4.2-fold (95% CI: 3.95–4.41) higher risks, respectively. In ILC, the impact of tumor size was less pronounced, with T2 and T3 stages showing 1.9-fold (95% CI: 1.69–2.04) and 2.4-fold (95% CI: 2.17–2.71) increased risks. However, nodal involvement in ILC again showed higher impact on the hazard ratio, with N2 and N3 stages having 3.5-fold (95% CI: 1.66–16.57) and 6.2-fold (95% CI: 5.53–6.90) increase, compared to 2.8-fold and 4.2-fold in IDC for N2 and N3, respectively (Fig. 3B, D). To validate that the greater impact of nodal involvement in ILC is independent of tumor size, we performed T stage-stratified analysis. This confirmed that ILC consistently showed more pronounced hazard ratio escalation with advancing nodal stages compared to IDC across all tumor size categories (T1, T2, and T3, Table S5).

Discussion

This study offers valuable insights into the unique characteristics of ILC by highlighting its distinct clinicopathological features and prognostic significance. Consistent with previous studies, we observed that ILC was associated with a higher proportion of HR+/HER2- breast cancer, lower tumor grade, and lower ki-67 index than IDC [26, 27]. Although these features typically suggest a favorable prognosis, the unique growth pattern of ILC presents a diagnostic challenge that complicates this outcome. The characteristic single-file growth pattern of ILC typically results in a less palpable mass formation. This unique feature frequently leads to delayed diagnosis, resulting in larger tumor sizes and more advanced stages at detection, as demonstrated in our study [12, 13, 28]. Here, we demonstrated that the prognostic value of tumor size and nodal involvement in ILC substantially differed from the well-known patterns observed in IDC. Our data suggest the need to improve the current practice of incorporating anatomic staging features regardless of the breast cancer histological type when deciding on multimodal adjuvant therapies.

In this study, we examined the prognostic importance of tumor size and nodal involvement in the context of histologic types of breast cancer. Although ILC tends to present with larger tumor than IDC, we found that the impact of tumor size on survival was not as substantial in ILC. Our multivariate analysis using the SNUH dataset showed no significant differences in BCSS between T1 and T2/T3 stages of ILC. This finding was further supported by our SEER data validation, which demonstrated that the adjusted HRs for T2 and T3 stages increased less in ILC than IDC. Narbe et al. showed that the tumor size was not an independent prognostic factor for survival in ILC [29]. Similarly, Chamalidou et al. reported that while relative survival did not differ between IDC and ILC for tumors ≤ 10 mm in size, ILC showed better survival for larger tumor sizes [14]. Collectively, these data suggest that the prognostic importance of tumor size is less substantial in ILC than in IDC. The lower impact of tumor size on survival in ILC may be explained by its unique growth properties. ILC often grows in single line strands, resulting in relatively sparse cellularity [30, 31]. This hypothesis is supported by the findings of Tian et al., who demonstrated that ILC exhibits lower tumor purity (defined as proportion of malignant cells relative to stromal and immune cell components) [30].

Compared to the limited prognostic value of tumor size in ILC, our study revealed the critical prognostic importance of nodal involvement in ILC. A previous analysis of the SEER data also showed that, in N2/N3 breast cancer, ILC exhibited worse survival outcomes than IDC [15]. Similarly, the SUCCESS trial demonstrated that while patient with ILC showed better survival in pN0/pN1 tumors, they had worse survival in pN2/pN3 tumors compared to those with non-special type carcinoma [32]. The loss of E-cadherin, a key protein associated with epithelial-mesenchymal transition and cancer cell spread [33, 34], is a well-known feature of ILC that contributes to its unique molecular mechanisms of metastasis. ILC also exhibits substantially lower response rates to systemic chemotherapy, as evidenced by the lower rate of pathological complete response after neoadjuvant therapy [11, 35]. Furthermore, the tumor cells in ILC are relatively bioenergetically quiescent and have lower protein translation efficiency suggesting a more ‘dormant’ status. Indeed, these cells can be more ‘dormant’ compared to that in IDC [30]. This biological characteristic is further supported by a large-scale study comparing IDC and ILC, which found that only 2% of ER-positive ILC patients were classified as high risk using genomic assays [3]. The relatively low chemo-sensitivity and more dormant status of ILC contribute the observed prognostic significance of nodal involvement of ILC since systemic chemotherapy is less likely to eradicate the disseminated tumor cells.

Interestingly, our study demonstrated higher lymph node metastasis rates in IDC compared to ILC, which contrasts with some previous literature [3–5]. This discrepancy can be explained by several factors. First, there is a possibility of selection bias due to the relatively small proportion of ILC patients (6%) in our dataset and the inclusion of a higher proportion of advanced-stage patients, which is characteristic of a tertiary referral center. Second, the higher lymph node positivity rates reported for ILC in previous studies may be attributed to the fact that ILC is generally diagnosed at larger tumor sizes than IDC, and lymph node metastasis shows a strong positive correlation with tumor size. In SNUH dataset, T stage stratified analysis demonstrated that IDC consistently showed higher lymph node metastasis rates than ILC within the same tumor size category, and this finding was also confirmed in the independent SEER dataset (Table S1). These findings suggest that adjustment for tumor size and consideration of selection bias when understanding lymph node metastasis patterns according to histologic types.

Despite the findings of the present study, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the retrospective nature of this study introduced potential selection and information biases. Although we used both single-institution and SEER data to strengthen our observations, the SEER dataset lacked detailed information on specific treatment protocols and adherence to long-term endocrine therapy. These factors may differ between patients with ILC and those with IDC, potentially influencing outcomes. Additionally, information on ILC histologic subtypes (classic, pleomorphic, solid, etc.) was not available in our datasets. Given that different ILC subtypes may have distinct prognostic implications [36], the inability to stratify patients by these histologic variants represents an important limitation that may have influenced ILC survival analyses. Therefore, future prospective studies with more comprehensive data collection and longer follow-up periods are needed to validate and expand upon our findings, address these limitations, and further elucidate the complex relationships among tumor size, nodal involvement, and survival in ILC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our data suggest that the prognostic importance of tumor size and nodal involvement differ in ILC. Notably, this information should be integral to the decision-making process for patients with this histological type of breast cancer.
