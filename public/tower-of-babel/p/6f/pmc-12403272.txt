Usefulness of biofeedback as a potentially educational treatment of temporomandibular disorders: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Ada María González-González, Manuel Tello Pellitero, Natalia López Tejerina, Xabier A Soto-Goñi, Juan Nicolás Cuenca-Zaldívar, Eleuterio A Sánchez-Romero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of biofeedback (BF) as a potential educational treatment for temporomandibular disorders (TMD) through a systematic review and a network meta-analysis.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across five electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, PEDro, Scopus, and Cochrane Library) to identify clinical trials using BF as a therapeutic intervention in patients diagnosed with TMD according to RDC/TMD or DC/TMD criteria. A frequentist fixed-effects network meta-analysis was performed with robust variance estimation to account for within-study correlations in the repeated measures. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using an autoregressive lag-1 (AR1) model at correlation levels ranging from 0 to 0.8. Model fit was assessed using likelihood ratio tests (LRT) and corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc).ResultsNine studies (N = 758) were included. No statistically significant differences in pain intensity were observed between the interventions at post-treatment or follow-up. The fixed-effects model without autocorrelation showed the best fit (AICc = 54.293). According to the P-score ranking, occlusal splints and no intervention showed the highest probabilities of effectiveness, whereas biofeedback ranked lower. No publication bias was detected according to Egger’s test and funnel plot symmetry.ConclusionsAlthough BF did not outperform other interventions in terms of pain intensity, it showed comparable efficacy and may offer additional benefits by promoting self-regulation and psychological resilience. These findings suggest that BF can be explored as a complementary tool within the biopsychosocial approach to TMD rehabilitation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06789-3.Keywords: Temporomandibular disorders, Biofeedback, Meta-analysis, Education, Systematic review

Introduction

Biofeedback (BF) is a technique that trains individuals to consciously regulate certain physiological processes. It offers real-time feedback to users regarding changes in their physiological activity [1]. Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are a group of clinical conditions that affect the masticatory muscles, temporomandibular joints, and related structures [2]. According to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD), TMD is the second most prevalent musculoskeletal condition leading to pain and disability [3]. Regardless of the cause, these clinical conditions can lead to centrally mediated chronic muscle pain, myalgia, myofascial pain, myofibrotic contracture, myositis, myospasm, headaches, and various types of neck, shoulder, upper-back, and lower-back pain. In addition to pain, TMD is marked by difficulty in fully opening the jaw, locking in either an open or closed position, and clicking or grating noise in the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) [4].

BF has been identified as an effective tool for treating TMD, both on its own and in conjunction with other treatments [5]. BF is part of the clinical practice guidelines for managing temporomandibular disorders and is particularly beneficial in chronic patients [6]. Several systematic reviews have identified TMD as the leading cause of pain in the orofacial region, with diagnoses becoming more frequent [5, 7, 8]. Psychological and psychosocial disorders have been found to be significantly related to the duration and persistence of symptoms in patients with chronic TMD pain, thus affecting their quality of life [9].

Many patients with chronic TMD have been assigned to BF treatment, which has proven effective in reducing electromyographic activity, even in the long term. The physiological systems that can be used to measure BF include the neuromuscular, respiratory, and cardiovascular systems, whereas biomechanical BF involves assessing movement, postural control, and force [8].

BF has been applied in various ways, often in conjunction with oral appliances. Most studies have focused on patients with bruxism [5]. However, in most studies, BP therapy was performed using a splint, with the patient remaining awake to be aware of its use in bruxism cases [6, 10]. Some researchers suggest that more evidence is needed, particularly regarding occlusion splints such as the BF [11]. Similar findings have been reported for electromyographic biofeedback (EMG-BF), the most commonly used and documented biofeedback method. However, the limited number of randomized controlled trials and comprehensive systematic reviews indicates the need for further research. The current evidence regarding the use of biofeedback in musculoskeletal and neurological rehabilitation is promising [4].

Beyond its physiological effects, biofeedback may also serve as an educational treatment by helping patients consciously recognize and regulate patterns of muscle tension and parafunctional activity. By providing real-time visual, auditory, or tactile feedback, BF promotes learning and behavioral changes, particularly in relation to stress, posture, and mandibular function. In this sense, BF aligns with the principles of the biopsychosocial model by targeting both physical and psychological components of chronic TMD. Educational interventions of this kind are designed to increase self-efficacy, support cognitive-behavioral adaptation, and foster long-term engagement in self-management strategies.

Therefore, this systematic review and network meta-analysis were conducted to address this important gap in the literature. While biofeedback is included in clinical guidelines and supported by various narrative reviews for its role in managing TMD symptoms, no prior network meta-analysis has synthesized its comparative effectiveness across different delivery modalities. Furthermore, the potential educational value of BF, particularly in terms of promoting self-regulation and psychosocial adaptation, remains poorly conceptualized and rarely measured. This review aimed to provide an updated and structured synthesis of the available evidence, clarify the role of BF in multidisciplinary TMD rehabilitation, and identify areas that require further empirical development. In this context, BF may not only contribute to pain reduction in patients with TMD but also improve their overall quality of life by influencing psychological variables associated with the disorder, such as anxiety and stress.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and network meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA-NMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [12], and was registered in PROSPERO before the review commenced (CRD42024598509).

Search strategies

An electronic systematic search was performed across several databases, including PubMed, Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro), Web of Science (WOS), Scopus, and Cochrane, without any limitations on publication date up to December 2024. The review was guided by the following research question, structured according to the PICO framework: In individuals diagnosed with temporomandibular disorders (population), is the effectiveness of biofeedback interventions (intervention) compared to other conservative treatments or no treatment (comparison) in reducing pain intensity and improving functional or psychological outcomes (outcomes)?

The search strategy used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms) and non-MeSH terms and employed Boolean operators (AND/OR) to refine the search results. In line with clinical guidelines [13, 14], no language restrictions were imposed.

The search was narrowed to include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published in peer-reviewed journals in English, Portuguese, or Spanish. The main MeSH terms employed were “temporomandibular disorders” and “biofeedback,” which were tailored to the search capability of each database. Eligibility criteria

This review encompasses clinical trials that have explored the application of biofeedback as educational therapy for individuals diagnosed with temporomandibular disorders (TMD) based on the Research Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (RDC-TMD) or the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC-TMD) [3]. Only studies that diagnosed TMD using RDC/TMD or DC/TMD criteria were eligible for inclusion. Studies that focused exclusively on patients with bruxism, without a confirmed diagnosis of TMD according to these criteria, were excluded. The focus was on studies in which biofeedback was utilized as a primary therapeutic method, rather than merely as a supplementary tool.

This review offers a targeted examination of research that evaluates the use of biofeedback as an educational and therapeutic instrument for managing TMD by applying these criteria. For the purposes of this review, an “educational” biofeedback intervention was operationally defined as one that aimed to promote self-regulation, behavioral change, or physiological awareness through real-time feedback and therapist-guided interpretation. This included interventions in which patients were instructed to modify muscle activity based on visual, auditory, or vibratory cues or in which biofeedback was combined with cognitive or behavioral strategies. Studies were excluded if biofeedback was applied passively, solely during sleep, or if there were no elements of patient interaction or feedback interpretation. Studies in which biofeedback was used solely as a monitoring tool, without a therapeutic role or patient feedback-based engagement, were excluded.

Study selection and data extraction

All references retrieved from the database searches were first imported into Rayyan® (Qatar Computing Research Institute), a web-based platform designed to support systematic reviews. Software was used to automatically identify and remove duplicate records before screening.

Initially, two independent researchers (A.M.G.G. and E.A.S.R.) conducted preliminary screening of all articles obtained from the database search. This initial review involved examining titles and abstracts to assess their potential eligibility according to predefined inclusion criteria. Both reviewers independently evaluated the studies and agreed on the articles to be included in this review.

For studies that satisfied the initial criteria, a full-text analysis was conducted to ensure that they met the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion in cases of uncertainty or disagreement regarding a study’s eligibility. If needed, a third reviewer was consulted to make the final decision.

After selecting the studies, data extraction was performed independently by two researchers (J.N.C.Z. and E.A.S.R.). The extracted data included information, such as author(s) and publication year, study objectives, research design, characteristics of the study population, intervention methods, outcome measures, and key findings. Additional data were extracted for the meta-analysis of the studies included in the quantitative synthesis. These included group means and standard deviations (SDs) for post-treatment and/or follow-up pain intensity, electromyographic (EMG) muscle activity, and any psychological outcome measures when available. Information on the sample size, intervention type, comparator group, and outcome timing was also collected.

Risk of bias assessment

The revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized controlled trials (RoB 2.0) was used to evaluate the potential for bias. The RoB 2.0 tool evaluates five domains: (1) bias arising from the randomization process, (2) bias due to deviations from the intended interventions, (3) bias due to missing outcome data, (4) bias in measurement of the outcome, and (5) bias in selection of the reported result [15]. Each area was assessed as either “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” and studies were categorized into one of three overall risk levels: “low risk of bias,” “some concerns,” or “high risk of bias.”

Two independent reviewers (M.T.P. and N.L.T.) conducted the bias assessment using this methodology. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus. To evaluate inter-rater reliability, the kappa coefficient (κ) was calculated, with κ > 0.7, indicating strong agreement between reviewers; κ between 0.5 and 0.7%, indicating moderate agreement; and κ < 0.5 indicating low agreement [16].

Analyses

The primary objective of this analysis was to compare the effectiveness of different biofeedback interventions and comparators in managing TMD-related outcomes. A network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted using a frequentist approach to simultaneously synthesize direct and indirect comparisons across the included studies. Owing to the sparse geometry of the network and the limited number of studies per comparison, a fixed-effects model was selected based on model fit criteria. P-scores were calculated to rank the treatments based on their probability of being the most effective intervention.

For statistical analysis, R Ver. 4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Institute for Statistics and Mathematics, Welthandelsplatz 1, 1020 Vienna, Austria), and metafor packages were used [17].

A multilevel frequentist network meta-analysis (NMA) with repeated measures was performed to evaluate the moderating effect of the time of evaluation (post-treatment and follow-up) on pain intensity using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) between the two groups.

Because of the violation of the principle of independence when analyzing repeated measures in the studies, the standard errors were adjusted using the robust variance estimator (RVE) proposed by Pustejovsky and Tipton [18] and applying the Satterthwaite adjustment to the degrees of freedom. The effect size thus calculated was defined as small (< 0.2), moderate (0.2–0.8) and large (> 0.8).

Because different measurement moments appear in the studies, the procedure described by Efthimiou et al., [19], was followed, performing a sensitivity analysis on the meta-analysis with an autoregressive model of lag 1 (AR1) adjusting the correlation between the different measurement moments (pre-post-treatment and follow-up) with a correlation of 0, 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8, taking as a selection criterion the model with the most precise results in its confidence intervals.

The selection of a fixed- or random-effects model was carried out using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) between both, evaluating the level of significance and the values of the Akaike Information Criterion corrected for small samples (AICc), with the objective of detecting overfitting of the models. While the model comparison criteria (AICc and likelihood ratio tests) favored the fixed-effects model, we recognize that this choice assumes homogeneity across studies. Given the clinical variability in interventions and populations, results from the fixed-effects model should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis generating rather than conclusive. Additionally, although the fixed-effects model was preferred, a sensitivity analysis using a random-effects model was performed to account for potential clinical heterogeneity. This analysis yielded similar patterns of non-significant pairwise differences, but with wider confidence intervals and substantially poorer model fit (AICc = 171.561), reinforcing the hypothesis-generating character of our findings.

The transitivity assumption was evaluated by assuming that all the analyzed interventions presented the same results, regardless of the study to which they belonged. To this end, the model was controlled by adjusting for age, male/female ratio, and number of months with pain as confounding variables, after comparison with the initial model using an LRT and evaluation of the AICc, as mentioned above.

Inconsistency was evaluated by comparing the consistency model with the inconsistency model and analyzing the contribution of each study to the value of the Cochrane test.

The effectiveness of the treatments was analyzed using the P-score ranking and league table for pairwise comparisons.

Finally, publication bias was analyzed using a funnel plot adjusted for each comparison and the Egger’s test.

Results

The search of electronic databases resulted in 337 articles: 113 from PubMed, 106 from Web of Science (WOS), 8 from PEDro, 81 from Scopus, and 29 from Cochrane. Seventy-four articles were screened, after eliminating 51 duplicates, a total of 74 articles were screened. A thorough review and application of the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria led to the inclusion of nine articles in the review. Seven of these studies [20–23, 27, 28], evaluated patients with chronic temporomandibular disorders. Two studies [24, 25] reported no history of facial pain, chronic headaches, or other chronic pain conditions at the start of training. In one study, patients had acute jaw or facial pain for les tan six months [26]. In all nine studies, diagnoses were established using the RDC/TMD criteria. None of the included trials focused exclusively on bruxism, and participants met the TMD diagnostic thresholds as defined by the standardized clinical criteria. All the research team members were trained by an experienced oral surgeon using this tool.

These studies have been conducted in different countries and institutions. Three of them were reported to the Department of Dental Public Health and Behavioral Science, University of Missouri, Kansas [20, 24, 25]. Four of them were from the Department of Psychiatry, Division of Psychology at the University of Texas Southwestern Medicine Center at Dallas [21–23, 26]. Two were reported as Clinical Psychology and Psychotherapy; Prosthetic Dentistry; and Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery at Philipps University of Marburg in Germany [27, 28].

Patients with chronic TMD were referred by dentists and oral surgeons to participate in these studies. In some cases, patients were paid for their participation, as reported explicitly in the respective methods Sects. [21–23, 26].

No studies were rated with concerns in Domain 3 (D3): Bias due to missing outcome data, indicating that attrition or incomplete data were adequately managed across all trials.

These concerns primarily revolved around the control of confounding variables, as several studies did not fully account for age, sex, baseline pain intensity, psychological distress (anxiety and depression levels), physical activity, and co-interventions, such as analgesics and occlusal splints. Additionally, issues related to outcome measurement and the handling of missing data were observed in some cases, potentially affecting the robustness of the findings.

The overall pattern suggests that the most consistent limitations were associated with deviations from the intended interventions and challenges in outcome measurements or reporting. Despite these concerns, no study was rated at a high risk of bias and most domains were judged to be at low risk, supporting the overall methodological quality of the evidence included.

Inter-rater reliability was perfect (κ = 1), reflecting full agreement between the independent reviewers during the risk of bias assessment.

Meta-analyses

It was confirmed that the degree of autocorrelation did not modify the level of significance; however, as the autocorrelation between the measurement moments increased, the precision of the results decreased with increasing confidence intervals; therefore, the model without autocorrelation was selected (Supplementary material. Table 1).

Model selection: The non-significant LRT (X2 (5) = 3.068, p = 0.381) and lower AICc in the reduced fixed-effects model (54.628) than in the full random-effects model (171.561) indicate that the fixed effects present a better fit.

Transitivity: The non-significant LRT (X2 (5) = 4.905, p = 0.179) and lower AICc in the reduced model without covariates (54.628) than in the full model adjusted for age, time with pain, and male/female ratio (169.723) indicated that the model without covariates had a better fit. It was confirmed that age, time with pain, and male/female ratio did not significantly influence the results; therefore, it was not necessary to adjust for these confounding variables (Supplementary Material. Table 1).

Consistency: The non-significant LRT (X2 (6) < 0.001, p > 0.999) and lower AICc in the consistency-reduced model (109.604) than in the inconsistent full model (139.604) indicated that model consistency was adequate. It is evident that the study by Glaros and Burton (2004) is the one that most influenced this inconsistency (Supplementary Material. Figure 2).

Significant interventions and measurement time: There were no significant differences in pain intensity between the different interventions, either post-treatment or follow-up, compared with baseline values (Table 4; Fig. 4). However, despite the lack of statistically significant differences, the intervention ranking based on P-scores suggested a modest and clinically cautious hierarchy. Occlusal splints ranked highest, followed by no intervention and parafunctional clenching. In contrast, biofeedback had the lowest P-score, indicating the least comparative impact among the analyzed interventions. These exploratory trends underscore the need for more rigorous and isolated evaluation of biofeedback-based strategies.

Discussion

This systematic review and network meta-analysis aimed to assess the effectiveness of biofeedback (BF) as an educational treatment for temporomandibular disorders (TMD) and evaluate its impact on pain reduction, psychological well-being, and physiological adaptation. Our findings suggest that BF is a viable therapeutic option for TMD patients, particularly when used as a structured educational intervention. However, the results highlight several methodological considerations that should be considered when interpreting outcomes.

Summary of main findings

The analysis included nine studies spanning a diverse range of patient populations, intervention protocols, and methodological approaches. The risk of bias assessment indicated that most studies exhibited a low risk of bias, whereas some concerns were noted in a minority of studies, particularly regarding randomization processes, deviations from intended interventions, and selection of reported results.

Our network meta-analysis revealed that although no statistically significant differences were found, the standardized mean differences between biofeedback and no intervention fell within the range typically associated with moderate clinical effects. This may reflect possible benefits in terms of increased somatic awareness and reduced parafunctional activity, although further confirmatory studies are required. However, biofeedback consistently ranked lower than occlusal splints in terms of effectiveness, suggesting that its use may be more appropriate as a complementary rather than primary intervention. Clinically, biofeedback could be particularly useful in cases where splint therapy is contraindicated, or when a behavioral and self-regulation component is needed as part of a multimodal treatment strategy. However, when BF was compared with occlusal splints, the results were less conclusive, suggesting that splints may provide comparable or greater benefits in certain patient subgroups. However, given that several studies have combined BF with additional therapeutic components (e.g., splints or CBT), disentangling the isolated effects of BF is not always feasible. This methodological limitation should be considered when interpreting the comparative effectiveness. Nonetheless, it is important to interpret these findings cautiously. The moderate level of statistical precision and absence of significant pairwise differences suggest that while trends exist, definitive clinical recommendations cannot yet be drawn from the current evidence. Importantly, no significant differences were observed between the intervention groups in post-treatment or follow-up measures, suggesting that BF may offer effects comparable to other therapeutic approaches, although this must be interpreted cautiously.

Beyond pain relief, our review identified that BF interventions contributed to improvements in psychological parameters, such as anxiety and stress reduction, reinforcing the hypothesis that TMD symptoms have a strong biobehavioral component. This aligns with previous evidence suggesting that behavioral therapies can play a crucial role in the management of chronic orofacial pain disorders.

Of the nine studies included in this review, five incorporated measures of psychological variables. Mishra et al. [21] found improvements in anger/hostility, confusion/bewilderment, depression/dejection, fatigue/inertia, tension/anxiety, and vigor/activity in participants who underwent biofeedback therapy and those who received cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) or a combination of both. No differences were found between the groups.

Gatchel et al. [26] showed that a combined early intervention of BFB and CBT, in addition to preventing the onset of chronic TMD in high-risk patients, was effective in reducing the development of somatoform, anxiety, and mood disorders during the follow-up. Furthermore, it led to reductions in depression and improvements in coping skills among participants.

Mora et al. [27, 28] compared combined BFB and CBT interventions with an intervention based on oral splints. They demonstrated that, while both interventions were effective in reducing pain, the BFB-CBT group achieved improvements in psychological variables that were not observed in the oral splint group. Specifically, they showed reductions in depression and somatization as well as improvements in pain coping. It is important to note that the psychological benefits discussed here, although consistent with the broader literature, were not uniformly reported across the studies included in our analysis.

These findings are congruent with the biopsychosocial perspective on the interactive and reciprocal nature of pain and psychosocial functioning. These factors likely influence one another, with improved mood contributing to decreased pain and decreased pain contributing to improved mood.

Bernstein et al. [22] reported that the greatest pain reduction outcomes were observed in individuals previously classified as having a high level of somatization. This suggests that these types of interventions may have different effects, depending on the patient’s profile. This study highlights the importance of patient classification when determining the most appropriate treatment for each case.

Although not included in our meta-analysis, Florjanski et al. [4] conducted a separate systematic review evaluating the use of BF in the management of masticatory muscle activity. They concluded that BF, especially electromyographic biofeedback (EMG-BF), was effective in reducing muscular hyperactivity associated with TMD. They also emphasized the heterogeneity of BF protocols, ranging from visual and auditory feedback to vibratory signals, as well as the variability in electrode placement and session duration. This methodological diversity, also present in our dataset, underscores the need for standardized BF treatment protocols.

Similarly, Crider et al. [29] assessed the efficacy of various biofeedback-based interventions and classified them into three categories: (1) EMG-BF alone, (2) EMG-BF combined with cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and (3) biofeedback-assisted relaxation training (BART). Their findings, while external to our dataset, suggest that EMG-BF with adjunctive CBT meets the criteria for an efficacious treatment for TMD, while EMG-BF alone and BART were deemed “probably efficacious.” This hierarchy supports the use of BF not only as a standalone tool, but also as a valuable adjunct in psychologically informed care models.

Further supporting this, Orlando et al. [30] conducted a literature review on biobehavioral therapies for TMD, including EMG-BF, CBT, and relaxation techniques. They reported that BF was particularly effective in patients with daytime parafunctional habits and could enhance mandibular range of motion in addition to reducing pain. Interestingly, although occlusal splints provide short-term symptom relief, their benefits tend to diminish over time. By contrast, BF appeared to promote longer-lasting improvements, especially when combined with psychological interventions. These conclusions from an independent review conceptually align with the idea that beyond its mechanical effects, BF plays a critical educational and regulatory role in patient care.

Strengths and limitations

One of the primary strengths of this review was its rigorous adherence to PRISMA-NMA guidelines, which ensured methodological transparency and reliability. In addition, by implementing a network meta-analysis approach, we integrated and compared multiple interventions simultaneously, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of BF’s relative effectiveness of BF in TMD management.

Another limitation is the compositional heterogeneity of the intervention formats. While some studies tested BF as a standalone therapy, others combined it with cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) or occlusal splints. This mixing complicates the interpretation of the effect size estimates as improvements may result from co-interventions rather than from BF itself. The inclusion of these combined formats may have introduced bias or diluted the specific contribution of the BF. Subgroup analyses were not feasible because of the small number of studies, but future meta-analyses should explicitly separate these modalities to improve interpretability.

However, this study had some limitations. First, the heterogeneity in study design and outcome measures presents a challenge when drawing definitive conclusions. Although we adjusted for confounding variables, such as age, sex, duration of symptoms, and psychological distress, the included studies varied in terms of intervention protocols, follow-up durations, and outcome assessment methods. This variability may have influenced the observed effects and reduced comparability between studies.

Second, while the risk of publication bias was assessed and found to be low, the relatively small number of included studies limited the robustness of our findings. Additionally, the lack of standardized BF protocols across studies suggests that treatment effects may be influenced by variations in the device technology, feedback modality, and patient adherence. Another limitation is the use of a fixed-effects model, which may underestimate uncertainty in the presence of clinical heterogeneity. Although this model was selected based on statistical fit criteria and a small number of available studies, it may not fully capture the variability across intervention protocols or patient characteristics. To explore this limitation, we performed a sensitivity analysis using a random-effects model, which confirmed that while the direction of estimates remained stable, the confidence intervals were wider and the model fit was poorer. These findings support the use of the fixed-effects model for hypothesis generation but limit the generalizability of the conclusions. Furthermore, the limited number of included trials and lack of statistically significant contrasts across intervention groups restrict the strength of the conclusions. Although ranking analysis offers directional insight, its interpretability must be weighed against the statistical limitations. Additionally, the interpretation of effect sizes as “clinically relevant” was based on post hoc conventions (e.g., Cohen’s criteria for SMDs) and not on the prespecified thresholds defined in the protocol. As such, these interpretations should be viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, rather than conclusive.

An additional limitation concerns the temporal range of the included studies, which spanned the period 1996–2019. Advances in diagnostic criteria (e.g., RDC/TMD to DC/TMD), electromyography technology, and treatment protocols during this period may have influenced the comparability. Although we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding older trials, the small sample size and reduced network connectivity rendered this approach statistically unviable. As more recent trials are published, future meta-analyses may be better positioned to assess the impact of the publication date on treatment effectiveness.

Implications for clinical practice and future research

The findings of this review support the clinical utility of BF as an educational tool for TMD treatment, particularly in settings where patients require active engagement in pain management strategies. Given the observed benefits on both physiological and psychological outcomes, BF could be integrated as part of a multidisciplinary approach, alongside manual therapy, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and pharmacological interventions. However, it is important to note that although several of the included studies implicitly incorporated educational elements, such as patient self-monitoring, voluntary muscle regulation, or therapist feedback, few explicitly measured outcomes related to learning, self-efficacy, or behavioral change. While biofeedback may function as an educational intervention in practice, the evidence base does not uniformly support this characterization in a quantifiable manner. Future trials should consider including validated educational or behavioral outcomes to more accurately assess this dimension of biofeedback.

Importantly, none of the included studies formally assessed educational outcomes such as self-efficacy, knowledge acquisition, or behavioral change. As such, the designation of BF as an “educational” intervention in this review is conceptual and based on its feedback-mediated nature. Future clinical trials should incorporate validated educational or behavioral outcome measures to more accurately assess this component and distinguish it from passive or mechanistic modalities.

Conclusions

This systematic review and network meta-analysis highlight the exploratory potential of BF as a possible educational intervention for patients with TMD, offering benefits beyond pain relief by addressing the psychological and biobehavioral aspects of the disorder. However, due to the inclusion of studies combining BF with other therapeutic modalities, such as CBT or occlusal splints, it remains difficult to isolate the specific effects of BF. Although methodological limitations exist, these findings support the further exploration of BF in clinical practice and research settings.
