Heritability of early seedling growth in Taxus baccata, a declining conifer
Authors: Igor J Chybicki, Jan Suszka, Katarzyna Meyza, Grzegorz Iszkuło
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the face of ongoing climate change, understanding adaptive potential is crucial for conserving the functional biodiversity of forests. In trees, early growth influences competitive ability, rendering seedling attributes suitable candidates for adaptive traits. However, determining whether any trait contributes to the evolutionary potential requires proving its heritability. Such knowledge is important, among others, for understanding the current status of declining tree species, such as the common yew. In this study, we aimed to estimate the heritability of early seedling growth in yew. To achieve this, we collected seeds from two remnant populations and subjected them to germination. We then established a greenhouse experiment to grow seedlings. Using paternity analyses, we determined the relatedness between seedlings. Subsequently, the additive genetic variance in the height of 4-month-old seedlings was estimated using a Bayesian model, taking into account maternal and population effects. Our results indicated that seedling height heritability was 0.17 to 0.46, depending on the level of conservatism applied, while we found no apparent maternal effect, despite the significant variation in germination between mother trees. Introduction

Maintaining forest biodiversity is a strategic component of sustainable forest management1. However, direct and indirect human impacts, including forest management practices (e.g. industry-driven increases in the proportion of forest plantations2) and climate change, are placing many forest biotic components to be at risk of local extinction. While direct human impacts can potentially be managed through forest policy adjustments, climate change, due to its complexity and global reach, requires tree species to exhibit natural adaptive responses. For many trees, the genetic variation is maintained at high levels within a single population due to intensive gene flow3, resulting in individual trees exhibiting high heterozygosity, broad phenotypic plasticity4 and adaptability5,6. However, for a rare tree species characterized by a highly fragmented distribution, the potential to accommodate to a changing environment may be naturally limited due to insufficient adaptive genetic variation of local populations.

Natural tree populations require seed production and seedling recruitment for long-term population persistence. Importantly, sexual reproduction provides the opportunity to rearrange genetic variants, which is crucial for adaptation to changing conditions. The success of seedling recruitment depends on both intrinsic and extrinsic factors7,8. To the extent that seeds are not a limiting factor, recruitment is determined by seedling survival rates9. Seedling survival rates, in turn, are limited in a number of ways, including local resources, herbivory, and intra- and interspecific competition, with the latter being shaped by individual differences in resource acquisition and the resulting differences in early growth rate. Since the strongest reduction in the number of individuals takes place in the early period of the plant’s life cycle10, early seedling growth is a decisive stage that determines whether individuals will survive to maturity11,12. Importantly, early growth traits may contribute to individual fitness13, influencing the overall evolutionary success of a plant14,15. For example, faster-growing seedlings may outcompete others for light and space, increasing their chances of survival. To understand whether early stage traits contribute to the plant fitness (via shaping inter- and intraspecific competitive ability16,17), assessing the heritability of those traits is a prerequisite. Unfortunately, data on genetic variability underlying early growth traits remain scarce for non-timber tree species18.

Accumulated evidence shows that in large, demographically stable tree populations, the level of relatedness among offspring is rather low19–21. This is due to intensive pollen dispersal which maintains weak correlations in paternity (i.e. low frequency of full-siblings) among progeny of a single mother tree. As a result, seedlings derived from seeds produced by a single tree reveal a phenotypic correlations mostly due to sharing a maternal parent22,23. For such tree species, half-sib design-based approaches to assess quantitative trait heritability are often a good approximation of the true mating patterns. On the contrary, due to limited pollen donors, in small and highly isolated populations, correlation in paternity can be high24,25, generating a stratified relatedness structure, with a non-trivial frequency of full-sibs among progeny. Thus, compared to large populations, heritability assessments in small tree populations need to account for possible correlated paternity to avoid underestimating heritability and (potentially) overestimating maternal effects26,27.

Here, we focused on the yew (Taxus baccata L.), a European dioecious conifer characterized by wide but highly fragmented natural distribution28. According to the IUCN Red List, the species is listed as Least Concern (LC)29. However, the woodlands with Taxus baccata have received priority habitat status under Annex I of the EU Habitats Directive30. Yew is known to experience severe problems with natural regeneration across a natural range, which is one of the main drivers of the observed species decline31–33. Natural yew populations are usually small, spatially isolated and show high genetic differentiation34–36. Furthermore, the level of genetic variation varies considerably among populations reflecting individual demographic histories36,37. Notably, pollen and seed dispersal potential in yew is mostly insufficient to overcome the barrier of spatial isolation between populations38. As a result, the contemporary levels of gene flow in the species tend to be low, highlighting the importance of retained within-population adaptive potential39. Despite significant reductions in local effective size, studies from the western part of species’ distribution suggest that remnant yew populations preserve adaptive genetic variation40. Nonetheless, it remains to be seen whether quantitative traits related to adaptation, such as early growth13, show heritability in natural remnants functioning under obvious demographic and genetic constraints imposed by intense genetic drift and limited gene flow.

The aim of this study was to test whether the variation in early seedling performance in yew has a genetic component. To this end, we collected open-pollinated seeds within two populations of the species. Using a greenhouse experiment, we characterized the variation in aboveground seedling height at 4 months of age. In Taxus baccata, seedling development is particularly slow, with full morphological establishment (emergence of radicle, hypocotyl, and cotyledons) typically completed by 6–8 weeks after germination41,42, and only minimal shoot elongation occurring thereafter in the first year43. The early 4-month developmental stage analyzed in our study therefore represents a biologically meaningful and measurable window in which differences in early vigor are likely to reflect intrinsic factors, including genetic and maternal effects, while limiting the influence of additional environmental variation introduced in later growth stages. With genetic markers, we then reconstructed seedling paternity using an approach that explicitly accounts for differences in male fecundity, the distance effect (i.e. pollen dispersal), and genotyping errors/mutations. Finally, the additive genetic contributions from both maternal and paternal parents (i.e. the additive genetic variance) to phenotypic variation were estimated using paternity-based reconstructed relatedness between progeny, taking maternal and population effects into account as the two possible covariates of early growth variation.

Materials and methods

Study sites and fieldwork

The study was conducted in two natural yew populations in Poland (Łagów 15.3°E, 52.4°N and Tychowo 16.3°E, 53.9°N) (Fig. 1). Łagów (a nature monument, the Cisy Łagowskie) occurs in a fresh mixed forest habitat. The dominant species in the upper tree layer are Scots pine and spruce. The accompanying species include beech, oak, and yew. In the case of Tychowo (the Cisy Tychowskie reserve), the habitat is more fertile, taking the form of a moist forest. The species composition is also richer. The reserve area is covered by a beech-dominated stand with an admixture of hornbeam, oak, alder, birch, spruce, larch, and yew. Noteworthy, in both sites, yew is present in a sub-canopy or shrub layer only. The two populations can be considered representatives of bottlenecked yew remnants of historically larger populations, that occur widely across the current species’ range. According to our earlier study, Łagów experienced size reduction relatively recently compared to Tychowo, and the genetic bottleneck was also more severe there36. However, the two adult populations are currently nearly identical in terms of neutral genetic diversity levels.

Locations of the study populations and distributions of sampled trees. In the case of Tychowo, the orange area shows the borders of nature reserve. Mother trees are shown in red, sampled male trees (candidate fathers) are shown in green, and other female trees are shown in grey.

In September 2015, 15 randomly selected female trees within each site were sampled for open-pollinated seeds. We aimed to collect approximately 100 seeds per tree, but due to the variation in fecundity, the resulting number of seeds varied between trees, especially in Łagów (see Fig. 2). In addition, the proximity of females was inspected for the presence of potential pollen donors (males) (Fig. 1). As a result, we sampled 21 and 27 male trees in Łagów and Tychowo, respectively. The resulting sampling areas covered 0.5 ha in Łagów and 7.1 ha in Tychowo, reflecting the sharp difference in population density between the two sites. Taking into account the known censuses of adult trees36, the sampling intensity reached about 30% and 80% of the total number of males in Łagów and Tychowo, respectively. After formal identification of plants (undertaken by Grzegorz Iszkuło), geographical coordinates and stem circumference (measured at breast height, 1.3 m) were recorded for all sampled trees. In addition, short twigs were collected for DNA extraction. However, because the collected plant material was subjected to destructive processing, we did not deposit a voucher specimen. In the case of both study sites, the relevant state administration entities granted permission to conduct research in protected areas.

Seed germination and seedling growth

Collected seeds were subjected to the 10-month germination protocol44. The procedure involved cyclic temperature changes to stimulate embryo growth, break a deep dormancy, and stimulate germination. Gradually germinating seeds were collected in containers and stored temporarily (2–3 weeks) at 0 °C to inhibit their growth. To develop seedlings, successfully germinated seeds (up to 40 per tree) were transplanted into plastic propagation trays with 125 ml pots containing a standard soil substrate used in container nurseries (peat pH 5.5–6.5 and perlite in a 2:1 ratio plus Osmocote Exact Standard 3 g l− 1). Containers were maintained at 20 °C for four months. Four-month-old seedlings were measured for height (from soil to top of apical bud, in centimeters; hereafter referred to as aboveground height) and destructively sampled for DNA extraction. If the number of emerged seedlings was greater than 20 per family, we took a random sample of 20. Otherwise, we took all seedlings per family. As a result, height was measured for a subsample of 470 developed seedlings out of 699 in total (see also the “Results” section). The remaining 299 seedlings were transplanted to the field and were not subjected to further treatment or monitoring. Most of them did not survive in the following seasons. In addition, to increase the statistical power of the mating model used to resolve paternities (see the next section), when the seedling count per maternal family was less than 20, additional progeny samples were collected by extracting from the soil seeds with radicles (which did not develop into seedlings). These samples (called supplemental progeny) were used in paternity analysis, but omitted in the estimation of heritability (see below).

Paternity analysis

For paternity analysis, all trees and a sample of seedlings were genotyped using 18 nuclear microsatellite markers45 and one (paternally transmitted) mitochondrial microsatellite marker (mtSSR)46 (see the Supplementary Materials; Tab. S1). Paternity was reconstructed using the hierarchical mating model implemented in the NMπ 2.0 software47. The method was designed to estimate mating model parameters, including pollen immigration, non-random pollen dispersal, non-uniform male fecundity distribution, and possible parent-offspring genotypic mismatches. NMπ 2.0 offers important advantages, including robustness to genotyping errors and the proportion of true parents in a sample of candidate parents47. Locus-specific genotyping error rates are treated as unknown parameters that are estimated within the framework of the full probability mating model. Consequently, they do not need to be known or assumed a priori. The proportion of true fathers in a sample is controlled by the pollen immigration parameter, which is also estimated without relying on prior knowledge of this quantity, allowing for obtaining unbiased estimation of pollen immigration and reducing false positive parentage assignments.

In a usual application, dispersal and fecundity-related parameters are goal parameters. In our study, on the other hand, all these parameters were treated as so-called nuisance variables, which are required to infer unbiased seedling genealogies, but are not directly related to the study goals. Due to dioecy, we set the self-fertilization parameter to zero. Similarly, for mtDNA, we set the probability of maternal transmission to zero. For pollen dispersal, we assumed the exponential-power (EP) dispersal kernel, but tested both a simple exponential function (a thin-tailed degenerate EP form) and a full EP version (with an unknown shape). The best-performing dispersal kernel was determined using the Widely Applicable Information Criterion (WAIC)48. In addition, to reflect possible differences in pollen productivity, we assumed that male fecundity can potentially covary with a stem circumference, but allowed the program to validate our assumption via the implemented model selection procedure. Parameter estimates, including paternity assignments, were obtained based on 120,000 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations, rejecting the initial 20,000 iterations, and keeping every 20th iteration in a resulting MCMC chain.

Paternity assignments were post-processed to extract a subset of robust genealogies characterized by the posterior probability > 0.8, which were used to assess the heritability of early seedling growth (see the next section).

Heritability estimation

Heritability was estimated using a mixed effects model framework, often referred to as an animal (or individual) model49,50. In our study, we considered several sources of variation in seedling height including additive genetic effect, the maternal effect, and the effect of population. We included the maternal effect (i.e. the effect of maternal environment) separately from the additive genetic effect to control for potential environmental influences associated with the mother tree (e.g. via seed provisioning) that may cause consistent within-family similarity in height among seedlings. In a similar way, we added the population effect to control for the impact of population, i.e. an unknown factor common for all trees within a single population. Consequently, our focus was on estimating narrow-sense heritability from additive genetic effects, which are most relevant for predicting the response to selection in the study context.

Random effects and variance components were estimated using a Bayesian approach implemented in the MCMCglmm (version 2.35) R library51. Since the variance components cannot be tested directly for their deviance from zero (the prior distribution is strictly positive), the analysis was repeated for all nested models (after removing one or more random effects) to select the most plausible model for seedling height. The best model was selected based on the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC; the lower the value, the better the fit)52.

Results

Seed counts and germination

The number of seeds collected from individual trees varied from 31 to 117, with a mean of 92.8 (SE = 18.7) (Fig. 2). Fewer seeds were collected (1327 vs. 1456) and maternal family seed counts showed greater variability (CV = 28.3 vs. 7.7%) in Łagów than in Tychowo, respectively. The number of germinated seeds varied between 7 and 81, with a mean number of germinated seeds of 43.0 (SE = 22.1) per tree. Again, the total number of germinated seeds was higher in Tychowo (786) than in Łagów (504). The mean germination proportion was 0.464 (SE = 0.009), but the populations differed significantly (the likelihood ratio test; p-value < 0.001) in germination proportion (0.380 ± 0.013 vs. 0.540 ± 0.013 for Łagów and Tychowo, respectively). In addition, at the population level, germination proportions showed significant variation among families, as revealed by the likelihood ratio test (p-value < 0.001).

Seedling height

Out of 1290 germinated seeds, 959 seeds were used in the growth experiment (7–40 per mother tree). After 4 months, 699 seeds developed into seedlings (0–40 per mother tree), out of which a random-per-family subset of 470 (1–22 per family, excluding one family with no developed seedlings) were measured for height (Fig. 3A).

Seedling height ranged from 10 to 50 mm (Fig. 3B-C), with the mean 31.46 mm (SD = 6.15, CV = 19.5%). Seedlings in Tychowo were slightly taller than those in Łagów (32.31 vs. 30.90 mm) (Fig. 3D), and the difference was significant (t = -2.443, p-value = 0.015). However, after controlling for a maternal effect (the mother as a random effect), the difference in height between the populations was no longer significant, as shown by the linear mixed model analysis (t = -0.727, p-value = 0.474). The mean height per family showed a significant positive correlation with the logit-transformed germination proportion (Pearson r = 0.378, p-value = 0.043). However, the variation in germination proportion among families explained only 14.3% of the total variation in height (Fig. 3E).

Paternity reconstruction

A total of 470 seedlings with height records were genotyped. In addition, in the case of 14 families, a total of 36 (from 1 to 5 per family) supplemental progeny samples were genotyped. Thus, the total number of genotyped progeny was 506 (5–22 per family): 216 and 290 for Łagów and Tychowo, respectively.

In general, the estimates of the neighborhood model parameters were very similar in both populations (Table 1). Only 11.2% and 9.7% of seedlings in Łagów and Tychowo, respectively, were sired by unsampled pollen donors (pollen immigration). In both populations, the analysis revealed quite a lot of variation in female fecundity, which did not covary with stem circumference. Mean pollen dispersal distance was 38.4 and 49.7 m, in Łagów and Tychowo, respectively, and these estimates did not differ from each other, as can be deduced from overlapping HPD intervals. On the other hand, comparisons of the dispersal kernels showed that the simple exponential kernel had better support than the exponential-power kernel in Łagów (WAIC = 9,119.7 vs. 9,124.1, respectively) while the exponential-power kernel prevailed over the exponential kernel in Tychowo (WAIC = 10,189.3 vs. 10,206.7, respectively). Thus, the pollen dispersal kernel in Tychowo showed greater potential for long-distance pollen dispersal compared to Łagów. Estimates of mistyping errors obtained simultaneously with the other parameters were generally low to moderate (Fig. S1 in the Supplementary Materials), with a mean of 0.024 (SE = 0.005) and 0.011 (SE = 0.004) in Łagów and Tychowo, respectively.

The number of progeny with robust genealogies (Pr > 0.8) was 197 (out of 216) and 288 (out of 290) in Łagów and Tychowo, respectively. Among these individuals, 18 and 27 had a father unambiguously out of the sample. In the case of seedlings with height records, 173 and 280 progeny were obtained in Łagów and Tychowo, respectively. Since one of the families was represented by a single seedling with known genealogy, after pruning, 452 seedlings with robust genealogies were used for heritability estimation. Among progeny, 353 seedlings were grouped into 69 full-sib families (2–19 seedlings per family). In addition, we detected 49 unique parentages where both parents were known and the father was assigned to one or more full-sib pairs.

Seedling height heritability

Among the tested models, the model including only the additive genetic effect revealed the lowest DIC value (Table 2; Fig. S2 in the Supplementary Material). The second-best model, which included the additive genetic effect and the population effect, was only slightly worse, with a difference in DIC of 1.54. The other models, including the full model, were not competitive, with an increase in DIC of 78.6 or more compared to the best model. These models included, among others, the maternal effect, which showed a strong effect only in the absence of the additive genetic effect. Comparing the estimates between the best and the second-best models revealed that the additive genetic effect () showed stable and informative posterior estimates for both models. On the other hand, the population effect () present in the second-best model was characterized by a high level of uncertainty, with the credible interval for including virtually zero (up to the output precision; Table 2). Taking into account the low precision of estimated under the second-best model, one may favor a more parsimonious model (i.e. involving only) as the best-performing model. However, ignoring a possibly important variance component could result in an overestimation of the importance of additive genetic variance in the total variance, yielding an anti-conservative estimate of heritability. Consequently, we considered both the best and the second-best model as two plausible models for assessing the heritability. Under the best model, the posterior median heritability was 0.536, with a credible interval between 0.292 and 0.803. For the second-best model, the posterior median heritability dropped to 0.300, but also the inclusion of population effect reduced precision of the estimate. Using the model averaging, with a weight equal to , the average estimate of heritability was 0.462. We considered this value a statistically valid estimate, though likely somewhat optimistic. Additionally, we regarded the full model-based heritability estimate of 0.173 as a conservative lower bound.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrated that the germination success of seeds produced spontaneously within natural yew remnants can differ significantly, with these differences being explained by maternal and population effects. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the height of derived seedlings can vary significantly at the individual level after four months of growth under benign conditions. Finally, we showed that variation in early height is influenced by the breeding values of both parents, indicating a significant level of narrow-sense heritability for this trait in yew.

The magnitude of genetic vs. residual components in the total variance of a heritable trait can be shaped by environmental conditions53, leading to different estimates of heritability in different environments54,55. The heritability found in this study was estimated under benign conditions, as seedlings grown in a greenhouse experiment received satisfactory nutrition, and regular watering, and experienced no competition. Under such conditions, the environmental variance was presumably very low, and the observed growth was mostly the result of intrinsic factors. It is not clear, however, whether benign conditions led to an over- or underestimation of heritability56–58, compared to more stressful natural environments, as a potential bias depends on which conditions produce more uniform response: uniformly good growth in benign conditions vs. uniformly poor growth in stressful environments18. Nevertheless, our results provide an evidence that up to 46% (and not less than 17%, under a conservative approach) of the total variance in early growth is shaped by an additive genetic component in the study species. It is worth noting that the study populations have been shown to represent below-average genetic diversity in a region (Poland), probably due to a strong genetic bottleneck occurring either in a long-term (Tychowo) or more recently (Łagów)35,36. Nevertheless, our study showed that, despite relatively low genetic variation at the DNA level, small bottlenecked yew remnants can still maintain a non-trivial standing genetic variation in early seedling growth.

In plants, the variation in performance at early developmental stage often reflects a maternal effect23,59,60. Maternal effects can be expressed as a result of direct genetic effect, the effect of the maternal phenotype or the maternal environment61. Maternal effects on seed traits, especially on seed mass, are particularly well documented in gymnosperms62,63, where a storage tissue (a haploid megagametophyte) is exclusively of maternal origin. The maternal environment also has a broad potential influence on seed traits64, determining seed size, seed coat structure, nutrient and hormone content, among others65–67. In addition, although less frequently documented, maternal effects can also be observed for early seedling traits68,69. Our study revealed a significant maternal effect at the seed stage, shaping the apparent differences in germination success among maternal families. The variation in germination proportions among mother trees under experimental laboratory conditions has long been known in yew44,70,71. However, it remains unclear whether the observed maternal effect on seed germination is determined by genetic or environmental factors. Only because plastid and mitochondrial DNA are paternally inherited in yew46,72, the maternal effect due to a direct cytoplasmic genetic effect can be ruled out (while asking whether a paternal cytoplasmic effect exists instead). It should be noted that differences in germination success between mother trees tend to disappear as the stratification treatment progresses, and may even vanish completely71. Therefore, maternal effects observed in this study must be considered as specific to the germination protocol, and should not be extrapolated to field conditions.

Unlike seed germination, aboveground seedling height did not seem to depend much (if any) on the maternal individual (or maternal environment) as a factor independent from additive genetic effect. Maternal effects are known to decay rapidly as offspring become dependent on their photosynthetic apparatus23,59. On the other hand, we cannot exclude that the progeny sample size used in this study provided insufficient statistical power to separate the (possibly relatively weak) maternal effect from the main effect of additive genetic variance.

Despite being a wind-pollinated species, yew has been demonstrated to show a limited potential to disperse pollen, making it difficult for pollen to overcome the spatial isolation of scattered natural populations38,39. In this study, about 10% of genotyped seedlings were produced with pollen coming from unsampled males, suggesting that pollen was occasionally able to disperse further than the dimensions of our study plots. However, based on the earlier findings38,39, we treated pollen immigration estimates with reservations, suggesting that they may reflect the presence of unsampled pollen donors in a local neighborhood rather than be signs of long-distance pollen dispersal. It is worth recalling, that estimates of heritability and breeding values were obtained under the assumption that seedlings classified as pollen immigrants did not share pollen parents. Thus, the genetic relatedness among these seedlings could be underestimated if the number of unsampled males is very low73,74. Fortunately, given the small number of such seedlings, we do not expect the estimates of quantitative genetic parameters to be significantly affected.

To fully characterize the heritability of a trait in natural populations, the proportion of additive genetic variance in the total variance should be characterized in different environments18,54. In natural conditions, the residual variance in seedling height may depend on environmental factors and seedling age. Environmental factors can be accounted for directly by including field measurements as fixed effects or indirectly through an appropriate statistical model in which the individual is treated as a random factor with spatially structured covariance75. On the contrary, seedling age is more difficult to account in field conditions since the ease of dormancy breaking is highly variable among individual seeds in yew, affecting the timing of germination and seedling development15. Therefore, studying the heritability of early growth in the wild poses methodological challenges and limitations. A good alternative would be a greenhouse experiment with alternative physicochemical (e.g. benign vs. harsh)18 or nutrient regimes76. The latter may be an important next step, e.g. to understand the evolutionary potential of yew populations to respond to anthropogenic eutrophication of forests77.

Advances in genome sequencing have made it possible to complement or even replace a pedigree-based relationship matrix (the G-matrix) with a multi-marker genomic estimate. Such an approach offers important improvements, including the possibility to investigate and account for individual inbreeding78,79 and so-called hidden relatedness between individuals80,81. The structure of relatedness in natural tree populations can be complex, and yew is no exception. For example, our recent study showed that relatedness can covary with tree age82, possibly reflecting the colonization history of the local forest patch. Importantly, individuals representing the same relatedness class (e.g., full-sibs) can exhibit different levels of the realized relatedness due to Mendelian sampling83. Therefore, in natural tree populations, the realized relationship between individuals can form a continuum of genetic correlation values, including full and half siblings, as well as cousins of different degrees or intergenerational relationships. Because realized relatedness leads to more accurate heritability estimates than the pedigree-based approach84, future studies, especially field assessments of heritability, should make use of a genomic approach to the G-matrix.

Conclusions

We have demonstrated that additive genetic variance is present in early seedling growth in yew. In other words, we indirectly demonstrated that adult trees with the potential for faster early growth tend to pass this trait on to their offspring. However, it is unclear whether the heritability of early growth has any adaptive consequences. Future studies should therefore focus on assessing the relationship between seedling growth and individual fitness to evaluate the potential of early growth variance as an indicator of the evolutionary potential of natural yew remnants. Such knowledge would be valuable for predicting the future shape of yew remnants and could be potentially helpful in adjusting the existing conservation programs.
