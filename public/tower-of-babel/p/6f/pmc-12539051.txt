Holistic approach to Turkish children with autism spectrum disorder: diet quality and diversity, gastrointestinal and nutritional problems and quality of life perspective
Authors: Yaren Sağlam Şahinoğlu, Hande Bakırhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBecause of behaviors specific to autism spectrum disorder (ASD), children with ASD may have a poor quality of life, and maintain their nutritional and gastrointestinal (GI) health can be quite challenging. This study aimed to holistically examine the diet quality and diversity, quality of life, and nutritional and GI problems in children with ASD and compare their outcomes with healthy peers.MethodsNutritional and GI problems were evaluated through a questionnaire according to the parents’ responses. Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015) was used to evaluate diet quality, diet diversity score (DDS) was used to examine the food groups consumed, and Pediatric Quality of Life (PedsQL) was used to evaluate the quality of life.ResultsA total of 56.0% of children with ASD had a normal body weight for their age, 66.0% had a normal height for their age, and 70.0% had a normal body mass index (BMI) for their age. Various parameters were evaluated for ASD and healthy children, respectively and children with ASD had lower BMI Z scores (ASD = 0.35 ± 0.90, Healthy = 1.03 ± 1.44), lower quality of life (5–7 years ASD = 49.0 ± 5.94, Healthy = 89.0 ± 13.24; 8–12 years ASD = 47.1 ± 7.59, Healthy = 86.9 ± 14.07) and lower diet quality (Healthy = 73.8 ± 3.92, ASD = 71.5 ± 4.11) (p < 0.05). No significant difference was found between the two groups in terms of diet diversity (p > 0.05). Gastrointestinal system problems, food refusal, food neophobia, food obsession and fast eating were seen more frequently in children with ASD compared to healthy children (p < 0.05).ConclusionIn conclusion, children with ASD were found to have more nutritional and GI problems, a lower quality of life, and poorer diet quality compared to their peers. Growth curves, diet quality and diversity, quality of life, nutrition and GI problems of children with ASD should be carefully monitored and individualized nutrition strategies should be developed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06207-1.

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD), defined as a developmental disorder characterized by the presence of repetitive and restrictive behaviors along with deficiencies in communication and social skills, has a worldwide prevalence of 1.8%, and its incidence is increasing [1]. The etiology of ASD is not clear, but genetic, metabolic, immunological and environmental factors are thought to be effective. Genetic predispositions, maternal diabetes and prenatal and perinatal exposures may increase the risk of ASD. Various negative behaviors that may vary from person to person, such as avoiding eye contact, a lack of response to communication, excessive interest in various objects, rocking, struggling, repetitive movements, obsessive behaviors, hyperactivity, tantrums, eating and sleeping problems, can be observed in individuals with ASD [2].

Prenatal maternal nutrition and postnatal nutrition from the neonatal period to childhood and adulthood are important in the prognosis of ASD. Furthermore, managing the nutritional status of children with ASD can be quite challenging [3]. Frequently observed feeding problems include food selectivity, eating behavior problems, refusal to try new foods, refusal of foods with different consistencies and appearances, and persistent consumption of a single type of food. In particular, food selectivity is the most frequently observed finding among nutritional problems. Being selective based on food type (consuming a limited number of foods), texture (consuming only soft, hard, crushed puree, raw), taste (frequently consuming sugary, salty or sour foods), temperature (hot or cold), color or smell (food selectivity) is the most reported behavioral pattern among the feeding problems experienced by children with ASD [3]. Nutritional problems may lead to low diet quality and inadequate nutritional intake, and may lead to various vitamin, mineral, protein and fatty acid deficiencies in the future life (Berding & Donovan, 2016). It has been shown that vitamins A, D, E, K, folic acid, calcium, iodine, biotin and omega 3 fatty acids are not taken in sufficient amounts in children with ASD due to excessive selectivity and unbalanced nutrition [3]. Gastrointestinal system (GIS) problems may frequently be observed in individuals with ASD for reasons such as disruption of the intestinal flora and increased intestinal permeability, restrictive diets, limited or no consumption of some foods, and deficiency of some enzymes (disaccharidases, etc.) [4]. The increase in the incidence of food allergies, the abnormal microbiota structure of children with ASD, and the importance of the intestinal-blood‒brain barrier and nutrition relationship have led to an increase in research on the relationship between ASD and GIS [4]. The quality of life of children with ASD may be lower than that of both healthy children and children with chronic diseases because of the symptoms they experience [5]. It has been suggested that a nutritional approach with good diet quality and nutritional diversity has the potential to positively impact quality of life by alleviating various ASD symptoms (hyperactivity, repetitive behaviors, communication disorders, etc.) [6]. Additionally, few studies have investigated the GIS symptoms, including diet diversity and quality of life, of children with autism. We hypothesized that children with ASD experience more severe nutritional and GIS problems, including quality of life, than their healthy peers. This study was conducted to examine the nutritional quality and diversity, gastrointestinal and nutritional problems, and quality of life of children with ASD using a holistic approach. The main aim of the study is to compare these children with their healthy peers and to shed light on nutritional management in children with ASD.

Materials and methods

Study plan and sample selection

This study was conducted with 50 children with ASD, aged between 3 and 11 years, living in Trabzon, diagnosed with ASD by a medical doctor in accordance with the DSM-V criteria, studying in a private institution or a state institution, and with 50 healthy volunteer children. The data for the study were collected between June 2021 and June 2022. Power analysis was performed via the G*Power program (version 3.1.9.4) to calculate the number of samples included in the study, and the number of samples for 95% power was calculated as 100 (n = 50 for children with ASD, n = 50 for children in the control group). The parents of the participants were informed about the study and asked about their participation in the study. Individuals who agreed to participate in the study by reviewing the volunteer consent form were included in this research and answered the questions asked about themselves and their children. Those with chronic diseases such as hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, neurological, hepatic and renal diseases; those following a special diet program; and those under the age of 3 and over the age of 11 years were not included in the study. Ethical permission for the study was obtained by the decision of the Istanbul Medipol University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee dated 01.06.2021 and numbered E-10840098-772.02-2513. Parents of all participants provided informed consent for this study that adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaire

Data were collected using a questionnaire form developed by the researchers and not used anywhere else, which asked for information about the child (See Supplementary Material-1). In the questionnaire, the child’s gastrointestinal (GI) problems (constipation, diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, reflux, difficulty swallowing, nausea, etc.), nutritional problems (loss of appetite, choking during meals, food rejection, fast eating, overeating, food neophobia, etc.), number of defecations and stool consistency were assessed (See Supplementary Material-1). 24-hour Dietary Intake Record (24 h) and Pediatric Quality of Life (PedsQL) are used to evaluate the diet quality, quality of life and DDS of children. The survey questions were answered by the families and recorded by the researcher.

Evaluation of anthropometric measurements

Measurement values of children with ASD who did not comply with the researchers’ instructions/guidance for body weight and height measurements (crying, exhibiting non-compliance, etc.) were determined based on their parents’ self-reports. In light of this information, BMI, weight-for-age Z score (WAZ), and height-for-age Z score (HAZ) were calculated, and BMI-for age Z score (BMIZ) classification was performed.

Body weight and height

Participants’ body weight (kg) and height (cm) were determined based on their parents’ self-report. Anthropometric values were evaluated specific to age and sex. In the evaluation of the HAZ score, the WAZ score was evaluated as follows: <-2SD as stunted/too thin, ≥-2SD<-1SD as short/thin, ≥-1SD < + 1SD as normal/normal, ≥+1SD < + 2SD as tall/overweight, and ≥ + 2SD as too tall/obese [7, 8].

Body mass index (kg/m2)

Body mass index (BMI) values were obtained by dividing the participants’ body weight (kg) by the square of their height (m2). The obtained BMI values were evaluated via the World Health Organization (WHO) AnthroPlus program and WHO-MGRS growth reference values-2007 for children aged 5–19 years [7, 8].

Food consumption record

The participants’ food consumption status was determined by recording the food and beverages they consumed for a total of 3 days (2 nonconsecutive weekdays and 1 weekend day) on a food consumption record form by their parents. The researcher explained to the participants how the parents would fill out the food consumption record verbally and use visual materials (replicas, food photo catalog). Daily energy and nutrient intakes were calculated via the BEBİS program (Ebispro for Windows, Stuttgart, Germany; Turkish Version (BeBiS 9), Pasifik Elektirik Elektronik Ltd. Şti. (www.bebis.com.tr); Istanbul, 2021) from the data in the food consumption record form.

Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015)

The diet quality of the children was assessed via the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015), which was prepared based on the American Dietary Guidelines. There are thirteen components in HEI-2015, and the first component indicates the adequacy of the diet, whereas the last 3 components indicate those desired to be consumed in limited amounts. For those that should be consumed in limited amounts, the score increases as the amount consumed decreases. The highest score for some components in the HEI-2015 is 5, and the highest score for some components is 10. The maximum total score that can be obtained is 100. The quality of a diet with a score above 80 is classified as “good”, a score between 51 and 80 is classified as “needs improvement”, and a score of 51 or lower is classified as “poor” [9].

Dietary diversity score (DDS)

The dietary diversity score (DDS) is a dietary diversity assessment index obtained by calculating the number of food groups consumed according to a 24-hour food consumption record. The DDS index defined by Kennedy et al. was used according to the recommendation of the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). The DDS includes 9 food groups: grains, dark green leafy vegetables, root and tuber vegetables, other vegetables, fruits (including 100% fruit juices), legumes and oilseeds, meat (red meat, poultry), fish and seafood, milk and dairy products, and eggs [10]. There are base values ​​for the food groups to be considered consumed. When the food diversity score is calculated, if the participant consumes enough food according to the 24-hour food consumption record, they receive 1 point, and if they do not consume 0 points. The total score is evaluated based on the total number of food groups consumed and is considered low when ≤ 4, medium when 5–6, and high when ≥ 7 [10].

The Turkey Dietary Guidelines-2015 (TUBER) was used as the second assessment method, and there are 5 basic food groups and 23 subnutrient components [11]. Fats and sugars were excluded from the dietary diversity score. For food to be considered consumed, at least half of the amount stated in the 2-day food consumption records, including weekdays and weekends, must be consumed. Each of the five main groups is evaluated as 2 points and calculated with the DDS formula ((main food group score/number of subfood groups) * number of subnutrient groups consumed) [11]. The total score is calculated by adding the scores of all subgroups, and the DDS classification is made as low variety (< 3.5), medium variety (3.5 ≤ DDS ≤ 6.5) and variety (> 6.5) [12].

Pediatric quality of life (PedsQL)

Pediatric quality of life (PedsQL) was developed by Varni and colleagues to assess the health-related quality of life of children and adolescents aged 2–18 years [13]. The scale has four separate forms, designed according to age group characteristics, for the 2–4, 5–7, 8–12 and 13–18 years age groups. The validity and reliability of the Turkish form for the 5–7 age group was determined by Uneri et al., and the forms for the 8–12 and 13–18 years age groups were determined by Cakin Memik et al. [14–16]. The scale consists of four subsections in which physical, emotional, social and school-related functionality are questioned. While a five-choice Likert-type response scale is used throughout the scale (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, 4 = always), a three-choice Likert-type response scale is used to facilitate understanding in the 5–7 years-old child form. (0 = never, 2 = sometimes, 4 = always). The scores obtained from the items are converted linearly into values between 0 and 100 points (0 = 100, 1 = 75, 2 = 50, 3 = 25, 4 = 0). The scores of the eight items in the physical functioning section are linearly converted, summed, and divided by the number of items, which is eight, to obtain the total physical health score. The psychosocial health total score is calculated by adding the scores of five items in the emotional functioning section, five items in the social functionality section, and five items in the school-related problems section after linear conversion and dividing by the total number of items, 15. The scale total score is obtained by linearly converting all scale item scores, adding them, and dividing by 23, which is the total number of items [17].

Statistical assessment of data

The analysis of the research data was performed with the SPSS (23.0) program. The number, percentage distribution, mean, standard deviation and maximum and minimum values were determined via chi-square analysis and descriptive statistics tests. The normality of the data was determined via the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. To compare whether there is a difference between the averages obtained in terms of a variable measured in independent group(s) (two or more) in the research, an independent groups t test for two groups and two-way ANOVA for the analysis of those providing parametric test assumptions were used. analysis of variance was used. Pearson correlation analysis was used to determine the direction and level of relationships between the dependent variables and independent variables that were normally distributed. The results were evaluated at the 95% confidence interval, and the significance level was p < 0.05.

The findings regarding the diet quality and diet diversity of the participants are presented in Table 6. No significant difference was found between the two groups in terms of diet diversity score or classification (p > 0.05). The majority of the participants with ASD and the control group (60.0% vs. 66.0%, respectively) had high diet diversity according to the FAO and had a diet quality that needed improvement (96.0% vs. 94.0%, respectively). In addition, the diet quality score of the ASD group (71.50 ± 4.11) was significantly lower than that of the control group (73.86 ± 3.92) (p = 0.004).

Correlation analysis of HEI-2015 and DDS total scores with PedsQL scores and anthropometric measurements was performed, and a positive and low-level significant relationship was determined between the participants’ HEI-2015 total score and PedsQL score (r = 0.295; p = 0.003). Discussion

The primary aim of this cross-sectional study was to investigate the diet quality and diversity, quality of life, nutrition status and GIS problems of children with ASD. In accordance with the hypothesis, we found that children with ASD had lower BMI Z scores, lower quality of life and lower diet quality than healthy peers. We also noticed that GIS problems, food refusal, food neophobia, food obsession and fast eating were seen more frequently in children with ASD compared to healthy peers.

Various factors, such as frequent food selectivity, excessive consumption of foods with high energy content, low consumption of vegetables and fruits, low physical activity due to inadequate motor skills, use of neuroleptic drugs, GIS problems (reflux, constipation, etc.), and irregular sleep patterns, pave the way for excessive weight gain and obesity in children with ASD [18]. The prevalence of overweight in children and adolescents has increased threefold in the last 20 years; 19.0% of children with ASD are overweight (BMI Z score > 85 percentile), and this rate is similar to that of the general child population [19]. Mills et al. examined the height, body weight, BMI and growth hormone (IGF-1, IGF-2, IGFBP-3 and GHBP) levels of children with ASD (n = 71) and healthy children (n = 59). The body weights, BMI values ​​and growth hormone levels of children with ASD were found to be greater than those of healthy children (p < 0.05) [20]. In contrast, Barnhill et al. (ASD = 86, control = 57) reported that children with ASD had significantly lower BMIs and BMI Z scores [21]. In our study, when BMI, height and body weight z scores were evaluated, the WAZ score and HAZ score were similar between the two groups (p > 0.05), whereas the mean BMI Z score was greater in the healthy control group (0.35 ± 0.90 and 1.03 ± 1.44, respectively, p < 0.05). A limitation of the study is that height and body weight were obtained from parental self-reports, which may have led to a margin of error. Additionally, in our sample, children with ASD and their typically normal developing peers were not fully matched for sex and age because males were significantly overrepresented in the ASD group than in the typically developing group, which is consistent with the male-to-female ASD ratio reported in the literature [22]. Therefore, the difference in average age may have affected body weight and height. In our study, when BMI was evaluated, 10.0% of the children with ASD were underweight, 56.0% were normal, 28.0% were slightly overweight, and the distribution was similar to that of the healthy control group. In a study examining the anthropometric characteristics of children with ASD (n = 50) in a private rehabilitation center, 4.0% of the children were underweight, 36.0% were normal, 18.0% were slightly overweight, and 42.0% were obese [23]. In another recent large-sample study (n = 5910), the prevalence of overweight, obesity and underweight was found to be 14.2%, 11.6% and 10%, respectively, and it was determined that underweight, overweight and obesity rates were high, especially in children with ASD over the age of 6 [24]. In a study examining the nutritional status of children with ASD (n = 276) compared with healthy controls (n = 544), the mean age of the sample was 7.9 ± 2.6 years, and 84.4% were male. The prevalence of overweight (42.4% vs. 26.1%) and obesity (21.4% vs. 12.0%) was found to be significantly greater in children with ASD than in the control group (p < 0,001) [25]. Another study reported significantly higher BMIs and rates of overweight and obesity in school-age children with ASD than in healthy children (63.4% vs. 46.3%) [26]. In contrast, it has been reported that there is no difference between autistic children and normal children in terms of body weight, height or head circumference according to age [27]. A recent study revealed that children with ASD are 4.1 times more likely to be stunted, 3.3 times more likely to be overweight, and nearly 11.4 times more likely to be obese than their healthy developing peers are [28]. In line with the literature, it is possible to say that children with ASD are at risk of underweight due to malnutrition and obesity due to unhealthy or overnutrition. In addition, nutritional problems that develop due to ASD may be effective in this situation. As a result, nutritional problems such as obesity and food selectivity, food refusal and obsessive eating behavior are frequently observed in children with ASD, and this situation affects children in terms of growth and development. In particular, the management of nutritional problems should not be ignored when planning the treatment of children with ASD [29].

In our study, 68.0% of the children with ASD had feeding problems, and the most common feeding problems were food neophobia/food obsession/selectivity (44.0%), crying fits/tantrums during eating (42%), food refusal (40%), fast eating (38%) and overeating (32.0%). Children with ASD had more problems than did the control group in terms of feeding problems, food refusal, rapid and excessive eating, food neophobia, food obsession/selectivity, crying fits/tantrums during eating, and the accumulation/holding of food in the mouth (p < 0,05). In support of our findings, studies emphasize that the nutritional problems of children with ASD are serious [30–34]. Kral et al. investigated the nutritional problems of children with ASD (n = 25) and healthy children (n = 30) aged 4–6 years and reported that reluctance to try new foods, food refusal, and food neophobia were significantly greater in children with ASD than in healthy children (p < 0.01) [30]. In a study evaluating children with ASD (n = 55) and healthy children (n = 91) between the ages of 6 and 18, it was determined that food refusal, food selectivity and tantrum during eating were more common in children with ASD (p < 0.01) [31]. Another study examined the feeding problems of children with ASD (n = 137) aged 3–17 years and reported that 84.6% of the participants had food selectivity [32]. Gok et al. reported that food refusal, GIS (especially abdominal pain) and sleep problems are serious in Turkish children with ASD (n = 108) aged 3–18 years [33]. Behavioral strategies should be developed to support children in response to food selectivity and aim to optimize nutrition in children with ASD [35]. The development of behavioral strategies that are important for minimizing feeding problems can ensure that children with ASD receive adequate nutrition. Limited food variety and inadequate intake of some nutrients suggest that routine follow-up of children with ASD should include evaluation of their eating habits in addition to anthropometric measurements [36]. A more in-depth examination of the food preferences and eating habits of children with severe ASD symptoms may shed light on early intervention strategies to improve diet quality [35].

The prevalence of nutritional problems has led to studies investigating the diet diversity and quality of children with ASD [34, 36–39]. In a study evaluating children with ASD (n = 41) and healthy children aged between 2 and 17 years, no significant difference was found in HEI-2015 scores [37], whereas in other studies, the diet scores and diversity scores of children with ASD were lower than those of their healthy peers (p < 0.05) [34, 38]. Another large sample study revealed that children with ASD (n = 12980) had lower dietary diversity than their healthy peers did [39]. It has been emphasized that children with ASD, in particular, have more eating and mealtime problems than their typically developing peers do and that their diet quality and variety are lower [34]. In their study examining the diet quality of children with ASD (n = 105) and healthy children (n = 495), Mari-Bauset et al. reported that the diet quality and diversity of the two groups were similar and that the majority (81.0%) had a diet that needed to be improved [36]. Similarly, in our study, both groups were found to have similar dietary diversity according to the FAO (60.0% vs. 66.0%), and in terms of diet quality, almost all of the groups (96.0% vs. 94.0%) had diet quality that needed improvement. This result may be due to low sample size. Additionally, the diet quality score of the ASD group (71.5 ± 4.11) was significantly lower than that of the control group (73.8 ± 3.92) (p = 0.004). In a study that revealed a relationship between ASD traits and diet quality, children with high and increasing ASD traits had lower diet quality than those with low and stable ASD traits did (β=−0.28, 95% CI). ASD severity may affect diet quality [35]. ASD characteristics in early childhood are associated with poorer dietary quality in later childhood, and food selectivity is thought to mediate this relationship [35]. In particular, food selectivity was found to mediate the relationship between ASD traits at age 1.5 years and diet quality at age 8 years (β=-0.03; 95% CI) [35]. Based on this hypothesis, nutritional problems experienced in individuals with ASD, especially food selectivity, may play a role in reducing diet quality. Higher rates of feeding problems (food refusal, rapid eating, overeating, food neophobia, food obsession, food selectivity, spells during meals and holding food in the mouth) in children with ASD, especially rapid eating and overeating behavior, may be associated with low HEI-2015 scores. Various symptoms of ASD (hyperactivity, attention deficit, repetitive behaviors, tantrums, and crises) may negatively affect children’s food intake, leading to a worsening of diet quality along with existing nutritional and GIS problems. In addition, the results of the studies may differ depending on the methodology of the studies and the characteristics of the sample. Studies examining eating habits, food intake and preferences face a number of inconsistencies and shortcomings due to different sample designs and different methodologies [40]. The limited sample size and lack of long-term follow-up may be limitations of this study. Some limitations may have arisen because the relationships between diet quality and diversity and other factors have not been investigated. Further studies with large sample sizes and follow-up are needed to investigate this issue in depth.

Compared with healthy controls, children with ASD were found to have a less variety of foods, higher levels of inadequate/unbalanced dietary intake, poor diet quality, and more severe GI symptoms. ASD symptom severity and an unhealthy diet were found to be the most important contributors to poor diet quality and GI symptoms [41]. Although the prevalence of GIS problems in ASD is unknown, a comprehensive study on this subject (n = 2973) revealed that 42.0% of children presented with GIS symptoms and that abdominal pain, diarrhea, nausea and dyspepsia were frequently observed among the common symptoms [42]. Chaidez et al. reported that 70.0% of children with ASD experienced GI problems such as diarrhea, constipation, dyspepsia and abdominal pain [43]. Children with ASD are 3 times more likely to have GIS problems than healthy children are [44]. In line with the literature, the prevalence of GIS problems in children with ASD was greater in our study than in healthy children (44.0% vs. 18.0%, respectively, p < 0.05). The prevalence of GIS problems in children with ASD varies between 23.0% and 70.0% [45]. GIS problems can be observed in individuals with ASD for reasons such as medication, impaired intestinal flora, increased intestinal permeability, restrictive diets, limited or no consumption of certain foods, and deficiency of certain enzymes (disaccharidase, etc.) [4]. The increase in the incidence of food intolerance and allergies, the understanding of microbiota dysfunction and the effects of the microbiota on many systems in the body, the prominence of the gut–blood–brain barrier and the nutritional relationship have led to an increase in research on the relationships between ASD and GIS [4].

ASD can cause general functional impairment and reduced quality of life because it is characterized by difficulties in social life and restrictive/repetitive behaviors. Comorbid psychiatric conditions, more ASD-related symptoms, and behavioral problems have been reported to be associated with poorer quality of life [46]. Many studies have shown that the quality of life of children with ASD is lower than that of both healthy children and children with chronic diseases (p < 0.05) [5, 47, 48]. In our study, the total PedsQL score of children with ASD was 49.01 ± 5.94, the total PedsQL score of children in the control group was 89.01 ± 13.24, and the quality of life of healthy children was significantly better than that of the ASD group in all subdimensions. The data obtained in our study are consistent with the literature and indicate that the quality of life of children with ASD is lower than that of their healthy peers. In addition, a positive and low-level significant correlation was found between the participants’ HEI-2015 total score and the PedsQL score (r = 0.295; p = 0.003). Nutritional problems, mood disorders, GI problems, and accompanying diseases observed in ASD may cause a decrease in the quality of life of children [47, 48]. Early intervention programs that provide social skills and behavior management strategies may improve the quality of life of children with ASD [49].

Limitations, strengths and future directions

Although our study constitutes an important step towards understanding the problems of children with ASD, it has some limitations. Our research used a quantitative measure of the problems addressed, and its evaluation is limited to studies using similar methods. Therefore, it may have ignored qualitative studies examining this issue. However, since it is a study based on parental response, there is no direct objective evaluation opportunity like observational and experimental studies. Another limitation of our study is that there is a difference in mean age between the ASD and control groups, and this may affect the results. Since nutritional behaviors and problems in ASD vary according to ASD management, individual characteristics, age and ASD severity, there is a need for studies with large samples in which various age groups and all ASD severity levels are examined in more detail and long-term follow-up is performed.

Conclusion

As a result, children with ASD face more serious problems than their healthy peers do. The development of strategies to address the nutritional and GIS problems experienced by children with ASD and improve their diet quality may also lead to an improvement in their quality of life. It is thought that a nutritional approach with good diet quality and nutritional diversity has the potential to positively affect people’s quality of life by relieving various ASD symptoms (such as, hyperactivity, repetitive behaviors, communication disorders, etc.).
