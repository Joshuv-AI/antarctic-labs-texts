Can the icteric index be used instead of total bilirubin in addition to being a preanalytical marker? Icteric index in the laboratory
Authors: Merve Sena Odabasi, Zeynep Mine Yalcinkaya Kara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe icteric index (I-index) is a practical and low-cost method for screening hyperbilirubinemia and potentially reducing unnecessary total bilirubin (TBil) testing. This study was designed to determine a safe I-index to detect TBil above the upper reference range to avoid unnecessary TBil orders. The reflexive addition of TBil in samples where I-index is above the cut-off value and TBil measurement is not initially planned will be a secondary gain of the study.MethodsThis study included 267185 samples with TBil test results and corresponding I-index values. TBil and I-index values were measured with the Roche Cobas 8000 (c701) and Cobas 6000 (c501) analyzers. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 15.0, with ROC curve analysis used to determine the optimal I-index cut-off for TBil screening.ResultsAn I-index cut-off of 34.2 μmol/L provided the lowest false negativity (0.45 %), highest specificity (97.1 %), and negative predictive value (99.5 %). Using this cut-off point, if we had reached 100 % NPV, 87.4 % of TBil tests in the group receiving inpatient/outpatient treatment with Cobas-8000 and 89.8 % in the emergency group with Cobas 6000 could have been saved.ConclusionsThis is the study with the largest sample size. The sample size is critical in determining a reliable I-index cut-off point. We conclude that I-index should not replace TBil since the NPV was not 100 %; however, it seems reasonable to reflexively add TBil testing in samples where the I-index is above the cut-off value and TBil measurement was not initially planned.Keywords: Icteric index, Total bilirubin, Preanalytical indicator

Introduction

Preanalytical errors constitute a large portion of total laboratory errors and are preventable. The medical laboratory specialist is responsible for detecting the preanalytical error and taking corrective action to eliminate it. In vitro hemolysis (3.3 % of all routine blood samples) is the most common preanalytical error [1]. Although reported HIL prevalences vary, they remain clinically significant in different populations. For example, Salvagno et al. analyzed over 3.5 million samples and found haemolysed samples in 3.3 % of all unsuitable samples, icteric samples in 0.4 %, and lipaemic samples in 0.3 % [2]. More recent data obtained by Cadamuro et al. confirmed that haemolysis is the most common interference, seen in 3–4 % of routine samples. Icterus and lipaemia were less common (≤1 %), but could still significantly affect test accuracy [3]. Similarly, Nikolac emphasised that lipaemia alone could cause clinically significant deviations in 3 % of samples, depending on the test and detection method [4]. These findings highlight the importance of systematically monitoring HIL indices in terms of their potential role in guiding quality assurance and the use of laboratory tests. The incidence rate of lipemia varies between 0.5 % and 2.5 %, depending on the type of hospital and the ratio of inpatient and outpatient samples [5]. In a study, based on data obtained for 1 year, only 0.06 % of the patients had a high icterus index (I-index) [6]. Hemolysis, icteric, and lipemia (HIL) can interfere with the accurate measurement of various analytes, leading to misinterpretations. According to the CLSI C56A guideline, the use of HIL serum indices is recommended in clinical laboratories [7]. HIL indices provide approximate hemoglobin, bilirubin, and lipid concentrations by measuring the sample under different wavelengths [8].

HIL index measurement is a fast, practical, and inexpensive method that reduces the possibility of error in the preanalytical phase and increases patient safety. Although the measurement of HIL indices varies from manufacturer to manufacturer, it requires no specific reagent, only saline solution or water [9]. The HIL index is used to decide whether to reject, report, or report with an explanation of the result of a particular laboratory parameter [3]. Various strategies have been developed to eliminate the effects of hemolytic, icteric, and lipemic samples on analytes [10]. First of all, optimized automatic rule sets need to be developed to report HIL indices.

Icterus is a yellowish pigmentation of the skin and mucous membranes caused by increased bilirubin levels. Hyperbilirubinemia is serum total bilirubin (TBil) values greater than 20.5 μmol/L (1.2 mg/dL) in adults. Neonatal jaundice is a relatively common disease in newborns, and more than half of newborns, and 80 % of premature children develop clinical symptoms of jaundice [11]. Many pathogenic processes can cause jaundice, especially sepsis, hepatic necrosis, intrahepatic biliary obstruction, and alcoholic liver disease [12]. Although TBil testing is not prohibitively expensive, the large number of inappropriate testing requests throughout the year represents a high annual cost to the laboratory. The fact that the I-index is so cheap or has “zero cost” makes us wonder whether a TBil test is needed. This study aimed to determine whether the I-index could be used for TBil screening in our laboratory. We also wanted to understand the additional cost implications of ordering a TBil test reflexively based on a high I-index when a TBil test has not been requested.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted in the Biochemistry Laboratory of Health Sciences University, Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary hospital. The study used the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) guidance to ensure that all relevant information was presented.

We included the data of patients with total bilirubin test results between 01.01.2023 and 01.01.2024 in the study, together with their icteric index. The exclusion criterion was being in the neonatal period (0–28 days). Throughout the study period, 267185 patients’ test results could be retrieved from the laboratory information system (ALIS, Ventura Software Inc. version 10.28, Ankara, Turkey). The number of inpatient and outpatient samples tested with the Cobas 8000 (Roche Diagnostics International, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) device C701 module was 197316. All 69869 samples collected at the emergency department were analyzed on the c501 module of the Cobas 6000 device in the emergency laboratory. Samples with a hemolysis index >1000 g/L and a lipemia index >300, which affect the total bilirubin level, were excluded from the study. All venous blood samples were collected into standard serum tubes (S-Monovette Serum Gel CAT 4.9 mL; SARSTEDT, Nümbrecht, Germany) and centrifuged at 2500×g for 10 min at 18 °C. Total bilirubin concentration was determined using Bilirubin Total DPD Gen.2 (ref 03146022 for c501 and ref 05168414 for c701, Roche Diagnostics), and results are expressed in μmol/L. Hyperbilirubinemia was considered a case where the TBil value was higher than 20.5 μmol/L (1.2 mg/dL). The HIL index was measured with a commercial saline solution (NaCl 0.9 %, ref. 8063494190, Roche Diagnostics). The I-index was measured at 505/480 nm for c701 and c501 modules.

Daily internal quality controls and participation in an external quality assessment (Riqas, Randox Laboratories, United Kingdom) plan verified the data quality throughout the study period. Test results, patients' age, and gender were extracted anonymously from the local ALIS database, so no informed consent was obtained. Ethics committee approval was received by the Hamidiye Etfal Training and Research Hospital Clinical Research Ethics Committee (no: 4136, 24/10/2023).

SPSS 15.0 for Windows program was used for statistical analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk test was first used to evaluate the normal distribution of the data. In descriptive statistics, numbers and percentages were given for categorical variables, and median, minimum, and maximum values were given for numerical variables. Comparisons of numerical variables were made with the Mann-Whitney U test in two independent groups since they did not meet the normal distribution condition. They were interpreted with the effect size for clinical significance. Rates in groups were compared with the Chi-square test. Confidence intervals were obtained using the default method provided by SPSS, which is based on the Exact Binomial (Clopper-Pearson) approach. ROC for cut-off values was determined by Curve Analysis. The alpha significance level was accepted as p˂0.05. Also. The number of patients with an I-index limit of ≥34.2 μmol/L, and no Tbil test requests were taken from the data we obtained via the laboratory information system to determine the effect of requesting additional Tbil on the cost.

Results

Data on samples with I-index and total bilirubin level results are shown in Table 1. It was observed that the I-index values exhibited a proportional increase with rising total bilirubin concentrations (Fig. 1). The I-index level of those with hyperbilirubinemia (Tbil≥20.5 μmol/L) was significantly (p < 0.001) higher than that of those with normal bilirubinemia (Tbil<20.5 μmol/L) for all patients. We set the I-index cut-off at 34.2 μmol/L based on the value with the highest specificity, the lowest false negatives (FN), and the highest negative predictive values (NPV), which could yield the highest TBil savings for all devices (Table 2, Fig. 2). At this cut-off value, if we could achieve an NPV of 100 %, TBil savings rates would be similar for the groups (Table 3). Discussion

This study is the first to compare two Roche platforms and is conducted with a much larger patient population. Since the I-index is related to the total bilirubin level, various studies have been published to demonstrate the savings of the TBil test [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. Our retrospective analysis of the correlation between I-index and total bilirubin values confirms recently published findings. The remarkably high NPV (0.995), combined with very limited bias characterizing I-index FN tests (0.45), would also lead us to conclude that the I-index may be regarded as a possible surrogate of routine total bilirubin assessment.

The Roche device has shown more effective results in terms of TBil test savings in the literature. Pasqualetti et al. achieved approximately 35 % TBil savings with 99.6 % sensitivity, 0.1 % FN, and 99.7 % NPV by evaluating 18486 samples on the Abbott Architect platform [13]. In another study where 22485 samples were evaluated using the Abbott Alinity c platform, 66 % of TBil savings were achieved with 97 % sensitivity, 0.38 % FN, and 99.6 % NPV [14]. In a study in which 185791 samples were evaluated on the Roche Cobas 8000 c702 analyzer, Arbiol-Roca et al. reported sensitivity, FN, and NPV as 99.9 %, 0.01 %, and 100 %, respectively, at an I-index cut-off of 21.0 μmol/L. They found the saved TBil rate to be 88 % [15]. In our study, where we used the same device, we obtained a similar TBil saving rate (87.4 %) with higher FN (0.45 %) and lower sensitivity (95.8 %) and NPV (99.5 %) at a higher cut-off level (34.2 μmol/L). Salinas et al. in their study with 100207 samples on the Roche Cobas c711 analyzer, while the cut-off value was 34.2 μmol/L, they determined the sensitivity, FN, NPV, and TBil savings rate as 96.5 %, 1.20 %, 99.8 %, and 94 %, respectively [16]. To our knowledge, this study has identified the highest TBil savings recorded in the literature. However, Lippi et al., on the same device with 13024 samples at the same cut-off value, achieved a 49 % TBil saving with very similar sensitivity, FN, and NPV [17]. Torrado Carrión et al., in their study with 31161 samples using the Roche Cobas8000-c701 device, found 95.7 % sensitivity, 99.7 % NPV, and 88 % TBil savings at a threshold level of 34.2 μmol/L [18]. In our study conducted in the same module (c 701) in the inpatient/outpatient group, we obtained very similar data for the I index cut-off 34.2 μmol/L. In another study where 33657 samples were evaluated with the Roche Cobas c501 device, they determined the sensitivity, FN, and NPV as 99.6 %, 0.10 %, and 99.9 %, respectively, using the same cut-off value. However, they did not specify the TBil savings value [14]. We used this module for the emergency patient group (n = 69869) with a higher FN value and lower sensitivity and NPV.

Among the studies in the literature using the same device as ours, we have the largest sample size, the lowest NPV, and the highest FN. Arbiol et al. reported a 100 % NPV with a lower i-index cut-off value (21 μmol/L) than this study in a study with a sample of 185,791 people [15]. Although none of the studies with the same I-index cut-off value as ours achieved a 100 % NPV, they reported a higher NPV and a lower FN than this study.

The cost of the TBil test, which will be requested as a reflex test in 37,034 samples without TBil request and with an I-index limit ≥34.2 μmol/L, is approximately 5000 euros per year. If we had achieved a 100 % NPV rate, as in the study by Arbiol et al., the cost of the 233,663 TBil tests that would be saved annually would be approximately 31,551 Euro, resulting in a net savings of 26,551 Euro per year [15]. Unfortunately, we have decided that the I-index cannot be used instead of TBil in our institution, and the only option is to reflexively add the TBil test to samples with an I-index ≥34.2 μmol/L through the laboratory information system.

It is the first study with such a high sample size to demonstrate the usefulness of the I-index in screening for TBil. In this study, the I-index cut-off level was analyzed in two different modules of the Roche device, and the samples were grouped into emergency and inpatient/outpatient. The study has some limitations. We could not find 100 % NPV at any cut-off value, so it is impossible to reject Tbil requests for I-index <34.2 μmol/L. Interference in bilirubin analysis may not be the same as interference in icteric index. The accuracy of the icteric index may be affected by hemolysis and lipemia concentrations lower than the threshold values determined for bilirubin analysis. The lack of data on the accuracy of the icteric index in the presence of hemolysis and lipemia is a limitation of this study.

Conclusions

Although some studies have suggested that measuring the I-index could help reduce unnecessary TBil testing, our findings did not support this conclusion. To establish appropriate I-index cutoff values and optimize testing algorithms, laboratories should conduct local validation studies. The sample size is critical in determining a reliable I-index cutoff, and our study includes the largest sample size reported in the literature. While this spectrophotometric measurement could not replace the reference analytical procedure in our study (as the NPV was not 100 %), incorporating reflex TBil testing in cases where the I-index exceeds the locally validated cutoff and bilirubin measurement was not initially planned appears to be a reasonable approach. Future prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the clinical utility and potential impact on patient care of implementing reflex TBil testing based on elevated I-index values. Such studies should investigate whether earlier detection of hyperbilirubinemia facilitates prompt diagnosis of liver or biliary conditions, reduces unnecessary further testing, and improves overall patient management and clinical decision-making.
