Cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells exhibit an immunosuppressive shift in checkpoint immunotherapy resistance in melanoma patients
Authors: Heekyong R Bae, Byeongchan Son, Kyoungho Hwang, Suntae Kim, Howard A Young, Eun-Young Kwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough checkpoint immunotherapy has primarily focused on CD8⁺ T cells, emerging evidence highlights an important role for cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in mediating therapeutic responses. However, research on the functional properties of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in the context of immunotherapy is still at an early stage and remains insufficiently defined. Utilizing single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets obtained from metastatic melanoma patients treated with checkpoint inhibitors targeting PD-1 and/or CTLA-4, we performed transcriptomic profiling of conventional CD4⁺ T cells, excluding proliferative and regulatory (FOXP3⁺) subsets, and compared responders and non-responders as distinct groups. Importantly, our analysis identified distinct clusters that discriminate between responders and non-responders, with cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells occupying a central position within these clusters. In responder-specific clusters, cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells exhibited features of early activation, whereas clusters specific to non-responders were characterized by an exhausted phenotype. Notably, non-responder-specific clusters were positioned proximally to Treg-like clusters, suggesting a potential transition from cytotoxic to regulatory CD4⁺ T cell states in non-responders. Our findings reinforce the emerging concept that cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells play a central role in mediating immunotherapy responses. These results provide a foundation for the development of predictive biomarkers and novel therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating CD4⁺ T cell differentiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04145-6.Keywords: Checkpoint immunotherapy, Cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells, Metastatic melanoma

Introduction

The therapeutic efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors (CPIs) has been primarily attributed to the activation of cytotoxic T cells and their ability to sustain anti-tumor responses within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [1, 2]. Traditionally, research has focused on CD8⁺ T cells because they are the primary effectors responsible for directly killing tumor cells through MHC class I recognition. Their functional impairment, marked by high expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, CTLA-4, Tim-3, and LAG-3, is a well-established feature of checkpoint inhibitor resistance, particularly in non-responders [3, 4]. This exhaustion state diminishes their cytotoxic potential, allowing tumors to evade immune surveillance.

In parallel, the expansion and activation of regulatory T cells (Tregs) have been recognized as another critical factor contributing to the immunosuppressive milieu, actively modulating the TME [5]. Tregs, typically characterized by FOXP3 expression, suppress effector T cell functions and promote tumor immune evasion by secreting inhibitory cytokines and upregulating checkpoint molecules [6, 7]. Together, CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion and Treg-mediated suppression create an environment conducive to tumor progression and therapy resistance.

While much emphasis has been placed on CD8⁺ T cells and FOXP3⁺ Tregs, recent studies have increasingly highlighted the crucial role of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in anti-tumor immunity [8]. Initially considered an in vitro artifact, CD4⁺ T cells with cytotoxic potential have now been confirmed in vivo, contributing to antiviral defense, tumor clearance, and inflammatory responses [9, 10]. These cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells express key effector molecules, including granzyme B and perforin, executing MHC class II-restricted tumor cell killing. Their cytotoxic mechanisms closely resemble those of CD8⁺ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells, underscoring their therapeutic relevance [8].

However, despite their capacity to mount anti-tumor responses, the regulation of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells during CPI therapy, particularly in non-responders, remains poorly understood. It is unclear whether their functional impairment mirrors that of CD8⁺ T cells or if they undergo phenotypic transitions, potentially adopting immunosuppressive features like Tregs. Given the known plasticity of CD4⁺ T cells, which allows them to differentiate into various functional states in response to environmental cues [11], such transitions may represent a previously underappreciated mechanism of immune escape during CPI therapy.

In this study, we specifically aimed to investigate the fate of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in melanoma patients undergoing checkpoint blockade, focusing on their functional dynamics and potential transition toward an exhausted phenotype in non-responders. By delineating the molecular mechanisms underlying this exhaustion-associated phenotypic shift, we sought to uncover novel insights into CPI resistance and identify potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets to restore effective anti-tumor immunity.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and processing of scRNA-seq data from checkpoint-treated melanoma patients

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from 36 biopsies of 32 metastatic melanoma patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors were analyzed using the publicly available dataset GSE120575. As described in the original study [12], four patients (P1, P4, P5, P28) contributed both responder (R) and non-responder (NR) lesions, as response status was defined at the lesion level, with each biopsy evaluated independently. Patients received anti-PD-1, anti-CTLA-4, or combination therapies, and tumor responses were assessed according to RECIST 1.1 criteria, classifying patients as responders (complete or partial response) or non-responders (stable or progressive disease). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1A–B, this dataset includes a total of 16,291 cells and was selected due to its relatively large cohort size among CPI-treated melanoma datasets, with responder and non-responder groups. The processed and annotated dataset was sourced from the TISCH database (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org), which provides high-quality tumor immune single-cell profiles. To annotate cell types, we utilized the TISCH-based algorithm, which integrates both global transcriptome profiles and canonical marker gene expression (Supplemental Fig. 1B). TISCH utilizes the MAESTRO pipeline, which combines PCA-based dimensionality reduction, Louvain clustering, and semi-automated annotation based on canonical marker genes [13, 14]. The resulting cell type annotations have been widely adopted and manually curated to ensure consistency across datasets. We therefore adopted the TISCH-derived subtype annotation, focusing specifically on conventional CD4⁺ T cells for this study. For this study, we focused on the conventional CD4⁺ T cell population, excluding CD8⁺ T cells, exhausted CD8⁺ T cells, proliferative T cells, and canonical Tregs, which are typically characterized by CD4⁺CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ expression. We analyzed 3,341 conventional CD4⁺ T cells, subjecting them to downstream clustering and comparative analyses between responder and non-responder groups to elucidate their transcriptional differences and potential relevance to therapeutic outcomes.

Clustering analysis of conventional CD4⁺ T cells using scanpy and scVI-tools

The scRNA-seq data were processed and analyzed using Scanpy (v1.10.2) and scVI-tools (v1.1.5) implemented in Python (v3.9.5). Initial quality control steps included filtering out cells with fewer than 200 detected genes, cells with over 10% mitochondrial gene content, and genes expressed in fewer than three cells. Normalization was performed using log transformation, followed by data scaling to ensure comparability across cells. For dimensionality reduction, we applied the Single-cell Variational Inference (scVI) framework to embed high-dimensional gene expression profiles into a lower-dimensional latent space optimized for clustering. A nearest-neighbor graph was constructed based on the scVI latent variables, and clustering was performed using the Leiden algorithm at a resolution of 0.5, resulting in distinct CD4⁺ T cell subpopulations. The clusters were visualized with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to explore their spatial distribution. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for each cluster were identified using the rank_genes_groups function in Scanpy, employing the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to determine cluster-specific markers.

Pre-ranked gene set enrichment analysis using GSEApy

To explore the functional characteristics of each CD4⁺ T cell cluster, we conducted pre-ranked GSEA using GSEApy (v4.3.3) under Python (v3.12.6). Gene rankings were based on log fold changes obtained from differential expression analyses. We utilized the Hallmark and C2 gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) to assess pathway enrichment relevant to T cell function and immune responses. GSEA was performed with standard parameters, including an exponent of 1, and gene sets with a q-value < 0.05 (FDR-corrected) were considered significantly enriched.

Results

Classification of conventional CD4⁺ T cells and their distribution in responders and non-responders

To investigate the existence and role of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in the context of checkpoint immunotherapy, we utilized scRNA-seq data from 32 metastatic melanoma patients treated with checkpoint inhibitors. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, conventional CD4⁺ T cells were classified using the TISCH algorithm, which integrates global transcriptomic features and canonical marker expression to exclude proliferating and regulatory T cell subsets. While low-level CD8A expression was detected in a minor subset of cells, these did not form a transcriptionally distinct CD4⁺CD8⁺ cluster in UMAP analysis. Furthermore, cells exhibiting low-level CD8A expression continued to express MHC class II genes, consistent with a CD4⁺ T cell transcriptional identity. This pattern suggests that the observed CD8A expression is unlikely to represent a bona fide double-positive T cell population. Instead, it likely reflects transient transcriptional plasticity within cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells, as supported by recent studies suggesting that CD8A can be inducibly expressed under specific immunological conditions, such as chronic inflammation during viral infection or within the tumor microenvironment [15]. Supplementary Fig. 1C shows that conventional CD4⁺ T cells exhibit distinct distributions between responders and non-responders, similar to well-established cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cell patterns. These observations led us to further investigate subtype-specific differences, including the presence of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cell populations.

Figure 1 illustrates the clustering and distribution of these conventional CD4⁺ T cells between responders (R) and non-responders (NR). In Fig. 1A, the scatter plot shows the three-dimensional distribution of clusters, where cluster 6 appears relatively dispersed compared to other clusters despite being spatially connected. The scatter plot in Fig. 1B displays the proportion of R- and NR-derived cells within each cluster. The hierarchical clustering in Fig. 1C displays selected representative marker genes, associated phenotypic features, and the relative proportions of each cluster, providing a simplified visualization of their transcriptional relationships. To support transparent reporting, a comprehensive list of the top 20 differentially expressed genes for each cluster, including log fold-change and adjusted p-values, is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Figure 1D shows the percentage composition of R- and NR-derived cells within each cluster using bar graphs. Cluster 7 was composed almost entirely of NR-derived cells (> 90%), and cluster 8 was predominantly composed of R-derived cells (> 90%), suggesting potential differences in immune composition between responders and non-responders. However, as shown in Fig. 1D, the composition of conventional CD4⁺ T cell clusters varied substantially across patients, reflecting individual-level immune heterogeneity. Clusters 7 and 8, for instance, were primarily enriched in specific patients (e.g., P15 and P1, respectively), indicating patient-specific immune states.

Clusters 0, 2, 6, 7, and 8 exhibited higher gene expression levels and more distinct patterns compared to clusters 1, 3, 4, and 5 (Fig. 2 and Supplemental Fig. 2). The heatmap in Fig. 2A displays the top 20 marker genes enriched in each cluster, highlighting the distinct transcriptional signatures of clusters 0, 2, 6, 7, and 8. Scatter plots provide a broader view of the distribution of key marker genes across all clusters, allowing for the visualization of cluster-specific expression patterns (Fig. 2B). Additionally, Fig. 2C highlights genes with significantly elevated expression in each cluster, further confirming the transcriptional uniqueness of clusters 0, 2, 6, 7, and 8. In particular, cluster 0 showed high expression of TIGIT and CTLA4, suggesting features associated with exhausted T cells. The elevated expression of HLA-A and PIM2 in the same cluster also implies an activated state with concurrent exhaustion features. Cluster 2 exhibits elevated expression of heat shock proteins, suggesting substantial cellular stress within the tumor microenvironment. The upper region of this cluster, enriched in non-responders, shows increased expression of TIGIT and CTLA4, consistent with an exhausted or suppressive phenotype. In contrast, the lower region, enriched in responders, displays higher levels of GZMB and IFNG, reflecting a more cytotoxic and active functional state. Cluster 6 is characterized by high expression of cytotoxic markers such as GZMK, GZMA, and CST7, along with activation markers HLA-DRA and HLA-DRB1, suggesting a cytotoxic and activated CD4⁺ T cell population. Cluster 7 is characterized by high expression of metabolic and stress-response genes such as PLIN2, GK, SOD1, and DUSP4, indicating a metabolically adapted CD4⁺ T cell population capable of regulating activation-induced stress. In contrast, Cluster 8 is characterized by high expression of CXCR4, GPR183, BTG1, and ZFP36, suggesting a migratory and memory-like CD4⁺ T cell population.

Clusters 1, 3, 4, and 5 exhibit relatively lower overall gene expression compared to clusters 0, 2, 6, 7, and 8, suggesting a less distinct transcriptional identity. However, characteristic patterns are still observed among these clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results suggest that clusters 1 and 5 (SF 2B and 2E, respectively), which are enriched for ribosomal protein genes such as RPS3A, RPL10, and RPL32, may reflect a transcriptionally active or proliferative state commonly observed in activated T cells. In contrast, clusters 3 and 4 (SF 2C and 2D, respectively), which express genes associated with cytoskeletal remodeling (e.g., ACTB, VIM) and stress response or migration (e.g., ANXA1, CXCR4), may represent populations undergoing transitional changes following activation.

Identification of cytotoxic CD4+ T cell and treg-like subpopulations

Based on our previous analysis, we hypothesized that cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells and exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells might serve as potential indicators of responsiveness to checkpoint immunotherapy. To further investigate this possibility, we examined the expression of cytotoxic and regulatory T cell markers across the identified clusters (Fig. 3). Figure 3A demonstrates that clusters 2, 6, and 8 exhibit higher CD8A expression compared to other clusters, suggesting the presence of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells. Notably, clusters 2 and 6 show elevated expression of key cytotoxic T cell-associated markers, including CD8A, NKG7, PRF1, TNFRSF9, IFNG, and GZMB, indicating their strong cytotoxic potential. This observation is further supported by Fig. 3B, where violin plots depict the distinct expression patterns of these markers across clusters. Conversely, Figs. 3C and 3D indicate that clusters 0 and 7 exhibit strong expression of Treg-associated markers, including FOXP3, IKZF2 and CTLA4. Since conventional Tregs were specifically excluded from this analysis, the presence of FOXP3⁺ CD4⁺ T cells in cluster 0 suggests the emergence of a Treg-like subset among non-regulatory CD4⁺ T cells. Given the overall transcriptional similarity between clusters 2 and 7 (Fig. 1C), cluster 7 may represent a population undergoing a transcriptional shift toward an immunosuppressive phenotype. This supports the hypothesis that these cells could correspond to exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells transitioning toward a Treg-like functional state.

It is also noteworthy that clusters 2 and 6 exhibit relatively strong expression of cytotoxic markers while concurrently expressing exhaustion-related genes such as TIGIT and PDCD1. Given that activated cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells are often classified within the proliferating T cell compartment, their presence within the conventional CD4⁺ T cell subset, by exclusion of proliferative populations, may facilitate more accurate detection of exhausted yet cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cell populations. Supplementary Table 2 provides detailed cell counts per patient and cluster, including responder (R) and non-responder (NR) status. It is important to note that approximately 60% of identified clusters (~ 62% in the NR group and ~ 58% in the R group) consist of fewer than 10 cells per patient. Despite the small cell numbers, consistent patterns were observed across multiple independent datasets (e.g., Supplementary Fig. 3), supporting the biological relevance of these clusters. In Fig. 3E, we compare the average cell frequency of each cluster between R and NR groups on a per-patient basis, while Fig. 3F reports the proportion of patients exhibiting each cluster, independent of total cell number. Notably, clusters 2 and 6 show a trend toward higher representation in NR patients. In Fig. 3G and H, we further compare the expression of cytotoxic and Treg-like markers between NR and R groups within clusters 2 and 6. Particularly in cluster 6, NR-derived cells show a more distinct co-expression of cytotoxic and exhaustion-related genes, suggesting that this cluster may represent a more specific subset of exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells enriched in non-responders.

Cytotoxic CD4+ T cell to treg-like transition as a mechanism of therapy resistance

To investigate whether cluster 7 may have originated from cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells, we performed pathway analysis within each cluster, comparing responders and non-responders. Our analysis specifically focuses on the potential transition of cluster 7 from cluster 2 and its contribution to therapy resistance. Figure 4A shows that clusters 2 and 7 exhibit similar enrichment patterns, particularly in T cell functional pathways and inflammatory hallmark gene sets, suggesting that these two clusters share common transcriptional programs. Considering that cluster 2 expresses cytotoxic marker genes, while cluster 7 expresses exhausted marker genes, the mechanistic similarity between clusters 2 and 7 implies a shift from a cytotoxic toward an exhausted transcriptional trajectory. Additionally, Fig. 4B lists genes that are significantly downregulated in both clusters 2 and 7, supporting their related functional states. In contrast, cluster 8 shows a trend toward increased expression of these genes compared to clusters 2 and 7, although the differences were not statistically significant. As mentioned previously, Fig. 4C further highlights that within cluster 2, R and NR cells are spatially separated, with NR cells positioned closer to the NR-specific cluster 7 and R cells located toward the R-specific cluster 8.

Further investigating transitional states among cell populations, we applied dynamic trajectory inference based on transcriptomic changes, as our analysis was confined to clearly defined conventional CD4⁺ T cells, excluding proliferative, regulatory, and CD8⁺ T cell subsets. This allowed us to explore potential phenotypic transitions within this compartment with reduced confounding from heterogeneous cell states. As shown in Fig. 4D, cluster 4 exhibits strong transcriptional trajectories toward clusters 2 and 6. The expression of CXCR4, EZR, and MYADM in cluster 4 suggests partial migratory characteristics and supports its potential role as the origin of these diverging lineages. We also observed a directional shift from cluster 7 to cluster 0, suggesting that exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells (cluster 7) may represent an intermediate state preceding the acquisition of an immunosuppressive, Treg-like phenotype (cluster 0).

Finally, our findings are summarized in the schematic diagram (Fig. 4E). At the branching point of cluster 4, cells diverge toward either cluster 2 or cluster 6, both of which exhibit an exhausted cytotoxic phenotype. Notably, cluster 6 is predominantly observed in non-responders (NR). These exhausted subsets may further transition into cluster 0, characterized by an immunosuppressive, Treg-like state, suggesting a potential trajectory underlying resistance in NR patients.

Discussion

Cytotoxic CD4 T cells have been reported in both human and mouse studies; however, they are not commonly observed under steady-state conditions and are typically found in specific contexts such as chronic infection, cancer, or persistent inflammation [10, 16]. In this study, we identified a transcriptionally distinct CD4⁺ T cell population characterized by concurrent expression of cytotoxic genes (e.g., GZMB, NKG7) and exhaustion markers (e.g., PDCD1, TIGIT), which were more enriched in non-responders (NR) to checkpoint inhibitor (CPI) therapy.

Based on our findings, we point to potential limitations of conventional in identifying cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cell populations, as these cells often share transcriptional features with CD8⁺ T cells, including CD8A expression. This overlap introduces ambiguity, particularly in distinguishing cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells from CD8⁺ T cell populations, thereby limiting their precise identification. Therefore, restricting the analysis to conventional CD4⁺ T cells, excluding proliferative and regulatory CD4⁺ subsets as well as CD8⁺ T cells, may enable more accurate tracking of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cell states.

Using this approach, we also identified similar subsets within untreated tumor tissues through single-cell analysis of conventional CD4⁺ T cells, driven from the GSE176078 dataset according to the TISCH algorithm, which includes 26 primary human breast tumors (ER⁺, HER2⁺, and TNBC), as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. In the UMAP projection (SF 3A), clusters 5 and 13 are positioned in proximity, suggesting potential transcriptional relatedness. Cluster 5 is characterized by distinct expression of Treg-like marker genes (SF 3B), whereas cluster 13 shows enriched expression of cytotoxic markers (SF 3C). Notably, cluster 13 also co-expresses TIGIT and PDCD1, indicating the potential presence of exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells within the tumor microenvironment. Therefore, single-cell analysis focused on conventional CD4⁺ T cells can consistently reveal the presence of exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells, even in non-immunotherapy-treated tumors.

Here, we uncovered a subset of exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells, predominantly present in non-responders (NRs), that exhibited transcriptional features indicative of a transition toward an immunosuppressive state, marked by increased expression of PD-1 and CTLA-4. Dynamic trajectory inference based on transcriptomic changes further supported this transition, revealing that exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells (cluster 6) in NRs are inclined to differentiate toward immunosuppressive, Treg-like clusters (cluster 0). Functional analysis also showed that exhausted T cell states (clusters 2 and 7) are suppressed, while cytotoxic T cell responses (cluster 8) are enhanced in responders relative to non-responders.

While our analysis offers transcriptomic evidence for the enrichment of exhausted cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in non-responders, it is derived entirely from single-cell RNA-seq data and does not include direct functional validation. This contrasts with earlier foundational studies that established CD4⁺ T cell cytotoxicity through in vitro and in vivo assays [17–19]. Nevertheless, our strategy focusing on conventional, non-proliferative CD4⁺ T cells, minimizes lineage ambiguity due to transient CD8A expression and reveals a distinct CD4⁺ T cell state with potential clinical implications. This transcriptome-based framework may serve as a practical tool to stratify immune responses in the tumor microenvironment and to identify predictors of immunotherapy resistance. Future studies incorporating TCR clonotype analysis and functional validation will be crucial to confirm the cytotoxic potential and tumor reactivity of these cells.

Cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells have drawn attention for their direct tumor-killing capabilities, akin to CD8⁺ T cells. These cells utilize granzyme B, perforin, and IFN-γ to mediate MHC II-dependent tumor suppression, even in the absence of CD8⁺ T cells [19–21]. In melanoma, these cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells have been shown to directly engage tumor cells via HLA class II-restricted mechanisms [22]. In line with these observations, our data reveal elevated HLA class II gene expression, such as HLA-DRA, in cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells. This suggests that, despite their cytotoxic phenotype, these cells retain activation-associated features of conventional CD4⁺ T cells, including HLA class II expression, highlighting their phenotypic plasticity within the tumor microenvironment.

One intriguing observation is the elevated expression of HLA class II genes, typically associated with professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs), in cluster 2 and 6 cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells. It raises the question of how HLA class II molecules are induced in these cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells. Interestingly, this expression co-occurs with exhaustion markers, such as PD-1 and CTLA-4, suggesting a potential link between aberrant HLA class II expression and the exhausted state. One possibility is that chronic antigen stimulation within the tumor microenvironment drives both HLA class II upregulation and functional exhaustion. Supporting this notion, a recent review underscores the need to fully leverage the potential of CD4⁺ T cells, given their polyfunctionality, ability to recognize both self- and tumor antigens, and capacity to modulate the TME [23]. Furthermore, melanoma studies have shown that HLA class II⁺ tumor cells can directly induce both cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells and FOXP3⁺ Tregs through neoantigen presentation, with cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells gradually transitioning into an exhausted phenotype and Tregs contributing to immune suppression [24]. This shift is further corroborated by Liu et al., who demonstrated that tumor-specific CD4⁺ T cells progressively increase PD-1 and CTLA-4 expression, accompanied by reduced cytokine production and proportional CD4 loss following chronic stimulation [25]. Therefore, it is plausible that sustained antigenic engagement in the tumor microenvironment not only induces HLA class II expression in cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells but also contributes to their functional exhaustion and potential reprogramming into immunosuppressive states.

Beyond this, the Treg-like conversion of exhausted CD4⁺ T cells may interfere with their capacity to regulate myeloid cells. Kruse et al. demonstrated that CD4⁺ effector T cells enhance anti-tumor immunity not only through cytotoxicity but by reprogramming myeloid cells into cytotoxic effectors via IFN-γ signaling [26, 27]. Loss of cytotoxic function and acquisition of regulatory features in exhausted CD4⁺ T cells likely impair this cross-talk, reducing IFN-γ production and, consequently, the anti-tumor activity of myeloid cells. Therefore, this phenotypic switch contributes to therapy resistance through multiple, interconnected pathways: both by enhancing immunosuppression directly and by undermining supportive myeloid cell functions within the TME. Preventing or reversing this shift may thus be critical not only for sustaining CD4⁺ T cell effector functions but also for preserving the anti-tumor capacity of other immune compartments.

Overall, our study provides novel insights into the dynamics of cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cells in the context of checkpoint immunotherapy. We propose that the functional impairment and transition of these cells toward an immunosuppressive state contribute to therapy resistance in non-responders. These findings underscore the need for alternative therapeutic strategies aimed at preserving cytotoxic CD4⁺ T cell function and preventing their conversion into immunosuppressive Treg-like cells. By further elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying this process, we may uncover new targets to enhance the efficacy of checkpoint immunotherapy and improve clinical outcomes for cancer patients.
