Implementation of bidirectional crusher gradient method for measuring labelling efficiency of pseudocontinuous arterial spin labeling MRI in mice
Authors: Xiuli Yang, Yuguo Li, Zhiliang Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe pseudocontinuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) MRI technique provides a powerful tool for in vivo evaluation of microvascular functions, including cerebral blood flow, blood–brain barrier permeability, and blood–cerebrospinal fluid exchange. These microvascular parameters have emerged as promising biomarkers for tracking pathological progression and therapeutic efficacy in both preclinical and clinical settings. Accurate quantification of pCASL signals requires consideration of labeling efficiency, a critical parameter that can vary substantially in mice due to their small vessel size and intersubject vascular heterogeneity. Determination of labeling efficiency on an individual basis is therefore desirable. Recently, the bidirectional crusher gradient (BIC) method was proposed to measure labeling efficiency in mouse pCASL MRI. The present study aims to outline step-by-step procedures for implementing the BIC method and associated validation studies.(1) Measurement of mouse pCASL MRI labeling efficiency in approximately 3 min(2) Improved accuracy in pCASL-based cerebral perfusion imaging of miceKeywords: Arterial spin labeling, ASL, Cerebral blood flow, Perfusion, Labeling efficiency, Crusher gradient

Background

Arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI is based on the spin tagging principle [1], in which flowing blood spins are selectively manipulated in control and label scans to generate a contrast related to hemodynamic parameters such as cerebral blood flow (CBF), blood–brain barrier permeability, and blood–cerebrospinal fluid exchange [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. To reduce hardware demands, pseudocontinuous ASL (pCASL) has become widely used in diverse pathological applications, including Alzheimer’s disease, stroke, and Moyamoya disease [[7], [8], [9]]. In parallel with its broad clinical use, pCASL MRI has gained increasing popularity in preclinical studies aimed at validating imaging biomarkers, characterizing pathophysiological profiles, and monitoring neurofunctional responses to treatment [4,10,11]. However, in mice, the small vessel size and intersubject vascular heterogeneity make it difficult to achieve consistent labeling performance. Consequently, evaluation of labeling efficiency on an individual basis is essential for accurate quantification of pCASL signals.

Numerical simulations based on experimental parameters were among the earliest approaches for evaluating labeling efficiency in pCASL MRI [12,13]. Subsequently, it became clear that experimental determination was preferable, as it accounts for intersubject variability and physiological noise. Such experimental estimates can be obtained either through phase-contrast–based normalization [14] or by measuring signals at a plane proximal to the labeling site [15]. These methods were primarily developed and optimized for human pCASL MRI and later extended to animal studies [16]. More recently, the bidirectional crusher gradient (BIC) method has been proposed and optimized for estimating labeling efficiency in mouse pCASL MRI [17]. In this work, we present step-by-step procedures for implementing BIC-pCASL MRI and validate its feasibility in improving quantification accuracy and robustness under pathological conditions.

Method details

An 11.7 T Bruker Biospec system (Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany) with a horizontal bore and actively shielded pulse field gradient (maximal intensity: 0.74 T/m) was used for imaging. Data were acquired using a 72-mm quadrature volume resonator as the transmitter and a four-element (2 × 2) phased-array coil as the receiver. B0 homogeneity across the mouse brain was optimized using global shimming (up to the second order) based on a subject-specific pre-acquired field map. Mice were anesthetized with 1.5 %–2.0 % isoflurane for 15 min as an induction. At the 10th minute of induction, each mouse was placed on a temperature-controlled, water-heated animal bed and immobilized using a bite bar, ear pins and a custom-built 3D-printed ramped holder before being positioned within the magnet. Maintenance isoflurane was typically set at 1.0–1.2 % to maintain a respiration rate of 70–120 breaths per minute or adjusted according to the study’s purpose [18].

As shown in Supplementary Fig. 1, the implementation of BIC-pCASL MRI begins with a localizer scan (Step 1) to provide anatomical reference for positioning the slices used in time-of-flight (TOF) angiography. Second, TOF angiography (Step 2) is acquired in sagittal and coronal views to visualize the feeding arteries—specifically the left and right carotid arteries (LCA and RCA)—in the neck region. Third, the labeling plane is positioned perpendicular to the LCA and RCA, guided by the TOF images; the phases of the control and label scans are optimized (Step 3), and the phase-offset combination that yields maximal control and minimal label signals is selected. Finally, BIC-pCASL (Step 4) and conventional pCASL (Step 5) acquisitions are performed using the optimized phase offsets; voxel-wise CBF was quantified using an established equation [[1], [16]].

A fast low-angle shot (FLASH) scan, referred to as the “localizer” on the Bruker Biospec system, is first performed to localize the mouse (Fig. 1A). The scan is conducted with the following parameters [19]: repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) = 120/2.3 ms, field of view = 40 × 40 mm2, matrix size = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 1.0 mm, flip angle = 30°, and scan duration = 0.3 min. Anatomical images are acquired from three orthogonal orientations: axial, sagittal, and coronal (Fig. 1A).

A sagittal TOF MRI is performed to visualize the feeding arteries of the brain in the sagittal orientation, referenced to the axial localizer image (orientation indicated by the dotted yellow line in Fig. 1A). The central slice of the sagittal TOF is positioned to cover the midsagittal plane of the mouse brain. Representative coronal TOF images are shown in Fig. 1C.

The labeling plane is positioned with reference to the LCA and RCA in the sagittal and coronal views, as indicated by the yellow rectangles overlaid on the TOF images (Figs. 1B and 1C). The LCA and RCA are identified based on their vascular morphology and relative positions to other arteries, including the basilar artery (BA), left internal carotid artery (LICA), and right internal carotid artery (RICA) (Fig. 1C).

A two-scan pCASL scheme developed to improve labeling performance under magnetic field inhomogeneity is adopted [16]. A pre-scan to optimize phase offsets for the control and label scans is first performed with the following parameters: TR/TE = 3000/6.6 ms; field of view = 15 × 15 mm²; slice thickness = 0.75 mm; interslice gap = 0.25 mm; matrix size = 64 × 64; number of slices = 12; receiver bandwidth = 250 kHz; labeling duration = 1800 ms; inter-labeling pulse delay = 1.0 ms; labeling pulse width = 0.4 ms; labeling plane thickness = 1.0 mm; labeling pulse flip angle = 40°; mean gradient = 0.01 T/m (label scans) and 0 T/m (control scans); post-labeling delay = 300 ms; and scan duration = 2.5 min using a single-shot gradient-echo EPI acquisition. The gradient-echo EPI readout shortens TE and thus improves detection sensitivity. The phases of the labeling pulses in the control and label scans are incremented from 15° to 360° in 15° steps. The ΔM/M₀ map is then calculated from the pre-scan data, where ΔM represents the signal difference between control and label scans and M₀ represents the equilibrium magnetization (which can be estimated from the control signals to save scan time [16]). This map demonstrates the dependence of perfusion-weighted signals on the phase combinations (Fig. 2A). Optimal phase combinations that maximize perfusion-weighted signal (ΔM/M₀) can be obtained over a continuous range, such as the upper left and upper right regions in Fig. 2A. Note that radiofrequency pulse phases are periodic with a fundamental period of 360° The upper left and upper right regions should be considered as a single continuous region due to the phase periodicity. A phase combination near the center of this region is selected for subsequent BIC-pCASL and conventional pCASL scans to ensure robust detection sensitivity. In addition, the two-scan pCASL scheme can suppress bilateral hemispheric asymmetry in perfusion imaging that can arise from technical factors. With the pre-scan data, a bilateral hemisphere asymmetry analysis can be performed by calculating [(ΔMM0)Right−(ΔMM0)Left]/(ΔMM0)Right, where “Right” and “Left” denote the brain hemispheres. By definition, an asymmetry index of 0 indicates perfect balance between the right and left hemispheres in terms of perfusion signals. The asymmetry index may be displayed in absolute-value mode for reading convenience. Using the criteria of high ΔM/M₀ and low asymmetry, the optimal phase combination for the demonstration condition is 75° for the control scan and 360° for the label scan. Note that the optimal phase combination may vary with the labeling condition, for example when testing different mice, testing the same mouse across different sessions, or changing the labeling settings without repositioning the mouse.

Phase offset optimization for two-scan pCASL MRI. (A) Dependence of the ΔM/M0 signal on the phase combination between control and labeled scans. (B) Bilateral hemisphere asymmetry of the pre-scan signal.

The labeling setup determined in the pre-scan is applied to the ensuing BIC-pCASL and conventional pCASL scans, using the optimal phase combination identified earlier. BIC-pCASL consists of four interleaved scans: Scan 1, control with an X-axis crusher gradient; Scan 2, label with an X-axis crusher gradient; Scan 3, control with a Z-axis crusher gradient; and Scan 4, label with a Z-axis crusher gradient. Key acquisition parameters are: TR/TE = 3000/11.8 ms, slice thickness = 0.75 mm, labeling duration = 1200 ms, post-labeling delay = 100 ms, crusher gradient duration = 2.0 ms, crusher gradient strength = 0.148 T/m, readout orientation (X gradient) = ventral to dorsal, phase-encoding orientation (Y gradient) = rostral to caudal, slice-selection orientation = left to right (Z gradient), number of averages = 8, and scan duration = 3.2 min using a two-segment spin-echo EPI acquisition. The labeling duration can be tuned according to the achieved detection sensitivity of BIC-pCASL signals at the azPA. The conventional pCASL scan uses the same parameters as BIC-pCASL, except for the following: labeling duration = 1800 ms, number of averages = 25, and scan duration = 5.0 min. After pairwise subtraction between the four BIC-pCASL scans (Fig. 3A), two difference images are generated (Fig. 3B) that provide clear contrast in the azPA region with selective vascular suppression. Signal intensities are then extracted by averaging the voxel values within the azPA across the four scans (Fig. 3C). Finally, the labeling efficiency (denoted as α) is calculated according to: α=(Mscan1−Mscan2)−(Mscan3−Mscan4)2(Mscan1−Mscan3).

Yellow arrows indicate the azygos pulmonary artery (azPA). (C) Signal intensity within the azPA across the different scans. (C) Signal intensity within the azPA across the different scans.

BIC-pCASL MRI evaluates labeling efficiency at the azPA, a distal branch of the major feeding arteries to the mouse brain. After spins are labeled at the neck, they require a transit period to travel along the vasculature before reaching the azPA. During this time, T₁-related decay occurs, necessitating a calibration based on the bolus arrival time (BAT) to the azPA. According to the original report [17], BAT to the azPA (denoted asBATazPA) is 209.2 ± 19.1 ms (N = 8). Assuming a blood T1 of 2813 at the 11.7 T [20], a scaling factor of eBATazPAT1blood=1.08 should be applied to the estimated α. Although subject-specific measurement of BATazPA is ideal, it is time consuming as it requires acquisitions at multiple post-labeling delays. The resulting data can be fitted with a Gaussian function, consistent with the Taylor dispersion process that governs the transit of labeled spins in an artery [21], where the velocity gradient across the vessel radius leads to dispersion along the flow direction. In contrast, kinetic models such as Buxton’s and its variants assume a plug-flow arrival without velocity gradients [22]. For this reason, Gaussian fitting is used instead of multiparametric kinetic modeling. Because blood T₁ at 11.7 T is relatively long, in practice one may use the reported scaling factor to correct for T₁ decay during transit. For example, a 20 % decrease or increase in BATazPA produces only a −1.9 % or +0.9 % change in the scaling factor. Alternatively, phase-contrast MRI can be used to estimate mean blood flow velocity (Vm, cm/s) in the internal carotid arteries. For reference, a BATazPA of 209.2 ms ± 19.1 ms corresponds to a Vm of 7.8 ± 1.3 cm/s. Assuming a constant distance from the labeling plane to the azPA, BATazPA can be approximated as BATazPA=26.82Vm ms. Taking all these considerations into account, a typical labeling efficiency achieved under the current protocol is approximately 0.78.

Complete crushing of arterial signals is ideal but not mandatory in BIC-pCASL MRI [17]. Therefore, the crusher gradient parameters used in this study represent a feasible, though not exclusive, configuration. Smaller or shorter crusher gradients may also be employed depending on the hardware capability of the MRI system. At magnetic field strengths lower than 11.7 T, a longer labeling duration may be required to maintain detection sensitivity. The post-labeling delay is primarily determined by mouse physiology (e.g., bolus arrival time) rather than by magnetic field strength. Therefore, mouse-strain-dependent variations may occur, and dedicated optimizations, as outlined previously [17], are recommended.

Method validation

CBF quantification accounting for labeling efficiency variations

This study was designed to demonstrate how labeling efficiency measurements improve the accuracy of pCASL-based CBF quantification. During phase optimization, the optimal phase combination was used as the ground truth for perfusion (denoted as “Opt”), while a suboptimal phase combination (denoted as “Subopt”) producing ∼50 % of the maximal perfusion-weighted signal was included for comparison. In groups with BIC-pCASL scans (“Opt + BIC” and “Subopt + BIC” in Fig. 4A), CBF maps for each mouse were calculated using its individually measured labeling efficiency. In groups without BIC-pCASL scans (“Opt” and “Subopt” in Fig. 4A), a constant labeling efficiency of 0.78 was assumed. When an assumed labeling efficiency was used, suboptimal labeling led to markedly reduced whole-brain perfusion compared with optimal labeling (Fig. 4A). In contrast, when BIC-pCASL was applied to account for labeling efficiency on an individual basis, suboptimal labeling produced perfusion maps comparable to those obtained under optimal labeling. At the group level of global CBF, a linear mixed-effects model revealed a significant interaction between labeling condition (“Opt” vs. “Subopt”) and labeling efficiency strategy (assumed vs. measured) (estimate = –43.529 ml/100 g/min, 95 % CI = [–56.811, –30.246], p < 0.001) (Fig. 4B), highlighting the risk of false hypoperfusion when an assumed labeling efficiency was used under suboptimal labeling. A similar finding was observed for cortical CBF (Fig. 4C). In contrast, measuring labeling efficiency on an individual basis minimized CBF differences across varying labeling conditions.

Proof-of-principle demonstration of CBF quantification using individual labeling efficiency. (A) Averaged CBF maps under optimal and suboptimal labeling conditions, with or without BIC-pCASL (N = 7). (B, C) Quantitative CBF values of the whole brain (B) and cortex (C) across different conditions. Curves in different colors represent individual mice.

Effect of flow pattern on labeling efficiency

We performed Bloch equation–based simulations using the experimental parameters described in the “Method details” section. Simulations were performed using previously shared code available at https://github.com/zyliamwei/pCASL_Simulation [17]. The previously reported one-to-one simulated relationship between flow velocity and labeling efficiency was extended in this study to fully account for voxel-wise variations in flow velocity. All newly developed code has been deposited in the same GitHub repository to facilitate reproducibility. The dependence of labeling efficiency on flow velocity and artery radius was investigated (Fig. 5A). Labeling efficiency showed a significant dependence on flow velocity (estimate = 0.007 s/cm, 95 % CI = [0.007, 0.007], p < 0.001) but not on artery radius (estimate = –0.268 m⁻¹, 95 % CI = [–1.799, 1.263], p = 0.731). Consistent with this strong velocity effect, the labeling efficiency map was heterogeneous across the arterial cross-section in a laminar flow, with higher values near the center and lower values toward the boundary (Fig. 5B). A mean labeling efficiency was then obtained by spatially averaging the labeling efficiency map, representing the overall labeling condition within the simulated artery. The same procedures were repeated for a plug flow condition with a constant velocity of 15 cm/s (Fig. 5C). A clear separation in mean labeling efficiency was observed between laminar and plug flow patterns (Fig. 5D), indicating that blood flow pattern influences labeling efficiency. In addition, although the two-scan pCASL scheme is effective in alleviating the effect of magnetic-field inhomogeneity on labeling performance, other experimental imperfections remain, including intersubject vascular heterogeneity and physiological perturbations. In principle, perfect labeling (100 %) would occur when the maximal difference between control and label scans is achieved. Here, the term “maximal difference” refers to the maximal difference possible, not the maximal difference observed. Because of confounding factors beyond field inhomogeneity, the maximal difference observed is smaller than the maximal difference possible. Therefore, perfect labeling was generally not achievable in practice, and a labeling efficiency of ∼0.78 represents a technically robust performance under the current protocol.

Mechanistic link between blood flow velocity and labeling efficiency

Under the hypercapnia challenge, an increase in labeling efficiency was observed concurrently with elevated peak and mean flow velocities [17]. To further test the mechanistic link, we examined whether reduced flow velocities would lead to lower labeling efficiency. The neuroinflammation model induced by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has been reported to exhibit hypoperfusion [23], and we leveraged this physiological feature to test our hypothesis. A cohort of seven 9-month-old C57BL/6 mice (4 females, 3 males) was studied pre- and post-LPS injection. As a control group, five age-matched mice (3 females, 2 males) were tested pre- and post-saline injection. A previously reported phase-contrast MRI protocol [24,25] was employed to examine velocity changes in the internal carotid arteries. Linear mixed-effects modeling revealed a significant effect of LPS (estimate = –4.572 cm/s, 95 % CI = [–6.688, –2.456], p < 0.001) but not saline (estimate = –0.576 cm/s, 95 % CI = [–3.021, 1.870], p = 0.629) on peak flow velocity in the LICA (Fig. 6A). Similar results were obtained for the RICA (Fig. 6B) and for mean flow velocity (Fig. 6C). Concurrently, labeling efficiency was significantly reduced by LPS injection (estimate = –0.086, 95 % CI = [–0.134, –0.039], p = 0.001) but not by saline injection (estimate = –0.017, 95 % CI = [–0.071, 0.037], p = 0.509) (Fig. 6D). These findings indicated that labeling efficiency decreased in parallel with reduced blood flow. Collectively, results from the hypercapnia experiment, the neuroinflammation model, and the simulations support a mechanistic relationship between blood flow velocity and labeling efficiency: changes in flow velocity directly drive changes in labeling efficiency.

Anesthetic agents can have distinct vasoactive effects. For example, isoflurane is vasodilatory and increases flow velocity and CBF in a dose-dependent manner [26], whereas dexmedetomidine is vasoconstrictive and reduces flow velocity and CBF [27]. In a validation experiment, we measured flow velocities in the internal carotid arteries using phase-contrast MRI [28]. Peak flow velocities were 10.43 ± 1.76 cm/s in the awaking C57BL/6 mice (N = 5), 17.04 ± 1.01 cm/s under 1.5 % isoflurane (N = 5), and 6.95 ± 0.90 cm/s under a combination of 6 µg dexmedetomidine and 0.5 % isoflurane (N = 3). Analysis of variance (ANOVA) confirmed a significant anesthetic effect on flow velocity (F[2, 10] = 59.579, p < 0.001). Considering the dependence of labeling efficiency on flow velocity, these findings suggest that anesthetic agents modulate labeling efficiency indirectly through their effects on cerebral hemodynamics.

Generality across pathological conditions

We tested BIC-pCASL in three mouse models to evaluate its generality across pathological conditions, including two amyloidosis models of Alzheimer’s disease (APP23 and 5xFAD) and a neuroinflammation model induced by 2 mg/kg lipopolysaccharide (LPS). Clear selective suppression of pCASL signals at the azPA was observed in the APP23 (Fig. 7A), 5xFAD (Fig. 7B), and neuroinflammation (Fig. 7C) models, as indicated by the red arrows, suggesting that BIC-pCASL is effective for these applications. The labeling efficiency was 0.728 ± 0.056 for APP23 mice (N = 10; 7 females, 3 males) compared with 0.784 ± 0.108 for matched wild-type (WT) controls (N = 9; 5 females, 4 males), 0.792 ± 0.104 for 5xFAD mice (N = 10; 5 females, 5 males) compared with 0.810 ± 0.084 for matched WT (N = 8; 5 females, 3 males), and 0.671 ± 0.055 for mice after LPS injection (N = 8; 5 females, 3 males) compared with 0.724 ± 0.075 for mice after saline injection (N = 5; 3 females, 2 males). When all control groups were pooled, the labeling efficiency was 0.780 ± 0.094 (N = 22; 13 females, 9 males). Changes in labeling efficiency were attributed to pathology-driven alterations in blood flow velocity.

Using baseline measurement from the mouse hypercapnia experiments [17], the phase-contrast normalization method yielded a labeling efficiency of 0.346 ± 0.058, whereas BIC-pCASL produced a labeling efficiency of 0.723 ± 0.044. This substantial underestimation arises from the violation of a key assumption underlying the normalization method—namely, that all delivered water spins are fully extracted by tissue. In mice, however, the measured water extraction fraction was 59.9 ± 3.2% [2]. Accounting for this factor reduces the discrepancy; for instance, a simple scaling according to the water-extraction fraction yields a mean labeling efficiency of 0.578. Labeling efficiency measured by BIC-pCASL (0.780 ± 0.094) showed good agreement with previously reported values (0.782 ± 0.042) obtained using a method that tracked labeled spins at a plane adjacent to the labeling plane [29].

In summary, this study establishes and validates the BIC-pCASL framework as a robust approach for quantifying labeling efficiency in preclinical MRI.

Limitations

BIC-pCASL MRI was developed to measure the labeling efficiency of pCASL MRI on an individual basis. Currently, the method is primarily intended for preclinical applications. Extension to human studies would require systematic optimization and redevelopment to fully account for species-specific differences in physiology and anatomy. It reflects the nominal labeling efficiency value under varying conditions but does not improve labeling performance when labeling itself is poor. Previous studies have reported labeling improvements in mouse pCASL MRI by deliberately optimizing the positioning of the labeling plane [29]. Importantly, a technique that measures nominal labeling efficiency provides insight into how much improvement is achieved when advanced labeling schemes are applied. Without such measurement, assuming identical improvements across all practical conditions in mice may introduce additional inaccuracy into CBF quantification. Thus, BIC-pCASL serves as a valuable complement to existing techniques for pCASL MRI in mice.

The scan durations of BIC-pCASL and conventional pCASL MRI were approximately 3 min and 5 min, respectively, when implemented separately under the current protocol. This ∼60 % increase in scan time may limit applications requiring high temporal resolution, such as real-time studies of reperfusion after stroke. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers a powerful tool for enhancing the performance of magnetic resonance techniques [[30], [31], [32]]. Further developments that incorporate AI may open important avenues to overcome this limitation.
