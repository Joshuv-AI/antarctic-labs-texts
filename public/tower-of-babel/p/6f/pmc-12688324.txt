Cost-Effectiveness of Photodynamic Therapy and 5-Fluorouracil Cream versus Surgical Excision in Treatment of Bowen’s Disease: A Trial-Based Economic Evaluation
Authors: Shima Ahmady, Charlotte A.M. van Riel, Nicole W.J. Kelleners-Smeets, Klara Mosterd, Brigitte A.B. Essers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGiven the increasing incidence of Bowen’s disease, treatment leads to a substantial economic burden for healthcare services. There are several treatment options for Bowen’s disease, of which surgical excision, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and methyl aminolevulinate photodynamic therapy (MAL-PDT) are the most commonly used. Recently, results from a randomized controlled non-inferiority trial showed that 5-FU was non-inferior to excision and was associated with a better cosmetic outcome. MAL-PDT was not shown to be non-inferior to excision. Although 5-FU and MAL-PDT were expected to be cheaper than excision, it remains to be determined whether the potential cost savings compensate for the loss of effectiveness. The aim of this study was to determine the most cost-effective treatment for Bowen’s disease when comparing surgical excision, MAL-PDT, and 5% 5-FU cream from a healthcare perspective.MethodsData were collected alongside a randomized controlled trial with 250 patients in the Netherlands. Valuation of treatment costs was based on documented resource use and Dutch cost prices. A cost-effectiveness analysis was performed from a healthcare perspective. The primary outcome was the decremental cost-effectiveness ratio (DCER), expressed as the cost-savings per additional recurrence or residual Bowen’s disease. Bootstrap analysis and sensitivity analysis were performed to address uncertainty. This trial is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov number, NCT03909646.ResultsAt 12 months after treatment, the costs for 5-FU cream were significantly lower (EUR 311 [CI: −240 to −378]) and the costs for MAL-PDT were higher (EUR 3 [CI: −74 to 65]) compared to excision. 5-FU cream offers cost savings compared to excision, but is less effective although within the non-inferiority margin of 22%. Our results showed that 5-FU has the highest probability of being cost-effective at willingness to accept threshold values of EUR 2,500 and lower compared to MAL-PDT and surgical excision.Conclusion5-FU cream is a cost-effective treatment at a threshold value of EUR 2,500 and lower when compared to surgical excision and MAL-PDT. Therefore, from a cost-effectiveness point of view, 5-FU is considered the first-choice treatment option for Bowen’s disease.

Introduction

Invasive cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) is the second most common keratinocyte carcinoma, characterized by atypical keratinocytes invading the dermis [1–6]. In Bowen’s disease, also called SCC in situ, the atypical keratinocytes are limited to the epidermis. Typically, Bowen’s disease presents as a sharply demarcated, erythematous, scaly patch in areas with high sun-exposure that slowly grows over time [7]. In the Netherlands, incidence rates of Bowen’s disease showed a statistically significant increase between 1989 and 2017 in both men and women [8]. If left untreated, Bowen’s disease may invade the basement membrane and evolve into invasive SCC, although progression rates are probably very low [7, 9]. To prevent any risk of progression, Bowen’s disease needs to be treated. There are multiple treatment options for Bowen’s disease, including invasive and noninvasive treatments [7]. The treatments differ in duration, clinical effectiveness, side effects, costs, and possibly in impact on quality of life. For example, surgical excision is considered highly effective in terms of lesion clearance. However, it is invasive and often results in less favorable cosmetic outcomes than noninvasive alternatives. Noninvasive treatments, such as methyl aminolevulinate photodynamic therapy (MAL-PDT), typically produce better cosmetic results. However, they require multiple hospital visits and may be associated with treatment-related pain. Furthermore, they are generally less effective. Both MAL-PDT and surgical excision are administered in a hospital setting, often resulting in longer waiting times and higher procedural and facility-related costs. In contrast, topical 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) is a noninvasive treatment that can be self-administered, reducing the need for hospital visits and potentially lowering overall treatment costs.

Given the increasing incidence of Bowen’s disease, treatment leads to a substantial economic burden for healthcare services [8]. It is important to justify the cost of treatment, especially when less expensive but highly effective treatment alternatives exist [10].

Recently, we performed a randomized controlled trial (RCT), comparing the clinical effectiveness of surgical excision, MAL-PDT and 5% 5-FU cream in Bowen’s disease [11]. Results showed that 5-fluorouracil is non-inferior to excision and associated with better cosmetic outcome. For MAL-PDT non-inferiority to excision could not be concluded. Although 5-FU and MAL-PDT were expected to be cheaper than excision, it remains to be determined whether the potential cost savings compensate for the loss of effectiveness. Therefore, parallel to the clinical trial, we performed an economic evaluation to analyze the cost-effectiveness of MAL-PDT and 5-FU cream compared to surgical excision, from a healthcare perspective.

Methods

Study Design and Population

Data for the economic evaluation were collected alongside a Dutch randomized controlled non-inferiority multicenter trial (Maastricht University Medical Center+, Catharina Hospital Eindhoven, VieCuri Hospital Venlo, and Zuyderland Hospital Heerlen) in which 250 patients were randomized to three treatments: surgical excision, MAL-PDT or 5-FU cream. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Ethics Review Board. All patients gave written informed consent. Full details of the clinical trial are given elsewhere [11].

Eligible for inclusion were patients 18 years or older with a histologically proven primary Bowen’s disease lesion (diameter ≥4 mm and ≤40 mm). Excluded were patients with genetic skin cancer disorders, lesions located at specific sites of the body unsuitable for any of the treatments, such as ears, periocular, mucous membrane, nose, and nail unit, lesions that were difficult to reach by the patient (considering self-appliance of 5-FU cream), lesions suspected for invasive SCC, immune comprised status, and immunosuppressant drug use in the past 3 months and/or at time of treatment.

Treatment Procedures

Surgical Excision

Standard surgical excision with 5-mm safety margin was performed under local anesthesia by the treating physician in the hospital, followed by histological examination. Sutures were removed in primary care setting or in the hospital, 1 or 2 weeks after the procedure.

Methyl Aminolevulinate Photodynamic Therapy

Photodynamic therapy involved application of a 1-mm thick layer of MAL cream (Metvix®, Galderma) to the lesion including a of 5–10 mm margin of normal skin. The applied cream was covered by an occlusive dressing (Tegaderm®) blocking ultraviolet light. After 3 h, the skin was illuminated with Aktilite® (Photo Therapeutics Ltd) or Omnilux® (Galderma) (632 nm, 37 J/cm2). Afterward, the lesion site was covered again by the occlusive dressing for 48 h blocking daylight. This procedure was performed twice with 1 week interval and was performed in the hospital by an authorized nurse.

5% 5-FU

The patient applied a thin layer of 5% 5-FU cream (Efudix®, Mylan bv) on the lesion twice a day for 4 weeks.

Follow-up took place at 3 months after treatment with MAL-PDT and 5-FU (according to the summary-of-product-characteristics), and at 12 months for all patients. During follow-up, the lesion was evaluated by the supervising dermatologist to determine treatment failure. In case of clinically suspect residue or recurrence at 12 months posttreatment, a 3-mm punch biopsy was taken. After histological confirmation of Bowen’s disease, a standard surgical excision was advised, but another treatment was also possible if preferred by the patient.

Economic Evaluation

A cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) was performed with a time horizon of 12 months from a healthcare perspective. The reason for not choosing a societal perspective was that productivity loss and out of pocket costs were expected to be minimal because patients with Bowen’s disease are generally above retirement age.

A distinction was made between pretreatment, treatment, and posttreatment costs. Pretreatment costs consisted of a regular outpatient visit, a medical photograph, a biopsy and afterward a telephone consultation to discuss the results of the biopsy and diagnosis. Treatment costs for surgical excision and MAL-PDT both include personnel costs, materials and an overhead of 38% over the total treatment costs since they are in-hospital treatments [12].

The costs for surgical excision vary, depending on the location on the body and on the participating hospital; surgery either took place in a common treatment room of the outpatient clinic or at the surgical theater. Treatment costs for MAL-PDT include cream and a light source used (Aktilite/omnilux) for which annual depreciation costs were calculated over 10 years with an interest of 4.2% [12]. Treatment costs of 5-FU only include the price of the cream. Posttreatment costs consist of a telephone consultation after the end of treatment and a control outpatient visit at 3 and/or 12 months posttreatment. Additional telephone consultations, outpatient control visits, biopsies, and/or treatments in case of residue/recurrence, visits to the general practitioner or treatments in case of side effects related to the included lesion, were also part of the posttreatment costs. Data on resource use were collected from the participating hospitals.

Cost prices were derived from the Dutch costing manual or the hospital financial department [12]. Detailed costs per unit of resource use for each treatment are presented in Table 1. All costs were indexed to 2020 and are presented in euros [13]. Discounting of costs and effects (QALY) was not applied due to the 1-year time horizon.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the decremental cost-effectiveness ratio (DCER), expressed as the cost-savings per additional recurrence or residual Bowen’s disease. The DCER was calculated by dividing the difference in mean costs between treatment arms by the difference in effectiveness, where effectiveness was measured as the proportion of patients with sustained clearance at 12 months. Thus, a lower proportion of sustained clearance reflects higher recurrence or residual disease, consistent with the interpretation of the DCER.

Analysis

The base case of the cost-effectiveness was analyzed according to the modified intention-to-treat principle (mITT) in line with the clinical data [11]. The mITT consisted of patients who were randomized and for whom a primary clinical endpoint was available.

Cost-Effectiveness

Since costs data are generally skewed, nonparametric bootstrap analysis was performed by which a total of 10,000 random resamples were taken from the original data and replaced while maintaining the original size and number of patients per treatment group [14, 15]. The bootstrap analysis estimated the difference in mean costs between the three treatments with a 95% CI (Microsoft Excel version 2016) [14]. Nonparametric bootstrap analysis was also applied to calculate an acceptability curve, showing the probability that surgical excision, MAL-PDT, or 5-FU is cost-effective for different willingness to accept thresholds. Since Bowen’s disease is not invasive and it does not have an impact on life expectancy, we considered a willingness to accept threshold value of EUR 2,500 and lower to compensate for an additional recurrence acceptable.

Sensitivity Analysis

To examine the impact of uncertainty on the results of the CEA, two sensitivity analyses were performed. First, for the per protocol analysis, patients were analyzed according to the treatment they actually received. Second, a cost-utility analysis (CUA) was performed with the quality-adjusted life year (QALY) as outcome measure. The QALY combines life expectancy with health-related quality of life, the latter measured with the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire [16]. This questionnaire includes five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain or discomfort, and anxiety or depression. Each dimension has 5 levels varying from no problems to severe problems. Patients were asked to complete the EQ-5D-5L questionnaire at baseline and 12 months. The Dutch tariff for the EQ-5D-5L was used to convert the patients’ experienced health state into an utility score [17]. Subsequently, quality-adjusted life years were calculated by multiplying the average of the baseline and 12-month utility score with 12 months. Finally, the ICER was calculated as the difference in cost savings divided by the difference in QALYs at 12-month follow-up. A regression-based correction method was used to correct for differences in baseline utility scores [18].

p values of ≤0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with IBM SPSS Statistics, version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and OpenEpi software.

Results

Study Population

A total of 250 patients were included and randomized to either surgical excision (N = 84), MAL-PDT (N = 84) or 5-FU cream (N = 82). Main reasons for exclusion were death unrelated to Bowen’s disease, refusal of the treatment, refusal to attend follow-up visits, or inability to visit the hospital due to old age or comorbidities. Fourteen patients crossed over before starting their assigned treatment. In the per protocol analysis, these patients were analyzed according to the treatment they received.

Cost-Effectiveness

Table 4 presents the DCERs of the base case analysis. Results show that 5-FU has the largest cost savings per additional recurrence. The cost-effectiveness plane (Fig. 1a) demonstrates that all ratios comparing 5-FU to excision are located in the south-west quadrant, indicating lower effectiveness. Results for MAL-PDT versus excision show that the majority of all simulated ratios are also located in the south-west quadrant, although some ratios fall in the quadrant indicating inferiority. The acceptability curve illustrates the cost-effectiveness of 5-FU compared to excision across different willingness to accept thresholds (Fig. 1b). The curve shows that 5-FU cream is a cost-effective treatment at a willingness to accept threshold value of EUR 2,500 or less. On average, MAL-PDT was more expensive and less effective (with a probability of being cost-effective of 0%).

Sensitivity Analyses

Results of the sensitivity analyses in Table 5 show that 5-fluorouracil always has the highest probability of being cost-effective compared to MAL-PDT and surgical excision. In the per-protocol analysis, there is a 72% probability that 5-FU is cost-effective compared to surgical excision at the same threshold. The CUA with the QALY as outcome shows that the difference in mean QALY was 0.02 in favor of 5-FU and 0.01 in favor of MAL-PDT when compared to surgical excision. Dividing the difference in costs by the difference in QALY led to dominance for 5-fluorouracil, indicating that 5-FU gained more QALYs and was less expensive than surgical excision. For MAL-PDT, the ICER was EUR 4,900 per QALY gained compared to surgical excision. 5-FU has the highest chance of being cost-effective compared to MAL-PDT and surgical excision with a probability of 100%.

Discussion

From a cost-effectiveness point of view, 5-FU cream is the preferred option for treating Bowen's disease, over MAL-PDT and surgical excision. The results showed that, on average, 5-FU cream was the least expensive. In terms of the DCER, 5-FU cream offers cost savings compared to excision, but it is less effective, although within the predefined non-inferiority margin of 22% of the clinical trial [11]. In this context, the question is how much monetary gain is required to compensate for the loss in effectiveness. We considered a threshold, i.e., the amount of cost savings a stakeholder is willing to accept as compensation for a residual or recurrent lesion of EUR 2,500 acceptable because Bowen’s disease is a premalignant condition and recurrences can be treated easily and at low cost (around EUR 800), without compromising patients’ health or quality of life.

For the sensitivity analysis, the observed difference in QALYs between the treatments was 0.02 in favor of 5-FU and 0.01 in favor of MAL-PDT when compared to surgical excision. One might question whether these QALY differences are clinically meaningful since they are below the minimal important differences for the EQ-5D-5L scores (between 0.037 and 0.069) [19]. However, if we consider the mean QALYs comparable between treatments and only take costs into consideration, 5-FU cream would still be preferred to excision because the total costs including the costs for treating a recurrence are the lowest for 5-FU compared to MAL-PDT and surgical excision.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the cost-effectiveness of three frequently prescribed treatments for Bowen’s disease within a RCT. Only two small non-prospective European studies analyzed costs and clinical effectiveness of different treatments of Bowen’s disease [10, 20]. Ramrakha-Jones and Herd [10] performed a cost minimization study of six different treatments and found that Bowen’s disease is most cheaply treated by curettage or excision biopsy, and most expensively treated by PDT. However, as this was a cost-minimization study, all treatments were considered to be equal in effectiveness. Aguilar et al. [20] compared surgical excision, MAL-PDT and imiquimod for superficial basal cell carcinoma and Bowen’s disease and found that the nonsurgical therapies were associated with significantly lower costs than surgery. One possible explanation for the fact that MAL-PDT was much cheaper than in our study, could be that the authors did not include the costs of a light source used in the treatment of PDT. Furthermore, details of the treatment procedure are lacking, making it difficult to compare the costs.

Our study is a valuable addition to the previous studies since data were collected alongside a RCT [11]. Therefore, it was possible to measure real resource consumption. The treatment procedures, follow-up visits, and additional treatments in case of side effects reflect daily clinical practice. Sensitivity analyses were performed to test the robustness of the CEA. The results of these analyses were similar to the mITT findings, indicating the robustness of the conclusion.

This study has some limitations. As the study was performed in the Netherlands, the costs prices used were specific to the Dutch healthcare system. However, our detailed description of resource use allows for comparison in other settings, despite cost prices differing per country. This trial-based economic evaluation had a one-year time horizon, which was in line with the follow-up period of the clinical study. This means that information about potential long-term consequences on effectiveness and quality of life is missing. However, it is expected that the cumulative probability of treatment failure after excision and 5-FU remains stable at 1 year, as is shown in a previous study [21]. Additionally, the differences in QALYs between treatments observed in this study are small and are not expected to change in the long-term.

In conclusion, the results of the cost-effectiveness analyses showed that 5-FU cream has the highest probability of being a cost-effective treatment for Bowen’s disease compared with surgical excision and MAL-PDT at thresholds of EUR 2,500 and lower. Therefore, 5-FU cream should be considered as the first-choice treatment for Bowen’s disease.
