Estimated disability weights for the severity of health outcomes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaoxue Liu, Yongbo Wang, Fang Wang, Haoyun Zhou, Qiuxia Zhang, Runtang Meng, Yong Yu, Yongchao Liu, Chuanhua Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe disability weight quantifies the severity of health states from diseases and injuries. It is a fundamental index to estimate the disability-adjusted life year in the Global Burden of Disease studies. Disability weight estimates have been shown to vary across different national populations, suggesting the influence of cultural differences. However, survey data of disability weights in the Global Burden of Disease study is still limited worldwide.ObjectivesTo more accurately reflect the true health conditions of global populations, this study aims to systematically summarize the disability weight values from international authoritative surveys, and explore the influential factors of disability weight estimates.MethodsBased on the Global Burden of Disease study, surveys used paired comparison questions wherein respondents considered two hypothetical individuals with different health states and specified which person was healthier. This study comprehensively searched multiple databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Science Direct, and Google Scholar. We identified disability weight studies that utilized the paired comparison method and were conducted in national populations, published in international peer-reviewed journals. A meta-regression analysis was conducted to estimate the overall summary effect of disability weight values for 235 unique health states. These health states were estimated for all non-fatal consequences of disease and injury, including infectious diseases, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, neurological disorders, mental, behavior, and substance use disorders, hearing and vision loss, musculoskeletal diseases, injuries and others. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistics. Univariate meta-regression analysis was conducted to explore the impact of age, sex, education, population composition, and survey regions, respectively, on the summarized effect of each health state.ResultsThe total analysis sample consisted of 610,818 respondents from the Global Burden of Disease 2013 disability weight surveys, the Japanese disability weight survey, and the Chinese disability weight survey. The summarized disability weights of health states ranged from mild anaemia (summarized disability weight = 0.008, 95% uncertainty interval 0.001–0.016, I2 = 0.95) to heroin and other opioid dependence (moderate to severe) (summarized disability weight = 0.737, 0.651–0.823, I2 = 0.823). Pearson correlation analysis showed that high correlation was observed between the set of overall summary disability weights of 235 health states from this meta-analysis and those from all included disability weight studies (all Pearson’s r > 0.9, P < 0.001). Univariate meta-regression analysis indicated that age, sex, education level, panel composition of survey populations, and the survey regions were associated with the summarized disability weights of some health states.ConclusionsThe overall summary of disability weights obtained from this meta-analysis is reliable. This study indicates that respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics may impact a population’s preference for health states, which should be considered in future disability weight assessments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-025-00425-6.

Introduction

The disability weight (DW) quantifies the severity of health states from disease and injury. It is a fundamental index to estimate the disability component of the disability-adjusted life year (DALYs) [1, 2]. In the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, the DALY measures and aggregates the total health loss at the population level into a single index by summarizing years of life lost (YLLs) due to premature death and years lived with disability (YLDs) [3, 4]. Since 1990, the GBD study has continuously explored and updated DW measurement methods, and the methodology for estimating DWs has gradually matured. After the publication of the ground-breaking GBD DW study in 2015 [5, 6], a new set of unique health states from disease and injury was estimated. This has been applied in the iteration of the GBD studies. Since 2015, a series of DW surveys based on the GBD DW study have been conducted worldwide, including in countries such as Japan [7], China [2], and various European nations [8]. However, despite a high level of consistency in DW values across countries, studies conducted in various national populations have confirmed the presence of cultural differences [1, 2, 7, 8]. Specifically, compared to the GBD 2013 DW study, many health states with higher DWs in the Japanese population were injuries, while mental disorders generally tended to be lower [7]. In China, applying the Chinese DWs to the GBD estimates for China, total YLDs increased by 14.9% to 177 million; and the lower estimates of YLDs were more than offset by higher DWs of common, low-severity conditions [1]. In Europe, absolute differences between the GBD 2013 DW study and European DWs ranged from − 0.165 (HIV, symptomatic, pre-AIDS) to 0.185 (fracture of pelvis, short term) [8]. Some health states showed differences between the Dutch DWs and the GBD 2013 DWs [9, 10]. However, survey data from the GBD 2013 DW study are still limited worldwide. The data sources still lack population data at the national level, for example, from countries such as China and Japan. Thus, to obtain more accurate DWs for a set of health states relevant to the global population, this study employs a meta-analysis to systematically estimate the overall summary effect of DW values for diseases and injuries, drawing on all avaliable international authoritative surveys worldwide. Considering education, profession, and other sociodemographic characteristics of respondents may impact the assessments of DW [11–14], this study also explores the influential factors of DW estimates.

Method

Searching strategy

This study conducted a comprehensive search of multiple databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, Science Direct, and Google Scholar, to identify relevant articles on DW measurement. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) checklist was used [15]. The searches were limited to articles written using the English language. The search keywords employed were: ((((((disability weight) OR (disability weights)) OR (DW)) OR (DWs)) OR (severity weight)) AND (((((((((disability adjusted life year) OR (disability-adjusted life year)) OR (DALY)) OR (DALYs)) OR (years lived with disability)) OR (YLD)) OR (YLDs)) OR (burden of disease)) OR (disease burden))) AND ((“1990/01/01“[Date - Publication] : “2023/12/25“[Date - Publication])). The final date of searching was December 25, 2023. Additionally, we have supplemented the search to October 2024. The detailed search strategy for PubMed database is provided in the supplemental file.

Study population and outcome

In this study, the study population comprised the global population. The study outcome is to obtain the average level of disability weights of different disease sequelae across this population. Referring to the GBD 2013 DW study, these health states correspond to 10 major categories of diseases or injuries, including infectious diseases, cancer, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases, neurological disorders, mental, behavior, and substance use disorders, hearing and vision loss, musculoskeletal diseases, injuries and others. This meta-analysis aimed to obtain the summarized DW estimates for 235 unique health states.

As for these health states, the authoritative GBD DW study established DWs for 235 unique health states across all diseases and injuries in 2015 [6]. The set of health states continues to be applied in the estimation of global YLDs and DALYs, as seen in the GBD 2021 which convered 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories, including 811 subnational locations[4]. Moreover, several national studies from European countries, China, and Japan have estimated DW values for their population using this same set of 235 unique health states from the GBD DW study, employing the PC method [2, 7, 8]. Thus, this study included 235 health states from the GBD 2013 DW study. The health states are presented in the supplemental file (pp 4–29).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria included the following: (1) the study provided a set of DW estimates for unique health states based on the GBD 2013 DW study; (2) it used PC method for health state assessment; and (3) it involved a human population aged 18 years and above (including male and female). Articles with unclear methodologies, and articles that didn’t report the outcome of health assessment were excluded. Quality assessment

According to Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [16] 2020 Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews and Research Syntheses (URL: https://joannabriggs.org/critical-appraisal-tools), the quality of the included studies was assessed by two independent authors (X.L. and Y.W.). The discrepancy during the quality appraisal of the studies was resolved by the agreement of the two reviewers. Finally, the studies that met the inclusion criteria and reported the outcome of interest were included in the meta-analysis (Supplementary Table 1).

Study selection and data extraction

All the articles searched from Databases were imported into EndNote (version X9), and duplicates were removed. Based on the inclusion criteria, two authors (X.L. and Y.W.) independently assessed and identified studies by titles, abstracts, and full texts. The following data were extracted from each study: first author, publication year, region/country, study population characteristics (age, sex, panel composition), study design (health states, description, valuation methods), time presentation, and sample size.

Heterogeneity analysis

The I² statistic was employed to assess statistical heterogeneity across studies. I² quantifies the percentage of total variation that is due to between-study differences rather than chance. It ranges from 0% to 100%, with lower values indicating less heterogeneity. An I² value of 0% suggests no observed heterogeneity. As the I² increases, so does the degree of heterogeneity. Although a I² threshold of 50% is often used to indicate substantial heterogeneity, we adopted a random-effects model for all meta-analyses regardless of the I² value. This approach was chosen because the random-effects model accounts for both within-study and between-study variability, providing a more conservative estimate of the overall effect size. Additionally, in meta-analyses involving clinical or observational studies, it is often reasonable to assume that some degree of heterogeneity exists, even if it appears low, due to variations in study design, population characteristics, and other factors. Univariate meta-regression analyses were conducted to examine the potential influence of study-level covariates—including age, sex, education, population composition, and regions—on the overall summary effect across the 235 health states. We performed all statistical analyses with Stata/MP (version 15) and R (version 4.2.2).

Paired comparison method

The DW, anchored between 0 (perfect health) and 1 (equivalent to death), quantifies the severity of a health state. The paired comparison (PC) method is a technique used to quantify differences between different health states, thereby establishing a severity ranking of each health state. The disability weights of health states are generally obtained through population surveys, and the PC method is used to collect populations’ preferences on health states from diseases and injuries. Specifically, PC method asks respondents to consider descriptions of two hypothetical people, each with a particular health state, and specify which person was healthier. In line with the study objective, we conducted a meta-analysis using survey data from different countries, using the PC method and the DW results derived from a probit regression model.

Results

Study characteristics

Four articles invloving 610,818 participants were finally included in this meta-analysis, which pooled the overall DW values for 235 unique health states. These DW studies were performed in China, Japan, and the GBD survey regions. The GBD survey regions included Bangladesh, Indonesia, Peru, Tanzania, USA, four European countries (Hungary, Italy, the Netherlands, and Sweden), and a web-based survey including respondents from 167 countries (Table 1).

The summarized DW results for 235 health States

This meta-analysis provided overall summary estimates of DWs and 95% uncertainty interval (UI) for these health states based on the included DW measurement studies (Table 2). Among 235 health states, the summarized DW of health states ranged from mild anaemia (0.008, 95% UI: 0.001–0.016, I2 = 0.95) to heroin and other opioid dependence (moderate to severe) (0.737, 95% UI: 0.651–0.823, I2 = 0.823). This result was consistent with the GBD 2013 DW study. The median DW value across all states was 0.192 (Interquartile Range: 0.070–0.270), with 35 (15%) of the states having DW of less than 0.04. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a high correlation between the summarized DW estimates of 235 health states from this meta-analysis and DW results from all included DW studies (0.940 < Pearson’s r < 0.964, P < 0.001). The results are presented in Supplementary Table 2. The original DW values for 235 health states from the four included DW studies are presented in Supplementary Table 3.

Univariate meta-regression analysis results

Univariate meta-regression analysis showed that age, sex, education, panel composition of populations, and survey region were associated with variations in DW values of some health states (Supplementary Table 4).

Age

We found that for each 1% increase in the proportion of respondents aged above 49 years in the study population, the DW value of “dementia, severed” increased by 0.69% (R2 = 100%, P = 0.001), and that of “dementia, moderate” increased by 0.66% (R2 = 99.95%, P < 0.001), and that of “terminal phase of cancer, without medication” increased by 0.56% (R2 = 100%, P = 0.013). For each 1% increase in the proportion of persons aged above 49 years, the DW value of “intestinal nematode infections, symptomatic” and “lymphatic filariasis, symptomatic” decreased by 0.62% (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001) and 0.53% (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001), respectively.

Sex

For each 1% increase in the proportion of males in the study population, the DW value of “traumatic brain injury, long-term consequences, minor, with or without treatment” increased by 1.26% (R2 = 100%, P = 0.011). This was followed by “amputation of toe” and “stroke, long-term consequences, severe”, which increased by 0.87% (R2 = 75.327%, P = 0.017) and 0.86% (R2 = 90.40%, P = 0.014), respectively. For each 1% increase in the proportion of males, the DW value for “Drowning and non-fatal submersion, short or long term, with or without treatment” decreased by 2.19% (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001), followed by “end-stage renal disease, on dialysis” and “severe chest injury, short term, with or without treatment”; The DW values of them decreased by 1.92% (R2 = 98.54%, P < 0.001) and 1.72% (R2 = 100%, P = 0.006), respectively.

Education

For each 1% increase in the proportion of people with relatively high education (college and above) in the study population, the DW value of “intestinal nematode infections, symptomatic” increased by 0.75% (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001), followed by “lymphatic filariasis, symptomatic” and “disfigurement, level 1, with itch or pain”; The DW values of them increased by 0.63% (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001) and 0.60% (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001), respectively. For each 1% increase in the proportion of relatively high education, the DW value of gastric bleeding decreased by 1.21% (R2 = 76.987%, P = 0.004), followed by “amputation of one arm, long term, without treatment” and “terminal phase of cancer, with medication”. The DW values of them decreased by 0.80% (R2 = 100%, P = 0.005) and 0.73% (R2 = 99.603%, P < 0.001), respectively.

Panel composition of survey populations

When the panel included both health professionals and the general population (compared to the general population), the DW values of “severe alcohol use disorder” and “moderate and severe dementia” increased by 0.21 (R2 = 99.939%, P < 0.001) and 0.18 (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001), respectively. Conversely, the DW values of stoma and “fracture of patella, tibia or fibula, or ankle, long term, with or without treatment” decreased by 0.16 (R2 = 91.166%, P < 0.001) and 0.15 (R2 = 76.262%, P = 0.008), respectively.

Survey region

Compared to the Asian regions (including China and Japan), the DW value of severe alcohol use disorder increased by 0.27 (R2 = 83.220%, P = 0.009) in the GBD survey regions, followed by “AIDS, not receiving antiretroviral (ARV) treatment” and “traumatic brain injury, long-term consequences, severe, with or without treatment”, with an increase of 0.24 (R2 = 92.443%, P = 0.007) and 0.21 (R2 = 100%, P = 0.010), respectively. The DW values of “fracture of face bone, short or long term, with or without treatment” (R2 = 95.501%, P < 0.001), “epilepsy, less severe (seizures 1–11 per year)” (R2 = 100%, P = 0.005), and “fracture of patella, tibia or fibula, or ankle (short term, with or without treatment)” (R2 = 100%, P < 0.001) decreased by 0.15, 0.15, and 0.14, respectively.

Discussion

The set of summarized DW for 235 health states from the meta-analysis was highly correlated with the authoritative GBD 2013 DW study. These DW results in this study indicate that the overall summary DW values for these health states may be applicable to the calculation of disease burden indicators in global populations. DW is an important parameter for disease burden research. Obtaining accurate DW values can truly reflect the severity of the health states caused by diseases in the global populations, and can accurately quantify disease burden indicators between different countries or regions. However, previous DW studies discussed that considerable variation in DW estimates between national DW studies and the GBD 2013 DW study was related to cross-cultural or contextual differences [17–21]. This is because differences in cross-cultural or contextual backgrounds can influence how people perceive health issues and assess their impact on daily life. As reported, demographic and socioeconomic status may also impact people perceptions on assessing health status and their quality of life [22–28]. Further research is needed to further analyze the underlying reasons for these differences on DW estimates.

Through univariate meta-regression analysis, we found that age, sex, education level, panel composition of populations, and the survey region were associated with variations in the summarized DWs for some health states. Previous studies indicated age has impacts on health valuations [22, 25, 28]. The underlying reason may be that the older individuals on health preference place greater weight on functional limitations and social functioning, and less on morbidities and pain experience, compared to the younger individuals [22]. Educational levels may also have an impact on the DW valuation and result in greater attention to the population health equivalence question and care in weighing the responses to comparative health program comparisons of different functional outcomes [8, 26–28]. Profession was also suggested to be taken into account in survey populations of future DW studies [29, 30]. Specifically, the severity ranking of 24 health conditions by healthcare professionals differed from estimates from the general public [29]. Apart from the above factors, income level [14, 21] and disease experience [31] may be related to health valuation. A subnational DW study also indicated the DW differences across similar-cultural and cross-cultural regions [32]. Furthermore, this meta-analysis observed the GBD survey regions were associated with the magnitude of DW of some health states, compared to the Asian survey region. Compared with European countries, Juanita A Haagsma et al. also found differences that the highest DW of severe multiple sclerosis (DW = 0.677) was obviously lower than that of psychotic state of schizophrenia (DW = 0.778) in the GBD 2010 study [8]. Besides, this meta-analysis also found 73% of the 235 health states with more obvious heterogeneity (I2 >50%) between national DW surveys, and 27% of the states with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0). This may be related to that different regions having different true effects on DW valuation [33–35]. Therefore, this study suggests that future disability weight assessments should consider the above-mentioned factors to obtain more accurate DW results.

In summary, DW is the fundamental parameter for accurately estimating disease burden indicators such as DALYs, health life expectancy, and other disease burden indicators [36, 37]. In this way, the DALYs allow comparability between the impacts of diseases and provide insights into the magnitude of health problems and the potential benefit of proposed measures against other health problems [4]. The DW and DALYs approaches have been shown to be feasible and have been suggested for improving the methodology frameworks and their applicability [38, 39]. The most recent GBD study still relies on the DW survey data conducted in 2015, which mainly covers populations in high-level countries [6]. However, cultural differences in DW valuations across countriesmay lead to variations in disease burden estimates. Currently, DW survey data in various countries are relatively limited. Therefore, conducting further DW surveys and obtaining accurate DW values are important for accurately quantifying disease burden indicators. The GBD study also suggests that solving DW estimation problems or innovating DW methodologies in the future is an important issue. The latest outlook on the Chinese Burden of Disease study points out the need to continuously improve the methods and estimation results for the quantitative and comprehensive evaluation of disease burden indicators [39].

Limitations

There are some limitations. Firstly, few studies were included to pool the overall summary effect of these health states based on GBD 2013 DW study, as national DW study is limited worldwide. Secondly, when summarizing and analyzing literature, this meta-analysis only included studies using cutting-edge PC method from the GBD study and did not consider including studies using PHE method. The PHE method was excluded because it may have measurement errors and its methodological research is limited, warranting further exploration. Considering the limited data of DW studies at the national level, the number of studies included in this meta-analysis may need to be further expanded, and the results of this study should be interpreted and applied with caution by readers.

Conclusion

This study estimated the overall summary effect of the DW values from international authoritative surveys worldwide. A high correlation was observed between the summarized DW values from this meta-analysis and those from all included DW studies. The overall summary of DW estimates from disease and injury from this meta-analysis might be reliable and applicable. This study indicates that respondents’ sociodemographic characteristics may impact a population’s preference for health states, and suggests that these factors should be considered into future disability weight assessments.
