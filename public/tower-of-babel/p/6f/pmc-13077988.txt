‘Phase of Illness’ in oncology patients receiving specialist palliative home care: a mixed methods study on phase-specific burden, needs and use of resources
Authors: Daniela Bernhardsgrütter, Eleonore Baum, Larissa Gehrig, Ursula Klein, Andrea Neher, Stefan Nertinger, Stefan Ott, Ursula Ziltener, Andrea Kobleder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on ‘Phase of Illness’ in specialist palliative home care are not available in Switzerland. Our aim was to investigate oncology patients’ and family caregivers’ experiences of burden, needs and corresponding resource use within each ‘Phase of Illness’ and therefore to obtain an in-depth description of the characteristics of each ‘Phase of Illness’.MethodsConvergent parallel mixed methods design. Quantitative study strand (91 adult oncology patients and 54 family caregivers): medical/sociodemographic characteristics, phase-specific symptoms, burden, needs, use of resources. Qualitative study strand: semi-structured interviews with 8 patients and 12 family caregivers; focus group interviews with 20 nurses. Data analysis: factorial ANOVA, repeated-measurement ANOVA, qualitative content analysis, joint display tables.ResultsOur analysis revealed the following core themes and priorities for action of each ‘Phase of Illness’: “ambivalence” with the aim to “keep it under control” (stable), “anxiety and uncertainty” with the aim of “de-escalation” (unstable), “the tangible end of life” with the aim of “enduring” (deteriorating), “clarity” with the aim of “focusing on essentials” (terminal), and “finality” with the aim of “finding a conclusion” (bereavement).ConclusionsThe findings highlight the vulnerability and complexity of the burden and needs of oncology patients and their families receiving specialist palliative home care in all phases. Particularly in the stable and the bereavement phases, care concepts and funding principles within specialist palliative home care must be ensured to address the psychosocial vulnerability of patients and family caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02045-9.Keywords: Palliative care; Phase of Illness; Neoplasms; Home care services; Outcome assessment, health care; Patient reported outcome measures

Background

Casemix classifications categorize patients into homogeneous groups based on key variables (e.g., diagnoses) in terms of their needs and use of resources. In addition to supporting the health care reimbursement system, casemix classifications are a tool for mapping and communicating patients’ care needs [1]. Nevertheless, the prevailing diagnosis- and intervention-based casemix classifications are not appropriate to capture the holistic needs of palliative care patients. Moreover, they cannot predict the associated use of resources [2]. Therefore, the Australian Health Care System has established a casemix classification for subacute and non-acute health care. Palliative care is one of five case types included in this classification [3]. Variables for classifying patients within the palliative care case type are age, problem severity, functional status and ‘Phase of Illness’ [4]. ‘Phase of Illness’ includes five distinctive phases: ‘stable’, ‘unstable’, ‘deteriorating’, ‘terminal’, and ‘bereavement’. These do not rely on the stage of the disease and prognosis and can thus (except for the terminal and the bereavement phase) occur in any order. Instead, the main criteria for classification are care needs and the appropriateness of the care plan for meeting those needs [5]. Mather et al. [1] emphasize the value of ‘Phase of Illness’ as a tool to assess overall palliative care needs and to predict the corresponding use of resources.

The Australian casemix classification has become the basis for a sustainable collection of outcome data in palliative care. Continuous improvement in palliative care has been achieved by a combination of routine clinical outcome measures, benchmarking, feedback on collected and analyzed data, and quality improvement initiatives [6]. There are also ongoing efforts in Europe to develop, implement and establish palliative care casemix classifications and outcome measurements [7–9]. Although funding and service structures differ substantially within and between health systems, the use of standardized outcome measures is crucial for international comparison and collaboration [10]. It serves as a basis for developing a common language between different health care professions, institutions and countries [11]. Establishing this common language has been shown to improve clinical communication, streamline assessments, and enhance patient monitoring by providing a structured, consistent framework for evaluations and handovers [12].

The implementation of patient- or proxy-related outcome measurement is receiving increasing attention in Switzerland. However, ‘Phase of Illness’ has not been fully established in Switzerland, especially within specialist palliative home care. Previous projects have focused on inpatient specialist palliative care units [13, 14]. Furthermore, there is no data available in Switzerland on the burden, needs and use of resources of patients and family caregivers within each phase. Therefore, our aim was to investigate patients’ and family caregivers’ experiences of burden, needs and corresponding resource use within each ‘Phase of Illness’ and therefore to obtain an in-depth description of the characteristics of each ‘Phase of Illness’. We focused on oncology patients receiving specialist palliative home care and on their family caregivers. This focus was chosen because the participating specialist palliative home care services predominantly cared for oncology patients. Moreover, physical and psychosocial burdens differ significantly between patients with oncological and non-oncological diagnoses in palliative home care [15]. Therefore, we chose to focus on oncology patients only.

Methods

We chose a convergent parallel mixed methods design [16]. The quantitative part followed a cross-sectional survey design [17, 18]. The qualitative part had a qualitative descriptive design [19]. Both parts were considered equally important to gain a deeper understanding of the characteristics of each ‘Phase of Illness’. The mixed methods approach was deemed appropriate to address the complexity of the subject of interest and to reduce participant burden by tailoring data collection to the research question, ensuring relevance. Additionally, this approach is particularly suited to palliative care, where interventions are often holistic and individualized, requiring both depth and breadth of understanding [20, 21]. The Good Reporting of a Mixed Methods Study (GRAMMS) Checklist was applied [see Additional file 1].

The data collection took place between March 2020 and April 2022. Eligible were adult patients with a cancer diagnosis and their family caregivers. The patients received palliative home care by one of three specialist nursing teams located in two Swiss cities. All three teams operated under similar organizational conditions and consisted of specialist palliative care nurses. Depending on the complexity of the care situation, the teams collaborated with general practitioners (with palliative care training) or with palliative care physicians.

We applied a convenience sampling for quantitative data collection and a purposeful sampling (maximum variation) for qualitative data collection. Members of the specialist palliative care teams recruited the participants. They screened all current patients as well as newly admitted patients for the inclusion criteria (adult patients with cancer) and invited potential participants to participate in quantitative data collection. Patients scoring ≤ 20 on the Australia-modified Karnofsky Performance Status (AKPS) [22] were excluded from data collection, due to high vulnerability and burden. All participants provided written informed consent prior to data collection. The recruitment for the qualitative data collection was carried out in consultation with the research team. The selection of participants was consecutively conducted based on the insights gained from the previous analysis of the qualitative interviews. Qualitative data analysis was conducted while data collection was ongoing. We stopped qualitative data collection as we reached data saturation, meaning that no new themes emerged from the data [23].

We collected quantitative data once per participant by means of several paper-based patient- and proxy-related outcome measurement tools (Table 1). In the bereavement phase, we only collected family caregivers’ sociodemographic data and medical data of the deceased patient. Qualitative data collection occurred in semi-structured, individual qualitative interviews with patients and family caregivers and in three focus group interviews with the nursing staff of the involved specialist palliative home care services. The interview guides were developed for this study [see Additional file 2]. We pilot tested all questionnaires and the interview guide for individual interviews with patients and family caregivers.

A statistician conducted the quantitative analysis using IBM SPSS Statistics 28 software. Data was characterized according to their level of measurement by frequencies and appropriate parameters. Differences in independent subsamples (e.g., ‘Phase of Illness’) were analyzed by ANOVA with post-hoc tests (Bonferroni). In case of violated assumptions (e.g., variance homogeneity), Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied. For repeated-measurement analyses (e.g., differences in symptom assessment from different perspectives) repeated-measurement ANOVA was used. In case of violation of the assumption of sphericity (tested by Mauchly’s test), the Greenhouse-Geisser modification was applied. For all statistical tests the significance level was 5%.

Three authors (DB, EB, AK) conducted qualitative content analysis according to the principles mentioned by Schreier [30]. (1) The first author thoroughly reviewed the transcripts and highlighted key sections. Comments were added with ideas and observations. (2) Five main categories were deductively developed based on “Phase of Illness”. (3) Two authors (DB, EB) independently coded the transcripts section by section during the initial coding process, using the main categories. (4) A list was compiled that included all coded text segments corresponding to one of the main categories. (5) Subcategories were then developed inductively based on the data by two authors (DB, EB). They discussed all identified subcategories, consolidated them into broader categories, and formulated definitions for each of them. (6) All transcripts were re-coded within a second coding process by the first author. (7) A detailed analysis was carried out by three authors (DB, EB, AK) to explore and discuss the relationships between categories.

Three authors (DB, EB, AK) used joint displays [31] to merge qualitative and quantitative data. An excerpt of a joint display is provided in the additional files [see Additional file 3].

Phase-specific symptoms, needs and use of resources

We found statistically significant differences when comparing MIDOS scores, but not when comparing POS scores by patients, family caregivers and staff (repeated-measurement ANOVA, POS: F(1.82, 124.06) = 1.00, p = 0.37; MIDOS: F(1.62, 109.85) = 4.74, p = 0.016). However, the significant differences were considered clinically not relevant, as the average Mean Difference was below 0.3 – indicating no change in the response category. Therefore, we will only refer to the results of the staff versions of POS and MIDOS since the data regarding this perspective are most abundant. Across all phases, we found hardly any deterioration of family caregivers quality of life (Factorial ANOVA, CQOLC, F(3,83) = 1.12, p = 0.344). However, qualitative data revealed their burden in each phase. Figure 1 summarizes our main findings. Detailed quantitative findings are included in the additional files [see Additional file 4].

The stable phase: “Ambivalence”

The stable phase was described as a phase during which all involved parties aimed to keep everything under control. Symptom burden and unmet needs were significantly lower in the stable phase compared to any other phase (MIDOS S, Bonferroni: p < 0.001; POS S, Bonferroni: p < 0.001). Functional independence was significantly higher in comparison with the deteriorating and the terminal phase (Barthel, Bonferroni: p = 0.008 and p < 0.001). However, being confronted with the uncertainty of the further course of disease triggered tension, anxiety, despair and frustration in both patients and family caregivers. One patient described this as follows:

There are simply days when you can’t make excuses. […] You’re caught between two worlds. You feel like you are not with the living and you are not with the dead. (interview 9, patient, pos. 29)

On the other hand, patients and family caregivers experienced confidence, hope and optimism: The symptoms and the overall clinical situation were again under control and the care plan was appropriate to meet the patients and family caregivers needs. Patients and family caregivers expressed their need for “normality” and “being human”. For some of them this meant that they postponed conversations about the disease, death and dying. However, others expressed their need for advance care planning. According to the specialist palliative home care team, the absence of acute issues creates a valuable space for discussions, making the stable phase particularly suitable for building a trusting relationship and anticipating the transition to another phase. Nevertheless, patients, and especially family caregivers, had limited or no contact with the specialist palliative home care team during the stable phase since there were no acute practical or organizational problems. Compared to the unstable, deteriorating, or terminal phase, the nurses spent significantly less time on counseling (Resource Use Questionnaire, KLV-a, Bonferroni: p = 0.001, p = 0.002 and p = 0.019).

The unstable phase: “Anxiety and uncertainty”

Functional independence remained high in this phase (Barthel, M: 16.33, SD: 5.27). Although quantitative data showed that symptom burden and unmet needs did not differ significantly from the deteriorating or terminal phase, they were significantly more elevated than in the stable phase (MIDOS S, Bonferroni: p < 0.001; POS S, Bonferroni: p < 0.001). Qualitative data indicated that new and overwhelming symptoms were often a trigger of instability – associated with restlessness and intensive anxiety. Patients and family caregivers were confronted with the reality of the unfavorable prognosis (“mirror of reality”). This confrontation could result in existential suffering:

Sometimes, a sense of hopelessness comes up in these situations. Or the desire to die. People say: ‘It’s too much for everyone’ – due to this powerlessness. […] In this phase, spiritual questions come up – questions that were not important before. (focus group 2, pos. 83)

Family caregivers were confronted with overwhelming support needs and had to make many decisions:

I was told that [my mother] also had a bout of dementia. I can’t tell this to my father. He would be completely out of his mind if he found out … […] The support allowance for my father has been refused because his situation is not as bad as necessary. I’m in constant conflict with the authorities. There’s no help anywhere, no support, nothing. (…) I can’t go on like this. And I’m still doing everything for free. I’ve lost my job and have no income. (interview 2, family caregiver, pos. 16)

Actions were predominantly reactive rather than proactive – the primary goal in this phase was to de-escalate. Hospital stays frequently occurred as an “escape to safety” during this phase. Afterwards, however, some family caregivers recognized that the specialist palliative home care team would have been able to calm the situation, to provide security and to avoid hospital admissions. The total service time was higher than in the stable phase. However, the difference was not statistically significant. The counseling service time was significantly higher in this phase than in the stable phase (Resource Use Questionnaire, KLV-a, Bonferroni: p = 0.001). We found no differences regarding service time in comparison with the deteriorating or terminal phase.

The deteriorating phase: “the tangible end of life”

All persons involved were able to watch the “physical decay” of the patient in this phase. Even if the patients were able to revert to a stable phase, patients and family caregivers were made aware of the approaching end of life. Often various symptoms increased at the same time. Functional dependence, symptom burden and unmet needs did not significantly differ from the unstable or terminal phase. However, they were significantly higher than in the stable phase (MIDOS S, Bonferroni, p < 0.001; POS S, Bonferroni, p < 0.001; Barthel, Bonferroni: p = 0.008). Some patients or family caregivers were still unable to accept the reality of the approaching end, leading to a demand for further active medical treatments that were deemed inappropriate by health care professionals when burden was weighed against benefit. This situation was difficult to bear for those who recognized and accepted the deterioration. In contrast, situations calmed down when all persons involved were aware that “the inevitable (focus group 2, pos. 119)” was approaching. The team was able to address anticipatory grief and coming to terms with the end-of-life situation in discussions about advance care planning:

Sometimes there is a form of deterioration … Then clarity comes up – a clarity that is difficult to bear. You can only keep your ears open and look: What is the person’s priority now? And what is in the foreground for the relatives? And these are sometimes the difficult moments. We can perhaps only endure them [moments]and accompany and be there with them. (focus group 1, pos. 124)

In this phase, the needs of family caregivers – such as addressing administrative matters or initiating final clarifying discussions – sometimes outweighed those of the patients. Being alone with a very sick person without being able to help was difficult to endure and an overwhelming experience. In this phase, the team was on firm ground, which made it possible to keep the situation under control with small adjustments, such as providing education on the use of prescribed reserve medication. The total service time did not differ significantly from any other phase. However, the time spent on counseling services was significantly higher than in the stable phase (Resource Use Questionnaire, KLV-a, Bonferroni: p = 0.002).

The terminal phase: “clarity”

For all persons involved, it was clear that death was imminent in the terminal phase. At this stage, discussions focused more on the imminency of death and supporting the patient and family caregivers through this. It also became possible to address final unspoken matters. In contact with the dying person, caregivers focused on what seemed to be most important: being there and performing only interventions that were beneficial for the dying person:

In a certain sense, this is a beautiful phase. You are back on the road together and it is clear where you are going. The restlessness is gone. There is no longer anything you absolutely have to do. It is also a very important phase for the relatives – to experience this last time together. (focus group 1, pos. 146)

The last time we were really able to talk to each other was 24 h before she died. She said to me: I would like you to stay here overnight. Now I’m entering a phase that is scary for me and that I do not know. (interview 7, family caregiver, pos. 46)

The patients were often no longer conscious during this phase. Functional independence was lowest in the terminal phase, differing significantly from the stable and the unstable phase (Barthel, Bonferroni, p < 0.001 and p < 0.001). Symptom burden and unmet needs were significantly higher than in the stable phase (MIDOS S, Bonferroni: p < 0.001, POS S, Bonferroni: p < 0.001). However, they did not differ significantly from other phases. Some patients seemed calmer, while others were more restless and anxious. This affected the family caregivers and sometimes led to hospital admissions.

The terminal phase was characterized by an even more intense focus on family members caring for the patient. Some caregivers now realized how stressed and exhausted they had been for a very long time. They recognized the limits of their strength. Furthermore, they expressed their needs for specific information about the dying process and administrative aspects after the death of their relative.

The total service time was higher than in the stable, unstable, and deteriorating phase. However, the differences were not statistically significant.

The bereavement phase: “finality”

Immediately after the patient’s death, family members were preoccupied with various administrative tasks. Therefore, the absence of the deceased person initially moved to the background. However, it came to the fore as soon as the bereavement phase progressed. Family caregivers experienced loneliness and found themselves confronted with the loss in everyday life:

I am now a complete orphan, so to speak. I’ve never done my taxes. My mother always did them. […] Now I suddenly realize: I cannot make a phone call and ask my mother: ‘So, what do you think?’ (interview 18, family caregiver, pos. 89).

For some family caregivers, the final meeting with the specialist palliative home care team was an opportunity to discuss the experiences lying behind them. Others, however, wanted to draw a line under the events of the past weeks and months. They needed a “change of scenery” and did not want to reflect on the stressful period again. Caregivers also felt “worn out” and experienced signs of physical and mental exhaustion. In this context, the need for ongoing professional support became apparent:

[The physician said: ] ‘You need time to regain your strength and to recover. You are simply exhausted’. Always getting up in the night and […] getting there and back again in a hurry and so on. And you just function, don’t you? One day, nothing worked anymore. I was tired. […] I couldn’t have done anything but sleep all the time. I could have stood in a corner and slept. And all I did was sleep. I was tired. (interview 6, family caregiver, pos. 25)

Discussion

Our analysis revealed the following core themes and priorities for action of each ‘Phase of Illness’: “ambivalence” with the aim to “keep it under control” (stable), “anxiety and uncertainty” with the aim of “de-escalation” (unstable), “the tangible end of life” with the aim of “Enduring” (deteriorating), “clarity” with the aim of “focusing on essentials” (terminal), and “finality” with the aim of “finding a conclusion” (bereavement).

One of the most clinically relevant findings of our study is a pronounced and overlooked psychosocial vulnerability of patients and family caregivers in the stable phase. The concept of vulnerability in this context is closely linked to the idea of ambivalence. Both reflect the emotional and psychological complexity experienced by patients and family caregivers during the stable phase. Just as ambivalence captures the tension between conflicting emotions - such as hope and despair - vulnerability in this phase arises from an oscillation between the present and the future, between life and illness. The overlooked psychosocial vulnerability in this phase highlights the role of hope as valuable strategy for patients and relatives to manage conflicting emotions, making it essential to navigate the complex emotional landscape [32]. In their systematic review on hope in palliative care Velić et al. point out, that patients consider hope as an inherent part of life. As long as symptoms are more or less under control, they focus on enjoying the present moment while also maintaining personally significant roles. By focusing on hope, the aim is to strengthen the family bonds among those affected [32]. This might explain why both patients and relatives tend to avoid discussing psychosocially distressing symptoms, also with health care professionals. Nevertheless, addressing psychosocial as well as spiritual problems and needs requires open conversations between professionals, patients and family caregivers. In our study, nurses emphasized that the stable phase opened up a valuable space for discussions and relationship-building, as there were no acute issues at that time. However, we found that especially family caregivers were barely involved in specialist palliative home care services in the stable phase. A possible explanation from the professional perspective might be that the care plan may have focused too heavily on physical symptoms and needs of patients, thereby neglecting the potential needs of relatives or missing the opportunity to involve them early on. Instead, psychosocial and spiritual concerns should be integral to evaluating whether the care plan is appropriate or not [33].

As opposed to the ambivalent stable phase, the unstable phase was marked by severe anxiety and uncertainty. The hope once held onto gave way to hopelessness. This instability called for more intensive involvement from health professionals, including potential hospitalization to attempt regaining stability. The findings highlight the importance of stable phases in preparing for unstable ones, which were marked by reactive approaches and limited decision-making options under pressure.

During the deteriorating phase, more counselling was needed. Notably, relatives found it especially difficult to endure the approaching end of life, feeling helpless as bystanders. Linderholm and Friedrichsen found that a weak relationship with health professionals in palliative home care can increase relatives’ feelings of powerlessness [34]. Such emotions can also be accompanied by feeling guilty for not doing enough [35], which can foster demands for inappropriate interventions [36]. Laryionava et al. stated the important role of health professionals in taking on a family-oriented approach to limit expensive and burdensome futile treatments [37]. Moreover, treatments that are unlikely to benefit the patient can also increase moral distress among nurses [38].

Another interesting finding was that families were barely in contact with specialist palliative home care teams in the bereavement phase, suggesting that the lack of involvement in the stable phase carried over into this later stage. There are several possible explanations for this result. First, there was no official funding of specialist palliative home care services after the patient’s death. As a result, there was no standardized care provision available. Additionally, not all families desired ongoing support; some preferred closure and avoided further contact, while others experienced significant stress, leading to physical and mental breakdown. These findings highlight the varied nature of bereavement experiences, suggesting that palliative home care teams should consider more personalized, ongoing support and provide information about additional bereavement support services to better address the diverse needs of relatives during this phase [39].

Strengths and limitations

The mixed methods approach resulted in a comprehensive insight in the phase-specific burden of patients and family caregivers. However, a limitation of this approach was the complexity of integrating and interpreting data from multiple sources, especially when quantitative and qualitative findings were contradictory or divergent. To address this challenge, we used joint displays to visually compare qualitative and quantitative results, facilitating the identification of patterns and inconsistencies. Regular discussions among the research team, including both qualitative and quantitative experts, further enhanced the integration of insights and ensured a more holistic interpretation of the data.

By means of a one-time assessment, we wanted to relieve participants from additional stress. However, a longitudinal approach would have allowed a deeper understanding of transitions associated with ‘Phase of Illness’. As a prerequisite for a longitudinal approach, the used assessment tools must be embedded as a routine part of daily practice within specialist palliative home care. In that sense, a single system and measurement approach combining outcome measurements, clinical documentation, online assessment, visualization for team meetings, quality assurance, and data collection for research should be promoted [9].

In contrast to the recruitment for the interviews, recruitment for quantitative data collection proved to be challenging. Feedback from nurses indicated that the vulnerable situation of patients and family caregivers was a barrier to recruitment. These findings are in line with previous studies showing that the use of patient-reported outcome measures declined in the later, more intensive phases of care [12, 40]. Regarding MIDOS and POS, the perspectives of patients, family caregivers and nurses were consistent. This suggests that “Patient-centered Outcome Measures [41]” are a possible way of ensuring rich data in a field in which data collection can be very burdensome.

Finally, we chose the pragmatic approach of only including oncology patients, given that most patients of specialist palliative home care services have cancer. However, future research should consider other life limiting diseases for reasons of inclusivity.

Conclusions

The findings highlight the vulnerability and complexity of the burden and needs of oncology patients and their families receiving palliative home care in all phases. However, family members were rarely in contact with a specialist palliative home care team during the stable phase – even though some of them were highly involved in the caregiving situation. Even if the care plan is appropriate to address existing symptoms, problems, and needs, professionals have to be particularly attentive in this phase to identify changes in the physical and psychosocial condition of patients and family caregivers. For the bereavement phase, it is necessary not only to provide competencies and care concepts within specialist palliative home care, but also to establish funding principles to ensure ongoing support for family caregivers in need of support. The contact during the bereavement phase provides specialist palliative home care teams with the opportunity to identify the need for additional support services (e.g., grief cafés). Specialist palliative home care teams play a crucial gatekeeper role in this regard. Additionally, further research on phase-specific burden and needs of non-oncologic patients and their family caregivers is required.
