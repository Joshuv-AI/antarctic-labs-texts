Inflammatory markers and clinical outcomes in acute/subacute severe cerebral venous thrombosis: the role of glucocorticoid therapy
Authors: Xue Li, Shuyuan Hu, Qi Kong, Yiwei Zhang, Xunming Ji, Haiping Zhao, Jiangang Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) presents clinical challenges due to non-specific symptoms and limited biomarkers for assessing severity. Effective treatments for severe CVT are lacking. This study investigates inflammatory biomarkers linked to disease severity and evaluates glucocorticoids’ therapeutic potential in severe CVT.MethodsA prospective cohort study of 78 CVT patients assessed disease severity using neurological scores and analyzed inflammatory biomarkers, including hs-CRP, IL-6, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, and NLR. Changes in severity and biomarkers were evaluated before and after glucocorticoid treatment.ResultsSevere CVT patients had elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and neutrophil percentage, with reduced lymphocyte counts compared to non-severe cases. Neutrophil count, NLR, hs-CRP, and IL-6 positively correlated with neurological scores at admission, while lymphocyte percentage showed a negative correlation. Glucocorticoid treatment improved neurological function and reduced hs-CRP and IL-6 levels, suggesting anti-inflammatory benefits.ConclusionNeutrophils, hs-CRP, and IL-6 are promising biomarkers for CVT severity and treatment response. Glucocorticoids may improve outcomes by modulating inflammation, warranting further research into their mechanisms and long-term efficacy in CVT management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04475-z.

Introduction

Cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT), a rare yet increasingly recognized stroke subtype, has an estimated incidence of 0.1–0.2 cases per 100,000 individuals [1]. It is a leading cause of stroke in young and middle-aged adults, but its heterogeneous clinical presentation often leads to misdiagnosis, delaying treatment and worsening outcomes. Severe cases can have mortality rates up to 30% [2–4], highlighting the need for reliable biomarkers to assess disease severity and prognosis, enabling personalized therapeutic strategies.

CVT obstructs venous outflow, increasing intracranial pressure [5] and causing nonspecific symptoms like headache [6], which lack sensitivity and specificity for evaluating severity [7]. The clinical utility of invasive ICP monitoring is limited by the inherent limitations of the procedure itself, emphasizing the need for non-invasive biomarkers. CVT pathophysiology is closely tied to inflammation [8], with peripheral blood immune cells playing a critical role in thromboembolic disease severity and outcomes [8]. Neutrophils, key innate immune cells, release inflammatory proteins and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), disrupting coagulation and promoting thrombosis [9–11]. NETs are implicated in CVT thrombus formation [12], and elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) correlates with poor outcomes [13]. Eosinophils, known for their role in allergic diseases, also contribute to vascular pathology by promoting microemboli, atherosclerosis, and thrombosis [14–17]. However, the relationship between peripheral immune cell profiles and CVT severity remains underexplored.

Circulating inflammatory factors, central to immunothrombosis, are pivotal in CVT pathogenesis [18, 19]. While C-reactive protein (CRP) is linked to CVT, its prognostic value remains unclear. Interleukin-6 (IL-6), a key inflammatory mediator, drives acute-phase protein synthesis, including CRP, and is associated with poor outcomes in acute ischemic stroke and venous thrombosis [20–23]. IL-6 levels are elevated in acute and subacute CVT compared to chronic cases [18], yet its prognostic role in CVT requires further investigation. Understanding these inflammatory markers could refine risk assessment and optimize treatment.

Corticosteroids are commonly used to attenuate inflammatory responses, and have shown promise in CVT management. Case reports highlight their efficacy in thrombocytopenia-associated CVT when combined with anticoagulants and intravenous immunoglobulin [24]. Steroid pulse therapy with anticoagulation and rituximab has benefited CVT secondary to nephrotic syndrome [25], while glucocorticoids combined with anticoagulants and immunosuppressants are effective in systemic lupus erythematosus-related CVT [26]. Our prior studies suggest glucocorticoids may improve outcomes in CVT patients without immune disorders or hypercoagulable states [27]. This study aims to evaluate the association of peripheral immune cell profiles, CRP, and IL-6 with CVT severity and assess changes in neurological function and inflammatory biomarkers in severe CVT patients receiving glucocorticoid therapy.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This prospective study was approved by the ethics committee of Xuanwu Hospital([2020]098), and informed consent was obtained from all participating patients. This study included 78 patients diagnosed with acute or subacute CVT between August 2019 and July 2022. Inclusion Criteria: Newly diagnosed acute or subacute CVT confirmed by X-ray computed tomography (CT)/CT venography (CTV), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)/magnetic resonance venography (MRV), or digital subtraction angiography (DSA). MRI and magnetic resonance black-blood thrombus imaging (MRBTI) from 2018 to 2021. Patients of any gender, aged 14 to 70 years. CVT is diagnosed by experienced neurologists based on clinical manifestations (such as increased intracranial pressure resulting from focal brain injury caused by venous drainage obstruction or venous ischemia/infarction or hemorrhage) and confirmed by neuroimaging. Neuroimaging involves conventional T1 or T2 weighted sequences, contrast-enhanced MRI, and thrombus signals detected in the novel MRBTI [28–31]. Exclusion Criteria: Patients with severe systemic diseases within the past year with poor prognosis, such as acute cerebral infarction, unstable angina, or uncontrolled asthma; those with immune-related diseases, such as Behçet’s disease and systemic lupus erythematosus; patients who have undergone mechanical thrombectomy of the venous sinus; and those with absolute contraindications to corticosteroid use, including uncontrolled infections, severe diabetes, severe hypertension, and severe osteoporosis were excluded. Clinical data collection

Patients were recruited according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Basic clinical data from CVT patients, including age, gender, and laboratory parameters: triglycerides, homocysteine, antithrombin III, protein C, protein S, anticardiolipin antibodies, and anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies were collected. Also, document the history of ANA antibody positivity, hyperhomocysteinemia, autoimmune diseases, hematologic disorders, smoking, alcohol consumption, and deep vein thrombosis. Assess neurological symptoms, including papilledema, and collect National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [32, 33] and Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores [34, 35] at admission and discharge to evaluate neurological function. Measure intracranial pressure via initial lumbar puncture. Collect peripheral immune cell markers from venous blood at admission, including total neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, their respective percentages, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and eosinophil counts and percentages. Additionally, assess circulating inflammatory markers with ELISA, specifically plasma levels of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6).

Treatment protocol in patients with severe and non-severe CVT

The patients with CVT were classified into severe and non-severe ones. The severe CVT is defined as CVT with concomitant venous cerebral infarction/hemorrhage, seizures, impaired consciousness (GCS ≤ 9), or thrombosis of the straight sinus and deep cerebral veins [36, 37].

All patients with CVT received standard anticoagulant therapy upon admission. Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was administered for 7 to 14 days at the following dosages: 0.4 mL twice daily for patients weighing ≤ 50 kg, 0.6 mL twice daily for those weighing between 50 and 70 kg, and 0.8 mL twice daily for patients weighing > 70 kg. Subsequently, patients have transitioned to warfarin (with INR maintained between 2.0 and 3.0) or direct oral anticoagulants (dabigatran or rivaroxaban) for 3–12 months.

In severe CVT patients without contraindications, corticosteroid therapy was initiated. The medication regimen consisted of intravenous methylprednisolone 500 mg daily for 3 days, followed by a reduction to 80 mg daily for the next 3 days, and then 40 mg once daily for another 3 days. Then switched to oral methylprednisolone tablets: 20 mg once daily for 3 days, followed by 8 mg once daily for another 3 days, and then discontinued.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism Version 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) and IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were reported as mean ± SD, and non-normally distributed continuous and ordinal variables were expressed as median (IQR). Comparisons between severe and non-severe groups employed the independent samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Changes between pre- and post-corticosteroid treatment were tested by paired samples t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Pearson or Spearman bivariate correlations were employed to assess the linear relationships between various biomarkers and the severity and outcomes of CVT. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of baseline demographics and characteristics between severe and non-severe CVT patients

This study involved 78 patients with acute or subacute CVT. Of these, 52 were classified as severe CVT (median age 36.5 years; 33 female), while 26 were classified as non-severe CVT (median age 39.5 years; 15 female). Baseline demographics (age, gender) and disease characteristics (triglycerides, homocysteine, protein C, protein S, anticardiolipin antibodies, anti-β2-glycoprotein I antibodies) were comparable between the severe and non-severe CVT groups (Table 1). However, antithrombin III levels were significantly lower in the severe CVT group compared to the non-severe group (107.00 [89.75 to 116.25] vs. 94.00 [80.00 to 104.00]). The severe CVT group exhibited higher NIHSS scores (2.5 [0, 7.75] vs. 0 [0, 0], p < 0.0001) and mRS scores (1.5 [0, 3.75] vs. 0 [0, 0], p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A and B). Intracranial pressure did not significantly differ between severe and non-severe CVT groups (270 [175, 330] vs. 267.5 [182.5, 330], p = 0.1616) (Fig. 2C). However, both groups had elevated levels above the normal range (80–180 mmH₂O), suggesting that CVT is frequently associated with increased intracranial pressure.

The severe CVT group exhibited significantly higher levels of hs-CRP (14.41 [2.43, 27.24] vs. 4.87 [3.05, 10.91], p = 0.0040) and IL-6 (10.85 [6.02, 17.94] vs. 3.19 [1.18, 6.47], p = 0.0004) compared to the non-severe group (Fig. 2D and E), indicating a more pronounced inflammatory response in these patients. The severe CVT group exhibited a higher neutrophil percentage (72.95 [67.10, 80.00] vs. 68.90 [61.98, 73.70], p = 0.0368) and lower lymphocyte count (1.38 [1.04, 1.76] vs. 1.70 [1.33, 2.11], p = 0.0011) and percentage (19.75 [13.43, 24.03] vs. 24.65 [18.23, 29.30], p = 0.0236) compared to the non-severe group (Fig. 2F, G, and Fig. S1B). No significant differences were observed between the severe and non-severe CVT groups regarding neutrophil count and NLR (3.71 [2.75, 5.40] vs. 2.73 [2.08, 3.99], p = 0.0503) (Fig. 2H and Fig. S1A). Eosinophil count and percentage showed a decreasing trend in the severe CVT group (Fig. S1C, D).

Correlation of immune cell and inflammatory factor levels, and intracranial pressure with neurological deficits in severe CVT patients at admission

To identify clinical indicators associated with neurological injury severity in severe CVT patients, we analyzed correlations between admission metrics and neurological function, specifically NIHSS and mRS scores. Our findings revealed positive correlations between NIHSS scores and neutrophil count (r = 0.3374, p = 0.0145) (Fig. S2 B), neutrophil percentage (r = 0.3782, p = 0.0057) (Fig. 3A), NLR (r = 0.3718, p = 0.0085) (Fig. 3C), hs-CRP (r = 0.3178, p = 0.0261) (Fig. 3D), and IL-6 (r = 0.3457, p = 0.0186)(Fig. 3E). Conversely, lymphocyte percentage showed a negative correlation with NIHSS scores (r=−0.4034, p = 0.0037) (Fig. 3B). Neutrophil count (r = 0.3229, p = 0.0196) (Fig. S2B), neutrophil percentage (r = 0.3639, p = 0.0080) (Fig. 3F), NLR (r = 0.3562, p = 0.0120) (Fig. 3H), hs-CRP (r = 0.2888, p = 0.0442) (Fig. 3I), and IL-6 (r = 0.3445, p = 0.0191) (Fig. 3J) showed positive correlations with mRS scores at admission. In contrast, lymphocyte percentage (r=−0.2474, p = 0.0047) (Fig. 3G) was negatively correlated with mRS scores at admission. Intracranial pressure, lymphocyte count, eosinophil count, and eosinophil percentage at admission showed no association with either NIHSS or mRS scores (Fig. S2A, C-F, H-J).

Corticosteroid treatment significantly improves neurological functions and lowers inflammatory markers in severe CVT patients

At discharge, NIHSS scores improved (2 [0, 8] vs. 0 [0, 2], p = 0.0185), as did mRS scores (1 [0, 4] vs. 0 [0, 2], p = 0.0038) (Fig. 4A and B). Patients with severe CVT presented elevated intracranial pressure at onset, which corticosteroid treatment significantly reduced (230[160, 330] vs. 180 [155, 235], p = 0.0019), restoring it to normal levels (Fig. 4C).

Concurrently, lymphocyte count increased significantly (1.38 [1.02, 1.75] vs. 1.76 [1.14, 2.71], p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4G). Following corticosteroid treatment, inflammatory markers, including hs-CRP (20.65 [6.03, 32.58] vs. 1.72 [0.70, 8.57], p = 0.0003) and IL-6 (14.12 [7.68, 24.20] vs. 3.29 [1.58, 9.37], p = 0.0335), significantly decreased in peripheral blood (Fig. 4D and E). Although there was a slight increase in neutrophil count post-treatment (5.29 [4.32, 7.90] vs. 6.73 [4.24, 9.19], p = 0.0358; Fig. 4F), no significant changes were observed in neutrophil percentage (75.95 [66.63, 80.20] vs. 71.10 [63.40, 81.85], p = 0.7588), lymphocyte percentage (20.25 [13.28, 24.08] vs. 23.70 [13.70, 31.30], p = 0.6863), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) (3.65 [2.74, 5.93] vs. 2.90 [2.06, 5.96], p = 0.4486), eosinophil count (0.03 [0.01, 0.07] vs. 0.03 [0.01, 0.07], p = 0.8648), and eosinophil percentage (0.4 [0.1, 1.1] vs. 0.4 [0.1, 1.1], p = 0.4551) before and after treatment (Fig. 4H, S1E-H).

Discussion

This study reveals that severe CVT is characterized by significantly elevated neutrophil percentages, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels, alongside reduced lymphocyte counts and percentages. In severe cases, neutrophil metrics, hs-CRP, and IL-6 levels positively correlated with admission neurological function scores, while lymphocyte percentage showed a negative correlation. Importantly, corticosteroid treatment significantly improved neurological function and reduced intracranial pressure in severe CVT patients. These findings suggest that neutrophils, hs-CRP, and IL-6 exacerbate CVT progression, whereas glucocorticoids (GCs) mitigate inflammation by lowering hs-CRP and IL-6 levels, enhancing lymphocyte counts, and promoting functional recovery.

Inflammation is a central focus in stroke research, with neutrophils emerging as key players in CVT pathogenesis [12]. Elevated NLR levels are observed in CVT patients [38] and serve as independent predictors of poor prognosis [39], and correlates positively with NIHSS scores [18, 40], a finding corroborated by our analysis. Similarly, hs-CRP levels are significantly higher in acute/subacute CVT patients with venous infarction compared to those without, and they positively correlate with admission NIHSS scores [37]. Hs-CRP is also associated with seizure incidence, underscoring its potential as a clinical biomarker for CVT. Our study further demonstrates that hs-CRP and IL-6 levels are elevated in severe CVT and correlate with disease severity, supporting their utility as indicators of CVT severity and potential therapeutic targets for GCs.

While inflammatory markers at admission correlate with initial disease severity, they do not reliably predict post-treatment severity following GC therapy. This suggests that GCs alter the inflammation-severity relationship, supporting the hypothesis that inflammation mediates GC efficacy. This study demonstrates that corticosteroid treatment significantly enhances neurological function in severe CVT patients. In severe CVT patients, GC therapy significantly increased lymphocyte counts, reduced NLR, and markedly decreased hs-CRP and IL-6 levels, paralleling improvements in intracranial pressure and neurological function. These results indicate that GCs improve prognosis in severe CVT by attenuating inflammation, thereby diminishing the predictive value of pre-treatment inflammatory biomarkers for post-treatment outcomes.

Current CVT treatment guidelines prioritize intracranial pressure reduction, anticoagulation, and thrombolysis, with endovascular interventions reserved for select cases [41]. Our clinical experience and prior case series suggest that GCs, by modulating inflammation, may enhance outcomes in acute severe CVT, even in patients with hereditary protein C deficiency (PCD) or protein S deficiency (PSD) [42]. Consistent with case reports demonstrating GC efficacy in CVT patients with diverse complications [24–26], our study found that GC administration normalized intracranial pressure in both severe and non-severe CVT patients. In severe cases, GCs significantly increased lymphocyte counts, reduced hs-CRP and IL-6 levels, and improved neurological function scores. However, the study’s small sample size and lack of a non-GC control group limit its generalizability. Future large-scale prospective cohort studies and clinical trials are planned to validate these findings. In this cohort follow-up study, we only observed the clinical manifestations from hospital admission to discharge, which limited our ability to evaluate long-term safety and durability of efficacy—one of the limitations of this research. Additionally, while we found that steroid therapy improved neurological function in severe cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) cases, the underlying mechanisms remain unexplained and warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that elevated neutrophil counts, NLR, hs-CRP, and IL-6 levels, along with decreased lymphocyte counts, are associated with greater CVT severity at admission. In severe CVT patients, GC therapy significantly improved intracranial pressure and neurological function, accompanied by reductions in hs-CRP and IL-6 levels and increased lymphocyte counts. These findings suggest that GCs, through their anti-inflammatory effects, represent a promising therapeutic strategy for severe CVT. Further research is warranted to confirm these results and optimize GC-based treatment protocols.
