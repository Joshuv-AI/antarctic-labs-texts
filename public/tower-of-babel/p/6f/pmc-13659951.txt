Work-to-sleep ratio as a novel marker of NAFLD risk: evidence from U.S. and Korean national cohorts
Authors: Yanxuan Zhong, Yanqi Kou, Zhuoyan Lu, Yujie Huang, Wanyi Yin, Qing Zhang, Zebin Wang, Shengyi Li, Bangna Du, Xiaole Li, Enhua Wu, Shicai Ye, Yuping Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now redefined as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), affects 25–30% of adults globally and is driven by metabolic dysregulation. Concurrently, prolonged work hours and insufficient sleep are increasingly prevalent, yet their combined relationship with NAFLD risk remains unexplored. The Work-to-Sleep Ratio (WSR), quantifying the balance between occupational time and sleep recovery, may serve as a novel behavioral marker of NAFLD risk. Unlike work hours or sleep duration alone, WSR integrates these two interdependent behaviors into a single metric, capturing the balance between occupational demand and physiological recovery.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized nationally representative data from the U.S. NHANES (2017–2023; n = 3,935) and Korean KNHANES (2019–2020 and 2022–2023; n = 10,729) cohorts. Multivariable logistic regression models with sequential covariate adjustment were employed to examine WSR-NAFLD associations. Restricted cubic spline analyses explored nonlinear relationships, while mediation analyses examined the potential involvement of adiposity (BMI), inflammation (CRP), and dyslipidemia (NHHR). Subgroup analyses assessed effect modification by demographic and clinical factors.ResultsWSR exhibited nonlinear associations with NAFLD risk in both cohorts. In NHANES, NAFLD risk increased with rising WSR and attenuated at higher levels. In contrast, in KNHANES, higher WSR was associated with progressively increased NAFLD risk that plateaued at elevated ratios. Associations were stronger and more consistent in KNHANES (fully adjusted OR per unit increase in WSR 1.57, 95% CI 1.38–1.78). Subgroup analyses revealed stronger associations in younger adults and metabolically vulnerable groups. Mediation analyses identified adiposity (BMI) as accounting for the largest proportion of the observed WSR-NAFLD association, with secondary roles for inflammation (CRP) and dyslipidemia (NHHR).ConclusionWSR may serve as a useful behavior-based marker associated with NAFLD in nationally representative U.S. and Korean cohorts, with associations most evident among younger adults. Adiposity appeared to play a central role in this association, with additional contributions from inflammation and dyslipidemia. These findings highlight WSR as a simple time-based indicator for identifying populations at elevated NAFLD risk. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm these associations and clarify temporal relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12986-026-01162-9.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), recently redefined as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), represents a substantial global health burden, affecting approximately 25–30% of adults worldwide [1]. It is intrinsically linked to metabolic dysfunction, with its pathogenesis strongly driven by obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and chronic low-grade inflammation [2, 3]. Beyond progressive liver injury, NAFLD/MASLD is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disorder that elevates the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [3, 4]. Although several behavioral and metabolic determinants are well-established [5–7], further investigation is needed to identify other modifiable behavioral drivers.

In parallel with these metabolic insights, global trends reveal a concerning misalignment between occupational demands and sleep health. According to the International Labour Organization, approximately 9% of the global workforce currently exceeds 55 working hours per week—a threshold strongly associated with heightened cardiometabolic risk [8]. Concurrently, population-based studies report that 20–30% of adults routinely experience insufficient sleep, obtaining less than 6 h per night, which has been independently linked to adverse metabolic outcomes including NAFLD [6, 9]. Critically, these factors frequently coexist: prolonged work schedules often encroach upon sleep opportunity, creating a state of chronic “metabolic wakefulness” that disrupts circadian rhythmicity and metabolic homeostasis. Experimental evidence demonstrates that even brief periods of sleep restriction elevate hepatic triglyceride accumulation [10], while extended working hours may amplify psychosocial stress and proinflammatory adipokine release, further exacerbating insulin resistance [11]. Despite these parallel findings, it remains unclear whether the balance between occupational burden and sleep recovery, quantified as an integrated ratio, is associated with NAFLD beyond the consideration of working hours or sleep duration alone. Importantly, work hours and sleep duration are not independent behaviors but are intrinsically linked within a finite 24-hour time budget. Individuals with similar working hours may experience substantially different metabolic burdens depending on the amount of compensatory sleep obtained. Therefore, modeling these exposures as a ratio may better reflect the relative imbalance between occupational demand and physiological recovery than considering either behavior in isolation.

To integrate these dual behavioral dimensions, we operationalized the Work-to-Sleep Ratio (WSR), calculated as the quotient of total weekly working hours divided by total weekly sleep hours. This composite metric synergistically captures the relative imbalance between occupational time commitment and compensatory sleep restriction within a single, quantifiable index, thereby reflecting the interaction between behavioral demand and recovery capacity rather than treating work and sleep as fully independent exposures. A recent cross-sectional investigation utilizing NHANES 2017–2023 data demonstrated a significant positive association between elevated WSR and obesity, supporting the potential utility of ratio-based behavioral indicators as markers of metabolic dysregulation [12]. Despite this preliminary evidence, the relationship between WSR and NAFLD risk, along with the potential mechanistic pathways underlying this association, remains entirely unexplored and constitutes a critical knowledge gap in the field.

Therefore, to address this critical gap, we conducted a comprehensive cross-sectional analysis leveraging two large, nationally representative cohorts: the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from the United States and the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (KNHANES). By utilizing the Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) derived from vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) to define hepatic steatosis in NHANES and employing the validated Hepatic Steatosis Index (HSI) in KNHANES, we aimed to determine the association between WSR and NAFLD prevalence across these ethnically and culturally distinct populations. Furthermore, we performed advanced mediation analyses to elucidate the potential mechanistic roles of adiposity (assessed by BMI), systemic inflammation (CRP), and atherogenic dyslipidemia (NHHR) in the WSR-NAFLD relationship. This integrative approach seeks to characterize WSR as a novel, behavior-based marker associated with NAFLD risk, thereby providing epidemiological insight to support early risk stratification and identification of populations warranting closer metabolic evaluation. It is noteworthy that the operational definitions of hepatic steatosis employed in this study align with the traditional NAFLD criteria, which effectively identify hepatic steatosis but do not incorporate the specific cardiometabolic risk criteria required for the formal diagnosis of MASLD. Therefore, to maintain conceptual and operational consistency throughout our analysis, we use the term NAFLD in this study.

Methods

Datasets and study population

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis utilizing data from two independent, nationally representative surveys: the KNHANES and the U.S. NHANES. Both surveys employ a stratified, multistage, clustered probability sampling design to obtain representative samples of the non-institutionalized civilian populations in their respective countries [13]. KNHANES is administered by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (KDCA), and NHANES by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). These surveys provide comprehensive data on sociodemographics, health behaviors, dietary intake, physical examinations, and laboratory measurements. All procedures were approved by the respective institutional review boards, and all participants provided written informed consent. Publicly available de-identified data were used for this analysis.

Data from the NHANES 2017–2023 cycles were utilized for this analysis. This period is critical as it includes the cycles where VCTE, providing non-invasive NAFLD assessment via CAP, was introduced. Although data collection operations were impacted by the COVID-19 pandemic, NHANES resumed fieldwork in 2021. The NCHS has rigorously evaluated and documented the representativeness and data quality for these survey cycles, supporting their use in nationally representative epidemiological studies [14]. Furthermore, these specific data have been successfully employed in prior peer-reviewed research [15, 16], affirming their validity.

The initial NHANES cohort comprised 27,493 participants. Based on the study objectives, we applied the following exclusion criteria: participants under 16 years of age, those with missing VCTE data, individuals with missing hepatitis B (HBV) or hepatitis C (HCV) serology data, those with excessive alcohol consumption (men > 30 g/day; women > 20 g/day), and those with missing key variables related to work hours or sleep duration. After these exclusions, a total of 3,935 participants were included in the final analysis.

We further analyzed data from the KNHANES. As key sleep-related variables were unavailable in other cycles spanning 2017–2023, we specifically restricted our sample to the 2019–2020 and 2022–2023 survey cycles, which together included 28,863 participants. We applied the following exclusion criteria: individuals younger than 20 years, those with missing information on work or sleep variables, those lacking essential biochemical or physical examination data, those with missing HBV or HCV status, and those with excessive alcohol consumption (men > 14 standard drinks/week; women > 10 standard drinks/week). After these exclusions, 10,729 participants were included in the final analytic sample. Additional details regarding the KNHANES survey design, variable collection, and quality assurance are available in the official documentation provided by the KDCA and in prior methodological reports [17] (Fig. 1).

Age and alcohol inclusion criteria differed between NHANES and KNHANES due to specific design and data availability. Given earlier workforce entry in the United States, broader age inclusion was applied in NHANES to maximize sample size and to reduce dilution by nonworking populations. Notably, employment participation among adolescents is substantially higher in the United States, with an employment rate of approximately 36.9% among individuals aged 16 to19 years [18], compared with about 6.6% among those aged 15 to19 years in Korea [19]. In addition, alcohol exposure in NHANES was defined using dietary recall data, as standard drink variables were available only for participants aged 18 years or older. These specific criteria for different cohorts were applied to preserve internal validity within each survey.

Work and sleep variables

Weekly working hours were assessed based on participants’ responses to the question, “Number of hours worked in the last week”, representing total weekly working hours. Sleep duration was assessed using participants’ self-reported responses to the questions regarding the “number of hours usually slept on weekdays/workdays” and “number of hours usually slept on weekends/non-workdays”. Weekly sleep duration was calculated as: (average weekday sleep hours × 5) + (average weekend sleep hours × 2). The WSR was then computed as follows: WSR = weekly working hours / weekly sleep duration. To quantify compensatory sleep patterns, Weekend sleep compensation was defined as the difference between average weekend sleep hours and average weekday sleep hours (hours/day). It was calculated as: weekend sleep compensation = average weekend sleep hours − average weekday sleep hours.

Hepatic assessment

Transient elastography (TE) has become the preferred noninvasive diagnostic modality in most hepatology clinics worldwide [20]. In this study, hepatic steatosis assessment differed between cohorts. In the NHANES cohort, we utilized the CAP obtained through VCTE to define NAFLD, with a CAP value > 274 dB/m indicating hepatic steatosis [16]. For the KNHANES cohort, where liver ultrasound or elastography data were unavailable, we employed the validated HSI, calculated as: HSI = 8 alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) ratio + body mass index (BMI) + 2 if diabetes + 2 if female [21], with HSI > 36 defining NAFLD [22, 23]. Both assessments were applied in the absence of excessive alcohol consumption and other known liver diseases. Although NAFLD definitions differed between cohorts due to data availability, both methods are validated approaches for identifying hepatic steatosis in population-based studies. While the exclusion criteria were harmonized as rigorously as possible between the two cohorts, direct comparability of the effect estimates should be interpreted with caution.

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on established associations with NAFLD risk from prior literature. We considered demographic factors (age, sex), socioeconomic factors (education, marital status, household income), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, energy intake, physical activity), and clinical measures (BMI, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, cancer history, ALT, AST, CRP, and the non-HDL to HDL cholesterol ratio [NHHR], calculated as [total cholesterol - HDL cholesterol]/HDL cholesterol). Due to inherent differences in survey design between NHANES and KNHANES, variable definitions were harmonized for cross-cohort comparability where possible. Specifically, race/ethnicity was included only in NHANES analyses, while income was treated continuously (PIR) in NHANES but categorized into quartiles in KNHANES. Education was dichotomized (above high school vs. high school or below) in NHANES but classified into four categories in KNHANES. Marital status was recorded in detail (married/cohabitating, widowed/divorced/separated, never married) in NHANES but simplified as married or unmarried in KNHANES. Cancer history was not included as a covariate in KNHANES analyses because it was not recorded or publicly available in the selected survey cycles. With respect to physical activity, measures differed between NHANES and KNHANES owing to survey design. To ensure cross-cycle consistency, NHANES analyses used leisure-time moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (LTMVPA) minutes per week, as later cycles collected only leisure-time data. In KNHANES, MVPA reflected total activity across occupational, transportation, and leisure domains. Finally, we operationally defined a composite cardiovascular disease (CVD) variable for NHANES participants as having at least one self-reported physician diagnosis of congestive heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina/angina pectoris, heart attack, or stroke. This comprehensive CVD definition could not be similarly applied to KNHANES due to more limited cardiovascular disease ascertainment.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are reported as means with standard deviations (Mean ± SD) and categorical variables as unweighted counts with percentages (n (%)). For variables with a small proportion of missing values, multiple imputation was performed using the “mice” package with random forest imputation (five imputations, 10 iterations), incorporating survey weights as predictors to preserve the complex sampling structure. Group comparisons for continuous variables employed design-based Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests, while categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s chi-square tests with Rao & Scott adjustment to account for complex survey design. In accordance with their respective official analytic guidelines, all analyses for both NHANES and KNHANES incorporated appropriate sample weights and accounted for the complex, multistage sampling design of each survey.

The relationship between WSR and NAFLD was assessed using multivariable logistic regression models with three sequential adjustment levels: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for basic demographics (age, sex, and race/ethnicity where available) and socioeconomic factors (education category, marital status, economic status); Model 3 further adjusted for lifestyle and behavioral factors (smoking status, energy intake, LTMVPA or MVPA per week) and clinical history (diabetes, cancer where available, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, or summarized as cardiovascular disease). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were used to examine potential non-linear dose-response relationships between continuous WSR and NAFLD risk. To facilitate clinical interpretation, we converted the spline-identified WSR turning point into hour-based benchmarks. For each cohort, we defined a narrow window around the threshold (NHANES: 0.73 ± Δ; KNHANES: 1.5 ± Δ) and used survey-weighted means to estimate the corresponding weekly work and sleep hours. The half width (Δ) was initialized at 0 and increased in increments of 0.005 until either an effective sample size of 50 was reached or a maximum half width of 0.10 was attained. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed to assess effect modification by key demographic and clinical factors. Mediation analysis was conducted to quantify the proportion of the association mediated through BMI, NHHR, and CRP. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the stability of spline-identified inflection regions. Segmented regression was applied to estimate change points in the association of WSR and NAFLD using the “segmented” package in R. Analyses were performed in the full sample and repeated after excluding the lowest and highest 2.5% of WSR values to assess the influence of extreme observations. All statistical tests were two-sided with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant, and analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1).

Results

Baseline characteristics stratified by NAFLD status

Among 3,935 participants in NHANES, those with NAFLD (n = 1,726) were older, more often male (60.1% vs. 50.5%), and more likely to be Mexican American, married/cohabiting, and to have diabetes or cardiovascular disease (all P < 0.001). They exhibited markedly higher BMI, CRP, NHHR, and CAP values (324 vs. 219 dB/m), along with higher daily energy intake and lower physical activity compared with non-NAFLD individuals. Sleep duration was modestly shorter on both weekdays and weekends, leading to a slightly higher WSR (0.76 vs. 0.73, P = 0.005) (Table 1).

In the KNHANES sample (n = 10,729), participants with NAFLD (n = 2,629) were more often male (65% vs. 47%) and showed substantially higher BMI (28.4 vs. 22.6 kg/m²). They had greater prevalence of diabetes (14% vs. 4.6%), hypertension (25% vs. 13%) and hyperlipidemia (24% vs. 14%) (all P < 0.001). NAFLD participants also displayed elevated CRP (1.47 vs. 1.06 mg/L), NHHR (3.35 vs. 2.53), and HSI (40.7 vs. 30.3). Behaviorally, they reported longer weekly work hours (40 vs. 36 h) and shorter weekday sleep duration (6.54 vs. 6.70 h), resulting in a higher WSR (0.86 vs. 0.77, P < 0.001) (Table S1).

Nonlinear association between WSR and NAFLD

RCS analyses revealed significant nonlinear relationships between the WSR and NAFLD in both cohorts (all P for nonlinearity < 0.001) (Fig. 2). In NHANES (Fig. 2A), the odds of NAFLD increased steeply with rising WSR, peaking at approximately 0.73, and then declined gradually thereafter. By contrast, in KNHANES (Fig. 2B), the association followed a different pattern: NAFLD risk rose progressively with higher WSR values and reached a plateau beyond a ratio of around 1.5. In the corresponding weighted windows, these WSR values corresponded to 40.6 ± 0.29 work hours vs. 55.6 ± 0.38 sleep hours per week in NHANES and 60.4 ± 1.51 work hours vs. 40.3 ± 1.00 sleep hours per week in KNHANES. These results indicate that although WSR is related to NAFLD in both populations, the shape and inflection point of the nonlinear relationship differ between the U.S. and Korean cohorts. Notably, the apparent attenuation at higher WSR levels was observed mainly in the extreme range of the distribution, where sample sizes were limited.

Association between WSR and NAFLD

In NHANES, higher WSR was associated with NAFLD in unadjusted models (Model 1: OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.10–1.86, P = 0.009), but the association attenuated after adjustment (Model 2: OR 1.23, 95% CI 0.92–1.64, P = 0.2; Model 3: OR 1.18, 95% CI 0.87–1.59, P = 0.3). Compared with Q1, odds were higher in Q2 and Q3 (fully adjusted OR 1.36, 95% CI 1.02–1.83, P = 0.039; OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.14–2.20, P = 0.008), but not in Q4 (OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.80–1.46, P = 0.6). Additional analyses based on RCS showed that within the range of WSR ≤ 0.73, each unit increase in WSR was associated with a substantially higher odds (OR 3.13, 95% CI 1.38–7.11, P = 0.007), whereas the association was attenuated at higher WSR levels beyond 0.73 (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis

As shown in Fig. 3, subgroup analyses in NHANES showed that the positive association between WSR and NAFLD was particularly evident among younger adults (< 40 years), non-Hispanic Whites, and participants without cancer or cardiovascular disease, while it was less pronounced in older groups and those with established comorbidities (P for interaction by age = 0.046). When analyses were stratified according to RCS ranges (Fig. 4), WSR ≤ 0.73 was associated with higher NAFLD odds, especially among women, non-Hispanic Whites, and individuals with normal glucose status or prediabetes, whereas no significant associations were observed when WSR ≥ 0.73. In KNHANES, the association between higher WSR and NAFLD was robust across most subgroups (Fig. 5), but was stronger in younger individuals, men, and those with higher education levels, while attenuated in older adults and those with hypertension (P for interaction < 0.05 for age, sex, education, and hypertension). Associations were also more consistent among participants with adequate sleep (> 6.5 h/day) or partial weekend sleep compensation. Collectively, these findings indicate that the association between elevated WSR and NAFLD prevalence is more pronounced in metabolically vulnerable and socio-demographically distinct populations, suggesting that the detrimental impact of high WSR may be amplified in these groups.

Mediation analysis

Mediation analyses suggested that BMI accounted for the largest proportion of the association between WSR and NAFLD. In NHANES (Fig. 6A), BMI explained the largest proportion of the association (78.1%; mediation effect = 0.2361, P < 0.001), while CRP and NHHR accounted for smaller proportions (31.4% and 25.3%, respectively). In KNHANES (Fig. 6B), mediation analyses were restricted to NHHR. BMI was not evaluated as a mediator because it is a component of the HSI, which defines NAFLD in this cohort, raising concerns of outcome circularity. CRP mediation was not examined due to substantial missing data and unstable estimates. NHHR mediated 18.8% of the association between WSR and NAFLD. These findings indicate that adiposity is the dominant factor statistically associated with both WSR and NAFLD, whereas inflammatory and lipid-related markers contribute to a lesser extent.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the stability of the spline-identified WSR inflection regions. Using segmented regression, the estimated change point in NHANES was 0.81 (95% CI 0.65–0.97) in the full sample and shifted to 0.82 (95% CI 0.71–0.93) after excluding the lowest and highest 2.5% of WSR values. In KNHANES, corresponding estimates were 1.25 (95% CI 1.00–1.50) in the full sample and 1.31 (95% CI 0.98–1.63) after exclusion of extreme values. While modest shifts in estimated change points were observed, the overall nonlinear association pattern was preserved, supporting the robustness of the observed trend.

Discussion

This study is the first to systematically examine the association between WSR and NAFLD in two nationally representative cohorts from the United States (NHANES) and South Korea (KNHANES). In both cohorts, RCS analyses demonstrated nonlinear associations between WSR and NAFLD, but with distinct inflection ranges. In NHANES, NAFLD odds increased at lower WSR values and were highest around 0.73, followed by attenuation at higher levels, whereas in KNHANES, NAFLD odds increased progressively and appeared to stabilize beyond approximately 1.5. These patterns were most evident among younger adults. Mediation analyses further suggested that body mass index accounted for the largest proportion of the observed associations.

Global data indicate that NAFLD now affects about 30–32% of adults worldwide and is increasingly prevalent where obesity and metabolic syndrome are endemic [24]. NAFLD substantially increases the risk of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus, with meta-analyses reporting roughly a two-fold higher incidence among people with NAFLD (pooled RR ≈ 2.17, 95% CI 1.77–2.65) compared to those without it [25]. Cardiovascular disease risk is also elevated in NAFLD, independent of traditional risk factors [26]. Despite these serious public health implications, most prior work has examined single risk markers such as obesity, insulin resistance, or dyslipidemia, rather than integrated behavioral metrics. Because work hours and sleep duration are both measurable and modifiable, WSR provides a compact way to describe how these behaviors coexist within a finite time budget and may help identify patterns that warrant closer metabolic attention. This concept was recently introduced in obesity research and has been linked to metabolic outcomes in population-based studies [12].

Our findings extend and refine previous work on how work or sleep exposures relate to NAFLD risk. A large Korean cohort study of 79,048 adults reported that working over 60 h/week was associated with higher hazard ratios for incident NAFLD (HR 1.13, 95% CI 1.05–1.23) compared to those working 35–40 h/week, even after accounting for confounders [22]. In KNHANES data, Song et al. observed that working 53–83 h/week increased odds of NAFLD (OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.02–1.50, P = 0.033) relative to 36–42 h/week, and this association held across subgroups defined by work schedule or occupation type [27]. Our study complements these findings by examining working hours and sleep duration jointly as a composite ratio and demonstrating a nonlinear association between this integrated measure and NAFLD across populations. Although both work hours and sleep duration were individually associated with NAFLD, considering these behaviors separately may not fully reflect how they coexist under real-world conditions, where time allocated to one behavior inevitably constrains the other. In this setting, WSR summarizes the relative distribution of occupational demand and sleep recovery within a finite time framework. Consequently, individuals with comparable work duration but different sleep patterns, or similar sleep duration but different work demands, may exhibit distinct WSR profiles. This integrated perspective may offer additional insight into behavioral imbalance beyond evaluating either exposure alone. This finding is similar in spirit to recent work by Zhou et al., who demonstrated an inverted L-shaped nonlinear association between WSR and obesity in NHANES (OR 1.54, 95% CI 1.33–1.77, P < 0.001) when WSR exceeds certain levels.

The observed association between higher WSR and NAFLD may be interpreted in the context of synergistic metabolic perturbations reported in previous studies. Short-term sleep restriction studies in humans demonstrate that reduced sleep rapidly elevates circulating free fatty acids (FFAs), inducing peripheral and hepatic insulin resistance via lipotoxicity [28] while upregulating de novo lipogenesis, a process mirrored in animal models by increased hepatic expression of lipogenic enzymes such as lipin 1 and perilipin and by triglyceride accumulation [10]. Concomitant adipose tissue expansion amplifies this cascade by releasing pro-inflammatory adipokines (such as TNF-α and IL-6) and suppressing adiponectin, further impairing insulin signaling and promoting steatosis [29, 30]. Critically, sleep loss disrupts circadian regulation of lipid oxidation and mitochondrial function, reducing lipid clearance and increasing oxidative stress [31]. Our mediation results align with this framework: BMI (reflecting adipose-driven inflammation and lipotoxicity) accounted for > 78% of the WSR-NAFLD association, while CRP (systemic inflammation) and NHHR (atherogenic dyslipidemia) represented downstream effectors. Taken together, these findings suggest that adiposity may represent a central correlate within a broader metabolic profile linking work–sleep imbalance with higher NAFLD prevalence, accompanied by insulin resistance, inflammation, and dyslipidemia. Notably, this mechanistic framework primarily supports a monotonic adverse effect of increasing WSR. Therefore, the apparent attenuation of NAFLD risk at higher WSR levels observed in NHANES is unlikely to reflect a true biological protective effect and may instead be attributable to selection bias or residual confounding.

Subgroup analyses across NHANES and KNHANES consistently identified younger adults (< 40 years) as a group with a particularly strong association between elevated WSR and NAFLD. The association was also robust among individuals with normal BMI, a finding that was more pronounced in the NHANES cohort. This pattern may suggest that work-sleep imbalance contributes to NAFLD through pathways both dependent on and independent of adiposity [32]. Mediation analyses suggested that adiposity accounted for a substantial proportion of the association in NHANES, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously and warrants further investigation.

While the association was generally observed across most demographic strata, certain variations were noted. For instance, in KNHANES, the association appeared more pronounced among highly educated individuals, whereas previous studies have suggested that lower educational attainment is associated with an increased risk of NAFLD [33]. One interpretation is that the impact of WSR could be amplified in specific socioeconomic or occupational contexts, such as high-pressure professional environments, although this remains a hypothesis to be tested in future studies. Overall, the consistency of the age-specific effect across two distinct populations underscores WSR as a notable behavioral risk marker, particularly for younger, working-age adults.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. In particular, reverse causation is possible, as NAFLD and related metabolic conditions may themselves influence work capacity, fatigue, and sleep behaviors, thereby affecting the observed work sleep patterns and WSR. WSR was derived from reported work and sleep duration, which may be affected by recall or reporting bias. Because WSR is calculated as a ratio, measurement error may be amplified, particularly at extreme values, warranting cautious interpretation of inflection regions. In addition, WSR reflects average weekly duration but does not capture sleep quality or intra-week variability in work and sleep schedules, which may also be relevant to metabolic health.

Although two nationally representative cohorts were analyzed, the findings may not be generalizable to populations with different labor structures or sleep patterns. Mediation analyses were conducted using cross-sectional data and regression-based models, which assume a temporal ordering that cannot be confirmed in this design and may be susceptible to confounding related to time; additionally, BMI was used as the primary adiposity marker and may not fully capture central adiposity or visceral fat distribution relevant to metabolic dysfunction and NAFLD risk. Moreover, NAFLD was defined using different approaches across cohorts, with CAP in NHANES and the HSI in KNHANES, which may limit direct comparability of effect estimates between populations. In addition, the study used traditional NAFLD-based definitions rather than formal MASLD criteria, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. As HSI incorporates metabolic components that overlap with covariates included in the models, the KNHANES analyses primarily inform the robustness and directionality of the observed associations across populations, whereas effect size estimates should be understood with appropriate caution.

Conclusion

This study indicates that WSR may serve as a useful behavioral marker associated with NAFLD. Elevated WSR was consistently linked to a higher prevalence of NAFLD in nationally representative cohorts from the United States and Korea, with associations most evident among younger adults. Mediation analyses suggested that adiposity plays a central role in this association, with additional contributions from systemic inflammation and dyslipidemia. Collectively, these findings support the relevance of WSR as a simple, time-based indicator associated with NAFLD risk. WSR may help identify populations with a less favorable work-sleep profile who warrant closer evaluation. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to validate these associations, clarify temporal relationships, and determine whether modifications in work and sleep patterns translate into meaningful reductions in NAFLD risk.
