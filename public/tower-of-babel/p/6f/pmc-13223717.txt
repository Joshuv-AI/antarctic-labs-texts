Integrative multi-omics and network analysis identify potential targets of Chaihu Shugan Xiaozheng Formula in breast cancer

Introduction

Despite advances in oncology, 2024 statistics from the American Cancer Society reveal breast cancer (BC) remains the most diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States [1]. Parallel data from the China CDC indicate breast cancer ranks second in incidence among Chinese females, with its mortality position rising from sixth in 2005 to fifth in recent years [2,3]. These epidemiological patterns underscore persistent limitations in current tumor-centric therapeutic paradigms rooted in modern biomedical approaches. BC patients who share identical histological subtypes, molecular classifications, and clinical stages can still experience divergent outcomes [4,5]. Despite significant advances in targeted therapies and immunotherapies, substantial inter-patient heterogeneity in treatment response persists, highlighting the limitations of single-target interventions. This heterogeneity stems not only from tumor-intrinsic genomic variations but also from host systemic factors, including neuroendocrine and immune dysregulation. Currently, early detection and postoperative surveillance of BC rely primarily on serum biomarkers CA153 and CEA. Prospective cohort studies have shown that CA153 has a sensitivity of only 23–60% and a specificity of 80–90%, whereas CEA demonstrates even lower sensitivity (<50%) and yields significant false-positive results in benign liver diseases and among smokers [6]. Neither marker can reliably distinguish indolent lesions from aggressive phenotypes nor predict the efficacy of neoadjuvant chemotherapy or endocrine therapy, thereby limiting their clinical utility.

Contemporary tumor biology emphasizes the dynamic interplay between neoplastic microenvironments and host systemic factors, where host determinants profoundly influence malignant progression [7]. This paradigm shift necessitates holistic therapeutic strategies addressing both tumor biology and host physiology for comprehensive disease management. Modern societal challenges, including accelerated living/work rhythms and hypercompetitive environments, have exacerbated psychological stress exposure [8]. Chronic emotional stress induces persistent neuroendocrine dysregulation, elevating risks for various pathologies, including cancer.

Clinical evidence demonstrates a 2–3 fold higher prevalence of emotional distress among breast cancer patients compared to the general population [9,10]. Such psychopathological states adversely impact therapeutic responses, accelerate disease progression, and diminish quality of life. Depression emerges as the predominant psychological comorbidity, epidemiologically associated with a 30% increased breast cancer risk [11]. Mechanistically, chronic rather than acute stress exposure shows significant correlation with both primary incidence and recurrence rates [12]. Notably, depression independently predicts adverse outcomes, demonstrating 1.4–1.9-fold increases in all-cause mortality, cancer-specific mortality, and recurrence risks [13].

From a pharmacological perspective, the inadequacy of conventional "one-target, one-disease" approaches becomes apparent when addressing complex pathophysiological networks involving neuroendocrine-immune crosstalk. The polypharmacological strategy of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), particularly exemplified by Chaihu Shugan Xiaozheng Formula (CSXF), offers a promising alternative paradigm. CSXF, derived from the classical Chaihu Shugan San decoction, incorporates pharmacologically synergistic botanical drugs including Bupleuri Radix, Curcumae Radix, Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium Viride, Citri Reticulatae Pericarpium, Cyperi Rhizoma, Corydalis Rhizoma, Toosendan Fructus, Paeoniae Radix Alba, Poria, Sargassum, Curcumae Rhizoma, Leonuri Herba, represents a sophisticated multi-metabolite system with potential synergistic effects across multiple molecular targets. Pharmacokinetic studies suggest that key CSXF metabolites, such as paeoniflorin (from Paeoniae Radix Alba) and curcumin (from Curcumae Rhizoma), exhibit favorable bioavailability profiles and tissue distribution, with particular accumulation in mammary tissues. These bioactive compounds collectively target critical pathways involved in inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune regulation, positioning CSXF as a potential multi-target therapeutic agent. The botanical metabolites of CSXF collectively address these key viscera and channels, conferring a systemic regulatory effect that aligns with the pathogenesis of BC.

The therapeutic potential of CSXF in breast cancer was investigated through a multi-omics framework. Bioactive compounds of CSXF and their targets were systematically identified, followed by an intersection with breast cancer-related DEGs. Prognostic genes were screened through Cox proportional hazards regression and LASSO algorithms, with subsequent functional enrichment analysis performed to characterize associated biological pathways and immune signatures. A robust prognostic model was constructed and validated using these biomarkers. Mechanistic insights were obtained through integrated analyses of the tumor immune microenvironment, regulatory network construction, and molecular docking simulations, revealing CSXF-mediated modulation of immune cell infiltration and critical signaling pathways. Differential expression patterns of biomarkers were experimentally confirmed in clinical specimens via RT-qPCR validation. By integrating traditional Chinese medicine principles with modern precision oncology strategies, a scientific foundation is established for advancing the clinical translation of CSXF in breast cancer management.

This study aims to bridge TCM principles with modern pharmacological frameworks by systematically identifying CSXF's bioactive compounds and their molecular targets, constructing and validating a prognostic model based on these CSXF-targeted genes, elucidating the pharmacological mechanisms underlying CSXF's modulation of neuroendocrine-immune networks in breast cancer, and validating these key findings through molecular docking and experimental approaches. Through this focus on the multi-target pharmacological actions of CSXF, the work advances our understanding of how complex herbal formulations can simultaneously engage multiple nodes within disease-associated biological networks.

Materials and methods

Data preparation

The TCGA database (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/) provided the transcriptome data, clinical information, and survival information of breast cancer (BC) (accessed on June 18, 2024). Specifically, the TCGA-BC dataset contains 1104 BCE tumors and 113 control tissue samples. Of these, 1082 BCE samples contained survival information. Meanwhile, the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) provided GSE42568 dataset. The transcriptome data of 104 BCE breast tissue samples and 17 control breast tissue samples were included in GSE42568 (platform: GPL570).

Differential expression analysis

The differential analysis (P < 0.05, |log2FC| > 0.5) was undertaken through the DESeq2 package (v 1.34.0) [14] to determine the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between BC and control samples in the TCGA-BC dataset. The results of differential expression analysis were then visualized using ggplot2 (v 3.3.5) [15] and heat map (v 1.0.12) [16], respectively, to create a volcano plot and heat map.

Prediction of active metabolite targets

The active metabolites of each of the botanical drugs in the CSXF were acquired from the TCMSP (https://tcmspw.com/tsps) (OB ≥ 30% and DL ≥ 0.18). After extracting and deduplicating the Mol IDs of these active metabolites, a total of 73 compounds were obtained. The target genes were then obtained by performing gene conversion on the target proteins that corresponded to the active metabolites using the UniProt database (http://www.UniProt.org/). After extracting and deduplicating the Target names from the converted target genes, a total of 142 targets were identified. The Cytoscape software (v 3.7.2) [17] was applied to view the drug-target gene network that was created.

Determination and enrichment analyses of candidate genes

Overlapping genes of DEGs and target genes of active metabolites of Chaihu Shugan Xiaozheng Formula (CSXF) as candidate genes for subsequent studies. The Venn diagram was drawn with the VennDiagram package (v 1.7.1) [18]. Subsequently, GO and KEGG analyses (P < 0.05) were undertaken through the clusterProfiler package (v 4.6.0) [19] to explore the biological functions and signalling pathways involved in the candidate genes.

Determination of prognostic genes

The univariate Cox regression analysis (P < 0.2, HR ≠ 1) was undertaken through the survival package (v 3.3.1) [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survival] in TCGA-BC to identify genes related to the prognosis of BC. A forest plot was made to visualize the results through the forestplot package (v 2.0.1) [20]. The cox.zph function was then applied to conduct the pH assumption test (P > 0.05) threshold. Next, using glmnet package (v 4.1.2) [21], the LASSO was undertaken with 10-fold cross-validation. When lambda was at its lowest, and the gene coefficients were not zero, the prognostic genes were identified.

Creation and verification of a risk model

The prognostic genes obtained above were employed to construct a risk model.

The 'coef' stands for the coefficients of the prognostic genes in LASSO analysis, and 'expression' represents their expression levels. Based on the risk score's ideal cutoff value, BC patients from TCGA-BC (1082 samples) were divided into 2 groups: the high-risk group (HRG) and the low-risk group (LRG). The stratification was performed using the “surv_cutpoint” function from the survminer package (v0.4.9). Specifically, risk scores for each patient were calculated as the sum of expression levels of key genes (PGR, CD14, PPARG, CHRM1, HTR3A) screened by LASSO regression multiplied by their corresponding coefficients (riskScore_out = sum of gene expression × coefficient). The “surv_cutpoint” function was then applied with overall survival time (OS.time) and survival status (OS) as dependent variables, risk score as the independent variable, and a minimum sample proportion constraint (minprop=0.1, ensuring each group had ≥10% of total samples). The optimal cut-off value (2.651651) was automatically determined by maximizing the log-rank test statistic for survival differences between HRG and LRG. Patients with risk scores > 2.651651 were assigned to HRG, and those with scores ≤2.651651 to LRG, which maximally distinguished patient groups with different prognostic outcomes and ensured clinical and statistical validity of stratification. Meanwhile, the risk curve and survival status plots were created for HRG and LRG with survminer package (v 0.4.9) [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survminer]. The Log-rank test (P < 0.05) was then applied to compare the overall survival (OS) of BC patients in the HRG and LRG. The K-M curve was created through the survminer package (v 0.4.9). Furthermore, the ROC curves at 1, 2, and 3 years were displayed using the survivalROC package (v 1.0.3) [https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=survivalROC]. A similar study was conducted in GSE42568 to validate the risk model.

Independent prognostic analysis and construction of the nomogram

In TCGA-BC, the percentages of different clinical indicator subgroups (age, gender, race, T/N/M stage) in HRG and LRG were explored separately. Meanwhile, the Wilcoxon test was used to evaluate the differences in risk scores across subgroups of various clinical markers (P < 0.05). Afterwards, a prognostic model for predicting OS in BC patients was constructed by combining the risk scores with the 6 clinical indicators. Then, univariate Cox regression analyses (P < 0.05), pH assumption test (P > 0.05), and multivariate Cox regression analyses (P < 0.05) were performed on these 7 variables to identify independent prognostic factors. Subsequently, the rms package (v 6.2.0) [22] was applied to construct a nomogram for independent prognostic factors, and calibration curves were plotted to evaluate the nomogram's prediction accuracy. Furthermore, the ROC curve was created at 1, 2, and 3 years through the timeROC package (v 0.4) (https://cran.r-project.org/package=timeROC). Clinical phenotype data (age, TNM stage, survival info, riskScore) were cleaned (missing values removed, TNM converted to categorical variables) via dplyr (v1.1.4). A baseline Cox model (age + TNM) and an extended Cox model (adding riskScore) were built using survival (v3.7.0). Models were compared via AIC/likelihood ratio test; C-index (discrimination) was calculated (openxlsx, v4.2.5) and visualized (ggplot2, v3.5.2). Decision curve analysis (DCA) for clinical utility was done with rmda (v1.6). All analyses used TCGA BRCA data.

Functional enrichment analysis of prognostic genes

Using the DESeq2 package (v 1.34.0), between HRG and LRG, the differential expression analysis in TCGA-BC was conducted. Log2FC values were computed and arranged in ascending order of magnitude. The MSigDB provided the background gene set c2.kegg.v7.4.symbols (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/). Based on this background gene set, GSEA was then performed using the ClusterProfiler package (v 4.6.0) (P < 0.05, FDR < 0.25, |NES| > 1). Next, the gene-gene interaction (GGI) network was established through GeneMANIA (http://www.genemania.org/) to understand the relationships between prognostic genes and their functionally related genes. The Cytoscape package (v 3.7.2) was used to visualize the GGI network. Moreover, the localization of prognostic genes on chromosomes was explored by RCircos package (v 1.2.2) [23]. The subcellular localization of prognostic genes was examined using the UniProt database (http://www.UniProt.org/) and the HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/).

Immune-related analysis

To understand the immune microenvironment of BC patients, the ESTIMATE algorithm was employed to assess stromal score, immune score, tumour purity, as well as ESTIMATE score of BC samples in TCGA-BC. Then, the above metrics were compared between HRG and LRG by Wilcoxon test, respectively (P < 0.05). The 28 immune cells' infiltration abundance was then evaluated through the immunedeconv software (v 2.1.0) [24,25], quantified with the single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) algorithm (an extension of GSEA) [26], which calculates enrichment scores of immune cell signature gene sets for individual samples (without group information) by assessing gene expression rankings, suitable for bulk RNA-seq data. Analyses were performed with R package GSVA (v1.50.0, core enrichment score calculation), supported by ggplot2 (v3.5.2, visualization), pheatmap (v1.0.12, immune infiltration correlation heatmaps), rstatix (v0.7.2, differential testing of infiltration scores), corrplot (v0.92, immune cell correlation analysis), and estimate (v1.0.13, stromal/immune score and tumor purity calculation for immune microenvironment validation) [26,27]. The differences in infiltration abundance of 28 immune cells between HRG and LRG were examined through the Wilcoxon test. Then, Spearman correlation analyses were carried out between the prognostic genes and the risk score. In addition, Spearman correlations between differential immune cells and between differential immune cells and prognostic genes were also identified (|cor| > 0.3, P < 0.05).

Regulatory network analysis

The miRDB database (http://www.mirdb.org/) and Targetbase database (https://targetbase.com/) were applied to predict miRNAs1 and miRNAs2 targeting prognostic genes, respectively. Intersecting miRNAs of miRNAs1 and miRNAs2 were used as key miRNAs. Then, lncRNAs1 and lncRNAs2 targeting significant miRNAs were predicted using the Starbase database (http://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) and miRNet database (https://www.mirnet.ca/), respectively. As important lncRNAs, lncRNAs near the intersection of lncRNAs 1 and 2 were employed. The key miRNA-mRNA-lncRNA regulatory network was established and visualized by Cytoscape software (v 3.7.2).

Molecular docking

The small molecule metabolites with the greatest OB values, which correlate to the medications in the CSXF, were molecularly docked to the prognostic gene receptor proteins. Specifically, the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) provided the small molecule metabolites' 3D structures. Prognostic genes' protein 3D structures were discovered using the PDB database (https://www.wwpdb.org/). After that, PyMOL software (v 4.10.1) [28] was used for molecular docking of prognostic genes and small molecule metabolites.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) on clinical samples

RT‑qPCR was performed to verify the expression levels of candidate biomarkers, and a total of 10 frozen tissue samples (5 breast cancer, BC, tissue samples and 5 paired adjacent normal control tissue samples) from Shenzhen Hospital (Futian) of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine were included in this experiment; 5 biological replicates were set for each group, 3 technical replicates were carried out for each individual sample, and the mean Ct values of the 3 technical replicates were used for subsequent analysis. All patients gave their approved consent, and the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Hospital (Futian) of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine had approved the trial (Ethical approval number: GZYLL(KY)-2025–017, from 2025–03–16 to 2026–03–16). The BC tissue samples covered subtypes including Luminal A subtype (n = 2), Luminal B subtype (n = 1), HER2-overexpressing subtype (n = 1), and triple-negative breast cancer (n = 1). The control group tissues were paired adjacent normal tissues (not tissues from healthy individuals). First, the total RNA was extracted from the ten frozen samples using the TRIzol reagent (Cat. No 15,596,026, Invitrogen, USA). The NanoPhotometer® N50 (Implen, Munich, Germany) was then used to detect the concentration and purity of RNA. To create cDNA, an equivalent quantity of mRNA was then reverse transcribed using PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Cat. No RR047A, Takara, Japan). GAPDH was selected as the internal reference gene in this experiment to normalize the expression levels of target biomarkers, and the primer sequence of GAPDH was also listed in Supplementary Table 1 along with the primer sequences for the target genes. After that, 40 cycles of the RT-qPCR reaction were undertaken on CFX Connect Real-Time Quantitative PCR Detection System (Catalog No XLFZ006, BIO-RAD, USA), with the reaction system and procedure strictly following the kit instructions. Gene relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method. Experimental data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) and statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 5 software. The independent-sample t-test was applied to compare gene expression levels between the BC group and the Control group, and a P-value of < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for intergroup comparisons, while parametric Student's t-test (two-tailed) was applied to RT-qPCR datasets meeting normality assumptions as verified by Shapiro-Wilk testing. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 following Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

Results

Identification of candidate genes

A total of 37 therapeutic targets of Chaihu Shugan Xiaozheng Formula (CSXF) were systematically identified through the TCMSP and UniProt databases (Fig. 1A). Comparative transcriptomic analysis of 1082 TCGA-BC samples (tumor vs. adjacent normal tissues) revealed 9572 DEGs, including 5933 upregulated and 3639 downregulated genes. The top 10 most significantly dysregulated genes were annotated in the volcano plot (Fig. 1B) and visualized via hierarchical clustering (Fig. 1C). Intersection analysis identified 12 overlapping genes between CSXF targets and BC-associated DEGs, which were designated as candidate genes for subsequent analyses (Fig. 1D).

Functional enrichment analysis of candidate pathways

GO analysis demonstrated enrichment of the 12 candidate genes in 617 functional terms (510 BPs, 40 CCs, as well as 67 MFs), including intracellular receptor signaling pathway, cholinergic synapse (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Table 2). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant associations with 29 pathways, notably the estrogen signaling pathway (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 3). These findings suggest CSXF may exert therapeutic effects through modulation of hormone-responsive and immune-related pathways.

LASSO-based prognostic gene selection

Univariate Cox regression identified 8 genes significantly associated with overall survival (Fig. 3A) (HR ≠ 1, P < 0.2), all satisfying the proportional hazards assumption (Supplementary Table 4). LASSO regression analysis was further performed and 5 prognostic genes (PGR, CD14, PPARG, CHRM1, and HTR3A) were identified with a lambda.min of 0.008508188 and the regression coefficients of the genes were not penalised to 0 (Fig. 3B and C).

Prognostic gene selection.(A) Univariate Cox regression of 12 candidate genes (8 retained with P < 0.2). (B-C) LASSO coefficient profiles (B) and cross-validation curve (C) identifying 5 optimal prognostic genes (PGR, CD14, PPARG, CHRM1, HTR3A) at lambda.min = 0.0085.

Development and validation of risk stratification model

The risk score was calculated as: risk score = −0.04137313 × PGR + 0.03218436 × CD14 - 0.09693229 × PPARG + 0.03053907 × CHRM1 - 0.23795430 × HTR3A. Among the coefficients of these 5 prognostic genes, HTR3A (a core signaling receptor of the neuroendocrine-immune axis) has the largest absolute weight. HRG (n = 827) and LRG (n = 255) were generated in 1082 TCGA-BC samples using the optimal risk score cutoff value (−0.161911). Meanwhile, 104 BCE samples were divided into HRG (n = 43) and LRG (n = 61) in GSE42568 using the ideal risk score cutoff value (−0.8343453). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis demonstrated significantly poorer outcomes in HRG across both cohorts (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4A-B). HRG patients had shorter survival durations and greater mortality rates, according to the risk curves and survival status plots (Fig. 4C-D). Time-dependent ROC analysis confirmed robust predictive accuracy with 1,2, and3-year AUCs greater than 0.6 both in TCGA and GSE42568 (Fig. 4E-F). In the TCGA-BRCA cohort, the 1‑, 2‑ and 3‑year AUC values were 0.68, 0.64 and 0.61, respectively, showing a slight drop in prognostic discrimination over time; the C‑index was 0.616. In the GSE42568 cohort, the 1‑year AUC was 0.80, 2‑year 0.77 and 3‑year 0.65, with a gradual decline in prognostic performance and a C‑index of 0.703.The incorporation of these prognostic genes into the risk model demonstrated robust predictive accuracy in stratifying BC patients into distinct risk subgroups, thereby offering independent prognostic value for clinical decision-making.

Clinically integrated prognostic nomogram

Both HRG and LRG groups were predominantly composed of patients aged ≤60 years, female, White ethnicity, and early-stage tumors (T2N0M0) (Supplementary Figure 1). Significant intergroup differences in risk scores were observed across T-stage categories and ethnic groups (Supplementary Figure 2).

Univariate Cox regression incorporating six clinical covariates demonstrated significant prognostic associations between the prognosis of BC and the risk score, age, and TNM stage (Fig. 5A). Subsequent multivariate analysis confirmed the independent predictive value of four factors: risk score, age, N stage, and M stage, all satisfying proportional hazards assumptions (Fig. 5B, Supplementary Table 5). Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that after adjusting for clinical confounding factors (including age and TNM stage), the risk score remained an independent predictor of overall survival (HR=12.18, 95%CI:3.03–48.93, P < 0.05).

Prognostic nomogram construction.

(C) Nomogram integrating risk score, age, and TNM stage. (D) Calibration curve comparing predicted vs. observed survival probabilities. (E) ROC curves demonstrating AUC >0.7 for 1/3/5-year survival prediction. (F) C-index comparison of different prognostic models in TCGA-BRCA data. (G) Decision curve analysis (DCA) of different prognostic models.

The integrative prognostic nomogram was constructed by incorporating the risk score, age, and TNM stage (Fig. 5C). Calibration analysis demonstrated excellent concordance between predicted and observed 3-year survival probabilities (Fig. 5D). Time-dependent ROC analysis further validated the model's discriminative capacity with AUC values all above 0.7 (Fig. 5E). Compared with the traditional clinical model incorporating only TNM stage and age, the nomogram (Fig. 5C) integrated with the CSXF risk score exhibited significantly improved area under the time-dependent ROC curve (AUC) when predicting 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival rates (Fig. 5E). This demonstrates the incremental prognostic information provided by our model. The predictive accuracy (measured by C-index) of different prognostic models in TCGA-BRCA data (Fig. 5F) shows: the baseline clinical model (age + TNM stage) had a C-index of 0.782, while adding the risk score elevated the C-index to 0.791. The significant likelihood ratio test result (P < 0.05) confirms that the risk score notably improves the baseline model’s accuracy, indicating its additional prognostic value. The clinical net benefit comparison between the baseline clinical model (age + TNM) and the risk score-integrated model (Fig. 5G) reveals: across most threshold probability ranges (especially 0–0.2), the integrated model shows higher net benefit. This demonstrates that the risk score-integrated model offers superior clinical decision-making value compared to the baseline clinical model alone.

Functional characterization of prognostic biomarkers

Gene set enrichment analysis revealed distinct pathway activation patterns between risk subgroups. HRG tumors exhibited significant enrichment in energy metabolism pathways. Conversely, LRG samples showed preferential activation of sensory transduction pathways (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Table 6). GGI network comprising 20 functionally linked nodes (including LBP and ESR1) demonstrated connectivity to lipopolysaccharide response and reproductive behavior regulation (Fig. 6B). Chromosomal mapping identified spatial clustering of HTR3A, PGR, and CHRM1 on chromosome 11, while CD14 and PPARG localized to chromosomes 5 and 3 respectively (Fig. 6C). PGR was mainly distributed in the nucleus and cytoplasm, CD14 was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm and Golgi apparatus, PPARG was mainly distributed in the nucleus and cytoplasm, and CHRM1 and HTR3A were mainly distributed in the postsynaptic cell membrane (Fig. 6D-H).

Distinct immune microenvironment profiles between risk subgroups

The stromal score was significantly higher in LRG (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A-D). And the infiltration abundance of 28 immune cells in HRG and LRG was demonstrated in Fig. 7E.

Immune microenvironment analysis.

(A-D) Stromal/immune/ESTIMATE scores in HRG vs. LRG (Mann-Whitney U test, *P < 0.05). (E) Heatmap of 28 immune cell infiltration levels. (F) Differential infiltration of 12 immune subsets (*P < 0.05). (G) Spearman correlation network of immune cells. (H) Correlation heatmap between prognostic genes and immune infiltrates.

Distinct immune infiltration patterns were observed between HRG and LRG, with 12 immune cell subtypes exhibiting significant intergroup disparities. For example, CD56bright natural killer (NK) cells had higher infiltration abundance in HRG and plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) had higher infiltration abundance in LRG. Notably, HRG demonstrated elevated infiltration of CD56bright natural killer (NK) cells alongside diminished plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) compared to LRG (Fig. 7F), suggesting differential antigen presentation capacity. Subsequent analysis revealed coordinated immune network dynamics: 1) NK cells showed the highest positive correlations with effector memory CD4 T cells (cor=0.62) and MDSCs (cor=0.62); 2) The strongest negative connection (cor = −0.31) was found between Th2 cells and CD56dim NK cells (Fig. 7G, Supplementary Table 7), implicating Th2-mediated immunosuppression in NK functional impairment; 3) The CD14+ monocyte-MDSC axis (cor=0.70) and monocyte-driven IL-10/TGF-β cascade (cor=0.52) emerged as key immunosuppressive pathways (Fig. 7H). These multi-dimensional correlations substantiate that prognostic genes shape BC immunogenicity via orchestrating lymphocyte-myeloid cell crosstalk and metabolic reprogramming of tumor-associated macrophages.

Molecular docking analysis demonstrated robust binding affinities between the prognostic gene products and therapeutic metabolites:CD14-paeoniflorin: ΔG = −7.5 kcal/mol via LEU-168-mediated bonding (Fig. 8B), CHRM1-patchouli alcohol: ΔG = −9.1 kcal/mol through ASN-382 coordination (Fig. 8C), HTR3A-hyndarin: ΔG = −6.5 kcal/mol involving ARG-444 interactions (Fig. 8D), PGR-curcumol: ΔG = −8.6 kcal/mol via ASN-719 contact (Fig. 8E), PPARG-dihydrosanguinarine: ΔG = −9.8 kcal/mol through ASN-719 stacking (Fig. 8F). Notably, all CSXF metabolites bound to the functional core domains of the target proteins, confirming the specificity of CSXF’s targeting of these prognostic genes.

The expression levels of CD14, PPARG, and CHRM1 were obviously different in BC and control samples (P < 0.05), according to the results of an analysis of prognostic gene expression in the clinical samples. Quantitative analysis of RT-qPCR data revealed that, in breast cancer (BC) samples relative to control samples, PGR exhibited a 0.649-fold expression change with a non-significant difference (p = 0.0599); CD14 showed a 0.459-fold reduction with a highly significant difference (p = 0.0002); PPARG displayed a 0.480-fold downregulation with a significant difference (p = 0.0012); CHRM1 presented a 0.515-fold decrease with a significant difference (p = 0.0034); and HTR3A had a negligible 1.026-fold expression alteration with no statistical significance (p = 0.1223). All relevant data are summarized in Supplementary Table 8. Notably, control samples exhibit significantly higher expression levels of CD14, PPARG, and CHRM1 (Fig. 8G).

Discussion

Psychological burden and biological susceptibility interact synergistically, markedly increasing BC incidence and aggressiveness. To address these host factors, TCM offers a unique strategy for BC prevention and treatment [29]. CSXF may down-regulate stress-responsive cytokines, restore immune equilibrium, and remodel the tumor microenvironment, thereby reducing BC risk and impeding metastatic spread. The distinctive value of TCM in oncology lies in its holistic approach to restoring patients’ systemic homeostasis, prioritizing ecological balance over direct tumoricidal effects. Clinical evidence indicates that chronic emotional stress disrupts immune surveillance and growth factor equilibrium, thereby fostering protumorigenic niches that exacerbate cancer initiation and progression [30].

Despite the clinical promise of CSXF as a multi-target therapeutic candidate, its molecular mechanisms in breast cancer remain underexplored. This study integrates transcriptomic profiling and Network Analysis to identify five prognosis-associated genes mechanistically linked to CSXF efficacy. Rigorous bioinformatics validation revealed novel pathways and immune interactions underlying breast cancer pathogenesis. From a pharmacological perspective, the five core targets represent distinct yet complementary therapeutic axes.

The identified prognostic genes orchestrate multifaceted mechanisms in breast cancer pathogenesis through distinct yet interconnected pathways. PGR (Progesterone Receptor), a nuclear hormone regulator, demonstrates tumor-suppressive activity by antagonizing estrogen-driven oncogenic signaling, consistent with its established role in hormonal carcinogenesis [31]. On one hand, it induces a shift in the hormone regulation mode of mammary tissue from merocrine to autocrine secretion, while also increasing the proportion of ER/PR-positive and Ki67-positive proliferating cells, thereby facilitating the progression of mammary tissue from hyperplasia to malignant tumors. Meanwhile, BRCA1 indirectly participates in the development of hormone receptor-negative breast cancer by inhibiting its transcriptional activity and accelerating its degradation. On the other hand, it can synergize with or antagonize estrogen receptor to regulate tumor growth. For instance, the PR-A isoform inhibits ER transcriptional activity, redirects ER to apoptosis-related gene regions, and enhances the tumor susceptibility of mammary epithelial cells by activating the PR-RANK-RANKL pathway. Notably, increased progesterone exposure significantly elevates the risk of breast cancer, particularly hormone receptor-negative subtypes [32]. CD14, a pivotal innate immune mediator, exhibits dual immunomodulatory functions—its strong correlation with myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (r = 0.62, P < 0.001) suggests concurrent involvement in immunosuppressive niche formation and inflammatory microenvironment remodeling [33]. PPARG (Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma) emerges as a metabolic sentinel, where its downregulation in high-risk patients underscores a critical lipid metabolism-tumor suppression axis, aligning with pharmacological evidence that PPARG activation attenuates tumor invasiveness [34]. Complementarily, CHRM1 and HTR3A delineate novel neuroendocrine-oncology crosstalk, implicating acetylcholine and serotonin signaling in tumor innervation and proliferation, respectively, thereby expanding the mechanistic landscape of neural regulation in cancer progression [35,36]. The mechanism of CHRM1 in breast cancer remains unclear. However, based on previous studies, CHRM1 is highly expressed in early-stage prostate cancer tissues. Its agonist, carbachol, can promote the migration and invasion of cancer cells such as PC-3 and LNCaP, while its antagonist, pirenzepine, exerts an inhibitory effect. This regulatory process is mediated by the Hedgehog signaling pathway, through which CHRM1 regulates the expression of genes such as GLI1 and PTCH1 in this pathway [37]. Previous studies have shown that the mRNA expression level of HTR3A in breast cancer tissues is 3.24-fold higher than that in paired adjacent non-tumor tissues. Furthermore, studies have hypothesized that due to the mitogenic activity of this receptor, its high expression is likely to induce the accelerated proliferation of breast cancer cells, thereby facilitating tumor progression [38]. Collectively, these targets epitomize the convergence of hormonal, metabolic, immune, and neuroendocrine dysregulation in breast cancer, providing a multiscale framework for understanding CSXF therapeutic efficacy.

Functional enrichment analysis revealed oxidative phosphorylation as a hallmark of high-risk group (HRG), indicative of metabolic reprogramming toward aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect)—a recognized driver of therapeutic resistance [39].

In the present study, we found that the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) pathway is activated in tumors of HRG, which is typically associated with metabolic adaptability, chemoresistance, and poor prognosis of tumor cells [40]. Multiple active components in CSXF have been reported to interfere with mitochondrial function. For example, paeoniflorin, the main active component of Paeonia lactiflora Pall. in the formula, has been confirmed to improve mitochondrial dysfunction in nerve cells and reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [41]; curcumin, the core component of Curcuma longa L., is a well-known mitochondrial modulator that can inhibit tumor cell growth by inducing mitochondrial pathway-dependent apoptosis [42].

Combined with our molecular docking results demonstrating a strong binding affinity between CSXF metabolites and PPARG (a core metabolic regulatory nuclear receptor) (Fig. 8F), we propose the hypothesis that CSXF may regulate the metabolic sensing network centered on PPARG and other molecules through its multi-component synergistic effects, thereby reversing the abnormal OXPHOS activation in tumor cells and normalizing their metabolic status.

Meanwhile, the metabolic status of tumor cells is inseparable from their surrounding tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Abnormally active OXPHOS leads to the accumulation of metabolites (e.g., lactic acid, ROS) in the TIME, which are key drivers for recruiting, activating, and maintaining the immunosuppressive function of MDSCs [43]. This precisely explains the correlative finding of the present study: the HRG with OXPHOS activation is accompanied by significant infiltration of CD14+ monocytic MDSCs (Fig. 7H). Therefore, one of the downstream effects of the potential regulatory role of CSXF on mitochondrial metabolism of tumor cells is to cut off the metabolic source driving immunosuppression, thereby indirectly impairing the accumulation and function of MDSCs.

Notably, this metabolic-immune regulatory axis is consistent with the efficacy of CSXF in improving depressive symptoms in breast cancer patients at the neuroendocrine level. Chronic psychological stress (the core etiology of depression) leads to excessive activation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which continuously releases stress hormones such as glucocorticoids. These hormones not only directly affect central mood but also systematically reshape the peripheral tumor microenvironment: they can promote metabolic reprogramming of tumor cells (e.g., enhancing OXPHOS), while inhibiting anti-tumor immunity and promoting the expansion of suppressive cells such as MDSCs [44]. Therefore, as a classic liver-soothing and depression-relieving formula, CSXF improves mood and alleviates stress by regulating targets such as CHRM1 and HTR3A (Figs. 8C, D), which may fundamentally reduce the neuroendocrine signals that drive malignant tumor metabolism and immune escape.

Conversely, the enrichment of taste transduction pathways in the low-risk group (LRG) suggests sensory receptor dysregulation may modulate breast cancer cell differentiation, presenting a novel mechanistic axis requiring further investigation [45]. Immune profiling uncovered distinct microenvironmental landscapes between risk groups: the paradoxical association of elevated CD56bright NK cells with poorer survival in HRG may reflect NK cell exhaustion in advanced disease [46], while increased plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs) in LRG potentially foster immune tolerance. The robust correlation between CD14 and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (Spearman’s ρ = 0.698, P < 0.001) implies that CSXF may disrupt immunosuppressive niches by targeting CD14-MDSC interactions, offering a dual strategy for immune activation and tumor suppression [47].

Studies show elevated CD56brightCD16+ and CD56dimCD16- NK subsets in progressive breast cancer patients' blood [48]. Intratumoral CD56+ NK levels positively correlate with tumor grade/stage, where lower levels indicate better prognosis. Increased CD56bright NK and Tregs associate with poor treatment response. While CD56bright NK cells are potent cytokine producers with weak basal cytotoxicity, CD56dim NK cells mediate continuous tumor killing. In breast cancer microenvironment, CD56bright NK cells exhibit tumor-targeting capability but display dual roles: promoting immune escape via inhibitory checkpoints despite low tumor MHC-I, while retaining potential anti-tumor activity, particularly against triple-negative subtypes. Their enrichment in advanced disease may reflect tumor-driven immunosuppression [49].

Experimental validation via RT-qPCR confirmed significant downregulation of CD14, PPARG, and CHRM1 in breast cancer tissues (P < 0.01 or 0.001), reinforcing their diagnostic potential.

CD14 exhibits a positive correlation with MDSC accumulation in breast cancer, collectively establishing a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. MDSCs not only facilitate breast cancer immune escape but also promote metastasis through remodeling pulmonary vascular endothelial extracellular matrix and significantly impair the antitumor efficacy of immunotherapies. These cells suppress immune responses via multiple mechanisms: metabolic deprivation (secreting arginase-1 to degrade essential amino acids for T cells), inducing oxidative stress, inhibiting CTL activation, promoting Treg expansion, and secreting immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β [50,51].

Between RT-qPCR experiment and analysis from high-risk groups, CD14 exhibits a significant context-dependent expression pattern, with its expression levels showing an obvious contradiction between whole-tissue and immune cell levels: RT-qPCR confirmed that CD14 in whole breast cancer tissues was downregulated compared with normal tissues (P < 0.01 or 0.001), while bioinformatics analysis revealed that CD14 expression was elevated in the high-risk group and was closely correlated with MDSC infiltration (Spearman’s correlation coefficient ρ=0.698, P < 0.001).

This discrepancy may stem from differences in detection dimensions: the downregulation of CD14 in whole tissues could be due to the main components of the tissue being breast cancer epithelial cells that do not express CD14, which lowers the overall average level. The high CD14 expression in the high-risk group could be attributed to its specific enrichment in the MDSC subset within the tumor microenvironment.

However, discrepancies in PGR and HTR3A expression across cohorts may stem from limited sample sizes or tissue heterogeneity—a limitation addressable through future multicenter cohort expansion [52]. These findings collectively highlight metabolic-immune crosstalk as a critical determinant of CSXF therapeutic efficacy while underscoring the need for standardized biospecimen collection in TCM-related translational research.

This comprehensive immune modulation, combined with direct effects on tumor cells through PGR and PPARG signaling, positions CSXF as a promising adjunctive therapy that addresses both tumor-intrinsic pathways and host systemic factors. The immune profiling revealed distinct immune landscapes between risk subgroups, with elevated CD56bright NK cell infiltration in HRG and plasmacytoid dendritic cell enrichment in LRG. Notably, the paradoxical association of CD56bright NK cells with poorer prognosis suggests their potential exhaustion or conversion to an immunosuppressive phenotype in advanced breast cancer. The strong correlation between CD14 and MDSCs indicates that CSXF's multi-target mechanism may reshape the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. By targeting the CD14-MDSC axis and modulating neuroendocrine-immune crosstalk through PGR, PPARG, CHRM1, and HTR3A, CSXF could potentially convert "cold" tumors to "hot" ones, enhancing functional immune cell infiltration. This immunomodulatory capacity positions CSXF as a promising adjuvant to current immunotherapies, particularly for overcoming resistance in triple-negative breast cancer where immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown limited efficacy [53].

The integration of TCM's holistic approach with modern immunotherapy represents a paradigm shift toward personalized cancer management that addresses both tumor-intrinsic pathways and host systemic factors. Clinically, this paradigm holds promising prospects: the prognostic model based on the expression levels of PGR, CD14, PPARG, CHRM1, and HTR3A can assist clinicians in achieving refined stratification of breast cancer patients. Regarding potential adjuvant treatment strategies, CSXF may be explored as a supplement or sensitizer to conventional radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.

Molecular docking simulations demonstrated robust binding affinities between CSXF metabolites and prognostic targets, with several interactions exhibiting binding energies below −8.0 kcal/mol, indicating strong target engagement. The CD14-PPARG axis represents a particularly compelling pharmacological target for CSXF. Notably, paeoniflorin (a key metabolite of Paeoniae Radix Alba in CSXF) exhibited stable binding to CD14, while dihydrosanguinarine showed even stronger affinity for PPARG. Paeoniflorin has been shown to selectively inhibit monocytic MDSC expansion and function, reducing arginase-1-mediated T cell suppression and IL-10/TGF-β secretion [54]. This modulation shifts the balance from immunosuppressive CD56bright NK cells toward cytotoxic CD56dim NK subsets, enhancing tumor cell killing capacity [55]. Meanwhile, curcumin (from Curcumae Rhizoma in CSXF) targets multiple immunosuppressive pathways, including FATP2-mediated MDSC function and PD-L1 expression [56]. By inhibiting p38α signaling, curcumin reverses MDSC-induced immunosuppression and promotes CD8+ T cell infiltration [57]. The synergistic action of these bioactive compounds within CSXF creates a multi-pronged approach to dismantle tumor immune evasion mechanisms, potentially restoring effective anti-tumor immunity. This multi-target pharmacological profile aligns with TCM's holistic philosophy while providing mechanistic insights for integrating traditional medicine with contemporary cancer immunotherapy.

Our findings underscore active components of CSXF (including paeoniflorin and curcumin) can stably bind to multiple key targets such as PGR, CD14, and PPARG, thereby synergistically regulating pathways relevant to breast cancer progression and stress responses—including estrogen signaling, oxidative phosphorylation, and cholinergic neurotransmitter pathways. This provides a pharmacological basis for confirming CSXF’s potential to intervene in breast cancer biological processes via a multi-target mode of action. The strong binding affinities observed in molecular docking (particularly dihydrosanguinarine-PPARG at ΔG = −9.8 kcal/mol) provide evidence of direct target engagement, while RT-qPCR validation has confirmed significant downregulation of CD14, PPARG, and CHRM1 in BC tissues (P < 0.01), reinforcing their diagnostic and therapeutic relevance.

However, several limitations warrant consideration. This study is fundamentally a retrospective analysis, and the validation was merely performed via RT-qPCR with a limited clinical sample size. The lack of cellular functional experiments and in vivo animal validation substantially constrains the immediate translational impact of our findings. In addition, although CSXF possesses multiple interactive bioactive compounds, the synergistic pharmacological effects cannot be fully confirmed merely based on computational prediction data. Future studies will expand the scale of clinical samples and conduct comprehensive experimental validations using cellular functional assays, patient-derived organoids, and xenotransplant models to further elucidate the underlying mechanisms of core targets and the polypharmacological characteristics of CSXF, as well as to explore the potential synergistic effects and combinatorial regimens of CSXF combined with conventional breast cancer therapeutics. Furthermore, future isobolographic analysis and combination index studies are required to quantitatively evaluate the synergistic interactions between CSXF and conventional therapies. Additionally, pharmacokinetic investigations characterizing the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion profiles of CSXF metabolites are essential to solidify the pharmacological foundation for its clinical translation and practical application.

To advance these explorations, a stepwise preclinical and clinical research roadmap can be formulated. Specifically, preclinical and clinical research can be initiated by first testing CSXF and its core components in breast cancer cell lines and organoid models. This can be followed by in vivo animal experiments to assess efficacy and safety, and subsequently by clinical studies to validate the therapeutic potential of CSXF in human breast cancer patients.

In the preclinical phase, detailed mechanistic investigations should be integrated with efficacy evaluations—for example, examining the regulatory effects of CSXF and its core components on the OXPHOS pathway, MDSC infiltration, and HPA axis activity in both cell and organoid models. In animal experiments, optimizing the administration route and dosage of CSXF, monitoring key indicators such as tumor growth rate, survival time, and systemic toxicity, and collecting tissue samples for pathological and molecular biological analyses will provide critical data for subsequent clinical translations. For clinical studies, initial phase I trials can focus on safety and tolerability in breast cancer patients, followed by phase II trials to evaluate preliminary efficacy (e.g., tumor response rate, progression-free survival) and explore potential predictive biomarkers (such as the expression levels of PGR, CD14, PPARG, CHRM1, and HTR3A). Large-scale phase III trials can then be conducted to further validate the therapeutic benefits and clarify the optimal patient populations that may benefit from CSXF-based combination therapy.

Conclusion

This work establishes CSXF as a sophisticated multi-target therapeutic agent that modulates interconnected neuroendocrine-immune-metabolic networks in BC. By bridging ancient TCM wisdom with modern pharmacological principles, our findings provide a scientific basis for integrating CSXF into evidence-based cancer care. The identification of PGR, CD14, PPARG, CHRM1, and HTR3A as pharmacological targets not only explains CSXF's clinical efficacy but also offers novel avenues for developing multi-target strategies in precision oncology. Future clinical trials evaluating CSXF as an adjunctive therapy, particularly for BC patients with emotional stress-related pathophysiology, could translate these mechanistic insights into tangible patient benefits.
