Restoring mitochondrial quantity and quality to reverse the Warburg effect and drive neuroblastoma differentiation
Authors: Haowen Jiang, Sarah Jane Tiche, Clifford Jiajun He, Junyan Liu, Fuyun Bian, Mohamed Jedoui, Balint Forgo, Md Tauhidul Islam, Meng Zhao, Pamela Emengo, Bo He, Yang Li, Albert M. Li, Anh T. Truong, Jestine Ho, Cathyrin Simmermaker, Yanan Yang, Meng-Ning Zhou, Zhen Hu, Katrin J. Svensson, Daniel J. Cuthbertson, Florette K. Hazard, Lei Xing, Hiroyuki Shimada, Bill Chiu, Jiangbin Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceMitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of cancer, driving dedifferentiation and progression. Here, we introduce a mitochondria-centric differentiation therapy that combines retinoic acid (RA) with a mitochondrial uncoupler to restore mitochondrial quality and quantity. In neuroblastoma models, RA promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, while the uncoupler creates a pseudo-ATP (Adenosine triphosphate) demand and enhances respiration. This synergistic approach reverses the Warburg effect, reprograms cancer cell metabolism from anabolism to catabolism, and induces differentiation both in vitro and in vivo. Our findings highlight mitochondrial respiration as essential for differentiation and present a metabolic strategy to reprogram tumor cell fate for improved cancer treatment.

The Warburg effect, one major metabolic hallmark of cancer, is prevalent in cancers and underpins the principle of positron emission tomography–computed tomography imaging, characterized by a disproportionately elevated glycolytic flux compared to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle even under aerobic conditions (1–3). Although the Warburg effect appears to occur in the cytosol, it may be caused by mitochondrial dysfunction (4, 5).

Mitochondrial impairment in cancers can result from reduced mitochondrial quantity and/or quality. Many cancers exhibit lower mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy numbers and decreased levels of mitochondrial RNA and respiratory proteins encoded by nuclear DNA compared to their corresponding healthy tissues, all of which suggest a reduction in mitochondrial quantity within these tumors (6, 7). In addition, slow TCA cycle flux and low ATP synthesis rate were observed in cancers compared to their corresponding healthy tissues (8, 9), regardless of whether mtDNA copy numbers are reduced or unchanged, indicating the impaired mitochondrial quality in tumors. Additionally, compared to normal cells, the mitochondria in cancer cells exhibit higher levels of fission, lower levels of fusion, and disrupted quality control processes, resulting in mitochondrial fragmentation and enhanced aerobic glycolysis (10).

It may seem counterintuitive that mitochondrial impairment not only fails to impede but actually leads to cancer progression, even though cancer cells continue to generate most of their ATP through mitochondria (8, 9). Although mitochondrial impairment, particularly through electron transport chain (ETC) inhibition, reduces proliferation by disrupting aspartate and pyrimidine synthesis (11–13), it also significantly drives cell dedifferentiation in tumor cells (14–16). This dedifferentiation process initially reduces ATP demand by shedding energy-intensive tissue-specific functions, and ultimately leads to malignancy and a poorer prognosis. Mechanistically, mitochondrial impairment drives this malignant progression by disrupting NAD+ regeneration through respiration, leading to a lower NAD+/NADH ratio compared to noncancerous cells (17). This reduced NAD+/NADH ratio not only drives the conversion of pyruvate to lactate but also many other metabolic reprogramming events, such as reductive carboxylation (18–21) and L-2HG production (15, 22, 23), which inhibit normal differentiation while promoting tumor progression (24). Furthermore, an impaired TCA cycle reallocates metabolites from energy production to biosynthetic processes, actively facilitating uncontrolled malignant cell proliferation (1).

Differentiated cells generally consume more ATP than stem or progenitor cells, suggesting that the capacity to generate sufficient ATP has been essential for enabling cellular differentiation throughout evolution. But in modern multicellular organisms, maintaining this differentiated state requires not only ATP production but also its continuous consumption to sustain ETC activity, which drives the forward TCA cycle, producing intermediates essential for epigenetic modifications (16). These modifications, in turn, play a crucial role in maintaining the differentiated state by regulating gene expression patterns that define and stabilize cell identity (25). In this context, our study aims to address the critical question of whether restoring mitochondrial quantity and quality can drive tumor redifferentiation and to explore the methods for achieving this.

In our research, we utilize neuroblastoma as a model. Neuroblastoma accounts for 15% of pediatric cancer-related fatalities, and patient survival strongly correlates with its differentiation status, with more differentiated tumors predicting improved outcomes (26–28). Therefore, inducing differentiation in these tumors may significantly enhance survival and quality of life for affected children. Retinoic acid (RA), a derivative of vitamin A, is known to downregulate N-Myc and induce neuroblastoma differentiation (29–33). It is administered as part of the maintenance phase following intensive multimodal therapy in patients with high-risk neuroblastoma and has been approved by the Food and Drug Administration to improve event-free survival (34). However, some patients develop resistance to RA and experience relapse, indicating that RA alone is insufficient; combining it with other treatments may be necessary to achieve more effective differentiation. We demonstrated that RA treatment increased mtDNA copy number and promoted mitochondrial elongation in neuroblastoma cells; however, this upregulation of mitochondrial biogenesis did not lead to enhanced mitochondrial respiration. To overcome this limitation, we employed a mitochondrial uncoupler to dissipate the mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby activating mitochondrial respiration and the ETC, increasing the NAD+/NADH ratio, reversing the Warburg effect, and enhancing mitochondrial quality. Using this strategy, we were able to restore the mitochondrial quantity and quality, prompting a metabolic switch from biosynthesis to energetics in cancer cells. This metabolic reprogramming induced differentiation both in vitro and in vivo. Functional or genetic inhibition of mitochondrial respiration in cancer cells abolished the differentiation effect of RA and uncoupler, indicating that mitochondrial respiration is essential for tumor cell differentiation. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis further revealed changes in cell identity following RA and uncoupler intervention. Histological evidence and patient gene signature prognosis analysis suggest that this mitochondria restoration strategy could significantly improve patient outcomes. Our research introduces a therapeutic approach to efficiently restore mitochondrial quantity and quality, centered on the differentiation of tumors, and offers a promising avenue for cancer treatment.

Results

RA Increases Mitochondrial Quantity without Activating the TCA Cycle.

Previous studies have attributed the differentiation-inducing effects of RA to its function as a ligand for RA receptors (RAR), which are transcription factors that activate gene expression in the nucleus. By reanalyzing the RA-induced genes, we found that RA significantly upregulates nuclear-encoded genes, whose products function in the mitochondria, particularly those involved in the mitochondrial ribosome and complex I subunits (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), which are largely ignored in previous studies. The upregulation of mitochondrial ribosome (CHCHD1, MRPL36, and MRPL41) and complex I subunit genes (NDUF11, NDUFB9, and NDUFV3) upon RA treatment was also validated using RT-qPCR (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). This intriguing finding leads us to hypothesize that RA promotes differentiation partly through modulating mitochondrial function, alongside its established role in activating nuclear receptors (16, 25, 35, 36). To study this, we employed a 3xHA-OMP25-GFP reporter system (37) to monitor mitochondrial dynamics in neuroblastoma cells during differentiation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C and Movie S1). RA treatment significantly increased mitochondrial mass, as evidenced by increased GFP intensity and elevated mtDNA copy number (Fig. 1 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). The latter was determined by the ratio of mtDNA to genomic DNA, using mitochondria-encoded genes NADH dehydrogenase 1 (MT-ND1) or tRNA leucine 1 (MT-TL1), to nuclear-encode genes ACTB or B2M (β2 microglobulin) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Additionally, RA treatment significantly altered mitochondrial morphology, increasing the proportion of linear mitochondria relative to circular ones (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E), indicating an increase in mitochondrial fusion events.

Intriguingly, despite the increased mitochondrial content and changes in morphology, RA treatment did not enhance the basal respiration rate (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1F). This indicates an unchanged baseline energy demand and suggests decreased respiration activity per mitochondrion. However, both the spare and maximal respiration capacity were increased (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1F), suggesting that while the resting metabolic rate remains unchanged, the mitochondria are functional and can be activated to enhance respiration. To further elucidate RA-driven metabolic reprogramming, we performed U-13C-glucose tracing experiments. The results revealed a slight decrease in glucose entry into the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) following RA treatment, as indicated by the m + 5 fraction of ATP, ADP, UTP, and UDP, with neglectable changes in the glycolysis pathway and TCA cycle (Fig. 1D and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 G–I). This is consistent with the Seahorse data (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1F), confirming that although RA treatment increased mitochondrial quantity, overall respiration was not enhanced.

Mitochondrial Uncoupler Activates Mitochondrial Respiration, Redirecting Metabolism from Biosynthesis to Energetics.

We hypothesized that RA is unable to activate mitochondrial respiration in cancer cells due to the absence of a robust ATP consumption mechanism, which is usually associated with dedifferentiation (Fig. 2A). As a result, ATP accumulates, preventing ETC respiration from activating, despite an increase in mitochondrial number. To overcome this limitation, we employed niclosamide ethanolamine (NEN), a previously tested mitochondrial uncoupler (15), to create a pseudo-ATP demand by dissipating the mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby increasing respiration and lowering ATP synthesis efficiency simultaneously. As expected, NEN treatment significantly increased cellular basal respiration, and the combination of RA and NEN further enhanced this effect (Fig. 2B). Electron microscopy confirmed an increase in mitochondrial number upon RA treatment, whereas NEN treatment did not cause noticeable morphological changes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Meanwhile, Mitochondrial uncoupling activated the ETC increasing the intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio and reducing the RA-induced elevation of the ATP/ADP ratio (Fig. 2C).To evaluate the lasting effects of RA and the uncoupler on enhancing mitochondrial respiration, cells were pretreated with either RA, NEN, RA+NEN, or no treatment for 72 h, then reseeded without treatment for Seahorse analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Surprisingly, spare and maximal respiratory capacity were significantly increased by NEN pretreatments, with the RA+NEN combination treatment showing further enhancement compared to NEN treatment alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). Energetic mapping also revealed that RA+NEN treatment shifted the cells to a more energetic state (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). These findings suggest that the uncoupler does not only acutely increase oxygen consumption but also further enhances the maximal oxygen consumption capacity with prolonged effect.

To elucidate the metabolic reprogramming effects of RA+uncoupler combination, U-13C-glucose/glutamine isotope tracing was performed. The results showed that NEN treatments significantly reduced glucose carbon entry into PPP for nucleotide synthesis, as indicated by reduced m + 5 ATP, ADP, UTP, and UDP, and reduced glutamine-driven aspartate into pyrimidine biosynthesis, as indicated by reduced m + 3 UTP and UDP, while the combination of RA and NEN further enhanced these effects (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B). The treatments had no significant effect on the glycolytic pathway, as indicated by unchanged m + 3 fraction of dihydroxyacetone phosphate, 3-phosphoglycerate, phosphoenolpyruvate, pyruvate, and lactate (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). However, NEN treatment significantly increased glucose flux into the TCA cycle and accelerated the TCA cycle, as determined by the sustained increased fractions of m + 2 (2nd cycle) and m + 3/4 (2nd cycle) in citrate, α-ketoglutarate, glutamate, fumarate, malate, and aspartate, m + 5 (3rd cycle) in glutamate, and m + 6 (4th cycle) in citrate (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). The combination of RA+NEN showed the greatest increase in these labeling fractions. Meanwhile, glutamine tracing also supported the observation of TCA cycle acceleration, as determined by increased fractions of m + 3 (2nd cycle) and m + 1/2 (3rd cycle) in α-ketoglutarate, and m + 1 (3rd cycle) in fumarate, malate, and aspartate (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 E and F). NEN treatment inhibited reductive carboxylation, as indicated by reduced m + 3 fumarate, malate, and aspartate (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3E), consistent with our previous publication (21), and RA+NEN combination further reduced the labeling fraction of reductive carboxylation products m + 3 malate and m + 3 aspartate (SI Appendix, Fig. S3G). Together, the isotope tracing experiments demonstrated that RA+NEN combination shifts the TCA cycle’s substrate preference from glutamine to glucose and redirects metabolism from biosynthesis to energetics.

Restoring Mitochondrial Quantity and Quality Drives Differentiation.

Differentiation is associated with mitochondrial biogenesis and enhanced respiration (16, 36). Therefore, we asked whether the combination of RA+uncoupler could activate differentiation in cancer cells. RA or NEN alone initiated differentiation in MYCN-amplified neuroblastoma cell lines—SK-N-BE(2), CHP134, and LAN5, as well as MYCN-non-amplified neuroblastoma cell line—SH-SY5Y (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A), as determined by morphological changes and total neurite length per cell, with the RA + NEN group showing a synergistic enhancement in differentiation (Fig. 3A). Another MYCN-non-amplified cell line SK-N-AS did not exhibit obvious differentiation, with neurite outgrowth observed only in a small subset of cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Notably, the cause of increased total neurite length per cell was mainly due to a synergistic increase in neurite counts per cell, not average neurite length increase (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). Immunostaining for neuron differentiation marker β-Tubulin III confirmed the successful differentiation of treated cells into neurons (Fig. 3B). We also tested this combination on the nonmalignant retinal pigment epithelial cell line ARPE-19, which exhibited a neuronal differentiation response to RA+NEN combination, although higher concentrations of each drug were required to elicit this effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Other uncouplers, such as FCCP and BAM15 (38), were also tested in combination with RA and showed similar synergistic effects observed with RA + NEN, confirming that the differentiation depends on mitochondrial uncoupling (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). RNA-seq showed that the combination treatment synergistically induced genes associated with favorable prognosis in neuroblastoma, including those involved in RA signaling (RARB and RARG), differentiation (HOXC5 and PBX1), and chromatin remodeling (RYBP and JADE2), with their expression changes validated by time-course RT-qPCR (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). Further analysis of gene set enrichment (GSEA) revealed positive enrichment of pathways related to neuronal differentiation and development, including “anterior–posterior pattern specification,” “regulation of axonogenesis,” “neuron maturation,” and “regulation of neurogenesis” in the RA + NEN group (Fig. 3D). Additionally, negative enrichment was observed in pathways related to cell cycle progression and DNA replication, including “DNA replication initiation” and “positive regulation of the cell cycle phase transition” (Fig. 3D). These findings indicate that the combination treatment not only promotes neuronal differentiation but also suppresses proliferative pathways, aligning with the fact that proliferation arrest is usually associated with terminal differentiation.

To functionally validate the synergy between RA and NEN, we employed the Chou-Talalay method to calculate combination index (CI) (39), using cell proliferation as the readout in four neuroblastoma cell lines: SK-N-BE(2) and CHP134 (MYCN-amplified), as well as SH-SY5Y and SK-N-AS (MYCN-non-amplified). The results demonstrated a strong synergistic effect of the combined RA and NEN treatment across different concentrations until the effect was saturated at higher concentrations, confirming that the combination therapy is more effective in inhibiting proliferation than either treatment alone, regardless of MYCN status (Fig. 3E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). Given the isotope tracing result demonstrated that RA+NEN combination reduced 13C-labeling in nucleotides (Fig. 2 D and E), we examined whether the RA+NEN-induced proliferation inhibition was due to impaired nucleotide biosynthesis. The treated cells were supplemented with ribose, a nucleoside mix, or their combination. However, none of these conditions rescued the proliferation defect in SK-N-BE(2) or CHP134 cells upon NEN or RA+NEN treatments (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D), indicating that the observed growth suppression is not simply due to a shortage of nucleotide precursors, but likely due to cell differentiation. To assess lasting effects on proliferation inhibition, cells were pretreated for 3 d with RA, NEN, RA+NEN, or vehicle, then reseeded at equal live-cell numbers without further treatment and monitored over time (Fig. 3F). Even after treatment withdrawal, the RA+NEN combination led to a significantly greater reduction in proliferation than either agent alone in both SK-N-BE(2) and CHP134 cells. This suggests that RA and NEN reduce proliferation not through a temporary effect on blocking the cell cycle, but more likely through a lasting effect on inducing differentiation.

Cleaved-PARP, a marker of apoptosis, was induced by NEN treatment but not by RA. Importantly, antioxidant N-acetyl cysteine supplementation did not suppress PARP cleavage, indicating that oxidative stress is not the primary driver of NEN-induced apoptosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S5E).

Pharmacological or Genetic Inactivation of Mitochondria Blocks Differentiation.

To determine whether mitochondrial respiration is essential for neuroblastoma differentiation, we assessed the impact of mitochondrial dysfunction on RA and NEN-induced differentiation. Treatment with complex I inhibitor rotenone significantly blocked differentiation in neuroblastoma cells, as indicated by decreased total neurite length per cell, fewer neurite counts, and reduced expression of neuron differentiation-related genes (Fig. 4 A–C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). This suggests that mitochondrial respiration is essential for neuronal differentiation induced by RA and NEN. In addition, inhibition of complex I activity with rotenone further reduced cell proliferation, particularly in NEN-treated cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B), consistent with previous reports that ETC activity is required for NAD+ regeneration and cell proliferation (12, 13, 40).

Additionally, we employed a prolonged ethidium bromide (EB) treatment protocol to generate ρ0 cells (40), which lack mtDNA and thus have severely compromised mitochondrial respiration (Fig. 4D). We also examined mitochondrial morphology using the 3xHA-OMP25-GFP reporter system. Interestingly, the mitochondria lost their linear morphology and formed a hollow cycle (Fig. 4E). The ρ0 cells had almost undetectable mtDNA content compared to wild-type cells (Fig. 4F). Seahorse analysis further confirmed diminished mitochondrial respiration, as indicated by low oxygen consumption rates and no response to uncoupler treatment, along with increased extracellular acidification rates, a sign of the Warburg effect (Fig. 4G and SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). Notably, the ρ0 cells formed multilayer colonies, a characteristic of cells that have lost contact inhibition, suggesting increased malignancy (Fig. 4H). These ρ0 cells treated with RA, NEN, or RA+NEN showed markedly reduced differentiation compared to wild-type (WT) cells, with significantly shorter neurite lengths and fewer neurites per cell (Fig. 4H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). To address potential off-target effects of EB treatment that could inhibit cell differentiation, we knocked out mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM), a key activator of mitochondrial transcription and DNA replication (Fig. 4I). TFAM knockout depletes mtDNA and abolishes respiration (Fig. 4J and SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). Like ρ0 cells, TFAM-knockout cells displayed resistance to RA and NEN-induced differentiation and lost contact inhibition (Fig. 4K and SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). These findings underscore the essential role of mitochondrial respiration not only in promoting neuroblastoma cell differentiation but also in maintaining a less aggressive and more regulated cellular phenotype.

Dietary RA and Mitochondrial Uncoupler Induce Tumor Differentiation in Orthotopic Neuroblastoma Xenografts.

Next, we investigated whether the combination of RA and uncoupler could induce tumor differentiation in vivo. To assess this, we administered diets containing either 83.3 ppm RA, 2,000 ppm NEN, or a combination of both (Fig. 5A). As we previously reported (15), NEN treatment significantly slowed tumor growth (Fig. 5B), while the combination of RA+NEN did not further reduce tumor size. Immunohistochemistry staining for Ki67, a marker of cell proliferation, revealed no significant differences among the treatment groups (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). None of these treatments affected the weight of the mice, indicating good safety and tolerance (Fig. 5C). H&E staining revealed histological features of tumor differentiation, including neuropil formation and rosette structures (Fig. 5D). Interestingly, the expression of the Schwann cell marker S100β—an indicator of favorable prognosis—was significantly elevated in both the RA and NEN groups, with the most pronounced effect observed in the combination group (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). In addition, the neuronal differentiation marker TrkA, which is associated with favorable prognosis, was induced by RA and NEN treatments (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). Along with inducing differentiation, RA or NEN treatment reduced tumor cell size and nuclear size, with the combination therapy yielding the most pronounced reduction—indicators associated with improved prognosis (Fig. 5D). Conversely, N-Myc, a differentiation inhibitor (30, 31, 41) and major unfavorable prognosis marker in neuroblastoma, was reduced by either RA or NEN, with the combination group showing the most significant reduction, consistent with our in vitro findings (Fig. 5 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B and C).

To further evaluate the potential therapeutic effects of RA and NEN, a bioinformatics analysis was conducted, focusing on the transcriptional changes induced by RA, NEN, or their combination, and their correlation with clinical outcomes. The average z-scores for both upregulated and downregulated gene lists were calculated using the published neuroblastoma patient data [GSE62564 (42)]. Using the median Z-score as a cutoff, patients were first separated into high and low gene signature expression groups. Based on this, we then stratified patients into four categories by considering both upregulated (high/low) and downregulated (high/low) gene expression levels: 1) Up high/Down Low, 2) Up Low/Down Low, 3) Up High/Down High, and 4) Up Low/Down High (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). These groups were then used to generate survival curves (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). Notably, groups (1) and (4) represent the two extremes of the treatment gene signatures, categorized as “High” and “Low” treatment gene signatures, respectively. Interestingly, the RA-induced High and Low gene signatures did not significantly separate the survival curves, aligning with the observation that RA alone does not improve overall survival in neuroblastoma (43–45). However, the NEN-induced High and Low gene signatures significantly separated the survival curves, with the High gene signature group showing a 92.4% overall survival rate, compared to 54.2% in the Low gene signature group, with a difference of 35.7%. Moreover, the combination of RA and NEN further enhanced this separation, with the High gene signature group achieving a 94.8% overall survival rate, compared to 51.8% in the Low gene signature group, with a difference of 41.7%, suggesting that using uncoupler or the combination of RA+uncoupler could be beneficial to patients (Fig. 5F). To assess the robustness of the RA+NEN gene signature, we analyzed two additional neuroblastoma datasets. In GSE85047 (46), the “Up High/Down Low” group also had the best prognosis. In GSE45547 (47), the Up high/Down Low group also indicate favorable prognosis, though slightly less than the Up low/Down Low group (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 F and G). In all three cohorts, the Up Low/Down High group exhibited the poorest prognosis. These results validate the RA+NEN signature as a reliable predictor of improved survival.

To evaluate whether N-Myc status affects the prognostic value of the RA, NEN, or RA+NEN gene signatures, we examined patient distribution by MYCN amplification across signature-defined subgroups (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). The RA+NEN signature tended to group most MYCN-amplified patients in the Low category, while the High group was enriched for MYCN-non-amplified cases, potentially explaining their better outcomes. Survival analysis within MYCN-amplified and non-amplified subgroups (SI Appendix, Fig. S8B) showed that the High RA+NEN signature remained prognostic in MYCN-non-amplified patients, with a similar but nonsignificant trend in the MYCN-amplified group, likely due to limited sample size. These results suggest that the RA+NEN signature is associated with improved prognosis regardless of MYCN status, aligning with its synergistic effects in MYCN-non-amplified SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C).

Single-Cell RNA Sequencing Reveals Differentiation Dynamics in Neuroblastoma under Treatment.

To further investigate the differentiation dynamics in neuroblastoma under RA and NEN treatment, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing using in vivo orthotopic neuroblastoma samples. Two mice from each group, selected based on similar treatment durations and tumor sizes ranging from 500 to 700 mm3, were collected and dissociated for single-cell analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). A total of 14,938 cells were analyzed and classified into six distinct clusters (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Using previously reported markers for cycling neuroblast—such as ASCL1, GATA3, PHOX2B, RET, ALK, and MKI67 (48, 49)—we identified cycling neuroblast and neuroblast. Additionally, using neuron markers including HADN2, TUBB3, NEFM, DCX, GAP43, TH, MAP2, and PHOX2A (50–57), we identified pre-neuron-like cells and neuron-like cells. The remaining two clusters were assigned as intermediate 1 and 2 (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). In general, neuron-like cells are expected to be more differentiated than cycling neuroblast. To validate this in our data, we performed RNA velocity analysis to determine the differentiation trajectory among the clusters. As expected, the trajectory arrows originated from cycling neuroblast and ended in neuron-like cells, confirming the differentiation hierarchy (Fig. 6A). In addition, we performed diffusion map analysis across all clusters to assess their differentiation potential (Fig. 6C). The results suggest that intermediate 2 is more differentiated than intermediate 1 and further validate the overall differentiation hierarchy across all clusters (Fig. 6C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9C).

Consistent with the observation that higher differentiation is associated with better prognosis in neuroblastoma, elevated cycling neuroblast markers were linked to worse prognosis, while elevated neuron-like cell markers were associated with improved prognosis (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). Additionally, based on the median Z-score, patients were stratified into four groups: 1) High/Low, 2) Low/Low, 3) High/High, and 4) Low/High for neuron-like cells and cycling neuroblast, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S9D). The best prognosis was observed in the High/Low group with a survival rate of 91.9%, while the worst prognosis was in the Low/High group with a survival rate of 42.1%, suggesting that a higher ratio of neuron-like cells to cycling neuroblast is significantly associated with better prognosis (Fig. 6D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9E). Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed a distinct progression of cellular states from low to high differentiation across treatment conditions. In the control group, 37.65% of cells were cycling neuroblast, while 20.75% were neuroblast, and only 6.04% reached the fully differentiated neuron-like cell state. RA treatment reduced the cycling neuroblast population to 12.76% while increasing the proportions of more differentiated cells, including 25.68% neuroblast, 23.09% intermediate 1 cells, and 9.59% neuron-like cells. NEN treatment further suppressed cycling neuroblast to 3.65%, with increases in intermediate 1 to 31.00% and pre-neuron-like cells to 16.92%, though neuron-like cells remained low at 6.51%. Notably, the combination of RA and NEN resulted in the greatest reduction of cycling neuroblast to 3.68% and further reduced neuroblast (20.85%) compared to NEN alone (23.01%), with Intermediate 1 cells increasing to 32.86% and neuron-like cells to 7.39%, indicating a significant shift toward more differentiated cell types (Fig. 6A and SI Appendix, Fig. S9E). Importantly, RA increased the ratio of neuron-like cells to cycling neuroblast to 75.16%, NEN to 178.36%, and the combination of RA and NEN achieved the highest ratio of 200.82%, indicating better prognosis with the combined treatment (Fig. 6 D and E).

Next, we investigated transcriptional hallmark dynamics along the differentiation trajectory, revealing distinct patterns in cell proliferation, stemness, and mitochondrial functions among the differentiation stages. Proliferation markers like E2F1, MCM2, MKI67, and stemness markers such as MYCN and CD24 were highly expressed in less differentiated states and decreased as cells progressed toward more mature, differentiated states (Fig. 6F). Interestingly, a group of stemness markers, including PROM1, NANOG, and KLF4, showed low expression in cycling neuroblast but were upregulated in neuroblast, aligning with the presence of a cell population in the neuroblast stage with high differentiation potential (Fig. 6 C and F). These markers later decreased as cells progressed toward more mature states. OXPHOS genes, mitochondrial ribosomal mitochondrial translation factors, and ribosomal genes showed the opposite pattern, with their expression increasing significantly as cells differentiated, reflecting enhanced mitochondrial function in more mature cells (Fig. 6 G–I and SI Appendix, Fig. S9G), highlighting the growing need for protein synthesis in transition from self-renewal to differentiation (58). Finally, transcription factor activity shifted from proliferation-related factors like MZF1 and HIF1A in less differentiated states to differentiation-related factors like NEUROD1 and ASCL1 in more differentiated states (SI Appendix, Fig. S9H). This progression demonstrates the transcriptional reprogramming that drives the shift from proliferation to differentiation.

Discussion

The symbiogenesis theory, proposed in 1905, suggests that eukaryotic cells evolved by engulfing a free-living aerobic bacterium, which enabled efficient ATP production through oxidative phosphorylation, driving the evolution of multicellularity and cell differentiation (16). This theory aligns with observations in developmental processes, such as neurodevelopment, where differentiation is associated with increased mitochondrial quantity and enhanced oxidative phosphorylation (59). Otto Warburg also proposed that impaired mitochondrial respiration in differentiated cells causes them to rely on glycolysis instead of oxidative phosphorylation, leading to dedifferentiation and driving tumorigenesis (4, 60). Thus, mitochondrial homeostasis directly regulates cell fate determination and is essential for maintaining cellular differentiation. Since many cancers exhibit reduced mitochondrial quality and quantity, restoring mitochondrial attributes could be a promising strategy for cancer differentiation therapy.

Neuroblastoma (NB) is one of the most common solid cancer types in childhood, with over 600 cases per year in the United States. In high-risk patients, disease relapse occurs frequently after surgery or chemotherapy and eventually becomes fatal. Notably, high-risk neuroblastoma displays metabolic heterogeneity and adaptability, which has been demonstrated in MYCN-amplified subtypes (61). The effect of RA has been mostly studied in NB among solid tumors because it induces clear neuronal differentiation morphology and proliferation arrest in many NB cell lines. One clinical trial showed that RA significantly increased event-free survival in patients who took myeloablative therapy followed by autologous bone marrow transplantation (ABMT) or intensive chemotherapy (34). However, even in the ABMT+RA group, over 40% of the patients did not respond to treatment and died within 5 y (62). Additionally, RA-responsive NB cells can develop RA resistance, particularly under hypoxic conditions (14, 63). Altogether, these studies underscore the need for a deeper understanding of cancer cell differentiation mechanisms and the development of more advanced differentiation strategies (44, 64). Along with previous studies that focused on RA’s role in activating RAR-dependent transcription, we further expand its role in regulating mitochondrial biogenesis partly through upregulating nuclear-encoded mitochondria genes to induce differentiation. In our study, we demonstrated that RA strongly promotes mitochondrial biogenesis and elongation. Although RA treatment decreased the glucose flux into the pentose pathway for nucleotide synthesis, it failed to activate cellular respiration or TCA cycle flux. This is likely because dedifferentiated cancer cells reduce high ATP-consuming functions, leading to a lower ATP demand, consistent with empirical data showing that cancer cells need less ATP for growth and division compared to normal cells for maintenance (65). As a result, the excess mitochondrial quantity under low ATP demand leads to ATP accumulation, which can inhibit ATP synthase activity. Since ATP syntheses use the proton gradient to generate ATP, reduced ATP synthesis leads to an elevated mitochondrial proton gradient, which in turn limits ETC activity. This restriction lowers the NAD+/NADH ratio, ultimately slowing or even partially reversing the TCA cycle through reductive carboxylation (15). TCA cycle metabolites are essential for maintaining the epigenetic landscape (35) and differentiation state (16). Therefore, a slow or dysregulated TCA cycle can block differentiation, leading to resistance to RA-based differentiation therapy.

To overcome this limitation, we employed mitochondrial uncouplers to create a pseudo-ATP demand, activating the ETC and TCA cycle. Mitochondrial uncouplers are small molecules that act as cross-membrane proton transporters, dissipating the membrane potential and reactivating the ETC. This increases the NAD+/NADH ratio, which not only reverses the Warburg effect and improves mitochondrial quality but also accelerates key steps of the TCA cycle. Isotope tracing using U-13C-glucose and U-13C-glutamine revealed that restoring mitochondrial quantity and quality with RA and uncoupler caused a metabolic transition, driven by the increased NAD+/NADH ratio, rerouted glucose and glutamine from the PPP toward oxidative phosphorylation. This transition was accompanied by a significant acceleration of the TCA cycle and reduced reliance on reductive carboxylation. Notably, the reprogramming of the TCA cycle by the combination treatment exhibited a higher preference for glucose as a primary substrate over glutamine, signifying a shift from biosynthesis to energy production. Differentiation requires a high ATP turnover rate to lower the mitochondrial membrane potential, enabling ETC activation and accelerating the TCA cycle, which in turn facilitates the epigenetic remodeling necessary for differentiation to occur. While differentiated cells typically possess more mitochondria than stem/progenitor cells (16, 36)—suggesting that the capacity to produce ample ATP was crucial for differentiation during evolution—maintaining this differentiated state in modern multicellular organisms requires not just ATP production but also continuous ATP consumption and sustained ETC activity.

Restoring mitochondrial function with both RA and uncouplers promotes neuroblastoma differentiation and triggers cell cycle arrest. While both neurite length and number increase during differentiation, the rise in neurite number plays a more significant role in determining the extent of differentiation. This suggests that neurite number may increase initially as the neuron forms multiple processes, and neurite length becomes more prominent as the neuron matures and elongates its processes for connectivity.

Previous studies have shown that mitochondrial number and activity increase during the normal differentiation process (16, 36). Here, both pharmacological and genetic tools were employed to inhibit mitochondrial respiration and prove that mitochondrial reparation is necessary for cell differentiation. Interestingly, the neuroblastoma ρ0 cells exhibit loss of contact inhibition, which is associated with enhanced malignancy and invasiveness (66). Together, our studies demonstrated that restoring mitochondrial quality and quantity is both necessary and sufficient to induce differentiation in cancer.

Recently, single-cell RNA sequencing has been used to identify the cell of origin in neuroblastoma and to reveal lineage alterations occurring during tumorigenesis (49, 67–72). These studies identified precursor/progenitor lineages from which neuroblastoma may originate; however, no existing studies have yet shown lineage transition changes in response to differentiation therapy. Using single-cell sequencing, we further validated the differentiation-inducing effects of RA and NEN. RA alone promoted cellular differentiation, while NEN effectively reduced the stemness population. Notably, the combination of RA and NEN demonstrated the strongest effect, inducing the most pronounced differentiation while simultaneously eliminating stemness characteristics, in line with the histological analysis. Interestingly, stemness markers such as PROM1, NANOG, and KLF4 exhibited low expression in cycling neuroblast but were upregulated in neuroblast, consistent with the presence of a highly differentiation-capable population at this stage. This could be explained by the fact that certain cancers may exist in a transdifferentiated state (73), requiring a reversion to a stem-like state before proper differentiation can proceed. This suggests that RA and NEN treatments may guide cells back through this critical state before driving full differentiation. Importantly, the upregulation of OXPHOS genes, mitochondrial ribosomal proteins, and translation factors along the differentiation trajectory supports our conclusion that mitochondrial homeostasis is crucial for successful differentiation.

In recent decades, substantial efforts have been directed toward targeting hyperactive glycolysis or inhibiting the relatively slow mitochondrial respiration in cancer cells. However, these strategies have largely failed due to limited efficacy and considerable toxicity to healthy tissues (2). In our study, we demonstrated that activating mitochondrial respiration is the key for tumor differentiation therapy. Mitochondrial dysfunction arises because dedifferentiated cancer cells had low ATP consumption, thereby limiting mitochondrial function. By employing uncouplers to create a pseudo-ATP demand, we were able to reactivate mitochondrial function and promote the differentiation of tumor cells. More importantly, our data revealed that the Warburg effect extends beyond cell proliferation and plays a crucial role in determining cell fate. Additionally, future studies could explore whether these differentiated neurons within tumors can develop intrinsic energy-consuming activities once treatment is withdrawn.

Study Limitations.

The combination of RA and the uncoupler achieved only partial differentiation in vivo, likely due to heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. Combining spatial transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses could help uncover these underlying factors. In addition, there are notable limitations in using current mitochondrial uncouplers due to their poor bioavailability and the narrow therapeutic window within which they function effectively. Future research should focus on identifying novel uncouplers with improved bioavailability and broader therapeutic windows, allowing for safer and more effective clinical applications. Due to funding and resource limitations, we tested only one dose combination of NEN and RA. Further investigation is also needed to refine dosing and delivery methods for improved therapeutic efficacy, as NEN and RA exhibit reduced synergistic effects at higher concentrations, as shown in the Chou-Talalay analysis (Fig. 3E). Nevertheless, our proof-of-principle study highlights the crucial role of mitochondrial homeostasis in cancer differentiation and offers a promising avenue for developing therapies that promote tumor differentiation through mitochondrial reprogramming.
