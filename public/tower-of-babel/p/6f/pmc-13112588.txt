The Spectrum of Iatrogenic Valvular Heart Disease
Authors: Giovanni Benfari, Christophe Tribouilloy, Maurice Enriquez-Sarano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
First Author

Valvular heart disease (VHD) has long been almost exclusively related to rheumatic heart disease, so that examining the cause of valve diseases used to be considered futile. With improving hygiene and the advent of antibiotics, rheumatic heart disease regressed, and with improved life expectancy, degenerative valve disease became widely predominant. Little attention was devoted to iatrogenic valve disease, but with expanding therapeutic means, it has become much more frequent and requires our attention to diagnose and possibly avoid this complication of well-intended therapies.

Drug-induced valvular heart disease (DIVHD), probably the best known iatrogenic VHD, remains an under-recognized yet pathobiologically distinct entity within the spectrum of acquired valvular disorders. A case in this issue of JACC: Case Reports illustrates the diagnostic difficulty in the absence of pathognomonic signs, association of a drug potentially causing DIVHD to an otherwise nonspecific VHD, with nonspecific pathology, diffuse valvular thickening, retraction, minimal calcification, and histologic evidence of myofibroblast proliferation.1 The case, associating long-term ergotamine exposure and valvular lesions resembling the classic phenotype of serotonergic, ergot-related valvulopathy, is strongly suggestive of DIVHD but never with absolute certainty. It is important by inviting attention to drugs as a potential, and often overlooked, driver of progressive VHD.

DIVHD was first linked to ergot derivatives in association with appetite suppressants (fenfluramine/dexfenfluramine) detected in population-based and echocardiographic surveillance studies linking them particularly to valvular regurgitations.2 Subsequently, the risk was also demonstrated for ergot-derived dopamine agonists (pergolide, cabergoline).3,4

More recently, amphetamine-derived appetite suppressants were associated with DIVHD.5 Mechanistically, all these agents share agonism at the serotonin 5-HT2B receptor, expressed on valvular interstitial cells (VICs), which mediates fibroproliferative signaling.6,7 The ergotamine involved in the present case possesses this serotonergic activity. While contemporary exposure is less common, case reports and surgical series continue to document ergotamine-associated multivalvular fibrosis, often requiring valve replacement.8 The echocardiographic signature is restricted leaflet motion with regurgitation, absence of commissural fusion, and relative paucity of calcification, differentiating this scenario from the more common degenerative or rheumatic etiologies.9, 10, 11 Notably, the histology described in this case is coherent with mechanistic evidence demonstrating VIC activation and extracellular matrix remodeling under chronic 5-HT2B stimulation.7,12 Anatomopathologically, drug-induced VHD is characterized by dense, noninflammatory fibrous thickening located on the endocardial surface of the valves, leading to leaflet stiffness and restrictive regurgitation while largely preserving the underlying valvular architecture. This pattern contrasts with rheumatic VHD, in which fibrosis is typically accompanied by inflammatory infiltrates and architectural distortion. The distribution of fibrosis, often basal and symmetric, also differs from the nodular calcific remodeling typical of degenerative aortic stenosis. Mechanistically, activation of 5-HT2B receptors on valvular interstitial cells within the serotonergic pathway promotes TGF-β–mediated signaling, cellular proliferation, and extracellular matrix deposition, processes that closely resemble those observed in carcinoid heart disease,13 as summarized in Figure 1. Despite these shared mechanisms, variable serotoninergic receptor stimulations variably affect left- or right-sided valves, without clear biological understanding of the drivers of this variability. Drugs Associated With Activation of the 5-HT2B Receptor and Downstream Fibrotic Signaling

Several pharmacological agents and their metabolites act as agonists of the serotonin 5-HT2B receptor located on the cell membrane. Activation of the receptor triggers G-protein dissociation and downstream pathways, including TGF-β receptor activation, within the intracellular space. These signaling cascades promote fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, processes that contribute to drug-induced fibrotic changes.

Another type of iatrogenic VHD, also often underdiagnosed, is radiation-induced heart disease (RIHD). Indeed, thoracic radiotherapy is often performed >10 years before the valvular diagnosis, with generally calcified valvular lesions that may be mistaken for a degenerative cause.16,17 Radiation induces endothelial injury, microvascular rarefaction, chronic inflammation, and activation of profibrotic pathways, ultimately leading to diffuse fibrotic thickening with superimposed calcification, often involving the aortic-mitral continuity and annular structures.18,19 In contrast to the predominantly noncalcific leaflet retraction observed in 5-HT2B–mediated disease, RIHD frequently evolves into fibrocalcific stenosis, particularly of the aortic valve. Importantly, valvular lesions in RIHD seldom occur in isolation and often coexist with pericardial constriction, restrictive cardiomyopathy, and premature coronary artery disease—hallmarks of the broader spectrum of radiation-associated cardiovascular injury.20

Additional forms of iatrogenic VHD are device- and procedure-related iatrogenic VHD. Chronic transvalvular lead placement (pacemaker or ICD leads) has been associated with progressive tricuspid regurgitation through leaflet impingement, perforation, or tethering, as demonstrated in echocardiographic and surgical series.21,22 Electrophysiologic ablations, myocardial biopsies, or catheterizations also rarely cause burning or perforating valvular lesions.

Collectively, these observations highlight the need for widespread awareness of iatrogenic causes of VHD and careful clinical history-taking among clinicians. In patients with restrictive valvular disease or unexplained valvular regurgitation, drug-induced causes should always be considered, and past exposure to potentially valvulotoxic substances—even many years earlier—should be carefully investigated. Mild forms of drug-induced VHD are relatively common and often present as isolated central mitral or aortic regurgitation, whereas rarer cases may include associated mitral or aortic stenosis. Continued vigilance is also required because new drugs may carry previously unrecognized valvular toxicity; therefore, compounds with potential 5-HT2B receptor agonist activity should undergo dedicated preclinical testing for valvular effects before clinical use. Finally, we can speculate that the variable occurrence of VHD among exposed patients suggests that individual susceptibility, possibly genetic, may influence the development of these lesions.
