Analytical policy commentary: Multi-prong strategies for effective climate-resilient community-based health programming in humanitarian settings and beyond
Authors: Akalewold T Gebremeskel, Mekdes Assefa, Ilja Ormel, Lillian Nyamuda, Bhanu Pratap Maurya, Salim Sohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe health impacts of climate change are increasingly evident, exacerbating health inequities and disproportionately affecting at-risk populations. Delays in addressing these challenges heighten risks, particularly in low-resource and humanitarian settings. Global health advocates call for immediate action to integrate health considerations into climate policies. Community Health Workers (CHWs) are essential in improving access to primary health services, but their roles must align with the Disaster Risk Management (DRM) framework. This commentary highlights multi-pronged strategies for effective climate-resilient community-based health programming to integrate CHWs into the national health system and DRM, fostering climate-resilient community health programs through system integration, climate-smart action, localized and evidence-based approaches, and stakeholder engagement.Keywords: Multi-pronged strategies, Disaster Risk Management (DRM), Climate-resilient actions, Climate-smart community health workforce, Community health programs, Humanitarian settings, Community Health Workers (CHWs)

Background

The climate crisis has intensified global public health emergencies, necessitating urgent health preparedness initiatives [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Climate change worsens health through increased pollution, heatwaves, wildfires, storms, floods, and infectious disease outbreaks, especially in low-income countries. It exacerbates heat-related illnesses, noncommunicable diseases, mental health issues, and maternal and neonatal health complications while increasing food insecurity and straining access to clean water and sanitation, undermining universal health coverage [[6], [7], [8]].

These impacts deepen inequities, hinder economic growth, drive migration, and perpetuate poverty, obstructing Sustainable Development Goals [1]. By 2050, climate change may cause 14.5 million additional deaths and $12.5 trillion in economic losses, with an estimated 250,000 additional deaths annually from specific climate-related causes between 2030 to 2050 [1,[9], [10], [11]]. The severity of these effects depends on healthcare systems' capacity to address climate-sensitive health risks [12]. Humanitarian assistance is already falling short, and demand may double by 2050 [[9], [10], [11]].

Global health actors advocate for integrating health considerations into climate policies [[2], [3], [4],6,7]. For over four decades, Community Health Workers (CHWs) have played a pivotal role in primary health services, particularly in humanitarian and development settings [5,13]. Humanitarian settings may include conflict zones, refugee camps, and disaster-affected areas, each presenting distinct challenges such as limited infrastructure, disrupted services, and heightened vulnerability. CHWs bridge communities and health systems, providing education, prevention, early detection, referrals, and clinical services for marginalized populations. Their roles encompass health promotion, psychosocial support, data collection, and emergency response [5]. Community health programs are globally recognized as essential for Universal Health Coverage (UHC) and health security. CHWs are uniquely positioned to provide primary health care access for disadvantaged and remote communities and build community resilience by offering culturally and contextually appropriate care within their communities, especially for marginalized populations. CHWs are at the forefront of addressing climate-sensitive health issues like malnutrition, infectious disease outbreaks (e.g., malaria, cholera), and heat-related illnesses. As CHW programs expand in low- and middle-income countries, addressing emerging climate challenges through CHW initiatives is critical to mitigate the global healthcare worker shortage and advance Universal Health Coverage [14].

CHWs operate under diverse conditions, with roles shaped by multilevel governance (Local to international), cultural norms, and available resources. CHWs are ideal community-based local actors to address geographically diverse climate risks, influencing the types of health threats communities face. CHWs are anchors of resilience in fragile health systems, including climate-induced health crises. Integrating climate action and Disaster Risk Management (DRM) into CHW programs is essential for global health security. Health equity, security, and sustainability depend on context-specific local leadership and global solidarity.

CHWs play a crucial role in responding to climate-related health impacts. While zoonotic diseases and respiratory conditions are particularly relevant due to their strong links with climate variability and past epidemic experiences, climate change also exacerbates a wide range of other health outcomes, including heat-related illnesses, malnutrition, waterborne and vector-borne diseases, maternal and neonatal complications, mental health challenges, and the rise of noncommunicable diseases. Recognizing this diversity is essential for designing comprehensive CHW strategies that address multiple climate-sensitive health risks. CHWs serve as educators and mobilizers, linking households to first-level healthcare facilities.

Beyond their role in long-term health promotion, disease prevention, and control, CHWs contribute to healthcare interventions addressing climate change impacts through prevention, health system strengthening, advocacy, education, and community-based surveillance [15]. A recent scoping review identified CHWs’ roles during extreme weather events (EWEs) in fragile health systems, including emergency preparedness, integrated risk monitoring, and early warning [5]. They provided vital services such as safe water testing, mental health referrals, coordination of maternal health emergencies, and treatment of malnutrition and waterborne diseases. CHWs enhanced health security by improving access to services, disseminating health information, and supporting surveillance efforts during the Ebola epidemic in 2014, Zika outbreak in 2015, and COVID-19 outbreaks [5,16]. This commentary reflects this complexity by drawing on global examples and proposing adaptable strategies for integrating climate-resilient actions into community health programs.

To clarify the basis of our recommendations, this commentary draws upon a scoping review of global health policies, frameworks, and peer-reviewed literature. These include the Sendai framework for disaster risk reduction, World Health Organization's (WHO) operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems, and recent scoping reviews on CHW roles in climate emergencies [3,4]. These sources provide the foundation for the strategic recommendations presented, ensuring that the arguments are grounded in established policy guidance and empirical evidence. This evidence informed the identification of essential CHW roles and strategic approaches presented in the manuscript. While not a formal policy analysis, the commentary integrates insights from policy documents and stakeholder-informed literature to ensure relevance and applicability across diverse humanitarian contexts.

Despite their critical role in long-term health programming and climate-related health responses, evidence is limited on how to align CHWs with climate emergency public health efforts throughout the DRM cycle. Disaster risk management is the application of disaster risk reduction policies and strategies to prevent new disaster risk, reduce existing disaster risk and manage residual risk, contributing to the strengthening of resilience and reduction of disaster losses [1]. The following section explores embedding CHWs in the DRM cycle and strategies for integrating climate action into CHW programming.

Embedding potential roles of CHWs throughout the DRM cycle

CHWs can play a critical role during climate emergency public health throughout the DRM [17], including pre-disaster preparedness and anticipatory action, during-disaster emergency response and risk reduction, post-disaster recovery and rehabilitation, and building resilience during blue sky periods (time when there is no emergency or we are not dealing with an emergency) with agile strategies for climate-resilient and climate-smart community-based health programming [4], [8], [17], [18], [19], [20]. These phases provide a structured framework for reducing disaster risks and strengthening community resilience by guiding coordinated actions across preparedness, response, recovery, and long-term adaptation. In humanitarian settings—such as conflict zones, refugee camps, and areas affected by natural or man-made crises—this framework helps ensure that health services remain accessible, responsive, and equitable. It guides and enables CHWs and other frontline actors to anticipate risks, mobilize resources, and deliver timely interventions that protect vulnerable populations and support sustainable recovery efforts. Blue sky periods encompass times of relative stability when no immediate disaster is occurring, allowing for proactive planning and resilience-building activities.

i.Preparedness and anticipatory action: The WHO evidence highlights CHWs' role in early warning systems, community education, and proactive disaster planning in fragile settings. CHWs often operate in environments with limited access to formal training and emergency supplies. Their lived experience navigating community trust, cultural sensitivities, and informal networks allows them to mobilize local knowledge and resources effectively during preparedness efforts. Pre-disaster, CHWs can strengthen preparedness and proactive responses to health impacts caused by climate crises [2,5,8,17]. Their roles include:•Climate vulnerability assessment: Assist in identifying at-risk individuals and communities through routine data collection.•Health education and community outreach: Share disaster preparedness information, awareness-raising, risk communication, evacuation plans and first aid, leadership, and coordination•Early case detection: Conduct early case detection, contact tracing, and quarantine follow-up.•Training: Provide basic disaster response training, including first aid and cardiopulmonary resuscitation.•Communication: Build relationships with community leaders to facilitate information sharing and community engagement.•Disaster plans: Collaborate to create tailored disaster response plans.

ii.Response and risk reduction: Evidence shows CHWs' effectiveness in emergency response, including supply distribution, health education, and evacuation support. During crises, CHWs are frequently the first responders in remote or underserved areas. Their familiarity with local terrain, dialects, and social structures enables them to deliver timely aid and communicate critical health information, even when formal systems are overwhelmed. During a disaster, CHWs provide essential support, including needs assessment, health education, outreach, and access to services while reducing risks [5], [18], [19], [20]. Their roles include:•Immediate response: Provide first aid and distribute essential supplies.•Needs assessment: Identify critical needs for food, water, shelter, and medical care and contribute to ongoing monitoring during emergencies.•Communication relay: Connect communities to emergency responders and translate information.•Health education and community outreach: Provide health education, awareness creation, and coordination and counter misinformation.•Referral and support: Offer emotional and psychosocial support to those in distress.•Evacuation assistance: Help vulnerable populations during evacuations.

Multi-pronged strategies for effective climate-resilient community-based health programming

Effective climate-resilient community-based health programming requires multi-pronged strategies that are holistic, systemic, and community-driven. Integrating climate action into community health programs through the Disaster Risk Management (DRM) cycle is essential for effective climate emergency response. Strategies for climate change adaptation ensure resilient, climate-smart health programs that protect vulnerable populations and mitigate adverse health effects [[21], [22], [23]]. CHWs often face systemic barriers to formal recognition despite their frontline contributions. Their lived experience underscores the need for inclusive policies that value community-based knowledge and integrate CHWs into decision-making processes.

This review highlights strategic approaches to integrate climate-resilient strategies into community health programs by aligning them with the disaster management framework. Key strategies include integrating CHW programs into health systems, building a climate-resilient workforce, ensuring evidence-based approaches, and engaging stakeholders.

i.Integration of CHW programs into health systems: Despite consensus on the value of CHWs, their formal integration remains a challenge, leading to uneven implementation [24]. Embedding CHWs within health systems and climate resilience initiatives improves access, reduces disparities, and promotes prevention, particularly for marginalized populations. CHWs play a vital role in DRM by identifying vulnerabilities, educating communities, mobilizing responses, and supporting recovery efforts [15,[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. Climate adaptation should be incorporated into health policies, CHW training, and capacity building. Adopting WHO’s "Operational framework for building climate-resilient and low-carbon health systems" optimizes resources through governance and emergency preparedness [26]. CHWs enhance resilience by promoting climate risk awareness, urban greening, food security, and sustainable energy use. Tailoring response plans to local climate challenges, with active participation from marginalized groups, strengthens social capital and grassroots resilience [9,25,26]. In resource-constrained settings, CHWs can realistically support integration by identifying local health vulnerabilities, facilitating community education, and coordinating with local health authorities. While they may not lead policy design, their grassroots insights are essential for informing inclusive health planning.

ii.Alignment of climate action into CHW programming and smart community health workforce: Alignment of climate action into CHW programming involves integrating climate resilience and sustainability measures into existing CHW activities, capitalizing on their trusted position within communities to promote both adaptation (preparing for climate impacts) and mitigation (reducing emissions). The concept of smart community health workforce refers to an enhanced, climate-competent workforce equipped with the necessary training, data tools, and systemic support to effectively address complex climate-related health challenges. CHWs frequently work under extreme conditions without adequate protection or support. Their resilience and adaptability highlight the importance of investing in climate-smart infrastructure, mental health resources, and continuous training tailored to local challenges. A climate-resilient workforce is essential for strengthening localized health programs and managing climate-related health impacts [25,27]. Key conditions include:•Improved working conditions: CHWs require adequate resources, infrastructure, and leadership, including early warning systems, climate risk monitoring tools, solar power, and fire mitigation measures.•Knowledge and skills empowerment: Training, mentorship, climate-focused curricula, and communication plans enhance preparedness for climate emergencies while addressing CHWs' health and safety concerns [25,[28], [29], [30]].•Adaptive support strategies: CHWs need psychosocial support, mental health training, and well-being strategies to sustain healthcare delivery amid climate challenges [29,31,32].

CHWs can contribute to climate resilience by engaging local communities in localized service provision, including training, using basic climate monitoring tools, and applying adaptive practices such as heat mitigation and water safety education. However, the deployment of advanced infrastructure and technologies should be led by health system planners and technical experts [8].

Ensuring a localized and evidence-driven approach: CHWs employ an evidence-driven approach by leveraging their unique position for localized data collection, community engagement, and locally tailored response to anticipate and address climate-related health hazards. CHWs play a crucial role in community engagement and data collection to anticipate climate-related health hazards. They facilitate and support early case detection, surveillance, and evaluation of climate-resilient health programs. CHWs contribute rich qualitative insights that complement quantitative data. Their lived experience in observing disease patterns and environmental changes enhances surveillance systems and informs context-specific interventions. Empowering the CHWs' data collection capacity and developing CHW-aligned surveillance and integrating climate data into health information systems enhances early warning capabilities and response mechanisms [5]. Strategies include climate vulnerability assessments, trend analyses, and real-time community-based disease monitoring. Ensuring CHWs are equipped with data-driven decision-making tools, targeted training, and technological support is key to effective intervention [13].

CHWs are well-positioned to collect community-level data, report early warning signs, and support disease surveillance using mobile tools and simplified reporting systems. They are not expected to conduct complex trend analyses but can collaborate with public health experts and data analysts to inform timely interventions (Fig. 2).

i.Optimization of multi-stakeholder engagement and investment: Multisectoral collaboration and investment in CHW-led climate resilience initiatives are essential pillars. CHWs often act as bridges between communities and external stakeholders. Their lived experience navigating power dynamics and resource gaps emphasizes the importance of participatory engagement and sustained investment in grassroots leadership. Mobilizing resources, advocating for funding, and building partnerships are essential for climate-resilient health programs. Collaboration across sectors with NGOs, development organizations, and private sector actors can secure technical, financial, and logistical support [25,30]. CHWs can engage stakeholders by facilitating community dialogues, identifying local priorities, and advocating for resources that reflect lived realities. While they may not manage funding portfolios, their experience navigating local dynamics is critical for shaping equitable and sustainable investment strategies [9,10]. Engaging communities in participatory decision-making strengthens resilience and collective impact.

Strengthening climate-resilient health systems in humanitarian settings requires a multidimensional approach that connects frontline actors with broader policy and system-level strategies. Integration enables CHWs to serve as the operational link between community needs and formal health systems. In humanitarian settings, this connection ensures that climate-resilient policies are grounded in local realities and supported by CHW-led outreach and surveillance [11]. Workforce development supports integration by equipping CHWs with the skills and tools needed to implement climate-resilient strategies. In practice, this includes training CHWs in early warning systems and psychosocial support, which are essential in disaster-prone and resource-limited settings [3,8]. Evidence generation by CHWs informs both workforce development and system integration. In humanitarian contexts, CHWs can collect real-time data and share insights that guide adaptive planning and resource allocation [4,12]. Stakeholder engagement ensures that integration, workforce development, and evidence use are adequately resourced and contextually relevant. CHWs play a key role in facilitating community participation and advocating for inclusive investment strategies [9,10]. Taken together, these interlinked strategies position CHWs as pivotal actors in advancing climate-resilient, community-driven health systems in fragile and humanitarian contexts.

Conclusion

Climate change necessitates urgent multi-pronged strategies for effective climate-resilient community-based health programming. Policy and decision-makers should integrate climate-resilient strategies into community health programs by aligning them with DRM frameworks. Approaches include embedding CHWs into health systems, fostering alignment of climate action into CHW programming and a climate-resilient workforce, leveraging localized and evidence-based methods, and engaging stakeholders to secure investments that protect and improve community health in an unstable climate.
