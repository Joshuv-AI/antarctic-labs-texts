Linking soil enzymes and microbial community dynamics with organic carbon fluctuations for sustaining the soil health
Authors: Manisha Negi, Pardeep Kumar, Anjali Chauhan, Saurabh Sharma, Happy Dev Sharma, Kapil Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In the mountainous regions, where irrigation water is scarce and temperatures are less than ideal, we aimed to explore the combined effects of mulching and organic manure on organic carbon fluctuations, microbial populations and enzymatic activities in soil under ginger. A two-year field study (2021 and 2022) was conducted at Dr. YS Parmar University, Solan, Himachal Pradesh, using a split plot design. Main plot treatments included grass (straw) mulch, black plastic mulch (25 and 100 µ thickness). Sub plot treatments consisted of farmyard manure (FYM), vermicompost (VC) and sheep-goat manure (SGM), alone or in combination. Grass mulch showed the highest soil organic carbon content (19.02 g kg− 1), viable bacteria (132.65 × 106 ± 1.66 cfu g− 1 soil), fungi (35.73 × 103 ± 1.86 cfu g− 1 soil), actinomycetes count (45.50 × 103 ± 0.78 cfu g− 1 soil) and enzymatic activities (646.17 ± 8.77 µg of p-NPP g− 1 soil hr− 1 acid phosphatase, 6.16 ± 0.15 mg TPF g− 1 soil per 24 h dehydrogenase and 17.84 ± 0.20 mg NH3-N g− 1 soil per hr urease) among mulches. Among the sub plot treatments, FYM combined with 50% RDF (recommended dose of fertilizers) through FYM and SGM generated the best results in terms of soil organic carbon (17.63 g kg− 1) microbial counts (135.40 × 106 ± 2.58 cfu g− 1 soil viable bacteria, 39.93 × 103 ± 1.92 cfu g− 1 soil viable fungi and 44.48 × 103 ± 0.99 cfu g− 1 soil) and enzymatic activities (524.06 ± 11.33 µg of p-NPP g− 1 soil hr− 1 acid phosphatase, 6.55 ± 0.10 mg TPF g− 1 soil per 24 h dehydrogenase and 18.04 ± 0.17 mg NH3-N g− 1 soil per hr urease). Positive correlations were observed between soil organic carbon, microbial biomass carbon, microbial populations and enzymatic activities. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed two key components explaining 94.5% of the total variance in soil health parameters under mulching and manuring, with PC1 (79.4%) dominated by organic carbon, microbial counts and enzymatic activities and PC2 (15.1%) mainly associated with enzymatic functions. The synergistic effects of grass mulch and FYM with SGM provide a blueprint for resilient soil ecosystems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43619-0.

Introduction

The terrestrial ecosystems contain an immense reservoir of soil organic carbon (SOC), holding an estimated 379 petagrams (Pg) of carbon (C) globally, which surpasses the combined C stocks present in the atmosphere and phytomass1–3. The vital role of SOC pools in terrestrial ecosystems has been demonstrated by current research, which affects the physical and chemical characteristics of soil as well as the dynamics of microfloral communities4. Furthermore, the build-up and mineralization of SOC profoundly impact the C balance, with SOC mineralization being a significant contributor to the elevation of atmospheric CO2 levels5,6. Owing to the cumulative influence of SOC on soil quality and the global climate, preserving and increasing SOC has become a top priority in modern land management efforts and legislative initiatives7–10.

Researchers are increasingly recognizing the vital functions that soil micro-flora play in maintaining the ecological stability of agricultural systems11. The decomposition of organic waste, the cycling of nutrients and the enhancement of plant nutrition availability are all attributed to soil microbes. These microbial communities are influenced by abiotic conditions, agricultural management techniques and plant species12. The level of SOC in agro-ecosystems is regulated by the equilibrium between C intake through residual plant matter and C losses, primarily through the process of decomposition, known as heterotrophic respiration in soil13,14. Farm management strategies that amplify inputs, such as organic manuring and/or diminish decomposition rates, such as mulching, are envisaged to increase SOC stocks13.

Soil moisture levels have a pivotal function in soil aggregation, microbial and enzymatic activity expression15–17, which can potentially impact SOC levels18. Notably, in soils with low moisture levels, microbial activity is hindered due to the limited diffusion of substrates19. Conversely, improved hydrologic connectivity fosters greater microbial access to substrates, thereby enhancing soil respiration rates in moist soil conditions20,21. Temperature variations influence enzyme reaction rates by affecting activation energy22. By influencing microbial activity, temperature also has a direct impact on microbial respiration of the soil23 and modifies how microorganisms use C as a substrate24,25. To regulate extreme variations in soil temperature and moisture loss due to evaporation, organic and inorganic materials, ‘mulch’, are used to cover the soil. It is crucial to take into account the thickness of the plastic film used as mulch, as thin mulches (25 µ) may be cheaper but comparatively less durable and may tear during field operations. Conversely, thick mulches (up to 100 µ), although three times as expensive as thin mulches, can be reused for the subsequent crop, as they have high strength and longevity and tearing issues are less likely.

Organic matter is a crucial factor that impacts the quantity of CO2 in the atmosphere26–28. The application of organic manuring is widely acknowledged as the most effective method to enhance soil organic matter (SOM) content and promote soil fertility29–31. The particular organic materials used have a significant influence on how organic manures affect soil microorganisms32,33. Applying organic manure such as, sheep-goat manure (SGM), farm yard manure (FYM) and vermicompost (VC) to the soil can help replenish SOM and enhance nutrient cycling. Farmers can increase crop yields and simultaneously enhance soil fertility and structure, retaining more water in the soil and lowering erosion by using organic manure34.

Ginger (Zingiber officinale Roscoe), having its place in the Zingiberaceae family is cultivated for its rhizome. Its history dates back over 3000 years and it has been utilized as a staple food, spice, supplement and flavour enhancer across various cultures and regions35–37. Ginger is shade-loving plant, can suffer from radiation stress in regions with intense sunlight due to factors like latitude and climate38, which can also cause radiation stress and various growth issues, such as leaf tip burn and reduced yield39. Recent studies suggest that cultivating these crops in shaded environments enhances key factors such as transpiration, photosynthesis and growth, thereby improving yield and dry matter build-up40–42. Intercropping systems offer an alternative way to optimize land use and available shade, particularly for small land holdings in mountainous areas where cultivated land is under increasing stress. This also allows farmers to cultivate more than one crop simultaneously on the same plot of land, boosting their financial returns.

Ginger is a usual under-story or intercropping plant in tree based and orchard systems as it is a shade tolerant rhizomatous spice crop that is physiologically adapted to high humidity microclimates and partial canopy38. Ginger is also a suitable crop for agroforestry and as an orchard component, the latter of which has reported input from rhizome development combined with constant plant residue supply and a soil biological life enhanced by constant organic C inputs, reduced temperature variations at the soil surface and control of radiation43. Mulching is an essential management technique for ginger production, especially in shady areas. It provides a significant amount of labile C that controls soil moisture regimes, microbial biomass and residue derived SOC pools44. The combined effects of mulch application and tree canopy further alter soil microclimate and microbiological activities in perennial orchard systems like apricot, resulting in conditions that significantly control SOC turnover and stability38,44. Therefore, a physiologically relevant and management-realistic model system for examining SOC microbial interactions under interacting influences of shade, perennial tree covern and organic C inputs is ginger grown under apricot with organic mulching.

We intended to investigate the combined impacts of mulching techniques and various organic manures on soil characteristics, microbial populations and enzymatic activities in the mid-hill region of Himachal Pradesh, India, where irrigation water is limited and temperatures are below optimal. The research gap lies in the fact that while mulching and organic manures have been examined separately, their combined impacts on soil microbial abundance, enzyme activity and SOC dynamics in perennial orchard based ginger systems are poorly understood. There is no comparative research on how organic manures change in nutrient composition and decomposition behaviour under organic versus plastic mulches, especially in apricot based intercropping systems in cool, rain-limited mid-hill habitats. The rationale of this study is to evaluate how soil biological characteristics and system sustainability under conservation agriculture are impacted by the use of organic manures and mulching techniques. Through perpetual root turnover, ongoing litter deposition and a controlled microclimate, apricot-based intercropping modifies microbial habitats and below ground C inputs, in contrast to annual cropping systems. Enzyme activity, microbial movement and SOC stability can all be specifically regulated by these system-specific circumstances. This study is interesting because it clarifies the integrated SOC enzyme microbial reactions to combined organic mulching and manuring techniques in an apricot - ginger system of the mid-hill Himalayan agroecosystem, which has not been thoroughly studied. It is hypothesized that in comparison to plastic mulch, grass mulch along with organic manures would raise SOC by around 15–25 per cent and enhance microbiological and enzymatic activities by at least 10 per cent. This is based on previous results45–47 indicating increased SOC buildup under organic mulches.

Materials and methods

An outline of the field of study

The experimental fields was positioned within the premises of the Department of Soil Science and Water Management at Dr. YS Parmar University of Horticulture and Forestry in Nauni, Solan, Himachal Pradesh (HP), India (Fig. 1). Geographically, the site is positioned at 30° 52´ North latitude and 77° 11´ East longitude, falling within Agroclimatic Zone-II in the sub-temperate, sub-humid agro-climatic zone of HP. The region gets an average yearly rainfall of approximately 1100 mm, with 75% of precipitation occurring during the monsoon period spanning from mid-June to mid-September. Conversely, winter rains are scarce and confined to the months of January and February. The soil composition within the experimental area conforms to the Typic Eutrochrept classification at the subgroup level as per the Soil Taxonomy of USDA. The soil texture is sandy clay loam, composed of 57.2, 22.0 and 20.8 per cent proportions of sand, silt and clay, respectively. Additionally, the soil was towards slight acidity in reaction with a pH (soil: water ratio of 1:2.5 w/v) value of 6.30 and maintained a normal electrical conductivity of 0.302 dS m-1. The experimental soil before the experimentation was also characterized by a SOC content of 13.12 g kg-1 and microbial biomass carbon (MBC) of 231.86 µg g-1 soil. The measured viable bacterial count for the site was 101.60 × 106 cfu g-1 soil, the fungal count stood at 14.60 × 103 cfu g-1 soil, while the actinomycetes count was 24.50 × 103 cfu g-1 soil. Enzyme activity on the site indicated acid phosphatase levels at 472.86 µg of p-NPP g-1 soil hr-1. Dehydrogenase activity measured 4.33 mg TPF g-1 soil per 24 h and urease activity was recorded 13.74 mg NH3-N g-1 soil per hr.

Experimental design and field layout

The research was performed over two consecutive years, 2021 and 2022, using a split plot design. It comprised 3 main plot treatments and 4 sub plot treatments, each replicated 4 times. The elaboration of these treatments are outlined in Table 1. The split plot design was implemented following standard experimental design principles to ensure replication validity, proper randomization, and control of spatial heterogeneity. Each mulch × manure treatment combination was replicated four times using independent experimental units, allowing reliable estimation of treatment effects and experimental error. Randomization was conducted in two stages, wherein mulching treatments were first randomly assigned to main plots within each replication, followed by random allocation of organic manure treatments within each main plot48. Spatial heterogeneity was addressed through blocking (local control) by grouping experimental units into replication blocks that were as homogeneous as possible along underlying soil and micro-environmental gradients. To avoid erroneous linear blocking, each replication block contained the full set of treatments, thereby minimizing the influence of unknown or multidirectional spatial heterogeneity on treatment comparisons. The field preparation was thorough and comprehensive, ensuring optimal soil aeration and structure. The soil was ploughed to a depth of 15–20 cm and refined with a tractor to facilitate optimal conditions for plant growth. Planking was performed a few days before sowing the rhizomes and the entire field was partitioned into main plots and sub plots, resulting in a total of forty-eight beds measuring 3 m × 1 m each.

Treatment application

Before application, FYM, VC and SGM were subjected to elemental analysis for total organic carbon (TOC) and total N using a CHNS analyzer. The results showed that TOC concentrations were 13.33% in FYM, 30.96% in VC and 60.40% in SGM, whereas total N contents were 0.43, 1.29 and 3.02%, respectively. To ensure consistent nutrient supply across treatments, the application rates of organic manures were standardized on a N equivalent basis. Based on the total N content of each amendment, manure quantities were determined to satisfy the necessary N dose of 100 kg N ha⁻¹ for ginger. In order to provide the crop with an identical amount of nitrogen, FYM (0.43% N), VC (1.29% N) and SGM (3.02% N) were treated at rates proportionate to their N contents. To allow for partial breakdown in the field, all manures were evenly introduced two weeks prior to sowing. The uniform field conditions and regular application time made it possible to compare the impacts of manure on soil biological characteristics, even though decomposition rates were not explicitly measured. After the soil was adequately prepared and the manures were applied, mulching was executed with precision. This encompassed using two types of inorganic mulch (black plastic mulch with a film thickness of 25 µ i.e.PM1 and black plastic mulch with a film thickness of 100 µ i.e. PM2) along with an organic straw mulch material (grass mulch i.e. GM) for each bed. Subsequent to the mulching process, holes were established on the mulch sheets at optimal spacing to facilitate the sowing of ginger rhizomes.

Sowing and intercultural operations

The apricot block of the organic experimental unit of department of Soil Science and Water Management, Dr. YS Parmar University of Horticulture and Forestry, Nauni, Solan was used for the experimentation. The apricot trees were 9 years old and these are typically planted with a 4.5 m spacing in rows that are 3.5 m apart, providing ample inter-row space for the cultivation of vegetable intercrops. In this particular case, ginger rhizomes (Solan Giriganga variety procured from department of Seed Science and Technology, Dr. YS Parmar university of Horticulture and Forestry Nauni, Solan) were sown in the differently mulched inter-row space of the apricot orchard. The seed rate for ginger rhizomes was 18–22 q ha− 1, with a row spacing of 30 cm and 20 cm within each row. Each bed contained five rows, with ten rhizomes planted in each row. After planting, the ginger rhizomes were given a light irrigation, followed by intermittent irrigations every 10–12 days based on the soil’s moisture levels. The first weeding was carried out before applying manure and mulch to soil and further weeding activities continued if necessary to maintain crop health also with mulching. Manual weeding was preferred over other methods to prevent any damage to the ginger rhizomes.

Soil analysis

Soil samples from a depth of 0 to 15 cm were systematically collected in a zigzag pattern from each treatment plot during the ginger harvest season for two consecutive years. Samples from each treatment were kept separate, thoroughly mixed within their respective treatment and then coarsely ground using a mortar and pestle to ensure uniform particle size distribution. The investigation was conducted on fresh soil samples in accordance with the methodologies outlined below:

Viable microbial (bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes) count

The serial dilution and pour plate techniques described by Subbarao51 were used to isolate viable bacteria, actinomycetes and fungi from soil samples. Nutrient agar, Potato dextrose agar and Kenknight’s medium were used for culturing bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes, respectively. Sterilized media were autoclaved, cooled to 45 °C, poured into sterile petri plates and allowed to solidify. One gram of 2 mm-sieved soil was added to 9 ml of sterile water and shaken for 15–20 min. Serial dilutions from 10⁻² to 10⁻⁸ were prepared and 0.1 ml aliquots from selected dilutions were spread on solidified media using a sterile glass spreader. The plates were incubated in an inverted position at 28 °C for 2–4 days. Colony growth was recorded and expressed as colony-forming units (cfu g− 1 soil).

Acid phosphatase activity

Acid phosphatase activity was measured using p-nitrophenyl phosphate (p-NPP) as the substrate, following the method outlined by Schneider et al.52. For this, 5 g of moist soil was incubated with 20 ml of acetate buffer (pH 5.2) and 100 mM p-NPP (p-nitrophenol) at 30 °C for 30 min. To stop the enzymatic reaction, 1 ml of CaCl₂ and 4 ml of 0.2 M NaOH were added post-incubation. The resulting absorbance was recorded at 405 nm using a spectrophotometer. Acid phosphatase activity was expressed as µg of p-NPP hydrolyzed per gram of soil per hour at 30 °C.

Dehydrogenase activity

Dehydrogenase activity was assessed following the method of Casida et al.53 using 2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) as the substrate. One gram of field-moist, homogenized soil was mixed with 2.5 ml phosphate buffer, 0.2 g CaCO₃ and 1 ml of 3% TTC (v/w) in test tubes and incubated at 25 °C for 24 h. After incubation, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was discarded. The formed triphenylformazan (TPF) was extracted twice with 5 ml methanol, shaken thoroughly and the pooled extract was transferred to clean tubes. Absorbance was measured at 485 nm and results were expressed as mg TPF produced per gram of oven-dry soil per 24 h.

Urease activity

Urease activity was measured using urea as the substrate, following the method of Yao et al.54. Five grams of moist soil were incubated with 1 ml of methylbenzene, 10 ml of 10% urea and 20 ml of citrate buffer (pH 6.7) at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, 1 ml of the filtered soil extract was mixed with 1 ml of sodium phenolate and 3 ml of sodium hypochlorite, then diluted to 50 ml. The absorbance was recorded at 578 nm using a spectrophotometer. Urease activity was expressed as NH₃-N released per gram of soil per hour at 37 °C.

Statistical analysis

A split-plot design was used to precisely record, tabulate and evaluate the data. R-Studio (R version 4.5.1), Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) were used for statistical analyses. The effects of mulching treatments (main plots) and manure treatments (sub plots) were assessed using a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) suitable for split plot layout. Data were checked for homogeneity of variances and normality prior to ANOVA and microbial count data were log10-transformed when necessary to satisfy model assumptions. Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was used to compare treatment means at P ≤ 0.05. The data are shown as mean ± standard error, with various letters denoting significant differences. While principal component analysis (PCA) was used on z-score standardized data to remove scale effects and visualize multivariate associations across treatments, Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to investigate relationships between SOC fractions, microbial populations and enzyme activities.

Results

SOC and SOC stock

An initial examination of the data obtained for SOC, elucidated in Fig. 2a, has shown that different organic manures and mulching practices had a significant impact on SOC levels during the entire study. The highest SOC (19.02 g kg− 1) was recorded under GM in the main plot treatments, while the lowest (15.16 g kg− 1) was recorded under PM1. Among the sub plot treatments, the significantly highest SOC (17.63 g kg− 1) was observed under S3 (recommended FYM + 50% RDF through FYM + 50% RDF through SGM), which was found to be statistically similar (17.34 g kg− 1) to S4 (recommended FYM + 50% RDF through VC + 50% RDF through SGM), while the lowest (16.21 g kg− 1) was recorded under Control i.e., S1 (recommended FYM + RDF through FYM). In the analysis of SOC stock (Fig. 2b), the pooled data demonstrated a significant increase in SOC levels under GM treatment, reaching 5.71 kg, while the lowest SOC stock was observed under PM1 treatment. Further analysis of the sub plot treatments showed that the highest SOC stock was achieved under S3 treatment at 5.29 kg, which was statistically equivalent to SOC stock levels under S4 treatment, measuring 5.20 kg. Conversely, the lowest SOC stock among the sub plot treatments was recorded under S1 treatment, registering at 4.86 kg. The interactions between mulches and manures were found to be non-significant in all cases.

Viable bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes count and MBC

Examining the data in Table 2, a notable impact of various organic manure and mulches on soil microbial populations was observed. A pooled data analysis indicated that among the main plot treatments, GM had the highest bacterial count at 138.65 × 106 ± 1.66 cfu g− 1 soil, while PM1 had the lowest count at 115.98 × 106 ± 1.32 cfu g− 1 soil. In the sub plot treatments, S3 (recommended FYM + 50% RDF through FYM + 50% RDF through SGM) had the highest viable bacterial count at 135.40 × 106 ± 2.58 cfu g− 1 soil. The bacterial count was at its lowest under S1, the control treatment with recommended FYM + RDF through FYM, at 119.57 × 106 ± 2.03 cfu g− 1 soil. Regarding viable fungal counts, the main plot treatments showed GM with the highest count of 35.73 × 103 ± 1.86 cfu g− 1 soil and PM1 with the lowest at 27.49 × 103 ± 1.40 cfu g− 1 soil. Discussion

SOC and SOC stock

Organic mulch has the potential to retain a higher proportion of OM within the soil in comparison to plastic mulch55. This retention of OM may contribute to an increase in SOC concentration. By lowering temperature (grass mulch recorded minimum soil temperature of 17–25 °C and maximum soil temperature of 26–31 °C) and preserving more water-filled pore space, organic mulching might have modified the soil microclimate. This directly would have controlled the microbial respiration rates and enzyme kinetics, especially by lowering the activity of oxidative enzymes like phenol oxidase and peroxidase that are in charge of quick SOM mineralization56. Surki et al.57 observed the highest SOC content beneath the shaded canopy of almond trees. This outcome may be due to the increased rates of leaf litter deposition and expansion of plant roots, which could promote enhanced C accumulation in the soil. Additionally, the grass mulch have resulted in reduced soil temperatures (under grass mulch, soil temperature during both years remained lower, ranging from 17 to 25 °C minimum soil temperature and 26–31 °C maximum soil temperature), which might have slowed down the decomposition rate of SOM, leading to the accumulation of SOC. Sheep manure application was shown to significantly increase SOC content by Liu et al.58. Through physiological and physical stabilization routes, manure derived organic substrates further might have promoted microbial biomass synthesis and aggregate associated C protection, strengthening SOC persistence59. Saha and Ghosh,60 also noted an increase in SOC stock within organic mulch.

Viable bacteria, fungi and actinomycetes count and MBC

The higher viable microbial count and MBC observed under the organic mulch, as opposed to black plastic mulch, may be attributed to the increased OM content facilitated by GM. The GM could have provided an abundant food supply for soil microbes, promoting their growth and activity61. Through improved substrate driven C uptake, organic mulching may increase the supply of particulate and dissolved organic C fractions, which may preferentially boost copiotrophic microbial groups and lead to increased microbial biomass growth (Cotrufo et al., 2012)59. Tu et al.62 found a positive correlation between the direct C inputs associated with organic mulching and improved soil moisture levels. In the present study, grass mulch effectively maintained good soil moisture levels during both years of the study, averaging 14.4–15.3 per cent in the upper soil layer (0–7.5 cm) and 12.8–13.1 per cent in the lower layer (7.5–15 cm). This was achieved by reducing surface evaporation, thereby mitigating the adverse impact of soil desiccation on microbial populations, particularly during the summer season. This resulted in an increased microbial abundance. The application of OM was associated with an increase in microbial count, which may be attributable to multiple sources. In order to facilitate the depolymerization of organic substrates and the consequent accumulation of microbial biomass, organic inputs may provide reduced C compounds and organically complexed nutrients that could drive the synthesis of extracellular C and N acquiring enzymes63. The existence of vital nutrients, vitamins, amino acids and chemicals that promote growth, such as gibberellic acid and indole-3-acetic acid, may have contributed to this increase. These substances likely provided nutrition to rhizospheric microorganisms, supporting their growth, leading to an overall increase in population. This finding aligns with results reported by Larkin64 who showed that biological amendments can effectively introduce microorganisms into natural soil environments.

Soil enzymes

The variation in enzymes under different treatments could be ascribed to organic manure constitution used65. For example, the soil supplemented with SGM showed an elevated intensity of urease activity compared to other manures may be linked to the higher N content present in SGM. The higher availability of mineralizable organic N in SGM may act as a regulatory signal for urease synthesis, as urease activity is commonly induced in response to increased urea and organic N substrates, thereby accelerating N mineralization processes. Paramesh et al.,66 also confirmed the higher enzymatic activities in an intercropping technique based on arecanuts with the use of organic supplements. The utilization of GM resulted in an augmented enzymatic activity in the soil, which may be credited to the increased addition and mineralization of organic residues from the mulch, as well as the decomposition of litter. The introduction of GM might have provided an additional N source, which likely would have supplied the sufficient N to support the soil microbes. Additional N inputs may reduce microbial N limitation, allowing microorganisms to direct absorbed N toward protein synthesis, including the creation of extracellular enzymes, rather than diverting metabolic energy toward resource acquisition mechanisms67. This, in turn, might have alleviated the race for N between microorganisms and plants, leading to an enhancement in microbial activity within the soil. As a measure of intracellular oxidative metabolism, dehydrogenase activity may rise in response to increased availability of decomposable substrates provided by organic amendments. Our results align with those reported in the study by Sun et al.68. Jadhav et al.69 also testified a higher activity of dehydrogenase by the utilization of organic manure. Our outcomes are consistent with those found by Luo et al.70; Masto et al.71; Bol et al.72.

Correlation and regression studies

Soil aggregates are pivotal centres for micro floral activity and OC retention, as noted by Young and Ritz73. The pore structure of soil has a major impact on microbial activity, which microbes themselves can reshape during the decomposition of OM, as highlighted by De Gryze et al.74. The dynamic interaction between SOC and pore structure can significantly affect microbial diversity and enzymatic activity. The increased pore space resulting from higher organic C content likely fosters greater microbial and enzymatic activity, creating a synergistic relationship among soil structure, organic C dynamics and microbial function75. Research by Ding et al.76 indicates that the quantity and quality of SOM are critical factors determining the composition of soil microbial populations. The intricate link between fungal and bacterial variations underscores their interdependence in soil ecosystems. Fungi play a pivotal role in shaping bacterial communities through trophic interactions77 with studies showing that fungal inoculation selects specific bacterial populations and fungal mycelia exudates can directly influence bacterial assemblages. Furthermore, fungi can serve as carriers for bacterial spread, as illustrated by78. Such coordinated fungal-bacterial interactions may enhance functional complementarity within the microbial community, allowing several species to contribute to similar enzymatic pathways, implying functional redundancy in C rich environments79.

Our study demonstrated a positive correlation between soil microbes and phosphatase activity, suggesting that microorganisms are key players in this enzymatic process, as plant roots typically do not release significant amounts of phosphatases. This significant enzyme-microbial interaction may be due to functional specialization, in which select microbial groups actively invest in phosphatase production in response to phosphorus need rather than uniform enzyme expression throughout the community80. The release of organically bound phosphate involves the coordinated action of phytases and other phosphatases81. Soil dehydrogenase activity provides insights into soil microbial activity, serving as an indicator of microbial respiration rates. This parameter is a useful measure for assessing overall microbial function within the soil environment82,83. Our study found a positive correlation between microbial count and dehydrogenase activity, likely due to a higher microbial count driving increased respiration rates. The broad relationship between microbial abundance and dehydrogenase activity may indicate functional redundancy, as dehydrogenase activity represents cumulative metabolic activity across distinct microbial species. Urease is a critical enzyme in soil, breaking down urea into ammonia and CO2, thus playing a role in N transformations84. The connection between soil microbes and urease activity may be credited to the dominance of heterotrophic bacteria in the soil, which depend on organic substrates for energy and C and represent almost 50 per cent of the organisms efficient in generating urease85. The reported microbe-urease association may indicate partial functional specialization, considering that urease synthesis is frequently restricted to specific heterotrophic bacterial groups80. Comparable results were documented by Kiasari et al.86, reinforcing the prominence of soil microbes in the context of urease activity and soil health.

Principle component analyses

The first principal component (PC1) explained 79.4 per cent of the total variance and consisted of SOC, SOC stock, MBC, viable bacterial count, viable actinomycetes count, viable fungal count, phosphatase, dehydrogenase and urease activity. The second principal component (PC2) accounted for 15.1 per cent of the variance and was mainly related to enzymatic activities (phosphatase, dehydrogenase and urease). Thus, PC1 reflected the overall improvement in soil microbial and C dynamics influenced by mulching and manure application, whereas PC2 represented enzymatic variations independent of these treatments. Conclusion

The study found that grass mulch significantly improved soil biological properties when compared to plastic mulching, increasing SOC by 11.25%, viable bacterial count by 10.44%, viable fungal count by 13.62%, viable actinomycetes count by 15.38% and microbial biomass carbon by 0.80%, indicating improved carbon retention and microbial substrate availability. Among manurial treatments, the combination of recommended FYM + 50% RDF through FYM + 50% RDF through sheep goat manure proved most effective, resulting in 1.67% higher SOC, 6.02% higher viable bacterial count, 17.44% higher viable fungal count, 10.54% higher viable actinomycetes count and 5.15% higher microbial biomass carbon compared to other manurial combinations, demonstrating the synergistic effect of diverse organic inputs on microbial proliferation. SOC mediated microbial enzymatic interaction is the main mechanism controlling soil biological responses under mulching and manuring practices, according to strong positive correlations (r = 0.62–0.95) between SOC, microbial populations, and enzyme activities as well as PCA results demonstrating that SOC, SOC stock, microbial biomass, microbial populations and enzymes loaded strongly on PC1 (79.4% of total variance).

Limitations and scope for scaling up

Although the current work offers strong evidence of SOC microbial enzyme relationships under mulching and manuring systems The observed improvements in SOC should be viewed as short-term responses to management interventions rather than signs of long-term C stabilization, thus, long-term monitoring is needed to demonstrate the durability and stabilization of these C benefits. Molecular characterisation of microbial populations and functional groups could be added to culture based evaluations to further refine these pathways. Future research including in situ kinetic data across diverse soil moisture and temperature regimes would improve process level resolution. Enzyme activities were assessed as potential activities. Scaling these results and strengthening their relevance to broader agroecosystem management and climate oriented soil health assessments would be made easier by extending the experimental framework across several agro-climatic regions and seasons and incorporating the discovered SOC microbial enzyme relationships into process based soil C models. Although the experiment was carried out in an apricot orchard with uniform tree age, tree-related factors such as canopy cover, root density and litterfall were not measured and hence did not serve as covariates in the analysis. Future studies should quantify these parameters to better account for their potential influence on soil health.
