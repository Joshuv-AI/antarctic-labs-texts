The intersecting effects of race/ethnicity, wealth, education, and gender on AIDS among 28.3 million Brazilians
Authors: Iracema Lua, Laio Magno, Andrea F Silva, Priscila FPS Gestal, João Luiz Bastos, Gabriela Jesus, Ronaldo de Almeida Coelho, Maria Yury Ichihara, Mauricio L Barreto, Carlos AS Teles Santos, Corrina Moucheraud, Pamina M Gorbach, James Macinko, Luis Eugênio Souza, Davide Rasella, Ines Dourado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth inequities in Latin America are rooted in historical systems of oppression, including slavery, patriarchy, and colonialism, which disproportionately affect socially marginalized groups. Intersectionality theory offers a framework to examine how overlapping social markers interact to shape health outcomes. While HIV/AIDS research has explored the effects of individual markers, few studies have addressed their intersections. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from 28.3 million Brazilians aged ≥13 years. We examined associations between race/ethnicity, education, and wealth—individually and in combination—stratified by gender. Over nine years, AIDS incidence was 23.37 and 18.63 per 100,000 person-years among men and women, respectively; mortality rates were 8.06 and 5.81 per 100,000 person-years. Isolated markers of social difference were associated with increased risk, but combined markers revealed pronounced intersectional effects. Here we show that intersections of race/ethnicity, education, and wealth substantially amplify the risk of AIDS-related illness and death in Brazil, with greater effects among women. These findings underscore the necessity of incorporating intersectionality into public health research and policy, and of addressing structural racism, sexism, and poverty through coordinated intersectoral actions to reduce HIV/AIDS inequities.Subject terms: HIV infections, Risk factors, Epidemiology

Introduction

Health inequities are rooted in historical processes of oppression and marginalization1. In Latin America, major social forces such as slavery, patriarchy, and colonialism have long driven the exclusion and subjugation of large segments of the population segments2,3. Socially excluded groups– including Black and mixed populations, migrants, women, individuals with low literacy, or those with limited educational attainment—tend to have lower incomes, reduced capacity to accumulate and transfer wealth across generations, and diminished social status3,4, These structural disadvantages ultimately contribute to poorer health outcomes and reduced overall well-being1,2,4.

An intersectionality perspective—which recognizes that historical contexts, power dynamics, and structural forms of oppression shape individual and collective experiences—has increasingly been adopted to understand complex patterns of social marginalization and health inequities1. Applying an intersectional approach to health highlights how the interplay of multiple social categories, such as race/ethnicity, gender and social class, reflects the interconnected and overlapping nature of systems of oppression and privilege (e.g., racism, sexism, classism)1,5–8. These systems of oppression/privilege are power structures that result in systematic unequal and unjust treatment, expressed in multiple levels and forms: through individual actors (interpersonal discrimination), within organizations and institutions (institutional discrimination), and across the interconnected network of social institutions that collectively contribute to the oppression and marginalization of Black people, women, and the poor9–11. The term “social markers of difference” originates from the social sciences and is used to conceptualize and analyze how experiences of inequality are generated and maintained, contributing to the construction of social differences and hierarchies12.

HIV/AIDS researchers have long examined the impact of individual social markers, particularly race/ethnicity13,14, education13,15 and poverty/wealth on health outcomes14,15. However, there remains a significant gap in research exploring the intersections of these markers. Much of the existing literature on intersectionality and HIV/AIDS centers on the stigmas experienced by people living with HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) particularly how these stigmas intersect with other socially marginalized identities16–19. These studies provide important insights into how overlapping identities shape the experiences of stigma among PLWHA. Nevertheless, a previous literature review identified only five studies20–24. that explicitly address how the intersectionality of social markers of differences influences the risk of AIDS-related illness and mortality.. These studies explored intersections between gender, race/ethnicity, sexual orientation20,21,24, and transphobia23 with outcomes such as risk of HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs)20,23,24, prevention of HIV/STIs21, and retention in healthcare22.

Collectively, they reveal complex patterns of health inequity in settings marked by systemic racism and ethnic segregation. As a result, most existing research in the field of HIV/AIDS tends to overlook the intersections between multiple dimensions of social marginalization. Categories such as race/ethnicity, gender, education, and income are often analyzed in isolation, as if they operate independently. This fragmented approach limits our ability to fully understand and address health disparities rooted in historical processes that shape contemporary systems of oppression and privilege. These structural forces produce distinct social experiences and patterns of marginalization for specific population groups.

Our hypotheses are grounded on the theoretical framework of intersectionality and the recognition of enduring structural and historical systems in Brazil—such as racism, sexism, and classism- that sustain the marginalization of specific groups3,25. These systems operate both independently and interactively, reinforcing one another. The experience of marginalization is significantly intensified when multiple marginalized identities intersect across different forms of social inequality.

To understand these phenomena, we leveraged a large cohort of vulnerable individuals in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), a population-based dataset of 28.3 million Brazilians followed over nine years (2007–2015). Using an intersectional perspective, we examined how individual characteristics related to key social markers of difference - race/ethnicity, education, wealth, and gender—function as proxies for socially constructed and stratified identities in an LMIC. Specifically, we investigated the associations and intersections of these markers with AIDS-related illness and mortality in a retrospective cohort design.

Results

Among the 28.3 million individuals in the cohort, 13,398,482 (47,3%) were men and 14,920,049 (52,7%) women, with incidence rates of 23.37 (n: 15,625; 95% CI: 23.00–23.73) and 18.63 (n: 13,251; 95% CI: 18.31–18.95) per 100,000 person-years (PY), respectively. For mortality rates, the estimates were 8.06/100,000 PY (n: 5394; 95% CI: 7.85–8.28) for men and 5.81/100,000 PY (n: 4134; 95% CI: 5.64–5.99) for women. Considering the most vulnerable groups, that is, those who accumulate social markers of difference the highest incidence and mortality rates were observed among Black women with lower educational attainment (Incidence Rate (IR): 39.25; 95% CI: 37.27–41.34; Mortality Rate (MR): 15.18; 95% CI: 13.97–16.50) and Black women with lower wealth and lower educational attainment (IR: 39.09; 95% CI: 36.26–42.15; MR: 15.53; 95% CI: 13.78–17.50) (Tables 1, 2).

The effects of social markers of difference were consistent in showing a higher risk of AIDS-related illness and death in intersectional groups, that is, those who accumulate two or more social markers of difference. A clear dose–response gradient was observed when assessing the adjusted risk ratios (aRR), with intersectional groups facing substantially higher risks of AIDS-related disease and death (Fig. 1). All measures of additive interaction (Tables 1–4, Supplementary Information Tables S6, S7) reinforce the presence of intersectionality among the evaluated social markers of difference.

Risk of AIDS

Among men, the risk of developing AIDS was significantly higher among Black individuals (aRR: 1.33; 95% CI: 1.25–1.42), those with lower wealth (aRR: 1.49; 95% CI: 1.36–1.63), and those with lower educational attainment (aRR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.08–1.23). The second-order intersectionality showed higher combined effects, with increased risk observed among men who were simultaneously of lower wealth and lower educational attainment (aRR: 1.71; 95% CI: 1.51–1.93), Black and with lower educational attainment (aRR: 1.56; 95% CI: 1.40–1.74), or Black of lower wealth (aRR: 1.93; 95% CI: 1.69–2.20), with additional AIDS risk ranging from 0.44 (RD: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.33–0.55) to 0.66) per 100,000 PY (RD: 0.66; 95% CI: 0.53–0.79 compared to men without social markers of difference. These risks and risk differences increased substantially when considering three simultaneous exposures (third-order intersectionality), with Black men of lower wealth and lower educational attainment having double the risk (aRR: 2.08; 95% CI: 1.77–2.46) of AIDS compared to White men of higher wealth and higher educational attainment. Considering these same comparison groups, there were 0.74 additional AIDS risk per 100,000 PY (RD: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.57–0.90), indicating an additive interaction of the evaluated markers (Table 1). Complementary measures of additive interaction support these findings (Tables S6, S7 in Supplementary Information).

Among women, a similar pattern of increased risk was observed, though on a larger scale, demonstrating a greater impact of social markers of difference for women. The isolated effects of these markers show a higher risk of developing AIDS among women with lower educational attainment (aRR: 1.95; 95% CI: 1.85–2.05), Black women (aRR: 1.76; 95% CI: 1.64–1.90), and those with lower wealth (aRR: 1.31; 95% CI: 1.23–1.41), when compared to the respective reference groups: women with higher educational attainment, White women, and women with higher wealth. For the second-order, intersections doubled or even tripled the risk of AIDS among women with two social markers of difference, with an additional AIDS risk as high as 1.23 per 100,000 PY (RD: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.12–1.34), considering Black women with lower educational attainment when compared to White women with higher educational attainment. Women who were both Black and had lower educational attainment had a 3.4 times higher risk of AIDS (aRR: 3.42; 95% CI: 3.05–3.83) compared to those without these markers (White and with higher education attainment) (Table 2).

In other intersectional groups, the highest risk was observed among women with lower wealth and lower educational attainment (aRR: 2.57; 95% CI: 2.33–2.84), and Black women with lower wealth (aRR: 2.42; 95% CI: 2.13–2.76), when compared to the respective reference groups: women with higher wealth and higher educational attainment and White women with higher wealth (Table 2). When a third marker was added, women with all three social markers had nearly five times the risk of AIDS (aRR: 4.83; 95% CI: 4.10–5.70) compared to those without these markers (White, higher wealth, and higher educational attainment), with an additional 1.58 per 100,000 PY increase in AIDS risk (RD: 1.58; 95% CI: 1.41–1.74) (Table 2). Complementary measures of additive interaction support these findings (Tables S6, S7 in the Supplementary Information).

Risk of death from AIDS

The risk of death from AIDS showed the same pattern of isolated and combined effects as the risk of developing the disease. Among men, the risk of dying from AIDS was significantly higher among Black individuals (aRR: 1.48, 95%CI: 1.33–1.64), those with lower educational attainment (aRR: 1.71, 95%CI: 1.53–1.90), and those with less wealth (aRR: 1.65, 95%CI: 1.38–1.98). These effects increased in the two-order intersectionality, with those who were simultaneously less wealthy and with lower educational attainment (aRR: 2.84, 95%CI: 2.37–3.39), Black with lower educational attainment (aRR: 2.35, 95%CI: 2.03–2.73), and Black with less wealth (aRR: 2.38, 95%CI: 1.84–3.07) showing more than double the risk of dying from AIDS compared to their respective reference groups: men with higher wealthy and higher educational attainment, White men with higher educational attainment, and White men with higher wealth. The combined effect of all three markers showed a threefold increase in the risk of death from AIDS among those who were Black with less wealth and lower educational attainment (aRR: 3.48, 95%CI: 2.61–4.64) when compared to White men with higher wealthy and higher educational attainment (Table 3).

RD risk difference.

Risk differences (RD) further underscored these disparities, there were 1.04 additional AIDS deaths per 100,000 PY among men with lower educational attainment and less wealth (RD: 1.04, 95%CI: 0.86–1.22), 0.85 additional AIDS deaths per 100,000 PY among Black men with lower educational attainment (RD: 0.85, 95%CI: 0.71–1.00), and 0.87 additional AIDS deaths per 100,000 PY among Black men with less wealth (RD: 0.87, 95%CI: 0.61–1.12), compared to their respective reference groups: men with higher educational attainment and higher wealth, White men with higher educational attainment, and White men with higher wealth. The greatest absolute inequality was observed among individuals at the third-order intersectionality (RD = 1.25, 95%CI: 0.96–1.53) (Table 3). This is supported by the complementary additive interaction measures estimated (Tables S6, S7 in Supplementary Information).

Similar to what was observed for the risk of developing AIDS, the increased risk of death is even more pronounced among women, confirming the greater impact of social markers of difference on women. The isolated effects of these markers revealed a higher risk of dying from AIDS among Black women (aRR: 2.05, 95%CI: 1.87–2.25), those with lower education attainment (aRR: 2.52, 95%CI: 2.27–2.79), and those with less wealth (aRR: 1.58, 95% CI:1.41–1.77). The risk was three to four times higher among women with second-order intersectionality. The risk of death was higher among women who were simultaneously Black with lower educational attainment (aRR: 4.97, 95%CI: 4.14–5.97), with less wealth and lower educational attainment (aRR: 3.93, 95%CI: 3.32–4.66), and Black and with less wealth (aRR: 3.22, 95%CI: 2.69–3.87), when compared to their respective reference groups: White women with higher educational attainment, women with higher wealth and higher educational attainment, and White women with higher wealth. The combined effect of all three social markers showed a sevenfold increase in the risk of dying from AIDS among women who were Black with less wealth and lower educational attainment (aRR: 7.45, 95%CI: 5.64–9.85) when compared to White women with higher wealth and higher educational attainment (Table 4).

Additive interaction was evidenced by risk differences, showing a 1.17 additional AIDS deaths per 100,000 PY among Black women with less wealth (RD: 1.17, 95%CI: 0.99–1.35), 1.37 additional AIDS deaths per 100,000 PY among women with less wealth and lower educational attainment (RD: 1.37, 95%CI: 1.20–1.54), 1.60 additional AIDS deaths per 100,000 PY among Black women with lower educational attainment (RD: 1.60, 95%CI: 1.42–1.79), and 2.01 additional AIDS deaths per 100,000 PY among Black women with both less wealth and lower educational attainment (RD: 2.01, 95%CI: 1.73–2.29) when compared to their respective reference groups: White women with higher wealth, women with higher wealth and higher educational attainment, and White women with higher educational attainment (Table 4). These combined effects of social markers of difference were corroborated by the complementary interaction measures (Tables S6, S7 in Supplementary Information).

Sensitivity analyses

All sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the findings were consistent, even after fitting models with alternative structures and specifications. The analyses restricted to municipalities with adequate QVI, or those in which the income variable replaced the expenditure variable, showed no significant changes in the model estimates (Supplementary Tables S11–S15, Table S17 in Supplementary Information).

In the analyses by distinct time periods (2007–2012 versus 2013–2015), the second period showed an increase in the 95% confidence intervals and variations in the magnitude of the rates, which may be explained by the smaller sample size for this period. Nevertheless, the same groups remained at a higher risk of becoming ill with and dying from AIDS, and even presented an increase in the strength of the association. These findings suggest that, over time, the effects of social determinants on HIV/AIDS outcomes tend to consolidate (Supplementary Table S16 in Supplementary Information).

Discussion

We investigated the relationship between intersectionality and AIDS-related outcomes in Brazil. Thanks to the use of a large cohort of vulnerable individuals available in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC), combined with the inclusion of four key social markers of differences: gender, race/ethnicity, education, and income.

We demonstrated that men and women experiencing racial-, educational-, and poverty-based disadvantage had a significantly higher risk of illness and death from AIDS when compared to their socially privileged peers. Each of these social markers of difference was strongly associated with the study outcomes, yet the variability in AIDS-related conditions was more accurately explained when examined through an intersectionality perspective. It is also worth highlighting the greater impact of social inequalities on women.

From a theoretical perspective, there are multiple ways in which structural sexism can account for these differences. This approach sheds light on the broader set of institutional arrangements that privilege men, beyond explicit and intentional individual sexist behaviors10. At its core, structural sexism refers to gender-based hierarchies and inequalities that shape exposure to living and working conditions, leading individuals to internalize social systems of inequality. This may restrict women’s access to health-promoting factors—such as healthcare resources and services, safe and healthy living conditions, and supportive social relationships—while increasing their exposure to health-damaging conditions, including violence, harassment, unsafe work environments, low social status, and perceived discrimination10,11. Our findings support this perspective and advance it by incorporating intersectionality theory, highlighting the pronounced disparities between men and women in less privileged socioeconomic and racial positions. Even though overall AIDS-related morbidity and mortality rates are higher among men, when accounting for social inequalities, women—particularly those who are simultaneously Black, have lower levels of education, and limited income—accumulate more social markers of disadvantage and experience more adverse outcomes. In such cases, the risk of AIDS infection increases fivefold, and the risk of death from AIDS increases sevenfold.

The strength of our empirical approach to evaluating intersectionality is based on the incorporation of four social markers of difference of intersection, a broad set of measures, and the use of two primary outcomes. This framework enabled us to identify positive and high-magnitude interactions among the evaluated social markers, with consistent findings for both AIDS incidence and mortality. Moreover, the impact of social inequalities was most pronounced in relation to mortality—the most severe outcome—where measures of association and interaction reached high magnitudes. We found that Black men with low levels of education and income were nearly four times more likely to die from AIDS, with an excess risk of 1.25 per 100,000 PY. Among women with this same triple vulnerability, the figures were even more alarming: over seven times greater risk of AIDS-related death and an excess risk of 2.01 per 100,000 PY.

The intersections among various social markers of difference do more than merely adding or multiplying the effects of isolated factors; they enable a more nuanced understanding of the social determination of AIDS-related outcomes26. The disproportionate burden of AIDS among marginalized populations illustrates two primary mechanisms through which oppression and exclusion operate: i) increased vulnerability to HIV infection, as structurally disadvantaged individuals often lack the means to act on health information (including prevention, testing, and treatment), and face heightened exposure to sexual risk (e.g., transactional sex); and ii) accelerated disease progression, as health systems often fail to provide timely diagnosis and treatment for these populations.

For instance, until 2015, most reported AIDS cases were concentrated among white individuals. However, from 2016 onward, there was a marked shift, with the majority of AIDS cases occurring among Black populations—particularly among those identifying as mixed—who accounted for over 63% of notifications in 2023, compared to 30.7% among whites27. Moreover, the elevated risk of AIDS-related mortality among Black and marginalized groups underscores persistent gaps in access to and retention within the HIV/AIDS care continuum—despite the formal universality and equity principles of the healthcare system28,29.

The broader context of marginalized and oppressed groups may explain these results. Global studies indicate that Black people have worse HIV/AIDS-related outcomes5,30,31. There is no evidence for the existence of genetic factors to account for this reality, reinforcing the hypothesis that racial inequities driven by structural racism are responsible for worse health outcomes1,2, including higher AIDS rates32, lower ART use14, and poor adherence to ART13. Moreover, racism is a driver of social exclusion and less access to education and income, reinforcing the vicious cycle of oppression33 and structural violence that prevent individuals or groups from reaching their full potential1. Our findings also showed that women suffer more from the effects of social processes6. The intersection of racism and sexism, as well as other systems of oppression, shapes life experiences and opportunities1,34.

Strengths and limitations

This study has some limitations. The first refers to the composition of the cohort under study, which represents the Brazilian population with the lowest socioeconomic status and is obtained for administrative purposes in the Brazilian Single Registry for Social Programs (In Portuguese: Cadastro Único para Programas Sociais - CadÚnico). While this may limit the representativeness of the studied population, it can also be considered a strength of our analyses, as it allowed for longitudinal analysis of a large dataset of individual data, which is itself an advancement in quantitative research, offering a unique opportunity to evaluate the effects of diverse social identity groups with a larger number of individuals and subpopulations, often not included or underrepresented in traditional epidemiological studies.

However, this data source has some limitations regarding the inclusion of HIV cases and the measurement of certain variables. The reporting of HIV cases only began in 2014 in Brazil35, which made it impossible to include this outcome for the study period (2007–2015). The measurement of income and gender was not as extensive as expected. The former is due to the inherent limitations in measuring income in population surveys, although information on consumption and ownership of assets has been considered a good alternatives36, and our sensitivity analyses indicated no substantial variations in the results when using either reported income or expenditure as a proxy measure. The latter is due to the lack of inclusion of information on participants’ gender identity, as we only had access to information regarding biological sex, highlighting the need for further intersectional studies that can address the marginalization experienced by different gender groups. In Brazil, the HIV epidemic disproportionately affects men who have sex with men and transgender women, but this study did not consider gender identity and sexual orientation as markers of social difference that could also affect the associations found.

It was also not possible to include adjustments for individuals’ risky sexual behaviors or information on the treatment and continuum of care of individuals diagnosed with AIDS in the models. However, these risk factors should be considered mediators—rather than confounders—of the effects of the evaluated markers of social differences13,37; consequently, their inclusion in the regressions would lead to overadjustment of the models38,39.

Another limitation faced by the authors was the lack of comparable studies in the literature and of an established protocol for intersection analyses in quantitative studies with large cohorts of individuals. Many approaches are used, but a unified and consolidated methodology for studying the health effects of intersecting systems of oppression is lacking. Its inclusion in quantitative research is still recent, although its great potential for public health is already recognized.

In our interaction analysis, we find a coherent possibility as it allows us to examine how social identities intersect to understand social and structural inequalities more comprehensively. It is emphasized that statistical interaction on the additive scale is consistent with the intersectionality framework because these measures translate directly into excess cases of an outcome (which are caused or prevented) due to exposure or a combination of exposures6,40.

The analysis of interaction allows for an evaluation of the impact’s individuals experience by being simultaneously Black, poor, and with low education, and how these experiences differ according to gender. Our study advances this approach by introducing a wide range of measures of additive interaction for three markers of social differences, adapting existing formulas—typically applied to two-way intersections—for the inclusion of a third dimension. This allowed us to robustly confirm the presence of interaction between identities of social marginalization and to present a feasible approach for implementing intersectionality within quantitative research. From this perspective, this paper assessed micro-level characteristics that are highly relevant for analyzing social inequalities.

By examining them through the lens of intersectionality theory allowed us to understand the reflections of historical and macro-structural contexts on AIDS outcomes and to shed light on the development of well-targeted and cost-effective social and health public policies to address issues deeply rooted in the community41. Despite these limitations, the main strength of the study is the use of an large dataset cohort which allowed the evaluation of synergistic effects of various intersectional positions, providing an opportunity to add statistical power and a higher level of resolution to existing maps of social inequalities within populations, enabling the inclusion of new SDH in intersectional analyses to advance knowledge about how social identities intersect to determine different ways of illness and death, extending the classical triad of race/ethnicity, class and gender that is usually assessed by intersectional studies. The wide availability of socioeconomic data in the cohort further allowed us to consider the necessary adjustments and ensure the possibility of interpretations that approximate causal interactions40. To this end, a variety of individual, family, and municipal characteristics were included in the models.

Moreover, the inclusion of variables at the municipal level enables the consideration of heterogeneities present among Brazilian municipalities, whether in socioeconomic aspects or health service structure. Thus, sensitivity models were tested, including multilevel models and analyses restricted to municipalities with adequate quality of vital information and no correlations at the aggregate level (municipal, state or regional) or changes in the estimates of the restricted models were identified (Supplementary Information).

To mitigate the adverse health impacts of intersecting factors, coordinated public interventions addressing broader social processes should take place. Our application of the intersectionality approach to a large cohort enabled us to evaluate how social markers of difference intersect and shape AIDS-related outcomes in a highly unequal country such as Brazil. There is no doubt that social differences impair individuals’ health, and the intersection between these factors synergistically amplifies the marginalization of these groups and health inequities. To mitigate the adverse health impacts of intersecting factors, coordinated public interventions must be carried out that address broader social processes. Correcting oppressive social, economic, and political issues is essential to advancing better and more equitable health conditions. To this end, it is necessary to consider their multilevel, multifaceted, interconnected, systemic, and intersectional nature9.

Acting only on the improvement of healthcare is not enough, and profound societal and political changes are urgently needed. The tackling of the AIDS epidemic requires inter and intra-sectoral actions. Therefore, clear strategies for addressing these systems of oppression for a radical transformation of society need to be discussed. In Brazil, we already have some paths that can be followed and strengthened.

The implementation of poverty-reduction and primary health care policies, like the Bolsa Familia Program and Family Health Strategy, has been shown to have a synergistic effect on the improvement of child health42. Moreover, transversal policies of “racial quota” in public universities and other policies to provide access to higher education (such as the Unified Selection System-Sisu, University for All Program-Prouni, and Student Financing Fund-FIES) have been shown to be effective in reducing racial and social class disparities in educational outcomes. This can be the first step towards advancing the ideal of social transformation from an intersectional perspective. Interlocking systems of oppression and marginalization must be comprehensively addressed in order to effectively reduce social and health inequities.

Methods

This retrospective cohort study draws on a subsample from the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort43, a well-established dataset created through validated record linkage algorithms44. The cohort integrates data from the Unified Registry of Brazilians (Cadastro Único – CadÚnico), a national database used to determine eligibility for social protection programs, with two major health databases: the Brazilian National Surveillance System (In Portuguese: Sistema de Informação de Agravos de Notificação – SINAN) and the Brazilian Mortality Information System (In Portuguese: Sistema de Informação sobre Mortalidade-SIM) spanning a nine-year period. The record linkage was developed and implemented by the Center for Data and Knowledge Integration for Health (CIDACS) (Supplementary Information)43,44. The study protocol45 was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Collective Health Institute at the Federal University of Bahia, number 41691315.0.0000.5030 (Assessment n°:3.783.920).

Dataset and Study Population

We analyzed data from the 100 Million Brazilian Cohort, selecting a sample of 28.3 million individuals aged ≥13 years not living with AIDS and registered between January 1, 2007 and December 31, 2015. Individuals who were living with AIDS or who died before the start of follow-up were excluded. (description of the selection process in Supplementary Fig. S1 in the appendix, p. 4).

We stratify the analysis by the self-reported sex assigned at birth—men (N = 13,398,482) and women (N = 14,920,049)—used as a proxy for gender. For each group, the intersectional analyses included three markers of social differences (race/ethnicity, education and wealth), selected based on their established relevance to HIV/AIDS outcomes13–15,30, their strong health impacts observed in a prior study using the same cohort30 and supporting preliminary analyses (Supplementary Table S1 in the appendix, pp. 5–6). Based on intersectionality theory, each marker was divided into two comparison groups, contrasting those with greater vulnerability to those with greater social privilege: as follows: for race/ethnicity, Black individuals were compared to White individuals, for education and wealth, lower levels were compared to higher levels. Other racial categories (i.e., Asian, mixed and Indigenous) and the intermediate wealth level were not included in the main results but were incorporated into the models estimations (Supplementary Table S2 in the appendix, pp. 7-9).

Study variables and measures

The primary outcomes were AIDS incidence and mortality rates, aiming to consistently assess the effect of intersectionality. AIDS cases were defined using adapted criteria from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and the Rio de Janeiro/Caracas definitions, along with corresponding criteria for AIDS-related deaths. AIDS deaths were classified as those for which the underlying cause was coded within the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes B20 to B24 (Supplementary Table S4 in appendix, pp. 15–16). Follow-up time was calculated from the date of cohort entry until the earliest of the following events: a) an AIDS diagnosis (for AIDS incidence); b) an AIDS-related death (for AIDS mortality); c) death from other causes; or d) the study end date (December 31, 2015) (Supplementary Table S5 in the appendix, p. 17).

Associations between race/ethnicity, education, wealth, and AIDS-related outcomes were examined both individually and through an intersectional lens. The race/ethnicity and education variables required initial processing:

For the education variable, lower educational attainment was defined as having no formal education or having completed only elementary school. Higher educational attainment was defined as having completed secondary school or more.

Intersectionality theory operationalization

Intersectionality is not defined by the presence of statistically significant interactions between social markers of difference; rather, it constitutes a comprehensive analytical framework that informs the entire research architecture. Specifically, this orientation shapes: (1) visibility and inclusion, by determining who is centered and priorized in the analysis (e.g., intersections of gender, race/ethnicity, and class); (2) operationalization, by guiding how phenomena are defined and measured, including the selection of instruments and the construction of nuanced categories; (3) interpretive depth, by foregrounding power relations and social structures as fundamental drivers of observed outcomes; iv) transformative action, by informing recommendations aimed at challenging the status quo. From this perspective, intersectionality functions as a fundamental conceptual orientation rather than a statistical criterion to be satisfied46,47.

Although conventional statistical interaction analysis cannot fully capture the multidimensional and structural nature of intersectionality, they offer a limited parallel. In such an analysis, the joint effect of two exposures may be greater than the sum of their individual effects (synergistic interaction) or smaller than expected under additivity (antagonistic interaction).

Accordingly, for the variables race/ethnicity, education and wealth, dummy variables were created to model two- and three-way interaction terms. These were grouped into (1) a reference group (Whites with higher educational attainment and wealth); (2) groups with one social marker (isolated markers), and (3) groups with two or more overlapping markers (combined markers of social difference) (Supplementary Fig. S2 in the appendix, p. 19).

The following intersectional comparisons were analyzed: 1. Wealth and education: Individuals with higher wealth and higher education served as the reference group. They were compared to: (a) higher wealth & lower education attainment, (b) lower wealth & higher education attainment (isolated markers of social difference), and (c) lower wealth & lower education (combined markers of social difference); 2. Race/ethnicity and education: White individuals with higher education attainment were the reference group. Comparisons were made with: (a) White & lower education attainment, (b) Black & higher education attainment (isolated markers of social difference), and (c) Black & lower education attainment (combined markers of social difference); 3. Race/ethnicity and wealth: White individuals with higher wealth served as the reference group. They were compared to: (a) White & lower wealth, (b) Black & higher wealth (isolated markers of social difference), and (c) Black & lower wealth (combined markers of social difference); 4. Race/ethnicity, education, and wealth: White individuals with higher wealth & higher education attainment were used as the reference group. Comparisons included: (a) White & higher wealth & lower education attainment, (b) White & lower wealth & higher education attainment (c) Black & higher wealth & higher education attainment (isolated markers of social difference), (d) White & lower wealth & lower education attainment (e) Black & higher wealth & lower education attainment (f) Black & lower wealth & higher education attainment and (g) Black & lower wealth & lower education attainment (combined markers of social difference) (Supplementary Fig. S2 in the appendix, p. 19).

The multivariate analyses included individual-, household-, and municipal-level covariates associated with both the exposures and outcomes. These covariates comprised: geographic factors (Brazilian region and area of residence); receipt time of conditional cash transfers (Bolsa Família); household conditions, age, year of cohort entry; and health infrastructure indicators (primary health care coverage, number of specialized clinics, physicians, and hospital beds per 1000 inhabitants). Additionally, we included the average AIDS outcome rate during the follow-up period, as well as municipal-level socio-environmental indicators such as the Gini index, and extreme poverty and unemployment rates in the municipality of residence (Fig. 2).

Conceptual model.

Race/ethnicity: The variable race/ethnicity was categorized as White, Asian, Mixed, Black and Indigenous. Individuals self-declared as White or Black races/ethnicity were part of the intersectionality analysis (dummy variable). Individuals classified in the other racial categories (Asian, mixed and Indigenous) were not part of the dummy variable construction but remained in the estimated models. Wealth: the per capita family expenditure proportional to the national monthly minimum wage was constructed as a proxy of wealth level, from the sum of all family expenditures (e.g., water and electrical power, food, cooking gas, rent, medicines, transportation and others), divided by the number of residents of household and calculated in proportion to the minimum wage of the year of admission to the cohort. The result was divided into three wealth levels considering the tertile. For the intersectionality analysis, the two extremes (Level 1 and 3) were considered; the intermediate level (2) was not included in the construction of the social markers of difference dummy variable, but remained in the estimated models. Education: The education variable was dichotomized into two levels. Those with up to elementary school were categorized as Lower educational attainment, and those with high school or more were considered as higher educational attainment. * Household Conditions: A household conditions indicator was created from the sum of all variables related to household conditions. The households were categorized into “no unfavorable conditions” or “presence of some unfavorable condition”.

We are aware that, in many situations, controlling the family-wise error rate (FWER) is important to avoid misleading conclusions in models with many adjustment variables. Several methods can be used to correct the p-value for this purpose. Bonferroni correction is one of these options; however, its estimations are not feasible for large datasets in the Stata program due to limitations that hinder this correction. Given our low p-values (>0.00001) in most models, we chose to follow other studies that present consistent and coherent estimates in large datasets without the use of corrections for family-wise error30,39,48–50.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to examine the distribution of the three-way intersection groups across socioeconomic covariates. To estimate crude (RR) and adjusted (aRR) rate ratios, multivariable Poisson regression models were applied39,48, for each outcome separately. Robust standard errors were used, clustered by municipality of residence. Poisson regression models incorporating follow-up time as an offset variable were employed to produce interpretable risk measures51. This approach is commonly used in cohort data analysis52 and has been widely applied in previous observational studies using the 100 Million Brazilians Cohort30,39,48.

To address missing data, listwise deletion was applied. Comparative analyses of the individuals with complete versus incomplete data (Supplementary Table S8 in the appendix, pp. 25–27) indicated that the missingness was randomly distributed.

Intersections of social difference markers were further assessed using a range of interaction indicators on both additive and multiplicative scales6,53.

Although some scholars have raised concerns about the use of statistical interaction analysis in intersectional studies54, we argue that additive structures can serve as a valuable mode of understanding the relationality among markers of social difference. As Collins55 emphasizes, relationality is a core principle of intersectionality, underscoring that systems of oppression—such as racism, sexism, and classism—do not function independently but instead mutually shape and reinforce one another. Additive interaction measures offer a quantitative framework to capture these interdependencies, moving beyond the isolated effects of individual social markers. In this way, additive interaction helps identify intersectional patterns and quantify the cumulative effects of overlapping social inequalities on population health, thereby challenging the segregationist logic that underpins social inequities.

This analytical approach provides a more robust and comprehensive estimate of intersectionality by assigning both direction (positive, null, or negative) and magnitude (indicating how much greater the risk is due to combined characteristics) to the interaction under study. This is particularly valuable for informing the development and implementation of more equitable public policies40.

We first estimated the risk difference (RD) between the doubly or triply marginalized groups and the reference group, enabling a direct quantification of risk associated with a hypothetical intersectional position. To obtain these estimates, we used the lincom command in Stata.

Additionally, we calculated several measures of interaction to corroborate these findings: (a) Relative Excess Risk due to Interaction (RERI), indicating the extent to which excess risk deviates from the null value (RERI > 0 suggests interaction)53,56; (b) Attributable Proportion (AP), representing the proportion of cases attributable to the interaction between exposures (AP > 0 suggests interaction)53,56; (c) Synergy Index (S), which reflects the direction and strength of the interaction relative to the null (S = 1 indicates no interaction, S > 1 indicates synergy, and S < 1 indicates antagonism)53. Notably, we simultaneously evaluated three social markers - race/ethnicity, education, and wealth- which is innovative in both intersectional and HIV/AIDS research. This was made possible by the large sample size and methodological adaptations we introduced for computing interaction indicators (Supplementary Table S6 in the appendix, pp.21).

To evaluate the robustness of our findings (Supplementary Information, pp. 32–47) we implemented several strategies: 1) Sensitivity analyses using alternative models specifications (negative binomial regression; survival models, and multilevel models), to examine potential changes in estimates; 2) Additional covariate adjustments, re-estimated models with and without municipal-level variables; and 3) Data quality analyses restricting the sample to individuals residing in municipalities with validated adequate quality of vital information (QVI), to assess potential bias from differences in surveillance data quality; 4) Comparative analyses across two time periods were conducted to check possible changes in effects over time, either by substantial changes in SDH effects, or alterations to HIV/AIDS health policies; an 5) To verify whether the variable “expense” was adequate as a proxy for income, comparative analyses were conducted between models with three-way intersections using one variable or the other.

All analyses were conducted using Stata® 15.0.
