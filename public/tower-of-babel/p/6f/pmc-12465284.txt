Importance of skills development on labour market participation among workers aged 35 or older - a systematic review of prospective cohort studies
Authors: Karina Glies Vincents Seeberg, Mia Nyvang Stilling, Jonas Vinstrup, Emil Sundstrup, Ole Steen Mortensen, Lars Louis Andersen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveContinuous skills development is important for keeping up with new demands in workplaces and may also influence labour market participation. This systematic review aims to determine whether skills development affects labour market participation among workers aged 35 or older.Materials and methodsA systematic review following Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) was conducted. We searched Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed, EBSCO, ProQuest and Ovid in May 2023, and conducted an additional search in Google Scholar in November 2023.Eligibility criteria were; 1) prospective cohort studies 2) workers 35+ years 3) participation in, or opportunities to participate in skills development 4) outcomes related to labour market participation and 5) studies published in English or Scandinavian languages.Two independent reviewers’ extracted data, assessed the risk of bias (Newcastle Ottawa Scale) and evaluated the certainty of the evidence (GRADE) of the included studies.ResultsThe literature search identified 5,147 records, of which 19 (n=1,089,749 and 759,931 person-years) met the inclusion criteria. GRADE indicated a “very low” quality of evidence for all outcomes. Overall, 12 studies found an association: 1) four studies found that skills development / opportunities for skills development reduced early retirement, 2) one study found that a lack of skills development increased the likelihood of early retirement, and 3) seven studies found that skills development / opportunities for skills development were associated with working longer.ConclusionsThis systematic review presents varied findings concerning the link between skills development and labour market participation.Trial registrationThis systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023419005).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23874-y.

Introduction

The demographic in Europe is shifting towards an increasingly elderly population, resulting in rising public welfare costs and a higher risk of labour shortages [1–3]. As a means of postponing the retirement age, many EU countries have implemented a series of pension reforms. For example, Denmark has implemented a reform to incrementally raise the statutory retirement age from 65 to 68 years by the year 2030, with additional increases anticipated in the future [3]. Moreover, birth rates in Europe are declining, resulting in a further reduction of the available working-age population [4]. Consequently, the labour market in Europe is undergoing changes [5], highlighting the essential need for ongoing skills development among workers of all age groups to prevent early exit from the labour market. Skills development at different ages may be important for adaptability and continued participation in the workforce [6, 7].

We are currently between the ‘Fourth and fifth Industrial Revolution’, which reflects a shift in the labour market from traditional manufacturing and industrial processes to more automated tasks, along with increased use of digital technologies and data analysis [8, 9]. This places demands on both current and future generations in the workforce to adapt to constant labour market changes. Skills development may support workers in meeting future job requirements by improving the balance between job demands and individual resources. This may potentially contribute to preserving work capacity, reducing sick leave, and improving the ability to remain in the workforce until statutory retirement age in a meaningful manner [10, 11].

Several theories address the importance of skills development in relation to working life and advocate for its prioritization. One such theory is the Human Capital Theory. From this perspective, skills development, training, and knowledge are regarded as forms of capital that workers, leaders or organizations can invest in. By investing in these areas, workers can accumulate more human capital, leading to enhanced employability [12]. This, in turn, facilitates the individual in finding and retaining jobs, increasing job mobility, and promoting career progression. The theory also suggests that increasing human capital and skills development are linked to higher engagement, staying updated and refining skills. Consequently, this may help workers handle changes in workplace, particularly those related to technological advancements and shifts in industry demand [12]. This theory may contribute to clarifying skills development as a potential solution to the challenges of demographic changes and an increasingly specialized workforce. Furthermore concerning prolonging working life, the desire to work is also an important factor [10]. The opportunity to engage in skills development at the workplace has been shown to play a significant role in working beyond retirement age [13]. Continued skills development may thus be considered to enhance positive factors, such as motivation to work up to and beyond the statutory retirement age. There are several arguments supporting that skills development is essential for prolonging working life, alongside factors that influence the labour market participation. However, it can be speculated that there may be potential drawbacks to ‘forcing’ older workers to participate in skills development, such as stress and anxiety resulting from the pressure to learn new technologies or methodologies [14]. Additionally, there might be a perceived generational gap, where older workers feel marginalized or less valued compared to their younger colleagues who may be more adept with new technologies [15].

While multiple studies have investigated these factors within a broader perspective, a previous narrative review examined the indicators and determinants of years of working life lost [16]. They identified 13 indicators that shorten working life, including sociodemographic factors like level of education and work-related risk factors like physical work demand. Two reviews were identified: one scoping review that examined early retirement factors, including workplace issues and the nature of the work itself, which may affect the ability to extend working lives [17], and another review that identified barriers and facilitators to extended working life. The factors include lack of; training and opportunities, development possibilities and career progression as barriers for extended working life [18].

Two systematic reviews investigated a broader perspective of psychosocial work factors and retirement intention and actual retirement. The first systematic review found no intervention studies or objective evaluations of factors affecting retirement [19]. The second review found mixed result in factors including opportunities to develop [20]. A previous cohort study with an 11-year register follow-up found that possibilities for development in the job increased the likelihood of working beyond retirement age [13]. To our knowledge, this systematic review is the first to investigate the association of both opportunities for skills development and actual skills development on labour market participation.

We aimed to examine (1) whether skills development prolong working life or reduce early retirement and (2) whether lack of skills development increase the risk of early retirement among workers aged 35 or older.

Methods

Protocol registration

The systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21] (see additional file 1 PRISMA checklist). The search strategy and search string employed the PI/ECO framework [22]. This systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42023419005) [23].

Eligibility criteria

Population

Populations eligible for this review were healthy workers, aged 35 and above and working at least 8 h per week. Exclusion criteria were; Workers with chronic diseases, workers on sick leave, and those receiving retirement or disability pension.

Exposures

Studies eligible for inclusion pertained to the exposure of adult skills development, assessed through self-report or registers. We defined adult skills development as either a (1) course regardless of duration, formal education or informal education or (2) formal education is defined as bachelor´s degree or professional bachelor´s degree, diploma, academy, master, PhD, vocational education and training, general adult education, Higher preparatory examination single subjects, basic adult education, Adult vocational training programmes and preparatory adult education. Applies to both the entire programmes and a single module. Alternatively (3) other forms of education are defined as peer-to-peer training, mentoring, networks, conferences/seminars, learning courses and self-studies. After protocol registration, we identified an additional exposure, “opportunities or the possibility for skills development,” which we have incorporated into our systematic review.

Outcomes

We examined outcomes related to labour market participation, encompassing (1) outcomes pertaining to working up to or beyond the statutory retirement age, and (2) outcomes related to early retirement.

Study design

The principal methodological framework described in our PROSPERO protocol consisted of prospective open or fixed cohort studies, except in instances where studies adopting this particular design were unavailable; in such cases, alternative study designs were considered, such as cross-sectional or intervention designs.

Information sources

We searched the following bibliographic databases for relevant studies on specific dates. Ovid (including Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, Journals@Ovid Full Text, and APA PsycArticles Full Text) was searched on 24 May 2023. PubMed (including MEDLINE), Web of Science Core Collection (including ‘Science Citation Index Expanded’, ‘Social Sciences Citation Index’, and ‘Arts & Humanities Citation Index’), and EBSCO (including Academic Search Complete, socINDEX, CINAHL, APA PsycINFO, and Education Research Complete) were searched on 25 May 2023. ProQuest (all databases) was searched on 26 May 2023. An additional search in Google Scholar was conducted on 8 November 2023.

Studies published in English or Scandinavian languages were included without restrictions for year of publication. The search strategy was developed by thoroughly exploring the literature to identify relevant terms and their corresponding synonyms for the final search string. The final search string was modified into the different search databases based on PI/ECO framework (see Additional file 2 search protocol). After full-text screening, we used snowball search methods for manual search in the reference lists of the included studies.

Study selection

All records were imported into the Covidence systematic software program [24]. Duplicates were removed, and the remaining studies were initially assessed at the title and abstract levels, followed by a subsequent comprehensive evaluation at the full-text level by two reviewers independently (KS and MS or JV). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (ES).

Data collection process and data items

A data extraction sheet was developed and pilot-tested on five included studies. Data were independently extracted by two reviewers (KS, MS) on the main author, year of publication, country, study design, population, sample size, sex, age, exposure, measure of exposure, outcome, measure of outcome, follow-up, estimates and associations. If several follow-up periods were reported in the studies, data from the longest follow-up period were used, unless our outcome of interest was not mentioned at that time.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias and quality of the included studies were assessed independently by two reviewer (KS and MS) using a version of the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for Cohort Studies [25], applied with specific criteria based on a previous systematic review [26]. These criteria included evaluating response rates at baseline, identifying important confounders, adjusting for a certain number of confounders, and considering the length of follow-up (Table 1).

Modification of the Newcastle-Ottawa scale

Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third reviewer (ES). NOS evaluates studies based on the domains of selection, including representativeness of the sample, comparability (e.g. whether the study controls for important confounders), identification of the exposure (e.g. objectivity of the exposure measurement) and outcomes (e.g. independence of outcome measurement and follow-up periods).

Each question in NOS was rated on a scale of 0–2 stars. Poor quality and high risk of bias was considered as a score of 0–2 stars in the selection domain or 0 stars in the comparability domain or 0 or 1 star in the outcome/exposure domain. A score of 2 stars in the selection domain and 1 or 2 stars in the comparability domain and 2 or 3 stars in the outcome/exposure domain was considered as fair quality and moderate risk of bias. A score of 3–4 stars in the selection domain and 1 or 2 stars in the comparability domain and 2 or 3 stars in the outcome/exposure domain was considered as good quality and low risk of bias. The overall risk of bias between studies was assessed by calculating the total percentage for each question in each domain in the NOS that did not fulfilled the criteria for at least one star.

Synthesis and quality of evidence

To assess the overall quality and strength of evidence, a modified version of the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used. GRADE ranks the confidence of the evidence as high, moderate, low, or very low, depending on study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, impression and other limitations such as publication bias, large effects, confounding or dose-response gradient [27]. Our choice of a modified GRADE approach, as opposed to the original, involves using GRADE without the use of estimates to rank the level of evidence. This modification was implemented due to our deliberate decision not to conduct meta-analyses. As a result, we have not collected estimates for valid reasons, preventing us from evaluating the evidence based on estimates, as intended in the original GRADE methodology.

Additional analyses

Primary subgroup analyses were conducted for: (1) skills development or opportunities for skills development and reduction in early retirement; (2) lack of skills development and early retirement; and (3) skills development or opportunities for skills development and delay of retirement or prolongation of working lives.

Secondary subgroup analyses were conducted for; (1) skills development or opportunities for skills development and all retirement outcomes; and (2) actual skills development/training and all retirement outcomes; and (3) all exposure regarding skills development and intention to retire; (4) and all exposures regarding skills development and prediction of retirement; and (5) all exposures regarding skills development and actual retirement.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 illustrates the flow of studies throughout the review process. In brief, the database search yielded 5,147 records, 2,009 from Web of Science, 1,171 from ProQuest, 836 from OVID, 798 from PubMed, 225 from EBSCO, 100 from Google Scholar (grey literature), and eight records from other sources including, snowballing reference lists of included studies. After removing duplicates, 3311 titles and abstracts were screened. Following the initial screening, 115 full-text records were assessed for eligibility, and 96 records were subsequently excluded. A total of 19 cohort studies were included in this systematic review.

Study characteristics

The total number of participants in the included studies was 1,089,749 participants and 759,931 person- years. In a single study, reporting was limited to person-years rather than specifying the number of participants. Consequently, the study’s participant count is referred to interchangeably as both “participants” and “person-years” in the presentation of study [28]. The number of participants in each included study ranged from 519 to 695,506, with ages from 35 to 76 and a percentage of females ranging from 0 to 96%. The 19 studies were conducted across various European countries, with the majority originating from the Netherlands (n = 6), Denmark (n = 5), Sweden (n = 3), France (n = 1), Germany (n = 1), Finland (n = 1). Data from one study were collected in 15 different European countries (Belgium, Denmark, France, Germany, the United Kingdom, Greece, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Portugal, Spain, Austria and Finland) (n = 1), while further data from another study were collected in 20 different countries, representing the various regions in Europe, ranging from Scandinavia through Central to Mediterranean countries (including Israel) and Eastern Europe (n = 1).

All studies were prospective cohort studies. The follow-up period was between 1 and 57 years and were all published in English. The studies were carried out between 1999 and 2022 (Table 2).

Reported exposures and outcomes

The exposures of the 19 included studies were related to skills development, opportunities for skills development [13, 28–42], or lack of skills development [43–45].

The outcomes related to labour market participation in the 19 included studies were prolonging working lives, including intention to retire, prediction of retirement, or actual retirement [13, 28, 33–42], as well as early retirement [43–45] or reduction in early retirement [29–32].

The overall results from the 19 studies showed that 12 studies found an association. Among these, four studies found that skills development or opportunities for skills development reduced early retirement [29–32]. One study found that a lack of skills development increased the likelihood of early retirement [43], and seven studies found that skills development or the opportunities for skills development were associated with extended working lives [13, 33, 36, 38, 39, 41, 42].

Risk of bias within studies

Among the 19 studies included in our analysis, a subset of eight exhibited high methodological quality, thus indicating a low risk of bias, with ratings ranging from seven to nine stars [13, 32, 35, 37, 39, 40, 42, 43]. Conversely, 11 of the studies were characterised as low quality, thus indicating high risk of bias [28–31, 33, 34, 36, 38, 41, 44, 45] (Table 2, QA). It is noteworthy that nine out of 11 studies included in our investigation featured self-reported exposure and outcome measures, which may lead to both selection and information bias. Furthermore, it should be noted that five out of the 11 studies failed to provide comprehensive descriptions of loss to follow-up, and eight studies failed to provide descriptions of completeness. The low-quality ratings assigned to these 11 studies primarily stemmed from their performance in relation to the outcome categories delineated in NOS, which may contribute to information bias in the studies.

Risk of bias between studies

The overall quality of the 19 included studies shows that 42% were of high quality and low risk of bias, while 58% were of low quality and high risk of bias. All of the included studies fulfilled the selection criteria of representativeness of the exposed cohort as defined by NOS, which indicates low risk of bias in the selection of the population. Additionally, all studies satisfied the selection criteria for the non-exposed cohort in NOS, implying that the population was drawn from the same source, thereby minimising the risk of selection bias. Furthermore, all studies demonstrated that the outcome variable (retirement) was not present at baseline. Each study adhered to a prospective design, and the inclusion criteria specified that participants must be employed.

Of the 19 studies, 74% used written self-reported measures of exposure and 63% of the studies used self-reported outcome measures, which may introduce selection and information bias. 21% of the studies did not have a follow-up time of at least three years, and 47% did not provide sufficient descriptions of completeness or the loss to follow-up (Fig. 2).

Syntheses of the evidence

In our protocol, we stipulated that in instances where a meta-analysis is unfeasible, we would use the IWH (Institute for Work & Health) best evidence synthesis [46, 47] approach to ascertain the strength of evidence between the studies. However, due to the low quality of the included studies, we had to disregard the IWH methodology, as it inherently excludes studies of low quality [47]. Consequently, we adopted the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) approach instead.

The summary of the evidence demonstrated mixed results. The quality, based on GRADE for all analyses, including subgroup analyses, was rated as “very low” (Table 3). The quality of evidence was classified as ‘very low’ due to study design, the number of studies including participants, high risk of bias, lack of adjustment for confounding factors, inconsistency, indirectness and imprecision.

Additional analyses

In our literature search, we adopted a broad approach in accordance with the PICO framework. Consequently, this led to the inclusion of studies examining both skills development and opportunities for skills development, as well as the lack of skills development in relation to either prolonging working lives, early retirement or the reduction of early retirement. We grouped our 19 studies into three primary subgroups based on both the exposures and outcomes (Table 3). Additionally, we subsequently grouped the included studies into five secondary subgroups based on either exposure or outcome (Table 4).

Primary subgroup analyses

Skills development or opportunities for skills development and reduction in early retirement

Of the 19 included studies, four studies involving 9316 participants investigated skills development or opportunities for skills development in relation to the reduction of early retirement [29–32]. All four studies presented a positive association between skills development and a reduction in early retirement. Three studies were of low quality, while one was of high quality. The overall evidence, as assessed by GRADE, was rated as very low due to study design, the number of studies, risk of bias, and lack of adjustment for confounding factors (Table 3).

Lack of skills development and early retirement

Three of the 19 included studies, encompassing 6637 participants, investigated the lack of skills development and its association with early retirement [43–45]. One out of three studies presented an association between a lack skills development and early retirement. The study was of high quality. The overall evidence from GRADE was rated as very low due to study design, the number of studies, risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision (Table 3).

Skills development or opportunities for skills development and delay or prolong working lives

Twelve out of 19 included studies, with a total of 1,073,796 participants and 759,931 person years, investigated skills development or opportunities for skills development in relation to delaying retirement or prolonging working lives [13, 28, 33–42]. Seven studies found a positive association. Three studies were of high quality, while four were of low quality. The overall evidence from GRADE was rated as “very low” due to study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness and imprecision (Table 3).

Secondary subgroup analyses

Grouped by exposure

Opportunities for skills development and reduction in early retirement, delay retirement or prolong working lives

Of the 19 included studies, 12 studies involving 29,302 participants investigated opportunities or possibilities for skills development in relation to reduction of early retirement, delay in retirement or, prolonging working lives [13, 30–32, 34, 36, 37, 41–45]. Eight out of 12 studies found a significant association. Four of the studies were of high quality, while four were of low quality. The overall evidence from GRADE was rated as “very low” due to study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision (Table 4).

Four out of the seven studies found a significant association. One study was of high quality, while three were of low quality. The overall evidence from GRADE was rated “very low” due to study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision (Table 4).

Grouped by outcome

Skills development or opportunities for skills development and intention to retire

Four out of 19 studies involving 13,652 participants, investigated the relationship between skills development or opportunities for skills development and intention to retire [30, 38, 41, 45]. Three out of four studies found a significant association [30, 38, 41]. All of the studies were of low quality. The overall evidence from GRADE was rated “very low” due to study design, the numbers of studies, risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision (Table 4).

Skills development or opportunities for skills development and prediction of retirement

Three out of 19 studies, with 6156 participants, investigated the relationship between skills development or opportunities for skills development and prediction of retirement [31, 36, 44]. Two out of three found a significant association [31, 36]. Both studies were of low quality. The overall evidence from GRADE was rated “very low” due to study design, the numbers of studies, risk of bias, inconsistency, and imprecision (Table 4).

Seven out of 12 found a significant association [13, 29, 32, 33, 39, 42, 43]. Five studies were of high quality, while two were of low quality. The overall evidence from GRADE was rated “very low” due to study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision (Table 4).

Discussion

Summary of results and evidence

This systematic review of prospective cohort studies provides insights into the association between skills development, or opportunities for skills development, and labour market participation. We identified 19 studies and found no clear association between skills development and labour market participation. However, there were sufficient studies regarding opportunities for skills development (four studies) to conclude that these opportunities of skills development are associated with a reduction in early retirement. Nonetheless, the evidence is rated as “very low” according to GRADE criteria, indicating that additional studies with low risk of bias are necessary. The evidence regarding the lack of skills development and early retirement, as well as the relationship between skills development or opportunities for skills development and delayed retirement or prolonging working lives, remains insufficient or mixed.

One potential explanation for these mixed results could be that the availability of skills development or opportunities for skills development might create expectations for progression and pressure to develop. For workers who are content with their current level of skills development, the prospect of additional training may feel overwhelming and could lead them to consider retiring earlier [48]. A previous systematic review identified similar mixed results in seven studies, highlighting this challenge [20]. Thus, it is important to discuss whether skills development serves as a resource for workers or as a necessity to meet increasing demands.

Previous studies indicate that skills development can be viewed both as a resource that empowers workers by enhancing their capabilities and as a necessity imposed by evolving job requirements [49, 50]. For instance, Sousa et al. demonstrates that ongoing skills development not only improves employability but also fosters job satisfaction and engagement among workers [49]. Conversely, Goulart et al. highlights that the rapid pace of change in various industries often necessitates continuous learning to remain competitive [50]. This dual perspective emphasizes the importance of considering both the empowering and obligatory aspects of skills development in today’s labour market.

When implementing skills development in workplace, it may be necessary to adapt training initiatives to different types of job groups. For workers with higher education levels engaged in sedentary jobs (white collar), skills development may be considered as a valuable resource. Conversely, for workers with physically demanding work (blue collar), skills development might be deemed essential in performing their daily work. These considerations may help explain why no associations were found in seven out of the 19 studies. Furthermore, a recently published study has shown that providing adequate training in the use of new technology for older workers enhances their labour market attachment [14]. These findings are equally important to consider when implementing skills development programmes among senior workers at the workplace.

Quality within and between studies

The quality of the studies, as assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa scale, varied greatly. Eight studies were of high quality and 11 were of low quality, which indicates a high risk of bias in the majority of the studies. The risk of bias was particularly prominent in the way the studies collected data, both on skills development and when the workers retired. Many low quality studies used self-reported data, which may lead to recall bias, non-response bias, selection bias, as well as information bias. Another challenge is that some studies lacked complete follow-up data, and the completeness may differ among the exposed and unexposed groups; hence, the participants in each group are not fully described, which could lead to selection bias and, as a result, the association could be under- or overestimated. The mixed quality and the potential for high risk of bias in our systematic review hinder us from achieving a moderate degree of confidence in our subsequent evidence synthesise and subsequent recommendations.

Methodological considerations

We used the GRADE approach to summarise the overall evidence of the included studies. All our analyses, except for one, demonstrated high risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision, and were based on observational study designs. One subgroup analysis based on an observational study design, a low number of studies and risk of bias, demonstrated consistency, directness, and precision between the studies, though it included only one high quality study. It can be questioned whether GRADE is the most suitable tool for evaluating observational studies, as GRADE was primarily developed to assess study quality in the context of randomised controlled trials, without accounting for circumstances where observational studies might be more suitable. This limitation arises from the fact that certain research questions, including ours, may require a prospective observational study design, where outcomes, such as retirement and working life expectancy, develop gradually over time.

The optimal approach to evidence selection is subject to debate, particularly regarding the trade-off between the highest level of evidence from studies with a limited follow-up time and the second highest level of evidence from studies that incorporate an extended follow-up periods. There is an on-going debate on whether or not it is warranted to downgrade the evidence in the GRADE framework based on study design considerations. A study from 2016 addressed the same concerns due to the rigid lines separating study designs in the evidence pyramid [51]. In this article, the author argued that a modification of the evidence hierarchy from a straight line to more “wary lines” was needed when rating the quality of evidence according to study design. A similar consideration arises concerning the sample size, as observational studies typically exhibit larger sample sizes in comparison to randomised controlled trials. If the elements of GRADE didn’t downgrade the quality of the evidence due to study design, a greater degree of confidence would be placed in the results, particularly in the subgroup where all four studies point in the same direction and investigated skills development or opportunities for skills development and their association with the reduction of early retirement.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review is the first that presents a comprehensive analysis of existing literature on skills development, opportunities for skills development or lack of skills development and labour market participation, including prolong working lives, and reduction in early retirement or early retirement. A strength of this systematic review is that the review is registered in PROSPERO. In instances where we deviated from the registered protocol, we have provided the rationale behind such decisions, thereby preserving transparency in our reporting. We followed PRISMA, NOS, and GRADE guidelines, which strengthen the review, making it more rigorous, transparent and systematic. The search strategy is thorough and comprehensive, and we have included several databases. We collaborated with two search specialists on the construction of the search string and selection of databases. Throughout the review process, both in relation to screening and quality assessment and data extraction, we had two independent reviewers, which has strengthened the quality of the review. The included studies exhibited relatively long follow-up periods, with participation from over one million participants. Regarding our protocol and the preceding systematic literature search, there was initial uncertainty about the feasibility of identifying a sufficient number of prospective studies. However, we ultimately identified and included 19 relevant studies. All studies, except one, were of recent origin (2005–2022) and represent the latest research within the area. While this review contributes to an understanding of skills development and labour market participation, it is important to acknowledge the limitations that may affect how the findings are interpreted and applied.

The questions regarding opportunities for skills development require the individual to predict their future to the best of their ability, which can be difficult to measure. The same applies to the outcome measure ‘intention to retire’. If the measures had focused on the actual skills development and the occurrence of retirement, one could anticipate more consistent and reliable outcomes. The study design of the included studies is identified as a limitation, especially concerning the potential for selection bias in the observational studies, particularly when utilizing self-reported measures. However, While self-reported data is often associated with selection and information bias, it remains a valuable tool for capturing specific information that may not be readily available through register data. For exposures such as adult skills development, self-reported measures may be the only feasible way to obtain comprehensive data. Therefore, despite the inherent biases, self-reported data can contribute meaningfully to understanding the relationship between skills development and labour market participation.

Due to inconsistent results, different estimates, and variations in measurement approaches across the included studies, methodological heterogeneity exists. Consequently, conducting a comprehensive meta-analysis is not feasible. Imprecision, indirectness, and inconsistency between the overall and subgroup results are too prominent.

The future labour market

In the present and the future alike, there is a need for more skilled workers. As indicated in the introduction, demographic changes are occurring throughout Europe. Without interventions regarding demography and lack of skills, potential consequences include a reduction in productivity and the challenges in maintaining the current service level in the welfare sector [8]. Achieving this necessitates skills development alongside an understanding of the factors that encourage workers to remain in the workforce. In this context, it is possible to potentially mitigate both push and pull factors by reducing demands through the upskilling of workers engaged in physically demanding jobs and increasing resources for workers with sedentary jobs (white collar) [10, 39, 40].

While the evidence may not uniformly point in the same direction or exhibit robustness regarding the association between skills development and labour market participation, several studies indicate an association. This review suggests the need for future studies that specifically examine the relationship between actual skills development and labour market participation by using registry data. Such an approach would greatly enhance the strength of the evidence and reduce the risk of bias. To this end, Prospective register studies are not infallible, as they can be affected by various forms of bias, such as selection bias, and challenges in establishing clear causal relationships. To clarify the causality between skills development and labour market participation, randomised controlled trials would be appropriate if the follow-up period is suitable.

Conclusion

This systematic review yields mixed results regarding the association between actual skills development, opportunities for skills development and prolonging working life, lack of skills development and early retirement. We cannot conclude that skills development is associated with delaying retirement or prolonging working lives. Four studies suggest a positive association between opportunities for skills development or actual skills development and a reduction in early retirement; however, due to low certainty of the evidence, we have very little confidence in the estimate, and the true association may be substantially different. Despite the lack of strong evidence to suggest that skills development prolongs working life, we refrain from conclusively asserting the absence of such an association. Therefore, we conclude that skills development remains a viable tool that can potentially be utilized to enable workers to extend their working lives.
