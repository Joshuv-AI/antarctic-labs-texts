Mannose-modified miR-223 nanoparticles remodel pathological microenvironment to suppress inflammation and angiogenesis for neovascular AMD therapy
Authors: Yuanli Wang, Wenyi Zhang, Feifan Cheng, Jianghao Wang, Kai Xiao, Danxia Wang, Xueqin Zhao, Ruitao Zou, Qingqing Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a leading cause of vision loss in the elderly, with neovascular AMD (nAMD) driven by choroidal neovascularization (CNV), persistent inflammation, and oxidative stress. Although combinatorial strategies targeting these pathological factors hold therapeutic promise, their clinical translation is constrained by the lack of effective delivery systems. In this study, we designed a mannose-functionalized poly(aspartic acid)-based nanocarrier bearing quaternary ammonium and boronic acid groups (MDA/QPABA) for the targeted delivery of microRNA-223 (miR-223). The system exploits charge-driven self-assembly to form stable nanoparticles with high loading efficiency, favorable colloidal stability, and tunable surface properties. The mannose moieties enable specific recognition by mannose receptors on target cells, facilitating cellular uptake and subsequent lysosomal escape. The resulting MDA/QPABA/miR-223 nanoparticles demonstrated pronounced anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-angiogenic activities in vitro. In a laser-induced CNV mouse model, they effectively reduced inflammatory and angiogenic cytokines, suppressed pathological neovascularization, restored retinal and choroidal structure, and preserved photoreceptor function, with an excellent biosafety. These findings highlight the potential of rationally engineered poly(aspartic acid)-based nanoarchitectures with tailored surface chemistry and biointerfacial properties for multifunctional nucleic acid delivery in nAMD therapy.

Introduction

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a chronic degenerative disease of the macula and the leading cause of irreversible vision loss in people over 60 years of age. Its prevalence increases with age, affecting over 196 million in 2020 people worldwide (Zhang et al., 2022; Guymer and Campbell, 2023; Al-Zamil and Yassin, 2017). AMD is a multifactorial retinal disease associated with age, ethnicity, environmental factors, lifestyle, cataract surgery, and genetic variation (Saigal et al., 2025; Heesterbeek et al., 2020; Nouri et al., 2022). AMD causes central vision loss through macular degeneration, featuring drusen (subretinal lipid-protein deposits) (Brito et al., 2024). Basal laminar deposits form between retinal pigment epithelial cells (RPE) and Bruch's membrane (Pugazhendhi et al., 2021), with drusen indicating RPE dysfunction that progresses to RPE atrophy and photoreceptor death (Marazita et al., 2016). Clinically, there are two main forms of AMD: dry (atrophic) and wet (exudative or neovascular) (Mitchell et al., 2018). While neovascular AMD (nAMD) represents only 10–15% of AMD cases, it accounts for nearly 90% of severe vision loss in AMD patients (Wang et al., 2025). Wet AMD features choroidal neovascularization (CNV) secondary to retinal degeneration, where new vessels invade subretinal, intra-Bruch's, or sub-RPE spaces, causing rapid vision loss (Handa et al., 2019; Tong et al., 2016). These abnormal vessels lead to subretinal hemorrhage, exudation, ischemia, and RPE detachment (Badia et al., 2023; Spaide et al., 2020), ultimately progressing to fibrotic scarring around neovascular complexes (Handa et al., 2019).

Anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy currently represents the primary treatment for nAMD. Intravitreal injections of agents such as ranibizumab and aflibercept effectively inhibits pathological neovascularization and reduces vascular leakage (Luksa et al., 2021). In addition, laser photocoagulation and photodynamic therapy may be employed in select cases to complement primary anti-VEGF treatment. However, long-term intravitreal injection prevents normal repair of the retina-choroidal complex, exacerbates hypoxia and oxidative stress, and ultimately leads to RPE damage, photoreceptor degeneration, and irreversible visual impairment (Kim et al., 2022; Campochiaro and Akhlaq, 2021). Furthermore, this treatment modality carries a risk of injection-related intraocular infections (Kim et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2020). Notably, approximately 30% of patients exhibit poor or suboptimal response to anti-VEGF therapy, significantly limiting its clinical efficacy. Studies have shown that in addition to hypoxia, the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals contributes to the pathogenesis of nAMD. Elevated free radicals can trigger RPE cells to overexpression of growth factors (Liukkonen et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2024), leading to a pro-inflammatory state, compromising Bruch's membrane integrity, and ultimately driving abnormal angiogenesis. Given the critical role of inflammation in both physiological and pathological retinal angiogenesis, anti-inflammatory therapies may offer clinical benefits for ocular angiogenesis cases that are refractory to current anti-VEGF treatments (Yeh et al., 2023). Collectively, VEGF, ROS, and inflammation synergistically drive the pathogenesis of choroidal neovascularization (CNV). Thus, developing multifunctional therapeutic strategies that simultaneously target angiogenesis (anti-VEGF), oxidative stress (ROS scavenging), and inflammation holds significant promise for improving CNV treatment outcomes.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are evolutionarily conserved, single-stranded non-coding RNAs, typically 21–25 nucleotides in length. They regulate gene expression by binding to target mRNAs through diverse biological pathways, modulating critical cellular processes such as proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, and migration (Jang et al., 2025). Emerging evidence highlights the therapeutic potential of targeting miRNAs as a novel strategy for managing prevalent and vision-threatening retinal diseases, particularly AMD. Accumulating research reveals that miRNAs can regulate key pathophysiological processes implicated in age-related macular degeneration (AMD), including pathological angiogenesis, oxidative stress responses, and immune-inflammatory pathways (Martinez and Peplow, 2021). Our previous study demonstrated that microRNA-223 (miR-223) regulates microglial polarization and suppresses pathological angiogenesis by downregulating key components of the VEGF and Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling pathways (Huang et al., 2022). Despite their therapeutic potential, miRNA-based therapeutics face significant challenges including nuclease-mediated degradation in vivo, off-target effects, and poor cellular uptake due to their negative charge, compounded by limited blood-retinal barrier penetration (Sun et al., 2024; Long and Danesh, 2022). These challenges underscore the critical need to develop nanoparticle-based delivery systems capable of efficiently transporting miR-223 to target cells while preserving its biological activity.

Given the dual role of retinal macrophages in promoting both neovascularization and inflammation during retinopathy (Wynn and Vannella, 2016; Li et al., 2024), therapeutic strategies should aim to modulate both macrophage phenotypes to effectively suppress pathological angiogenesis and inflammation. Therefore, in the present work, we designed a mannose-modified poly(aspartic acid) (PASP) derivative (MDA/QPABA/miR-223) drug delivery system to target nAMD therapy (Scheme 1). During inflammation, the expression of the macrophage-specific mannose receptor (CD206) is upregulated. This allows PEGylated dopamine-conjugated mannose (MDA) to effectively target macrophages, enhancing the enrichment of nanocomplexes at inflammatory sites (Bera et al., 2024; Uehara et al., 2022). Additionally, the released dopamine scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) through reduction, owing to its catecholamine group, thereby mitigating inflammatory and oxidative stress effects in the pathological progression of AMD (Wang et al., 2021). Furthermore, the poly(aspartic acid) (PASP) derivatives-functionalized with quaternary ammonium and boronic acid groups (QPABA)-facilitate charge-driven self-assembly with miR-223. In the intracellular lysosomal environment (pH 4.0–5.5), the phenylborate ester bond cleaves, enabling lysosomal escape and promoting the rapid release of the loaded miRNA. Materials and Methods

Materials

Polyethylene glycol dicarboxylic acid (PEG-COOH 600 Da), 4-(4, 6-dimethoxytriazine-2-yl)-4-methylmorpholine hydrochloride (DMTMM), 1-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC), N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), polysuccinimide (PSI), 3-aminophenylboric acid (APBA), N, N-dimethyl-1, 3-propylenediamine (DMPA), sodium fluorescein purchased from Aladdin. Dopamine (DA) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Mannosamine hydrochloride (Man), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), Bromohexane, methanol purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co. (Shanghai, China). DMEM high-sugar medium was purchased from Gibco (USA) and Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM) from ScienCell Research Laboratories (USA). Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagents were purchased from Invitrogen (USA). Hoechst 33342, active oxygen detection kit, purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, All-in-one RT SuperMix for qPCR, Universal SyBR QPCR Master Mix, All-in-one RT Supermix for QPCR, miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit, miRNA Universal SYBR Master Mix was purchased from Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). miR-223 with the 5 ‘UGUCAGUUUGUCAAAUACCCCA-3’ sense sequence and 5 ‘GGGUAUUUGACAAACUGACAUU-3’ antisense sequence, as well as simulators and negative controls carrying FAM fluorescent labels were all from Qingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All chemicals were used without further purification unless otherwise specified.

Synthesis of Quaternary Ammonium-Modified Polyaspartamide Derivative (QPABA)

QPABA was synthesized through a two-step modification process (Li et al., 2023). Firstly, poly succinimide (PSI, 3.88 g, 32 mmol) and 3-aminophenylboronic acid (APBA, 1.64 g, 10.7 mmol) were dissolved in 40 mL DMSO. After stirring vigorously for 24 h at 60 °C under N2 flow, the intermediate was precipitated in 200 mL methanol. Then DMPA (5.3 g, 52 mmol) reacted with 3.0 g intermediate in 30 mL DMSO at 40 °C for 24 h under N2 flow to prepare PASP-APBA-DMPA (PABA). Subsequently, the PABA polymer (2.0 g) reacted with bromohexane (1.2 mL, 8.4 mmol) in 20 mL DMSO for 24 h at 50 °C under N2 flow to prepare quaternary ammonium-modified polyaspartamide derivative (QPABA). The reaction mixture was precipitated into diethyl ether, and the white powder was obtained after 72 h of dialysis (MWCO 3500 Da).

Synthesis of Mannose-Conjugated PEG-Dopamine (MDA)

PEG-COOH (600 mg, 1 mmol) was dissolved in PBS buffer (10 mL, 50 mM, pH 7.4), followed by activation with 1.1 g of DMTMM under magnetic stirring for 10 min at room temperature. Then 189 mg DA was added to the reaction mixture to proceed for 12 h in the dark. The product, HOOC-PEG-DA, was purified by dialysis (MWCO 500 Da) against PBS (50 mM, pH 7.4). For mannose conjugation, HOOC-PEG-DA (1 mmol) was activated using EDC/NHS (molar ratio 1:1:4) in MES buffer (50 mM, pH 6.0) for 30 min. The Man (1 mmol) was added to the above solution and stirred for 12 h. After dialysis and freeze-drying, the prepared MDA conjugate was dialysate, freeze-dried and stored at 4 °C.

Formulation of QPABA/MDA/miR-223 nanocomplexes

QPABA/MDA/miR-223 nanocomplexes were prepared using the ionic gelation method. In brief, the negatively charged component miR-223 was added in the positively charged component QPABA in RNase-free water with the different ratios and vortexed for 30 s. Then the mixtures were incubated with MDA for 4 h at room temperature to self-assemble the nano-formulations. The QPABA/MDA/miR-223 nanocomplexes were obtained by centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 30 min) and then freeze-dried for storage. The amount of MDA was optimized in order to obtain monodisperse nanoparticles.

Physicochemical characterization

The morphology of prepared MDA/QPABA and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs was acquired using a Japanese JOEL JEM-F200 field emission transmission electron microscope (JOEL, Janpan) at 120 kV acceleration voltage. Additionally, the particle size distribution of MSN and PMSN NPs was analyzed using ImageJ software. The zeta potentials of the MDA/QPABA and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs were measured using a Malvern Zetasizer apparatus (NanoZS90, Westborough, MA). A Nicolet 6700 spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to measure the FTIR spectra of samples collected in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 32 cm−1. To detect the successful synthesis of PABA and MDA, proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR, Qone AS400) measurements were performed.

Gel retardation assay

Firstly, the agarose powder was dissolved in TAE (1×) buffer, heated and dissolved, cooled to about 60 °C, and prepared into a 1% agarose gel. After cooling, GelRed was added and shaken to mix well, poured into the rubber plate and inserted into the comb, and left to solidify at room temperature. The RNA sample to be tested is then mixed with an equal volume of denatured loading buffer. A sufficient amount of TAE buffer was added to the electrophoresis tank to fully immerse the gel, and the sample and the RNA molecular weight standard were added to the sample hole successively. Power on, 100 V constant voltage electrophoresis was performed, stopping until the tracer dye migrates to 3/4 of the gel. After electrophoresis, the gel was directly observed in an ultraviolet imager.

In vitro release studies

We used sodium fluorescein (FS) as a drug model molecule. QPABA and FS were blended on a shader at 4 °C and 100 rpm at a mass ratio of 10:1 for 6 h, and then the mixture was mixed with MDA at a mass ratio of 20:1. The reaction was continued at 4 °C and 100 rpm for 4 h to obtain MDA/QPABA/FS NPs. Two NPs of the same volume and quality were dissolved in 1 mL ultra-pure water respectively, and then transferred to a boiled dialysis bag. The dialysis bag was placed into a centrifuge tube containing DPBS, H2O2, and pH = 5 solution respectively, and incubated on a rail shaker at 37 °C and 90 rpm. At 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h, 0.5 mL of supernatant was collected, and 0.5 mL of fresh DPBS, H2O2, pH = 5 solution was re-added to the centrifuge tube. The collected supernatant was used for the study and analysis of drug release at the wavelength of 460 nm by the fluorescent enzyme labeled M5.

In vitro cell culture

Mouse macrophage cell lines (Raw 264.7) and human endothelial cell lines (HUVECs) were purchased from Procell Life Sci Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and ScienCell (USA). Raw 264.7 cells are cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) containing 15% FBS (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, USA). HUVECs are cultured in ECM containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ScienCell, USA), 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS, ScienCell, USA), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (ScienCell, USA). All cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (Thermo Scientific, USA) with 5% CO2.

In vitro cytotoxicities and live/dead staining

Macrophages cells (Raw 264.7) and endothelial cells (HUVECs) were inoculated into 96-well plates (5 × 103/well) with 3–6 multiple wells in each group, and cultured in a 5% CO2 cell incubator at 37 °C. After 24 h, the prepared experimental group solution was successively added into the hole plate at 100 μL/well and cultured in the incubator for 24 h. After treatment, 100 μL/well of prepared CCK8 working solution was added to each well (CCK8: culture medium without FBS =1:9), incubated for 1–2 h, and the absorbance value at 450 nm was measured using a multifunctional enzyme marker.

Cell uptake assay

Raw 264.7 cells and HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator for 24 h until adherent. Subsequently, the experimental group (QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs) configured with 2 mL/well was added to the corresponding well plate and incubated in the incubator for 6 h. After observing the growth of the cells with a microscope, the plate was washed twice with DPBS, trypsinized, and centrifuged, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells were resuspended with 200 μL/tube medium, then moved into a 1.5 mL EP tube and pipetted slowly to mix. The resuspended cells were filtered with a strainer to prevent the cells from blocking the injection needle, and the FAM-positive cell counts in Raw 264.7 cells and HUVECs cells were counted using a C6 Plus flow cytometer. The targeting of NPs to Raw 264.7 was analyzed by comparing the number of FAM+ cell in MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs with QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs, and the statistics were plotted using FlowJo-V software.

Transfection and lysosome escape abilities of MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs

Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells/well and cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. Once adherent, cell confluence reached 80–90%, as confirmed by microscopy. The cells were then treated with 50 μg/mL MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs. After 24 h, total RNA was extracted, quantified, and reverse-transcribed into cDNA for real-time PCR analysis, using U6 small nuclear RNA (snRNA) as the reference gene.

For fluorescence imaging, cells were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/well in a 24-well plate containing crawler slides and incubated under the same conditions. Upon adherence, the cells were treated with QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs and MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs. After 6 h, the medium was discarded, and cells were washed with DPBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (50–10 min), and washed again. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:1000 dilution, 5 min), followed by additional DPBS washes. The crawler slides were mounted on glass slides using an anti-fluorescence quencher, and images were acquired using an LSM 880 confocal microscope for transfection efficiency analysis.

Following the same protocol, lysosomes were labeled with LysoTracker Red (50 nM, 1:20,000 dilution), incubated at 37 °C for 30 min (protected from light), and imaged using an LSM 900 microscope. Fluorescence signals were assigned as follows: FAM (miR-223, Green), DAPI (nuclei, Blue) and LysoTracker Red (Red).

Quantitative Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells and purified using the RNAprep Pure Cell Kit. RNA was then converted to cDNA with a HiScript III All-in-one RT SuperMix apparatus. The expression of genes (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-4, IL-10, CD206, ARG-1, VEGFR-2, CTGF, CYR61, and CDH5–1) was quantified by RT-PCR. β-actin was used as the internal reference gene. The 2-ΔΔCt method was used for relative quantification. Primers are listed in the supporting information (Table S1).

Wound healing assay

5 × 105 cells were seeded in 6-well plates, evenly spread in the plates, and placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator for 24 h. After the cell density reached 95%–100%, discard the waste solution, wash the plate twice with DPBS, and starve the cells with FBS-free medium. Scratch the plate perpendicular to the ruler with a 200 μL pipette tip to remove scraped cell debris, wash twice with DPBS, and add 2 mL/well of FBS-free diluted material (MDA/QPABA NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs) to the experimental group. It was incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator for 24 h and 48 h. Remove the plate at the desired corresponding time point and use the DMi8 to photograph the scratch width at the same location in the plate. Cell mobility was analyzed by measuring the width of cell migration using ImageJ software.

Transwell invasion assay

Dilute Matrigel 30:1 with FBS-free ECGS-free culture and add vertically dropwise in a transwell upper chamber, then incubate overnight in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator. Add 50 μL/well of FBS-free ECGS-free medium to the transwell upper chamber and place it in the incubator for 1 h of hydration. Cells were digested after 24 h of material action (MDA/QPABA NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs) and then resuspended in FBS-free and ECGS-free medium and seeded 4 × 104/well cells into the upper chamber. At the same time, 300 μL/well of material diluted with 10% FBS and 10% EGCS medium was added to the lower chamber and incubated in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator for 24 h. Add 400 μL/well of 4% paraformaldehyde to the lower chamber for 15 min, wipe off the cells in the upper chamber with a moistened cotton swab, add 400 μL/well crystal violet to the lower chamber, and stain in the dark for 30 min. After staining, wash off excess dye in the upper chamber and store at room temperature in the dark. The invaded cells were photographed using DM4B, and the invaded cells were counted and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

Add 50 μL/well of Matrigel to a pre-chilled 96-well plate with three replicates in each set. The cells (MDA/QPABA NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs) were digested and resuspended overnight in a 37 °C incubator, and then added to a 96-well plate dropwise at 3 × 104/well and 50 μL/well, and cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator for 6 h. The tube-forming state of the cells was observed under the microscope. Cells were photographed using DMi8.

Fluorescence imaging of ROS scavenging

5 × 105 cells were seeded in a 24-well plate, evenly spread in the plate, and placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator for 24 h. After that, remove the culture medium and treated with treated with QPABA/MDA and QPABA/MDA/miR-223 NPs for 24 h. Following treatment, the medium was discarded, and cells were washed with DPBS twice, cells were exposed to 400 μM H2O2 for 4 h. Subsequently, a DCFH-DA fluorescent probe (diluted 1:1000) was added and incubated for 30 min in the dark. Fluorescence images were captured using a DMi8 laser scanning microscope.

Flow cytometric measurement of ROS

1 × 106 cells were pre-seeded in a 6-well plate, evenly spread in the plate, and placed in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell culture incubator. After the cells were attached, remove the culture medium and treated with treated with QPABA/MDA and QPABA/MDA/miR-223 NPs for 24 h. After that, cells were stimulated with 400 μM H2O2 for 4 h. To measure intracellular ROS, cells were harvested, and the pellet was resuspended in DCFH-DA (diluted 1:1000), gently pipetted to mix, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 min. After incubation, cells were washed three times with DPBS to remove excess probe. The cell suspension was analyzed using a C6 Plus Flow Cytometer to quantify ROS levels.

Western blot analysis

Cells are completely lysed by sonication by extracting the proteins of the cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, USA) containing a mixture of 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of lysate and diluted protein loading buffer mixture (20 μg) were added to a 10% SDS-PAGE gel (Epizyme, China), transferred to a PVDF membrane (Epizyme, China), and blocked for 2 h at room temperature in 5 wt% milk. At 4 °C, the membrane was placed in a primary antibody solution (anti-iNOS, anti-ARG-1, anti-IL-10; Anti-VEGF, anti-CTGF, anti-CYR61) and then probed with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Tubulin was used as a standard control. Imaging was developed using an all-rounder gel imaging system (Thermo Scientific, USA).

In vivo animal experiment

All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the Association for the Study in Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) statement approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Wenzhou Medical University. Male C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old, 18–22 g) were obtained from Zhejiang Vitron Life Sciences and housed under SPF conditions at a density of 6 per cage (22 ± 2 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity, 12-h light/dark cycle) with free access to sterilized food and water. The litter was changed regularly, and water and feed were added in time. After a 7-day acclimation, mice were randomly assigned (by random number tables, stratified by body weight, with cage rotation) to six groups (n = 8 per group): blank control, untreated CNV model, Conbercept positive control, MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC, and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 groups. All experiments were repeated at least three times, and data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical comparisons used one-way ANOVA followed by t-tests, with significance set at p < 0.05.

In vivo laser-induced mouse CNV model

The purchased C57BL/6 J mice were placed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) for one week and then subjected to follow-up experiments. According to the body weight of the mice, 1% sodium pentobarbital was injected for general anesthesia, the mice were dilated with 1% tropicamide eye drops, and 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops were instilled for local anesthesia after 5 min, and levofloxacin ophthalmic ointment was applied to the ocular surface of the mice in time to prevent the cornea from drying out and cataracts in mice. After the mouse was completely anesthetized, the slit lamp laser modeler was set up for 200 mW and 100 ms to perform laser modeling around the mouse at 2–3 PD from the optic disk. The white bubbles appeared after photocoagulation under the microscope and gradually became smaller with time, indicating that the Bruch membrane had been broken. The day of modeling is recorded as day 0. Predefined exclusion criteria (failed Bruch's membrane rupture, massive hemorrhage, anesthesia non-recovery, severe debilitation, or death) were not met during the study; thus, all 8 mice per group were included in the final analyses.

Intravitreal injection administration in mice

On the first day after modeling, the mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (CNV group, Conbercept group, MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs) after general anesthesia and local anesthesia. They were observed under the operating microscope, and poked a small hole with a sterile insulin needle at 2 mm behind the corneal limbus of the mice. After the drug was evenly pipetted, 2 μL of the drug was absorbed into the hole. The fluid was injected slowly as the needle reached the center of the vitreous cavity. After the operation, levofloxacin eye ointment was applied to the ocular surface of the mouse, which was then placed on a heating pad and put back into the cage after it woke up, and the ocular surface of the mouse was observed, and the ocular ointment was replenished in time. The day of dosing is recorded as the 1st day.

Fundus Fluorescein Angiography (FFA) and Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) evaluation

On days 7 and 14 post-intervention, mice were anesthetized and their pupils dilated. Following body weight measurement, each mouse received an intraperitoneal injection of 4% sodium fluorescein solution (100 μL/10 g body weight). After a 3-min circulation period, fundus photography was performed using a mouse retinal microscopy system. Fluorescein leakage areas were quantified using ImageJ software.

For optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging, mice were properly restrained and positioned. Retinal scans were acquired using Heidelberg OCT, with particular focus on laser photocoagulation sites. Following imaging procedures, levofloxacin ophthalmic ointment was applied to protect the ocular surface. Mice were maintained on a warming pad until fully recovered from anesthesia before being returned to their home cages. Retinal thickness measurements were analyzed using ImageJ software for comparative assessment.

Electroretinography (ERG) analysis

Mice underwent electroretinography on the 14th and 21st days after intravitreal injection and were placed in a dark room overnight one day in advance. Routine anesthesia and pupil dilation were performed on the mice after dark adaptation. The mice were fixed in the center of the operating table, the ground electrode was inserted into the subcutaneous tail of the mouse, the left and right reference electrodes were inserted into the middle of the left and right eyes and ears of the mouse, and the left and right corneal contact electrodes were fixed on the corneas of the left and right eyes respectively. The mice were placed in the most comfortable position in front of the flash stimulator, and after ensuring that the electrodes were inserted correctly, the operating table was slowly pushed into the stimulation ball. The blackout cloth was pulled, the parameters of the stimulation light were set, and the dark adaptation, 25 cd/m2, and 10 were recorded in turn. To avoid individual differences and diurnal fluctuations in the mice, we chose to perform fERG detection at the same time period every day. The entire process was carried out under dark red light.

Retinal/choroidal flat mounts, Immunofluorescence (IF), and H&E staining

Following enucleation, eyes from CNV model mice were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Tissues were subsequently paraffin-embedded and sectioned for histological examination. For morphological assessment, selected sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), with quantitative analysis performed using ImageJ software.

For immunofluorescence staining, tissue sections were first blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by DPBS washes. Corresponding secondary antibodies were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (10 min, dark conditions), and after final DPBS washes, sections were mounted with anti-fluorescence quencher. Fluorescence images were acquired using a DM750 microscope (Leica, Germany).

Biosafety analysis

Following enucleation, the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys of the mice were collected and placed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube containing 4% paraformaldehyde and fixed at room temperature for 24 h. After fixation, tissues were transferred to labeled cassettes for further processing. Tissues underwent the same staining protocol as described previously. Briefly, sections were stained (H&E or immunofluorescence), and images were acquired using a bright-field/fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated more than 3 times, and all results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD) or mean ± standard error (Mean ± SEM). GraphPad Prism 8 and Origin software were used for mapping and statistical analysis. ANOVA analysis of variance was used to compare the data by two-sample t-test, and a difference of p < 0.05 was statistically significant, which was expressed as: * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, *** p < 0.001, n.s.: no significant statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of nanoparticles

To target inflammation and oxidative stress in nAMD pathogenesis, we developed a macrophage-targeting nanoparticle system by synthesizing a PASP backbone (QPABA) functionalized with phenylboronic acid and quaternary amine groups. The QPABA carrier was complexed with miR-223 through electrostatic self-assembly, then conjugated via boronic ester bond formation with the catechol groups of MDA, thereby achieving macrophage-specific targeting capability. The successful synthesis of QPABA and MDA was confirmed via FTIR spectroscopy and 1HNMR. In QPABA, the PSI peak at 1579 cm−1 shifted to 1660.43 cm−1 following ring opening, while the characteristic B—O stretching vibration of phenylboronic acid at 1345 cm−1 shifted to 1371 cm−1. Additionally, the -CH₃ symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of N, N-dimethyl-1,3-propanediamine (observed at 2956.38 and 2861.89 cm−1, respectively) exhibited reduced intensity after quaternization. As for MDA, the peaks at 1600 cm−1 and 817 cm−1 were assigned to the amide band II, and the bending vibration of C—H on 1,2,4-trisubstituted benzene (Fig. 1B). The 1HNMR of PABA showed peaks at δ8.0–7.4 ppm due to the hydrogen atoms on the benzene ring and the characteristic peaks on DMPA could be found at δ3.1–1.5, which indicated that the successfully phenylboronic- and N, N-dimethyl-bonds onto the poly-aspartic acid polymer chain (Li et al., 2023). To determine the optimal mass ratio for the reaction between QPABA and MDA, we prepared mixtures at different ratios (Fig. 1E-J). TEM analysis revealed that at a 1:20 (QPABA: MDA) ratio, the NPs exhibited uniform size and monodispersion (Fig. 1H). Deviating from this ratio led to irregular NP sizes and incomplete morphologies. Based on these findings, we selected the 1:20 mass ratio for NP synthesis in subsequent experiments. Subsequently, we conducted TEM analysis of QPABA/miR-223 NPs and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs (Fig. 1K, L). The images revealed that the NPs without MDA modification exhibited irregular aggregation, whereas the MDA-conjugated NPs formed uniform, spherical, and stable nanoparticles. This distinct morphological transformation confirmed the successful loading of MDA. Next, we assessed miRNA loading by QPABA using agarose gel electrophoresis (Fig. 1M). The results demonstrated that miR-NC was completely encapsulated by QPABA at a mass ratio of 10:1 (QPABA: miR-NC, μg/μg). We further characterized the NPs using dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 1N). The hydrodynamic diameters of MDA/QPABA, QPABA/miR-223, and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs were measured as 712 nm, 220 nm, and 342 nm, respectively, confirming successful nanoparticles formation. Zeta potential analysis (Fig. 1O) revealed that QPABA exhibited a positive charge (+20.3 mV), while MDA was slightly negative (−1.45 mV). Upon reaction with MDA, the zeta potential of QPABA decreased from +19.7 mV to +11.1 mV. Further reduction in potential occurred after loading negatively charged miR-223, demonstrating successful encapsulation of both MDA and miR-223.

The stimuli-responsive release profile of miR-223 from MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs was evaluated using FS as a model drug. Release studies were conducted in DPBS, hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and pH 5.0 DPBS solutions to assess responsiveness to different conditions. Data analysis revealed significantly enhanced nanoparticle release in pH 5.0 and H₂O₂ solutions compared to DPBS over 96 h (Fig. 1P), demonstrating the dual ROS- and pH-responsive release properties of MDA/QPABA/FS NPs. Notably, FS exhibited maximal release efficiency under H₂O₂ conditions, highlighting its superior responsiveness to oxidative environments. These responsive behaviors promote lysosomal escape of miR-223, facilitating its cytoplasmic release and subsequent biological activity. We also used TEM to observe the morphological changes of MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs after treatment in DPBS, H2O2, and pH 5 DPBS solutions for 1 and 7 days (Fig. 1Q). The TEM images revealed that the MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs initially maintained structural integrity in DPBS but underwent gradual degradation over time. In contrast, structural collapse was observed in both H₂O₂ and pH 5.0 conditions, with the most pronounced disintegration occurring in H₂O₂ solution, which was consistent to the release profiles of FS in H2O2 (Fig. 1P).

In vitro cell cytotoxicities and targeting capabilities

To evaluate in vitro cytotoxicity, we treated macrophages (Raw 264.7 cells), human retinal epithelial cells (ARPE-19), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) with varying concentrations of QPABA NPs. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that QPABA exhibited no significant cytotoxicity toward any of the three cell lines at 50 μg/mL after a 24-h incubation (Fig. S1-S3). However, marked cytotoxicity was observed at 100 μg/mL. Based on these findings, we selected 50 μg/mL QPABA for subsequent experiments. We further evaluated the cytotoxicity of MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs, and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs on the three cell lines. Following a 24-h co-culture, all three NP formulations demonstrated significant cytotoxicity to both Raw 264.7 and ARPE-19 cells, yet maintained cell viability above 85% (Fig. 2A, B). In contrast, no significant cytotoxicity was observed in HUVECs under the same treatment conditions (Fig. 2C). Consistent to the CCK8 data, similar results were observed on the live/dead staining of MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC, and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs on Raw 264.7 (Fig. S4) and HUVECs (Fig. S5). Notably, HUVECs exhibited no apparent morphological changes following exposure to MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC, and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs (Fig. S6). To assess the cellular targeting effects of MDA-modified nanoparticles, we performed uptake studies on RAW 264.7 macrophages and HUVECs using flow cytometry. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that both QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs and MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs were efficiently internalized by Raw 264.7 macrophages and HUVECs after 6 h of incubation (Fig. 2D, E). Notably, MDA modification significantly enhanced cellular uptake, with MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs exhibiting 1.20-fold higher fluorescence intensity than unmodified NPs in Raw 264.7 cells. Furthermore, Raw 264.7 cells showed 7.62-fold greater uptake of MDA-modified NPs compared to HUVECs, demonstrating cell-type-dependent targeting efficiency. Subsequently, we used Raw 264.7 cells to measure the transfection efficiency of MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs. Confocal microscopy revealed minimal miR-223-FAM delivery by QPABA alone (Fig. 2H), while MDA/QPABA complexes demonstrated significantly enhanced cellular uptake. Quantitative PCR analysis further confirmed this observation, showing a 30-fold increase in miR-223 expression in cells treated with MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs compared to both MDA/QPABA and MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NP controls (Fig. 2I).

Effective lysosomal escape is critical for nanoparticle-mediated microRNA delivery (Parodi et al., 2015). In the acidic lysosomal environment (pH 4.0–5.5), the phenylborate ester bond in MDA/QPABA NPs breaks, facilitating lysosomal escape and triggering rapid miR-223 release. In order to detect the lysosomal escape ability of MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM NPs, we used Lipo2000 as a control and labeled lysosomes with Lysotracker (red), confocal microscopy images showed that after transfection of miR-223-FAM with Lipo2000 (Fig. 2J), FAM-labeled miR-223 (green) overlapped with lysosomal fluorescence signal, while MDA/QPABA/miR-223-FAM was transfected after 10 h of co-incubation with Raw 264.7 cells, the red and green fluorescence did not overlap, and the fluorescence signal was displayed in the cytoplasm, indicating that the encapsulation of miR-223-FAM with QPABA/MDA could effectively reduce the uptake of miRNA by lysosomes and successfully achieve lysosomal escape.

In vitro anti-inflammatory and ROS-scavenging capacities of MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs

To investigate the efficacy of QPABA/MDA/miR-223 NPs in reducing inflammatory factors and ROS in the inflammatory microenvironment, we induced inflammatory damage and oxidative stress in Raw 264.7 cells using lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), respectively (Zuo et al., 2025). RT-PCR analysis revealed that low LPS concentrations upregulated both M1 markers (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and M2 markers (IL-4, IL-10, ARG-1, CD206) (Zhang et al., 2024). QPABA/MDA and QPABA/MDA/miR-223 NPs effectively reduced the expression of both M1 and M2 macrophage phenotypes upon cellular interaction, with QPABA/MDA/miR-223 NPs exhibiting a stronger effect than QPABA/MDA alone (Fig. 3 A-G). Western blot analysis revealed that LPS stimulation upregulated the protein expression of IL-10, ARG-1 and iNOS. However, treatment with MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs significantly reduced the levels of these proteins, which was consistent with the RT-PCR results (Fig. 3H). We further evaluated the in vitro antioxidant activity of MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs using ROS fluorescence staining (Fig. 3I). Intracellular ROS levels in Raw 264.7 cells were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. Upon H₂O₂ stimulation, a marked increase in fluorescence intensity confirmed successful ROS induction. After the treatment of MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs, the fluorescence intensity of cells decreased significantly, demonstrating their ROS-scavenging capacity. Notably, quantitative analysis revealed that MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs exhibited superior antioxidant effects compared to MDA/QPABA alone (Fig. 3 J). Flow cytometry analysis corroborated the ROS fluorescence staining results (Fig. 3 K). While H₂O₂ treatment induced a rightward peak shift (indicating increased ROS), both MDA/QPABA and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NP treatments caused leftward shifts, demonstrating their ability to mitigate H₂O₂-induced ROS production. Quantitative analysis of mean DCFH-DA fluorescence intensity further confirmed the superior in vitro ROS-scavenging capacity of MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs (Fig. 3 L). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that miR-223 exhibits dual functionality: exerting anti-inflammatory effects in LPS-stimulated Raw 264.7 cells and enhancing the nanoparticles' antioxidant capacity in vitro. Furthermore, MDA's catechol groups contribute to both the anti-inflammatory response and the synergistic ROS-scavenging capability of the NPs.

In the Transwell invasion assay, crystal violet staining revealed that HUVEC migration to the lower chamber was significantly reduced upon treatment with MDA/QPABA, MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs, and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs (Fig. 4D). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that all three NPs markedly suppressed endothelial cell migration, with the MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs exhibiting the strongest inhibitory effect (Fig. 4F).

The tube formation assay is also used to evaluate the anti-angiogenic ability of HUVECs on NPs (Fig. 4E). Compared to the control group, HUVECs treated with MDA/QPABA and MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs exhibited a significant reduction in nodule formation, junctions, and mesh networks. Notably, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs completely suppressed mesh formation (Figs. 4G, H, S7). Quantitative analysis confirmed that all three nanoparticle formulations markedly decreased mesh formation, with the most pronounced inhibition observed in the MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs group.

RT-PCR analysis revealed that MDA/QPABA and MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs had some inhibitory effects on VEGFR-2, cysteine-rich protein 61 (CYR61), while MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs can significantly downregulate VEGFR-2, connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), and vascular endothelial cadherin (CDH5) expression compared to the control group (Figs. 4I-L) (Lin et al., 2024; Jacobson and Cunningham, 2012; Chang et al., 2016; Cheng et al., 2025). Consistent with these findings, Western blot analysis confirmed substantial reductions in CYR61, VEGF, and CTGF protein expression in HUVECs treated with MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs (Fig. 4M).

Angiogenesis is critically influenced by inflammation, as well as by reactive oxygen species (ROS) and free radicals, which upregulate VEGF expression in vascular smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells, thereby promoting neovascularization (Yao et al., 2023; Kambhampati et al., 2021). MDA/QPABA exerts a dual inhibitory effect by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, and scavenging ROS to mitigate oxidative stress. Furthermore, miR-223 intrinsically inhibits angiogenesis through its regulatory effects on endothelial cell function. MDA/QPABA inhibits the migration of HUVECs, while has no significant effect on HUVECs invasion and tube formation. While MDA/QPABA effectively inhibits HUVEC migration, it shows limited effects on invasion and tube formation - processes potentially governed by distinct molecular pathways involving CYR61 (CCN1) and other matricellular proteins. That's may due to invasion and tube formation are more closely associated with genes such as CYR61 (CCN1) (Yu et al., 2008; Quintero-Fabián et al., 2019). The synergistic combination of MDA/QPABA and miR-223 in the MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs thus enhances anti-angiogenic efficacy, resulting in superior suppression of neovascularization.

Photoreceptors in the retinal periphery rely on choroidal blood supply to sustain their high metabolic activity, making choroidal integrity crucial for photoreceptor health and function. To assess retinal function, we first performed full-field electroretinography (fERG) on healthy, non-lasered mice after overnight dark adaptation, establishing baseline values for normal retinal function. Subsequent fERG evaluations were conducted for all experimental groups under identical conditions (Fig. 6C-I, Fig. S10). After 28 days of treatment, the CNV group showed significantly reduced amplitudes of both a-waves (reflecting photoreceptor function) and b-waves (indicating bipolar cell and inner plexiform layer activity) under dark- and light-adapted conditions compared to healthy controls, demonstrating impaired rod/cone and bipolar cell function following laser induction. While MDA/QPABA and MDA/QPABA/miR-NC NPs partially restored a- and b-wave amplitudes, Conbercept and MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs showed more substantial functional recovery, with MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs performing comparably to Conbercept. Quantitative analysis of electroretinogram waveforms clearly illustrates these therapeutic effects, confirming that MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs effectively repair laser-induced retinal damage.

Finally, the biosafety of the prepared NPs was systematically evaluated through H&E staining of major organs and blood biochemical analysis. On day 28 post-modeling, we collected the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys from the CNV group and all treatment groups to assess potential systemic toxicity. Histological examination revealed intact organ morphology and structure (Fig. S11) with no abnormal discoloration, indicating no significant organ damage or NP deposition in any experimental group. Additionally, blood samples collected at day 28 were subjected to routine hematological analysis (Fig. S12). All groups maintained normal levels of white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), lymphocytes (Lymph), mean corpuscular hemoglobin content (MCH), and hemoglobin (HGB). Furthermore, kidney function markers (UREA and CREA) remained within normal ranges. These results demonstrate the excellent biocompatibility of the NPs following long-term administration.

Conclusions

The pathogenesis of age-related macular degeneration (AMD) involves not only angiogenesis but also chronic inflammation and oxidative stress. To address these mechanisms, we developed a novel nanodrug delivery system that effectively encapsulates miR-223, targets macrophages, and facilitates lysosomal escape, thereby offering a promising therapeutic strategy for nAMD. This microRNA delivery system incorporates PEG-modified dopamine and mannose, which synergistically enhance macrophage targeting while mitigating oxidative stress through dopamine-mediated antioxidant activity. In vitro studies demonstrated that MDA/QPABA/miR-223 nanoparticles (NPs) exhibit robust anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects, alongside significant inhibition of HUVEC migration, invasion, tube formation, and angiogenic factor expression. Moreover, in vivo evaluations further confirmed the therapeutic efficacy of MDA/QPABA/miR-223 NPs, showing significant reduction in laser-induced vascular leakage, restoration of retinal and choroidal structure, preservation of photoreceptor function, and demonstrating excellent biosafety. In conclusion, MDA/QPABA/miR-223 delivery system represents an innovative and multifaceted therapeutic approach for CNV, addressing key pathological features including angiogenesis, inflammation, and oxidative stress. The consistent efficacy observed in both in vitro and in vivo models underscore the miR-223 delivery system's translational promise in nAMD. Future studies should focus on optimizing controlled drug release kinetics to enhance therapeutic durability and elucidating underlying mechanisms to further refine the delivery system while ensuring biosafety.
