Impact of electronic cigarette use on the oral microbiome: a protocol for a systematic review of clinical studies
Authors: Giusy Rita Maria La Rosa, Lakshman Perera Samaranayake, Egija Zaura, Virginia Fuochi, Pio Maria Furneri, Jan Kowalski, Iain Chapple, Riccardo Polosa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe oral microbiome plays a pivotal role in maintaining both oral and systemic health, yet it can be disrupted by lifestyle factors such as tobacco use. Electronic cigarette (e-cig) has emerged as a popular alternative to conventional smoking, often perceived as a harm reduction tool. While combustible tobacco smoking is known to promote pathogenic shifts in the oral microbiota, evidence on the impact of e-cig use remains limited and inconsistent. A systematic synthesis of current evidence is therefore warranted to assess the potential microbiological and clinical implications of e-cig use. Eligible studies will include randomized controlled trials, cross-sectional, and longitudinal observational designs comparing e-cig users with current smokers and/or never smokers, and using culture-independent, next-generation sequencing techniques for microbiome profiling. By summarizing key microbial diversity patterns, identifying taxa-level differences, and highlighting methodological strengths and limitations, it will clarify the extent to which e-cig use may influence oral microbial communities. The anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, outcome measures, and sampling methods underscores the need for cautious interpretation and for standardization in future research. Introduction

Electronic cigarettes (e-cigs) have gained substantial popularity among people who smoke as a perceived harm reduction tool and smoking cessation aid [1, 2]. Their appeal lies in lower long-term costs and the ability to reproduce certain sensory aspects of smoking without the combustion of tobacco [3, 4]. E-cigs are battery-powered devices that heat a liquid (containing nicotine, flavorings, and other additives) to produce an inhalable aerosol [5]. While the detrimental effects of conventional cigarettes are well documented and primarily attributable to combustion-derived toxicants [6], the long-term health consequences of e-cig aerosol remain incompletely understood, particularly regarding their local effects in the oral cavity.

The oral cavity is the initial site of exposure to e-cig aerosol and is directly subjected to nicotine and a range of chemical constituents. It hosts one of the most complex microbial ecosystems in the human body, second only to the gut microbiome, comprising over 700 bacterial species as well as fungal and viral communities [7, 8]. This microbial community plays a central role in maintaining oral and systemic health, acting as a gateway to the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts and contributing to immune homeostasis [9–11]. However, it is highly dynamic and can be altered by diet, medications, immune status, oral hygiene practices, and exposure to tobacco products [12–14]. Disruption of this balance, known as dysbiosis, has been linked not only to oral diseases such as dental caries and periodontitis, but also to systemic conditions including atherosclerosis, autoimmune diseases, and impaired glycemic control [15–19].

Conventional cigarette smoking is known to promote the overgrowth of potentially pathogenic anaerobic bacteria within the oral cavity, creating an environment favorable to disease progression [20–22]. By contrast, the impact of e-cig use on the oral microbial community is less clearly established. Some studies suggest that e-cigs may have milder effects than combustible tobacco [23, 24], whereas others report that vaping can increase the abundance of microorganisms associated with periodontal disease, alter host immune responses, and trigger gum inflammation [25, 26]. The variability in these findings is often compounded by methodological limitations, different study design and comparators, small sample sizes, and confounding due to the high prevalence of current or former smoking among e-cig users.

These inconsistencies underscore the need for a systematic synthesis of the available evidence. The present systematic review aims to critically assess clinical studies investigating the effects of e-cig use on the oral microbiome, comparing outcomes among e-cig users, current smokers, and never smokers, with the goal of summarizing current knowledge, evaluating study quality, and identifying areas where further research is needed.

Material and methods

The review will be conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for systematic reviews [27] and reported following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [28]. In addition, the review has been registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420251120281). The protocol has been reported following Preferred reporting items for systematic review and meta-analysis protocols (PRISMA-P) 2015 [29] (Appendix 1).

Research question

This systematic review addresses the following research question: “Does the oral microbiome profile of e-cigarette users differ from that of current smokers and never smokers?”

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search has been conducted to identify all relevant studies published from 2010 (the first year that e-cig clinical trials were published) to August 27, 2025. The search strategy was developed by a review team member (GRMLR) with expertise in systematic reviews, in consultation with specialists in oral microbiology (LPS and EZ) and e-cig use (RP) to refine relevant terms and variants. Searches have been performed in three electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, using a combination of MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to e-cigarettes, vaping, oral microbiome, and microbial diversity. Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”) have been used to appropriately combine the search terms. The full search strategy for each database is presented in Appendix 2.

In addition to database searches, we will conduct a manual screening of the reference lists of all included articles to identify additional eligible studies. We will also apply snowballing technique, reviewing all articles that cite the included studies. A targeted search of gray literature will be conducted using the OpenGrey database (https://opengrey.eu/) to minimize the risk of publication bias. Furthermore, we will consult experts in the field to ensure that no relevant studies are unintentionally missed.

No language or publication date restrictions will be applied.

Titles and abstracts will be independently screened by two reviewers using EndNote version 21 (Clarivate). Full texts will be retrieved for all potentially relevant studies, and inclusion will be confirmed based on the pre-specified criteria. Discrepancies will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

We will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cross-sectional, and longitudinal observational studies that examine the oral microbiome of e-cig users, with at least one comparison group consisting of either current smokers or never smokers. Only studies using culture-independent, next-generation sequencing techniques for microbiome profiling will be considered eligible.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies will be assessed independently by two reviewers (GRMLR and RP) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools, selecting the appropriate checklist based on each study’s design. Discrepancies will be resolved by consensus or with the help of a third reviewer (EZ). The JBI critical appraisal tools are freely accessible at https://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools.

Data synthesis

Given the expected heterogeneity in study designs, populations, sequencing methods, and reporting of microbial outcomes, a meta-analysis will not be conducted. Instead, results will be synthesized narratively and tabulated to provide a structured comparison of findings across studies. A detailed summary will be provided, highlighting differences and similarities in microbial diversity indices and taxonomic composition between e-cig users, current smokers, and never smokers. Whenever possible, the direction and magnitude of differences in microbial outcomes will be described, along with relevant methodological factors (e.g., sequencing platform, sample collection method) that may influence results.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses will be planned to explore whether the presence of oral diseases (e.g., periodontitis) modifies the association between e-cigarette use and oral microbiome alterations, to compare exclusive e-cig use (i.e., individuals who report only e-cig use) with dual use (i.e., individuals who concurrently use both e-cig and combustible cigarettes), and to assess differences according to sampling site (e.g., saliva, subgingival plaque, oral mucosa).

Results

Database searching was completed on Aug 27, 2025, yielding the following results: PubMed = 39 records, Scopus = 46 records, and Web of Science = 83 records. Secondary searches have not yet been conducted. Abstract screening is currently in progress. Screening and selection of retrieved articles are in progress, with review completion expected by November 2025.

Discussion

The oral microbiome plays a crucial role in maintaining both local and systemic health, contributing to oral homeostasis, immune regulation, and protection against pathogenic colonization [11]. Under physiological conditions, the oral microbiota displays a balanced distribution of microbial taxa that supports a symbiotic relationship with the host; disturbances to this balance may predispose individuals to disease or exacerbate existing pathologies [19, 30–32]. In healthy adults, the predominant taxa at the phylum level have been reported to belong to Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Fusobacteria [33]. It is important to note that microbial profiles may vary substantially depending on the oral site sampled, as the oral cavity harbors multiple distinct niches; this variability needs to be carefully considered when synthesizing evidence, since it may lead to misleading generalizations if not explicitly acknowledged.

Although current evidence suggests that e-cigs are generally well tolerated at the local level [34], their medium- and long-term impact on the oral microbiota remains unclear. The principal limitation of the available literature is that most studies are cross-sectional in design, providing only a snapshot of microbial composition at a single time point and thus precluding causal inferences [35, 36]. Furthermore, many e-cig users are either dual users or former smokers, which often makes it difficult to isolate the independent effects of vaping from those of concurrent or past smoking [37]. In order to provide a comprehensive overview of the current evidence, and given that cross-sectional studies represent the predominant form of data currently available on this topic, these studies will not be excluded. Instead, the review will critically appraise their findings, highlighting the limitations and the need for well-designed longitudinal studies with optimal control for residual confounding. By contrast, in vitro studies will be excluded, as they are subject to important methodological constraints and are not representative of clinical conditions. In particular, these models do not adequately account for the complex metabolic interactions between the host and the microbiota, nor do they incorporate other host-related elements such as immune responses and systemic feedback mechanisms [38]. Moreover, many in vitro systems oversimplify the conditions for microbial growth, neglecting key factors such as nutrient flow, mechanical forces, and oxygen gradients that shape microbial behavior in vivo [38]. Similarly, animal studies will be excluded, as although they have been instrumental in advancing our mechanistic understanding of oral diseases, no single model can faithfully reproduce all aspects of the corresponding human condition [39].

Given the anticipated heterogeneity in outcome measures, in the methodologies used to identify and quantify microbial taxa, and in the oral sites sampled, a meta-analysis is unlikely to be feasible and, even if conducted, would need to be interpreted with caution. A qualitative synthesis will therefore be prioritized, identifying the main reported outcomes and summarizing the differences in microbial taxa between e-cig users, never smokers, and current smokers. The definition of “e-cig user” adopted in each included study will be reported, acknowledging that this definition may be subject to bias related to the duration of smoking cessation and possible dual use. Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses on patterns of e-cig use will be conducted to further explore these aspects.

The results will be published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and disseminated more broadly through targeted newsletters to experts in the field, professional networks, social media channels, and presentations at academic meetings.

Conclusions

Our systematic review will provide the first comprehensive synthesis of clinical evidence on the impact of e-cig use on the oral microbiome. By critically appraising the available studies and highlighting methodological limitations, the review aims to clarify current knowledge, identify research gaps, and inform future investigations. Ultimately, these findings are expected to contribute to a better understanding of the potential oral health implications of e-cig use.
