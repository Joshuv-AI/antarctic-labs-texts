Genotype-immunophenotype relationships in NPM1-mutated AML clonal evolution uncovered by single-cell multiomic analysis∗
Authors: Morgan Drucker, Darren Lee, Michael Bowman, Xuan Zhang, Bailee N Kain, Deedra Nicolet, Roopsha Bandopadhyay, Varsha Singh, Zhe Wang, Richard M Stone, Caner Saygin, Krzysztof Mrózek, Andrew J Carroll, Daniel T Starczynowski, Ross L Levine, John C Byrd, Nathan Salomonis, Sarah J Skuli, H Leighton Grimes, Ann-Kathrin Eisfeld, Robert L Bowman, Linde A Miles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•In NPM1-mutated AML, comutations in epigenetic modifier genes can dictate unique lineage trajectories in subsequent clones.•Signaling gene comutations in NPM1-mutated AML at relapse are correlated with increased clonal diversity and shortened overall survival.

Introduction

Nucleophosmin 1 (NPM1) mutations1,2 are found in ∼30% of patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML)3,4 who also harbor epigenetic and signaling gene mutations.5,6 Previous studies identified synergy between NPM1 and co-occurring mutations in leukemogenesis; however, the impact on clonal fitness remains unknown. We and others previously performed single-cell multiomics7, 8, 9, 10 to identify distinct consequences of epigenetic and signaling mutations on clonal architecture, heterogeneity, and differentiation.7,8 Longitudinal studies on AML-targeted therapy identified clonal dynamics within individual patients, with specific clones expanded under selective pressure.11, 12, 13 These studies highlight biases of genetic mutations on leukemic clonal evolution and demonstrate how combinations of mutations create divergent evolutionary trajectories even within similar genetic subsets.

Here, we investigate how comutations influence evolutionary trajectories in NPM1-mutated AML through single-cell multiomics. Our studies (1) assess how comutations affect clonal architecture across disease time points, (2) identify novel genotype-immunophenotype interactions to determine how comutations affect differentiation, and (3) identify distinct clonal evolution patterns during chemotherapy in paired samples.

Single-cell DNA plus protein library preparation, sequencing, and analysis

Sample processing steps are detailed in the supplemental Methods, as previously described.7,10 Sequencing reads are processed by the Mission Bio Tapestri version 2 pipeline, and analyzed using the scDNA R package (version 1.01; https://github.com/bowmanr/scDNA).

Results and discussion

We performed simultaneous single-cell molecular profiling and cell surface protein expression (scDNA+protein) sequencing on 609 314 cells from 43 samples of 32 patients with NPM1-mutated AML. We also integrated previous scDNA sequencing from 23 patients with NPM1-mutated AML.7 The most common comutations were IDH1/2 (n = 30), FLT3 (n = 27), and TET2 (n = 26) (Figure 1A-B; supplemental Table 2). We queried samples from patients at different disease time points, including diagnosis, complete response (CR), and relapse (Figure 1B; supplemental Figure 1A). Eight patients were longitudinally sampled during cytotoxic chemotherapy (7+314; supplemental Figure 1A; supplemental Table 1).

We first investigated clonal architecture at different disease time points (Figure 1C), including mutant and wild-type (WT) cells. Although there was no difference in clonal complexity metrics between diagnosis and relapse, we identified a significant decrease in clone number and diversity from diagnosis to CR (clones, P < .009; Shannon P < .00013) and increase in CR to relapse (clones, P < .01; Shannon P < .002; Figure 1D; supplemental Figure 1B-E). Expectedly, excluding WT cells resulted in increased diversity and decreased clone number at CR (supplemental Figure 1F-G). We then stratified samples by single or combination epigenetic mutations (DNMT3A, IDH1/2, and/or TET2 comutation) and signaling mutations (FLT3 and/or RAS/MAPK comutation). We observed no differences in any clonal complexity between samples stratified by different epigenetic mutations (Figure 1E; supplemental Figure 1H-J). Signaling-mutant samples, however, showed increased mutation counts and clones (none vs RAS, P < .007 vs FLT3, P < .003 vs RAS and FLT3 [RAS+FLT3: (R+F)], P < .027; supplemental Figure 1K-M). Further, all signaling-mutant samples had increased clonal diversity (none vs RAS, P < .004 vs FLT3, P < .00024 vs R+F, P < .00029; Figure 1F). Critically, the increases in signaling mutants in clonal complexity were uniquely identified in relapse samples, with no significant differences at diagnosis except mutation count (Figure 1G-H; supplemental Figure 1N-O). This was not observed when we stratified samples based on epigenetic mutation (all relapse P > .530; supplemental Figure 1P). We next investigated associations between clonal complexity and overall survival. Although there were no significant associations at diagnosis, clonal diversity at relapse was significantly correlated with overall survival (hazard ratio, 2.07; P = .05; Figure 1I-J). These findings add clinical context for increased clonal diversity in signaling-mutant samples previously described.7 These data suggest that patients with signaling mutations relapse with increasing clonal diversity and polyclonality, resulting in multiple clones competing for dominance and driving worsened survival. Similar prognostic value of clonal diversity has been identified at remission.9 Collectively, our results indicate that disease monitoring for clonal complexity may complement single-allele molecular measurable residual disease testing.15,16

We next assessed single-cell surface protein expression (scProtein) across the cohort. Although we observed significant differences in average protein expression from patient to patient, we found that 23 of 42 proteins showed significant clone-specific enrichment. We found CD117 (P < 1.86 × 10−5) and CD11b (P < .007) enriched in IDH2- and TET2-mutant clones, respectively, suggesting that certain markers may be enriched in clones of specific genotypes (Figure 2A). Moreover, we found 19 of 42 proteins were significantly enriched at different disease time points. CD44, a stem cell marker in hematopoiesis and cancer,17, 18, 19 showed an increase at relapse, indiscriminate of clone genotype (P < 4.94 × 10−6; supplemental Figure 2A). Similarly, progenitor cell markers CD38 (P < .002), CD45RO (P < 1.96 × 10−4), and the CD44 coreceptor, CD49d (P < 1.76 × 10−10) also showed increased expression at relapse, indicating genotype-agnostic immunophenotype alterations during clonal evolution. We then assessed protein expression heterogeneity across clones within individual patients. We found minimal differential expression between TET2/NPM1-mutant and TET2-mutant clones; however, CD14 was significantly increased in TET2/NPM1/RAS compared to TET2/NPM1-mutant clones in 5 of 8 samples (Figure 2B; supplemental Figure 2B). These findings indicate that despite interpatient and intrapatient heterogeneity, genotype-specific immunophenotype expression patterns exist in patients with NPM1-mutated AML.

To evaluate recurrent immunophenotypes across samples, we clustered cells into 31 unique communities (C0-C30) based on aggregated marker expression (Figure 2C; supplemental Figure 2C-E). Overall community representation was not significantly different across disease time points, but certain communities were enriched or depleted at different time points (Figure 2D; supplemental Figure 2F-G). C3/C4 cells had the highest expression of T-cell markers CD3, CD4, and CD8 and were enriched in CR (28.7%), compared with diagnosis (6.26%) and relapse (6.16%) (Figure 2D; supplemental Figure 2E-F,H). Conversely, C0, C2, and C16 were enriched in diagnosis (26.4%) and relapse (24.2%) leukemic cells. These clusters expressed higher levels of leukemic blast markers1,2,20 (CD117 and CD123), and CD11b, CD64, and CD14 were shown to be expressed on monocytic AML blasts1,2,21 and myeloid progenitors21 (supplemental Figure 2E-F,H).

Next, we identified how comutations altered lineage output. Although most T cells were WT, TET2-mutated cells were the most abundant among mutated cells (odds ratio [OR], 0.379 [C3] and 0.292 [C4]) compared to all other mutants (DNMT3A OR, 0.085/0.121; NPM1 OR, 0.057/0.063; FLT3 OR, 0.159/0.157 for C3/C4, respectively; Figure 2E; supplemental Figure 3A). We next queried cluster expression across clones harboring DNMT3A, TET2, and IDH2 mutations (Figure 2F; supplemental Figures 2E and 3A). DNMT3A-mutated clones were enriched for CD123 and had decreased CD11b compared with TET2- and IDH2-mutated clones (CD123, P < 2.24 × 10−294; CD11b, P < 7.05 × 10−247; supplemental Table 3). IDH2-mutated clones showed increased stem/progenitor markers, including CD141, which represents neoplastic clones,22 CD34 and CD117 (CD141, P < 2.52 × 10−114; CD34, P < 2.84 × 10−76; CD117, P < 1.83 × 10−78; supplemental Table 3). Strikingly, TET2-mutated clones diverged significantly from DNMT3A- and IDH2-mutated clones, with an enrichment for CD14, CD11b, and CD64, prominent mature monocytic markers (P < 2.70 × 10−304). We found that immunophenotype patterns were most pronounced in the relapsed setting, specifically when comparing TET2- and IDH2-mutant samples (supplemental Figure 3B). These findings suggest that epigenetic mutations, DNMT3A, TET2, and IDH2, dictate lineage biases and differentiation potential that which are inherited by subsequent clones. The remarkable divergence between DNMT3A and TET2, the 2 most frequently mutated genes found in clonal hematopoiesis,23 suggests that the immunophenotypes of the mutant leukemic clones may be influenced by these early mutations during leukemogenesis.

Next, we found that NRAS- and FLT3-mutant clones with the same epigenetic comutation possessed different immunophenotypic patterns (Figure 2F; supplemental Figures 2E and 3A). Compared with NPM1/NRAS-mutated cells with DNMT3A or TET2 mutations, DNMT3A/NPM1/FLT3 (DNF) and TET2/NPM1/FLT3 (TNF) cells expressed more CD123, whereas DNR cells expressed less CD117 than DNF cells, as previously suggested24,25 (CD123: DNR-DNF, P < 3.77 × 10−15; TNR-TNF, P < 3.50 × 10−6; CD117: DNR-DNF, P < 3.99 × 10−10; supplemental Table 3). In IDH2-mutated clones, an opposite trend was observed with NRAS-mutated clones expressing higher CD117 and CD34 compared to FLT3 comutant clones, suggesting the IDH2/NPM1/NRAS combination harbors a strong stem/progenitor phenotype (CD117, P < 1.024 × 10−55; CD34, P < 6.32 × 10−67; supplemental Table 3). FLT3-mutated clones also showed increased expression of CD6926,27 (supplemental Table 3). Within an individual sample, we found that epigenetic immunophenotype patterns were conserved, albeit with a relative increase in monocytic marker expression in RAS-mutant compared to FLT3-mutant clones (supplemental Figure 3C), which we described previously.7 These results demonstrate that signaling mutations can refine lineage trajectories established by epigenetic mutations in antecedent clones.

To investigate how standard cytotoxic chemotherapy affects clonality and immunophenotypes, we assessed longitudinal samples from patients (n = 8) undergoing 7+3 chemotherapy (supplemental Table 2; supplemental Figure 1A). Most patients (6/8) displayed alterations in mutation and clone numbers from diagnosis to CR and/or relapse (ie, dynamic; Figure 3A; supplemental Figures 4 and 5A), 4 of whom specifically gained or lost signaling mutations during therapy. In patient G, a dominant TET2/NPM1/NRAS–mutated clone was lost at relapse (Figure 3B-C; supplemental Figure 5B) concomitant with increased expression of dendritic (CD135, P < 1.1 × 10−209) and monocytic markers (CD16, P < 9.6 × 10−15; Figure 3D-E). We also observed decreased myeloid and stem/progenitor markers CD33 (P < 7.2 × 10−211), and CD117 (P < 5.2 × 10−57; Figure 3E; supplemental Figure 5C). Epigenetic mutations were also dynamic during therapy in 2 patients. Patient F harbored both TET2 and NPM1 mutations at diagnosis, the latter which was likely to be the initiating event (Figure 3F; supplemental Figure 5D). However, upon relapse, patient F harbored IDH1 and NPM1 mutations with no evidence of TET2-mutated clones (Figure 3F; supplemental Figure 5E). Whereas the TET2-mutanted clones at diagnosis had increased expression of monocytic markers CD14, CD11b, CD16, and CD64 (all P < 1.0 × 10−80), the IDH1-mutated clones identified at relapse had decreased monocytic marker expression and instead higher CD117 expression, suggesting a more immature phenotype following therapy (CD117, P < 8.24 × 10−11; Figure 3G-H; supplemental Figure 5F-G). These findings indicate that dynamic clonal evolution involves both signaling and epigenetic mutations (Figure 3D), demonstrate the variable responses of leukemic clones to therapeutic pressures, and underscore how clonal complexity affects immunophenotypes coincident with alterations in clonal architecture.

Analysis of clonal evolution at a single-cell level provides insights into how NPM1 mutations cooperate with epigenetic and signaling mutations to generate clonal complexity. Collectively, our investigation underscores the need to further longitudinal study of patients with AML and at high cellular resolution, to discover mechanisms of response to current therapies and relapse. We anticipate that similar multiomic approaches will enable new risk stratifications that predict treatment responses and inform therapeutic strategies that target cancer as an evolving, multiclonal disease.
