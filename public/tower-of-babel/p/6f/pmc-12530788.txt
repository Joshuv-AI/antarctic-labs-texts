Transradial catheterization for prone neurosurgical treatment in a hybrid operating room: Technical considerations
Authors: Taichi Ikedo, Masakazu Okawa, Hideo Chihara, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Shigeki Takada, Takeshi Funaki, Takayuki Kikuchi, Chiaki Sakai, Yoshiki Arakawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Surgical management of cerebrovascular diseases in a hybrid operating room (HOR) is advantageous in patients requiring postoperative cerebral angiography to confirm complete obliteration or in procedures requiring a combined open and endovascular approach. However, in the prone position, conventional transfemoral arterial access is challenging, as the introducer sheath is positioned between the patient and the surgical table, impeding catheter maneuverability. This case series outlines the technical details and potential advantages of the transradial approach in patients undergoing cerebrovascular surgery in the prone position.Case Description: Case 1 – A 55-year-old man was admitted with a subarachnoid hemorrhage. Cerebral angiography revealed a ruptured fusiform aneurysm at the origin of the left posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA). A hybrid surgical strategy consisting of an occipital artery-PICA bypass followed by endovascular internal trapping of the aneurysm was planned. Because the left radial artery puncture site was located on the dorsal surface near the operator with the patient in the prone position, catheter manipulation was more straightforward than with the conventional transfemoral approach. The procedure was completed without complications, except a cerebral infarction related to internal trapping. Case 2 – A 78-year-old man presented with a subarachnoid hemorrhage and was subsequently diagnosed with a craniocervical junction arteriovenous fistula. The draining vein was ligated with the patient in the prone position. Intraoperative cerebral angiography through the left radial artery confirmed complete shunt occlusion.Conclusion: The transradial approach is technically advantageous in prone-position procedures in the HOR; it facilitates catheter manipulation during complex cerebrovascular surgeries.

INTRODUCTION

A hybrid operating room (HOR) is a specialized surgical suite that integrates advanced imaging technologies with traditional surgical capabilities, allowing minimally invasive endovascular therapies and open surgeries to be performed in a single space.[9] This setup facilitates complex surgeries by enabling real-time imaging and interventions without the movement of patients between rooms. One-stage combined endovascular therapy and bypass surgery may be performed to treat complex cerebrovascular diseases in the HOR.[5,8] The HOR is also useful for confirming complete occlusion of intracranial aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, or arteriovenous fistulas (AVF).[2] However, treatment in the HOR has some disadvantages, such as longer procedure times and higher radiation doses due to more complex patient positioning and preparation and the more frequent use of imaging devices.[9] Surgery in the prone position in the HOR is especially complicated and has limited application. Furthermore, the transfemoral approach requires extended femoral sheath preparation and the use of a kink-resistant sheath, which is associated with thromboembolic complications.[10] Catheter manipulation is also complicated because the femoral sheath is located immediately below and lateral to the patient.

The transradial approach has recently been reported to have a low complication rate in the treatment of cerebrovascular diseases.[11] Among a large cohort in which 1524 procedures were performed, no complications with permanent deficits related to the transradial approach occurred.

We used the transradial approach for interventions in the prone position in the HOR in two patients with subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) due to a ruptured fusiform aneurysm in the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) and a craniocervical junction dural AVF. Using this case series, we aimed to describe, in detail, the procedure and benefits of the transradial approach performed in the prone position.

Detailed explanation of the procedure used in the transradial approach to hybrid surgery in the prone position

Using a conventional transfemoral approach, the femoral artery is punctured in the supine position, followed by a positional change to the prone position. The transradial approach enabled easy introducer sheath insertion in the prone position without positional changes after sheath insertion [Figures 1a-c]. Radial artery access was obtained at the conventional forearm site rather than the distal site at the anatomical snuff box. For sheath insertion in the prone position, an exchange technique from the arterial line using a guidewire or ultrasound-guided puncture is desirable to avoid puncture failure. A continuous perfusion line with heparinized saline was connected to the introducer sheath during the microsurgery to prevent thrombus formation. Swelling at the puncture site on the radial artery could be easily detected even in the prone position. Because the radial artery puncture site was on the patient’s upper side, close to the operator, with the patient in the prone position, catheter manipulation with the transradial approach was easier than that with the conventional transfemoral approach [Figure 1d].

(b) The arterial line was moved to the introducer sheath using a guidewire. (c) The introducer sheath on the palmar side of the hand and wrist. (d) The operator could easily access and manipulate the catheter. The white arrow shows the catheter for cerebral angiography.

CASE PRESENTATION

Case 1

A 55-year-old man suddenly experienced severe headaches and nausea. He was urgently admitted to our hospital with a diagnosis of SAH based on an initial computed tomography (CT) scan. Because no neurological abnormalities other than severe headaches were observed, the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) grade was I. The initial CT scan showed SAH in Fisher Group 3 without acute hydrocephalus. Cerebral angiography revealed a fusiform aneurysm (6.3 mm × 4.0 mm) at the origin of the left PICA [Figure 2a]. Occlusion of the fusiform aneurysm with the proximal PICA was required to prevent aneurysm re-rupture. Occipital artery (OA)-PICA bypass surgery was indispensable to preserve the blood supply to the cerebellum fed by the left PICA. For occlusion of the fusiform aneurysm, endovascular internal trapping was selected because the aneurysm was located mid-front of the medulla, where direct surgery was not considered safe. Therefore, we planned a hybrid surgery involving direct surgery of the OA-PICA anastomosis and endovascular therapy for internal trapping of the aneurysm in the HOR. The patient was affixed to the surgical bed in the prone position. In the prone position, sheath insertion was easier because the palm of his left arm was on top and close to the operator. As the arterial line was placed in the left radial artery, it was replaced with a 6 Fr introducer sheath using an exchange guidewire. The left OA was harvested, and the caudal loop of the left PICA was exposed, followed by OA-PICA anastomosis. The distal portion of the exposed aneurysm was partially thrombosed [Figure 2b]. Following bypass surgery [Figure 2c], endovascular internal trapping was performed using a transradial approach. An AXS Vecta 71 intermediate catheter (Stryker, Portage, MI, USA) was inserted into the left vertebral artery through a 6 Fr sheath. A microcatheter (SL-10; Stryker) was placed in the PICA distal to the fusiform aneurysm; this was followed by coil embolization with 16 coils from the intact PICA distal to the aneurysm [Figure 2d]. The contrast of the aneurysm disappeared after coil embolization [Figures 2e and f]. The proximal portion of the OA-PICA bypass was occluded using a surgical clip before completion of the surgery. Postoperative magnetic resonance imaging revealed a patent OA-PICA anastomosis. Although internal trapping of the PICA and the ruptured aneurysm led to a partial cerebral infarction in the lateral medulla, the patient experienced no complications related to the transradial approach.

Case 2

A 78-year-old man was diagnosed with a craniocervical junction AVF after transfer to our hospital due to a subarachnoid hemorrhage of WFNS grade V [Figure 3a]. Because his consciousness gradually improved from a deep coma to the communicative level, radical surgery for the AVF was planned. The arterial line was exchanged to a 4 Fr introducer sheath in the left radial artery in the prone position. Ligation surgery of the vein draining from the AVF was performed following craniotomy of the posterior cranial fossa and laminectomy of C1 and the upper half of C2 in the prone position. Cerebral angiography from the left radial artery confirmed shunt occlusion [Figure 3b]. The patient did not experience any complications associated with the transradial approach.

DISCUSSION

We report two cases in which the transradial approach was used for cerebral angiography and endovascular intervention in the prone position in the HOR. Transradial access enabled easy catheter manipulation without complicated preparation, even in the prone position.

The transradial approach to cerebral angiography offers several benefits over the conventional transfemoral approach. Although no statistical differences have been observed between the approaches in terms of procedural success, major and minor complication rates are lower when using the transradial approach.[1]

When using this approach in neuroendovascular therapies, the major access site complications are low at 1.2%; the complications include extravasation (0.3%), arterial occlusion (0.6%), pseudoaneurysm (0.1%), compartment syndrome (0.1%), and infection (0.1%). Sheathless systems reduce access site complications by minimizing arterial trauma through smaller outer diameters and smoother insertions without a separate introducer sheath.[4] The hydrophilic coatings and streamlined device profiles further lower the risk of radial artery occlusion and spasm. With ongoing improvements in device technology, the transradial approach may become the mainstream access route for neuroendovascular procedures.

The current report showed the additional possible superiority of the transradial approach in cerebral angiography and endovascular treatment in an HOR. While cerebral angiography can be performed as usual using a transfemoral approach in the supine position, the transfemoral approach in the prone position makes catheter manipulation uncomfortable because of its complicated preparation. Other disadvantages of the transfemoral approach include inaccessibility of the femoral artery until the end of surgery, difficulty in maintaining a sterile environment, and sheath kinking due to the patient’s weight.[6] Inaccessibility of the puncture site may lead to a lack of awareness of hematoma formation or continuous bleeding. As in the transfemoral approach, the transbrachial approach has the disadvantage of limited access to the puncture site when the patient is in the prone position.

Transradial angiography can address these challenges, even in neurosurgical treatments in a complicated prone position. The palmar surfaces of the hand and wrist are on the upper side close to the operator in a natural posture, with the arms resting at the patient’s side. This setting offers distinct advantages including continuous accessibility to the puncture site, improved maintenance of a sterile field, and the option to use arterial access later in the procedure. This approach avoids sheath kinking, thrombus formation within the sheath, and skin injury caused by compression from the femoral access device, as can occur with a transfemoral approach in the prone position.[6] In a previous report, the transradial approach was also applied in the treatment of thoracolumbar spinal lesions.[3] Considering catheter navigability, access to the radial artery on the same side as the vertebral artery intended to guide catheter placement may offer technical advantages.

One limitation of this approach is its applicability to cases requiring treatment through both vertebral arteries. In such cases, a combination with the transfemoral approach may be required. Another limitation is that catheter navigation to the contralateral vertebral artery may not be feasible, depending on the case. In contrast to the two cases presented here, the right radial artery approach should be considered when navigating the right vertebral artery. Furthermore, anatomical variations in the course of the radial artery and its branching pattern can make the transradial approach technically challenging.[7] When the cubital crossover between the radial and ulnar arteries serves as the primary access route, forearm rotation in the prone position, which is opposite to that in the supine position, may result in vessel angulation and restricted access. Therefore, preoperative assessment of vascular anatomy is particularly important for the transradial approach. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to demonstrate the usefulness of the transradial approach for endovascular treatment in an HOR setting.

CONCLUSION

The transradial approach has several advantages over the transfemoral approach. These include the facilitation of catheter manipulation in the prone position for the treatment of complex cerebrovascular diseases.
