Laser-induced graphene/Cu-based fully-porous flexible capacitive pressure sensor with ultra-fast response and wide measurement range
Authors: Yimeng Jiang, Misheng Liang, Ying Xie, Ruige Su, Bo Wang, Fan Wu, Xiaomeng Bian, He Tian, Rui You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recently, advances in human-machine interfaces have raised performance demands on flexible sensors. Achieving fast response, wide detection range, and high sensitivity remains a key challenge in flexible pressure sensor design. Here, we present a novel fully-porous flexible capacitive pressure sensor (FPFCPS) with a sandwich structure, comprising laser-induced Cu-composited graphene (LICuG) electrodes and a polyurethane foam dielectric layer modified with ionic liquid and coated with PVA hydrogel. The hierarchical porosity and conductivity of LICuG enable coupled mechanical interlocking and electronic polarization at the electrode-dielectric interface, improving interfacial contact, compressibility, and capacitive response. The sensor achieves a high sensitivity of 863.17 kPa⁻¹, a wide detection range (10 Pa–500 kPa), and a rapid response time of 5 ms, along with excellent flexibility and long-term stability. Notably, FPFCPS enables rapid tactile feedback and material recognition in dynamic robotic operations, offering high-resolution sensing in complex environments. This work represents the first application of LICuG in electric double-layer capacitance-pressure sensors and establishes a new structural design paradigm based on a fully porous architecture. It provides theoretical insights and practical strategies for advancing material development, mechanistic understanding, and implementation of intelligent sensing systems. In this study, femtosecond-laser-induced Cu-composited graphene (LICuG) was successfully synthesized. By incorporating this novel composite material into an electric double-layer capacitance sensing system, a fully-porous flexible capacitive pressure sensor (FPFCPS) was constructed. Through the synergistic regulation of the sensor’s interlayer structural and electrical properties, its overall performance was significantly enhanced. When integrated at key robotic joints, the FPFCPS successfully detected collisions within ~5 ms and was capable of identifying the type of obstacle through tactile contact.

Introduction

With the accelerated integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and the Internet of Things (IoT), the application scope of intelligent sensing systems has continuously expanded across fields such as health monitoring, intelligent interaction, and robotics1,2. This evolution imposes increasingly stringent demands on the comprehensive performance of sensors3. As the core sensing units, pressure sensors’ response speed and detection accuracy directly determine a system’s ability to perceive external physical stimuli4. Such sensors are required to exhibit excellent mechanical flexibility, environmental adaptability, and multi-scale mechanical signal acquisition capabilities5. However, conventional rigid pressure sensors—characterized by bulky size, poor conformability, and high fragility—are increasingly inadequate to meet the practical demands of next-generation intelligent systems for highly adaptable and stable tactile interfaces6,7. In contrast, flexible pressure sensors, owing to their lightweight, bendability, and superior conformability, have rapidly emerged as a pivotal technological route for constructing novel intelligent sensing interfaces7,8.

Currently, flexible pressure sensors are primarily based on four principles: piezoresistive, capacitive, piezoelectric, and triboelectric mechanisms9. Among these, piezoresistive flexible pressure sensors are the most widely used type in industry at present, featuring simple preparation processes and convenient, efficient signal collection. Nevertheless, this study still adopted capacitive flexible pressure sensors, which involve a relatively more complex fabrication procedure. The key reason is that the comprehensive advantages of capacitive sensors in low power consumption, dynamic response speed, micro-pressure resolution, and long-term stability are irreplaceable by piezoresistive sensors, enabling them to better meet the stringent requirements of scenarios such as tactile feedback for intelligent robots and high-precision health monitoring10–12.

However, flexible capacitive pressure sensors still face substantial challenges—particularly in achieving a rapid response time of <10 ms and balancing high sensitivity with a wide detection range13,14, which are critical for collision detection and fast obstacle avoidance15. These core issues are largely constrained by the inherent limitations of existing capacitive pressure sensor structures and materials16. Current research has predominantly focused on the design of microstructures for the dielectric layer. For example, Wan et al. employed nanoimprint lithography to construct biomimetic pyramid arrays, enhancing sensitivity to 1.2 kPa⁻¹, with a response time of 36 ms. However, the detection range is limited by the collapse threshold of the microstructure, capping the detection limit at 20 kPa17. Wu’s team developed biomimetic microstructure sensors via 3D printing, extending the detection range to 200 kPa, but the complexity of the manufacturing process has hindered large-scale production18. These existing strategies largely overlook the structural design of the electrode layers. Traditional static electrode layers cannot dynamically adjust the interfacial contact area with the dielectric layer through deformation, resulting in a compromise between response speed, low-pressure sensitivity, and high-pressure range, limiting further performance improvements18,19. Additionally, many devices, in pursuit of a flexible, compressible structure, often use intrinsically low-conductivity semiconductors as electrode layers in the sandwich structure. The inherent electronic characteristics of these materials impose limitations on the sensor’s response speed and overall performance20.

In this context, this study proposes a novel fully-porous FPFCPS, where the electrode layers are composed of porous femtosecond-laser-induced Cu-composited graphene (LICuG) with copper nanoparticles (Cu NPs). The physical image of each part of the device is shown in Fig. S1a–c. Femtosecond-laser (Fs-laser) was employed in this study, with the laser optical path depicted in Fig. S2. Fs-laser processing offers ultra-high precision, minimal thermal damage, and compatibility with a wide range of materials, making it highly suitable for applications in micro- and nano-fabrication21,22. By adjusting the laser energy density, the porosity and crystallinity of the graphene can be precisely controlled, enabling the directed optimization of its electronic conductivity23. More importantly, LIG technology can be extended to the fabrication of functional composite materials24. In this study, copper precursor (anhydrous copper chloride powder) was doped into the PI film, and laser irradiation simultaneously induced graphene formation and in situ reduction of Cu NPs25,26. This “laser-induced one-step integration strategy” not only simplifies the complex multi-step fabrication processes of traditional metal-carbon composite materials (such as chemical vapor deposition and electroplating)27, but also significantly enhances carrier density and mobility through the Schottky effect and electron tunneling at the metal-graphene interface28. Traditional graphene-based pressure sensors are often only used as the sensing layer of piezoresistive sensors due to insufficient conductivity. Meanwhile, the inherent toughness and chemical bond defects of graphene make it difficult to meet the requirements of long-term stable sensing conditions, leading to bottlenecks in the development of current graphene-based pressure sensors. Experimental results show that the conductivity of LICuG can reach up to 5.26 × 10³ S m⁻¹, which is 50 times higher than pure LIG under the same experimental conditions, while maintaining excellent mechanical stability. This approach provides a novel pathway for optimizing the conductivity of graphene and offers a new theoretical foundation and practical method for the application of doped composite graphene in electronic devices. By introducing a new functional composite material, LICuG, into the electric double-layer capacitance (EDLC) sensing system, and redefining the mechanical and electronic synergistic mechanisms between the electrode and dielectric layers, this study not only overcomes the trade-off between high sensitivity and wide detection range, as well as the conventional response speed limitations, but also provides a paradigm for the design of next-generation tactile interfaces in intelligent systems. Given its excellent sensing performance and environmentally friendly, simple fabrication process, this sensor shows significant potential and application prospects in robot tactile feedback, rapid obstacle avoidance, and obstacle material identification. In the future, by integrating self-healing materials and multimodal sensor arrays, such devices are expected to play a more central role in tasks such as autonomous navigation in AI-driven robots, human-machine interactive interfaces, and wearable medical monitoring, thus opening new boundaries for the sensory capabilities of intelligent systems.

Results and discussion

Preparation process, working principle, structural design, sensing performance and robot-related application display of FPFCPS

Figure 1a illustrates the fabrication process of LICuG. Copper precursors are first dissolved into a polyimide (PI) precursor solution of poly(amido-amine) (PAA), which is then spin-coated, dried, and imidized to yield a uniformly copper-doped PI film. A single-step Fs-laser direct-writing procedure subsequently induces graphene formation and in situ reduction of Cu NPs (Fig. 1c), producing LICuG electrodes with both high electrical conductivity and excellent mechanical flexibility. For the dielectric layer, a porous polyurethane (PU) foam pre-impregnated with ionic liquid (IL-foam) was selected to form EDLC structure10,29. To prevent the sensitivity from sharply decreasing and the detection range from narrowing due to structural densification under high pressure30, PVA hydrogel was introduced as both the binder and support layer for the dielectric, as shown in Fig. 1b. By coating PVA hydrogel onto the IL-foam’s skeleton, the densification of the structure was effectively prevented, and the detection range was stably extended to 500 kPa. This design leverages the interlocking effect of the fully-porous architecture to significantly enhance the device’s compressive limit, interlayer contact area, and overall capacitive response performance (Fig. 1d, e)30, achieving high sensitivity of 863.17 kPa⁻¹, 385.76 kPa⁻¹, and 154.27 kPa⁻¹ within the pressure ranges of 0–100 kPa, 100–300 kPa, and 400–500 kPa. And then, through the combined experimental and finite element analysis (FEA), it was verified that the Cu NPs doped into the graphene electrodes collaborate with the ions in the dielectric layer during the formation of the EDL capacitance, accelerating the capacitive polarization process and resulting in an exceptionally fast response time (~5 ms). In addition, in robotic applications, achieving efficient and reliable obstacle avoidance in complex environments remains a major technological challenge31. Current mainstream active avoidance strategies primarily rely on infrared ranging and vision-based feedback systems32,33. However, infrared techniques suffer from high failure rates when detecting transparent objects or operating under intense lighting conditions, and they are incapable of discerning material properties. While visual systems offer rich spatial information, their reliance on pre-defined models and manual calibration makes them susceptible to recognition delays during structural deformation, resulting in lagged avoidance responses and elevated collision risks15,25. These limitations highlight the urgent need for passive tactile sensing strategies that enable direct and robust acquisition of contact mechanical signals. Against this backdrop, FPFCPS demonstrated excellent performance in robotic application testing. In high-speed collision dynamics experiments, FPFCPS module, integrated into robotic joints, produced a transient capacitive response within ~5 ms upon impact with rigid obstacles (Fig. 1g). Furthermore, in tactile recognition experiments involving multi-material obstacles, the system accurately identified the material type by analyzing characteristic features of the response curves—such as the magnitude of capacitance change and the deformation time constant—achieving a detection accuracy of 100% (Fig. 1h).

Characterization of the LICuG

A systematic analysis of the microstructure, composition, and electronic conductivity of LICuG was performed through multi-scale characterization techniques. As shown in Fig. S3, the surface morphology of LICuG was scanned using confocal microscopy. SEM images at a 50 μm scale, as shown in Figs. 2a and S4, visually present the layered structure of LICuG, with the lower layer consisting of a doped polyimide (PI) substrate and the upper layer comprising the laser-induced composite graphene. Further magnified SEM images (Fig. 2b) reveal a regular layered porous morphology, which originates from the synergistic effects of photothermal and photochemical reactions induced by femtosecond laser irradiation34. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) analyses (Figs. 2c, d and S5) confirm that the Cu NPs are uniformly dispersed within the graphene matrix. HRTEM observations reveal two distinct lattice fringes: a 0.21 nm spacing corresponding to the Cu (111) crystal plane, confirming the presence of elemental copper, and a 0.33 nm spacing matching the graphene (002) plane37, indicating that Cu NPs are fully encapsulated between graphene layers38. SEM images at different scales (Figs. S6 and S7) further confirm the uniform distribution of Cu NPs on LIG. Elemental mapping via SEM (Figs. 2e–h and S8) further supports the homogeneous distribution of Cu within the graphene, with normalized elemental mass and atomic mass distributions shown in Fig. S9. The formation of Cu NPs is attributed to the carbon thermal reduction reaction induced by the laser’s thermal field39, and the lattice-filling effect effectively repairs graphene structural defects, providing a structural basis for enhancing carrier mobility40.

Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 2i) reveals three characteristic peaks of LICuG: the D peak (1341 cm⁻¹), G peak (1577 cm⁻¹), and 2D peak (2683 cm⁻¹). The intensity of the D peak reflects the defect density in the material, the G peak intensity is related to the degree of graphitization, and the 2D peak’s single Lorentzian shape indicates a single-layer graphene stacking structure41,42. The ratio of D to G peak intensities (ID/IG = 0.78) suggests the material possesses a low defect density and high crystallinity41. In the Raman spectrum of copper in different chemical states (Fig. 2j), the metallic copper exhibits dominant peaks at 283 cm⁻¹, 268 cm⁻¹, 498 cm⁻¹, and 530 cm⁻¹, confirming the prevalence of elemental copper in the sample. For the minor presence of Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺, peaks appear at 235 cm⁻¹, 296 cm⁻¹, and 630 cm⁻¹, as well as at 50 cm⁻¹, 224 cm⁻¹, 438 cm⁻¹, 738 cm⁻¹, and 903 cm⁻¹, corresponding to their specific chemical bond vibrational modes. These peak positions and intensity differences not only reveal the Raman spectral features of copper in various oxidation states but also provide crucial spectral information for distinguishing and identifying different copper valences24,38.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) were further employed to confirm the chemical composition and crystal structure of the material. The full XPS spectrum (Fig. 2k) exhibits characteristic peaks at 285 eV, 534 eV, 687 eV, and 934 eV, corresponding to C 1 s, O 1 s, Cu LM4, and Cu 2p orbitals, respectively38. High-resolution Cu 2p spectra (Fig. 2l) show characteristic peaks at 932.5 eV and 952 eV, along with satellite peaks, confirming the partial presence of Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺ on the surface of the Cu NPs, likely originating from surface oxidation or oxygen-containing functional groups42. A small amount of oxidation is unavoidable during the laser fabrication process. However, XPS characterization results—including the full-spectrum Cu LM4 characteristic peak and the peak positions in the high-resolution spectrum—confirm that Cu in LICuG primarily exists in the metallic Cu⁰ state. Most Cu NPs are encapsulated by graphene, with only a slight degree of surface oxidation. Studies have found that the oxidized Cu⁺ and Cu²⁺ on the surface exhibit substantial charge transfer properties, further confirming the positive regulation of copper nanoparticles on the graphene conductivity24,40. To further demonstrate the oxidation resistance of LICuG, additional comparative XPS experiments were conducted on samples stored under the same conditions, with the results presented in Fig. S10. The XRD patterns (Fig. 2m, n) show diffraction peaks at 26.4° and 40.3°, corresponding to the graphene (002) and (100) planes, respectively, while peaks at 43.4°, 50.6°, and 74.3° are attributed to elemental copper (111), (200), and (220) planes, consistent with TEM observations, indicating that the material combines both highly crystalline graphene and copper nanoparticles43.

Conductivity measurements (Fig. 2o, Table S1) reveal that the conductivity of the LICuG films is closely related to the copper loading. As the copper loading increases, the conductivity rises, but this trend sharply declines beyond 0.5 mmol due to the aggregation of excess Cu NPs, which form insulating grain boundaries that hinder interlayer electron transport and increase interface scattering, resulting in a significant drop in conductivity24,42. Under the processing parameters mentioned above, LICuG with a 0.5 mmol copper loading achieved a conductivity of 4013 S m⁻¹, 40 times higher than pure LIG (~107 S m⁻¹) under the same conditions. Additionally, for experimental rigor, after determining the optimal conductive Cu loading, systematic tests were conducted to obtain the highest conductivity to enhance the electronic and sensing performance of the device (Fig. 2p, Table S2). Furthermore, to verify the reproducibility of the experiments, five batches of samples were prepared for comparison, and the relevant data are provided in Table 3 of the Supporting Information. The final optimal parameters of 1 MHz, 1.5 W, and 5 mm/s processing conditions yielded a conductivity of 5260 S m⁻¹ for LICuG, nearly 50 times higher than that of pure LIG under the same conditions, reaching the standard range for metallic-like conductivity44,45. This high-conductivity composite graphene film material will significantly expand its application value.

The dynamic formation process of the EDLC exhibits a three-stage evolution (Fig. 3d). In the initial stage (I), in the absence of an electric field, the cations and anions are uniformly distributed in a free state within the porous dielectric layer. Upon the application of a small pressure and an electric field, the system enters stage II, where the potential gradient between the electrodes drives the ions to migrate towards the electrode interface. During this process, the Schottky heterojunction formed between Cu NPs and graphene generates a local electric field enhancement effect, significantly accelerating the ion polarization rate and rapidly forming an initial double layers near the electrode48,49. According to the capacitance formula C = εA/d, the increase in the electrode-dielectric interface contact area A during this stage leads to the rapid establishment of the double layers, resulting in a significant change in capacitance C and enabling the sensor to exhibit high sensitivity for micro-pressure detection. As the pressure increases and the system enters stage III, the compression of the dielectric layer’s pores causes ions to accumulate at the electrode interface. The Cu NPs enhance the chemical adsorption of ions, inhibiting their desorption, while the continued expansion of the interface contact area A further drives the substantial increase in capacitance C50.

Microstructural analysis (Fig. 3e) further reveals the mechanism by which Cu NPs strengthen the formation of double layers. In the pure graphene region, BF₄⁻ ions adsorb in a monolayer parallel arrangement on the electrode surface; in the Cu NPs-doped region, due to defect healing in graphene and reduced oxygen vacancies51, ions cluster in three-dimensional formations around the nanoparticles, significantly increasing the effective specific surface area and enhancing the double-layer charge density24,52. The equivalent circuit model (Fig. 3f) shows that the double-layer capacitance (CEDL up, CEDL down) of the upper and lower electrodes are coupled in series, with the total capacitance C = (CEDL up⁻¹ + CEDL down⁻¹)⁻¹ exhibiting a gradient response to external pressure53. This design, through the synergistic effects of the porous electrodes and dielectric layer, not only achieves the dynamic expansion of the contact area A, resulting in a synergistic enhancement of the sensor’s sensitivity and detection range, but also leverages the catalytic effects at the metal-carbon composite electrode and dielectric interface to overcome the traditional limitations of EDLC charge storage, providing theoretical and technical support for high-speed response and high-sensitivity tactile sensing54.

To further clarify the microscopic mechanism by which Cu NPs and ionic liquid synergistically accelerate interfacial polarization, we quantitatively analyzed the EDL formation process using finite element simulation (Figs. 5e–h and S15). In the pure LIG electrode system, the insufficient active sites on the electrode surface and high charge transport resistance lead to slow ion migration and interfacial rearrangement, resulting in a large time constant for EDL formation. In contrast, in the LICuG electrode system, Cu NPs act as highly active charge-concentrating sites and significantly modify the ion distribution and migration pathways near the interface via the local electric field enhancement effect. On the one hand, their metallic nature exerts directional Coulombic attraction on mobile ions, effectively shortening the average distance for ions to migrate toward the interface. On the other hand, the incorporation of Cu NPs into the LIG framework reduces the overall electrode resistance, accelerates charge transport and accumulation, and enables faster establishment of the interfacial electric field. As observed from the simulation results, within the same time step, ion aggregation and charge density on the Cu NPs surface are significantly higher than those on the pure LIG electrode (Fig. 5f–h), and the spatial distribution of the EDL is more uniform. This indicates that Cu NPs not only improve the electrode conductivity but also fundamentally alter the EDL formation kinetics through local electric field regulation and ion attraction, thus increasing the ion enrichment rate and greatly reducing the time constant for interfacial charge rearrangement. Compared with the pure LIG system, the synergistic effect of LICuG and ionic liquid significantly shortens the characteristic time of EDL formation. FEA of FPFCPS

The mechanical and electronic properties of the FPFCPs were analyzed by finite element simulation, focusing on the impact mechanisms of the fully-porous structure, PVA hydrogel coating, and Cu NPs doping on the device’s sensing performance. The analysis shown in Fig. 5, based on qualitative simulation data, emphasizes the synergistic relationship between interface deformation and capacitance regulation, providing theoretical support for device structural optimization.

First, a comparative analysis was conducted between the fully-porous structure and a single porous dielectric layer. Figure S16 reveals the compression process of the fully-porous device, as shown in Fig. 5a, b. A finite element model was constructed using IL-foam with a low Young’s modulus and without a PVA hydrogel coating as the dielectric layer, paired with a smooth electrode layer (Fig. 5a). When a 100 kPa pressure was applied, the single porous structure only formed local synaptic contacts, with the contact area remaining at a very low level. In stark contrast, the fully-porous device (with high Young’s modulus LICuG porous structures in the upper and lower layers and a low-modulus, large-pore IL-foam skeleton in the middle layer) exhibited significant deformation at 10 kPa, with the electrode-dielectric interface contact area growing exponentially (Fig. 5b, d). This rapid growth continued up to 50 kPa, and within the range of 50–130 kPa, the fully-porous structure maintained the expansion of the contact area, whereas the single porous structure reached a stagnation point. This result highlights that the fully-porous architecture maximizes the control over the contact area of the EDLC through cross-scale deformation coupling between the electrode and dielectric layers, overcoming the limitations of traditional single-dielectric-layer optimization strategies and providing a new pathway for addressing the compatibility challenges of fast response, high sensitivity, and wide detection range in sensors.

A further comparative analysis is performed on the influence of the PVA hydrogel coating on the mechanical response and sensing mechanism of the devices (Fig. 5b, c). The IL-foam device without the PVA coating exhibited a two-stage compression behavior. In the low-pressure range (0–50 kPa), the physical mechanism dominating the sensitivity change was the weak elastic deformation of the dielectric layer, while the deformation of the electrode layer was negligible; ions gradually migrated to the interface to form an electric double-layer, leading to a slow increase in capacitance. In the high-pressure range (50–100 kPa), the dominant mechanism shifted to the rapid compression of the dielectric layer, which gradually reached deformation saturation; the porous electrode began to collapse significantly, and the contact area further increased, but the overall structure quickly reached the compression limit, resulting in a significant decrease in sensitivity (Fig. 5b).

The IL-PVA-foam device coated with the PVA hydrogel layer also showed a two-stage sensing behavior, but there were obvious differences in the dominant physical mechanisms in different pressure ranges compared with the IL-foam device. In the low-pressure range (0–50 kPa), the PVA hydrogel layer appropriately increased the effective Young’s modulus of the dielectric layer, which could induce the electrode to start deforming under lower pressure, enabling the capacitance to produce a significant response even under weak pressure. This mechanical modulation effect became the core mechanism dominating the sensitivity change in this range, thereby effectively reducing the lower detection limit of the device. In the high-pressure range (50–100 kPa), the dominant mechanism turned into the PVA layer delaying the rapid collapse of the dielectric layer, making the structural deformation more uniform and controllable, and significantly broadening the effective detection range of the device (Fig. 5c). As can be intuitively seen from the comparison of strain curves (Fig. 5e): the strain of the IL-foam device reached 98% at 40 kPa, and the premature structural saturation limited the further increase of capacitance; in contrast, the IL-PVA-foam device required a pressure exceeding 100 kPa to achieve equivalent deformation. This difference further confirms that the PVA coating realizes the synergistic optimization of low-pressure sensitivity and high-pressure detection range through mechanical structure modulation, and also clearly distinguishes the differences in the physical mechanisms dominating the sensitivity change of the two devices in different pressure ranges.

Analysis of the polarization behavior further reveals the core regulatory role of copper nanoparticle (Cu NPs) doping and ionic liquid pre-impregnation in the interfacial charge transfer process (Figs. 5f and S17). Its essence lies in synergistically optimizing the efficiency of charge separation, migration, and accumulation to enhance the sensor’s response performance—a mechanism that also underpins the superiority of LICuG-based sensors over traditional devices55,56.

The nature of polarization is the directional separation and accumulation of charges at the interface, and the sensor’s response speed and sensitivity depend directly on the polarization kinetic rate and total charge accumulation. The synergistic effect of Cu NPs and ionic liquid specifically addresses these two core issues. Among them, the device with LICuG electrodes and ionic liquid pre-impregnated foam (red curve) exhibits the optimal polarization kinetic characteristics, primarily due to the following: As conductive active sites dispersed in LIG, Cu NPs not only significantly reduce the charge transfer resistance of LIG electrodes, improve overall conductivity, and accelerate charge conduction at the electrode-dielectric layer interface, but their metallic properties also exert a directional attraction on mobile ions in the ionic liquid. This promotes the rapid migration of ions toward the electrode interface, shortens the time required for charge separation and accumulation, and thus enables the device’s relative capacitance to reach a stable state quickly. In contrast, the device with pure LIG electrodes (blue curve), lacking the regulatory effect of Cu NPs, has insufficient conductive active sites on the electrodes, leading to high charge transfer resistance and slow ion migration. This results in a significantly delayed and decelerated polarization process. For the LICuG device without ionic liquid (green curve), although it achieves a high initial polarization rate through the conductive and ion-attracting effects of Cu NPs, the insufficient concentration of mobile ions in the system prevents sustained charge accumulation. This ultimately leads to a remarkably low capacitance change, making high-sensitivity sensing unattainable.

The differences in the aforementioned polarization behaviors clearly illustrate the intrinsic logic by which the synergistic effect of Cu NPs and ionic liquid enhances the sensor’s response speed and sensitivity from the perspective of microscale charge transfer mechanisms—Cu NPs reduce charge transfer resistance and accelerate ion migration, while the ionic liquid provides sufficient mobile ions to ensure continuous charge accumulation. Their synergistic optimization of the polarization kinetic process offers solid microscale theoretical support for the device’s excellent sensing performance.

Sensing properties and performance test of FPFCPS

Sensitivity is a core performance indicator for pressure sensors, reflecting the sensor’s ability to respond to external mechanical stimuli50. It is defined based on the following equation: S = (ΔC/C0) /ΔP, where C0 is the initial capacitance, ΔC is the change in capacitance, and ΔP is the applied incremental pressure. The sensitivity parameter can be obtained by calculating the tangent slope of the capacitance-pressure curve, with the coefficient of determination ΔR (ranging from 0 to 1) used to evaluate the model fit. A value of ΔR approaching 1 indicates a higher degree of linearity in the fitting. As shown by the red curve in Fig. 6a, this represents the sensitivity curve of the FPFCPS over the pressure range from 0 to 500 kPa. To ensure the rigor and reproducibility of the experimental data, six sets of parallel samples were prepared in this study. The curve was plotted by statistically analyzing the test data from each group and taking the median value, which effectively reduces the impact of random errors from a single sample on the results. It can be observed that FPFCPS exhibits a sensitivity of 863.17 kPa⁻¹ in the 0–100 kPa pressure range. As the pressure gradient increases to 100–300 kPa, the sensitivity decreases to 385.76 kPa⁻¹ (R₁ = 0.994, R₂ = 0.994). Subsequently, as the device deformation saturates and ion migration stabilizes, the sensitivity drops further to 154.27 kPa⁻¹. Although the sensitivity decreased, it still maintained a high level. To verify the impact of IL pre-impregnation on the device’s performance, a comparison was made between the device without pre-impregnation and the FPFCPS. To further assess the specificity of the sensor’s identification performance, we designed a low-speed (0.4 mm/s) and small-distance (2 mm) progressive contact experiment. This system systematically analyzed the differentiated response characteristics triggered by three types of obstacle materials: glass, rubber, and foam (Fig. 8g, i, k). In addition, we integrated the FPFCPS into the robot system to perform tests related to obstacle material identification. Three typical obstacle materials—glass, rubber, and foam—were selected in the experiments. First, a material characteristic database was established through multiple sets of touch tests. Then, the three types of material obstacles were randomly arranged, and the robot completed multiple random touch detection tests according to a preset program. The breakthroughs in millisecond-level response and material identification capabilities of the sensor lay a hardware foundation for autonomous decision-making in robots operating in unstructured environments.

Conclusion

This work proposes an FPFCPS through material-structure-process collaborative innovation, achieving breakthrough improvements in core performance metrics such as sensitivity, detection range, and dynamic response time. Based on Femtosecond-laser-induced graphene (Fs-LIG) technology, a laser-induced one-step integration strategy for doping with copper precursors and in situ reduction was employed to synthesize three-dimensional porous graphene electrodes (LICuG) embedded with Cu NPs. The conductivity of LICuG (5.26 × 10³ S m−1) is 50 times higher than that of pure LIG under the same processing conditions, while also maintaining excellent mechanical flexibility. This fabrication strategy pioneers the application of LICuG into the domain of EDLC pressure sensors. As a bold and unconventional choice for the electrode layer, LICuG exhibits high electrical conductivity, hierarchical porosity, and exceptional interfacial polarization capability, effectively extending the functional limits of capacitive pressure sensing architectures. The sensor integrates an ion-functionalized polyurethane foam (IL-foam) and a gradient modulus design of the PVA hydrogel coating, establishing an electrode-dielectric cooperative deformation mechanism. Through pore interlocking and elastic buffering, dynamic regulation of the contact area is achieved, allowing for the compatibility of high sensitivity (863.17 kPa⁻¹) and wide detection range (10 Pa–500 kPa). Furthermore, the interaction between Cu NPs and ions in the dielectric layer catalyzes the electric double-layer capacitance polarization process, enabling FPFCPS to achieve instantaneous response times on the order of 5 ms. Finite element analysis in this study reveals the multiphysical field coupling at the interface between the porous electrode and dielectric layers, confirming that the fully-porous structure regulates the EDLC contact area through cross-scale deformation. The PVA hydrogel coating reconstructs the mechanical properties, while Cu NPs doping optimizes ion polarization dynamics. In addition, the FPFCPS exhibits excellent dynamic response performance in biomechanics monitoring and human–machine interaction, enabling high-precision detection of subtle pressure signals from the human body and real-time interactive perception. In the field of intelligent robotics, its millisecond-level response speed and wide-range sensitivity allow for accurate capture of transient collision signals. It also enables real-time analysis of contact pressure gradients for high-accuracy classification of obstacle material types.

Experimental section

In this study, a poly (amic acid) (PAA) solution (Type 217, Mw ≈ 60,000–80,000) was employed as the film-forming precursor. The PAA solution was supplied by Changzhou Ya’an Advanced Materials Co., and was dissolved in a binary solvent system consisting of N,N-dimethylacetamide (DMAC) and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP). Prior to casting, the PAA solution was transferred into a beaker and diluted with NMP at a volume ratio of 1:3 to modulate viscosity and facilitate uniform film deposition.

Anhydrous copper(II) chloride (CuCl₂) powder (Purity: > 98%), purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., was added to the diluted PAA solution at various concentrations. After thorough stirring to ensure homogeneity, the resulting composite sol was spin-coated onto glass slides. The coated films were subjected to a multi-step thermal imidization process: sequential baking at 80 °C for 20 min, 100 °C for 20 min, 120 °C for 20 min, 150 °C for 30 min, 180 °C for 20 min, 220 °C for 20 min, and finally 250 °C for 10 min. During the initial stages (<180 °C), residual solvents were removed, while temperatures above 180 °C triggered the cyclodehydration reaction between the amic acid and amine groups, forming rigid, thermally stable polyimide (PI) chains. After thermal curing, the films transformed into dark purple PI membranes containing a homogeneously dispersed mixture of Cu NPs.

Laser-induced graphene (LIG) formation was driven primarily by the synergistic photothermal and photochemical effects of femtosecond laser irradiation. A Ti:sapphire femtosecond oscillator (Model YF-FL-20-100-1R, manufactured in Hangzhou, China) was employed for direct laser writing. The system operated at a central wavelength of 1030 nm, with an average output power of 1.5 W, a repetition rate of 1 MHz, a scanning speed of 5 mm/s, and a ×2 objective lens. The femtosecond pulses (~fs duration) provide high peak power and ultrashort pulse widths, making them highly effective for precision micro/nanofabrication and nonlinear optical applications. Upon irradiation, the Cu-composited PI film underwent localized carbonization to form graphene, while simultaneously inducing the in situ reduction of CuCl₂ into uniformly distributed Cu NPs (10–50 nm) embedded within and on the surface of the graphene domains.

To characterize the resulting LICuG, a series of advanced techniques was employed. The surface morphology was investigated using a high-resolution field emission SEM (Geminisem360, Zeiss, Germany). Lattice structure analysis was conducted via TEM (FEI TALOS F200X G2, USA). Raman spectra were acquired using a Horiba LabRAM HR Evolution Raman spectrometer (Japan) with a 532 nm excitation wavelength. XPS was performed on a Thermo Scientific K-Alpha instrument (USA), and spectra were fitted using Avantage software. XRD analysis was carried out using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer (Japan) operating at 40 kV and 40 mA, with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 nm). Electrical conductivity was measured using a four-point probe system (ST2258C, Suzhou Jingge Electronics Co., Ltd., China), with measurements taken at four distinct positions per sample to obtain averaged values.

The dielectric layer utilized in this work was a PU foam, obtained from Hangmei Paomo Electronic Commerce Co., Ltd., selected for its accessibility and ease of processing. The IL employed was 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium tetrafluoroborate ([BMIM][BF₄] Purity ≥ 98%(HPLC)), sourced from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Institute of Chemistry. The PU foam was fully immersed in the IL for 10 minutes, then gently compressed to remove excess IL, followed by air drying for 30 minutes to enhance the presence of mobile surface ions and improve polarization and capacitive properties. The ionic liquid was controlled by varying the soaking time and air-drying time, and the selected time points correspond to the optimal values determined from the tests. Following IL infiltration, a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) hydrogel layer was prepared by dissolving PVA powder (from Yatai United Chemical Co., Ltd., Wuxi, China) in chilled water (18 °C), thoroughly stirring, and applying the solution onto the IL-foam surface using a syringe. After air drying, a uniform hydrogel coating was formed atop the foam structure.

The modeling of the ion accumulation-diffusion process was performed in COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0.0.318 software with the diluted species transport module and electrical module. First, we established two models: undoped copper nanoparticles and doped copper nanoparticles, with a basic size of 100 × 60 µm. In order to reflect the influence of the graphene structure, we use a two-dimensional mesh structure to replace the impact of graphene and randomly arrange some nanoscale spheres on the graphene mesh structure to represent copper nanoparticles. In the simulation, we set the same initial ion concentration of 70 mol/m³ for all three models. In the simulation, we consider the currently established model as a representative unit cell, which means that identical models can be assumed to exist on the left and right of the model, because symmetric ionic boundary conditions are applied at the side boundaries of the current model. The model also considers the influence of the electric field, setting up an electric field pointing from top to bottom in our structure, where ions migrate and diffuse from top to bottom under the drive of the electric field, forming a non-uniform ion concentration distribution.

We use COMSOL Multiphysics 6.0.0.318 for 3D models and numerical simulations. The Young’s modulus in the IL foam PVA region is 100 MPa, and the Poisson’s ratio in both sides is 1 GPa. The default value is 0.3. The model of polarization process is to simulate a rectangular region with a relative dielectric parameter of 3.2 and an ion diffusion coefficient of 2e-9 m2/s.

The mechanical and electronic characteristics of the flexible sensors were tested by FE-60PT Flexible Electronic Array Analysis System (Beijing Sino Aggtech Co., Ltd) (Fig. S21). The stress-strain characteristics, response & recovery properties and hysteresis effects were precisely measured by synchronizing motion, force and electronic signals at acquisition frequencies up to 1000 Hz. In addition, a brief discussion is presented in this manuscript on the robustness and applicable scope of the adopted response time-capacitance change threshold decision criterion. Constructed based on the statistical results of 100 repeated tests for each material, this criterion can stably output identification results even when there are slight fluctuations in the experimental environment and the robot’s contact speed, demonstrating excellent robustness. This criterion is mainly applicable to the three typical materials (glass, rubber, and foam) tested in this study, as well as the corresponding conventional contact conditions. It can be further extended to the identification of similar single materials with comparable mechanical properties, but it is not temporarily suitable for contact scenarios involving multi-component composite materials or extreme conditions.
