Comparative analyses of regional and temporal transcriptomics of early human and mouse central nervous systems
Authors: Zhe Wei, Xiaojie Zhou, Boyu Li, Junjie Gu, Laixiang Ge, Fan Tang, Mingyan Lin, Ying Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe unique gene expression program driving early human brain development remains incompletely defined. In this study, we profile and compare transcriptomes of human and mouse embryo central nervous system (CNS) across Carnegie stages (CS) 11–14, a critical window spanning neural tube closure to early brain regionalization, including 4 CNS regions at CS13-14. Our analysis reveals that human CNS is less mature than mouse CNS at CS13-14. Extracellular matrix (ECM) organization/structure emerges as the most divergent biological process, underscoring species-specific ECM roles in early CNS development. Co-expression module analysis reveals CS14 as the most conserved stage, CS12-13 as the most divergent, and CS11 as encompassing both conserved and species-specific events. Early spinal cord development shares regulatory mechanisms between species. This study uncovers previously unrecognized spatiotemporal/species-specific gene expression features, offering a valuable resource for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) developmental origin research and highlighting ECM pathways as promising regenerative medicine targets.

Introduction

Human organogenesis starts on embryonic day 20 (E20; Carnegie stage 9, CS9) and finishes at E56 (CS23).1 The neural tube, which forms between E20 and E27, is the earliest well-defined structure in the developing nervous system.2,3 Its closure initiates at the hindbrain/cervical boundary and then proceeds bidirectionally toward the cranial and caudal ends, with a second initiation point at the most rostral end of the forebrain, where the closure proceeds backwards. The rostral end closes at E25, followed by the caudal end at E27.3,4 Prior to complete closure, the anterior neural tube expands, forming three primary brain vesicles.3 These vesicles give rise to the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain, respectively,5 while the posterior neural tube develops into the spinal cord.6 The forebrain governs higher-order functions such as cognition, memory, emotion, sensory processing, and homeostatic regulation through structures like the cerebral cortex, thalamus, hypothalamus, and limbic system. The midbrain acts as a critical relay hub, integrating visual and auditory information, regulating reflexive responses as well as contributing to motor control, and arousal. The hindbrain, which includes the cerebellum, pons, and medulla oblongata, coordinates essential motor activities such as balance and movement, while also sustaining vital autonomic functions like breathing, heart rate, and sleep. Extending downward, the spinal cord serves as the communication pathway between the brain and body, transmitting sensory and motor signals and mediating reflexes for rapid, automatic responses. Critical morphogenetic changes in the CNS, including neural tube closure and the initial formation of distinct CNS regions, occur from CS11 to CS14. These stages represent a pivotal window for the establishment of proper CNS architectures and functions.

Throughout embryogenesis, the CNS development is orchestrated by a series of precisely regulated molecular events that ultimately lead to the formation of functional neural circuits.7,8,9 While similarities in developing brains have been documented across the species,10 increasing evidence highlights key differences between humans and mice, underscoring the necessity of comparative studies to delineate conserved and species-specific developmental programs.

Compared to other mammals, the human brain undergoes extensive expansion, primarily due to an increased number of neurons.11 This complex morphogenesis requires the precise coordination of cellular and molecular events.4,12 Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has been performed for several human neural regions, including the cortex, pituitary gland, retina, midbrain, as well as the spinal cord and cerebellum, to assess cell-type heterogeneity across different developmental time points and neural regions. However, these studies have typically focused on later stages, starting around CS14-15.12,13,14,15,16,17,18 Recently, single cells from whole human embryos at CS10 and CS12 were collected to study the cellular and molecular landscape of early gastrulation and neural development. Additionally, single cells of the whole human head of CS12-13, CS14-15, CS16-17, CS18, and CS20 were used to investigate the cell diversity, neural tube patterning, and signaling involved in the transition from epiblast cells to neuroepithelial cells and radial glia.19 Nevertheless, due to limited availability of tissue samples, detailed neural development events between every single Carnegie stage from CS11 to CS14 remain insufficiently characterized. Since neural tube closure and early CNS regionalization occur during these stages, a thorough understanding of temporal and regional gene expression during CS11-CS14 is critical for elucidating how the early human CNS develops.

Early mouse neural development shares similarities with that of humans, encompassing the conversion of epiblast cells into neuroepithelial cells, neural plate formation, and early brain patterning.3,5,20 Due to the ease of obtaining mouse embryos, more studies of gene expression profiles have been conducted in the mouse. scRNA-seq analyses of whole mouse embryos from E6 to E13 have been published,21,22 along with transcriptomic profiles for the CNS regions including the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, cerebellum, retina, and spinal cord at various developmental stages.23,24,25,26 However, there were only few comparative analyses of transcriptomes between human and mouse CNS development at early embryonic stages. One recent study compared single-cell transcriptomes from human (the mixed cell population of CS10 and CS12 embryos) with those of mice (mixed cell population from E8.5 to 10.5 embryos), identifying a human-dominant neural cluster.19 Another study analyzed human and mouse neural progenitor populations from CS12 to CS22, revealing two distinct human neural progenitor clusters in early stages of cortical development.12 Differences in cell proliferation and dopaminergic neuron development were also reported between the human ventral midbrain at week 6 to week 11 and the mouse ventral midbrain at E11.5 to E15.5, E18.5 and P21, despite overall conservation in gene expression and cell types.18 These findings emphasize the need for further in-depth comparative research into early neural development between species.

To date, a systematic comparison of gene expression across the entire CNS and its distinct regions between humans and mice at matched early developmental stages is lacking. Although scRNA-seq is powerful for identifying specific cell subtypes, it typically captures fewer transcripts than bulk RNA-seq.27 Therefore, bulk RNA-seq of distinct CNS regions at different developmental stages would provide valuable insights into both conserved and species-specific regulatory mechanisms. Such knowledge would not only deepen our understanding of human CNS development but also could guide efficient differentiation of human pluripotent stem cells into region-specific neural progenitor cells, which are critical for the regenerative medicine.

In the present study, we conducted the bulk RNA-seq on the CNS of human embryos at CS11 to CS14 (E24 to E32 human embryos), and that from mouse embryos at equivalent stage (E8.75 to E11.0 mouse embryos). To analyze region-specific gene expression, we dissected the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, and spinal cord of CS13 and CS14 embryos of both species and conducted transcriptomic profiling. Altogether, we analyzed 15 groups of human and 36 groups of mouse CNS samples. This comprehensive design enabled us to systematically explore temporal and regional gene expression features in early CNS development across species. In this particular sample pool, our temporal transcriptomic analysis reveals that human CNS development is less advanced compared to that of the mice at CS13 and CS14. Nevertheless, neurogenesis appears as a conserved process between the two species. The most prominent species-specific differences involve genes related to extracellular matrix (ECM) organization. Our analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across the four CNS regions identifies synaptic signaling and ECM structural constituents as the most divergent biological processes and molecular functions, respectively, between humans and mice, while overall CNS developmental processes are conserved. Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to evaluate conservation of gene co-expression modules, we show that CS14 is the most conserved developmental stage, while CS12 to CS13 are the least conserved. CS11 features both conserved and non-conserved developmental events. The regulatory mechanisms governing early spinal cord development are highly conserved. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comparative transcriptomic study that comprehensively profiles both the whole CNS and its subregions across matched early embryonic stages in humans and mice. The information obtained here not only enriches our understanding of early human neural development but also provides a useful resource for exploring the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) developmental origin and the use of ECM as a potential option of the treatment target for spinal cord injury in humans.

Results

The human CNS exhibits position-related gene expression signatures along the anterior-posterior axis at CS13 and CS14 and is less mature than the mouse CNS

With an aim to understand the dynamic gene expression program during early human neural development, we collected whole CNS neural tissues of human embryos from CS11 to CS14 (E24 to E32), representing the crucial stage from the neural tube closure to the establishment of primary brain vesicles,3 for RNA sequencing (Figure S1A). To explore regional features of gene expression profiles along the anterior-posterior axis, the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, and spinal cord of the CS13 and CS14 CNS were also respectively sequenced1 (Figure S1B). A total of 15 groups of human samples were used for the subsequent analyses. Top 100 highly expressed genes (in TPM: transcripts per million) across the 15 groups of human samples were listed (Table S1). In addition, to make direct comparison of temporal and region-specific transcriptomes in human versus mouse early CNS, 36 groups of mouse samples were also collected and analyzed, including whole CNS tissues from E8.75 to E11.0 (equivalent to human CS11 to CS14), as well as regional neural tissues (the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, and spinal cord) from CS13 and CS14 embryos, each with three biological replicates. Top 100 highly expressed genes in TPM across the 36 groups were also listed (Table S2). All human and mouse datasets were processed in parallel using the same analysis pipeline.

The human brain becomes 1000 times larger than that of the mouse at late stages.28 This vast difference is partly due to the greater complexity of human neural progenitor cell (NPC) types and their prolonged maturation period.29 We anticipated that the human CNS may already display a delayed maturation compared to mice, even at early embryonic stages. To test this hypothesis, hierarchical clustering was performed on both human and mouse datasets. Different developmental stages were clearly segregated in these human samples, indicating profound alterations over early neural development (Figure 1A). Although clear regional segregation was not observed, transcriptome differences were evident between the midbrain and hindbrain at both CS13 and CS14, revealing specifically regional regulation of gene expression along the anterior-posterior axis. Consistent with this observation, OTX2, which is instrumental in positioning the isthmic organizer at the midbrain-hindbrain boundary (MHB) in mouse, was expressed in the human developing forebrain and midbrain, but not in the hindbrain and spinal cord (Figures S2A and S2B). Moreover, region-specific expression profiles, in general, were not clustered together with those of the whole CNS neural tissues, suggesting distinct gene expression programs were active in different CNS regions (Figure 1A). In contrast, mouse samples were neatly separated into 6 successive groups. Notably, transcriptomic changes between CS12 and CS13 were more pronounced in mice than in humans, suggesting a faster pace of CNS development in mice (Figure 1B). Similar to human samples, molecular changes occurred between the midbrain and hindbrain at both mouse CS13 and CS14 (Figure 1B), which was consistent with both our own finding (Figure S2C) and previous studies demonstrating that the isthmic organizer located at the MHB defines mesencephalic (midbrain) and metencephalic (hindbrain) territories.30,31 Immunostaining of the mouse embryo validated that Otx2 was expressed in the forebrain and midbrain, with a sharp boundary at the MHB (Figure S2D).Figure 1Broad transcriptome changes during early CNS development of humans and mice(A) Hierarchal clustering based on transcriptomics from human samples.(B) Hierarchal clustering based on transcriptomics from mouse samples.(C) Lines showing predicted alignment of human and mouse bulk RNA-seq samples to their respective developmental trajectories of developing brains using partial least squares (PLS) analysis. To further assess the functional maturation at each stage, we leveraged two publicly available scRNA-seq datasets from developing human (1.6 million cells) and mouse (0.2 million cells) brains.23,32 Through cross-dataset alignments, we found that human samples CS11-CS14 corresponded most closely to the human brain at post conception weeks (pcw) 6–7, while mouse samples ms11-ms14 aligned with the mouse brain at embryonic days (E) 7–11 (Figure 1C). Furthermore, by projecting the human bulk transcriptomic data onto the mouse developmental trajectory, we compared the relative degree of maturation between human CS11-CS14 and mouse CS11-CS14 samples. Consistently, ms13-ms14 appeared to represent more advanced developmental stages, which was further supported by a higher predicted proportion of glioblast cells as inferred by CIBERSORTx (Figure 1D).

Taken together, similar to mice, the human CNS exhibits region-specific gene expression patterns along the anterior-posterior axis, particularly between the midbrain and hindbrain, at CS13 and CS14. However, the human CNS displays a less mature state than the mouse embryo at equivalent developmental stages.

Human and mouse early CNS exhibit species- and stage-dependent expression of ECM genes

To explore the basis for the observed differences in CNS maturation between humans and mice during early development, we performed differential gene expression analyses between adjacent stages. For the mouse CNS, we identified 398 DEGs between CS12 and CS11, 1088 DEGs between CS13 and CS12, and 3 DEGs between CS14 and CS13 (Figure S3A). The small number of DEGs between the mouse CS13 and CS14 CNS suggested that mouse CNS development might reach a relatively stable transcriptional state by CS13. Moreover, the large number of DEGs between CS12 and CS13 corroborated previous findings of major transcriptional shifts at this stage.21 Differentially, the human CNS showed 176 DEGs between CS12 and CS11, 43 DEGs between CS13 with CS12, and 86 DEGs between CS14 with CS13 (Figure S3B), indicating relatively modest changes between CS12 and CS13. This suggests that transcriptional transitions in human CNS development during this interval are less pronounced than those in mice.

To further understand regulatory dynamics, we applied soft clustering to DEGs across all stages (CS11 to CS14), identifying nine distinct temporal expression clusters for mouse and human datasets, respectively (Figures 2A and 2B). For mouse samples, clusters 3 and 8 were classified as early DEGs, clusters 2, 4, and 6 as middle DEGs, and clusters 1, 5, 7, and 9 as late DEGs (Figure 2A). In human samples, clusters 1, 4, 7, and 9 were classified as early DEGs, clusters 5 and 8 as middle DEGs, and clusters 2, 3, and 6 as late DEGs (Figure 2B). A lineage network based on differentially expressed transcription factors (TFs) was constructed to infer the regulatory chain across the developmental stages. The analysis revealed a clear temporal progression in transcriptional regulation during mouse CNS development, which was not observed in human samples (Figures 2C–2E), suggesting distinct regulatory trajectories between species. For example, Klf6, Nr1d2, Egr1, Pou4f1, and Mybl1 were apparently differentially expressed across stages in mice, with the first three being highly expressed at CS12 and the latter two at CS13, respectively (Figure S3C). The enrichment analysis showed that human early and late DEGs shared similar developmental processes to those of mouse early and late DEGs, respectively. However, mid-stage DEGs diverged substantially: mouse mid-stage DEGs were enriched for anterior/posterior pattern specification and regionalization, while human mid-stage DEGs were predominantly associated with protein translation and localization (Figure 2F). These differences indicated that patterning processes might occur at different developmental time points between species. Further analysis of patterning-related DEGs revealed that the expression of most of these genes peaked at CS12 and CS13 in mice. However, the expression of more than half of the genes peaked at CS11 or CS14 in humans, although several genes also showed peak expression at CS12 or CS13 (Figures S3D and S3E). These findings suggest that CS12 and CS13 represent critical periods for patterning during mouse CNS development, whereas patterning may occur across a broader developmental window from CS11 to CS14 in humans.

To further characterize molecular distinctions between humans and mice, we compared temporal expression patterns from CS11 to CS14, restricted to orthologous genes between the two species. More than half of these genes showed conserved temporal expression (Pearson correlation >0.5) and were enriched for neurogenesis, neuron differentiation, and neuron development (Figures 3A and 3B). Of note, terms associated with ECM organization, extracellular structure organization, and collagen fibril organization were enriched among divergent genes (Pearson correlation <0) (Figure 3C). Particularly, expression levels of the majority of the ECM genes were highest in human CS11 samples, and their abundance then decreased toward the late stage. Synaptic signaling and ECM structural constituent are the most non-conserved features across the species during CNS regionalization

To explore the similarities and differences in the CNS regionalization between humans and mice, region-specific patterns of gene expression across four regions were investigated. First, we performed DEG analysis for various CNS regions between CS13 and CS14. In mice, we discovered 8 DEGs between CS13fore and CS14fore samples, 4 between CS13mid and CS14mid samples, 8 between CS13hind and CS14hind samples, and 10 between CS13spin and CS14spin samples (Figure S4A). The minimal difference supported the notion that mouse CNS samples at CS13 and CS14 might represent comparable developmental stages. In humans, 82 DEGs were discovered between CS13fore and CS14fore, 1889 DEGs between CS13mid and CS14mid, 171 DEGs between CS13hind and CS14hind, and 581 DEGs between CS13spin and CS14spin samples (Figure S4B), indicating more pronounced developmental shifting, particularly in the midbrain, hindbrain, and spinal cord. We then combined CS13 and CS14 samples by regions and performed pairwise DEG analyses among the four CNS regions. In mice, we detected 950 DEGs between forebrain and midbrain, 595 DEGs between midbrain and hindbrain, and 603 DEGs between hindbrain and spinal cord samples (Figure S4C). In humans, there were 103 DEGs between forebrain and midbrain, 356 DEGs between midbrain and hindbrain, and 90 DEGs between hindbrain and spinal cord samples (Figure S4D). Subsequently, we softly clustered these DEGs to reveal distinct region-specific patterns. In mice, 13 clusters were identified. Clusters 1 and 12 were defined as forebrain DEGs, clusters 7 and 11 as midbrain DEGs, cluster 13 as hindbrain DEGs, clusters 8 and 9 as spinal cord DEGs, clusters 2 and 5 as forebrain+midbrain DEGs, cluster 3 as midbrain+hindbrain DEGs, and clusters 4, 6, and 10 as hindbrain+spinal cord DEGs (Figure 4A). In humans, among 9 clusters identified, cluster 2 was defined as forebrain DEGs, clusters 4 and 9 as midbrain DEGs, cluster 1 as hindbrain DEGs, cluster 5 as spinal cord DEGs, clusters 7 and 8 as forebrain+midbrain DEGs, and clusters 3 and 6 as hindbrain+spinal cord DEGs (Figure 4B). Interestingly, fewer DEGs were shared between midbrain and hindbrain in mice, and none were clustered into the midbrain+hindbrain group in humans (Figures S4E and 4B), supporting the existence of distinct genetic programs between these regions along the anterior-posterior axis. This observation aligned with our earlier results (Figures 1A, 1B, and S2A–S2D). Lineage analysis of known TFs further revealed that forebrain+midbrain and hindbrain+spinal cord genes occupied transitional positions between the forebrain and midbrain, and hindbrain and spinal cord, respectively (Figures 4C and 4D). Early CNS development in humans and mice involves both conserved and non-conserved regulatory mechanisms from CS11 to CS14

To further validate the conservation of temporal and region-specific gene expression modules, we applied the Zsummary, a permutation-based preservation statistic from the WGCNA.33,34 The Zsummary provides a quantitative measure of module preservation between datasets: Zsummary <2 implies no evidence for module preservation, weak to moderate evidence is suggested by 2 < Zsummary <10, and strong evidence for module preservation is implied by Zsummary >10. We assessed module preservation between humans and mice using orthologous genes and the temporal gene clusters defined in human CNS development (Figure 2B). Among the human defined clusters, clusters 3, 5, and 7 had Zsummary >10 when being tested against mouse data (Figure 6A), indicating strong preservation of these gene expression modules. Cluster 7 was categorized as an early DEGs participating in CS11 development, while Cluster 5 was defined as middle DEGs involved in CS11 and CS12 development (Figure 2B). Cluster 3 was categorized as the late DEGs that were associated with CS14 development (Figure 2B). These findings suggested that key regulatory programs were shared between species particularly during early (CS11) and late (CS14) stages of CNS development. According to the enrichment analysis, the most preserved processes at CS11 and CS12 included modulation of chemical synaptic transmission, axonogenesis, cognition, learning or memory, and neurotransmitter transport (Figure 6B). The most conserved processes at CS14 included CNS neuron differentiation, forebrain development, axon development, cognition, gliogenesis, and learning or memory (Figure 6C). In contrast, the most poorly preserved clusters, Clusters 4 and 8, were categorized as early DEGs involved in CS11 development and middle DEGs involved in CS12-13 development, respectively, based on the thresholds (Figures 6A and 2B). Human and mouse early spinal cord development share similar regulatory mechanisms

Orthologous genes were also used to evaluate the preservation of regional gene expression clusters between humans and mice, based on the clusters defined in human CNS development (Figure 4B). The same thresholds were applied. Cluster 5 and cluster 3 detected in mice had Zsummary >10 in the human data (Figure 7A), suggesting a well preserved connectivity level of these clusters between human and mouse CNS regional development. Both cluster 3 and cluster 5 included DEGs contributing to the formation of the spinal cord (Figure 4B). Pattern specification process, regionalization, neuromuscular synaptic transmission, and neurotransmitter biosynthetic process were among the most preserved processes involved in mouse and human early spinal cord development (Figure 7B). Interestingly, actin-mediated cell contraction and muscle tissue growth also emerged as highly preserved processes in early spinal cord development for both species (Figures 7B and 7C). Discussion

Despite significant advances in identifying transcriptional cell types in the brain based on scRNA-seq analysis in both humans and mice, our understanding of the transcriptional programs regulating early human neural development and regionalization remains limited due to the scarcity of suitable embryonic tissues. Moreover, the size of samples in scRNA-seq studies was often small, leading to the failure to capture the broader gene expression landscapes and regulatory programs governing early CNS development across time and regions. Additionally, TFs are often missed in scRNA-seq analysis due to their relatively low expression levels. In this study, we present a comprehensive transcriptional landscape of early CNS development by bulk RNA sequencing of human neural tissues across key stages, from neural tube closure to early regionalization following gastrulation. Through parallel analysis of human and mouse samples spanning CS11 to CS14, including region-specific samples at CS13 and CS14, we identified both temporally and regionally conserved as well as species-specific gene expression patterns across different developmental time points and neural regions. Notably, in this sample pool, the human CNS was functionally less mature than the mouse CNS at CS13 and CS14, and the clear developmental transition observed in mice from CS11 to CS14 was absent in humans. These are possibly attributed to species-specific regulatory mechanisms. Some brain regions developed at similar paces in the two species, but the development of other regions delayed in human. Among the stages examined, CS14 was the most conserved stage across species, while CS12 to CS13 exhibited the greatest divergence. CS11 displayed a mix of conserved and unique transcriptional events. Although the forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain regions might follow different regulatory programs, early spinal cord development was governed by shared molecular mechanisms in the two species. Collectively, our findings underscore the importance of studying human-specific developmental processes directly in human tissues to better understand early human brain development.

The ECM, which accounts for approximately 40% of the total volume of the developing brain and 20% of the adult brain,35 forms a non-fibrous scaffold unique to the CNS. The ECM regulates various processes throughout brain development, including proliferation and differentiation of NPCs, migration, axonal/dendritic growth and guidance, connectivity, and synaptic plasticity.36,37,38 The ECM is also dynamically modulated by stress, which can influence emotional regulation, learning, and memory,39 and undergoes pathological remodeling in conditions such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).40 A recent study showed that human and mouse brain vascular ECM shared about 66% ECM markers.41 In addition, considerably more ECM genes were expressed in the developing human neocortex than in the mouse embryonic neocortex.42 Furthermore, the ECM plays a complex role in the development of the nervous system. Despite advances in recent years, the full complexity of ECM functions in the CNS across species remains to be elucidated.37 Our data revealed highly divergent temporal and regional expression of ECM genes across species. Notably, more differentially expressed ECM genes were found in the human midbrain and spinal cord, whereas prominent changes in ECM gene expression were detected in the mouse forebrain, hindbrain, and spinal cord. These results highlight the potential of ECM genes not only as developmental markers but also as therapeutic targets. For example, early spinal cord ECM has been shown to promote NPC proliferation, neuronal maturation, and axon growth, as well as improve the therapeutic efficacy of spinal cord organoids in injury models.43

AD is one of the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorders in the elderly, with a multifactorial and still poorly understood pathogenesis.44 Emerging evidence suggests that early neurodevelopmental events may influence later susceptibility to AD. For instance, mutations in PSEN1, a major familial AD gene, can promote premature neuronal differentiation and apoptosis in patient-derived NPCs, while disrupted Notch signaling in cortical organoids derived from AD iPSCs has been implicated in early pathological changes.45 Moreover, pre-tangle pathology has been detected in children as young as six years old.46 These findings raise the possibility of a developmental origin of AD.47 By integrating 75 AD risk loci identified from a large-scale GWAS48 with our early human CNS RNA-seq data, we found several AD risk genes expressed in a region- and stage-specific manner (Figures S6A and S6B). Among them, ANXA3 and GSTA4 were predominantly expressed in the developing forebrain and were known to regulate cell death.49,50,51 Moreover, SMPD3, PLD4, and CNPY1 were enriched in the spinal cord, consistent with clinical observations linking spinal cord injury to increased AD risk.52 Our functional enrichment of 16 stage-specific AD genes revealed a strong association with immune regulation (Figure S6C), supporting previous reports that immune-related pathways contribute to AD pathogenesis.53,54,55 Our network analysis further indicated that SMPD3, CD2AP, CLU, APH1B, and ANK3 may interact with APP (Figure S6D), the central gene in familial AD.56,57 Nevertheless, AD-susceptibility genes may possess distinct, stage-specific functions and their early developmental activity could be independent of their later involvement in neurodegeneration.

Another noteworthy finding is the presence of TFs involved in muscle development in the developing spinal cord. Communications between the nerve and muscle are essential for the movement of our body. The spinal cord forms a nutritional relationship with the skeletal muscle via peripheral nerves. By transmitting action potentials to the motor endplate, spinal motor neurons trigger the contraction of skeletal muscles.58 Although trophic and functional interactions between the spinal cord and muscle have been studied,59,60,61 roles of muscle-related genes in spinal cord development have not been fully understood. In a recent work that sequenced the human spinal cord from gestation week (GW) 7 to 25 in a single cell resolution, some myosin light chain genes, such as MYL12A, MYL12B1, and MYL12B6, were identified as DEGs in microglia, neuronal types, and mature oligodendrocytes.62 Here, we also identified certain TFs involved in muscle tissue development in the developing human spinal cord. For example, ACTC1 was previously reported to be expressed and functional in both medulloblastoma and glioma63,64,65; MYL1 was reported to be involved in the re-organization of cytoskeletal network required for migration of dopamine neurons.66 These findings suggest potential actin-related mechanisms linking muscle gene networks to spinal cord development.

Our finding of differential expression of genes involved in functions of mature neurons at these early stages before the onset of neuronal differentiation and synaptogenesis is interesting. During early neurogenesis, progenitor cells and nascent neurons initiate “priming” transcriptional programs that include genes encoding ion channels, synaptic proteins, and neurotransmitter machinery, even before morphological or electrophysiological maturation occurs. For example, in the mouse adult hippocampus, genes for synaptic signaling were upregulated in immature neurons prior to full functional incorporation.67 Thus, the early activation of neuronal gene expression would probably represent transcriptional priming for subsequent differentiation and maturation, rather than the evidence of functionally mature neurons.

The similarities and differences in regional-specific early CNS development across species were also compared. Consistent with previous studies, we found that forebrain markers such as FOXG168,69,70 and SIX371 were highly expressed in both human and mouse forebrains. Similarly, midbrain markers like EN1 and EN272 were prominently expressed in both species. The hindbrain and spinal cord markers, including HOXA2, HOXA4, and HOXB4,73 were also shared between humans and mice. Certain regulatory mechanisms in human brain development appeared to be distinct, while there were shared regional-specific markers between humans and mice. For instance, the mouse forebrain marker Gsx2, which is known to play a crucial role in the patterning of the ventral lateral ganglionic eminence (vLGE) and dorsal lateral ganglionic eminence (dLGE),74 was found to be highly expressed in the developing human hindbrain, suggesting a human-specific function of GSX2 in hindbrain development.

In summary, this study offers a comprehensive transcriptional landscape of CNS development from neural tube closure to early regionalization in humans and mice. It identifies conserved and species-specific regulatory features, highlights the unique expression dynamics of ECM genes, and suggests a potential developmental origin of AD. These data serve as a valuable resource for future research in developmental neurobiology, neurodegenerative diseases, and regenerative medicine.

Limitations of the study

Despite aforementioned advances, this study has several limitations. First, each human sample group in this study contained at least 5 embryos for sequencing. However, certain samples, such as CS11 and regional tissues from CS13 and CS14, had limited biological replicates. This could affect statistical robustness. Second, bulk RNA-seq lacks spatial and cell type resolution. Third, we identified several AD-related genes, which were expressed during CNS development. However, their specific roles in neural development and disease progression require functional validation. Finally, we cannot rule out the possibility that residual non-neural tissues might contribute to the gene detection, although we did our best to maximize the neural tissue purity. The non-neural tissues, including the muscle tissue, may have contributed to the detection of muscle-associated genes in the spinal cord, though our findings favor a potential developmental role of these genes.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human embryos

Collection and experiments on human embryos were reviewed and approved by the Ethical Review Board of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (ER-SIBS-241407), in compliance with the National Ethical Guideline on Human Embryo Research issued by the Committee on Bioethics, Chinese National Human Genome Center (Southern Headquarter). Human embryos were obtained during clinical drug abortion at Xinhua Hospital. Briefly, medical abortion was conducted in donors within 49 days of gestation who had no pharmacological contraindications, using a regimen of 200 mg mifepristone followed by 800 μg misoprostol 24–48 h later. All donors signed informed consent after learning about the research’s objective and the procedures with which the embryos would be used. Human embryos from E24 to E32 (CS11 to CS14) were collected. Embryos at the same developmental stage (CS12-CS14) were randomly grouped to serve as biological replicates. Biological sex of human embryos was not explicitly included in our study because we did not intend to investigate differences in early human CNS development caused by biological sex.

Mouse embryos

Mouse embryo collection was performed in compliance with protocols approved by the Animal Committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine. Embryos from E8.75 to E11 (CS11 to CS14) were isolated from C57BL/6J female mice aged 8–10 weeks. Mice were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment with a 12-h light-dark cycle. Mice were humanely euthanized by gradual exposure to carbon dioxide in accordance with institutional animal care and use guidelines. Embryos at the same developmental stage (CS11-CS14) were randomly grouped to serve as biological replicates. Biological sex of mouse embryos was not explicitly included in our study because we did not intend to investigate differences in early mouse CNS development caused by biological sex.

Method details

Human sample collection

Human embryos from E24 to E32 spanning four Carnegie stages (CS11 to CS14) were collected carefully according to standard morphological criteria.1 The neural tissues of the CNS, which includes the entire brain vesicle and the whole spinal cord, were dissected manually. Embryos were treated with Dispase (Gibco, 17105041) at 1 mg/mL for 3–5 min at room temperature. Neural tissues were collected by microdissection under the stereomicroscope in cold PBS, according to anatomical landmarks at the corresponding developmental stage. Non-neural tissues were removed carefully during dissection. The boundary between the forebrain and midbrain was determined based on the natural curvature at the transition region. Neural tissues of CS13 and CS14 were further dissected into four parts, the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain and spinal cord. These samples were then subjected to RNA extraction and sequencing. Two biological replicates were used for neural tissues from CS12 to CS14 with 5 embryos for each replicate. Due to the limited material availability, there were only one replicate of neural tissues for CS11 with a total of 10 embryos, and one replicate with 5 embryos for each neural region of CS13 and CS14 embryos.

Mouse sample collection

CNS neural tissues of mouse embryos from E8.75-E11 (CS11 to CS14) were dissected carefully and manually, divided by stages according to standard morphological criteria.1 Embryos were treated with Dispase (Gibco, 17105041) at 1 mg/mL for 3–5 min at room temperature. Neural tissues were isolated by microdissection under the stereomicroscope in cold PBS. CS13 and CS14 neural tissues were further dissected into the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain and spinal cord. These samples were then subjected to RNA extraction and sequencing. There were three biological replicates for each experimental group (S11, S12, S13, S14, S13fore, S13mid, S13hind, S13spin, S14fore, S14mid, S14hind and S14spin), and 5 embryos in each replicate.

RNA extraction, library preparation, bulk RNA sequencing and data analysis

RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, #15596026). Libraries were prepared and sequenced on the Illumina platform in accordance with Illumina manufacturer instructions. Gene expression profiling of human samples was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform with a 100 bp paired-end (100 PE) sequencing mode, generating no less than 4 Gb of data per sample. Reads containing adapter sequences were filtered out. Paired-end reads were discarded if either read contained more than 3% ambiguous bases (N), or if over 50% of bases in either read had a quality score below 30. Mouse samples were sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform in a 125 bp paired-end (125 PE) mode, with each sample producing about 1 Gb of data. Sequencing quality was high, with Q30 scores exceeding 83%. Alignment was conducted by STAR. The GC content and duplication bias were corrected in human datasets. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with an adjusted p - value (false discovery rate, FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute log2 fold change >1 and representing spatiotemporal markers across brain regions and developmental stages, were identified using DESeq2.77 The gene enrichment analysis was performed using ToppGene78 and clusterProfiler.79 Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess similarities in gene expression profiles across samples and visualized through hierarchical clustering dendrograms.

Prediction of functional maturation

Two publicly available scRNA-seq datasets from developing human (1.6 million cells) and mouse (0.2 million cells) brains23,32 were utilized to predict the functional state of samples from stages CS11 to CS14. Partial least squares components (PLSCs) that showed a linear correlation with the development trajectory were extracted. The first two components, PLSC1 and PLSC2, together accounted for >80% of the variance associated with early neuronal development. These components effectively separated and ordered the reference dataset according to their development stages. Based on the loadings of gene expression data onto PLSC1 and PLSC2, a linear model was constructed to predict the developmental alignment of each bulk RNA-seq sample. To further characterize the cellular composition underlying these samples, we utilized the single-cell RNA-seq dataset from the developing mouse brain as a reference and applied CIBERSORTx for digital cytometry. Cell-type proportions were inferred from the bulk transcriptomic profiles, and only cell populations with non-zero estimated fractions, such as radial glia, glioblasts, and neuroblasts, were retained for visualization.

Construction and preservation analysis of gene co-expression modules using WGCNA

Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed to identify co-expressed gene modules across temporal and regional CNS transcriptomic data.80 Gene expression matrices were filtered to include the top varying genes, and normalized expression values were used to construct a signed network using the WGCNA R package. An appropriate soft-thresholding power (β) was selected based on scale-free topology criteria. Adjacency matrices were transformed into topological overlap matrices (TOMs), and hierarchical clustering was conducted to define gene modules using dynamic tree cut algorithms. Modules were assigned unique colors for visualization, and module eigengenes (MEs), representing the first principal component of each module, were calculated to summarize module expression profiles.

To evaluate the conservation of co-expression modules between human and mouse CNS development, module preservation analysis was conducted using the modulePreservation() function in WGCNA. Orthologous genes shared between species were used as the input. The analysis was based on permutation testing (n = 200) to compute Zsummary statistics, which quantify the preservation of module connectivity and density between reference (human) and test (mouse) datasets. As commonly accepted, Zsummary >10 indicates strong evidence of preservation, 2 < Zsummary <10 suggests weak to moderate preservation, and Zsummary <2 implies no preservation. This approach enabled the identification of both conserved and species-specific regulatory gene modules during early CNS development.

Lineage analysis to reveal regulatory dynamics across CNS regions and developmental stages

DEGs were clustered using Mfuzz,81 a soft clustering algorithm, to identify spatiotemporal gene expression patterns, which were then used to determine stage- and region-specific genes. To further explore the regulatory dynamics across CNS regions and developmental stages, known stage- and region-specific transcription factors (TFs) were visualized in a lineage network constructed using the igraph package.82 In this network, each node represents a TF, and edges connect pairs of TFs with expression correlation coefficients greater than 0.8. The network layout was generated using the Fruchterman-Reingold algorithm, which optimizes region-specific organization to reveal potential regulatory relationships.

Conservation analysis of temporal and region-specific expression patterns between humans and mice

To assess the conservation of gene expression dynamics during CNS development between humans and mice, we first identified orthologous genes that showed significant differential expression in at least one developmental stage or CNS region in either species. These genes were selected based on DESeq2 results and filtered to include only high-confidence one-to-one orthologs.

Temporal conservation was assessed by calculating the Pearson correlation coefficient for the expression profiles of each orthologous gene across four developmental stages (CS11 to CS14 in humans and the corresponding stages in mice). Similarly, region-specific conservation was evaluated based on expression patterns across four neural regions: the forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain, and spinal cord. For each gene, we computed the correlation between its expression trajectory in human and mouse datasets.

Genes with a Pearson correlation coefficient >0.5 were classified as having conserved temporal or region-specific expression patterns, indicating potential conservation of developmental roles between the two species. In contrast, genes with a Pearson correlation coefficient <0 were considered to have divergent or potentially altered functions in early CNS development across species. This analysis allowed us to distinguish genes with conserved regulatory functions from those involved in species-specific developmental processes, providing insights into evolutionary divergence in CNS patterning and maturation.

Immunofluorescence staining

Embryos were fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 12 h at 4°C. After washing with 1 × PBS for 10 min × 3 times at room temperature, samples were dehydrated in 30% sucrose/PBS solution and stored at 4°C overnight. When embryos were completely dehydrated, they were embedded in O.C.T Compound (Sakura Finetek) and frozen at −80°C. Following sectioning and drying, the samples were washed with 1 × PBS for 10 min to remove the embedding medium. After being fixed in ice-cold acetone for 10 min and washed with 1 × PBS for 5 min × 3 times, they were incubated with 0.3% Triton X-100/PBS for 1 h at room temperature, rinsed 5 min × 3 times with 1 × PBS, and incubated in the blocking buffer (10% BSA/PBS) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies in 2% BSA/0.2% Triton X-100/PBS overnight at 4°C. Primary antibodies used were: anti-Sox1 (R&D, AF3369) and anti-Otx2 (Abcam, ab183951). On the next day, the sections were washed 5 min × 3 times with PBS before incubating with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated or 546-conjugated secondary antibodies (ThermoFisher) for 2 h at room temperature. After being washed with 1 × PBS for 5 min × 3 times, DNA was stained by DAPI (Sigma, D9542). Samples were mounted using the fluorescence mounting medium (DAKO). Images were captured with a Zeiss Cell Observer microscope.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2) unless otherwise stated. Differential gene expression analyses were conducted using DESeq2, applying the Wald test, with multiple testing correction performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using hypergeometric tests as implemented in clusterProfiler.

For bulk RNA-seq analyses, n represents the number of independent replicates. No technical replicates were treated as independent samples.

Details of the statistical results of differential gene expression analyses are provided in the Tables S1 and S2. All statistical tests were two-sided, and the statistical significance was defined as adjusted p < 0.05.
