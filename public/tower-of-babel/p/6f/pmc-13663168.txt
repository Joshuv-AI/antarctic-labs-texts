Transcriptomic profiling of pediatric asthma by diseases state and severity
Authors: Jihoon Kim, Jelte Kelchtermans, Frank Mentch, Fengxiang Wang, James Snyder, Yichuan Liu, Joseph Glessner, Hakon Hakonarson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the high prevalence of pediatric asthma, the molecular basis of its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, in part due to the complex and heterogeneous nature of the disease.MethodsIn this study, we performed bulk RNA sequencing of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 183 children (114 asthma cases, 69 controls) across two independent cohorts as a minimally invasive approach to capture the gene expression changes underlying asthma status and severity.ResultsOur analysis identified robust signatures of pediatric asthma, including differential expression of a canonical asthma gene (RAB7B) and two novel genes (RPP38-DT, NCAL1), which encode long non-coding RNAs. Additionally, severity-stratified analyses revealed upregulation of several immune-related genes (CCL8, ACOD1, CXCL9, SLAMF1, EPN2) and downregulation of an uncharacterized transcript (ENSG00000288973) as asthma severity increased. Enrichment and co-expression analysis further implicate impaired type I interferon signaling in asthma disease severity.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate contributions to the pathobiology of asthma among several novel molecular candidate genes, which may support future efforts towards molecular stratification of pediatric asthma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03805-0.

Background

Asthma is the most common chronic disease of childhood. [1] Despite its high prevalence, the underlying pathophysiology of asthma remains incompletely understood, in part due to the complex and heterogeneous nature of the disease. [2, 3] Consequently, current treatment strategies for asthma are primarily aimed at managing clinical phenotypes (e.g. symptom severity, lung function), rather than targeting the underling molecular mechanisms, which can limit their effectiveness, particularly in severe cases. [4, 5].

Transcriptomic analysis, which profiles the complete set of ribonucleic acid (RNA) transcripts under specific biological conditions, offers a powerful approach to identify gene signatures, uncover molecular mechanisms, and characterize disease subtypes. Specifically, advances in high-throughput technologies, such as microarrays and bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), have enabled efficient capture and comprehensive analysis of gene expression patterns. [6] Previous transcriptomics analysis of airway-derived cells, including the sputum and bronchial epitheliums, have successfully characterized subtypes and immune signatures in adult asthma. [7–9] However, sampling airway-derived cells is invasive and less feasible in pediatric populations.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) are minimally invasive alternatives for transcriptomic analysis; PBMCs can reflect over 80% of gene expression changes relevant to systemic immune processes and have been effectively used in transcriptomic analyses of various immune-mediated diseases. [10] In asthma, previous studies have shown that children and adults with severe disease exhibit distinct blood transcriptomic profiles compared to controls, underscoring the potential of PBMC-based gene expression analysis for identifying molecular signatures and stratifying disease subtypes in asthma. [11–14].

In this study, we performed transcriptomic profiling of PBMCs from pediatric asthma patients and controls. Our goal was to identify gene expression patterns and pathways associated with asthma status and severity in children to better understand the molecular basis of and develop precision diagnostics and targeted therapeutics for pediatric asthma.

Methods

Study population and phenotyping

We conducted an asthma case-control study using two independent pediatric cohorts, the asthma TopMed cohort and the KidsFirst pediatric cohort, both of which were assembled and submitted for whole genome and RNA sequencing by the Center for Applied Genomics (CAG) at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP). Briefly, the TopMed cohort comprises patients who received healthcare at CHOP, with asthma cases recruited from pulmonary and allergy specialty clinics. The KidsFirst cohort comprises pediatric subjects enrolled through the CAG biobank of whom a subset has Down syndrome and asthma. Asthma cases from both cohorts were defined by the eMERGE electronic-phenotyping criteria, as previously described. [15] Controls were selected from the respective cohorts, with exclusion criteria relaxed to increase the number of available. Demographic information, including age at sample collection, sex, body mass index (BMI), and self-reported race and ethnicity was extracted from the electronic medical record for each subject. Patients’ most severe recorded ICD10 codes were used to classify asthma severity.

Transcriptomic profiling

Primary blood specimens were collected between 2006 and 2024 for RNA sequencing. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from whole blood by Ficoll density gradient centrifugation. Total RNA was extracted from PBMC using the Maxwell RSC simplyRNA Cells Kit (Promega, cat#AS1390) following manufacturer’s protocol. Library preparation and sequencing was performed at the University of Washington Northwest Genomics Center. Briefly, ribosomal depletion and cDNA synthesis were performed using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA LP Globin kit (Illumina, cat#20020612), with all steps automated on the Perkin Elmer Sciclone NGSx Workstation to minimize batch variability. Final RNASeq libraries were quantified with the Quant-iT dsDNA High Sensitivity assay, and insert size was evaluated using HS NGS Fragment Kit (Agilent, cat#DNF474). Libraries with > 4% adapter dimers were excluded. Technical controls (K-562; Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# AM7832) were included to monitor batch consistency. Final libraries were normalized to 10nM and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Base calls (RTA 3.1.5) were demultiplexed to produce unaligned BAM files using Picard, then converted to FASTQ format using Samtools (v1.4). [16, 17] Sequences were aligned to GRCh38 reference transcriptome (GENCODE v39) using STAR (v2.6.1d), and transcript-level quantification was performed using Salmon (v1.10.0). [18–20].

Differential expression analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to assess global expression structure and potential confounding by asthma status and severity, age, sex, BMI category, and cohort membership. DESeq2 was used to assess differential expression between patients with and without asthma and to identify genes associated with asthma severity. [21] To identify genes with consistent associations across both cohorts, all 183 samples (114 asthmatic, 69 control) were pooled and cohort membership was included as a covariate in the design formula to account for technical variation across cohorts. Prior to differential expression analysis, genes on the sex chromosomes (X/Y) were removed. For analyses involving the subset of KidsFirst samples with Down syndrome, chromosome 21 genes were removed to account for Down syndrome–related transcriptional effects. Low expression genes (< 10 counts in < 10% of samples) were also excluded. P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons by the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure (FDR < 0.05 threshold). As modest effect sizes are common in transcriptomics analysis of complex traits, no additional log2 fold change threshold was applied. [22, 23].

Results

Population characteristics

The TopMed data generated cohort comprised 75 children with asthma and 44 controls. Cases had a lower mean age than controls (12 ± 5.5 years vs.14 ± 4.9 years, p = 0.013), while sex distribution (p = 0.101) and racial composition (p = 0.225) did not differ significantly between groups; distribution of BMI categories differed significantly (p = 0.039), with a greater proportion of asthma cases classified as overweight (Table 1). The KidsFirst data generated cohort included 39 children with asthma and 24 controls of similar demographics. Cases were younger than controls (9.7 ± 4.4 vs. 14 ± 5.5 years, p = 0.001), while sex distribution (p = 0.339), racial composition (p = 0.257), and BMI category distribution (p = 0.267) did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1). When pooled, the combined dataset of 182 samples showed no significant difference in racial composition across asthma status (p = 0.173); however, the distribution of sex (p = 0.042), age (p < 0.001) and BMI category (p = 0.028) differed significantly (Table 1) supporting the use of principal component analysis (PCA) to ensure potential confounding by these variables was addressed. Despite these differences, PCA revealed no clustering or discernible patterns associated with sex, race, age, BMI category, or asthma status (data not shown), but did demonstrate a modest batch effect corresponding to cohort membership, prompting the inclusion of an additional covariate to account for batch effect (Supplementary Fig. 1A). To determine that the observed differences in age and BMI did not drive asthma-associated transcriptomic differences in our downstream analyses, we performed additional stratified differential expression analyses by predicted pubertal status and BMI category. Pubertal status was approximated using age stratified by sex (male: pre-puberty < 11 years, post-puberty > 15 years; female: pre-puberty < 10 years, post-puberty > 14 years), with samples in the predicted puberty range excluded due to increased biological variability during this developmental period. BMI categories were assigned using BMI percentile thresholds (underweight: 0–5th percentile, healthy: 5th–85th percentile, overweight: 85th–100th percentile). Across all analyses, log2 fold change directions were fully consistent with the full dataset analysis, suggesting that imbalances in age and BMI did not meaningfully affect asthma-associated differential expression analysis (Supplementary Table 1).

Cohort-specific expression profiles

In the TopMed cohort, PCA of gene expression values did not separate samples by asthma status, age, or sex. However, two genes, SCN5A (log2 fold change = 3.74; FDR-adjusted p-value < 0.001) and NCAL1 asthma (log2 fold change = 0.57; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.017) were significantly upregulated in patients with asthma. The KidsFirst cohort likewise showed no distinct expression clustering by disease, and four transcripts, ENSG00000270022 (log2 fold change = 2.25; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.019), ENSG00000285646 (log2 fold change = 2.12; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.002), ENSG00000276216 (log2 fold change = 1.39; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.039), and RPP38-DT (log2 fold change = 1.32; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.011), were up-regulated in asthma.

Pooled differential expression

After combining cohorts and adjusting for cohort membership in the differential expression model, PCA showed no clear separation by asthma status, but modest separation by cohort (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Importantly, PCA visualization of expression values after regression of cohort effects using limma’s removeBatchEffect demonstrated loss of separation by cohorts, consistent with appropriate adjustment in the DESeq2 covariate-based design formula (Supplementary Fig. 1B). [24] Two genes were significantly up-regulated in children with asthma: RPP38-DT (log2 fold change = 0.76; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.005) and NCAL1 (log2 fold change = 0.43; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.040). One gene was significantly down-regulated in asthma: RAB7B (log2 fold change = − 1.07; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.027) (Fig. 1). Notably, SCN5A (FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.448), ENSG00000270022 (FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.209), ENSG00000285646 (FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.098), and ENSG00000276216 (FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.095) lost significance in the combined dataset, indicating they may represent cohort-specific signals. To further confirm the robustness of our cohort adjustment, we applied ComBat-seq batch correction and repeated the differential expression analysis. This sensitivity analysis yielded highly concordant log2 fold-change estimates (Supplementary Fig. 1C) to our primary analysis (r = 0.918) with minor differences in the differentially expressed genes identified: RAB7B lost significance (log2 fold change = − 0.81; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.256) and one additional gene, ENSG00000279602 (log2 fold change = 1.10; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.034), was up-regulated in asthma. [25] Additionally, to account for potential residual age-related confounding, we included scaled sample age as an additional covariate in the DESeq2 design formula and repeated the differential expression analysis. Similarly, this age-adjusted sensitivity analysis also yielded highly concordant log2 fold-change estimates (Supplementary Fig. 1D) to our primary analysis (r = 0.846) with minor differences in the differentially expressed genes identified: RAB7B lost significance (log2 fold change = − 0.98; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.104), and two additional genes MTRNR2L12 (log2 fold change = − 1.44; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.009) and OTOAP1 (log2 fold change = − 1.93; FDR-adjusted p-value = 0.002) were down-regulated in asthma. Given our limited sample size and the potential for overparameterization and overfitting with increasing number of covariates, these differences must be interpreted with caution.

Differential expression across asthma severity

Pooled across the TopMed and the KidsFirst cohorts, the combined datasets included 29 patients with mild intermittent, 39 patients with mild persistent, 30 patients with moderate persistent, and 6 patients with severe persistent asthma, for a total of 104 samples (Table 2). Due to comparatively low sample size (n = 6), the severe persistent category was added to the moderate persistent category to create a ‘moderate/severe’ category to mitigate potential distortion of the linear model. The combined dataset showed no significant differences in age, sex distribution or BMI categorization across asthma severity groups (p > 0.150); however, a greater proportion of individuals identifying as Black or African American were classified into higher asthma severity categories (p = 0.007). PCA revealed no clear separation by asthma severity (Supplementary Fig. 2). A linear model was used to explore the relationship between gene expression and asthma severity, with severity modeled as a continuous covariate in the DESeq2 design formula. As the distribution of severity classification differed between cohorts, a covariate for cohort was included in the model. We found upregulation of five transcripts (CCL8, ACOD1, CXCL9, SLAMF1, EPN2), and downregulation of one transcript (ENSG00000288973) significantly associated with asthma severity (Fig. 2). Functional enrichment analysis with Enrichr revealed a pronounced overrepresentation of the IL8 Pfam domain (Pfam_Domains_2019; q-value < 0.001). [26–28] Co-expression profiling using ARCHS4 transcription-factor datasets further implicated three human transcription factors (ETV7, BATF2, and MSC, q-value for each = 0.004). Disease‐term enrichment via DisGeNET linked our signature most strongly to experimental lung inflammation (q-value = 0.033) and to allergic reaction (q-value = 0.033). [29, 30] Consistent with these disease associations, Gene Ontology analysis highlighted enrichment of several chemokine‐related biological processes: chemokine‐mediated signaling pathway (GO:0070098; q-value = 0.010), cellular response to chemokine (GO:1990869; q-value = 0.010), neutrophil chemotaxis (GO:0030593; q-value = 0.010), neutrophil migration (GO:1990266; q-value = 0.010), and granulocyte chemotaxis (GO:0071621; q-value = 0.010) (Fig. 3). As PBMC isolation excludes polymorphonuclear cells, these enrichments should be interpreted as chemokine-related transcriptional programs within circulating mononuclear cells rather than direct evidence of granulocyte transcription. Cellular deconvolution using CIBERSORTx did not identify meaningful differences in major PBMC cell proportions across asthma severity groups, suggesting that the observed pathway enrichments are not driven by large compositional shifts but instead reflect shared transcriptional programs across PBMC populations (Supplementary Fig. 3). [31] Similar enrichment of chemotaxis and migration pathways have been previously reported in severe asthma. [11].

Discussion

To our knowledge, the current study is among the largest PBMC RNA-sequencing study exploring the transcriptomic signature of asthma in children to date. By conducting bulk PBMC RNA-sequencing in 182 pediatric samples across two independent cohorts, we have identified transcriptomic signals of childhood asthma that encompass both well-established and novel loci. In contrast, prior studies have mostly focused on the inflammatory signature of asthma sub-types, including a recent study by Lee et al. profiling mRNA from PBMCs of 17 T2‐high and 11 non‐T2 children. [32] Similarly, Resztak et al. used single-cell RNA-sequencing data to explore the response to glucocorticoid administration in children and Lee et al. demonstrated that differentially expressed microRNAs are associated with downregulated expression of IgE in nonallergic childhood asthma. [33, 34] As opposed to these prior studies, where only modest gene-level differences were reported, our study revealed significant differential expression of a canonical asthma-related gene (RAB7B) as well as two novel loci (RPP38-DT, NCAL1). [35, 36] We note that RAB7B encodes a small GTPase protein that has been experimentally linked to the regulation of TLR4, a key immune receptor that plays a central role in asthma. [37, 38] Further, one of the three significant associations with asthma and two of the six significant associations with asthma severity are with non-coding genes, including the long non-coding RNA gene NCAL1. While models of long non-coding RNA’s contribution to disease are still being investigated, it is worth noting that these transcripts are being increasingly recognized as key regulators of disease, including asthma. [39, 40].

In our severity-stratified analysis, eight transcripts exhibited expression changes that tracked with worsening asthma: five immune‐related genes (CCL8, ACOD1, CXCL9, SLAMF1 and EPN2) were upregulated, while one uncharacterized transcript (ENSG00000288973) was downregulated. Given the limited sample size within each severity category, these findings are exploratory and require further validation. Enrichment of the IL8 Pfam domain is consistent with prior studies linking IL8 both to pediatric asthma incidence and severity. [41] These findings should be interpreted in the context of PBMC-based profiling. Enrichment of neutrophil and granulocyte chemotaxis pathways likely reflects chemokine signaling programs within mononuclear cells rather than granulocyte transcription. While deconvolution did not identify major differences in PBMC composition, this approach cannot resolve activation states or rare subpopulations. Cell-resolved or airway-based studies will be required to establish the cellular origin and mechanistic relevance of these signals.

Co-expression analysis identified ETV7 as a potential regulatory factor, which has been shown to act as a negative regulator of type I IFN signaling. [42] This is consistent with the known link between an impaired IFN-β response and pediatric asthma severity and may provide a first hint at the mechanistic drivers of this impaired response. [43, 44] However, upregulation of ACOD1, a key regulator of immunometabolism, runs contrary to the expected downregulation of type I IFN signaling. While the underlying cause remains unclear, a potential contributor might be exposure to environmental inflammatory stimuli as recent work by Schwab et al. reported elevated ACOD1 expression in response to environmental inflammatory stimuli, including endotoxins and organic dust exposure. [45] While environmental exposure data was not available in this study, future work incorporating such measurements could assess whether environmental factors contribute to ACOD1 expression differences in pediatric asthma severity.

Our findings highlight several blood-based transcripts, including RAB7B, that warrant further evaluation in longitudinal pediatric cohorts as potential early indicators of asthma. Non-coding RNAs like RPP38-DT and NCAL1 may also represent markers for disease risk, though their functional relevance remains to be determined. Similarly, baseline IFN-β responsiveness could be explored as a potential marker of asthma severity in future studies.

Key strengths of our study include our total sample size and inclusion of two independent pediatric cohorts. Limitations include reliance on PBMCs, which are more accessible, but may not fully capture asthma-associated transcriptomic signals observed in airway cells and the absence of detailed clinical phenotyping, including lung function measures, exacerbation history, and treatment exposure at the time of sampling. Asthma severity was defined using ICD-based classifications, which do not capture disease activity, control, or treatment response. Accordingly, associations between gene expression and severity should be interpreted as reflecting differences in administrative severity categories rather than physiologic impairment or clinical trajectory. The lack of medication data, including corticosteroid use, further limits interpretation, as treatment effects on immune gene expression cannot be separated from disease-related signals. In addition, differences in baseline demographic characteristics between cases and controls, limited sample size within asthma severity categories, and other unmeasured factors, including genetic ancestry and environmental exposures, may contribute to residual confounding. Finally, while our PCA and stratified analysis supported that age was not a dominant driver of transcriptomic variability in this dataset, better powered future studies should consider incorporating sample age to explore this further.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study highlights both developmental regulators and novel non-coding RNAs as candidate markers of pediatric asthma and asthma severity. Next steps are aimed at independent validation of these candidates and longitudinal assessment of non-coding RNAs in at-risk infants. With rigorous diagnostic validation and integration with detailed clinical phenotyping, these findings may support future efforts towards molecular characterization and stratification of pediatric asthma.
