IRF7 Modulates Inflammatory Pain Through Upregulating IFNβ in Mice Trigeminal Ganglion
Authors: Lijia Mai, Xiaoxia Ma, Hui Yang, Shilin Jia, Yifan He, Hongwen He, Wenguo Fan, Fang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite the high global burden of chronic pain, current therapeutic options remain limited. The transcription factor IRF7, primarily known for its role in immunity, has emerged as a potential regulator of nociception. However, a significant knowledge gap exists regarding its peripheral functions and mechanisms in orofacial pain. This study investigates the hypothesis that IRF7 and its downstream effector IFNβ act within the trigeminal ganglion (TG) to modulate orofacial pain pathways. The expression levels of IRF7 and IFNβ in the TG were evaluated in a mouse model of orofacial inflammatory pain. IRF7 was knocked down in the TG using AAV-mediated shRNA delivery. The functional interaction between IRF7 and IFNβ was tested through applying exogenous IFNβ, blocking its receptor, and performing a key rescue experiment to test if IFNβ reconstitution could reverse the effects of IRF7 loss. Inflammatory pain led to a significant upregulation of IRF7 and IFNβ in the TG neuron. IRF7 knockdown alleviated mechanical allodynia, reduced spinal neuronal activation, and suppressed pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6 and NLRP3). Exogenous IFNβ exacerbated pain hypersensitivity and the nociceptive effects was attenuated by IFNAR antagonism. IFNβ rescued the pain modulation deficits resulting from IRF7 knockdown. Supplementing exogenous IFNβ rescued the pain modulation deficits resulting from IRF7 knockdown in an inflammatory pain condition. Our findings demonstrate that the peripheral IRF7–IFNβ axis drives inflammatory pain pathogenesis, identifying a novel therapeutic target for chronic pain management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02483-w.Keywords: Orofacial pain, Interferon regulatory factor 7, Interferon Beta, Trigeminal ganglion, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Chronic pain is a major global health issue, affecting more than 30% of the adult population in the world, and posing significant individual, societal, and economic burdens [1, 2]. Despite progress in understanding the mechanisms underlying pain, gaps remain in our knowledge of chronic pain – including inflammatory pain – resulting from tissue injury, infection, or immune disorders. Currently available drugs for this type of pain are often ineffective and carry significant adverse effects [3]. The development of chronic pain is correlated with neuroinflammation, manifested as increased leukocytes infiltration, glial activation and cytokine expression within the nervous system [4]. However, the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying inflammatory pain remain unclear, highlighting the need for improved analgesic strategies. Given the central side effects of current treatments, exploring peripheral sensitization mechanisms is crucial for developing targeted therapies that may offer a more effective approach to chronic pain management.

The interferon regulatory factors (IRF) family plays pivotal roles in host defense mechanisms, immune cell differentiation, and oncogenesis. Initially cloned in 1997, the Irf7 gene has emerged as a critical regulator of type I interferon (IFNα/β) responses, which are essential for both innate and adaptive immunity [5, 6]. Receptors for IFNβ (IFNAR1/2) extensively expressed throughout the sensory nervous system and have been implicated in the regulation of nociceptive process [7, 8]. Emerging evidence suggests the contribution of neuroimmune interactions in pain modulation [9], raising the question of whether IRF7, in addition to its established role in immunity, may also contribute to pain development.

In this study, we characterized transcriptomic changes in the trigeminal ganglion (TG), the primary sensory relay for orofacial region, in a complete Freund’s adjuvant (CFA)-induced rodent orofacial pain model. We identified IRF7 as one of the most upregulated genes, with its expression along with IFNβ signaling increasing following CFA injection. The role of IRF7 and IFNβ in pain was confirmed by TG-specific shRNA knockdown and pharmacological interventions. We found that IRF7 promoted inflammatory pain through IFNβ, using co-immunoprecipitation and functional rescue experiments. We further demonstrated that the IRF7–IFNβ axis mechanistically linked trigeminal neuroinflammation and neuronal sensitization. In summary, our study reveals that the IRF7–IFNβ axis may play a critical role in inflammatory pain, providing a mechanistic link for between neuro-immune crosstalk and chronic pain.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Animal Experimental Center of the East Campus of Sun Yat-sen University (Guangzhou, China) and used in this study. The animals were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility under controlled conditions (25 ± 1 °C) with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and provided free access to food and water. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the International Association for the Study of Pain and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Sun Yat-sen University [Approval No. SYSU-IACUC-2024-001161].

Drug Administration

To induce inflammatory pain in mice, 20 µL of CFA (Sigma Aldrich) or 0.9% NaCl solution was injected into the right whisker pad of each mouse. Recombinant mouse IFNβ (8234-MB-010/CF, R&D Systems) was dissolved in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and administered intraperitoneally (i.p.) at a final concentration of 104 or 105 U, in a volume of 0.25 mL per injection, at 7 days post CFA injection (dpi). To assess sustained effects, IFNβ (10⁴ U) was administered i.p. in 0.25 mL daily from 7 to 10 dpi. The direct pronociceptive effect of IFNβ was assessed via whisker pad injection (300 U/25 µl). The IFNAR1 neutralizing antibody MAR1 or mouse IgG (HY-P99137 and HY-P99977 purchased from MedChem Express LLC) was delivered intra-ganglionically on 3 dpi, at a dose of 1 µL(10 µg/1 µL). To modulate gene expression in the TG, we utilized adeno-associated viral (AAV) to mediate shRNA transduction into TG. AAV-U6-shRNA-IRF7-CMV-EGFP-SV40- polyA and its control vertor AAV-shRNA-scramble-GFP were designed and manufactured by BrainVTA Co., Ltd. The virus was administered via intra‑ganglionic injection 4 weeks before model induction, in a 1 µL bolus at a titer of 5.0 × 10¹² vg/mL. For in vitro experiments, cells were treated with 1 mg/mL MAR1 or IgG overnight and then stimulated with 100 U IFNβ for 2 h.

Animal Experimental Design

This study aimed to delineate the role of the IRF7-IFNβ axis in orofacial inflammatory pain. Mice received a unilateral right whisker pad injection of CFA to establish an inflammatory pain model, with saline-injected mice serving as controls. To dissect the pathway, four independent intervention series were conducted: a) Direct whisker pad injection of IFNβ (300 U/25 µl) or PBS to reveal local IFNβ effect. b): Daily i.p. injection of IFNβ or PBS, starting at 5 dpi to reveal systemic effect of IFNβ. c) Intra-ganglionic microinjection of the IFNAR1 neutralizing antibody MAR1 or control IgG at 3 dpi to investigate local blockade IFNβ signaling. d) Intra-ganglionic injection of Irf7 shRNA or a control vector 4 weeks prior to CFA induction. To test if IFNβ is the key downstream mediator of IRF7, a separate cohort of mice with TG-specific Irf7 knockdown received daily i.p. injections of either IFNβ or PBS, starting at 5 dpi. A control group receiving scrambled shRNA plus i.p. IFNβ was included.

Behavioral Assays

Behavioral tests were performed at indicated time points. Mechanical allodynia of the right whisker pad was assessed using von Frey filaments (North Coast) and the up-and-down method, as previously described [10]. Briefly, animals were acclimated for 30 min before testing. A series of von Frey monofilaments (0.008, 0.02, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, 0.6, 1, 1.4, 2 and 4 g) were applied perpendicularly to the skin surface for 5 s with a 15-second interstimulus interval. A vigorous head withdrawal or flinching was recorded as a positive response and each stimulus was applied 5 times. If positive (or negative) responses persisted through the entire stimulus set, a cut-off value of 4 g (or 0.008 g) was assigned accordingly. The mechanical allodynia test was conducted in a blinded manner.

Stereotaxic Injection

To investigate the effects of genes or receptors at the TG level, the AAV or receptor antagonist was injected into the right TG as previously described [11, 12]. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with 40 mg/kg pentobarbital sodium and secured on a robot stereotaxic microinjection apparatus (Neurostar). A longitudinal skin incision was made along the midline to expose the skull. After the right TG was accurately located based on the stereotaxic atlas [13], a small cranial opening was drilled to allow access (-1.5 ± 0.05 mm posterior to bregma; 1.3 ± 0.05 mm lateral to the midline). A 33-gauge micro‑syringe (Hamilton, US) was inserted ventrally through the cranial opening, with a final depth of 6.3 ± 0.10 mm below the skull surface. The virus or drugs was injected at a controlled speed of 0.2 µl/min and the needle was held for 10 min before withdrawal. The sham surgery control group received identical procedures except for the TG injection.

RNA Extraction and Real‑time Quantitative PCR

TG tissues were harvested from anesthetized mice after transcardial perfusion with PBS. Total RNA extracted using the Trizol reagent (R1105, GBCBIO) and then RNA (1 µg) was reversely transcribed into cDNA using a commercial PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix Kit (YEASEN Biotechnology), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green I Master Mix (YEASEN Biotechnology) on a Roche real-time PCR system. The specific primers used for detecting mRNA are listed in Supplemental Table 1. The relative mRNA expression of target genes was normalized to β-actin (Actb) and calculated by the ΔΔCT method with data expressed as fold-change from control.

RNA-Sequencing

High-throughput sequencing was performed on TGs from mice injected with either CFA or saline at 3 dpi. After transcardial perfusion with PBS, TGs were harvested, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then processed for RNA sequencing. Library preparation for Illumina sequencing was conducted using KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Illumina). Stranded-mRNA was performed sequenced on Illumina Platform NovaSeq 6000. Raw reads were processed using SolexaPipeline and aligned to the mouse reference genome utilizing Hisat2. Gene expression levels were quantified using StringTie and expressed as Fragments Per Kilobase of exon model per Million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed mRNAs were identified using Ballgown R package. The criterion for statistical significance was P ≤ 0.05, log2FC ≥ 0.5.

Single-cell data

Single-cell sequencing data on mouse TG from our previously published work [14] was utilized to identify gene distribution. The data set is available through the NCBI GEO accession number GSE213105. The single-cell RNA-seq data were analyzed using the Seurat package (version 2.2.1) for clustering and visualization. All the analysis was performed using R version 4.2.3.

Western Blotting and Immunoprecipitation

Mice were anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital (40 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused with PBS. Subsequently, the TG and spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (SpVc, extending from lower pons to medulla) were harvested, lysed, and centrifuged at 14,000 g for 20 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (CW0014S, Cwbio). Samples containing 20–30 µg of protein were separated by 4–12% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore). After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were washed and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the indicated primary antibodies (information is provided in Supplemental Table 2). Signals were detected using ECL Chemiluminescent Substrate (Abbkine) and visualized with Bio-Rad ChemiDoc Touch. Immunoblot images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

For immunoprecipitation, TG tissues were collected at 3 dpi as described above. Following homogenization in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors, lysates were centrifuged to obtain the protein supernatant. The supernatant was incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation using either the target-specific antibody for IP or a corresponding isotype IgG control. The next day, protein A/G magnetic beads (Beyotime) were added to each sample and incubated for 1 h. After extensive washing with lysis buffer, the immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted and subjected to immunoblot analysis.

ELISA

Blood was collected via cardiac puncture in deeply anesthetized mice at designated time points. Serum was obtained after clotting at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. To measure local, tissue-specific level of IFNβ and minimize potential blood contamination in tissues, mice were then perfused transcardially with PBS. TGs were harvested, homogenized in 0.3 mL of PBS and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. IFNβ levels in serum and TG were quantified using ELISA kits (E-EL-M0033, Elabscience Biotechnology), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The final absorbance at 405 nm was detected by a Synergy-H1 microplate reader (Biotek).

Immunostaining

For immunofluorescence, animals were deeply anesthetized and subjected to transcardial perfusion-fixation with PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde. The TG and SpVc were dissected, post-fixed for 12 h, cryopreserved in 30% sucrose and embedded in OCT compound (Sakura). Tissue sections were prepared as free-floating slices (12 μm for TG and 40 μm for SpVc), blocked with a solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100 and 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature, and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Supplemental Table 2) in various combinations. Then, the slices were incubated with a mixture of corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Images were taken using a laser confocal microscope (Olympus FluoView 3000).

To ensure objectivity and reproducibility, immunofluorescence co-localization analysis followed a strict pipeline: (1) application of a uniform, algorithm-determined threshold to all images; (2) immunoreactivity within the ipsilateral SpVc (laminae I/II) and maxillary TG was quantified in a blinded manner using five randomly placed, non-overlapping square region of interests (ROIs, 400 × 400 pixels each) per image; and (3) quantification using the Cell Counter plugin to determine the number of positive cells within each ROI, with simultaneous measurement of the mean gray value. Venn diagrams depicting cellular co-localization were generated using BioVenn [15], based on the calculated proportion of dual red/green-positive cells per image. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SD, pooled from at least 15 images across 3–5 animals per group.

For immunohistochemistry, paraffin-embedded 4-µm-thick sections were deparaffinized and subjected to antigen retrieval using a microwave oven. Sections were washed with PBS, incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and blocked with 5% goat serum containing 0.3% Triton X-100. Primary antibodies against IRF7 or IFNβ were applied overnight at 4 °C. Thereafter, the sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 30 min and subsequently with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) working solution for 1–3 min. Finally, the sections were stained with hematoxylin prior to be dehydrated, and mounted with cover slips. Images were captured on digital slide scanner (Aperio AT2, Leica) and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Primary cell Culture of Mouse TG Neurons

For primary TG neuron culture, TGs were dissected from 3–5-week-old anesthetized mice, placed in ice-cold complete medium (DMEM/F12, 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin). The tissues were then minced into small pieces and subjected to enzymatic digestion in 2 mg/mL papain solution (Worthington) at 37 °C for 45 min. The dissociated cells were collected and resuspended in DMEM/F12 complete medium. Cells were seeded onto plates or coverslips pre-coated with 0.1 mg/mL poly-L-lysine at a density of 6 × 105/mL and incubated for 4 h. The medium was replaced by serum free Neurobasal cultured medium (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 2% B27 (Thermo Fisher), 1% GlutaMaxTM, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Half of the medium was changed every 2 days. Cells were used for experiments after cultured for 7 days. Following treatments, cells were rinsed with chilled PBS buffer, harvested in Trizol reagent for PCR arrays. The coverslips were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for immunofluorescence.

Detection of Intracellular Calcium Levels in TG Neurons

Fluo-4 AM probe was utilized to assess intracellular calcium dynamics in TG neurons and their sensitization to varying concentrations of IFNβ. Primary TG neurons at a density of 8 × 10⁵ cells/mL were seeded onto black 96-well plates at 100 µL per well. On day 7, cells were stimulated with 30, 100, 300 or 600 U of IFNβ for 2 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, the medium was removed, and cells were incubated with 100 µL of 2 µM Fluo-4 AM (prepared in PBS) for 30 min under light-protected conditions. Fluorescence intensity was measured every 3 min for 60 min using a microplate reader (Ex/Em = 490/525 nm) at 37 °C. Relative fluorescence units (RFU) were calculated by subtracting background values, and mean RFUs were plotted over time using GraphPad Prism 8.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Statistical differences were determined using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups, or one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc tests for multi-group comparisons. P value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The Expression of IRF7 and IFNβ Signal was Increased in the Inflamed TG in a CFA-Induced pain Model

The inflammatory pain model using CFA was established as described previously [16]. Briefly, a single injection of CFA elicited significant mechanical allodynia starting from day 1 and persisted for up to 2 weeks (Fig. 1A, B). The right whisker pad displayed obvious signs of tissue swelling and edema, confirming the development of inflammation (data not shown).

To reveal the transcriptional profiles of the TG during orofacial pain, we performed high-throughput RNA sequencing on TG samples from mice injected with CFA or saline at 3 dpi. A total of 187 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the CFA- and saline-injected group (Fig. 1C; P ≤ 0.05 and log2FC ≥ 0.5). Transcriptional analysis revealed that upregulation of ion channels (Kcnt1, Slc1a2, Slc7a10), along with increased expression of satellite glial cells (SGCs) markers (Fabp7 and Gfap) and macrophage markers (Mac1), as well as cytokine production (Ccl12 and Zbp1). These findings suggest that chronic inflammatory pain is closely associated with ongoing neuroinflammation. Additionally, we detected a significant increase in the expression of a panel of interferon stimulated genes (including Bst2, Ifit1, Ifit3, Irf7, Isg15, Oasl2, Oas2, Zbp1, Rtp4), highly analogous to the transcription profiles from persistent pain models as previously reported [17] (Fig. 1D). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the DEGs were enriched in IFNβ and cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (Fig. 1E). Cytoscape analysis identified 10 hub genes with higher potential of being drug targets. These 10 genes were: Irf7, Usp18, Rtp4, Ifit3, Ifit1, Isg15, Ifi47, Oasl2, Zbp1 and Gbp3 (Fig. 1F), ranged by priority.

Since the DEGs were highly enriched in IFNβ response, we detected both the transcript and protein levels over time. CFA injection triggered a rapid induction of IFNβ in the TG, detectable as early as 6 h, which further increased at day 1 before returning to baseline by day 3. In contrast, serum IFNβ levels showed no significant change at any tested time point (Fig. 2A). qPCR and western blot analysis revealed a coincident induction of IFNβ transcription and translation in the early stages of pain development. Notably, CFA injection led to a sustained increase in Irf7 expression and an early increase in the TG, which is required to sustain IFNβ gene expression (Fig. 2B). However, we observed only a transient peak in IRF7 protein, possibly serving as a feedback mechanism to avoid sustained or excessive signaling (Fig. 2C). CFA also induced upregulation of the upstream factors Irf3, Sting1 and Tlr4, mirroring the same time course of IFNβ mRNA expression, but had minimal effect on Tlr3 expression (Fig. 2D).

Together, our findings reveal that the IRF7 and IFNβ is rapidly and transiently activated during the early phase of inflammatory pain, while its sustained transcriptional activity maintains peripheral sensitization.

IRF7 and IFNβ Signal was Primarily Expressed by Neurons in the TG

To determine the localization of the IRF7 and IFNβ in the TG, we performed double immunofluorescence staining. Tissue sections were co-stained with antibodies against IRF7, IFNβ, and specific cellular markers. IRF7 was predominantly co-localized with NeuN that marks neuron (84.75%), with minimal co-staining observed in CD68+ (1.73%) that marks macrophage or GS+ (4.15%) that marks SGC in the TG (Fig. 3A). Additionally, co-expression analysis revealed that IRF7 was predominantly expressed in CGRP-positive (36.91%) and neurofilament heavy chain (NF)-positive (14.02%) neuronal subpopulations, with minimal co-localization observed with P2RX3 (0.23%) (Fig. 3B). Similarly, IFNβ was found to co-localize with NeuN-positive (73.24%), CGRP-positive (39.07%), and NF-positive (21.02%) neurons (Fig. 3C, D). To identify the effector cell of IFNβ in the TG, double immunofluorescence staining was performed for IFNAR1. Co-localization analysis indicated that IFNAR1 was primarily expressed in NeuN-positive neurons (76.84%), with limited expression in CD68-positive macrophages (3.0%) and GS-positive SGCs (3.29%) (Fig. 3E).

To further investigate the expression of IRF7 and IFNβ signal across different cell types in the TG, transcriptional analysis of naïve mice TG was performed using a publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing dataset. We identified 11 clusters and 7 main neuronal subtypes within TG, based on the highly variable genes of each cluster (Fig. 3F). The single cell RNA sequencing data revealed that Irf7 is predominantly expressed in neuron expressing Nefh that encode NF (95.12%) and Calca that encode CGRP (97.10%), rather than in neurons expressing P2rx3, which is consistent with our immunostaining results (Fig. 3F, G). Additionally, Ifnar1 and Ifnar2 were enriched across multiple clusters and were primarily expressed in neurons, as well as in endothelial cells, SGCs and immune cells within the TG (Fig. 3H).

Taken together, these findings suggest that IRF7 and IFNβ signal is primarily active in specific subsets of TG neurons, particularly those expressing NF and CGRP. Receptors for IFNβ are predominantly expressed by neurons, yet also detectable in non-neuronal cells within the TG, suggesting its primary role in neuronal function alongside potential immune modulation.

The activation of IRF7 and IFNβ was required to sustain mechanical allodynia after CFA treatment

To functionally validate the role of peripheral IRF7 in pain modulation, we stably silenced IRF7 expression in the TG, using AAV-mediated shRNA transduction. The efficiency of AAV carrying Irf7 shRNA was validated in 293T cells (Data not shown). Immunoblot analysis revealed that mice intra-ganglionically injected with Irf7 shRNA (sh-Irf7) exhibited a significant reduction (61.24%) in IRF7 expression in the TG compared with mice injected with scramble shRNA (sh-Scr) (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence staining also revealed a robust expression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP), confirming the successful transduction of the AAV-shRNA into the TG (Fig. 4B). Mice injected with Irf7 shRNA showed a significant reduction in IRF7 level in the TG, compared to those received scramble shRNA or sham surgery control (Fig. 4B). We measured the mechanical pain threshold with von Frey filaments before (baseline) and after CFA injection. Baseline mechanical sensitivity did not differ among groups. However, following CFA injection, sh-Irf7 mice exhibited enhanced withdrawal thresholds compared to the sh-Scr controls (Fig. 4C). No statistical difference was detected between the sh-Scr and sham groups at this stage. Consistently, c-Fos expression in the SpVc was significantly reduced in the sh-Irf7 group than in controls, suggesting attenuated neuronal activation (Fig. 4D).

To determine the role of IFNβ in pain modulation, mice received daily i.p. injections of recombinant protein beginning at 7 dpi, and pain responses were subsequently assessed (Fig. 5A). Administration of IFNβ (10⁴ or 10⁵ U) significantly enhanced mechanical hypersensitivity in CFA-injected mice in a dose-independent manner. However, this pro-nociceptive effect lasted no more than 12 h (Fig. 5B). Daily IFNβ treatment produced a sustained reduction in mechanical withdrawal thresholds that persisted for over 24 h compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 5C). To determine the TG-specific effect of IFNβ in mechanical allodynia, we performed intra-ganglionic injection of the IFNAR1 antagonist (MAR1). This local blockadesignificantly increased mechanical withdrawal thresholds compared to IgG control injection (Fig. 5D-E). Additionally, the direct nociceptive effect of IFNβ was tested by local administration of exogenous IFNβ (300 U/25 µl) into the whisker pad. A single dose of IFNβ induced rapid mechanical allodynia, which peaked at 3 h and maintained for at least 24 h (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, IFNβ treatment significantly increased the number of c-Fos–positive cells in the SpVc, whereas treatment with the IFNAR1-neutralizing antibody MAR1 at 3dpi, markedly reduced it (Fig. 5G-J). These results suggest that IRF7 and IFNβ play a critical role in maintaining nociceptive responses in the CFA-induced inflammatory pain model.

IRF7 Promotes Mechanical Allodynia Through Modulating IFNβ Expression

Previous works have demonstrated that IRF7 is a key positive regulator of IFNβ gene transcription during immune defense [6]. The IRF7 binding sites were identified within the promoter region of IFNB1, which is located from − 1237 to -1230 bp upstream of the IFNB1 transcriptional start sites (Fig. 6A). Among the top-ranked motifs, the canonical E-box sequence GGATAATGAAAAT, which is associated with IFNB1 binding in fibroblasts, was highly enriched in human datasets (Fig. 6B). Immunohistochemical staining and co-localization analysis displayed that IFNβ and IRF7 could be expressed by the same cells in the TG (Fig. 6C-D). Additionally, immunoblot result showed that knockdown of IRF7 in the TG via shRNA significantly reduced IFNβ expression (Fig. 4A), demonstrating that IRF7 is required for maintaining IFNβ levels in sensory ganglia. To confirm their interaction under inflammatory conditions, we performed co-immunoprecipitation on TG tissues at 3 dpi. Immunoprecipitation using an anti-IRF7 antibody, but not control IgG, specifically co-precipitated endogenous IFNβ (Fig. 6E). To establish the TG-specific role of IRF7, we performed a rescue experiment by supplementing exogenous IFNβ to the mice with IRF7 knockdown. Administration of exogenous IFNβ significantly reversed the analgesia observed in sh-Irf7 mice compared to the mice injected with PBS. In addition, in the presence of exogenous IFNβ, sh-Irf7 mice exhibited a higher mechanical withdrawal threshold than the sh-Scr controls (Fig. 6F). IFNβ treatment markedly increased the number of c-Fos-positive cells in the SpVc, compared to IRF7-knockdown mice receiving PBS (Fig. 6G). These findings indicates that IRF7 is an essential upstream mediator of IFNβ signaling in the TG for pain regulation.

IRF7-IFNβ Promote Mechanical Allodynia Through Modulating TG Neuronal Sensitization and Inflammation

Given that IFNARs were predominantly localized on TG neurons, we sought to assess whether IFNβ plays a critical role in the hyperexcitability of TG neurons. The intracellular calcium dynamics were assessed by measuring fluorescence intensity following stimulated with IFNβ. The results showed that neurons treated with different concentration of IFNβ displayed increased intracellular calcium level (Fig. 7A). We found that IFNβ activates TG neurons, as evidenced by the upregulation of P2rx3 and Tac1 at a dose of 100 U (but not 300 U). This effect was specifically mediated by IFNAR1, as it was blocked by the neutralizing antibody MAR1 (Fig. 7B). Consistent with this, IFNβ stimulation also increased the expression of the neuropeptide substance P (ecoded by Tac1), as demonstrated by immunofluorescence (Fig. 7C).

We next asked whether IFNβ contributes to TG inflammation—a key driver of neuronal sensitization and chronic pain [4]. Inflammatory cell infiltration and the expression levels of pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6 and NLRP3) within the TG were analyzed to assess the local inflammatory response. Immunofluorescence staining revealed IFNβ treatment significantly increased expression of GFAP and CD86, indicating the activation of satellite glial cells and M1 macrophages (Fig. 7D-E). Immunoblot analysis revealed that the expression of NLRP3 was significantly upregulated following IFNβ injection (Fig. 7F). The expression of NLRP3 and IL-6 was significantly reduced by inhibition of IFNAR using MAR1, compared the control group (Fig. 7G). IRF7 knockdown in the TG resulted in a significant reduction in the expression of IRF7, IFNβ, NLRP3 and IL-6 (Fig. 7G). IL-6 expression did not differ significantly between IFNβ or PBS -treated groups.

Collectively, our results highlight the pivotal role of IRF7-IFNβ signal in neuronal sensitization. This may attribute to the establishment of a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, facilitating chronic pain conditions.

Discussion

This study investigated the peripheral mechanism by which IRF7 modulates inflammatory pain in mice. Our results demonstrate that IRF7 regulates inflammatory pain by upregulating IFNβ expression. In addition, IRF7-IFNβ signal was found to contribute to neuronal sensitization and inflammation within the TG (Fig. 8).

Schematic of the effect of IRF7-IFNβ signal in the TG during orofacial inflammatory pain. Our work demonstrates a pro-nociceptive effect of IRF7 in the TG using AAV-mediated shRNA transduction. The effect of IRF7 on pain modulation may depend on IFNβ, which induces TG neuroinflammation and pro-inflammatory cytokines production through autocrine or paracrine signaling

In this study, transcriptomic profiles of TG, a cranial analog of the dorsal root ganglia that innervating the orofacial region, were analyzed in a well-characterized rodent orofacial inflammatory pain model. Notably, Irf7 was identified as one of the most significantly upregulated genes. Then, the pivotal role of IRF7 in this pain model was further confirmed through RNA interference approaches, with a particular focus on its peripheral role in nociception (Fig. 4). As a transcriptional factor, IRF7 is known to play key roles in IFN-mediated immune responses, regulation of cell differentiation, oncogenesis, apoptosis, infectious diseases and certain autoimmune disorders [18]. Previous studies have reported IRF7 expression in both neurons and astrocytes within the dorsal horn of the spinal cord [19]. However, our findings indicate that IRF7 is exclusively expressed in neurons within the TG. A recent study demonstrated that IRF7 overexpression in the spinal cord can alleviate CFA-induced inflammatory pain by suppressing the NF-κB/p65 signaling pathway and reducing inflammatory cytokine expression in rats [19]. In contrast, our results suggest a hyperalgesic role of IRF7 in the peripheral nervous system. We propose that this discrepancy may stem from distinct expression patterns and functions of IRF7 in the peripheral versus central nervous system. To determine whether TG-specific knockdown of IRF7 attenuated nociception, we evaluated mechanical allodynia and c-Fos expression in the SpVc. The results showed that Irf7 knockdown markedly reduced mechanical allodynia and attenuated c-Fos expression in the SpVc, suggesting a critical role of TG-IRF7 in pain processing. However, IRF7 knockdown alone did not induce mechanical hypersensitivity, as withdrawal thresholds did not differ significantly between the sh-Irf7 and sh-Scr groups prior to CFA administration.

Previously study reported that IRF7 is a critical regulator of IFN-α/β gene expression during immune responses [6]. In our study, GO enrichment analysis of upregulated DEGs revealed a prominent enrichment for the IFNβ response pathway in the TG during inflammatory pain. PCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation in the mRNA levels of STING and TLR4. We also detected that sustained elevation of IRF7 mRNA from 1 to 7 day alongside the transient peak of IFNβ protein. A conserved IRF7 binding motif within the promoter region of IFNB1 was identified. As key upstream sensors, TLR4 and STING activation can trigger IFNβ synthesis [20]. Based on these, we hypothesize that IRF7, acting as their common downstream mediator [21, 22], may play a critical integrator for regulating IFNβ expression in the TG within this signaling axis during orofacial inflammatory pain. The IRF7-IFNβ pathway initially supported by immunohistochemical co-localization and functionally corroborated by the reduction in IFNβ levels following IRF7 knockdown. The functional relationship was further confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation, which verified their direct physical interaction in the TG during inflammatory pain. The rescue experiments provided additional evidence that IRF7 contributes to orofacial inflammatory pain by regulating IFNβ expression. These findings establish the IRF7-IFNβ axis as a key pathway in peripheral nociceptive sensitization.

To specifically quantify the IFNβ level within the tissues, we perform transcardially perfused with PBS on mice prior to TG collection. This step standardizes tissue samples by removing a major variable source of circulating factors, ensuring that ELISA and western blotting measurements reflect local TG inflammation. However, the observed temporal disconnects between IRF7 transcription and IFNβ protein levels may reflect a tightly regulated, dynamic inflammatory response. IRF7-IFNβ may function as a critical initiator in the TG, which may be transiently elevated to launch downstream effects. Thereafter, protein levels are likely stabilized via negative feedback or rapid turnover to prevent signal overamplification and maintain homeostatic control. This pattern is consistent with the known short half-life and rapid secretion of many inflammatory mediators, which are subject to stringent post-transcriptional control and active clearance to prevent excessive inflammation [23, 24].

Although the IRF7-IFNβ pathway is well characterized in immune responses and cancer immunology [6, 25, 26], its potential role in nociception is poorly understood. In addition, the role of IFNβ in pain is highly context-dependent, with opposing effects observed based on the site of action. For instance, intrathecal application alleviates neuropathic and inflammatory pain [27–29], whereas peripheral delivery (e.g., intraplantar) promotes mechanical hypersensitivity [8]. Similarly, systemic IFNβ neutralization can reverse pain [30], while local blockade may be ineffective in certain models [31]. Clinically, systemic IFNβ therapy is associated with pain-related side effects [32]. These varying contribution of IFNβ to pain may be attributed to differences in drug dosages, administration methods or the distinct distribution and biological functions of IFNβ. In our study, to investigate the effect of IFNβ in pain, we selected i.p. injection of IFNβ based on previous works [7, 33], which provides reliable systemic delivery with minimal surgical intervention. Consistently, we found that i.p. delivery of IFNβ promote mechanical allodynia, while TG-specific inhibition of IFNβ receptor alleviate pain hypersensitivity. This is accompanied by increased c-Fos expression in the SpVc, indicating central sensitization at the level of the spinal trigeminal nucleus. Additionally, local administration of IFNβ (300 U/20 µl) into the whisker pad was sufficient to induce mechanical allodynia. Calcium imaging experiments revealed that IFNβ can directly activate neurons. These findings support that peripheral IFNβ signaling plays a necessary and sufficient role in driving orofacial mechanical hypersensitivity.

Numerous studies have provided compelling evidence that the persistence of orofacial pain is largely driven by neuroinflammation [34]. This process is manifested as leukocyte infiltration, glial cell activation, and the release of pro-inflammatory mediators [4]. IRF7, a known facilitator of inflammatory responses, was implicated in facilitating M1 macrophage polarization [35, 36], and modulating inflammasome components like NLRP3 and IL1β in neuronal contexts [37]. Previous works have linked IFNβ to astrocyte and microglial activation and cytokine release [38, 39]. Our study demonstrates that IRF7 knockdown or IFNAR blockade is sufficient to suppress inflammatory factor expression (IL-6, NLRP3 [40, 41]) in the TG, during CFA-induced inflammation. This indicates that IRF7 likely orchestrates neuroinflammation primarily through the canonical IFNβ-IFNAR signaling pathway.

Future studies should elucidate the transcriptional profile of the TG in Irf7-deficient mice to further elucidate the mechanistic pathways involved in IRF7-mediated peripheral sensitization. Additionally, some interferon stimulated genes may also contribute to the hyperalgesic and immunomodulatory effects of IFNβ, potentially serving as compensatory mechanisms following an inflammatory response. While this study primarily focused on the role of IRF7 in the TG, further investigations are necessary to determine whether similar mechanisms operate in other sensory ganglion or within the central nervous system.

In summary, this study demonstrates a clear association between IRF7-IFNβ signal and inflammatory pain through a range of experimental approaches. This not only deepens our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying inflammatory pain, but also highlights the significance of transcription factors within the pain regulatory network. Additionally, the role of IRF7 and its regulatory mechanism mediated by IFNβ introduces a novel and crucial perspective in this field.
