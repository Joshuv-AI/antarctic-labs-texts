The thickness of the retinal nerve fiber layer, macula, and ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer in people with drug‐resistant epilepsy
Authors: Yujie Chen, Weixi Xiong, Lu Lu, Xintong Wu, Le Cao, Jiani Chen, Yingfeng Xiao, Josemir W Sander, Bo Wu, Dong Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveUsing Optical coherence tomography (OCT), we evaluated the association between peripapillary retinal nerve fiber, macular thickness, macular ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer, and drug resistance.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional study, we recruited people diagnosed with epilepsy and healthy controls. People with epilepsy were further stratified as drug‐resistant or non‐drug‐resistant based on their response to anti‐seizure medications. OCT measurements were conducted, and findings in right eye were analyzed.ResultsFifty‐one drug‐resistant participants, 37 non‐drug‐resistant, and 45 controls were enrolled. The average peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer, ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer, and macular thickness were thinner in the epilepsy groups than in controls. The drug‐resistant group had significantly lower average ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer thickness (p = 0.004) and a higher proportion of abnormal/borderline GC/IPL thickness (p = 5.40E‐04) than the non‐drug‐resistant group. Nevertheless, no significant differences were seen between the average thickness of peripapillary retinal nerve fiber and macular thickness. The temporal sectors of these three parameters were also significantly thinner in the drug‐resistant group than in the non‐drug‐resistant. In a multivariate regression model, drug resistance was an independent predictor of reduced ganglion cell‐inner plexiform thickness (Odds ratios OR = 10.25, 95% CI 2.82 to 37.28). Increased seizure frequency (r = −0.23, p = 0.039) and a higher number of anti‐seizure medications ever used (r = −0.27, p = 0.013) were negatively associated with ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer thickness.SignificanceIndividuals with drug‐resistant epilepsy had a consistent reduction in average ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer thickness and the temporal sector of peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer and macular thickness. This suggests that ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer thickness could potentially serve as an indicator of the burden of drug resistance, as it correlated with reduced thickness in individuals having more frequent seizures and greater exposure to ASMs.Plain Language SummaryIn our study, we used a special tool called OCT to measure how thick the retina is in people with epilepsy and in healthy control. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological conditions. Anti‐seizure medication (ASM) is the mainstay treatment. Despite appropriate treatment, up to one‐third of people with epilepsy become drug‐resistant (DRE), as defined by failure to control seizures with at least two appropriate ASM regimens. 1 There is no objective, supplementary, and epilepsy‐replicable metric for assessing the biological aspects of drug resistance or severity. The retina offers a unique and easily accessible insight into the brain to obtain information about disease progression, as it shares anatomical, embryological, and physiological similarities with the brain. 2

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) allows for quantification of the thickness of the retina and is widely used in neuro‐ophthalmology. OCT can assess the retrograde trans‐synaptic degeneration in optic fibers following optic radiation and visual cortex damage. 3 , 4 It is sensitive to identify the retina's distinctive layers and measures the thickness of macular parameters and retinal nerve fiber layer thickness (RNFL) around the optic disc. Macular thickness (MT) was positively correlated with the thickness of the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (pRNFL) in healthy children. 5 The macular region contains almost half of the retinal ganglion cells in the eye. It is thickest in this region, which is helpful in the early detection of glaucoma and retinal disease. 6 The perimacular ganglion cell complex (GCC) is the retina's inner layer, potentially involved in degeneration. GCC includes peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layers, macular retinal nerve fiber layers (mRNFL), ganglion cell layer (GCL), and inner plexiform layer (IPL). IPL and GCL are ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layers (GC/IPL). Compared to pRNFL, which is highly dependent on individual anatomy, the GC/IPL appears more uniform and less affected by systemic factors in the average population, demonstrating good interobserver reproducibility. 7 , 8 Using an automated intraretinal segmentation algorithm known as the macular ganglion cell analysis (GCA) algorithm, Cirrus high‐definition OCT (HD‐OCT) is capable of measuring the thickness of the retinal ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer (GC/IPL). 9 GC/IPL serves as a more direct marker for estimating retinal ganglion cell (RGC) injury than pRNFL thickness in people with brain lesions 10 and also as a better candidate for detecting early glaucoma progression. 8 , 9 , 11 , 12 The thickness of the GC/IPL and pRNFL in the retina undergoes thinning in neurodegenerative conditions such as Parkinson's disease, 13 Alzheimer's disease, 14 and multiple sclerosis. 15 This suggests that GC/IPL can be a marker for early detection of neurodegeneration, disease progression, and severity. OCT's use in epilepsy is also expanding. People with epilepsy on ASM have decreased GCC and pRNFL thickness compared to healthy controls. 16 Several risk factors for pRNFL thinning in epilepsy have been described, such as specific ASM exposure, 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 the presence of male sex, 21 unilateral hippocampal sclerosis on the same side, 22 and especially drug resistance. 21

We hypothesized that GC/IPL thickness is associated with pRNFL thickness in DRE. In this cross‐sectional study, we aim to evaluate the average and sector thickness of pRNFL, MT, and GC/IPL in people with DRE, non‐drug‐resistant epilepsy (NDR), and healthy controls (HC) and assess factors contributing to reduced average thickness in GC/IPL among people with epilepsy.

METHODS

Participants

Adults attending the epilepsy clinic of West China Hospital between December 31, 2017, and August 1, 2023, and diagnosed with epilepsy according to the accepted criteria 23 were asked to participate in the cross‐sectional observational study. After obtaining written informed consent, individuals filled out a uniform questionnaire about general medical history and the existence of eye diseases. We performed visual testing before the OCT scan to exclude those with more than six diopters of myopia or hyperopia and a best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA) <20/25 in both eyes. People were also excluded: if they had previous exposure to vigabatrin, evidence of vascular dementia, a history of alcohol abuse, and psychiatric disorders or a history of psychotropic drug consumption, in addition, people with metabolic diseases; arterial hypertension; other neurological diseases; previous history of optic media opacity, cataract, or early lens opacity; retinal detachment; early age‐related macular degeneration or further macular degeneration; or retinal vascular diseases or a past record of injury or surgical procedures involving the eye or orbit. We also stipulated that people should have no abnormal findings, in the visual pathways or hippocampal sclerosis, on neuroimaging (MRI, Magnetom trio Siemens 3 T). HC were simultaneously recruited mainly among relatives and friends of people with epilepsy attending the clinic, who met the relevant criteria and were matched by sex and age. The West China Hospitals’ Medical Ethics Committee scrutinized and approved the study protocol.

Clinical data

Clinical data, including age, sex, ethnicity, epilepsy (from the seizure onset to OCT assessment), neuroimaging data, and ASM history, were recorded. People were then stratified at the time of OCT assessment into two groups according to drug‐resistant status: DRE defined as failure of adequate trials of two tolerated, appropriately chosen and used ASM schedules (whether as monotherapies or in combination) to achieve sustained (>12 months) seizure freedom. 24 NDR is defined as those responsive to the first or second ASM. Seizure frequency recorded generalized tonic–clonic seizures (GTCS), focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (FBTCS), or focal seizures. ASM numbers are historical and present long‐term usage (at least 6 months).

OCT measurements

Retinal and macular RNFL of both eyes, ganglion cell‐inner plexiform layer were evaluated using an HD‐OCT 5000 (Carl Zeiss Meditec Inc., Dublin, CA) by two experienced operators blinded to group assignment. The scanner allows mapping of the average RNFL thickness data in the peripapillary area in four 90° quadrants (superior S, nasal N, inferior I, and temporal T) with a 200 × 200 optic disk cube scan pattern. The participant was positioned at the machine and asked to fixate on an internal target. Then, a 512 × 128 macular cube scan was performed. GC/IPL thickness was measured based on the three‐dimensional volume scan, further analyzed, and quantified by the ganglion cell analysis algorithm. For each cube, scan within a 6 × 6 mm2 region centered on the macular area. Average and six sectors around the macular (superior S, superotemporal TS, superonasal NS, inferior I, inferonasal NI, and inferotemporal TI) of GC/IPL thickness were measured following the above instructions. Macular thickness of the outer and inner regions in different areas (inner temporal: IT; inner nasal: IN; inner inferior: II; inner superior: IS；outer temporal: OT; outer nasal: ON; outer inferior: OI; outer superior: OS), as well as average macular thickness and fovea were measured (see Figure 1). Images with visible eye motion or blinking artifacts and those with signal strength <7 were discarded, and measurements were repeated. All OSCAR‐IB criteria should be fulfilled. 25

Statistical analysis

The evaluation was limited to the participant's right eye for statistical analysis. The chi‐square, Fisher's exact, and Mann–Whitney U tests were used for categorical and continuous variables. An independent‐sample t‐test (Wilcoxon test) was conducted to compare average and regional analyses of the pRNFL, MT, and GC/IPL thickness between the two groups (Figure 1). One‐way analysis of variation (ANOVA) was used to compare relevant parameters (p < 0.05 in the t‐test) obtained from OCT among the DRE, NDR, and HC (the Bonferroni correction). A scan was considered normal if the values obtained were between the 5th and 95th centiles, borderline if between the 1st and 5th, and abnormally thinned if < the 1st centile. The following variables were evaluated across people with epilepsy in the univariate analysis: age, sex, duration of epilepsy, drug resistance, ASM exposure, and GTCS presence. We also conducted multivariate logistic regression to find predictors of average or GC/IPL sector, average or quadrant pRNFL, and average or quadrant MT below the 5th centile in the participants, adjusting for significant confounding factors from univariate analyses. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were presented. Partial correlations were performed between seizure frequency and GC/IPL thickness, ASM numbers and GC/IPL thickness, controlling for age, duration of epilepsy, sex, and GTCS/FBTCS presence. Data processing and analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 and Storm Statistical Platform (www.medsta.cn/software), and significance was set at p < 0.05.

Predictors of pRNFL, MT, GC/IPL thickness changes in multivariate logistic regression models in participants with epilepsy

The percentage of people with abnormal/ borderline GC/IPL thickness (average or the thickness of each 60°sector) was significantly higher in DRE than in NDR (37.3% vs. 5.4%, p = 5.40E‐04):19.6% had abnormal average GC/IPL thinning, with 17.6% having borderline changes in DRE, GC/IPL‐TI found to be the most affected (borderline/abnormal) sector (15.6%) than the other sectors. Univariate logistic regression analysis showed a significant association between abnormal/ borderline GC/IPL and female sex (p = 0.014), exposure to lamotrigine (p = 0.021) and valproate (p = 0.016), and drug‐resistant epilepsy (p = 1.60E‐04) in the univariate analysis. The logistic regression model for multivariate analysis showed only drug resistance (OR = 10.25, CI 95% 2.82 to 37.28, p = 6.9E‐04) was an independent predictor of an increased probability of GC/IPL thinning (borderline or abnormal) (Table 3). A higher level of exposure to ASM (r = −0.27, p = 0.013) and increased seizure frequency (r = −0.23, p = 0.039) were associated with average GC/IPL thickness, after control for age, duration of epilepsy, and sex ratio (see Figure 2).

DISCUSSION

This study is one of the first to explore the potential use of GC/IPL as a biomarker for DRE. We have shown that some people with DRE had thinner average GC/IPL thicknesses, inner quadrant MT, and pRNFL thickness compared to people with non‐resistant epilepsy. Drug resistance seems independently associated with the thinning of GC/IPL. ASM numbers or seizure frequency directly correlate with average GC/IPL thickness thinning.

There is a shortage of evidence on GC/IPL in DRE, but a correlation between neurodegenerative diseases and GC/IPL has been identified. For instance, Parkinson's disease severity with GC/IPL measurements was determined, 13 , 26 a correlation also evident in Alzheimer's disease. 27 , 28 A report suggests that GC/IPL thickness and thinning rates effectively identify clinical progression in remission of multiple sclerosis. 29 While pRNFL is the most widely used OCT measure in epilepsy, we found that GC/IPL represents the more sensitive indicator of DRE than pRNFL. There are three possible explanations. First, GCL thinning precedes the axonal loss and follows retrograde visual pathway degeneration by acquired retrograde trans‐synaptic axonal degeneration (RTD), varying with lesion severity and location, making it more vulnerable to cell loss. 3 , 4 , 10 , 30 Second, GCL were the cell bodies, and IPL were the dendrites of RGCs. Measurements obtained through HD‐OCT can provide insight into the loss of RGCs, as approximately half of RGCs are concentrated in the macular region. GCL has seven layers of ganglion cell bodies, with RGC body size 10–20 times larger than axon, enhancing detectability. 31 Third, epilepsy was long thought to be the more gray matter‐related condition. Consequently, GC/IPL might be an easier and more potential biomarker of central neural loss than peripapillary RNFL.

We also established a link between ASM exposure and GC/IPL atrophy. Various ASMs can induce ocular issues, including color deficiencies, reduced contrast sensitivity, visual field defects, RNFL loss, and visual impairment. 32 GABA (gamma‐aminobutyric acid)‐ergic drugs like vigabatrin are often associated with retinal toxicity, impacting retinal cells from photoreceptors to the GCL, eventually resulting in GC/IPL loss. Notably, “temporal optic rim and macular sparing atrophy” may indicate vigabatrin toxicity. Early RGC axon loss manifests initially in the nasal aspect, with the temporal element affected subsequently, creating a distinctive C‐shaped pattern. 18 , 19 Ocular motility issues and visual field narrowing can be related to ASMs with sodium channel‐blocking properties, such as carbamazepine, lamotrigine, and topiramate. 33 The exact incidence of these adverse effects and the structure lesions by other ASMs, except vigabatrin, is unknown. Previous work has shown valproate and oxcarbazepine can cause RGC loss or atrophy in Wistar rats. 34 Investigations into the effects of ASMs (e.g., valproate, lamotrigine, carbamazepine, topiramate) on GCC thinning in adults have produced conflicting results. 16 , 20 , 21 , 35 Ocular adverse reactions to ASM may differ but are typically categorized as dose‐dependent (carbamazepine and lamotrigine), non‐dose‐dependent (topiramate), and cumulative effect (valproate and vigabatrin). 36 Structural lesions show variability based on specific ASM exposures and doses. In our study, obtaining data on ASM treatment duration and the estimated cumulative dose among our participants was challenging. We can't identify the specific ASMs potentially contributing to GC/IPL thinning.

We found drug resistance as an independent factor of borderline attenuation or abnormal thinning of the GC/IPL, and exposure to higher seizure frequency could be attributed to progressive atrophy. It's generally accepted that epilepsy is a progressive disorder with exposure to more seizures. 37 , 38 However, one study found disease duration, seizure frequency, status history, and multiple drug use showed no significant associations with OCT parameters (GCL, IPL, and RNFL). 39 There are three possible explanations for this difference: First, these results may be due to different sample sizes, criteria, devices, study populations, and ASM exposure. Second, epilepsy has a fluctuating course. Drug‐resistant and non‐drug resistance might change at different checkpoints; therefore, disease progression may not parallel the thinning of optic nerves. Third, the macular atrophy can be significantly slowed down and remain stable within time. 3

Consistent with available evidence, our study demonstrated a decrease in the average pRNFL thickness in people with epilepsy compared to controls. 21 , 22 , 39 , 40 The thickness of the pRNFL is associated with widespread axonal changes across the central nervous system. 41 Thus, in particular, previous studies have suggested drug resistance as the independent risk factor of pRNFL thinning and suggested that drug‐resistant epilepsy doubles the pRNFL thinning risk compared with non‐resistant epilepsy. 21 We found no significant differences in average pRNFL thickness between people with and without drug‐resistant epilepsy. This suggests that average GC/IPL may be a more effective biomarker for identifying individuals with drug‐resistant epilepsy than pRNFL thickness, indicating its superiority as a marker for this condition.

In our results, the thickness of the pRNFL and GC/IPL across all sectors of epilepsy was thinner than in controls. We also identified a significant decrease in the temporal sector of RNFL and GC/IPL thicknesses and inner MT in people with DRE compared with non‐drug‐resistant epilepsy. In people with Alzheimer's disease, 28 the inner segment of macular thickness measurements was also the most and earliest affected parameter. However, the most relevant sectors of pRNFL should always be assessed cautiously as different findings are available. Reports of significantly affected inferior quadrants of pRNFL in people with hippocampal sclerosis are available. 22 Average and superior quadrant RNFL thickness values were significantly lower in people involved in another study. 16 Likewise, one study reported that the superior and nasal aspect quadrant was markedly thinner in people with juvenile myoclonic epilepsy. 42 One finding suggests that temporal pRNFL thickness is most affected in MRI‐negative cases. 43 A more extensive longitudinal study is needed to control variables like seizure type to provide a more comprehensive picture.

Our approach has several limitations. This was a cross‐sectional case–control study, so no causal relationship can be derived. Longitudinal studies with repeated measures are needed to validate pRNFL thickness as a biomarker and further explore its relation to factors like seizure frequency and ASM use on brain structure. The potential effects of different ASMs and epilepsy phenotypes in these parameters also warrant further investigation.

Second, our study reported no statistical differences in pRNFL between DRE and NDR. The negative findings are also possibly due to the small sample size, age, and population. Given that pRNFL had more variability and lower reproductivity than GC/IPL, 6 more cases are needed to decrease the risk of Type II errors. The participants were Han Chinese, and the median age was approximately 25 years. It is essential to consider a more diverse range of ethnicities and age groups in future studies. Third, we did not correlate OCT parameters with brain volume quantification by MRIs, which is an essential next step.

CONCLUSION

Our preliminary findings suggested a significant macular GC/IPL thickness reduction in people with DRE, mainly in the temporal regions of GC/IPL and pRNFL. Frequent seizures and increased exposure to ASMs are associated with reduced average GC/IPL thickness. GC/IPL thickness might be a potentially valuable tool for assessing neurodegeneration and early detection of drug resistance in people with epilepsy. The mechanism of reduction and even cut‐off value of specific retinal thickness needs further investigation.
