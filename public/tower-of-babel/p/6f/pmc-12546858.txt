Myeloid-derived suppressor cells inhibit the replication of oncolytic virus by reducing intratumoral arginine levels
Authors: Parker Dryja, Erica B Flores, Mee Y Bartee, Viswanathan Palanisamy, Eric Bartee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOncolytic virotherapy uses replication-competent viruses to treat various solid tumors. While much of the clinical efficacy of oncolytic virotherapy is mediated by anti-tumor T cell responses, most of these therapies still rely on the in vivo replication of the viral agents within infected tumor cells. Understanding the fundamental mechanisms that govern this replication therefore remains essential to the clinical application of these therapies. As viruses, oncolytic agents rely entirely on host metabolites and resources for their propagation. To address this gap in knowledge, we asked which cells impacted the intratumoral replication of oncolytic myxoma virus during treatment of B16F10 melanomas. Our results demonstrate that myxoma replication is potently restricted by the presence of intratumoral arginase-1+ myeloid-derived suppressor cells, which prevent the spread of oncolytic infection by catabolizing intratumoral arginine supplies. Additionally, either pharmacological depletion of these cells or genetic ablation of their arginase-1 expression markedly improves intratumoral myxoma infection and enhances the therapeutic efficacy of viruses. Collectively, these results suggest that the clinical application of oncolytic viruses is likely to be impacted by the unique metabolic state of the tumor microenvironment and that myeloid-derived suppressor cell-mediated depression of arginine within tumors may play a critical role in suppressing these treatments.Keywords: MT: Regular Issue, oncolytic virotherapy, myxoma virus, arginine metabolism, immunotherapy

Introduction

Oncolytic virotherapy (OV) uses various types of replication-competent viruses to treat solid tumors. The replication of these viruses within malignant tissue results in both direct cellular lysis and the induction of subsequent anti-tumor immune responses.1 While the relative impact of these two mechanisms varies from virus to virus, in most instances, intratumoral replication of the oncolytic agent is thought to be beneficial to therapeutic success. Unfortunately, the fundamental pathways and mechanisms that influence the replication and spread of each oncolytic agent within treated tumors are not completely understood.

As with all viruses, oncolytic agents are entirely dependent on the metabolic resources of their host cells to complete their replication cycles. In stark contrast to the healthy tissues, these viruses have evolved to infect, however, the tumor microenvironments (TMEs) in which they are used to therapeutically present dramatically altered metabolic landscapes including altered energetics, reduced availability of exogenous amino acids, altered ionic states, extreme levels of hypoxia, and acidic pH.2,3,4 However, while an extensive body of literature exists on the general metabolic dependencies of viruses,5,6 the potential barrier to OV performance created by the altered metabolic landscape of the TME (particularly in regard to non-energetic metabolism7,8) remains largely understudied.

We have recently shown that replication of the oncolytic poxvirus myxoma (MYXV) is dependent on the bioavailability of the amino acid arginine (ARG).9 However, while removal of ARG from in vitro growth media is a simple endeavor, the metabolism and bioavailability of ARG in tumor tissue are highly complex. Most normal cells can directly biosynthesize ARG by converting the metabolic precursors citrulline and aspartate into ARG through the urea cycle enzymes argininosuccinate synthase 1 (ASS1) and argininosuccinate lyase.10 However, many solid tumors are functionally auxotrophic for ARG due to the epigenetic silencing of ASS1,11,12,13 and these tumors fail to support the replication of oncolytic MYXV when the supplies of exogenous ARG are limiting.9 In the current work, we show that the intratumoral replication of oncolytic MXYV is strongly influenced by the presence of ARG-catabolizing myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). This work identifies a novel mechanism restricting the replication of oncolytic viruses and supports the potential role for intratumoral metabolism in influencing the outcomes of these therapies.

Results

The intratumoral replication of oncolytic MYXV is inhibited by GR1+ cells

In order to understand factors that influence the in vivo replication of oncolytic viruses, we initially assayed the amount of infectious MYXV found in B16F10 tumors under various conditions. Consistent with reports from other viruses,14,15,16,17 replication of oncolytic MYXV appeared to be restricted by innate inflammatory signaling since tumors derived from cells deficient in STAT1 displayed increased overall viral burden 8 days after treatment. The magnitude of this increase, however, was significantly less than that observed in tumors established on NOD/scid/IL2Rγ−/− (NSG) mice suggesting that, in B16F10 tumors, the primary factor restricting MYXV replication was the presence of anti-viral immune cells (Figure 1A). To identify which immune cells might be restricting intratumoral viral replication, we further assayed viral burden in mice treated with depleting antibodies against T cells (αCD4/CD8), natural killer (NK) cells (αNK1.1), B cells (αCD19), or a subset of myeloid cells (αGR1). Interestingly, of these regimens, only treatment of mice with αGR1 antibodies increased overall viral burden (Figure 1A), suggesting that myeloid cells might play a role in determining the intratumoral replication of MYXV. This hypothesis was supported by additional experiments showing that mice treated with αGR1 antibodies also displayed increased areas of viral infection within tumors (Figures 1B and 1C) and that viral titers in non-treated animals negatively correlated with the number of intratumoral GR1+ cells (Figure 1D).

To better understand how depletion of GR1+ cells was enhancing overall MYXV replication, we next asked whether the elimination of these cells altered the initial establishment or subsequent persistence of MYXV infections. We observed that, up to 4 days post infection, the total viral burden in tumors was comparable between control and αGR1-treated cohorts. At 6 days post viral treatment, however, overall viral titers in control cohorts began to decay while titers in αGR1-treated animals were maintained (Figure 2A). Histological analyses of treated tumors revealed a similar phenotype, with the total number of viral foci being statistically similar until 4 days post infection but subsequently decreasing in control animals while being better maintained in αGR1-treated cohorts (Figure 2B). Visual analysis of viral foci throughout this time course revealed two distinct phenotypes: expanding foci that presented as large infections made up of numerous cells and stalled foci made up of relatively few cells (Figure 2C). Interestingly, while the total number of foci was similar at 4 days post viral treatment, a higher percentage of these foci displayed a progressing phenotype in αGR1-treated animals compared to controls (Figure 2D). Collectively, these results suggest that the presence of intratumoral GR1+ cells reduces viral burden by preventing sustained viral replication and spread.

Depletion of GR1+ cells improves the therapeutic efficacy of oncolytic MYXV

Since elimination of GR1+ cells appeared to improve the viral health of oncolytic MYXV during therapy, we hypothesized that this depletion might enhance the overall therapeutic response to MYXV-based OV. Since unmodified MYXV has only modest therapeutic efficacy in B16F10 tumors, to test this we used a more therapeutically active MYXV construct expressing soluble PD1 and IL12.18 This virus displays similar intratumoral replication and induces phenotypically similar overall immune responses to control MYXV but causes greater immune activation.18 Importantly, neither vPD1/IL12 nor control MYXV induced significant changes to the MDSC compartment in our previous studies. Since vPD1/IL12 is highly therapeutically effective against B16F10 tumors, to allow for potential improvements from increased viral replication to be visualized, we used a highly reduced therapeutic dose of only 1 × 103 foci forming units (FFU)/injection18 (Figure 3A). Under these low-dose conditions, treatment with αGR1 significantly improved tumor control in the injected lesions (67.8% ± 17.8% tumor growth inhibition for vPD1/IL12 + αGR1-treated mice compared to 29.3% ± 27.1% tumor growth inhibition for vPD1/IL12 alone, p = 0.029 at day 8 post treatment) and also had a non-significant but beneficial effect in non-treated tumors (33.3% ± 62.8% tumor growth inhibition for vPD1/IL12 + αGR1-treated mice compared to 3.7% ± 25.4% tumor growth inhibition for vPD1/IL12 alone, p = 0.35 at day 8 post treatment) (Figures 3B and 3C). These data suggest that preconditioning of tumors through depletion of GR1+ cells might allow for more effective OV treatment although the impact of depletion might be more profound in locations where ongoing viral replication is observed.

αGR1 treatment preferentially eliminates MDSCs

Our previous results suggested that αGR1 depletion improved the replication and therapeutic efficacy of oncolytic MYXV by eliminating an anti-viral immune cell. However, αGR1 antibodies are known to bind to two distinct epitopes including both Ly6c (which is expressed on a variety of cell types) and Ly6g (whose expression is predominantly limited to neutrophilic cells). We therefore used spectral flow cytometry to identify which immune cell population(s) found in B16F10 tumors were depleted following αGR1 treatment. This analysis identified 11 distinct populations of CD45+ cells that could be found within B16F10 tumors (Figure 4A). Consistent with previous reports, a variety of cells including neutrophils, monocytes, and several T cell populations expressed Ly6c. In contrast, expression of Ly6g was tightly restricted to neutrophils (Figure 4B). Interestingly, despite the apparent widespread expression of the dual GR1 epitope, treatment of mice with αGR1 antibodies only reduced the abundance of a single-cell population in B16F10 tumors, which corresponded to Ly6g+ neutrophilic cells. The total number of CD45+ cells found in tumors as well as the numbers of other Ly6c+ cell types remained unchanged (Figure 4C). Further characterization of the intratumoral Ly6g+ neutrophil population demonstrated that the majority of these cells were low in density (Figures 4D and 4E), and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted Ly6g+ cells from tumor-bearing mice expressed both the mRNA (Figure 4F) and protein (Figure 4G) for the urea cycle enzyme arginase-1 (ARG1). Taken together, these data suggest that injection of αGR1 antibodies depletes a single immune population from B16F10 tumors whose characteristics are consistent with a possibly heterogeneous neutrophilic group of cells often referred to as MDSCs.19,20,21,22

MDSCs inhibit MYXV replication in vitro by degrading ARG

We have previously shown that MYXV requires a significant pool of bioavailable ARG to complete its viral replication cycle,9 and one of the defining characteristics of intratumoral MDSCs is that they express the ARG-degrading enzyme ARG1.20,21,22 We therefore hypothesized that the improved viral replication we observed following treatment of mice with αGR1 antibodies might be related to these cells’ expression of ARG1 altering the intratumoral ARG concentration. Consistent with this hypothesis, we observed that incubation of MYXV-infected cells with concentrations of recombinant human ARG1 (hARG1) beyond 1 μg/mL resulted in a significant reduction in virally derived GFP (Figure 5A) as well as a ∼2log decline in overall viral titer (Figure 5B). These anti-viral effects were titratable with an IC50 for ARG1 of ∼0.8 μg/mL (Figure 5C), suggesting that hARG1 could potently inhibit MYXV replication in vitro. Similarly, in vivo analysis of untreated mice bearing B16F10 tumors indicated that the intratumoral concentration of ARG negatively correlated with the abundance of GR1+ cells (Figure 5D) and that treatment of mice with αGR1 antibodies altered a significant number of metabolic pathways involved in amino acid synthesis (Figure 5E) and increased the local ARG concentration ∼1.6-fold (Figure 5F). Finally, co-culture of MYXV-infected cells with ARG1+ MDSCs purified from B16F10 tumors (Figure 6A) reduced the production of infectious viral progeny by ∼50% (Figure 6B) and lowered the intensity of virally derived GFP (Figure 6C). These effects were not seen following co-culture of infected cells with purified intratumoral T cells (Figures 6B and 6C) and could be prevented by either treatment with the ARG1 inhibitor N-ω-hydroxy-L-norarginine acetate (Nor-NOHA)23 or addition of 800 mM excess ARG to the growth media (Figure 6B).

Finally, to test whether GR1 depletion was improving MYXV replication by reducing intratumoral ARG catabolism, we asked how these treatments impacted viral burdens in mice whose MDSCs lacked ARG1. ARG1fl/fl mice were crossed to LyMcre-expressing mice to generate conditional knockouts (KOs) that lacked ARG1 expression in the myeloid compartment (ARG1-KO) (Figure 7A). B16F10 tumors were then established on these mice and treated with MYXV ± αGR1 (Figure 7B). Consistent with GR1 depletion eliminating ARG1+ MDSCs, the results indicated that tumors on ARG1-KO mice contained increased levels of ARG compared to wild-type (WT) controls (Figure 7C) and supported increased viral replication following treatment with MYXV (Figure 7D). However, while treatment with GR1-depleting antibodies increased both the ARG content and viral burdens in WT mice, this treatment had only a minimal effect in ARG1-KO animals (Figures 7C and 7D). Taken together, these data demonstrate that GR1+ MDSCs can directly inhibit MYXV replication both in vitro and in vivo by reducing bioavailable ARG via expression of ARG1.

Discussion

In the current manuscript, we identify MDSCs as a major regulator of intratumoral MYXV replication. Importantly, in B16F10 tumors, the impact of MDSCs on intratumoral replication appears to be larger than that of other more canonical anti-viral immune populations, such as T cells, B cells, and NK cells, and at least equal to the role played by the interferon system (Figure 1). The reason that these other immune cells had minimal effects on viral titers in our system is not known but could have to do with the tumor model used in our study, the specific biology of MYXV, or the direct intratumoral injection of virus used. Interestingly, these results are largely counter to the canonical role of MDSCs in chronic viral infection. Indeed, previous work has suggested that the presence of these cells is often positively correlated with viral burden in patients with hepatitis C virus and HIV, a phenomenon largely attributed to their ability to suppress anti-viral immune responses allowing for robust infection.24,25 These chronic infection studies, however, occur in the context of viral infection of normal host tissue. Therefore, the apparent contradiction in the role of MDSCs might be due to the inherently stressed metabolic nature of the TME.4,26

Mechanistically, we have recently shown that the replication of MYXV is dependent on the bioavailability of ARG,9 and our current work suggests that MDSCs inhibit MYXV replication by regulating ARG bioavailability through expression of ARG1 (Figures 4, 5, 6, and 7). Histological analyses of infected tumors demonstrate that removal of these MDSCs does not influence the total number of infection events initiated after treatment but rather influences the percentage of these events that successfully propagate over time. This is consistent with our previous in vitro observations in which removal of ARG during viral infection results in non-productive single-cell infections much like the non-expanding foci observed in vivo.9 The presence of MDSCs may therefore be decreasing the areas within the TME that are amenable to viral infection by virtue of altering local ARG content. It is critical to point out, however, that our study cannot rule out MDSCs impacting MYXV replication through mechanisms other than ARG metabolism in vivo. Indeed, it is notable that GR1 depletion resulted in trends toward higher viral titers even in mice lacking ARG1 (Figure 7), suggesting that these cells could impact intratumoral viral replication through additional non-ARG-mediated mechanisms. While these mechanisms are likely to be complex, even within the realm of amino acid metabolism, MDSCs have also been shown to cause reductions in available cysteine and tryptophan—both of which are also required for MYXV replication in vitro.9 It is therefore possible that some of the observed data could be due to the culmination of several metabolic factors being reversed rather than a specific liberation of ARG, a notion that is further supported by the widespread changes to metabolic compartments observed following GR1 depletion (Figure 5). Alternatively, while no other immune subsets displayed a significant role in altering MYXV titers in our studies (Figure 1), MDSCs have been shown to impact the function of a variety of cell types that have displayed anti-viral functions in other studies, such as T cells, B cells, and NK cells. Therefore, the possibility that depletion of MDSCs impacts MYXV titers at least partially through a secondary immune mechanism cannot be completely ruled out. Additionally, virtually all the anti-tumor efficacy of MYXV against B16F10 tumors is mediated by the generation of anti-tumor immunity, and MDSCs have been shown to alter the function of various anti-tumor immune cells. Therefore, while we have attributed most of the improved efficacy of virus in our studies to increased intratumoral replication, it is possible that a portion is also due to directly improving the functionality of the anti-tumor immune response.

While our data show a relatively robust impact on intratumoral MYXV replication following depletion of ARG1+ MDSCs in mice, it is important to note that the biology of ARG1 expression within MDSCs differs significantly between human and mouse. In murine leukocytes, ARG1 is largely retained intracellularly and degrades ARG following its uptake by plasma membrane transporters such as the cationic amino acid transporter 2B.27,28 In contrast, in humans, ARG1 is expressed in a broader set of polymorphonuclear cells and is often secreted into the extracellular space upon stimulation.29,30 This could have a highly significant impact of how ARG is spatially regulated within tumors from each species. However, significantly more work is needed to validate this point.

Ultimately, our results demonstrate that considering alterations to tumor metabolism in the context of OV performance is a worthwhile endeavor. Compared to normal tissues, a plethora of metabolites have been experimentally shown to be altered within the TME. Though our current studies focused on the role of ARG in the replication of MYXV, it is virtually guaranteed that changes to other metabolites can also influence viral replication within the context of OV. Identifying how these changes affect both the quality of viral infection and the subsequent anti-tumor immune response may provide additional strategies to improve the therapeutic efficacy of OV and is therefore deserving of further study.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagents

B16F10 cells were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA) and maintained in complete Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Corning, Corning, NY) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (VWR, Radnor, PA) and 1× penicillin/streptomycin/glutamine (Corning, Corning, NY). Cultures were checked twice yearly for mycoplasma contamination using polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The following in vivo depleting antibodies were obtained from BioXcell (Lebanon, NH, USA) and used in this study: αCD4 (clone GK1.5), αCD8 (clone 53–6.7), αNK1.1 (clone PK136), αCD19 (clone ID3), αGR1 (clone NIMP-R14), and αLy6g (clone 1A8). Recombinant human ARG1 was obtained from VWR (Allentown, PA, USA). The ARG1 inhibitor Nor-NOHA was obtained from Bachem Bioscience (Torrance, CA, USA). Arginine concentrations were quantified using the fluorometric arginine assay kit from Abcam (Cambridge, UK).

Viral constructs, infection, and quantification of virus

The recombinant MYXVs expressing either green fluorescent protein (vGFP) or soluble PD1 and IL12 (vPD1/IL12) used in the current manuscript are based on the Lausanne strain of MYXV and have been previously described.18,31 In vitro infections were carried out by inoculating cells with virus for 30 min at room temperature, removing viral inoculum, and replacing it with fresh media. To quantitate infectious virus (from all in vitro and in vivo samples as well as produced viral stocks), cell pellets from either cell culture or infected tumor samples were freeze-thawed over three cycles in liquid nitrogen and a 37°C water bath. Resulting homogenates were serially diluted and plated onto confluent layers of BSC40 cells. The number of GFP+ foci was then quantified 48 h post infection and used to determine the titer of original samples. Histological analysis of tumors was conducted as previously described.9

Mouse models

All mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, MA) and maintained under specific pathogen-free biosafety level 2 conditions. For most experiments, female C57BL/6J mice (strain #000664) aged 6–10 weeks were seeded subcutaneously (s.c.) with 1 × 106 tumor cells in 50 μL of cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Tumors were allowed to establish until they reached ∼25 mm2 prior to use. For therapeutic studies (Figure 3), mice were treated with three doses of 1 × 103 FFU of vGFP administered intratumorally in 50 μL of PBS every other day over 5 days. For viral replication studies (Figures 1, 2, and 7), mice were treated with a single dose of 1 × 106 FFU of vGFP injected intratumorally in 50 μL of PBS. Mice were euthanized when their tumor reached 15 mm in any direction (determined using digital calipers). Antibody depletions were conducted by injecting 100 μg of the indicated antibodies intraperitoneally as indicated. Conditional ARG1-KO mice were generated by Jackson Laboratories by crossing ARG1fl/fl mice (C57BL/6-Arg1tm1Pmu/J, strain #008817) with LysMCRE mice (B6.129P2-Lyz2tm1(cre)Ifo/J, strain #004781). Genotype of progeny was confirmed by PCR analysis. All animal experiments were approved by the University of New Mexico Health Science Center institutional animal care and use committee under protocol #20-201002-HSC.

Flow cytometry analysis

B16F10 tumor-bearing mice were euthanized, and their tumors separated into single-cell suspensions by passing them through a 40 μM mesh. Cell suspensions were stained with an antibody panel and analyzed on a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer. Antibodies included in the analysis panel included the following: Ly6C, CD3, PD1 (CD279), CD45, CD11b, CD19, NKp46, CD49a, NK1.1, CD11c, Ly6G, I-A, Tim3, CD8a, CD4, CD25, CD69, F4-80, CD49b, B220, and Live-Dead stain Far-Red. T-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding analysis was conducted using FlowJo v.10.10.0 on events pre-gated for single, living, CD45+. The identified populations are defined as follows: B cells (CD3−/CD11b−/CD19+/B220+), CD4+ Tcon (CD11b−/CD3+/CD4+/CD8−/TIM3−), CD4+ Tregs (CD11b−/CD3+/CD4+/CD8-/CD25+/TIM3+),32,33 CD8+ CTL (CD11b−/CD3+/CD4-/CD8+), double-negative T cells (CD11b−/CD3+/NK1.1−/NKp46−/CD4−/CD8−), dendritic cells (CD11b+/CD3−/CD11c+), macrophages (CD11b+/CD3−/Ly6g−/Ly6c−/F4/80+), monocytes (CD11b+/CD3−/Ly6g−/Ly6c+/F4/80−), neutrophils (CD11b+/CD3−/Ly6g+/Ly6clo), NK cells (CD11b−/CD3−/NK1.1+/NKp46+), and unknown (CD11b−/CD3−/NKp46−/NK1.1−/CD19−/B220−).

Metabolomic analysis

For metabolic analysis, B16F10 tumors were established on syngeneic C57BL/6J mice until they reached ∼25 mm2. Mice were then injected with either control IgG or αGR1-depleting antibodies. Seven days post GR1 depletion, tumors were harvested and processed for analysis as previously described.9 In short, tumors were snap frozen in liquid nitrogen, mechanically disassociated using physical percussion and then resuspended in 80% methanol. Suspensions were further disassociated via sonication and then analyzed via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry by the Metabolomics Core Facility at Robert H. Lurie Comprehensive Cancer Center of Northwestern University (Chicago, IL).34 Analysis of the resulting data was performed using the “pathway analyst” feature of metaboanalyst35 to compare pathways enriched in metabolites, which significantly differed between control and αGR1-treated cohorts.

Neutrophil density analysis

For density analysis of neutrophilic cells, mice were implanted with B16F10 tumors s.c., which were allowed to establish until they reached 100–200 mm2. Mice were then euthanized, and both the tumors and spleens separated into single-cell suspensions by passing them through a 40 μM mesh. Cell suspensions were then layered over a Histopaque-1077 solution and centrifuged at room temperature for 30 min at 400 × g. Cells were recovered from the resulting band at the upper Histopaque interface (low density cells) and the pellet (normal density cells) and analyzed using flow cytometry. The ratio of high to low neutrophilic density was determined by comparing the percentage of neutrophilic cells (CD45+/CD11b+/CD3−/Ly6g+/Ly6clo) found in the pellet and band fractions.

MDSC co-culture

For MDSC co-culture experiments, established B16F10 tumors (between 100 and 200 mm2) were excised and processed into single-cell suspensions by passing them through a 40 μM mesh. Intratumoral MDSCs (CD45+/CD11b+/CD3−/Ly6g+/Ly6clo) or T cells (CD45+/CD11b−/CD3+) were then isolated from tumor suspensions using FACS. Sorted cells were placed onto cultures of BSC40 cells at a ratio of 1:1 and incubated for 24 h. Cells were then infected with vGFP at an MOI = 5. Following 24 h of co-culture, the progression of viral infection was analyzed either by visually assessing expression of the virally derived GFP using fluorescent microscopy or by quantitating the numbers of infectious viral progeny as described earlier.
