Rapid skill acquisition through task-specific ultrasound training for peripheral cannulation in medical students
Authors: Xu-Nian Lin, Cheng-Wei Lu, Tzu-Yu Lin, San-Fang Chou, Ya-Ying Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasound (US)-guided peripheral intravenous access is a core procedural competency. However, formal training opportunities for medical students are often limited by short clinical rotations. This study evaluated the feasibility and short-term outcomes of a brief, skill-focused training curriculum in enabling the medical rotating trainees to successfully perform cannulation using US guidance.MethodsIn this pilot study, 22 clerks and 8 postgraduate year (PGY) residents participated in a 25-min task-specific training program consisting of an instructional video, live demonstration, and supervised hands-on practice using the Blue phantom US training model and the handheld US device (Vscan air). This curriculum exclusively emphasized the out-of-plane approach with dynamic needle tip positioning method, the use of large-gauge needles, and activation of the on-screen midline to target the vessel center while maintaining a stable probe position. Self-confidence and perceptions on the use of US guidance were assessed using pre- and post-training questionnaires on a 10-point ordinal scale. Technical competence was evaluated using a post-training practical assessment.ResultsAfter the training, median self-confidence level increased from 5 to 8, and the willingness to try US-guided cannulation before calling for help increased from 7.5 to 10 (both P < 0.001). All participants successfully completed US-guided cannulation in the simulation model, while PGY residents spent less time than clerks (68.5 vs. 133 s, P = 0.011).ConclusionsA goal-directed US training curriculum using handheld US devices facilitates immediate skill acquisition and improves short-term confidence in novice learners within a limited training timeframe. Keywords: Ultrasound, Intravenous Cannulation, Difficult Intravenous Access, Dynamic Needle Tip Positioning

Introduction

Difficult intravenous access (DIVA), reported in 6% to 87.7% of patients, may result in delayed treatment, repeated cannulation attempts, and increased patient discomfort [1]. Ultrasound (US) guidance significantly improves the success rate of peripheral intravenous (IV) cannulation, particularly in patients with DIVA. In addition to enhancing first-attempt success, US assistance reduces procedure time and complication rates [2–3]. Compared with traditional cart-based systems, handheld US devices offer advantages including portability, rapid availability, ease of disinfection, and lower cost [4–5]. These advantages facilitate both clinical application and medical education.

US-guided vascular access has been recognized as a core procedural competency in postgraduate medical training by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) across multiple specialties [6]. Though the Objective Structured Clinical Examination (OSCE) provides medical students with opportunities to learn vascular cannulation skills, US guidance is not consistently included. Based on previous studies, novice learners commonly experience difficulties including poor hand–eye coordination [7], difficulty in maintaining needle tip visualization [8], and challenges in spatial understanding during US-guided needle procedures [9]. These factors contribute to a low success rate and lead to low confidence at the very beginning of learning. Most research on US education has largely emphasized comprehensive curricula, lasting from hours to days [10–12]. Such extended courses may not be feasible for medical students who rotate through multiple specialties within limited timeframes. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and short-term outcomes of a specifically designed skill-focused curriculum emphasizing rapid acquisition of procedural skills and improvement in self-confidence using handheld US devices.

Methods

Study design

This pilot study was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Far-Eastern Memorial Hospital and was granted an exemption from formal ethical review (FEMH No.: 113224-W). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We designed this study to evaluate the feasibility and short-term outcomes of a modified US training program on learning US-guided IV cannulation in medical students. The confidence levels, concepts on US guidance, and practical assessment were evaluated.

Participants

Undergraduate clerks and postgraduate year (PGY) residents rotating through the Department of Anesthesiology at Far-Eastern Memorial Hospital between March 2025 and December 2025 were enrolled. PGY residents are junior medical graduates who have not yet commenced formal specialty training. While some PGY residents had prior experience obtaining abdominal US images using cart-based systems, all clerks were naïve to US use. Clerks or PGY residents with prior experience in US-guided IV cannulation were excluded. Participation was voluntary and had no influence on academic evaluation or rotation grades. Prior to enrollment, all participants were provided with an information sheet. The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

Instruments

In this study, we employed a commercially available US-compatible vascular access model (Elevated Blue Phantom Branched 4 Vessel US Training Block) to simulate DIVA scenarios (Fig. 1A). This model is constructed with Simulex™ material, which mimics the acoustic and tactile properties of human tissue. The vessels embedded in the Blue Phantom Block were hardly visible or palpable. To facilitate US guidance, a handheld US device (Vscan Air; GE Healthcare, USA) was wirelessly connected to a tablet device (Samsung) (Fig. 1B).

Skill-focused contents

This training was intentionally designed as a skill-focused rather than a comprehensive curriculum. To facilitate rapid procedural acquisition within a limited period, the curriculum was modified from a comprehensive training program originally designed for anesthesiology residents. We refocused contents of the curriculum on a single objective which was successful completion of US-guided cannulation. This modified curriculum was implemented in the video and a demonstration by the supervisors. The video showed the method of dynamic needle tip positioning (DNTP) [13], while the supervisors emphasized the optimal insertion angles and bevel orientation to enhance needle echogenicity. A comparison between the original and modified curricula is presented in Table 1. In brief, several key modifications were made. First, knowledge of US physics, image formation, and artifact mechanisms were removed in order to shorten the training duration and reduce cognitive load. Second, only large-gauge (16G) needles were used. Dynamic needle tip positioning (DNTP) method with out-of-plane approach, which is suggested to be easier for novice learners, was taught in our teaching session [13, 14]. Third, an on-screen midline guidance indicator was activated to align the target vessel centers. All participants were instructed to maintain probe stability, and only advance or adjust the needle position instead.

Study protocol

In this study, we employed a 25-min training session consisted of a short instructional video, live demonstration by a supervisor, and hands-on practice on a model. The study protocol is illustrated in Fig. 2. Before and after the entire protocol, all participants were required to complete a questionnaire to evaluate their understanding of US use and their confidence levels using a 1–10 ordinal scale. The questionnaire was anonymous and unlinked to any identifying information. Prior to the training, all participants were allowed to freely approach the vascular access model, including attempts to perform cannulation without US guidance. The teaching session (the grey box in Fig. 2) then consisted of a 10-min instructional video followed by a 5-min live demonstration conducted by a supervisor. In this study, one attending anesthesiologist and one senior resident served as supervisors. Following the teaching session, all participants engaged in 10 min of supervised self-practice. During this period, real-time feedback was provided as needed. Subsequently, a practical assessment was conducted by the supervisor to evaluate the participants’ performance using a practical assessment form. Successful cannulation was defined as the clear visualization of the catheter within the vessel lumen under both short-axis and long-axis US views by the supervisor. During the practical assessment, the number of cannulation attempts and total procedure time were measured. A single attempt referred to the process starting from the insertion of the needle into the Blue Phantom model until its complete withdrawal from the model. The procedural time was measured from the moment the participant’s US probe first made contact with the model surface to the final advancement of the entire catheter into the model. During the development of the questionnaire and the practice assessment, we performed an exploratory assessment of face and content validity for the included items. These items were reviewed by three attending anesthesiologists experienced in US-guided vascular access training. All three reviewers were physician educators with more than 10 years of clinical teaching experience at a medical center teaching hospital, certified clinical instructors who had completed formal faculty development training in medical education. The internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using descriptive statistics. Differences in procedural performance between the Clerk and PGY groups were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Pre- and post-training questionnaire ratings were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 30 participants were enrolled in this study, including 22 clerks (73.3%) and 8 PGY residents (26.7%). The median age of participants was 23 years. 17 participants (60%) were male and 13 (40%) were female. The demographic characteristics of participants are summarized in Table 2.

Analysis of pre- and post-training questionnaire data demonstrated significant improvements across all evaluated domains (Table 3). The internal consistency reliability was 0.80 before training and 0.88 after training, suggesting acceptable to good internal consistency. After completion of the training program, participants reported significantly higher confidence in peripheral vessel imaging, needle visualization, and overall confidence in performing US-guided peripheral venous cannulation using a handheld US device (all P < 0.001). The median confidence level increased from 5 to 8. Participants also expressed a significantly greater willingness to attempt ultrasound-guided cannulation independently before calling for help (from 7.5 to 10, P < 0.001). In addition, learning satisfaction was high. Individual paired changes in questionnaire scores are illustrated in Fig. 3.

Before the training, all participants failed to successfully perform cannulation blindly on the Blue Phantom training block. After the training, all participants (100%) successfully achieved the key assessments, including clear visualization of the needle tip, identification of target vessel structures, and successful US-guided cannulation in the simulation model (Table 4). All participants performed US-guided cannulation using out-of-plane approach with DNTP method. The median number of attempts required for successful cannulation was 1, and the median total procedure time was 82 s. Then, we stratified the study population into the Clerk and PGY subgroups for further analysis. When comparing subgroups, the PGY group completed the procedure significantly faster than the Clerk group (68.5 vs. 133 s, P = 0.011). Although the PGY group also required fewer attempts, this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.146). Discussion

Our study demonstrates that a goal-directed, skill-focused curriculum facilitated rapid acquisition of US-guided skills and increased learners’ short-term self-confidence. In contrast to the comprehensive US education programs, our task-specific approach targeted at a single procedural objective and enabled medical students to acquire an initial conceptual and technical framework within a limited rotation period. We believe that exclusive use of a large-gauge needle, emphasis on insertion angles and bevel orientation, and only DNTP with out-of-plane method were key features. An on-screen midline guidance indicator also helped align the target vessel and ensure probe steadiness for the participants. Another contributing factor to the success of this curriculum was the high portability and rapid availability of the handheld US device. Handheld US devices are suitable not only for image acquisition training but also for teaching interventional skills such as IV cannulation.

For undergraduate medical students who have not yet entered clinical practice, the unpredictability in real clinical environments, including DIVA, may represent a significant psychological barrier. According to self-efficacy theory by Bandura [15], mastery experience through low initial threshold and achievement of early success are critical for building confidence and motivation in the early stage of learning. We believed that the aforementioned key features of our curriculum help achieve immediate success and reinforced participants’ belief in their technical capabilities. The employment of a simulation model may also allow medical students to practice hand–eye coordination in a low-stress setting. In addition, novice learners are particularly vulnerable to cognitive overload, as suggested by Sweller [16]. Introducing detailed or fragmented content at an early stage may overwhelm medical students. In our study, extraneous cognitive load was reduced by eliminating complex teaching contexts and avoiding clinical environmental distractions. The transition from passive observation (supervisor demo) to active integration (self-practice) represented a scaffolding process optimizing germane load and facilitate motor-visual schema development.

We suggest that the observed 100% success rate in this study should be interpreted with consideration of the study limitations. In addition to potential ceiling effects or limited assessment stringency, several other factors should be considered. The favorable outcomes may not be solely attributable to the designed curriculum itself, but also be related to the practice effects or increased familiarity during the self-practice session. The Hawthorne effect may have contributed to the observed achievement, too. In real clinical practice, a 100% success rate cannot always be expected. The Blue Phantom simulation model may not fully represent the complexity of clinical DIVA scenarios. The exclusive use of large-gauge needles may not reflect typical clinical practice. Other factors influencing clinical performance include the variations among handheld US devices and whether the midline guidance features are universally available. Therefore, skill transfer to clinical settings and performance under authentic clinical pressures remain to be further investigated.

Although all participants ultimately achieved procedural success, PGY residents completed cannulation significantly faster than clerks. This finding demonstrated the more advanced psychomotor skills and greater procedural familiarity in the PGY subgroup. In this study, some PGY residents had prior experience acquiring abdominal US images, whereas all medical clerks were using ultrasound for the first time. This difference may emphasize that repeated practice and experience accumulation cannot be ignored. While the present curriculum emphasized rapid skill acquisition, comprehensive US education and sustained practice remain essential to help medical students be better prepared for upcoming clinical settings. Future training should incorporate multiple cannulation techniques, such as the in-plane approach [17] or the oblique method [18], as well as dynamic probe manipulation [19] to track the needle. Novel needle guidance device applied in medical education is another promising teaching tool [20].

Although the results are clear, this study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center and single-group study without a control group, and the assessors were not blinded. We conducted this study as a pilot study with a relatively small sample size. Second, long-term skill retention was not evaluated. It remains unclear whether the observed improvements in confidence and performance persist over time. Third, the absence of assessor calibration or inter-rater reliability evaluation, together with the potential Type I error inflation due to multi-level outcomes analyses were acknowledged as limitations. In addition, the current study relied on only self-reported confidence and task completion rates. More objective skill-assessment approaches may be required. For example, recent studies using optical neuroimaging have successfully quantified motor skill acquisition and distinguished expertise levels by monitoring cortical activity [21–22]. Since US-guided IV cannulation shares similar learning mechanisms of fine motor control and spatial mapping, future research could benefit from such advanced and objective skill-assessment tools.

In conclusion, a brief, skill-focused curriculum is feasible for immediate US-guided skill acquisition in medical students. This task-specific approach leads to high procedural success rates and improved short-term confidence within a limited training timeframe. The potential risk of transient increases in confidence without durable competence warrants future cohort studies with longitudinal follow-up.
