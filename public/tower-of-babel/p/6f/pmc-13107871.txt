Multi-omics-based metabolomic kinetics study of corn stover fermented by combined probiotic and enzymatic treatment
Authors: Bin Wang, XinYue Liang, YingYing Huang, MengYue Zhou, SiMiao Liu, JinXiang Su, YuChao Zhang, GeGe Lan, YiRou Xu, ChunQiang Wang, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study employed metabolomics to systematically investigate the dynamic changes of metabolites during the Probiotics-enzyme composite fermentation of corn stover. The results showed that the early fermentation stage was primarily driven by indigenous microorganisms. As fermentation progressed, the abundances of Firmicutes and Bacillus gradually increased, becoming the core functional microbiota, and their metabolic activities significantly lowered the feed pH. Additionally, Burkholderiaceae and Rhizobiaceae played pivotal roles in enhancing the crude protein content, while microorganisms such as Desulfobacterota, Erwiniaceae, Monascaceae, and Burkholderiaceae effectively degraded fiber components, thereby improving the feed’s nutritional value.To elucidate the mechanisms underlying metabolic dynamics, metabolomics analysis was conducted on days 7, 14, 21, and 28 of fermentation. The study found that during the early stage (days 7–14), the metabolism of peptides and sugar alcohols was synergistically regulated by various exogenous microorganisms. In contrast, during the mid-to-late stages (days 14–28), Firmicutes and Bacillus gradually dominated the synthesis and transformation of organic acids and secondary metabolites. By integrating prior microbiome data, this study systematically reveals the correlation mechanisms between dynamic metabolite changes and microbial communities, providing a crucial theoretical basis for understanding feed conversion rules during bacteria-enzyme composite fermentation of corn stover.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04958-0.

Introduction

As one of the major agricultural wastes, the resource utilization of corn stover holds significant implications for the development of grain-saving animal husbandry and the promotion of sustainable agricultural development [1]. However, its inherent lignocellulosic structure restricts the accessibility of nutritional components, resulting in a relatively low direct feeding value. Microbial fermentation is an effective approach to improving the quality of straw-based feed. Among various strategies, the probiotics-enzyme composite technology—relying on the synergistic effects between exogenously added enzyme preparations and microbial agents—has been proven to more efficiently degrade fiber, increase crude protein content, and enhance fermentation quality, with effects significantly outperforming those of single microbial agent or enzyme preparation treatment [2]. Microbial fermentation stands as the core technology for improving the quality of roughage. Traditional ensiling or yellow silage fermentation relies primarily on natural microorganisms such as lactic acid bacteria attached to the raw materials, leading to an unstable process and limited improvement in silage quality [3]. In recent years, the bacteria-enzyme composite fermentation technology has attracted considerable attention owing to its synergistic enhancement fermentation technology, by exogenously adding specifically screened cellulases, hemicellulases and high-efficiency probiotic agents, can systematically deconstruct lignocellulose and convert it into nutrients that are easily digested and absorbed by animals [4]. Küçükgöz et al. [5] demonstrated that in probiotic fermentation systems, the synergy between enzymes and microorganisms enables more efficient release of bioactive substances from the substrates. Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated that the Probiotics-enzyme composite treatment outperforms the sole application of bacterial agents or enzymatic preparations in terms of fiber degradation, crude protein content enhancement, and aerobic stability improvement, with statistically significant differences.

With the advancement of molecular biology techniques, the understanding of fermentation processes has evolved from superficial observations to an in-depth exploration at the level of microbial community structure. Both domestic and international studies have consistently demonstrated that the fermentation system constitutes a dynamically successful microecosystem. Earlier research has demonstrated that during the Probiotics-enzyme composite fermentation of corn stover, the microbial community undergoes a regular succession: the initial stage is dominated by indigenous microorganisms from the raw material; as fermentation proceeds, the abundance of core functional microbial groups such as Firmicutes and Bacillus increases significantly. Their vigorous metabolic activities produce large quantities of organic acids, which rapidly lower the system pH, inhibit harmful microorganisms, and thereby create a stable acidic environment [6, 7]. Meanwhile, the study has identified microbial groups such as Burkholderiaceae and Rhizobiaceae that play key roles in nitrogen fixation or microbial protein synthesis, with these groups being responsible for the accumulation of crude protein. On the other hand, taxa including Desulfobacterota and Erwiniaceae have been confirmed to be closely associated with the effective degradation of fiber components [8]. Such studies have successfully established a microbial profile that clarifies which microbes are functioning during the fermentation process. Nevertheless, most existing studies have focused on the analysis of microbial community structure and its correlation with apparent nutritional indicators. In contrast, the intrinsic molecular drivers that propel this complex biological process—namely, the dynamic variation patterns of metabolites and their interaction mechanisms with microbial community succession—remain insufficiently elaborated systematically. The ultimate functional performance of microorganisms is manifested through their metabolites. Indeed, these metabolites—such as organic acids, antimicrobial peptides, vitamins, and the like—directly dictate the quality and functional properties of fermented feed [9]. Metabolomics, as a key branch of systems biology, enables the high-throughput and unbiased detection of global changes in small-molecule metabolites within biological systems, thereby directly reflecting the biochemical status and functional output of the system [10]. Analogous technologies have also been employed in studies on traditional fermented foods and some forages, shedding light on the association between metabolites and quality formation [11]. However, studies that deeply integrate metabolomics with microbiomics to systematically unravel the metabolic mechanisms throughout the entire process of Probiotics-enzyme composite fermentation of corn stover remain largely unexplored.

Thus, to bridge the cognitive gap from “microbial composition” to “functional output”, this study, building upon the prior completion of microbiomic analysis, further employed untargeted metabolomics technology to systematically track the dynamic changes in the metabolomic profile of corn stover during the combined Probiotics-enzyme fermentation on days 7, 14, 21, and 28. The present study aims to: characterize the dynamics of metabolites to comprehensively elucidate the temporal variation patterns of metabolite diversity and functional characteristics during the fermentation process; screen signature differential metabolites in different fermentation stages and elucidate their key metabolic pathways through KEGG enrichment analysis; thereby integrating microbiome and metabolome data, constructing an association model of microbial community and metabolic network, and ultimately elucidating the molecular ecological mechanism of the synergistic improvement of the feed quality of corn stover by the combination of bacteria and enzymes.

Materials and methods

Corn stover material

The corn stover employed in this experiment (cultivar: Demeiya No. 1) was harvested from Chaoyangchuan Town, Yanji City, Jilin Province in November 2022. Following natural air-drying, it had a moisture content of 15%. The stover was chopped into segments of 2–2.5 cm in length for subsequent use. Molasses was procured from the Feed Raw Material Market in Yitong Manchu Autonomous County, Siping City, Jilin Province.

Fermentation treatment

Into the corn stover was added 0.2% complex enzyme preparation (comprising cellulase, xylanase, lignin peroxidase, manganese peroxidase, and laccase) along with a complex microbial inoculant at a total inoculum size of 1 × 10⁸CFU/g, with the ratio of Lactobacillus: Saccharomyces: Bacillus subtilis set at 3:2:1. The mixture was thoroughly homogenized before being sprayed onto the surface of the corn stover. The treated corn stover was packed into fermentation bags with one-way exhaust valves (35 × 45 cm, made of food-grade seven-layer co-extruded nylon film PA/PE, with a thickness of 0.18 mm) and then vacuum-sealed using a vacuum packaging machine (Model: DZ-280/2SD). Each group was set with 12 fermentation bags as replicates and fermented at room temperature. On days 7, 14, 21, and 28 of fermentation, 3 bags were randomly selected from each group for sampling, which were sequentially labeled as groups MJ 7, MJ 14, MJ 21, and MJ 28.

Experimental design rationale

This study was designed to investigate the temporal metabolic dynamics during the probiotics-enzyme co-fermentation process, rather than to compare treatments. Therefore, no control groups (e.g., untreated, enzyme-only, or probiotics-only) were included. The efficacy and superiority of this combined treatment have been rigorously validated in our previous publications [12–14]. The present study builds upon these established findings by integrating metabolomics with previously published microbiome data to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this well-characterized system.

Microbial diversity analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from all fermented corn stover samples using a magnetic bead method. The samples were collected from fermentation packages at four time points, with each time point having three independent biological replicates. Afterwards, the purity and concentration of the DNA were checked by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. An appropriate amount of sample DNA was then transferred into a centrifuge tube and diluted to 1 ng/µL with sterile water. The DNA templates obtained above were used to identify the probiotics diversity using primers for the V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene (515 F: GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA and 806R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT). GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT) to identify probiotics diversity. ITS2 region primers (TS5-1737 F: GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC and fungal ITS2 region: TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATATGCV): to identify fungal diversity. All PCR mixes were added to 15 µL of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix. 0.2 µM primers and 10 ng of genomic DNA template were used for the first denaturation at 98 ℃for 1 min, followed by the first denaturation at 98 ℃(10 s), 50 ℃ (30 s), and 72 ℃ (30 s) for 30 cycles, and finally kept at 72 ℃ for 5 min. Agarose gel electrophoresis (2%) was used to verify the concentration of the PCR products and confirm the presence of the correct target bands. The library was constructed using the NEB Next® Ultra™ II FS DNA PCR-free Library Prep Kit. The constructed library was quantified using a Qubit and quantitative PCR (qPCR). After the library was qualified, NovaSeq 6000 was used for PE 250 online sequencing (Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd.). The sequenced sequences were processed by QIIME2 software for sequence decoding, primer trimming, quality filtering, and other data processing, and finally, the Amplicon Sequence Variant (ASV) was generated and aligned with the Greengens database to obtain the taxonomic information table and abundance data matrix of each ASV for analyzing the diversity of the microbial community [15].

Metabolite extraction and analysis

Weigh 100 mg of liquid nitrogen-ground samples and add 500 µL of 80% methanol aqueous solution. After thorough vortexing, the mixture was allowed to stand on ice for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 15,000×g and 4˚C for 20 min. The supernatant was collected and diluted with mass spectrometry-grade water to a final methanol concentration of 53%. After a second centrifugation step, the resulting supernatant was harvested for LC-MS analysis. QC samples and blank samples were processed following the same procedure.5.1.3.2 Instrument Parameters.

Chromatographic and mass spectrometric conditions

The chromatographic column employed was Hypersil Gold C18, with a column temperature maintained at 40˚C and a flow rate set at 0.2 mL·min⁻¹. For the positive ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of phase A (0.1% formic acid) and phase B (methanol). In the negative ion mode, mobile phase A was 5 mM ammonium acetate (pH 9.0), and mobile phase B was methanol. The gradient elution program is detailed in Table 6. For mass spectrometry, the scan range was adjusted to 100–1500 m/z, the electrospray ionization (ESI) spray voltage was set at 3.5 kV, the sheath gas flow rate was 35 psi, the auxiliary gas flow rate was 10 L·min⁻¹, and the ion transfer tube temperature was maintained at 320˚C.

Metabolomics data processing

Raw LC-MS/MS data were converted to the open-source mzXML format using ProteoWizard (version 3.0.8). Peak detection, alignment, and integration were performed using the XCMS package (version 3.12.0) in R environment (version 4.1.2).

Statistical analysis

Multivariate analysis was performed using SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Umeå, Sweden). Principal component analysis (PCA) was applied to visualize sample distribution and identify outliers. Subsequently, orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was used to maximize separation between experimental groups. Model validity was assessed using a 200-iteration permutation test, and model performance was evaluated by the cumulative R² (goodness of fit) and Q² (goodness of prediction).

Differentially abundant metabolites were screened based on a combination of fold change (|FC| > 1.2), variable importance in projection (VIP ≥ 1) from the OPLS-DA model, and p-value (p < 0.05) from a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Volcano plots were generated for visualization using the “ggplot2” package (version 3.3.5) in R.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to assess associations between metabolites and microbial taxa. Correlation coefficients and p-values were calculated using the “Hmisc” package (version 4.6-0) in R, and the results were visualized using heatmaps generated with the “pheatmap” package (version 1.0.12) incorporating hierarchical clustering based on Euclidean distance and complete linkage. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. Significantly altered metabolites were subsequently subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes, http://www.genome.jp/kegg/) using Fisher’s exact test.

Results and analysis

Effect of combined bacterial-enzyme fermentation on microbial community and fermentation characteristics

A LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) multi-level species differential analysis was conducted on the microbial community during the Probiotics-enzyme composite fermentation of corn stover. The LEfSe results showed that on day 7 of fermentation, 19 bacterial taxa exhibited significantly higher abundance compared to other groups: Enterobacteriaceae, Actinobacteriota, Sphingobacteriaceae, Sphingobacteriales, Sphingobacterium, Micrococcales, Novosphingobium, Brevibacteriaceae, Brevibacterium, Dubosiella_newyorkensis, Nocardiopsaceae, Parapedobacter, Methylobacterium _Methylorubrum, Promicromonosporaceae, Promicromonospora, Acidimicrobiia, Demequinaceae, Demequina, and Cellulomonadaceae. Analysis of the multilevel species hierarchy tree revealed that the main taxa contributing to the differences on day 7 were Acidimicrobiia, Brevibacteriaceae, Cellulomonadaceae, Promicromonosporaceae, Demequinaceae, Micrococcales, Nocardiopsaceae, Sphingobacteriaceae, Sphingobacteriales, and Enterobacteriaceae. On day 14, eight bacterial taxa were significantly enriched: Rhizobiales, Rhizobiaceae, Neorhizobium_Pararhizobium_Rhizobium, Microbacterium, Dermabacteraceae, Brachybacterium, Aureimonas, and Sphingomonas_phyllosphaerae. Multilevel species hierarchy analysis indicated that the key taxa driving the differences on day 14 were Dermabacteraceae, Rhizobiales, and Rhizobiaceae. On day 21, 12 bacterial taxa were significantly enriched: Bacilli, Firmicutes, Bacillaceae, Bacillales, Bacillus_thermoamylovorans, Lactobacillales, Streptococcaceae, Streptococcus, Pediococcus, Paenibacillus_barengoltzii, Bacillus coagulans, and Ornithinibacillus. Multilevel species hierarchy analysis showed that the primary taxa responsible for the differences on day 21 were Bacillales, Bacillaceae, Bacilli, Lactobacillales, and Streptococcaceae. On day 28, the significantly enriched bacterial taxa were Parasutterella and Parabacteroides. Regarding fungal communities, no significant differences were observed on day 14. On day 7, 17 fungal taxa were significantly enriched: Saccharomycetales, Saccharomycetes, Millerozyma farinosa, Millerozyma, Debaryomycetaceae, Saccharomyces, Saccharomycetaceae, Sordariomycetes, Dothideomycetes, Hypocreales, Nectriaceae, Fusarium, Pleosporales, Cladosporiaceae, Capnodiales, Cladosporium, and Stachybotryaceae. On day 21, one fungal taxon was significantly enriched: Ascomycota. On day 28, five fungal taxa were significantly enriched: Monascus, Eurotiomycetidae ord incertae sedis, Monascaceae, Monascus_purpureus, and Eurotiomycetes. Specifically, Desulfobacterota was negatively associated with acid detergent fiber (ADF), and Proteobacteria displayed a significantly negative correlation with crude protein (CP) (p < 0.01).

At the family level, Bacillaceae exhibited a negative correlation with pH, whereas Enterobacteriaceae, Sphingomonadaceae, Comamonadaceae, and Bacteroidaceae showed a positive association with pH. Xanthomonadaceae was negatively correlated with crude protein (CP), while Lachnospiraceae displayed positive correlations with lactic acid (LA) and dry matter (DM). At the family level, Debaryomycetaceae, Nectriaceae, Cladosporiaceae, Pleosporaceae, and Mycosphaerellaceae were positively associated with pH. Metabolic profile characteristics

A total of 1750 metabolites were identified, with 1080 detected in positive ion mode and 670 in negative ion mode. Of these, 1329 metabolites were qualitatively characterized and grouped into 14 categories (Fig. 3A). The major categories included lipids and lipid-like molecules (393 compounds), organic acids and their derivatives (206 compounds), phenylpropanoids and polyketides (193 compounds), benzoic acids (114 compounds), and organic heterocyclic compounds (176 compounds), among others.

KEGG pathway annotation revealed that 618 metabolites were enriched in 14 pathways (Fig. 3B), with global metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites as the dominant categories.

Composition of differential metabolites

During the 7–14 d fermentation period, multiple peptides and amino acid derivatives were significantly upregulated (Table 7). Notably, dipeptides such as L-Leucyl-L-alanine Hydrate (FC = 8.50), Ala-Val (FC = 7.42), and Ala-Leu (FC = 6.91), along with tripeptides like L-Gly-Tyr-Ala (FC = 8.33), showed dramatic increases. In contrast, metabolites associated with energy storage and cellular structures, including specific lipids (e.g., FAHFA (18:1/20:3), FC = 0.03) and nucleosides (e.g., Adenosine, FC = 0.04), were significantly downregulated.

Discussion

Investigation of metabolite diversity and its functional traits

Corn stover subjected to Probiotics-enzyme synergistic fermentation technology exhibits pronounced metabolomic alterations, with a marked enhancement in the diversity of its metabolites [6]. In this study, 1750 metabolites were detected and annotated altogether. Of these, lipids and lipid-like molecules comprised 393 compounds, organic acids and their derivatives 206 compounds, phenylpropanoids and polyketides 185 compounds, and organic heterocyclic compounds 162 compounds. This finding echoes the observations of Zhao et al. [7]who explored the fermentation characteristics and microbial community composition of mixed silage composed of wet brewer’s grains and corn stover, produced with the addition of cellulase and lactic acid bacteria supplements. It suggests that synergistic fermentation can markedly enhance the richness of metabolite composition.

This enhancement of metabolite diversity results from the synergistic action between exogenous enzyme preparations and the microbial community. The disruption of the plant cell wall by exogenous cellulases and xylanases releases encapsulated nutrients and generates oligosaccharides and reducing sugars, which serve as precursors for subsequent microbial secondary metabolism [16, 17].These bioactive small-molecule compounds exert multiple physiological functions in animal nutrition: lipids not only serve as efficient energy sources but also act as key components of cell membranes. Notably, the contents of unsaturated fatty acids—such as linoleic acid and α-linolenic acid—have increased significantly, a change that is of great significance for improving the quality of animal products [18, 19], This aligns with the findings reported by Bazzano et al. [20]in their research on dairy animal nutrition. Organic acids are effective in regulating the balance of intestinal microflora and enhancing the absorption of nutrients. Among the short-chain fatty acids identified in this study—including lactic acid, acetic acid, and propionic acid—they play a pivotal role in sustaining intestinal health, Jabłońska-Ryś et al. [21]demonstrated the beneficial effects of organic acids on digestive tract health in their research on lactic acid fermentation. Phenolic compounds possess marked antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, and the flavonoids and phenolic acids identified in the present study exhibited a notable increase in their contents, Billowria et al. [22]provided a detailed account of the health-promoting effects of these compounds in their comprehensive review focusing on bioactive flavonoids: pharmacokinetics and analysis. Of particular note, the present study also identified a variety of metabolites with unique bioactivities, such as γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and other neuroactive compounds, which hold potential for alleviating stress responses in animals. Based on the microbial community succession characteristics and Probiotics-enzyme correlation analysis in this fermentation system, the biosynthesis of GABA and other neuroactive substances was primarily mediated by the core functional taxa of Firmicutes, specifically Lactobacillus and Bacillus from the family Bacillaceae—these taxa became the dominant microbial populations during the mid-to-late fermentation stages (14–28 d). Lactobacillus and Bacillus are well-recognized for their capacity to synthesize GABA via the glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) pathway, and the GAD enzymes encoded by these strains display high catalytic activity under acidic conditions [23]. Notably, this acidic microenvironment is precisely shaped by the organic acids produced through the metabolic activity of Firmicutes themselves, thus forming a positive feedback loop that facilitates sustained GABA synthesis [24]. In the early fermentation stage (7–14 d), the vigorous metabolism of peptides and amino acids driven by indigenous microorganisms and exogenous inoculants provided an abundant supply of glutamate—the key precursor for GABA biosynthesis [25], laying a solid material foundation for the subsequent massive accumulation of GABA in the system. Furthermore, the various alkaloids and terpenoids identified in this study exhibit promising antibacterial and immunomodulatory activities. This enrichment of the metabolite profile has comprehensively enhanced the nutritional value and functionality of the fermented product, laying a solid material foundation for the development of high-quality functional feed.

Patterns of dynamic changes in metabolites during fermentation

Employing untargeted metabolomics combined with multivariate statistical analysis, this study systematically elucidated the patterns of dynamic changes in metabolites during fermentation. The number of differential metabolites exhibits distinct temporal variation patterns: as fermentation proceeds, the count drops from 494 differential metabolites at 7–14 days to 193 at 21–28 days. Such a declining trend indicates that the metabolic activities of microorganisms gradually stabilize, reflecting the maturation of the microbial ecosystem [26]. This variation pattern matches well with the findings of Rong et al. [27]from their untargeted metabolomic analysis of non-volatile metabolites, with both exhibiting the feature that the metabolite profile stabilizes in the late stage of fermentation.

Via hierarchical clustering analysis and principal component analysis, we observed that metabolites of different categories exhibit distinct phase-specific variation patterns. Among these, compositional changes in peptides were most pronounced during the middle fermentation stage (7–14 days), with the contents of small-molecule peptides—including dipeptides and tripeptides—showing a marked increase. This is primarily attributable to the hydrolysis of residual proteins in corn stover by extracellular proteases secreted by raw material-associated microorganisms and exogenous yeasts during the initial fermentation phase, while Bacillus and other microorganisms in their exponential growth phase also require substantial uptake of exogenous nitrogen sources [28, 29]. This aligns with the observations of Aksoy et al. [30], who investigated the dynamics of peptides released from yogurt microorganism-fermented milk during fermentation and storage. Peptides, as protein hydrolysates, accumulate substantially during the middle fermentation stage. During the middle-late stage (14–21 days), changes in organic acids become the dominant feature, with the accumulation of short-chain fatty acids—particularly lactic acid and acetic acid—peaking. This is closely associated with Firmicutes, including Bacillales and Lactobacillales, becoming the dominant taxa during this phase; they convert monosaccharides released in the early stage into organic acids through the glycolytic pathway, rapidly lowering the pH [31]. Jabłońska-Ryś et al. [21]reported a comparable phenomenon in their research on the kinetics of lactic acid fermentation. In the final stage of fermentation (21–28 days), secondary metabolites emerged as the predominant differential components, with the contents of flavonoids and alkaloids showing a notable elevation. Under conditions of fermentable sugar depletion and increased environmental stress, the core microorganisms initiate secondary metabolism, synthesizing antioxidant compounds and signaling molecules to adapt to the environment [32]. This accounts for the significant enrichment of pathways such as “phenylpropanoid biosynthesis” and “glutathione metabolism” in the later stage, as revealed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Balli et al. [33]noted in their investigation into whether fermentation enhances phenolic content in grains that such compounds are typically synthesized in substantial amounts during the late fermentation stage. This temporal variation pattern exhibits a strong correlation with the succession of microbial communities within the fermentation system [34]. Correlation analysis revealed that dynamic shifts in metabolites were significantly associated with fluctuations in the abundance of specific microbial taxa, demonstrating that metabolite production arises from the synergistic interactions of the microbial community.

Dynamic evolution patterns of metabolic pathways

Leveraging pathway enrichment analysis based on the KEGG database, this study systematically delineated the phased transition patterns of metabolic pathways during fermentation. In the early stage of fermentation (0–7 days), pathways were predominantly enriched in nucleotide metabolism—such as purine metabolism and pyrimidine metabolism—and energy metabolism pathways including unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis. This reflects the metabolic characteristics of microorganisms during their rapid growth phase, characterized by high demand for nucleic acid precursors and energy [35]. Alongside these, pathways related to protein synthesis, such as aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, also exhibited marked activity, This aligns with the findings of Swietalski et al. [36]regarding the production of orotic acid by Yarrowia lipolytica under pyrimidine-limited conditions, which reflects the metabolic characteristics of microorganisms during their rapid growth phase.

With the progression of fermentation (7–21 days), the metabolic focus gradually transitions toward secondary metabolic pathways—including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and flavonoid biosynthesis—as well as pathways for antioxidant synthesis such as glutathione metabolism. This transition indicates that following biomass accumulation, metabolic flux shifts toward secondary metabolism associated with environmental adaptation and interspecific competition [37]. Notably, the marked enrichment of the biosynthesis pathways for phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan lays a metabolic foundation for the substantial synthesis of aromatic amino acids and their derivatives, revealing the coupling between nitrogen metabolism and secondary metabolism. This is in agreement with the findings of Chen et al. [38]who carried out research on solid-state fermentation of corn stover for fermented feed production using synthetic microbial communities.

During the late fermentation stage (21–28 days), pathways including terpenoid backbone biosynthesis and the biosynthesis of diverse plant secondary metabolites exhibited marked enrichment. Meanwhile, the glutathione metabolism pathway—linked to stress responses—maintained sustained activity. This evolution of metabolic pathways mirrors the systematic refinement of substrate utilization strategies by the microbial community, from initial substrate degradation and biomass accumulation, through intermediate energy metabolism and environmental acidification, to the later-stage synthesis of functional molecules and stress adaptation [39], and serves as a pivotal mechanism underlying the formation of functional properties in the fermented product. Notably, the pronounced enrichment of the glutathione metabolism pathway in the late stage corroborates the findings of Shumaev et al. [40]regarding the synthesis of antioxidants, demonstrating that the enhanced antioxidant capacity of the fermented product has a clear metabolic basis.

Mechanism of microbe-metabolite synergistic interactions

By integrating metagenomic and metabolomic data, this study provides an in-depth elucidation of the synergistic interaction network between microbes and metabolites during fermentation. In the early fermentation stage (7–14 days), the synergistic actions of exogenously supplemented lactic acid bacteria, Bacillus spp.and Aspergillus spp. Predominantly modulate the hydrolysis of peptides and the conversion of polyols. The expression of numerous proteolytic enzymes and carbohydrate-active enzymes detected during this period lays an enzymatic foundation for the degradation of proteins and cellulose [41, 42]. Correlation analysis revealed that multiple unclassified bacterial families showed positive correlations with dipeptides, while Oxalobacteraceae was positively correlated with organic acids, confirming the involvement of multiple microbial taxa in the complex hydrolytic processes during the early stage [43]. Su et al. [44]highlighted the significance of synergy among multiple microbial strains in their research on dipeptide biosynthesis. Upon entering the middle-late stage, the microbial community structure exhibited remarkable shifts—indigenous microorganisms such as Firmicutes and Bacillaceae gradually became dominant, governing the biosynthesis of organic acids and the transformation of secondary metabolites, including polyphenols and alkaloids. Correlation network analysis revealed tight functional associations between specific genera and metabolites: for instance, Lactobacillus exhibited a positive correlation with lactic acid synthesis, while Bacillus was strongly associated with the production of various antimicrobial peptides. Notably, the association patterns of Firmicutes with metabolites differed markedly across stages: during 7–14 days, they showed negative correlations with various lipids, reflecting their consumption as energy substrates; during 21–28 days, they exhibited positive correlations with organic acids and amino acids, reflecting their predominant role in anabolic metabolism. This reveals the functional versatility of the same phylum across different ecological niches—from initial nutrient assimilation to later-stage biosynthesis and environmental modification [45]. Buntić et al. [46]in their research on Bacillus strains that produce antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) and are employed for managing fungal infections and promoting alfalfa growth, also observed a similar pattern.

The coevolution of microbial communities and metabolic networks constitutes the core mechanism for enhancing the feeding quality of fermentation products. Notably, the accumulation of organic acids and secondary metabolites during the middle and late stages of fermentation exhibited a significant positive correlation with the abundance of Firmicutes and Bacillaceae (correlation coefficient r > 0.8, p < 0.01). Ascomycota showed positive correlations with organic acids and amino acids during 14–21 days, but negative correlations with glycosides and nucleosides, reflecting fungal metabolic strategies involving glycoside hydrolysis for energy acquisition while simultaneously releasing or synthesizing amino acids that participate in cross-feeding interactions [47]. This finding not only confirms the critical role of specific microorganisms in metabolite synthesis but also offers a valuable theoretical basis for optimizing fermentation processes through regulating the composition of microbial communities. Küçükgöz et al. [48]likewise observed such a synergistic effect between microorganisms and metabolites in their studies on probiotic fermentation, arguing that this synergy constitutes the key to enhancing the quality of fermented products. Furthermore, the present study further revealed that interactions among microorganisms also exert a significant impact on the synthesis of metabolites. For instance, certain microorganisms may influence the metabolic activity of other microorganisms by producing signal molecules or metabolites, and this complex network of microbial interactions collectively determines the composition and functional properties of the final metabolites. While this study elucidates the dynamic metabolite succession driven by the bacteria-enzyme composite, the individual contributions of enzymatic pretreatment versus subsequent microbial fermentation to the observed profile remain to be disentangled. Future studies incorporating controlled comparisons with enzyme-only and bacteria-only treatments will help delineate their specific roles and synergistic effects.

conclusion

In this study, metabolomics was employed to systematically analyze the dynamic changes in metabolites during the probiotics-enzyme composite fermentation of corn stover over 28 days (days 7, 14, 21, and 28). The results revealed a distinct temporal succession in the metabolomic profile: peptide metabolism dominated the early stage (7–14 days), organic acids and lipids accumulated predominantly during the middle stage (14–21 days), and the synthesis of vitamins and secondary metabolites characterized the late stage (21–28 days). This study delineates the temporal metabolic dynamics within the probiotics-enzyme composite fermentation system, providing foundational insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying this bioconversion process.
