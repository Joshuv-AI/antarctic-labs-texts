Membrane-bound TRAIL-armoring augments CAR-T cells in mesothelin-positive solid malignancies
Authors: Alessia Volpe, Juan Zurita, Larissa Shenker, Mayuresh M Mane, Prasad S Adusumilli, Alexander Aleshin, Dmitri Rozanov, Vladimir Ponomarev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesothelin (MSLN)-targeted chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy shows an effective and long-lasting response in high MSLN-expressing tumors, but fails to treat tumors with heterogeneous levels of antigen expression. Here we describe an innovative approach where a stable tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) chimera, referred to as membrane-bound (MB)-TRAIL, is expressed on the cell surface of MSLN-specific (M28z) CAR-T cells. This potentiates their overall anticancer activity by overcoming heterogeneous antigen expression in tumor lesions.MethodsWe engineered MB-TRAIL-armored MSLN-directed M28z CAR-T cells and assessed their in vitro toxicity using flow cytometry against tumor target cells with heterogeneous MSLN expression. Triple-multiplex bioluminescence imaging using three different reporter genes enabled simultaneous in vivo non-invasive and longitudinal assessment of CAR-T cell dynamics, as well as MSLN+ and MSLN− tumor responses in xenograft models of MSTO-211H pleural mesothelioma (PM) and A549 non-small cell-lung cancer (NSCLC) with heterogeneous antigen expression. We further tested the therapeutic benefit of combining MB-TRAIL-armored CAR-T cells with the clinically used TRAIL sensitizer, doxorubicin, in an A549 xenograft model.ResultsMB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cell therapy provides pro-apoptotic stimuli to both MSLN+ and MSLN− tumors, significantly improving survival in models of MSTO-211H PM and A549 NSCLC with heterogeneous antigen expression. We also demonstrated that our treatment confers sustained protection against metastatic spread in the MSTO-211H model, whereas doxorubicin overcomes TRAIL resistance rendering the A549 model more susceptible to MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cell therapy and reducing metastatic growth.ConclusionsOur strategy confers superior antitumor efficacy relative to clinically trialed M28z CAR-T cells by directly targeting heterogeneous tumor antigen expression. These results constitute the foundation for developing a new class of clinically translatable TRAIL-armored CAR-T cell immunotherapies for the treatment of a wider range of malignancies.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Introduction

Mesothelin (MSLN) is a cell-surface tumor-associated antigen highly expressed in pleural mesothelioma (PM) and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)1 and a marker of tumor aggressiveness associated with recurrence and decreased overall survival.2 Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated that MSLN is a clinically relevant cancer antigen, hence successfully targetable with immunotherapy in multiple solid tumors.13 Targeted immunotherapy employing MSLN-directed chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells is an effective treatment option in high MSLN-expressing in vivo models and patients with PM.4 5 However, it fails to treat tumors with heterogeneous MSLN expression, ultimately promoting relapse.6

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL; TNFSF10) is a TNF family member7 8 and a mediator of tumor-specific apoptosis while sparing normal cells. It binds as a homotrimer to death receptors DR4 (death receptor 4; TNFRSF10A) and DR5 (death receptor 5; TNFRSF10B) triggering receptor trimerization and formation of the death-inducing signaling complex.9 10 Its tremendous potential for cancer treatment led to the generation of different classes of TRAIL-based therapies, including recombinant human TRAIL (rhTRAIL) and agonistic antibodies against DR4 and DR5.10 Despite their initial promise, they all posed limited successes in clinical trials as a monotherapy. The main limitation of rhTRAIL is likely to be attributed to the short half-life in the bloodstream (only 30 min in non-human primates).11 While the development of an arsenal of humanized and fully human agonistic antibodies against the relevant death receptors (with a half-life of several days) circumvented this problem, the majority of patients showed no changes in overall survival.10 One possible explanation for this limited response is the inability of dimeric agonistic antibodies to induce trimerization of DR4 and DR5, thus failing to elicit a clustering-dependent intracellular pro-apoptotic signaling.12 In addition, the suboptimal efficacy of agonistic antibodies targeting DR4/DR5 in solid tumors may be limited by the heterogeneous tumor penetration, which impairs effective receptor clustering. Because TRAIL is only active as a homotrimer,13 previous work was focused on improving its stability and extending its half-life compared with natural TRAIL and rhTRAIL, thereby potentiating its pro-apoptotic activity. One of these approaches introduced a modified yeast GCN4 leucine zipper (LZ) motif at the N-terminus of TRAIL to stabilize TRAIL quaternary structure.14 15 GCN4 LZ is a 33 amino acid peptide engineered through substitutions at the “a” and “d” positions to form a stable trimeric structure.16 Subsequently, a fully human and therefore less immunogenic ATF7 LZ was used to stabilize TRAIL trimers and exhibited prolonged stability.17 In our studies, we employed this ATF7-TRAIL-LZ chimera, referred to as membrane-bound (MB)-TRAIL, which carries additional substitutions at the “a” and “d” positions to stabilize the homotrimers, thus resulting in enhanced antitumor activity.18

Several lines of evidence suggest that TRAIL could serve as an attractive therapeutic molecule to improve outcomes in patients with mesothelioma and lung cancer. TRAIL receptors DR4 and DR5 are highly expressed in human histological samples of stage III human NSCLC,19 and treatment with rhTRAIL induces apoptosis both in vitro and in xenograft models.2022 However, it has become clear that a greater efficacy of treatment may only be achieved by either affecting multiple targets or through combination therapy. As new directions in CAR-T cell engineering aim to enhance CAR efficacy and address tumor antigen heterogeneity, armoring CAR-T cells with TRAIL offers an attractive strategy to overcome the current limitations of both CAR-T cell- and TRAIL-based monotherapies by combining their individual therapeutic strengths.

In the present study, we propose a promising strategy enabling tumor sensitization to CAR-T cell therapy based on MB-TRAIL-armored MSLN-directed CAR-T cells and investigated their therapeutic efficacy against MSLN+ and MSLN− tumors in xenograft models of MSTO-211H PM and A549 NSCLC with heterogeneous MSLN expression. Expressing MB-TRAIL on CAR-T cells allows constitutive, tumor-localized delivery while overcoming the limitations of rapid clearance and enabling effective tumor cell killing. Non-invasive monitoring of CAR-T and tumor cell fate can be achieved using an indirect labeling strategy via stable reporter gene expression, providing reliable, quantitative, and long-lasting in vivo detection capabilities.23 To evaluate our strategy, we employed triple-multiplex bioluminescence imaging (BLI) using three different reporter genes, enabling simultaneous in vivo non-invasive and repeated monitoring of MSLN+ and MSLN− tumors as well as CAR-T cells within the same animal.24

Methods

Viral constructs

The development of the MSLN-specific second-generation (M28z) CAR into an SFG gamma retroviral vector derived from Friend Murine Leukemia Virus (F-MuLV) was previously described.4 ATF7-TRAIL-LZ TRAIL chimera (see online supplemental material), also referred to as MB-TRAIL, containing a leucine zipper trimerization motif was cloned into an SFG retroviral backbone and linked to M28z CAR via a porcine teschovirus-1 2A (P2A) self-cleavage peptide sequence.25 To generate a control construct, the MB-TRAIL sequence was replaced with either an Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) sequence or a human TRAIL (h-TRAIL) sequence corresponding to TNFSF10 isoform 1 (derived from the pUNO1-hTNFSF10a plasmid, InvivoGen) followed by P2A and the M28z CAR. Successful cloning was confirmed by DNA Sanger sequencing (Genewiz; for sequencing primers see online supplemental table 1). The maCluc reporter gene with P2A and Green Fluorescent Protein (GFP) fluorescence tag was previously described.24 All used constructs are depicted in figure 1A. All SFG-based helper-free retroviruses were transfected into 293T RD 114 producer line (gift of Dr Michel Sadelain, Center for Cell Engineering, Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC)) in order to generate high-titer viral supernatant for T-cell transduction.

Human T-cell isolation and transduction

Buffy coats of healthy volunteer donors were obtained from the New York Blood Bank with previous informed consent. Human T lymphocytes were isolated under an institutional review board-approved protocol. On the same day, T cells were activated with anti-CD3/CD28 Dynabeads (4×107/mL; 1:3 T cell:bead ratio) and maintained in the presence of interleukin (IL)-2 (20 U/mL; Proleukin-Novartis). 2 days post stimulation with beads, 1×106/well T cells were plated on 6-well plates pre-coated with retronectin (50 µg/µL; TakaraBio) and retroviral supernatant containing the desired M28z CAR constructs (with and without MB-TRAIL) was added for the transduction. Both transduced and non-transduced T cells were kept in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) growth medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum, 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin and IL-15 (10 ng/mL; R&D Systems), which was added every 3 days. CAR-T cells were re-stimulated with MSLN-positive MSTO-211H cells at a tumor-to-CAR-T ratio of 1:40 for 24 hours, 7 days after the initial stimulation. 2 days later, cells were further transduced with the maCLuc-P2A-GFP BLI-fluorescence reporter using a two-step retroviral protocol to maximize transduction efficiency. Total in vitro expansion following T-cell isolation was approximately 14 days prior to in vitro functional assays and in vivo administration. CAR/GFP expression and T-cell viability were assessed 4 days after each transduction cycle and immediately prior to experimentation (online supplemental figure 4). Transduction efficiency of single and/or double transduced bulk CAR-T cells was monitored by flow cytometric analysis as described in the section below. Consistent with clinically relevant manufacturing workflows,26 no purification/selection was performed.

Flow cytometric analysis of CAR-T cells

1×105 cells were washed two times in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) prior followed by resuspension in Fluorescence-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS pH 7.4 containing 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS)). CAR cell surface expression was assessed by staining with the protein-L antibody (biotinylated, 0.5 µg/mL; GenScript #M00097, RRID: AB_3717790) recognizing the kappa light chain of the variable light(VL) domain of the M28z CAR. A streptavidin conjugated to allophycocyanin(APC) (0.2 µg/mL; BioLegend #405207) was used as a secondary antibody. Transduction of newly engineered CAR-T cells with maCluc BLI reporter was assessed using GFP fluorescence. CAR-T cell activation was assessed by surface staining for CD69 and analyzed on live CAR+CAR T cells 14 days post T-cell isolation and prior to in vitro or in vivo experiments. Cells were stained with anti-human CD69 (mouse; 0.5 µg/mL, BD Biosciences, #555531, RRID: AB_395916). Relevant cytokine expression of CAR+cells was assessed by intracellular staining using a Cyto-Fast Fix/Perm buffer set (BioLegend #426803). Experimental design was identical to monolayer killing assay. In addition, CAR-T cells were treated for 12 hours with brefeldin A (BioLegend #420601) and stained with antibodies specific for interferon (IFN)-γ (clone B27, 0.5 µg/mL; conjugated to BV421, BioLegend #506538, RRID: AB_2801098) and TNF-α (clone Mab11, 0.5 µg/mL; conjugated to PE, BioLegend #502909, RRID: AB_315261). Stimulation with phorbol myristate acetate (PMA) and ionomycin (Invitrogen) constituted the positive control. Antibodies used to assess CD4 and CD8 ratio of CAR-T cells in the in vitro killing assay were anti-human CD4 (mouse monoclonal, clone SK3; 0.5 µg/mL, BioLegend, #344604; RRID: AB_1937227) conjugated with FITC, anti-human CD8 (mouse monoclonal, clone HIT8a; 0.5 µg/mL, BioLegend, #300908, RRID: AB_314112) conjugated with phycoerythrin (PE). All profiles were acquired using an LSRFortessa II (BD Bioscience, USA) equipped with a FACSDiva analysis software. Data analysis was performed using FCS Express V.7 (V.7.08.0018).

CAR-T cell-based in vitro killing assay

5×104 MSTO-211H and A549 mTurquoise2 CBG68 MSLN+ and td-Tomato rs-Rluc MSLN− cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated overnight. On the next day, CAR-T cells (in T-cell medium supplemented with 10 ng/mL IL-15) were added at the indicated target-to-effector (T:E) ratios. A non-relevant (prostate-specific membrane antigen (PSMA)-targeting)27 CAR was used to validate the absence of non-specific tumor cell killing. Where indicated, doxorubicin (NovaPlus) and bortezomib (EMD Millipore) were added to target cells 24 hours prior to the addition of CAR-T cells. After co-culture for 24, 48 and 72 hours, effector and target cells (both floating and attached) were collected from the wells, washed two times with PBS and stained for apoptotic and cell death markers (see online supplemental methods). Cell death was measured by flow cytometric analysis. Effector cells were excluded from the analysis based on size and granularity (forward scatter/side scatter (FSC/SSC) gating).

Xenograft models

All in vivo studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, protocol no. 05–07-012) at MSKCC. We used the ARRIVE V.2.0 checklist when writing our report.28 NOD SCID gamma IL2rg−/− young adult male mice (5–6 weeks old, The Jackson Laboratory USA, RRID: IMSR_JAX:005557) were housed at the MSKCC Research Animal Resource Center, maintained under sterile conditions and with food and water ad libitum. A mixture of MSLN+/mTurquoise2+ and MSLN−/td-Tomato+MSTO-211H and A549 cancer cells co-expressing CBG68 and rs-Rluc BLI reporters (1×106 MSLN+: 5×105 MSLN−; 2:1 ratio) were intraperitoneally and intravenously injected, respectively. In the tolerability studies, mice received doxorubicin intraperitoneally (0.1, 0.25 or 0.5 µg/g) once MSLN+ tumors reached ~ 1×107 photons/second (p/s). Tumor engraftment and progression were monitored by non-invasive long-term BLI prior to randomization. In the therapy studies, tumor-bearing animals were randomly assigned to treatment groups according to in vivo BLI signal to ensure comparable tumor burden across cohorts. Tumor burden continued to be monitored for the duration of the studies. In the MSTO-211H model, a single intraperitoneal dose of 5×106 CAR-T cells was administered. Experimental groups were: tumor-bearing controls (N=8); M28z CAR-T (N=5); MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T (N=9). In the A549 model, doxorubicin treatment began when MSLN+ tumors reached ~1.5×107 p/s. 24 hours after doxorubicin administration (9 days post tumor establishment), a single dose of 5×106 CAR-T cells was systemically administered. Experimental groups were: tumor-bearing controls±doxorubicin (N=3 each); M28z CAR-T±doxorubicin (N=10 each); MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T±doxorubicin (N=12 each). A total of 72 mice were used in these studies. No formal power calculation was conducted. Group sizes were determined based on historical data from both xenograft models, published studies using similar endpoints, and feasibility considerations. The numbers used were adequate to detect biologically meaningful differences. Investigators were not blinded to group allocation or at any point of the therapy studies. However, tumor burden was quantified using objective, instrument-generated bioluminescence measurements (tumor flux) obtained using standardized acquisition parameters. Mice were monitored daily for signs of adverse effects and weighed one to two times a week. Humane endpoints, established a priori, included significant weight loss (≥20% of total weight), pain or distress, vocalization, peritoneal distension, or hyperventilation, at which point animals were euthanized. No animals were excluded during the experiments unless removal was required as humane endpoints were reached, as defined by the IACUC. BLI-based tumor flux and survival were used as primary endpoints.

In vivo triple-multiplex BLI and image-based analysis

To perform sequential in vivo BLI, CAR-T cells, MSLN+ and MSLN− cancer cells were engineered to express membrane-anchored Cypridina (maCluc:Vargulin), Click Beetle Green 68 (CBG68:D-Luciferin) and red-shifted Renilla (rs-Rluc:Coelenterazine), respectively. These non-cross-reactive reporter:substrate pairs enabled long-term in vivo BLI imaging of all three cell populations in the same animal. Each reporter was imaged sequentially within a 24 hours period and with ≥6-hour intervals to allow substrate clearance and minimize residual signal given that spectral unmixing was not performed in this study.24 Vargulin for maCluc was administered retro-orbitally (0.1 µg/µL; Nanolight Technology, 305–500), D-luciferin for CBG68 intraperitoneally (33.3 µg/µL; Gold Biotechnology, eLUCK-1G), and Coelenterazine for rs-Rluc retro-orbitally (0.2 µg/µL; Biotium, 10 111–1). Before every imaging session, mice were anesthetized with 2% (v/v) isoflurane (Piramal Critical Care, Pennsylvania, USA)/O2 and imaged ventrally using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) Spectrum (Perkin Elmer) at the MSKCC Small Animal Imaging Core. Acquisition time was adjusted to signal intensity of the three different reporters. Images were analyzed using Living Image Software (PerkinElmer; RRID: SCR_014247) and measurements were reported in p/s.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean±SD values of n≥3 biological replicates unless otherwise stated and sample sizes are detailed in figure legends. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism V.9.3.1 software (GraphPad, RRID: SCR_002798). Statistical significance was calculated using a Student’s t-test unless otherwise stated. Statistical differences were indicated as: ns p>0.05; *p≤0.05; **p≤0.01; ***p≤0.001; ****p≤0.0001. Exact p values are reported in online supplemental tables 3-14.

Results

Feasibility of TRAIL-based therapy in MSTO-211H PM and A549 NSCLC

Screening of multiple cancer lines for rhTRAIL sensitivity defined thresholds to classify them as sensitive, moderately sensitive, or resistant (online supplemental figure 1). We generated MSTO-211H PM and A549 NSCLC cells co-expressing MSLN+/mTurquoise2+fused to CBG68 and MSLN−/td-Tomato+fused to rs-Rluc (online supplemental figure 2). These cells were tested for DR4 and DR5 expression (figure 1A, B, E, F) and exposed to increasing concentrations of rhTRAIL (figure 1C, D, G, H). This treatment resulted in a dose-dependent in vitro cancer cell death in both cell lines. MSTO-211H cells were highly sensitive, with >40% cell death at a dose as low as 10 ng/mL of rhTRAIL within 24 hours, which further increased over time (figure 1C, D). In contrast, A549 cells required ≥50 ng/mL of rhTRAIL to achieve detectable cell killing, which was not durable over the time (figure 1G, H), consistent with the short half-life of rhTRAIL.10 This data also agrees with previous reports.20 21 Notably, despite high DR5 expression (cf. figure 1E, F), A549 cells thus still remain resistant to TRAIL-induced apoptosis, suggesting that factors other than the sole DR5 expression influence TRAIL sensitivity in this cell line.

Generation of MB-TRAIL-armored MSLN-targeted CAR-T cells

We engineered a TRAIL-armored CAR system by coupling two components. The first is a stable, constitutively expressed MB-TRAIL chimera, which forms a highly stable homotrimer (see online supplemental information) with potent antitumor activity. The second component is a second-generation anti-human MSLN-specific CAR (M28z) (figure 1I). An EGFR-bearing M28z CAR was used as a control. All CAR-T cells were co-transduced with the second retroviral vector encoding a membrane-anchored Cypridina luciferase (maCluc) BLI reporter,24 followed by a P2A self-cleavage site and GFP, allowing both reporters to be expressed from the same transcript. CAR-T cells were efficiently generated (figure 1J) without compromising their viability (online supplemental figure 4). Confocal microscopy confirmed the correct plasma membrane localization of both the M28z CAR and MB-TRAIL (figure 1K), and in vitro BLI verified the activity of the luciferase reporter (figure 1L).

MSLN-specific CAR-T cells armored with MB-TRAIL exhibit enhanced in vitro killing in both MSTO211H and A549 cells with heterogeneous mesothelin expression

As MSLN heterogeneous expression drives relapse in patients with PM and NSCLC undergoing anti-MSLN CAR-T cell therapy,6 we investigated the ability of MB-TRAIL-armored M28z CAR-T cells to target both MSLN+ and MSLN− MSTO-211H and A549 cells. First, we demonstrated that MB-TRAIL-armored M28z CAR-T cells display significantly greater cytotoxicity than M28z CAR-T cells expressing natural TRAIL (online supplemental figure 5A, B). We then moved towards comparing the antitumor properties of MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells versus M28z CAR-T cells in a functional assay at T:E ratio 1:2. Both CAR-T variants killed MSLN+MSTO-211H (figure 2A) and A549 (figure 2C) target cells. However, MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells exhibited enhanced killing as early as 24 hours post-treatment (58.5% vs 22.13% in MSTO-211 cells, **p=0.0019; 41.15% vs 14.84% in A549 cells, ****p<0.0001), most likely due to TRAIL-mediated antitumor activity. While M28z CAR-T cells did not lyse MSLN− cells, MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells elicited a significant cytotoxic effect when co-cultured with either MSLN− MSTO-211H (figure 2B; at 24 hours: **p=0.0032) or MSLN− A549 cells (figure 2D; at 24 hours: ****p<0.0001). This data suggests that cell killing in MSLN− cells is driven exclusively by TRAIL-mediated pro-apoptotic signaling. Importantly, tumor cell killing continued to increase over time across different T:E ratios in a dose-dependent manner in both MSTO-211H (figure 2E, F) and A549 cells (figure 2G, H). Cytokine profiling revealed comparable IFN-ɣ and TNF-α intracellular levels in MB-TRAIL M28z and unarmored M28z CAR-T cells, indicating preserved T-cell signaling (online supplemental figure 5G).

Collectively, these data provided evidence that MB-TRAIL-armored M28z CAR-T cells possess superior and durable antitumor activity than M28z CAR-T cells. Their performance exceeds that of the already therapeutic5 6 and trialed29 M28z CAR-T cells (cf. figure 2A–D). This enhanced efficacy arises from their ability to deliver additional pro-apoptotic stimuli to both MSLN+ and MSLN− tumor target cells.

Doxorubicin effectively sensitizes A549 cells to MB-TRAIL-armored MSLN-specific CAR-T cells in vitro

It is well known that chemotherapy at clinically relevant doses enhances TRAIL-mediated cell killing by rendering TRAIL-resistant cells more amenable to TRAIL treatment in NSCLC.10 30 Several reports described the upregulation of DR5 following doxorubicin-induced DNA-damage.31 32 We therefore speculated that doxorubicin treatment may affect DR5 expression in A549 MSLN+ and MSLN− cells. Following doxorubicin treatment, the overall frequency of DR5-expressing cells remained unchanged (online supplemental figure 6A, B). However, the mean fluorescence intensities (geometric), reflecting DR5 expression levels, increased in a dose-dependent manner (figure 2I and J), consistent with enhanced receptor density per cell. Significant DR5 upregulation was observed at a dose as low as 0.1 µg/mL in both MSLN+ (figure 2I; **p=0.0052) and MSLN− cells (figure 2J; **p=0.0055), while approximately 50–60% of cancer cell viability was retained after rhTRAIL treatment (online supplemental figure 6C, D). In agreement with previous reports,33 34 our data shows that doxorubicin downregulates c-FLIP expression (online supplemental figure 7A), thereby further sensitizing cells to TRAIL-mediated apoptosis. Combination treatment with 10 ng/mL rhTRAIL and different doxorubicin regimens enhanced killing of both MSLN+ and MSLN− A549 cells in a dose-dependent manner. Notably, 0.1 µg/mL doxorubicin significantly increased the sensitivity of the cells to TRAIL compared with rhTRAIL or doxorubicin alone (****p<0.0001 vs rhTRAIL; **p≤0.001 vs doxorubicin). Moreover, doxorubicin reduced the rhTRAIL dose required to obtain superior cell killing to as low as 10 ng/mL, compared with the ≥50 ng/mL needed for rhTRAIL alone (cf. online supplemental figure 6E, F and figure 1G, H).

Our data suggests that doxorubicin can further sensitize TRAIL-resistant A549 cancer cells through DR5 upregulation. Based on this, we investigated the in vitro therapeutic impact of doxorubicin in making both MSLN+ and MSLN− A549 tumor cells more responsive to MB-TRAIL-mediated M28z CAR-T cell treatment. Based on our results, 0.1 and 0.25 µg/mL doses of doxorubicin were selected for monolayer co-culture experiments. As expected, non-relevant CAR-T cells showed no cytotoxicity against either MSLN+ or MSLN− A549 target cells (figure 2J, K/red bars). Addition of doxorubicin significantly enhanced M28z-mediated killing of MSLN+ A549 tumor cells (figure 2K, p=0.0070), but not MSLN− A549 cell killing (figure 2L, p=0.0557). In MSLN− A549 cells, the observed loss of viability was mainly to be attributed to doxorubicin alone, as single-agent doxorubicin treatment (0.25 µg/mL) produced a similar effect (63.94%±3.94 SD) compared with combination therapy (57.49%±1.38 SD). In agreement with our initial hypothesis, combination treatment MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells and doxorubicin induced a synergistic cytotoxic effect in both MSLN+ and MSLN− A549 tumor target cells. This effect was significantly greater than that observed with unarmored M28z CAR-T cells (****p<0.0001) (figure 2K, L/petrol bars). Importantly, the tumor cell killing seen on combination therapy exceeded the effect of either agent alone, MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells or doxorubicin (****p<0.0001), further supporting the in vivo evaluation of this novel therapeutic regimen.

In addition, the proteasome inhibitor bortezomib has been reported to sensitize NSCLC cells to TRAIL-mediated apoptosis via DR5 upregulation,35 and XIAP downregulation36 at doses below systemic exposure achieved in standard patient regimens. We confirmed XIAP modulation in A549 parental cells on bortezomib treatment with or without rhTRAIL (online supplemental figure 7B) and tested its efficacy in combination with CAR-T cells. Our data showed that both low (5 nM) and high doses (30 nM) of bortezomib yielded significantly less cytotoxicity than doxorubicin (online supplemental figure S7C, D). In fact, combination therapy MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells and doxorubicin reduced A549 MSLN+cell viability to 18%±3.87 SD (figure 2K), whereas the same combination with high dose bortezomib (30 nM) reduced cell viability to only 32.43%±3.75 SD (cf. online supplemental figure 7C).

Based on these findings, we next evaluated the therapeutic potential of MB-TRAIL-armored M28z CAR-T cell therapy in a xenograft model of MSTO-211 PM. We further assessed MB-TRAIL-armored CAR-T cells alone or in combination with doxorubicin in a xenograft model of A549 NSCLC.

In the A549 NSCLC model, cancer cells injected via the tail vein disseminate through the pleural cavity within the peritoneum, often leading to the development of liver metastases. In our studies, in vivo BLI identified metastatic foci and enabled tracking of therapeutic CAR-T cells, demonstrating their redistribution and targeting of distant MSLN+ lesions within the peritoneal cavity, including liver (online supplemental figure 10/red arrows, figure 5A/red arrow, B). Ex vivo BLI (figure 5C, D, online supplemental figure 12) and histological analysis (figures5E 6, online supplemental figure 14) confirmed CAR-T cell infiltration into MSLN+ metastatic lesions.

Overall, these findings confirm that MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells eradicate MSLN+ tumors and delay MSLN− tumor progression. Moreover, they redistribute beyond the lungs to control metastatic spread, resulting in prolonged survival relative to M28z CAR-T cells in an A549 NSCLC model.

To evaluate combination therapy with doxorubicin as a strategy to sensitize A549 tumor cells to MB-TRAIL, a safe, intermediate dose of 0.25 µg/g doxorubicin was selected based on in vivo tolerability study (online supplemental material, figure 13A-C). Doxorubicin did not affect MSLN+ lung tumor burden in mice treated with either M28z CAR-T (figure 4B/Group 4) or MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells (Group 6). However, MSLN+ tumor burden was significantly lower in Group 6 compared with Group 4 (online supplemental figure 11A, day 43: ***p=0.0005). Doxorubicin also did not reduce MSLN− tumor burden in any treatment group compared with MSLN− tumors treated with either CAR-T cells or MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells alone (online supplemental figure 11B). Nonetheless, similar to observations in MSLN+ tumors, a significant difference in MSLN− tumor burden was observed between Group 4 (M28z CAR-T cells+doxorubicin) and Group 6 (MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells+doxorubicin), with Group 6 exhibiting lower tumor burden (day 42: ***p=0.0004). This effect was exclusively dependent on MB-TRAIL-mediated killing. Mirroring tumor growth, survival outcomes were not affected by doxorubicin treatment (figure 4D). However, MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells + doxorubicin significantly outperformed M28z CAR-T cells + doxorubicin, extending median survival from day 76 to 109 (****p<0.0001), thereby reflecting the added therapeutic value of MB-TRAIL.

Doxorubicin and MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells combination prevents MSLN+ metastatic growth

Although intraperitoneal administration of doxorubicin (0.25 µg/g) did not improve overall survival in either treatment group, its combination with MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells markedly reduced MSLN+ liver metastasis and enhanced tumor clearance, with MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cell alone resulting in fewer macroscopic lesions as assessed by in vivo BLI (figure 5E, online supplemental figure 11E/dashed petrol). MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cell treatment delayed the onset of MSLN− liver metastases; however, the addition of doxorubicin conferred no additional benefit, indicating that the observed effect of the combination therapy was driven primarily by MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells (figure 5F, online supplemental figure 11F/dashed vs continuous petrol). Consistent with the BLI data, a similar trend towards reduced metastatic spread was observed in the gross macroscopic appearance of livers of mice receiving combination treatment (online supplemental table 12).

Notably, combination treatment delayed the development of both MSLN+ and MSLN− peritoneal lesions (online supplemental figure 11C, D/dashed petrol).

Discussion

Tumor antigen heterogeneity remains one of the limiting barriers to successful CAR-T cell therapy in solid tumors. In PM and NSCLC, heterogeneous expression of MSLN limits the efficacy of MSLN-directed CAR-T cells, thereby driving tumor relapse.5 6 Strategies to address antigen escape include designing CARs with dual-antigen or multi-antigen specificity,33 expressing more than one CAR,34 37 or recruiting endogenous T cells through secretion of activating cytokines (ie, IL-18).38 Although these approaches hold promise, their dependence on the presence of one or more tumor-associated antigens makes them susceptible to tumor cell escape when antigens are lost or absent. To overcome this limitation, we explored a different approach by combining the specificity of MSLN-targeted CAR-T cells with the antigen-independent anticancer activity of TRAIL. The latter is a death-inducing ligand expressed on immune cells (including T cells) and exerting a potent antitumor activity through recognition of its death receptors on the cell surface of tumor cells.9 39 Expression of “decoy” receptors in normal tissues but not in tumors represents one explanation for the selective activity of TRAIL, allowing to spare normal cells while preferentially inducing apoptosis in tumor cells.9 20 Consistent with prior work, non-tumorigenic human mesothelial cells (eg, Met-5A) exhibit minimal apoptosis in response to TRAIL-based treatment compared with malignant mesothelioma cells, supporting the relative resistance of normal mesothelium to TRAIL-mediated cytotoxicity.40 Because of its potent anticancer properties, multiple TRAIL-based therapeutics have been developed and advanced in clinical trials, including rhTRAIL and agonistic antibodies against DR4 and DR5.10 Despite a favorable safety profile, the clinical development of these agents has been hampered by both pharmacologic limitations such as the short half-life of rhTRAIL in the bloodstream11 and the limited potency of agonistic antibodies,12 as well as biological barriers such as tumor-intrinsic mechanisms of TRAIL resistance.10

To overcome these hurdles and fulfill the potential of TRAIL treatment, we developed a new class of clinically translatable “armored” anti-MSLN CAR-T cells. These CAR-T cells are engineered to constitutively express and locally deliver a highly stabilized TRAIL chimera (MB-TRAIL), which overcomes the short half-life of rhTRAIL and maximizes therapeutic potency by tethering robust pro-apoptotic activity to the tumor site.18 The therapeutic superiority of this approach is defined by its quantitative efficiency. Based on conservative estimates, soluble TRAIL therapy in mice typically requires ~5 mg/kg (~1015 molecules) to see an effect.20 In contrast, our platform uses ~5×106 T cells expressing roughly 103–104 molecules per cell,41 yielding a >10,000-fold reduction in total TRAIL exposure while achieving superior antitumor efficacy. This demonstrates that TRAIL-expressing T cells lacking tumor targeting CAR function as subtherapeutic controls, whereas our MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells transform TRAIL into safe, potent, and localized biotherapeutic. In addition, this strategy transforms CAR-T cells into dual-function effectors: antigen-selective killers of MSLN+ cells and potent, antigen-independent drivers of apoptosis. This dual mechanism directly and efficiently addresses the challenge of tumor antigen heterogeneity. We determined that constitutive production of TRAIL chimera did not compromise CAR-T cell viability (online supplemental figure 4) or effector function (online supplemental figure 5C), despite prior concerns regarding TRAIL-induced apoptosis in activated T cells (through DR5 and Fas-L expression).42 43 Similar to normal cells, activated T cells may resist TRAIL-mediated apoptosis through upregulation of decoy receptors and intracellular modulation of the TRAIL apoptotic pathway.20 39 44 Compared with the already successful and trialed MSLN-directed CAR-T cells,5 29 MB-TRAIL-enhanced CAR-T cells demonstrated enhanced killing of both MSLN+ and MSLN− tumors, effectively overcoming the barrier of tumor antigen heterogeneity in both PM and NSCLC. Resistance to TRAIL-mediated apoptosis in NSCLC is driven by mutations in DR445 and DR5,46 upregulation of decoy receptors39 and inhibitor of apoptosis proteins (eg, XIAP),47 recruitment of FADD-like IL-1β-converting enzyme-inhibitory protein (c-FLIP) and altered regulation of Bcl-2 family proteins.10 30 As a result, inherently TRAIL resistant NSCLC cell lines may require a TRAIL-potentiating strategy to restore or enhance sensitivity to TRAIL-induced apoptosis. Radiotherapy or chemotherapy overcome TRAIL resistance by upregulating TRAIL death receptors expression and/or downregulating anti-apoptotic intracellular factors,31 32 48 thereby potentiating TRAIL antitumor activity.21 49 Because resistance to TRAIL treatment may limit the efficacy of our approach in NSCLC, we tested clinically approved sensitizing agents (eg, doxorubicin and bortezomib) to enhance TRAIL sensitivity in combination with MB-TRAIL-armored MSLN-directed CAR-T cell therapy. We further evaluated the impact of doxorubicin on local and metastatic growth in a xenograft model of A549 NSCLC. In line with prior studies, sensitization to TRAIL therapy can be achieved through chemotherapy-mediated DR5 upregulation31 or c-FLIP downregulation.50 In our studies, we were able to confirm these effects in A549 cells (figure 2I, J, online supplemental figure 7A) and determined that pretreatment with doxorubicin markedly increased tumor susceptibility to MB-TRAIL-armored CAR-T cells (figure 2K, L). Bortezomib, a clinically approved proteasome inhibitor, sensitizes cells to TRAIL at subtherapeutic concentrations. It achieves this by upregulating DR5 on the cell surface and inhibiting NF-κB and E3 ubiquitin ligase XIAP, making it an attractive therapeutic option for the treatment of NSCLC.51 Interestingly, we observed an upregulation of XIAP following rhTRAIL treatment as a mechanism of tumor defense, which was mitigated at high doses of bortezomib, underscoring the complexity of TRAIL resistance pathways. Although bortezomib enhanced killing of A549 cells when combined with MB-TRAIL CAR-T cells (online supplemental figure 7C, D), its effect was less pronounced than that of doxorubicin (cf. figure 2K, L). We then evaluated the feasibility of the MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T strategy in MSTO-211H PM and A549 NSCLC models with heterogeneous MSLN expression. Triple-multiplex BLI was employed for the non-invasive, longitudinal monitoring of MSLN+ and MSLN− tumors, as well as CAR-T cells, within the same animals.24 Differential imaging of MSLN+ and MSLN− tumors revealed that MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells efficiently targeted both tumor types. This effect was observed in MSTO-211H PM and A549 NSCLC xenografts (figures3B, C 4B, C). The enhanced therapeutic properties of MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells translated into a significantly improved survival in both tumor models compared with conventional, already therapeutic and trialed4 5 29 M28z CAR-T cells (figures3F4F; NCT04577326, NCT02414269).5 Consistent with previous studies,6 in vivo imaging confirmed that M28z CAR-T cell accumulation was antigen-driven, with robust expansion in MSLN+ lesions. In contrast, MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells mediated potent bystander killing of MSLN− tumors. Relapse in the MSLN− tumors of the NSCLC model is consistent with adaptive resistance. We observed that TRAIL sensitivity is dynamic, driven by death receptor modulation and induction of anti-apoptotic mediators, such as c-FLIP. In line with this, higher rhTRAIL exposure increased c-FLIP in vitro (online supplemental figure 7A). Ultimately, these molecular defenses, combined with progressive CAR-T cell exhaustion within the suppressive tumor microenvironment, converge to facilitate tumor escape. The superior tumor control in MSLN+ tumors likely reflects reinforcement provided by continuous antigen-dependent CAR-signaling, which may offset the suppressive effect of the tumor microenvironment. In the PM and NSCLC models used in this study, tumor dissemination followed distinct patterns: MSTO-211H cells spread through the pleural cavity into the lungs, whereas A549 cells disseminated into the peritoneum through the pleural cavity, leading to metastatic lesion formation. Non-invasive imaging revealed that CAR-T cells, administered intraperitoneally or intravenously, could traffic through the peripheral circulation and persist beyond the primary peritoneal or lung compartments. Using in vivo triple-multiplex BLI, we showed that both M28z CAR-T and MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells successfully targeted MSLN+ lesions. These results were also cross-validated using lesion-based BLI, ex vivo quantification, and histology. In contrast to conventional M28z CAR-T cells, MB-TRAIL M28z CAR-T cells provided sustained protection against metastatic spread of both MSLN+ and MSLN− MSTO-211H tumors in the lungs (figure 3B–C/bottom panels, online supplemental figure 9C, E/petrol).

In the A549 model, a single low-dose of doxorubicin (0.25 mg/kg) had a limited effect on primary tumor growth or overall survival. This lack of efficacy likely reflects suboptimal intratumoral drug exposure and a temporal mismatch between receptor induction and CAR-T cell infiltration. Furthermore, heterogeneous DR5 expression across the tumor, and the use of a single, subtherapeutic dose may have further limited the sensitization of the tumor to TRAIL-mediated killing. By contrast, a higher dose of doxorubicin (1.2 mg/kg) administered biweekly had been shown to inhibit metastatic growth and prolonged survival.52 To evaluate low-dose chemotherapy as a TRAIL-sensitization strategy, we intentionally selected a subtherapeutic dose of doxorubicin below clinically observed plasma levels. This approach was designed to sensitize the tumor to MB-TRAIL-armored CAR-T cells, while preserving CAR-T cell viability and function, thereby maximizing the potential for synergy without systemic or cellular toxicity.10 30 In addition, TRAIL-mediated antigen-independent killing in MSLN− tumors may already operate near maximal efficacy, further limiting the incremental benefit of additional DR5 upregulation. Despite the low dose of doxorubicin, this regimen was sufficient to reduce metastatic dissemination, preventing MSLN+ liver metastases (figure 5E, online supplemental figure 11E) and decreasing both MSLN+ and MSLN− peritoneal dissemination (online supplemental figure 11C, D). These findings highlight the importance of integrating intratumoral pharmacodynamics with CAR-T cell timing in the design of combination strategies.

A key advantage of our studies is the use of whole-body multiplex imaging to track tumor burden and CAR-T cell kinetics. This approach enabled long-term tracking of CAR-T cells, providing real-time information on their distribution, persistence, and antigen-dependent expansion. By capturing these dynamics in vivo, this platform establishes a direct link between CAR-T cell behavior and the simultaneous regression of both antigen-positive and antigen-negative tumors. Furthermore, it reveals the eradication of disseminated metastasis, offering critical mechanistic insights into therapeutic efficacy with precise spatiotemporal resolution. As such, this method significantly advances the preclinical evaluation of armored CAR-T cell therapies by providing dynamic insights that conventional endpoint analyses cannot capture. While we used the BLI reporter Cypridina for cost-effective preclinical proof-of-concept, this platform is readily adaptable to radiotracer-based clinically compatible reporter systems, offering a translational strategy to monitor CAR-T cell behavior and efficacy in patients.53

While the xenograft models used here do not fully recapitulate the complexity of human tumors, our findings nevertheless support the development of MB-TRAIL-armored CAR-T cells as a novel class of cell-based immunotherapies. This approach holds promise both as monotherapy and as a foundational component of rational combination strategies for PM, NSCLC and other malignancies with heterogeneous MSLN expression. Leveraging the anti-MSLN CAR vector, already in use in ongoing clinical trials at our institution allows the rapid translation of our findings to the clinic. Importantly, the modular design of this approach allows substitution of the anti-MSLN CAR with CARs targeting alternative antigens, thereby broadening its therapeutic potential across a broad range of solid tumors.
