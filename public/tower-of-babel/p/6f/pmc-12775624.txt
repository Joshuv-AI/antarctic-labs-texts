Lumbar interbody fusion via anterior lumbar interbody fusion versus oblique lumbar interbody fusion versus lateral lumbar interbody fusion: a narrative review for surgeons
Authors: Gregory M Malham, Kevin A Seex, Matthew H Claydon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAnterior column lumbar interbody fusion using an anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF)/anterior-to-psoas fusion (ATP), lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF), or combination technique allows insertion of a wide-footprint interbody cage and provides immediate segmental stability, indirect neural decompression, lower risk of subsidence, access for disc preparation, the ability to use a large volume of graft, and restoration of coronal and sagittal balance. We included studies describing the indications, approaches, patient positioning, surgical technique, learning curve, complications, radiation exposure, need for supplemental fixation, and clinical and radiologic outcomes of ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF procedures.Key Content and FindingsThis review compared the ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF techniques regarding patient factors, anesthetic factors, surgical factors, operative efficiency, surgical risks, and economic factors. We focused on differences between techniques to help clinicians choose between procedures and identified the preferred procedure(s) at each spinal level. We supplemented data from the literature with practical information obtained from our substantial clinical experience with these procedures.ConclusionsALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF are all effective techniques for anterior interbody fusion, which provide very good long-term clinical outcomes, excellent fusion rates, and low but specific complication rates.Keywords: Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), anterior-to-psoas fusion (ATP), lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF), oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF), lumbar spine

Introduction

Lumbar interbody fusion of the anterior column is typically performed via anterior, lateral oblique, or lateral approaches. Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) or anterior-to-psoas fusion (ATP), and lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) are anterior column fusion procedures in which the retroperitoneal space is approached during supine or lateral positioning using the oblique ATP or direct lateral transpsoas corridors to access the target disc (1).

ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF are most commonly used for symptomatic degenerative discopathy, foraminal stenosis, low-grade isthmic and degenerative spondylolisthesis, and coronal and sagittal lumbar deformities. Other applications include trauma, tumor, infection, and adjacent segment disease (1-3). All of these procedures provide indirect neural decompression by increasing the disc space height and foraminal height/area and improving segmental lordosis without compromising the posterior paravertebral muscles. Wide-footprint interbody cages can be used in all procedures to reduce the likelihood of subsidence and restore segmental lordosis. Fusion rates are high with all three techniques (4-6).

In this paper, we have integrated decades of our combined clinical experience with a narrative literature review of lumbar anterior column surgery. The objective was to compare ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF techniques to produce a contemporary pragmatic guide for spine surgeons. We first present key aspects of the different techniques and then compare the techniques according to patient factors, anesthetic factors, surgical factors, operative efficiency, surgical risks, and economic factors (as summarized in Table 1). We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-14/rc).

Methods

We used PubMed and Ovid Medline databases to identify relevant studies published in English from 2000 to 2024. The search was conducted on January 4, 2025, by all authors (G.M.M., K.A.S., and M.H.C.). The search terms included “anterior”, “fusion”, “interbody”, “lateral”, “lumbar”, and “oblique”. We included studies describing the indications, approaches, patient positioning, surgical technique, learning curve, complications, radiation exposure, need for supplemental fixation, and clinical and radiological outcomes of the ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF techniques. Surgical approaches

ALIF

ALIF can be performed using a supine anterior or lateral decubitus approach. For the supine anterior approach, the patient is placed in a true supine or low lithotomy (with hips flexed approximately 10 degrees) position. In supine or lateral ALIF at L5/S1 alone, a right-sided transverse incision and approach is preferred using fluoroscopically based skin markings over the L5/S1 disc space (7,8). For lateral ALIF at L4/5 plus L5/S1, a left-sided approach is used (2). For supine ALIF at L4/5 alone or multiple levels, a midline lower abdominal vertical incision with a left retroperitoneal approach is preferred (9).

In patients with a normal body mass index (BMI), L3/4 ALIF via a supine anterior exposure may be easier than L4/5 exposure because it is the aorta or proximal common iliac vessels that are retracted. Some patients have a relatively large gap between their aorta and the medial edge of the left psoas muscle (the aorto-psoas window), which allows adequate exposure to be achieved with less retraction of these major vessels.

In patients with an elevated BMI, ALIF is more difficult to perform via a supine anterior approach than via a lateral oblique approach, especially in males with predominantly intraperitoneal fat. Supine ALIF is easier for L5/S1, in comparison to ALIF at L4/5, as there is usually less retraction of the major vessels required, and ligation of the ascending lumbar vein is not required. ALIF at L3/4 is also more difficult because of the required retraction forces on the viscera and peritoneal contents, which may be transmitted to the mesenteric and renal vessels. The more recent lateral ALIF uses gravity to help retract the abdominal contents away from the surgical site and permits multilevel fusions using a single patient position, advantages that are especially useful in obese patients (10,11).

OLIF/ATP and LLIF

OLIF/ATP is an oblique approach with the patient placed in a lateral position, whereas LLIF is performed in a lateral decubitus position with a true 90-degree lateral approach. There are advantages to using a consistent side for the operative approach, including ease of operating room set-up, consistent patient positioning, anesthetic preference, and avoidance of the rarely admitted possibility of surgeon disorientation.

For LLIF, either a left- or right-sided approach can be used. It is often preferable to use the side that makes access to L4/5 easier, although one can also use the side providing the best access to the taller side of an asymmetric disc space collapse. For multilevel LLIF, either multiple transverse skin incisions (approaching the convexity of the curve) or a single oblique skin incision with multiple separate fascial incisions (approaching the curve concavity) can be used.

For OLIF/ATP, the anatomy of the vasculature [especially the inferior vena cava (IVC)] makes a left-sided approach preferable (12). However, a right-sided approach may be considered in select patients, especially when performed by surgeons experienced in dissecting the IVC (13). With both OLIF/ATP and LLIF, it is usually easier to approach a scoliosis on the convexity of the curve. For multilevel OLIF/ATP, an oblique incision along the lines of the external oblique fibers can be performed for 1–2 levels. This incision can be extended and curved upward to L2/3, allowing easier access to the retroperitoneum (and facilitating rib resection if required), or extended downward for lateral ALIF. A separate transverse incision is recommended for L5/S1 lateral ALIF (11).

For upper lumbar levels, L1/2 and L2/3 can be accessed between the lower ribs during LLIF, but resecting a small section of rib usually improves access. For multilevel OLIF/ATP, L2/3 may be approached without resecting the lower floating ribs, but resection is usually required to access L1/2. Remaining anterior improves access to the retroperitoneum and avoids violating the pleura, thereby eliminating the need for chest drains (14).

Sagittal balance considerations

Anterior column realignment (ACR) using a lateral cage may involve dividing both the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL) and lateral annuli, which leads to a wide “fish-mouth” configuration of the disc space and renders the target segment highly unstable. This necessitates both cage-integrated fixation and posterior screw-rod fixation. ACR was initially described using the LLIF technique (15) but lends itself favorably to OLIF/ATP because of easier access to the anterior aspect of the spine. Partial ALL resection has recently been described (16) and is especially useful for OLIF/ATP, compared with LLIF, for the same reason that posterior screw-rod fixation is recommended.

For surgery at L5/S1, higher fusion rates are achieved with ALIF than with OLIF/ATP [or posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) or transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF)] (17). Similarly, lordosis restoration is more effective with ALIF, compared with OLIF/ATP (or PLIF or TLIF), and is critically important at L5/S1 (18-20).

Patient factors

Patient demographics and medical comorbidities are important to consider when choosing the appropriate technique for anterior column interbody fusion. This is especially important when considering ALIF because of the higher risk of vascular injury (and resultant blood loss) with this procedure than with OLIF/ATP or LLIF. Age itself does not preclude the use of ALIF, OLIF/ATP, or LLIF. Morbid obesity (BMI >35 kg/m2) is a relative contraindication for supine ALIF but not lateral ALIF, OLIF/ATP, or LLIF. Significant iliac artery disease (e.g., heavy calcification, aneurysmal and severe stenosing atherosclerotic disease) precludes ALIF but not OLIF/ATP or LLIF. Previous complex or extensive retroperitoneal abdominal surgery precludes ALIF and is a relative contraindication for ipsilateral OLIF/ATP and LLIF. Prior abdominal and/or pelvic radiotherapy around the target disc level is a contraindication to ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF (9).

High-grade spondylolisthesis (grade 3 or 4) is an absolute contraindication for OLIF/ATP or LLIF and a relative contraindication for ALIF. A psoas muscle configuration resulting in more ventrally positioned lumbar plexus nerves (“rising psoas sign”) is a contraindication for transpsoas LLIF (21) but not ALIF or OLIF/ATP.

Anesthetic factors

Lateral decubitus positioning is used for lateral ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF, whereas a true supine or lithotomy position is used for supine ALIF. Differences in positioning can lead to differences in positioning-related complications between techniques. For example, the risks of position-related pressure areas and peripheral nerve injuries are higher with OLIF/ATP and LLIF than with ALIF.

Access to the airway is easier during supine ALIF than during lateral ALIF, OLIF/ATP, or LLIF. Muscle relaxation is required for ALIF and useful for OLIF/ATP, but not for LLIF. During supine ALIF, full muscle relaxation facilitates incision closure, especially in larger patients. Full muscle relaxation is also beneficial during difficult exposures or after vascular injury. An indwelling urinary catheter is required for ALIF but optional for OLIF/ATP and LLIF, depending on the length of the procedure.

Surgical factors

Surgical levels

The choice between ALIF, OLIF/ATP, or LLIF is based in large part on the level of surgery. Recommended technique(s) for each surgical level are as follows:

❖ L1/2: LLIF; not ALIF or OLIF/ATP (22);

❖ L1/2: LLIF; not ALIF or OLIF/ATP (22);

❖ L2/3: LLIF, optional OLIF/ATP; not ALIF;

❖ L2/3: LLIF, optional OLIF/ATP; not ALIF;

❖ L3/4: LLIF or OLIF/ATP; ALIF may be used (3);

❖ L3/4: LLIF or OLIF/ATP; ALIF may be used (3);

❖ L4/5: LLIF or OLIF/ATP or ALIF (23,24);

❖ L4/5: LLIF or OLIF/ATP or ALIF (23,24);

❖ L5/S1: ALIF or OLIF/ATP; not LLIF (1,2).

❖ L5/S1: ALIF or OLIF/ATP; not LLIF (1,2).

Thus, use of LLIF is limited to surgery at L1/2 to L4/5, whereas OLIF/ATP is mostly limited to surgery at L2/3 to L4/5 but is an alternative to lateral ALIF at L5/S1.

Multilevel surgery for deformity correction may require a combination of ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF techniques for sagittal and coronal plane realignment with supplemental fixation (as shown in Figure 1). When operating on multiple levels, we usually address L5/S1 first, regardless of the technique. We then deal with L4/5, followed by L2/3, delaying L3/4 to the end to reduce rib obstruction at L2/3.

In patients with severe rotatory scoliosis, accurate imaging is difficult, which causes LLIF and OLIF/ATP to become more risky because both techniques require accurate imaging for orthogonal positioning and instrumentation. Consequently, ALIF may be preferred even at higher levels (L3/4) in these patients, despite the more difficult access. Future improvements in navigation and robotics may alter this preference.

Intraoperative equipment

A radiolucent table for fluoroscopy is necessary for all three techniques. Intraoperative neural monitoring is standard practice for LLIF (22) but optional for OLIF/ATP and not useful for ALIF. Navigation and robotics (25) offer promise for LLIF and OLIF/ATP, but are less useful for ALIF. The number of sterile instrument trays is lowest with ALIF, intermediate with OLIF/ATP, and highest with LLIF, which affects the relative cost of these procedures.

Learning curve

An experienced mentor reduces the duration of learning for all three procedures, especially for lateral ALIF (2), OLIF/ATP (6), and LLIF (26). In ALIF, access and vascular safety are paramount, with vascular mobilization being the most difficult part of ALIF, whereas disc preparation and cage implantation can be taught quickly. By collaborating with an experienced access surgeon, spine surgeons can perform supine ALIF (27) and lateral ALIF (8) at an earlier stage than the other techniques. Competency takes longer to achieve for the multi-faceted OLIF/ATP and LLIF procedures.

As with ALIF, disc preparation and cage placement for OLIF/ATP are relatively straightforward. The most difficult part of OLIF/ATP is establishing a safe retroperitoneal corridor to the target disc, identifying and protecting the ureter, major vessels, segmental branches, and the colon, highlighting the potential importance of mentors and access surgeons in the early learning stages.

The most difficult aspects of LLIF are obtaining clear radiographic anteroposterior and lateral images, ensuring a true lateral approach, and maintaining a safe orthogonal passage of instruments and cage placement. To improve overall safety, we recommend the use of a mini-open “direct look” technique down to psoas (thus avoiding the peritoneum and its contents). Disc preparation, trials, and cage placement are more difficult with LLIF than with ALIF and OLIF/ATP. Thus, LLIF is associated with easier access but more difficult disc preparation and cage positioning, ultimately leading to the longest learning curve.

Access surgeon

Collaboration with an access surgeon differs considerably, depending on the geographic region, medicolegal concerns (justified or unjustified), surgeon availability, remuneration, training of the spine surgeon (i.e., whether they are trained to perform their own approaches), and degree of approach difficulty, as perceived by the spine surgeon. Collaborative preoperative discussion with vascular surgeon colleagues regarding potentially difficult cases will improve their availability and promote team relationships.

Data have not shown differences in vascular injury rates whether an access surgeon was or was not involved, but the existing studies are confounded by preferential involvement of an access surgeon in more difficult cases (27). Currently, access surgeons are predominantly involved in supine (9) and lateral ALIF (8), less commonly involved in OLIF/ATP, and infrequently involved in LLIF.

Operative efficiency

Operating room personnel play a key role in operative efficiency. A regularly trained operating room team with goal alignment anticipates each procedural step to optimize efficiency and minimize risk.

An assistant surgeon is essential for the ALIF technique and is also useful during patient positioning for OLIF/ATP and LLIF. Scrub nurse experience is particularly important for ALIF because training in both vascular/general and spine surgery is required. Operating room technicians are important for patient positioning for OLIF/ATP and LLIF, and less so for ALIF. Experienced radiographers are critical for OLIF/ATP and LLIF, as they can reduce radiation exposure to the patient and staff and reduce operative time. Experienced industry representatives are important for patient positioning and for indicating orthogonal instrument alignment during both OLIF/ATP and LLIF.

Surgical risks

Infection

The rate of superficial wound infection is very low with ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF, and deep wound infections are rare with all three techniques (24). Superficial infections tend to be more common in older and obese patients, but even in these high-risk patients, their rate is lower than with posterior lumbar spine approaches (28).

Neural injury

The rate of neural injury is higher with LLIF (6% of patients) (22,29) than with ALIF or OLIF/ATP. The most serious neural injuries involve the lumbar plexus. Femoral nerve injury is particularly likely during LLIF at L4/5 (30,31), where the plexus is most anterior (32). Multimodal evoked potential and electromyography monitoring is required during LLIF may provide early alerts about pre-pathologic lumbar plexopathy that may be reversed by adjusting the operating table position (“unbreaking” the table) or repositioning a retractor (22). Neural monitoring is not required during the OLIF/ATP nor the ALIF approaches.

Smaller lumbar plexus nerves can also be injured in the retroperitoneum during both OLIF/ATP and LLIF, but rarely during ALIF. Genitofemoral nerve injury may cause neuropathic groin and medial thigh pain and can now be monitored by electromyographic electrodes placed in the cremaster muscle (in males) (33).

Sympathetic trunk injury occurs most commonly during ALIF (2.3–6.1% of patients) (9,34) on the side of the approach and is particularly likely with multilevel surgery. It produces swelling and warmth of the affected lower extremity, resulting in a “warm” ipsilateral leg and “cold” contralateral leg, with normal pedal pulses. Findings in the affected limb may lead to the mistaken diagnosis of deep vein thrombosis. Sympathetic dysfunction is more severe after ALIF at L5/S1 than after OLIF/ATP at the upper lumbar levels, where sympathetic injury is common but usually asymptomatic. The effects of sympathetic injury in older adults are usually minimal, but the symptoms can be very troublesome for young women.

Subcostal nerve injury beneath the floating ribs can produce abdominal wall paresis (of the transversus abdominis muscle) necessitating mesh reconstruction. Subcostal nerve injury can be mitigated by careful blunt dissection, nerve identification, and avoidance of monopolar diathermy during exposure and closure.

Vascular injury and blood transfusion

The risks of significant vascular injury (1.9–6.1% of patients) (35) and blood transfusion are much higher with ALIF than with either OLIF/ATP or LLIF. Vascular injuries are most commonly venous (1.5–2.9% of patients). The risk of left common iliac vein (LCIV) injury is five times higher at L4/5 than at L5/S1 (36). Risk factors for injury to this vein include mobilizing the vein over adherent anterior osteophytes, absence of a perivascular fat plane, Modic (type 1 or 2) endplate changes on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and any smoking history (37).

Prophylactic ligation of the iliolumbar vein (ILV) is recommended during supine ALIF at L4/5 because ILV avulsion during LCIV mobilization to expose the L4/5 disc can be catastrophic (7). During both ALIF and OLIF/ATP, the ILV may be found to overlie or be in close proximity to the L4/5 disc space. Typically, it is not visible on standard preoperative duplex ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT), or MRI but can be seen on CT angiography and magnetic resonance angiography.

Arterial injuries are less common, as arteries are more robust and tolerant of retraction than veins. However, there is a risk of temporary iliac artery occlusion (29% of patients) (38) or arterial thrombosis (0.45% of patients), especially of the left common iliac artery (LCIA) at L4/5 (36).

To minimize the risk of vascular injury, preoperative radiographic imaging of vascular anatomy is mandatory for both spinal and access surgeons to assess the following:

❖ Levels of the IVC confluence and aortic bifurcation, as well as the relationships between these levels and the target disc(s);

❖ Levels of the IVC confluence and aortic bifurcation, as well as the relationships between these levels and the target disc(s);

❖ Presence of venous anomalies (e.g., dual IVC, left-sided IVC, hypoplastic IVC with extensive collaterals);

❖ Presence of venous anomalies (e.g., dual IVC, left-sided IVC, hypoplastic IVC with extensive collaterals);

❖ Presence of aorto-iliac stenosis;

❖ Presence of aorto-iliac stenosis;

❖ Presence of aorto-iliac atherosclerosis, noting especially whether calcification is present;

❖ Presence of aorto-iliac atherosclerosis, noting especially whether calcification is present;

❖ Presence of arterial aneurysmal disease, noting especially whether an intraluminal thrombus is present;

❖ Presence of arterial aneurysmal disease, noting especially whether an intraluminal thrombus is present;

❖ Presence of renal or renovascular abnormalities (e.g., duplicated renal arteries, anomalous venous drainage, horseshoe or pelvic kidney).

❖ Presence of renal or renovascular abnormalities (e.g., duplicated renal arteries, anomalous venous drainage, horseshoe or pelvic kidney).

In accordance with the difference in risks of vascular injury and blood loss between techniques, use of a Cell Saver or similar intraoperative blood salvage device is recommended for ALIF (7), optional for OLIF/ATP, and unnecessary for LLIF.

Bowel injury

Peritoneal laceration can occur with all three techniques, although it is rare with LLIF. Small tears are easily repaired immediately with a dissolving suture, which prevents extension to form a larger defect that is more difficult to repair. Maintaining peritoneal integrity greatly assists in restraining the bowel to reduce the risk of visceral injury.

Currently, the risk of bowel perforation is higher with ALIF than with OLIF/ATP and is least frequent with LLIF (3). In the early years of LLIF, a blind approach was advocated, which was associated with a substantial risk of bowel injury (26). This risk has been markedly reduced by the use of the mini-open “direct look” technique. Blind approaches in the abdomen should be avoided.

Ureter injury

The risk of ureteric injury is highest with ALIF (0.4% of patients) (39), rare with OLIF/ATP, and essentially nonexistent with LLIF. To reduce the likelihood of this complication, preoperative insertion of ureteric stents by a urologist should be considered for patients undergoing revision ALIF surgery.

Retrograde ejaculation

The anatomic position of the superior hypogastric plexus in the retroperitoneum—left of the midline and overlying the LCIA (40)—makes the risk of retrograde ejaculation relevant for ALIF but not for OLIF/ATP or LLIF. Preoperative sperm storage is recommended for males of child-producing age scheduled for ALIF. Avoiding monopolar diathermy anterior to the aorta and LCIA is strongly advised (9). The risk of retrograde ejaculation supports the use of a right-sided retroperitoneal approach for L5/S1 ALIF (2) and preserves the left side for subsequent revision or adjacent level fusion (13).

Radiation exposure

Both LLIF and OLIF/ATP require a radiographer and pulsed fluoroscopy throughout the procedure for strict lateral decubitus patient positioning and orthogonal instrumentation. LLIF necessitates more radiation than OLIF/ATP (41), whereas ALIF is associated with the least amount of radiation exposure to the patient and staff.

Cage subsidence

As ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF all rely on indirect neural decompression, cage subsidence is a potential complication of all techniques. Mild radiographic (asymptomatic) subsidence is common, but clinical (symptomatic) subsidence may cause recurrent pain and loss of segmental alignment. Early cage subsidence is the result of intraoperative endplate violation. Delayed cage subsidence is detected on follow-up imaging more than 6 months postoperatively. During endplate preparation, one of the main goals is to avoid intraoperative cortical bone damage, which increases the risk of early cage subsidence (14). All three techniques rely on sequential disc space distraction.

In ALIF (supine or lateral), the rate of subsidence is very low (9,10). Inserting and rotating shims at the lateral margins of the disc space is useful, especially in collapsed disc spaces alternating from side to side. One major advantage of supine ALIF is that it provides excellent visualization of both endplates. They are less well visualized during lateral ALIF (11).

In OLIF/ATP and LLIF, sequential cage trials are performed while the endplate cartilage is intact, before the final curetting and rasping (prior to cage impaction). In contrast to ALIF, LLIF disc space preparation relies mainly on palpation and auditory feedback (24). In OLIF/ATP disc space preparation is equally reliant on visual, palpation, and auditory feedback. With all techniques, the ideal instrument for disc space preparation is a stirrup curette to remove the cartilaginous endplate.

Cage malposition

At L5/S1, a cage placed during supine ALIF is typically located in the midline. However, a cage placed during lateral ALIF more frequently deviates from the midline. A lateral cage at L5/S1 impacted from an oblique position increases the risk of contralateral foraminal encroachment and inadequate decompression of the ipsilateral foramen.

Previously, instrumentation for OLIF/ATP with a lateral cage also risked placement of the cage so that it obstructed the contralateral foramen. However, this risk has been mitigated by more recent improvements in instrumentation. Similarly, the use of angled instruments in the cranio-caudal plane at L4/5 has enabled access to patients with a high iliac crest (42).

Economic factors

As with any procedure, economic factors are important considerations when choosing between the types of lumbar anterior column fusion techniques. In addition to direct costs, such as the number of surgical trays used during the operation (which is highest with LLIF, as discussed above), there are several other economic issues to consider.

Operative time

Patient positioning to obtain true lateral fluoroscopy is more time-consuming during OLIF/ATP, LLIF, and lateral ALIF than during supine ALIF (23). Access to the disc space is substantially quicker during LLIF, compared with OLIF/ATP. The time to access the disc space is longest during ALIF, supporting the role of access surgeons in improving operating room efficiency and lowering costs. Disc space preparation time is similar for all three techniques.

Supplemental fixation

Supplemental fixation in ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF provides higher fusion rates, facilitates deformity correction, maintains sagittal/coronal alignment until fusion, and reduces the risk of cage subsidence (43). Biomechanical data have demonstrated that mechanical stability is improved with lateral plates, posterior screw-rod fixation, or combinations of both (44). For supplemental fixation, patients can be repositioned from the supine or lateral position to the prone position, or both interbody fusion and fixation can be performed in a single lateral decubitus position, without repositioning (2).

Bilateral posterior screw-rod fixation is highly recommended in patients with reduced bone density, high sacral slope (more than 40 degrees), isthmic or degenerative spondylolisthesis (grade 2 or higher), facet arthropathy (grade 2 or higher), interbody cages at multiple levels (3 or more), and coronal deformity with laterolisthesis (43,45).

If the ALL is divided (intentionally or unintentionally), additional fixation of both vertebrae is recommended using either a cage with integrated fixation or a cage with separate plate screws. This recommendation applies to all three techniques (ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF). The technique of deliberate partial or complete ALL section (for ACR) during OLIF/ATP or LLIF necessitates immediate fixation to prevent anterior migration of the lateral cage (15). Partial ALL division (<50%) facilitates anterior disc height restoration, thereby improving segmental lordosis (16).

Supplemental fixation of a lateral cage can be achieved with either a separate lateral plate (1-, 2-, or 4-hole plate) or an integrated cage plate-screw construct (containing 1 or 2 screws) (46,47). The real or potential threat of complete ALL rupture necessitates bilateral posterior screw-rod fixation for optimal biomechanical support.

Adjacent segment disease above a previous posterior fusion can be successfully treated by LLIF with integral fixation, which appears to reduce the risk of vertebral fracture that occurs after separate lateral plate-screw fixation (46). For all techniques, bracing with external orthoses is optional to limit postoperative range of motion and can be used alone or in combination with internal fixation.

Postoperative course and hospital length of stay

The postoperative course can be complicated by ileus and, less commonly, pseudo-obstruction. The likelihood of these complications is proportional to the degree of retroperitoneal mobilization. Thus, the risk of ileus is higher after supine ALIF (2–5% of patients) than after lateral ALIF (10). It is uncommon after OLIF/ATP and rare after LLIF (3). The risk of postoperative ileus is also higher in patients with previous abdominal surgery, those undergoing multilevel procedures, and individuals undergoing transperitoneal ALIF (rather than retroperitoneal exposure).

Based on the risk of ileus, we use the following postoperative regimen for patients undergoing ALIF or OLIF/ATP at our institution (Epworth Richmond hospital): nothing by mouth for the first 24 hours after surgery, followed by initiation of clear fluids when bowel sounds and flatus occur, with introduction of free fluids and a light diet (if tolerated) over the next 48 hours (9). In contrast, patients are permitted to a light diet 4 hours after LLIF.

Hospital length of stay is typically longest after ALIF (approximately 3–5 days). Length of stay after OLIF/ATP and LLIF is usually 2–3 days.

Long-term clinical and radiographic outcomes

ALIF, OLIF/ATP, and LLIF are all effective interbody procedures (3,23) that produce very good long-term clinical outcomes (10,48). Rates of revision surgery and adjacent segment disease are similar to those reported for posterior fusion techniques (PLIF or TLIF) (49). The wide-footprint cages inserted during all three techniques lead to excellent fusion rates (24,50), low subsidence rates (51), and overall high patient satisfaction (52).

Conclusions

Anterior column lumbar fusion via ALIF, OLIF/ATP, LLIF, or a combination of these techniques enables the use of wide-footprint interbody cages and provides immediate segmental stability, indirect neural decompression, resistance to subsidence, access for disc preparation, the ability to use a large volume of graft, restoration of coronal and sagittal balance, and high fusion rates. Blood loss is generally lower than with posterior alternatives (PLIF/TLIF), except for rare cases of severe vascular injury.

At L1/2 and L2/3, LLIF is the preferred technique. A lateral cage inserted by LLIF is technically less demanding than with OLIF/ATP, and ALIF is not safe at these levels.

At L3/4, LLIF or OLIF/ATP is preferred. The required lordotic demands can be met with a lateral cage alone. Supine ALIF is especially difficult in patients with an elevated BMI, who require a larger incision and greater retractive forces, and in whom exposure is more challenging and associated with higher risks of vascular injury or thrombosis (involving the IVC or iliac, aortic, renal, or superior mesenteric arteries).

At L4/5, OLIF/ATP or lateral ALIF is preferred over supine ALIF. OLIF/ATP is usually the first-choice technique, as it provides reliable disc access, minimal risk of lumbar plexus injury, and easier access for partial or complete ALL section (for lordosis restoration). With an access surgeon, ALIF becomes straightforward for the spine surgeon, and the increased risks associated with vascular mobilization are mitigated. LLIF is generally not recommended because of the risk of lumbar plexus injury.

At L5/S1, supine ALIF is the preferred technique. Lateral ALIF at this level is technically challenging, and the ALL would require division to restore lordosis. LLIF is not feasible at L5/S1.
