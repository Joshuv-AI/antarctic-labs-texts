Alcalase hydrolysis modulates the techno-functional properties of dry-fractionated pea protein, reduces the off-flavor compounds and refines the aroma profile
Authors: Davide Falotico, Giuseppe Natrella, Giuseppe Perri, Davide De Angelis, Claudia Antonino, Federico Rametta, Giacomo Squeo, Erica Pontonio, Francesco Caponio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDry fractionation is recognized as one of the most sustainable protein extraction methods, but dry-fractionated proteins often show limited techno-functional versatility and unfavorable sensory profile typical of raw legumes. In this study, Alcalase hydrolysis of dry-fractionated pea protein was evaluated under different processing time (15, 97.5,140,180 min) and temperature (50-60 °C) to assess its effects on protein structure, techno-functional properties, volatile and odor-active compounds. Hydrolysis markedly increased the degree of hydrolysis (up to ⁓48 % after 180 min at 60 °C) and total free amino acids, leading to the formation of smaller and polar peptides, as also confirmed by fluorescence spectroscopy. The structural modifications directly affected the techno-functional properties. In fact, hydrolyzed samples exhibited higher water solubility and lower viscosity than non-hydrolyzed proteins, indicating improved dispersion properties. Conversely, prolonged hydrolysis negatively affected foaming ability and foam stability, due to the formation of low-molecular-weight peptides. A significant reduction was observed in marker volatile compounds associated with green and beany notes, including hexanal, 2-heptanone, 1-hexanol, and 1-penten-3-ol. GC-MS-O analysis identified 22 odor-active compounds. Most undesired odor-active compounds decreased following hydrolysis, whereas compounds contributing to more pleasant notes remained largely unaffected. Overall, Alcalase hydrolysis modulates the techno-functional properties and refines the aroma profile of dry-fractionated pea protein. These findings support the use of enzymatic hydrolysis to expand the use of dry-fractionated pulse proteins in food formulations while mitigating sensory limitations.Keywords: Alcalase hydrolysis, Dry fractionation, Techno-functional properties, Pea protein, Odor-active compounds, Protein structure

Introduction

Dry fractionation is a physical technology that does not employ chemical solvents or water to concentrate plant proteins. This makes it significantly more energy-efficient compared to the more traditional wet extraction processes (Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2021), and therefore a promising technology in the context of protein transition. From a techno-functional perspective, dry fractionation tends to preserve the native structure of proteins, leading to improved interfacial and gelling capacities when compared with wet-extracted protein isolates (De Angelis et al., 2021; De Angelis, Latrofa, Squeo, et al., 2024a, 2024b; Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2021). Additionally, dry-fractionated ingredients contain multiple components, including proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, fibers, and minerals, which may contribute positively to product formulation (De Angelis, Latrofa, Squeo, et al., 2024a, 2024b; Schutyser et al., 2025). Another advantage of dry fractionation is that the equipment can be easily adapted to several legume species (De Angelis, Latrofa, Caponio, et al., 2024) without particular adjustments. Despite these benefits, the intrinsic nature of dry fractionation limits the extent to which processing conditions can be modulated, therefore dry-fractionated ingredients tend to exhibit a narrower spectrum of techno-functional properties and consequently lower versatility compared to wet-extracted proteins. By contrast, wet extraction processes can be adjusted by modifying parameters such as pH, ionic strength, and temperature, and may be further assisted by enzymatic treatments. This allows the modulation of protein purity (Chandran et al., 2024) and, overall, broadens the range of techno-functional properties of the ingredients. In addition, the washing steps are useful for reducing the volatile compounds responsible for the undesirable odors, i.e., grassy, green, and beany, which are typical of the raw matter. On the other hand, these compounds are retained in dry-fractionated proteins, potentially contributing to negative acceptance by consumer (García Arteaga et al., 2020; Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2022). Consequently, these downsides limit the industrial application of dry-fractionated ingredients for food development. Furthermore, dry-fractionated protein ingredients often struggle to mimic and replicate the techno-functional properties of more conventional and widely used animal-based protein ingredients (Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2022).

To overcome these drawbacks, different strategies have been proposed, including physical and biotechnological treatments (Gasparre et al., 2025; Gulzar et al., 2024; Périé et al., 2025; Perri et al., 2024). Among these, enzymatic hydrolysis represents a strategy to improve both the techno-functional and sensory properties of plant-based proteins (Gasparre et al., 2025). The strengths of enzymatic hydrolysis are its high specificity, ease of control during processing, and minimal generation of by-products (Shuai et al., 2022). Enzymatic hydrolysis exploits the action of proteolytic enzymes to selectively cleave peptide bonds and modulate the properties of the hydrolysate (Rezvankhah et al., 2021; Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2023). The processing conditions that must be controlled include the type of enzyme, temperature, pH, and time of treatment. The final degree of hydrolysis represents a key parameter for monitoring the hydrolysis process, because as it is directly correlated with the techno-functional properties of the resulting ingredients (Gasparre et al., 2025; X. Xu et al., 2021).

Several food-grade proteases are available for enzymatic hydrolysis. Among these, Alcalase is an endoprotease that has been widely studied on protein ingredients obtained through isolation and wet extraction procedures. Alcalase is characterized by broad specificity and the ability to reach a higher degree of hydrolysis compared with other proteases (García Arteaga et al., 2020), making it suitable for different treatments and applications.

Previous research has highlighted how enzymatic hydrolysis markedly impacts the techno-functional and sensory characteristics of pulse protein hydrolysates (García Arteaga et al., 2020; Périé et al., 2025; Rodríguez-Martín et al., 2024; Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2023). For instance, Rodríguez-Martín et al. (2024) and Xu et al. (2020) reported an increase in solubility following Alcalase hydrolysis of chickpea protein concentrate and isolate, respectively. Similarly, Shuai et al. (2022) observed improved solubility after enzymatic hydrolysis of pea protein isolate with Alcalase, as well as an increase of foaming and emulsifying properties that varied based on the achieved degree of hydrolysis. García Arteaga et al. (2020) investigated the effects of enzymatic hydrolysis using different enzymes, including Alcalase, Savinase, and Esperase on the techno-functional and sensory properties of pea protein isolates. Their results showed improved techno-functional properties but also a DH-dependent increase in bitterness. Lastly, Li et al. (2025) evaluated the effect of controlled enzymatic hydrolysis on soy protein isolate, observing a significant reduction of aldehydes, which are typically responsible for the legumes' off-notes. However, as previously mentioned, enzymatic hydrolysis has been investigated predominantly on pulse protein isolates obtained through wet extraction, while dry-fractionated protein ingredients remain largely unexplored, representing a relevant research gap. Additionally, the effects of hydrolysis on the volatile compounds concentration and, in turn, their contribution to odor characterization are still unknown.

Based on these premises, we propose that enzymatic hydrolysis with Alcalase can be applied to dry-fractionated proteins to i) modulate their techno-functional properties, with the aim of expanding the range of possible applications, and ii) improve their aroma profile, reducing the odor-active compounds responsible for unpleasant legume-flavor. To address these objectives, a careful evaluation of the effects of the processing parameters is essential to achieve precise control of hydrolysis and to tailor ingredients for specific food applications. For this reason, the enzymatic hydrolysis of dry-fractionated yellow pea proteins with Alcalase was investigated under different processing conditions, evaluating the effects on protein structure, techno-functional properties, volatile profile, and odor-active compounds, while also providing indications for the technological utilization of the resulting protein hydrolysates.

Materials and methods

Materials

Dry-fractionated yellow pea protein (PG, with a protein content of 55 g/100g dry matter) was provided by Innovaprot Srl (Gravina in Puglia, Italia). The protein content (N × 6.25) was determined according to the Kjeldahl official method described in AOAC 979.09 (AOAC, 2006). Alcalase 2.5 L FG (2.5 AU-A g−1, endoprotease) was provided by Univar Solutions LLC (Downers Grove, Illinois, USA). All the employed solvents and reagents were purchased from Merck KGaA (Darmstadt, Germany), unless otherwise specified.

Design of Experiments (DoE) and enzymatic hydrolysis

The enzymatic hydrolysis was studied using the Design of Experiments approach, investigating the effects of the most relevant technological variables, namely temperature, hydrolysis time, and presence of enzyme. Specifically, a D-optimal design was employed to assess the effect of two different temperatures (50 °C and 60 °C) at four sampling times (15, 97.5, 140, and 180 min) and the presence/absence of enzyme (coded as 1 and 0, respectively). This last variable was included to replicate the experimental conditions as a control, in order to assess any possible effect of endogenous enzymes and/or any physicochemical effect given by the treatment at 50-60 °C during the time of processing. In total, 16 experimental trials with 8 replicates to estimate the lack of fit and the design error (De Angelis et al., 2022) were carried out (as reported in Table 1). The entire design was replicated twice in two different hydrolysis sessions.

A suspension of yellow pea dry-fractionated protein and ultrapure milli-q water (30% w/v) was prepared and homogenized for 15 min using an Ultra-Turrax (T-25, IKAWerke GmbH & Co. KG, Staufen, Germany). The suspension was adjusted to pH 8.0 (optimum pH value for the enzyme) with NaOH 2M and then stored overnight at 4 °C to ensure proper hydration. Then, for the trials containing the enzyme, Alcalase was added at 2% (w/v) with respect to the protein content of the suspension. This concentration was recommended by the supplier. The mixture was heated to reach the temperatures planned by the experimental design, using a thermostatic bath (GDE, VELP Scientifica Srl, Usmate, Italy). The selected temperatures represent the optimum range of operation for Alcalase and were chosen according to literature (Shuai et al., 2022). Hydrolysis was carried out for a total of 180 min, under continuous stirring. pH was measured and kept constant during the analysis by adjusting it with NaOH 2M; meanwhile 100 mL samples were collected at regular intervals (15, 97.5, 140, and 180 min). During preliminary trials, the protein content of each sampling was determined to verify that the sample remained representative of the protein concentration chosen for the hydrolysis during the whole time of processing.

Enzyme inactivation was achieved by incubation in a thermostatic bath (95 °C, 20 min). Samples were then frozen (−20 °C), freeze-dried (Lyovapor L-200, BUCHI, Flawil, Switzerland), finely ground, sieved (⌀ = 1 mm), and stored at −20 °C.

Degree of hydrolysis, total free amino acids, peptide profile, intrinsic fluorescence and antinutritional factors

Degree of hydrolysis (DH) was calculated in accordance with Nielsen et al. (2001) with some variations. 3.81 g of Di-Sodium Tetraborate Decahydrate and 0.1g of Sodium-Dodecyl-Sulfate were completely dissolved in approximately 80 mL of ultrapure milli-q water. 0.08 g of o-Phthaldialdehyde 97% (OPA) was dissolved in 2 mL of absolute ethanol, added to the solution together with 0.088 g of dithiothreitol 99%, and the solution was brought to 100 mL with ultrapure milli-q water. Subsequently, 0.025 g of sample was suspended with 5 mL of ultrapure milli-q water, stirred for 1 h, then centrifuged (11,000 rpm, 10 min). 1 mL of supernatant was filtered (0.22 μm, RC) and mixed with 5 μL of TFA 10% in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube, left to rest for 1 h, then centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 10 min) to recover the supernatant. 1.5 mL OPA solution and 200 μL of supernatant were mixed in a UV-VIS cuvette, which was left to rest for exactly 2 min before carrying out the reading at 340 nm in a spectrophotometer (Cary 60 UV-Vis, Agilent Technologies, Mulgrave, Australia). The blank was prepared similarly, replacing the sample with an equal amount of ultrapure milli-q water. DH quantification, expressed as g/100g, was carried out by means of a tryptone calibration curve (R2 > 0.99, 0.05 – 0.6 mg/mL).

For total free amino acids (TFAA) determination, water/salt-soluble extracts (WSE) were prepared from each sample according to the method originally described by Weiss et al. (1993). TFAA were determined after protein precipitation with 5% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid using the ninhydrin colorimetric assay, with glycine as the calibration standard, according to the method described by Doi et al. (1981). Peptide profiles were analyzed by reverse-phase fast protein liquid chromatography (RP-FPLC) using a Resource RPC column (C18, 3 mL volume, 6.4 × 100 mm, 15 μm particle size) and an ÄKTA FPLC system equipped with a UV detector operating at 214 nm (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences AB, Uppsala, Sweden) following the method reported by Rizzello et al. (2017). Briefly, 1 mL of WSE was mixed with 0.05% (v/v) trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) and centrifuged (10,000×g, 10 min). The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm pore-size filter and loaded onto the column. Gradient elution was performed at a flow rate of 1 mL/min using a mobile phase composed of water and acetonitrile containing 0.05% TFA. The concentration of acetonitrile was increased linearly from 5% to 46% between 12.5 and 53 min and from 46% to 100% between 53 and 72 min. Chromatographic data were processed using UNICORN software (version 4.0), and the results are expressed as percentage area of the peaks eluting under each gradient condition.

Regarding intrinsic fluorescence, the excitation-emission matrices (EEMs) of protein suspensions at 0.1 mg/mL in double-distilled water were acquired with a Fluoromax 4 spectrofluorometer (Horiba, Kyoto, Japan), in a right-angle acquisition geometry, with the method described by Caputi et al. (2025) with some modification. The excitation spectra was set in the range 250-310 nm, with emission wavelengths ranging from 280 to 430 nm, at 2 nm steps and integration time 0.1 s. The measurements were made in quartz cuvettes with an optical path length of 1 cm. The EEMs datasets were decomposed by Parallel Factor Analysis (PARAFAC) according to the statistical procedure described by Caputi et al. (2025). The scores of Component 1, identified as the optimal model component, were used to describe the effects of hydrolysis on protein fluorescence and, consequently, on protein structural modifications.

The quantification of antinutritional factors such as phytic acid and raffinose was carried out using the Megazyme K-PHYT 05/07 kit and the Raffinose/D-Galactose Assay Kit K-RAFGA (Megazyme International Ireland Limited, Bray, Ireland), respectively, following the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Techno-functional properties

Water absorption index (WAI) and water solubility index (WSI) were determined as described by De Angelis, Latrofa, Squeo, et al. (2024b) with a slight change. For WAI and WSI, 1.75 g of sample was mixed with 15 mL of distilled water in pre-weighed Falcon tubes. The suspension was heated at 80 °C for 30 min, then centrifuged (3000×g, 20 min). The supernatant was transferred into a pre-weighed porcelain evaporating dish and dried overnight at 105 °C, while the sediment was weighed. WAI was calculated as the ratio of the weight of the sediment to the weight of the protein ingredient, whereas WSI was expressed as the percentage ratio of the solid content of the supernatant relative to the weight of the ingredient.

The viscosity of the samples was measured with a rheometer (HAAKE MARS iQ Air, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) equipped with a parallel plate geometry (P35/Ti-02180932). The determinations were carried out at a 1 mm gap between the plates. The shear rate ramp test was performed by increasing the shear rate from 0.1 to 100 s−1 at 25 °C for a total of 320 s. The viscosity was examined at a shear rate of 10 s−1 (Steffe, 1996).

Foaming ability (FA) and foam stability (FS) at 20 min (FS20) were measured according to De Angelis, Latrofa, Squeo, et al. (2024b). FA was expressed as the % of volume increase, while FS20 was calculated as the percentage ratios of the foam volume at 20 min relative to the initial foam volume.

Determination of volatile organic compounds

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) analysis was carried out according to Caponio et al. (2024) with slight modifications, using a Thermo Trace 1300 gas chromatograph coupled with a Thermo ISQ series 3.2 SP1(Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA) mass spectrometer. Briefly, 0.5 g of sample, 4 mL of aqueous solution of NaCl (20 g/100 g), and 150 μL of 3-pentanone (81.3 ng) as an internal standard. The internal standard was selected after a preliminary characterization of the volatile profile of the samples. The chosen compound was not naturally present in the samples and exhibited a retention time that avoided co-elution or interference with endogenous volatiles. VOCs were adsorbed using a 1 cm DVB/CAR/PDMS SPME fiber (50/30 μm, Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) exposed in the vial's headspace (50 °C, 40 min), and subsequently desorbed in the GC's injection port operating in splitless mode at 200 °C for 2 min. Separation of the VOCs was performed on a HP-Innowax (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) polar capillary column (60 m length × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness). The oven temperature was initially held for 5 min at 35 °C, then increased to 50 °C by 5 °C/min and held for 5 min. Then, it was increased to 230 °C by 5.5 °C/min and maintained for 5 min. Helium at a 30 kPa pressure was used as carrier gas with a flow of 1.0 mL/min. The mass detector was set as follows: interface temperature of 250 °C, source temperature of 250 °C, ionization energy of 70 eV, and scan range of 33–270 amu. Compound identification was performed by comparing mass spectra with those in the library (NIST with mainlib as sub-library), applying a minimum similarity threshold of 80%. The matches exceeding this threshold were considered for tentative identification, while quantification was carried out by comparison with the internal standard. The concentration of VOCs was expressed as mg/kg of sample. The analyses were carried out in duplicate, and hexanal, 2-heptanone, 1-hexanol, and 1-penten-3-ol were used for model development as they are markers for possible off-flavor or unpleasant odors (Karolkowski et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025; Ponskhe et al., 2025).

Determination of volatile odor-active compounds

Solid-Phase Microextraction-Gas Chromatography-Mass Spectrometry-Olfactometry (SPME-GC-MS-O) analysis was performed according to Antonino et al. (2026). Specifically, volatile compounds were extracted via SPME by weighing 1 ± 0.05 g of sample into 20 mL vials. Using a PAL System autosampler, each vial underwent a 10-min incubation at 37 °C with agitation at 250 rpm. The extraction phase utilized a 2 cm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (50/30 μm, Supelco, Bellefonte, PA) for 15 min at 37 °C, followed by thermal desorption for 2 min at 200 °C within the injection port.

For GC-MS-O analysis, an Agilent 8860 GC integrated with a 5977C MS was used. Analytes were injected in splitless mode and separated on a VF-WAX MS capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm i.d. × 0.25 μm film thickness, Agilent Technologies). The oven temperature program started at 40 °C for 5 min, increased to 140 °C at 4 °C/min, and finally reached 210 °C at 10 °C/min, where it was maintained for 7.5 min. High-purity helium (99.99%) served as the carrier gas at a constant flow of 1.8 mL/min. The MS detector operated with an ionization energy of 70 eV, a source temperature of 230 °C, and a full-scan acquisition range from 10 to 300 m/z. Compound identification was performed by comparing mass spectra with those in the library (NIST11 containing sub-libraries: mainlib and replib), applying a minimum similarity threshold of 80%. Only matches exceeding this threshold were considered for tentative identification. While quantification was performed using 3-pentanone (81.3 ng) as an internal standard, with concentrations expressed in μg/kg.

Odor-active compounds were characterized using a Gerstel ODP 4 sniffing system (Gerstel, Mülheim, Germany), with the GC effluent split equally between the MS detector and the olfactometric port as determined by the Gerstel Column Calculator. High-purity nitrogen (99.99%) was used for the olfactometer, with the port maintained at 240 °C and the effluent mixed with humidified nitrogen. A panel of three experts (aged 28 to 61) evaluated the separated analytes, recording odor descriptors and intensities on a 5-point scale (0-4, absent to extremely intense).

Statistical analysis

The hydrolysis experiment was performed in two distinct sessions, and analyses were conducted on both replicates in duplicates. The results of the experimental design were modeled according to the postulated two-factor interaction model. For each response, the coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted coefficient of determination (R2 adj), and analysis of variance (ANOVA) at a significance level of 0.05 for the regression model and its coefficients were calculated using Design-Expert 11 software (Stat-Ease Inc., Minneapolis, USA). The same software was used for the graphical representations of the DoE results. Odor-active compound results were graphically represented by using OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Overall, the model appeared significant for all responses (p ≤ 0.05) and exhibited high R2 and R2 adj values. These results confirm that the experimental design adequately described the variability of the system within the investigated experimental domain and that the selected factors were appropriate for explaining the main changes occurring during hydrolysis. Specifically, among the processing conditions considered, the presence/absence of the enzyme (C) had the overall strongest and most significant influence on most of the responses. This behavior was indeed expected, considering that the enzyme was deliberately added to modulate the characteristics of the protein ingredients. In fact, the first inspection of the results clearly shows that hydrolysis positively affected all indices related to proteolysis, such as degree of hydrolysis (DH), total free amino acids (TFAA), and peptide profile. By contrast, the negative coefficients observed for the selected volatile compounds indicate that enzymatic treatment also influenced the volatile profile of the protein ingredients, which was a desired outcome according to the aims of this research.

Besides the effect of the enzyme, temperature (A) and time (B) were significant for some responses. However, the magnitude of the coefficients suggests that time had the strongest effect on the responses under investigation. In fact, Alcalase is able to hydrolyze proteins throughout the entire duration of the experiment (Dent et al., 2023). Additionally, significant interactions were found, especially between enzyme and time, suggesting that the effects of individual factors were not solely additive, and that hydrolysis kinetics depended on the combined effect of enzyme presence and reaction duration.

Finally, the limited statistical significance observed for raffinose and phytic acid indicates that the selected hydrolysis conditions had only a negligible impact on these antinutritional factors. This suggests that Alcalase treatment alone is not sufficient to address one of the limitations of dry-fractionated proteins, namely the presence of antinutritional compounds (De Angelis et al., 2021). These compounds are known to reduce protein digestibility and mineral bioavailability, and may also cause gastrointestinal discomfort. Therefore, additional processing strategies (e.g., fermentation or specific enzymatic systems such as phytases or α-galactosidases) may be required to achieve substantial reductions of these compounds (Schutyser et al., 2025).

Consequently, based on these findings, the effects of processing variables on each response were further explored through contour plots, allowing the visualization of the combined influence of time and temperature in the presence/absence of enzyme.

Protein hydrolysis, free amino acids, peptide profile, and intrinsic fluorescence

The effects of the processing variables on the protein hydrolysis are depicted in Fig. 1a–h, showing the contour plots of the degree of hydrolysis (DH, Fig. 1a and b), total free amino acids (TFAA, Fig. 1c and d), and the two major fractions of peptides separated through the RP-FPLC analysis (Fig. 1e–h). In the samples not processed with the enzyme (Fig. 1a,c,e,g), these indices remained nearly constant throughout the whole 180 min of hydrolysis at both 50 °C and 60 °C, indicating negligible hydrolytic activity given by the endogenous enzymes. Time of treatment was the second most influencing factor, after the presence/absence of enzyme, for all the indices related to proteolysis, indicating that prolonged treatment and, to a lesser extent, higher thermal conditions favor peptide bond cleavage.

Contour plots describing: (a, b) degree of hydrolysis (DH, expressed as %); (c, d) total free amino acids (TFAA, mg/kg); and (e–h) percentage area of the peaks eluting at 0% B and within the 0–46% B gradient during FPLC analysis. Non-hydrolyzed trials are shown in the upper panels, whereas the lower panels correspond to samples treated with Alcalase.

Overall, high DH was found even after 15 min of hydrolysis, while temperature also significantly affected DH, with the highest values obtained at 60 °C (Fig. 1b). These findings are consistent with previous studies on plant protein isolates treated with Alcalase, which reported similar increases in DH (Dent et al., 2023; García Arteaga et al., 2020; Shuai et al., 2022). Specifically, both Dent et al. (2023) and Shuai et al. (2022) noticed an increase in DH over processing time. The increase in DH is directly related to the increase in TFAA detected in hydrolyzed samples. For instance, Zheng et al. (2024) hydrolyzed whey protein isolates, using Alcalase and Neutrase in combination, observing an increase over time in low molecular weight peptides as well as free amino acids as a function of DH. Verni et al. (2025) achieved a 7-fold increase in TFAA by the end of a multi-step hydrolysis treatment on Tenebrio molitor powder with Alcalase and Flavourzyme.

To further investigate the protein hydrolysis process, peptide profiles were analyzed by RP-FPLC. As an example, Fig. 2 reports the chromatogram of the samples treated for 180 min at 60 °C. Peptides from control samples mainly eluted along the acetonitrile gradient (0-46% phase B), whereas hydrolyzed samples showed a shift toward early-eluting peaks, with most species detected at 0% of phase B. This shift suggests the formation of smaller and more hydrophilic peptides as a result of enzymatic hydrolysis. Overall, pea protein hydrolyzed with Alcalase appears to contain a higher proportion of polar peptides, which may reflect a greater degree of degradation and reduced hydrophobic interactions compared to the control (Shuai et al., 2022; Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2022). From a sensory perspective, this increase in more hydrophilic peptides may be associated with lower bitterness, as more hydrophobic peptides are generally linked to stronger bitter taste (B. Liu et al., 2022). Therefore, this aspect should be considered in future optimization of the enzymatic protocol. Also in this case, time of hydrolysis emerged as a major factor influencing these changes, with longer incubation favoring the polar fraction and reducing the proportion of peptides eluting within the 0-46% phase B range.

Samples treated with Alcalase are shown in red, whereas control samples incubated without enzyme are shown in black. The blue line represents the acetonitrile gradient (mobile phase B).

Fluorescence excitation-emission matrices were modeled using PARAFAC, which decomposes the three-way dataset into independent fluorescent components (Caputi et al., 2025). In our case, PARAFAC resulted in one significant component, therefore, the scores of Component 1 (Supplementary Table S1) were used as an indicator of the intensity variation of the dominant fluorescence feature, allowing the comparison of hydrolysis-induced structural changes among samples. Based on the model, neither temperature nor hydrolysis time significantly affected fluorescence patterns, whereas the enzyme presence represented the primary driving factor governing structural rearrangements detectable by fluorescence spectroscopy (Table 2). PARAFAC scores increased in hydrolyzed samples, suggesting increased exposure of aromatic amino acid residues, including phenylalanine, tryptophan, and tyrosine (Avramenko et al., 2013) to the aqueous environment (X. Xu et al., 2016). These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting increased fluorescence intensity following enzymatic hydrolysis (X. Wang et al., 2022; You et al., 2026), although opposite trends have also been described when aggregation may occur through hydrophobic interaction of the exposed groups (X. Xu et al., 2016; You et al., 2026). Consequently, the results observed under the present experimental conditions suggest that aggregation-driven phenomena were negligible and that peptide and amino acid formation predominated over aggregation processes.

Effects of alcalase hydrolysis on techno-functional properties

The contour plots for the techno-functional properties are depicted in Fig. 3. The results obtained for these properties are clearly explained by the hydrolysis of the proteins. In fact, a remarkable increase in WSI (Fig. 1a and b), accompanied by a decrease in WAI (Fig. 1c and d) and viscosity (Fig. 1e and f), was observed in hydrolyzed pea protein. Specifically, the protein treated with Alcalase showed a WSI higher than ⁓50%, whereas the non-hydrolyzed samples showed a WSI of approximately 35%. An opposite trend was instead observed for WAI. In fact, an inverse correlation between WSI and WAI is often reported (De Angelis, Latrofa, Squeo, et al., 2024b). WSI quantifies the soluble materials remaining after a heating process, whereas WAI explains the ability of the ingredient to retain water after a thermal treatment carried out in excess of water (De Angelis, Latrofa, Squeo, et al., 2024b). Consequently, the observed behavior can be justified by the presence of small and soluble peptides and amino acids in the hydrolyzed protein (Shuai et al., 2022), as also evidenced by the analysis of DH (Fig. 1) and by the peptide profile determined with RP-FPLC (Fig. 2). Indeed, DH and WSI are closely correlated (Xu et al., 2020). WSI was positively and significantly affected by the time of hydrolysis (Table 2). Moreover, a significant interaction between time and temperature was found, suggesting that the effect of processing time on solubility is modulated by temperature. In particular, at 60 °C, hydrolyzed samples exhibited a more pronounced increase over time compared to those treated at 50 °C. This behavior may be explained by the higher degree of hydrolysis achieved at 60 °C, as also described by Zhang et al. (2022). This, in turn, likely promoted the formation of more soluble peptides, thereby increasing the overall solubility and indicating enhanced sensitivity of WSI to prolonged hydrolysis under more severe thermal conditions (Shuai et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022).

Contour plots describing: (a, b) Water solubility index (WSI, expressed as %); (c, d) water absorption index (WAI, dimensionless); (e, f) viscosity at 10 s−1 (Pas); (g, h) foaming activity (FA, expressed as %); and (i, l) foaming stability after 20 min (FS20, expressed as %). Non-hydrolyzed trials are shown in the upper panels, whereas the lower panels correspond to samples treated with Alcalase.

The viscosity of the protein suspension was evaluated to better understand the behavior of the protein ingredient when dispersed in water. Viscosity was significantly reduced by Alcalase hydrolysis (Fig. 3e and f). Temperature also significantly contributed to viscosity reduction (Table 2), even in non-hydrolyzed samples (Fig. 3e). In fact, at 60 °C, the occurrence of protein denaturation phenomena might have limited the ability of the protein to form a viscous matrix (Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2022). Overall, the lower viscosity of hydrolyzed samples can be explained by the increase in protein solubility and formation of low molecular weight peptides, which are more readily solubilized in water and have a reduced ability to contribute to network formation or to the development of a structured system (Klost et al., 2020).

Based on these results, we suggest possible applications of hydrolyzed dry-fractionated protein in the beverage industry, where key techno-functional requirements include high dispersion ability, solubility, and minimal sedimentation, and a limited impact on the flow properties of the final product (Perri et al., 2026).

Foaming properties of the ingredients were modulated by Alcalase hydrolysis. Foaming ability (FA) was negatively affected by both temperature and time, while the enzyme presence had a significant positive effect (Table 2). The only significant interaction was between time and enzyme, indicating a progressive reduction in FA in hydrolyzed samples as treatment time increased. This is clearly visible from the contour plots (Fig. 3g and h). The reduction of FA was more pronounced under higher temperature conditions (Fig. 3h). In fact, it is well known that peptides with low molecular weight inhibit the adsorption of protein at the air-water interface, thus limiting the foaming ability (Damodaran, 2005). This also clarifies the observations related to foam stability after 20 min (FS20), which describes the ability to maintain the aerated structure over time. Enzymatic hydrolysis led to a deterioration of foam stability (Table 2 and Fig. 3d). The peptides formed during hydrolysis are likely too small to form a cohesive and resistant interfacial film, leading to rapid coalescence (Vogelsang-O’Dwyer et al., 2022). Similar behavior was reported by Ahmed et al. (2019) and Al-Ruwaih et al. (2019), who observed an increase in FA coupled with reduced FS following Alcalase hydrolysis of lentil and kidney bean proteins, respectively.

In view of these findings, we conclude that Alcalase hydrolysis modulated the techno-functional properties of dry-fractionated protein, making it more suitable for the development of protein beverages, but less adapted to applications where high water absorption, stable foams, or other interfacial properties are required, including creams and desserts. From this perspective, other authors recently proposed that further modulation of the protein functionality can be given by partial or limited hydrolysis protocols (X. Wang et al., 2022; X. Xu et al., 2016), which can selectively enhance the interfacial properties (M. Wang et al., 2025). Nevertheless, one of the major challenges in the utilization of dry-fractionated protein is given by the sensory properties, which closely resemble the green and beany notes typical of fresh legumes (De Angelis, Latrofa, Caponio, et al., 2024). For this reason, in the next section of this manuscript, the main volatile compounds and the olfactometric profile are discussed.

Effects of alcalase hydrolysis on volatile profile

To investigate the effects of hydrolysis on the volatile profile and, consequently, on the odor characteristics of the ingredients, two complementary approaches were adopted. First, selected volatile compounds previously reported in the literature as major contributors to undesirable green and beany notes were monitored. Specifically, the concentrations of hexanal, 2-heptanone, 1-hexanol, and 2-pentyl-furan (Karolkowski et al., 2021; Li et al., 2025; Ponskhe et al., 2025) were used as markers to evaluate the influence of the processing variables (Table 2, Fig. 4a–h). Subsequently, odor-active compounds were evaluated at four points within the experimental domain to better investigate the impact of the hydrolysis process as a strategy for reducing undesirable odors. In addition, this approach was useful in confirming the odor descriptors of markers reported in the literature and, eventually, suggesting new markers associated with undesirable sensory perceptions.

Contour plots describing the marker organic volatile compounds (expressed as mg/kg). Non-hydrolyzed trials are shown in the upper panels, whereas the lower panels correspond to samples treated with Alcalase.

Marker volatile organic compounds

The coefficients reported in Table 2 highlight a strong effect of Alcalase hydrolysis on all the selected compounds, leading to a significant reduction in their concentration. Among these compounds, hexanal, responsible for undesirable grassy and beany notes (Zhogoleva et al., 2023), was markedly reduced in hydrolyzed samples compared with samples not treated with enzyme. Hexanal is a product of 13-hydroperoxide degradation, formed during linoleic acid oxidation via lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway (Karolkowski et al., 2021) or by non-enzymatic autoxidation of linoleic acid. Temperature also negatively influenced hexanal content, although its impact was substantially lower than that of the enzyme action. In fact, the more pronounced reduction of hexanal observed at 60 °C compared to 50 °C may be attributed to the thermal inactivation of lipoxygenase, which typically begins at approximately 60 °C, limiting the formation of lipid oxidation products (Shi et al., 2020). For the enzyme-treated samples, the formation of low molecular weight peptides and protein conformational changes occurring during hydrolysis may lead to the exposure of specific binding sites with affinity for aldehyde functional groups, thereby reducing their volatility (Li et al., 2025). Furthermore, the model revealed significant negative time-temperature and time-enzyme interactions, highlighting a temperature and enzyme-dependent reduction of hexanal over time, which can be clearly observed in Fig. 4a and b.

2-heptanone is a ketone generated through LOX-mediated lipid oxidation and free fatty acids degradation (Karolkowski et al., 2021; Ponskhe et al., 2025). This compound was significantly affected by the enzymatic treatment, with negative interactions for both time-temperature and time-enzyme. Hydrolyzed samples exhibited the lowest 2-heptanone concentrations, as indicated by the predominance of blue regions in the contour plots (Fig. 4d). In control samples, 2-heptanone showed trends similar to those observed for hexanal, with differences between 50 and 60 °C likely due to partial inactivation of lipoxygenase at higher temperatures.

1-hexanol and 1-penten-3-ol were significantly affected by the enzyme presence and treatment time, while 1-hexanol was also influenced by temperature, indicating that a decrease was observed with increasing temperature and time, even in non-hydrolyzed samples (Fig. 4e,g). 1-hexanol is generally associated with herbaceous, woody, green sensorial notes and originates from lipid oxidation pathways. Specifically, fatty acid hydroperoxides are formed and subsequently cleaved by hydroperoxide lyase into aldehydes, which can then be reduced by alcohol dehydrogenase into their corresponding alcohols (Karolkowski et al., 2021; Ponskhe et al., 2025). The reductions observed in non-hydrolyzed samples may be explained by the partial inactivation of the enzymes involved in lipoxygenase-hydroperoxide lyase pathway, thereby limiting the formation of aldehyde- and alcohol-type volatile organic compounds (Ponskhe et al., 2025). A substantial reduction in 1-hexanol was also reported by Weng et al. (2021), following enzymatic hydrolysis with Alcalase on soy protein isolates.

Odor-active compounds

While the targeted analysis of selected VOCs provided quantitative evidence of the reduction of marker compounds associated with green and beany notes, instrumental data alone do not necessarily reflect their actual sensory relevance. Generally, the Odor Activity Value, determined by the ratio of a compound's concentration in the sample to its odor threshold in water or other media, provides only a theoretical estimation of each volatile's contribution to the overall aroma. However, olfactory perception is governed not only by individual detection thresholds but also by synergistic effects and complex matrix interactions (Antonino et al., 2026). Therefore, to complement the chemical characterization and better understand the sensory implications of the hydrolysis process, an olfactometric analysis was performed to identify the odor-active compounds, evaluate their positive/negative contribution to the aroma, and assess how the enzymatic process influenced them.

GC-MS-O analysis revealed the presence of 51 volatile compounds (Supplementary Table S2). The resulting volatile profile was characterized by molecules typically found in pea-based ingredients and derived products (Karolkowski et al., 2021; Y. Liu et al., 2023; Zhogoleva et al., 2023). Despite numerous studies on the volatile profiles of pea flours, isolates, and protein concentrates, there is currently a lack of literature data regarding the volatile compounds of dry-fractionated pea flour, both in its native and hydrolyzed forms. Among the detected compounds, 22 were identified as odor-active and selected to evaluate the effect of enzymatic treatment on the ingredients' aroma profile; their concentrations and odor intensities are detailed in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S3.

Graphical representation of the concentration of the 22 odor-active compounds identified by GC–O, their descriptors, and their odor intensities on a scale from 0 (absent) to 4 (extremely intense). H: hydrolyzed samples (filled symbols); N: non-hydrolyzed samples (empty symbols); 50 and 60 indicate the temperature of treatment (red and blue symbols, respectively); 15 and 180 indicate the time of treatment.

The ingredients prepared using the conditions described in this study revealed that most odor-active compounds belonged to the ketones and hydrocarbons classes, with six and four molecules, respectively. With respect to the descriptors of the odor-active compounds, eight of them, namely 2-heptanone, hexanal, 1-hexanol, 1-penten-3-ol, 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, 1,3-di-tert-butylbenzene, undecane, and 2-pentylfuran, are characterized by beany and grassy notes. Interestingly, we found that presence of 1,3-Di-tert-butylbenzene markedly contributed to the beany flavor. To the best of our knowledge, the odor perception of this compound has not been previously reported in the literature (Supplementary Table S3). Therefore, it may deserve further investigation to confirm the presence of this compound in dry-fractionated protein and study its metabolic origin. The remaining molecules are generally considered undesirable when developing versatile protein ingredients intended for broad food applications. Such compounds mainly originate from the oxidation of free fatty acids (Karolkowski et al., 2021; M. Xu et al., 2020; Zhogoleva et al., 2023). On the other hand, the remaining 14 odor-active compounds contributed to the overall aroma profile of the ingredients, mainly providing more pleasant odor notes.

Overall, hydrolyzed samples showed a significant reduction in the concentration of odor-active compounds (Fig. 5), corroborating the trends observed and already discussed for the marker compounds (Section 3.4.1). This was reflected in the lower odor intensities detected in samples treated with Alcalase compared with non-hydrolyzed ones. Specifically, five compounds responsible for the undesirable notes (hexanal, 2-ethyl-1-hexanol, 1,3-di-tert-butylbenzene, undecane, and 2-pentylfuran) showed a significant decrement in odor intensity. Remarkably, 2-pentylfuran was not perceived anymore in both hydrolyzed samples. This can be considered a positive outcome for the development of a more sensory-balanced protein ingredient, with lower beany flavor compared to the non-hydrolyzed material (M. Xu et al., 2020; Zhogoleva et al., 2023). Conversely, (E)-2-tetradecene (nutty, grassy) and ethyl octanoate (fruity, beany) were the only positive attributes showing a decrease following Alcalase hydrolysis. Particularly, ethyl octanoate significantly influences the odor perception due to its low odor threshold. This compound is a known predominant aroma component in dried pea protein concentrates and isolates (Y. Liu et al., 2023). Unlike aldehydes and ketones, which are derived from lipid oxidation, this ester is produced via lipid metabolism through the reaction between ethanol and fatty acid chains (Pires et al., 2014). Studies indicate that microbial enzymatic metabolic processes can significantly reduce green and beany aldehydes and ketones while simultaneously producing esters like ethyl octanoate, which effects that generate pleasant fruity notes (El Youssef et al., 2020).

Also temperature, regardless of the enzymatic treatment, appears to positively affect odor perception, supporting the observation reported in Table 2, as 1-hexanol and 1-penten-3-ol concentrations fell below their respective detection thresholds in samples treated at 60 °C, regardless of the enzymatic treatment, thereby preventing their contribution to beany, grassy, and pungent notes. In fact, it is well known that thermal treatments improve sensory perception of legume-based ingredients (Sahin et al., 2024).

By contrast, hydrolyzed samples showed higher concentrations and odor intensities of 2-nonanone, resulting in a more pronounced grassy and herbal perception compared with non-hydrolyzed samples. Finally, other compounds associated with pleasant notes, including 2-acetylpyridine (popcorn, nutty, toasty), 2-acetylthiazole (toasty), 4-methoxybenzaldehyde (grassy, sweet), benzaldehyde (earthy, nutty), acetic acid (pungent), and 2-phenylethyl acetate (sweet, fruity), butyrolactone (nutty), benzothiazole (vegetable), showed variations in concentration, but their olfactory perception remains unaffected by the enzymatic hydrolysis.

Overall, these results indicate that the positive aromatic attributes were preserved during the treatment. Alcalase hydrolysis therefore represents an effective strategy to refine the aroma profile of dry-fractionated pea proteins by mitigating the intensity of odor-active compounds associated with reduced consumer acceptance.

Main hypothesis concerning aroma profile modulation

Based on the results obtained in this research, some hypotheses may be proposed to explain the flavor modulation observed after enzymatic hydrolysis. However, these mechanisms were not directly investigated in the present study and therefore require a validation in similar matrices.

A first mechanism might be related to a partial inactivation of the endogenous enzymes commonly involved in lipid oxidation pathways and responsible for the generation of volatile compounds. Specifically, thermal treatment, particularly at 60 °C, may have partially inactivated lipoxygenase and hydroperoxide lyase (Shi et al., 2020). This hypothesis is supported by the reduction of several aldehydes and alcohols associated with green and beany notes, which was also observed in non-hydrolyzed samples treated at higher temperature.

Secondly, as supported by the intrinsic fluorescence analysis, Alcalase hydrolysis modifies the protein conformation, leading to the exposure of previously-buried hydrophobic and aromatic residues. These groups can interact with volatile molecules, binding them and decreasing their volatility (Li et al., 2025; Saffarionpour, 2024), consequently limiting their odor activity. This mechanism has been previously investigated by Li et al. (2025), where the authors suggested that both covalent and non-covalent interactions between aldehydes and hydrolyzed soy protein isolate are responsible for off-flavor mitigation.

As a final speculation, the hydrolysis process may alter volatile generating pathways through release of new precursors which can be involved in the formation or increased abundance of specific volatile compounds. This mechanism may explain the increased concentration of some compounds (such as 2-nonanone) observed in hydrolyzed samples, despite the overall reduction of undesirable odorants.

These results suggest that Alcalase hydrolysis affects aroma profile through the combination of several pathways. This may inspire future studies aimed at better understanding the mechanisms responsible for flavor modulation in hydrolyzed pulse protein, particularly the rate of enzymatic inhibition and the role of protein-volatile interactions.

Conclusion

Alcalase hydrolysis effectively modulated the techno-functional properties and aroma profile of dry-fractionated pea protein. The results of the experimental design highlighted that enzyme addition was the main factor driving the observed changes, whereas hydrolysis time further enhanced proteolytic effects, and temperature had a secondary but significant influence on some responses. Hydrolyzed samples exhibited high water solubility and low viscosity, whereas prolonged hydrolysis negatively affected the foaming activity and stability. Based on the techno-functional profile, hydrolyzed proteins appear to be suitable for beverage or liquid applications.

This study demonstrated that enzymatic hydrolysis can significantly modulate the odor profile of dry-fractionated pea protein. Several volatile compounds associated with beany notes decreased after hydrolysis, while most compounds associated with positive or neutral aromatic attributes remained unaffected. Moreover, an interesting finding of this study was that 1,3-di-tert-butylbenzene markedly contributed to the beany flavor. To the best of our knowledge, the odor properties of this compound have not been previously reported in the literature and therefore deserve further investigation. Although dry fractionation is specifically designed to minimize water and energy consumption, the introduction of a controlled aqueous enzymatic step can be considered a mild or hybrid strategy to functionalize protein ingredients. A comprehensive assessment of water and energy consumption, including comparison with conventional wet extraction followed by hydrolysis, is therefore required to properly evaluate the overall process impacts. This aspect should be addressed in future studies to better quantify the compromise between improved techno-functional performance and environmental impact.

Overall, the results support the use of enzymatic hydrolysis as a post-fractionation strategy to expand the application potential of dry-fractionated pulse proteins through the modulation of both functionality and aroma profile. In particular, hydrolysis may represent an effective approach to mitigate one of the main limitations of pulse protein ingredients, namely undesirable legume-like aroma.
