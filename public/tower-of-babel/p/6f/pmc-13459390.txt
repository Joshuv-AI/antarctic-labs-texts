Contributory and developmental social participation and depressive symptoms among older adults in China: urban–rural and gender disparities
Authors: Yao Yu, Jing Ma, Wei Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExisting research on social participation and later-life mental health often relies on fragmented classifications, pays insufficient attention to family-oriented contributory activities, and gives limited consideration to subgroup heterogeneity. To address these gaps, this study develops a contributory–developmental framework of social participation and examines the differential associations between various types of social participation and depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults.MethodsData were drawn from the 2020 wave of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS, N = 7,443 retired older adults). OLS regression models were used to estimate the associations between different participation types and depressive symptoms. Moderation models further examined whether these associations varied by urban–rural residence and gender. Sensitivity analyses included full-model estimation, inverse probability weighting, province fixed effects with clustered standard errors, and lagged dependent variable models using depressive symptoms measured in 2018.ResultsDifferent participation types showed distinct associations with depressive symptoms. Learning participation, active leisure, internet use, intergenerational childcare, and reemployment after retirement were associated with lower depressive symptoms, whereas intergenerational domestic support was associated with higher depressive symptoms. An urban–rural paradox emerged: rural older adults were more involved in some contributory activities, yet some of these activities showed weaker or less favorable associations with depressive symptoms than among urban respondents; by contrast, active leisure and internet use were more strongly associated with lower depressive symptoms among rural respondents despite their lower participation levels. Gender differences were more limited: they were mainly observed for learning participation and reemployment after retirement.DiscussionSocial participation is associated with depressive symptoms in later life in heterogeneous ways, depending on the type of activity and the social context in which participation occurs. Recognizing heterogeneity across participation types, urban–rural residence, and gender is essential for designing inclusive participation opportunities that support older adults’ mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07722-1.

Introduction

Improving the health of older adults is a key issue in actively addressing population aging. However, a striking contrast has emerged: while medical advances have significantly improved the physical health of older adults—with life expectancy in China reaching 79.25 years in 2025, an average annual increase of 0.26 years—mental health problems among the elderly, particularly the prevalence of depression, have been increasing year by year. According to the Fifth Sampling Survey on the Living Conditions of the Urban and Rural Elderly in China, individuals aged 60 and older are classified as elderly, and the prevalence of depression among the elderly in China has reached 49%, with the rate in urban areas at 61%, 26 percentage points higher than in rural areas. This widening disparity reveals a structural decoupling in the development of elderly health: we have successfully extended physical lifespan, but have failed to simultaneously safeguard the mental health of older adults.

How can we reduce depression levels among older adults? Existing research has explored this question from perspectives such as psychosocial factors, behavioral interventions, and environmental support. Most empirical analyses point to the positive role of social participation [7, 10, 16, 47]. However, within China’s unique social structure and institutional context, the relationship between social participation and depressive symptoms is far from a simple linear facilitation, and existing research remains inadequate in addressing this complexity. Specifically, first, there is the fragmentation of social participation typologies. The types of social participation among Chinese older adults are highly diverse, yet existing classification systems show significant divergence. Fragmented measurement and classification schemes across studies hamper precise assessment of the effects of social participation and severely impede dialogue between studies. Second, there is insufficient attention to family-oriented contributory activities. Chinese research has largely adopted classification frameworks derived from Western contexts [18, 1, 3], which fail to fully capture activities such as grandchild care and family caregiving that are commonly undertaken by Chinese older adults. Consequently, the impact of these activities on depression and mental health among Chinese seniors is often underestimated or misinterpreted. Third, there is inadequate explanation of the heterogeneous mechanisms underlying the effects of social participation. On the one hand, China’s flexible employment policies and delayed retirement reforms have promoted divergence in late-life trajectories [15, 32]. On the other hand, gender and urban–rural residence, as primary ascribed structural factors, fundamentally shape differences in values and accessibility of resources for social participation among older adults. However, there is a dearth of research examining how different types of social participation differentially affect depression across diverse older adult subgroups.

Motivated by these concerns, this study integrates the core principles of “Active Aging” [42] and “Productive Aging” [28] to construct an innovative analytical framework tailored to the Chinese cultural context—the “Contributory–Developmental” Framework of Social Participation. This framework, while giving full attention to diversified social participation, emphasizes the dual logics of “outward contribution” and “inward development,” enabling a deeper understanding of the real-life experiences of Chinese older adults situated at the intersection of traditional family responsibilities and modern self-fulfillment. Building on this integrated framework, and using data from the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS), this study addresses the following questions: How do contributory social participation and developmental social participation each affect depressive symptoms among older adults? Furthermore, do urban–rural residence and gender differences exacerbate or buffer these relationships, thereby shaping an “inequality pattern” in the health effects of social participation? By refining the typological logic of social participation and uncovering heterogeneous mechanisms within the Chinese context, this study aims to reveal the complex pathways through which social participation influences depressive symptoms among Chinese older adults. This paper provides a new analytical perspective for understanding disparities in mental health among the elderly in China and offers empirical evidence for promoting targeted and inclusive social participation policies for older adults.

Theoretical foundations and literature review

Divergences and integration in frameworks of social participation

Social participation is a multidimensional construct. Existing research, from various theoretical perspectives, has conceptualized social participation as encompassing formal and informal social activities [48, 49, 50], as a social role [16, 51], or as social interaction [52, 53, 54]. Some scholars have developed comprehensive definitions, characterizing social participation as an individual’s engagement in activities within community life and shared spaces that promote interaction, are compatible with one’s time and resources, and are shaped by social context and personal meaning [55].

This conceptual pluralism has led to significant inconsistency in the assessment of social participation effects and in variable operationalization. On the one hand, many studies employ broad concepts of social participation or binary indicators for particular types of activities, analyzing differences between participation and non-participation [3, 39]. This simplification implies a problematic “homogeneity assumption,” overlooking inherent differences in activity types and motivations. On the other hand, existing classification systems for social participation lack consensus and notably neglect family-oriented contributory activities. Current classifications range from specific activity types to abstract levels of participation. For example, [18] categorized social participation into paid work, political activities, and volunteering, Amagasa [1] distinguished community participation from individual participation [56], building on Levasseur’s framework, proposed a four-category typology; [3] differentiated between public and private sphere participation, and [30] used statistical models to identify patterns such as low, medium, high, economically active, and physically active participation. A close examination of these classifications reveals that most focus on formal roles in the public sphere or community-oriented activities, excluding contributory activities occurring within the household, centered on unpaid care or emotional support.

In China, the “family” is not only the core locus of emotional belonging but also a crucial vehicle through which older adults realize social value, maintain intergenerational ties, and derive a sense of meaning. The intergenerational reciprocity shaped by Confucian filial ethics means that older adults’ contributions within the family—such as caring for grandchildren, managing household chores, and providing economic and emotional support—constitute the primary, or even sole, form of social participation for many Chinese seniors. Classification frameworks that ignore family-oriented contributory activities not only severely narrow the overall picture of social participation among older adults but also lead to the statistical exclusion of large numbers of seniors who are actively engaged in family care, resulting in biased estimates and theoretical misinterpretations of the effects of social participation. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a more integrative and theoretically penetrating classification framework that can simultaneously accommodate pluralistic forms – public and private, formal and informal, contributory and developmental – and adequately reflect the complex realities of social participation among older adults in contemporary Chinese culture, thereby capturing its full richness and its pathways to mental health.

The theoretical dialogue between Active Aging and Productive Aging offers insights for constructing our analytical framework. The Active Aging framework proposed by the World Health Organization [42] posits that well-being in later life derives from extensive participation in social, economic, cultural, and spiritual domains, with core goals of maintaining health, ensuring security, and promoting participation. This theory incorporates all activities beneficial to overall well-being but risks analytical ambiguity by “counting everything as participation.” In contrast, Productive Aging theory [28] focuses more on activities that generate measurable value for others and society, such as paid labor, volunteering, and family care. This theory highlights older adults’ identity as “contributors” and emphasizes that society should value and create channels for investing older adults’ human capital. However, it may underestimate activities that do not directly produce external value but are nonetheless crucial for individual mental health, such as learning, leisure, and spiritual cultivation. The two theories are not opposed but complementary. Active Aging provides a foundation for “developmental” activities, while Productive Aging supplies the conceptual core for “contributory” activities.

Based on this, we attempt to integrate the two into a “Contributory–Developmental” framework of social participation, proposing that older adults’ social participation can be broadly divided into two categories: contributory participation and developmental participation. Contributory participation emphasizes that older adults provide value to the external world through their activities. Based on the target of contribution, it is further subdivided into family-contributory and society-contributory types: family-contributory participation includes activities such as grandchild care, helping adult children, and household chores, often occurring in the private sphere; although socially less visible, these activities significantly impact older adults’ role identity and family connectedness. Society-contributory participation includes volunteering, community affairs involvement, political activities, and paid work, taking place in the public sphere and more directly reflecting older adults’ social productivity and citizenship. Developmental participation emphasizes the value of activities for the individual’s own growth, enjoyment, and health maintenance. Based on activity content, it is further subdivided into learning-developmental and leisure-developmental types: learning-developmental participation encompasses activities such as attending senior universities, skills training, reading, and knowledge acquisition, focusing on cognitive stimulation and capability maintenance; leisure-developmental participation includes physical exercise, hobbies, social entertainment, and travel, focusing on psychological relaxation, emotional regulation, and social connection.

Additionally, drawing on [30], we incorporate personal, social, economic, and physical environmental factors as antecedent variables influencing the level and type of social participation, integrating them into our overall analytical model to systematically examine the formation mechanisms of social participation and its conditional effects on depression and mental health. Compared to existing research, the “Contributory–Developmental” framework fully attends to the plurality of older adults’ social participation and to family-oriented contributory activities, thereby remedying the theoretical lacuna of existing classification systems that overemphasize the public sphere and neglect the familial domain, and aligning more closely with the family-oriented nature of Chinese culture (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Research framework

Research framework

Same participation, different associations? Urban–rural and gender differences in social participation and depressive symptoms

Traditional research generally acknowledges the positive effects of social participation on the mental health of older adults, such as reducing depressive symptoms [7, 10, 16], alleviating loneliness [57, 47], and enhancing self-esteem, confidence, and life satisfaction [58, 59, 3]. However, most studies assume a “homogeneity hypothesis,” neglecting the differential effects of distinct forms of social participation on the mental health of different older adult subgroups. In fact, an individual’s structural position moderates the psychological consequences of social experiences by influencing both exposure to stressors and access to coping resources.

Urban–rural residence is one such structural stratifying factor; its differences essentially reflect the spatial projection of unequal resource distribution. Urban areas possess denser organizational networks, better public services, and higher levels of community capital [60, 61]. This not only increases older adults’ opportunities for participation but also enhances activity quality—for example, structured programs and normative feedback in urban senior centers can boost self-efficacy. In contrast, rural areas suffer from a scarcity of organizational resources, making social participation heavily reliant on family and informal neighborhood networks, with activities often taking the form of low-visibility, low-external-recognition family care and mutual assistance. Regarding developmental participation, urban areas offer richer public cultural services and organizational resources; therefore, urban older adults’ opportunities for leisure-developmental and learning-developmental participation are typically significantly higher than those of rural seniors. However, based on the logic of diminishing marginal utility, when a certain type of participation is common in the environment, the additional psychological gain from further participation is relatively limited. Conversely, in rural areas, where developmental participation is relatively scarce, the novel experiences, social contacts, and sense of self-development derived from such activities may yield stronger marginal psychological benefits in resource-deprived environments.

Regarding contributory participation, given the diverse participation options available, urban older adults are more likely to allocate time and energy to developmental participation, thus investing relatively less in contributory participation. However, once they do engage in contributory activities, urban seniors tend to prefer public-sphere society-contributory activities such as volunteering and political participation—because cities have numerous formal organizations (e.g., community service centers, volunteer associations) that can provide structured participation settings and external normative feedback, enhancing self-efficacy and social identity, thereby exerting significant protective effects against depression. In contrast, rural areas, with scarce organizational resources, offer extremely limited opportunities for public-sphere society-contributory participation. At the same time, opportunities for developmental participation are also relatively scarce. Constrained by these conditions, low-visibility family-contributory activities like family care and neighborly help become the primary, or even sole, form of social participation for rural older adults. Such activities are highly routine and obligatory, and are culturally viewed as expected responsibilities of older adults in rural society. Consequently, the overall level of contributory participation among rural older adults may actually be higher than that among urban seniors—not because rural seniors are more “active,” but because they lack alternative participation options, narrowing their social participation to the family-centered domain of responsibility. However, precisely because these family-contributory activities lack formal organizational support, external recognition, and structured feedback, they are more likely to be experienced as obligations rather than resources, with lower efficiency in translating into mental health benefits. Gender is another structural stratifying factor deeply embedded in the social fabric, moderating the impact of social participation on depression among older adults through mechanisms of role expectations and social cognition. Regarding developmental participation, the process of gender role socialization shapes distinct preferences. Social role theory suggests that women, socialized into role expectations of emotional expression and interpersonal connection, tend to seek psychological support and emotional regulation through social interaction [11]. This tendency persists into old age as a preference for leisure-developmental activities such as recreation, social gatherings, and entertainment. Such activities align well with women’s expressive communication style, directly providing emotional catharsis, social identity, and a sense of belonging, thereby providing relatively strong immediate relief of depression. In contrast, men are socialized into instrumental, competitive, and achievement-oriented behavioral patterns. After retirement, men face the loss of their work role and a “role vacuum,” requiring them to re-establish a sense of competence and social identity. Learning-developmental activities, such as skills training and educational courses, precisely meet this need—they provide clear goals, measurable progress, and potential social value (e.g., reemployment or community contribution), helping men rebuild role identity and self-efficacy. However, the psychological benefits of such activities may depend more on activity quality (e.g., challenge, external recognition) and may take longer to translate into protection against depression.

Regarding contributory participation, gender role socialization assigns women the identities of caregivers and maintainers of emotional bonds, while men are more often assigned instrumental roles and public-sphere responsibilities [11]. Based on this, older women are more likely to engage in family-contributory activities such as family care and intergenerational support. These activities are consistent with their long-internalized gender roles, bringing role identity and emotional satisfaction. However, because family-contributory activities typically feature low visibility, low external recognition, and lack of organizational support, they are more likely to be experienced as obligations rather than resources, and their efficiency in translating into mental health benefits may be constrained. In contrast, while older men’s overall level of contributory participation may be lower, when they do participate, they tend to prefer public-sphere society-contributory activities such as volunteering, community affairs, and political participation. These activities, relying on organized settings and external normative feedback, help enhance self-efficacy and social identity. Accordingly, we propose the following hypotheses:

Methods

Sample

This study uses data from the 2020 and 2018 waves of the China Longitudinal Aging Social Survey (CLASS). CLASS is a nationwide, continuous, large-scale social survey project conducted by the China Survey and Data Center at Renmin University of China. The target population consists of Chinese residents aged 60 years and above. CLASS adopts a stratified multistage probability sampling design. County-level units, including counties, county-level cities, and districts, were selected as primary sampling units, and village/neighborhood committees were selected as secondary sampling units. Within selected households, one respondent aged 60 or above was selected using the Kish method.

The primary analyses in this study are based on the 2020 wave, while the 2018 wave was used for supplementary sensitivity analyses. We focus on older adults who had either formally retired or exited paid work, in order to capture retirement and labor-market withdrawal in both urban and rural Chinese contexts. This broader definition is particularly important because many rural older adults do not experience formal retirement in the same institutional sense as urban employees.

In the 2020 wave, the original sample included 11,398 respondents. We first excluded 2,616 respondents who did not meet the retirement or labor-market-exit criterion, leaving 8,782 eligible older adults. Among these eligible respondents, 1,339 cases were excluded because of missing values on one or more key variables. The final analytical sample therefore included 7,443 respondents. Measures

Depressive symptoms

Depressive symptoms were measured using the nine-item CES-D scale available in CLASS, which is derived from the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale. Following the conventional scoring strategy, responses coded as 9 were treated as missing, and three positively worded items were reverse-coded so that all items were oriented in the same direction. The nine items were then summed to create a depressive symptom score ranging from 9 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. In the analytical sample, the scale showed moderate internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.676).

Contributory participation

Community volunteering

Community volunteering was measured on the basis of older adults' participation in community environmental protection activities during the past year. Participation in such activities reflects the core spirit of volunteerism—giving time and energy to a good cause without financial compensation—and is one of the most common forms of public service. Response categories were coded on a five-point scale, with 0 = never, 1 = several times a year, 2 = at least once a month, 3 = at least once a week, and 4 = almost every day.

Intergenerational domestic support

Intergenerational domestic support was measured by the average frequency with which older adults assisted their children with household chores in the past year, with higher scores indicating more intensive support (1 = never, 5 = almost every day).

Intergenerational childcare

Intergenerational childcare was assessed by the average amount of time older adults spent caring for the children of each adult child during the past year, with higher scores indicating more intensive childcare (1 = never, 5 = more than 8 h per day).

Reemployment after retirement

Reemployment after retirement was measured by whether respondents engaged in paid work or income-generating activities after retirement or labor-market exit. Because reemployment after retirement was substantively interpreted as reentry into paid work rather than routine participation intensity, this variable was coded as a binary indicator. Respondents who reported any paid work or income-generating activity were coded as 1, and those who reported no such activity were coded as 0.

Electoral participation

Electoral participation was assessed on the basis of self-reported participation in local council elections in the past three years, where 1 = don't know and did not participate, 2 = know but did not participate, and 3 = participated.

Developmental participation

Learning participation

Learning participation was measured by respondents’ participation in senior college or training programs during the past year. Because participation in organized learning activities was relatively uncommon and highly skewed, this variable was coded as a binary indicator. Respondents who reported any participation in senior college or training programs were coded as 1, and those who reported no participation were coded as 0. This coding captures whether older adults had access to or engagement with organized learning opportunities, rather than the intensity of routine participation.

Leisure activities

Leisure activities among older adults are diverse. To further distinguish the differential impacts of various types of recreational activities on psychological well-being, entertainment activities were categorized into two types based on the level of participation: active leisure activities and passive leisure activities [8].

Active leisure activities refer to those requiring physical, cognitive, technical, or social participation during participation, with the goals of entertainment, learning, or social interaction through active involvement. Active leisure was measured by the frequency of participation in three types of activities over the past year: instrumental activities (e.g., playing a musical instrument), board games or card games, and group dancing (e.g., public square dancing). Scores for each activity were coded from 0 = never to 4 = almost every day. Higher scores indicate more frequent participation, and the scores for the three activities were summed to produce a total score ranging from 0 to 12.

Passive leisure activities mainly refer to leisure through receiving external information or pre-prepared entertainment content. In this study, passive leisure activities were assessed in terms of the frequency of adults' use of traditional media, including watching television, listening to the radio, reading books or newspapers, and listening to traditional opera, with the same values assigned as above.

Internet use

Internet use was measured by asking how often respondents had used the Internet in the past year. Responses were coded on a five-point scale ranging from 1 = never to 5 = almost every day, with higher scores indicating more frequent Internet use. Because the CLASS item captures overall frequency of Internet use and does not distinguish specific purposes, such as learning, entertainment, information seeking, or social communication, this variable should be interpreted as a broad indicator of digital engagement. We classified it under developmental participation because Internet use in later life may expand access to information, services, learning resources, and social connections.

Control variables

Based on the theoretical framework described above, we incorporated control variables at the individual, household, and community levels. At the individual level, controls included age, gender, education, subjective economic status, self-rated health, and urban–rural residence. Gender was coded as a binary variable, with women coded as 1 and men coded as 0. Urban–rural residence was based on the interviewer-recorded residential area type: central urban areas and urban fringe areas were coded as urban, while urban–rural fringe areas, towns, and rural villages were coded as rural. This coding was used to distinguish respondents living in core urban residential settings from those living in more peripheral or rural residential settings, where community infrastructure, public services, and opportunities for organized participation may differ from those in central urban areas. For simplicity, this category is referred to as “rural” in the tables and figures, but it should be interpreted as a rural/non-urban category. At the family level, control variables included marital status, caregiving support from offspring, and financial support from children. At the community level, we included community atmosphere, which was measured using a composite index based on respondents' satisfaction with eight dimensions: road conditions, fitness/activity facilities, public safety, environmental health, atmosphere of respect for older adults, competence of community council staff, street lighting, and accessibility. Each item was assessed using a five-point Likert scale. The scale showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha = 0.862), indicating that it effectively reflects the overall quality of the community environment for older adults. Total scores ranged from 8 to 40, with higher scores indicating a better community atmosphere.

Statistical analyses

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression models were used to estimate the associations between different forms of social participation and depressive symptoms. Continuous participation variables were standardized in the regression models to facilitate comparison across activity measures, while binary and ordinal variables were retained in their original coding. All models included demographic, socioeconomic, family support, and community-level covariates.

To examine subgroup heterogeneity, we estimated moderation models by including interaction terms between each participation variable and urban–rural residence or gender. Predicted depressive symptom scores were plotted for statistically significant interaction terms to facilitate interpretation. Several sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, all nine forms of social participation were entered simultaneously to assess whether the baseline associations were sensitive to mutual adjustment for other participation types. Second, inverse probability weighting was used to assess whether the results were sensitive to observable sample selectivity due to listwise deletion. Third, because the analytical file did not contain an official final sampling weight variable, we could not conduct survey-weighted estimation; instead, we conducted a design-related sensitivity analysis with province fixed effects and standard errors clustered at the village/neighborhood committee level. Finally, we estimated lagged dependent variable models by additionally controlling for depressive symptoms measured in 2018, in order to assess whether the associations remained after accounting for prior depressive symptom levels. All analyses were conducted using Stata 17.0. The sensitivity analyses and supplementary tables are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

By contrast, intergenerational childcare (β = −0.250, p < 0.01) and reemployment after retirement (β = −0.544, p < 0.01) were significantly associated with lower depressive symptoms. Community volunteering and electoral participation were not statistically significant in the baseline models. Taken together, these findings suggest that the association between social participation and depressive symptoms depends strongly on the type of activity, rather than following a uniformly positive or negative pattern.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the sensitivity of the baseline findings, we conducted four additional analyses. First, we compared the separate OLS models with a full model including all nine activity types simultaneously. The associations of learning participation, internet use, intergenerational domestic support, intergenerational childcare, and reemployment after retirement remained statistically significant, whereas the association for active leisure was attenuated and became insignificant, suggesting some sensitivity to model specification. Second, we used inverse probability weighting to address potential observable sample selectivity due to listwise deletion. The IPW-weighted results were substantively consistent with the baseline models. Third, to account for regional heterogeneity and within-community clustering, we re-estimated the baseline models with province fixed effects and standard errors clustered at the village/neighborhood committee level. The main patterns remained largely unchanged. These three sets of sensitivity analyses are reported in Supplementary Table A5. Fourth, we estimated lagged dependent variable models by additionally controlling for depressive symptoms in 2018. In these models, active leisure, internet use, intergenerational domestic support, and intergenerational childcare remained statistically significant, whereas learning participation and reemployment after retirement were not statistically significant. These results are reported in Supplementary Table A6. Overall, these sensitivity analyses support the main pattern of findings, while also indicating that some associations, particularly those for active leisure, learning participation, and reemployment after retirement, should be interpreted cautiously.

First, for some activities, the direction of the association was similar across rural/non-urban and urban older adults, but the magnitude differed. These differences are not limited to stronger or weaker associations; for some activities, the direction of the association differs between rural/non-urban and urban settings. This finding underscores the need to consider the social meaning and contextual conditions of participation when interpreting its relationship with depressive symptoms among older adults.

To further contextualize the moderation effects, we examined how gender and urban–rural residence were associated with differences in levels of contributory and developmental participation (Supplementary Table A7). The results reveal distinct participation profiles across demographic groups. Urban older adults showed higher levels of learning participation, passive leisure, active leisure, internet use, community volunteering, and intergenerational childcare. In contrast, rural/non-urban older adults showed higher levels of intergenerational domestic support, electoral participation, and, to a lesser extent, reemployment after retirement. Gendered patterns were also observed: women reported higher levels of active leisure and intergenerational domestic support, whereas men reported higher levels of reemployment after retirement and electoral participation. These divergent participation profiles provide an empirical basis for understanding the uneven distribution of favorable associations observed in the moderation analysis.

Discussion

Our research found that different forms of developmental and contributory social participation were associated with depressive symptoms among older adults in distinct ways. These associations were not uniformly protective; rather, they varied by activity type and were shaped by urban–rural residence and, to a lesser extent, gender. This finding highlights the importance of distinguishing among specific forms of participation, rather than assuming that social participation has a universally beneficial relationship with mental health in later life.

Association between social participation types and depressive symptoms

This study categorized older adults’ social participation into two broad types: developmental participation and contributory participation. Among developmental forms of participation, learning participation, active leisure, and internet use were significantly associated with lower depressive symptoms among older adults, whereas passive leisure was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms. These findings are broadly consistent with previous studies showing that learning participation, active engagement, and internet use may be linked to better mental health in later life [9, 34, 45]. They also suggest that not all leisure-oriented or self-developmental activities are equally relevant to mental health. Activities that involve learning, active engagement, or digital connection may be linked to lower depressive symptoms by providing cognitive stimulation, fostering a sense of self-efficacy, and promoting social interaction and connectedness [6, 25, 34, 45, 46]. By contrast, passive leisure may have more limited psychological benefits because it often involves less cognitive, physical, or interpersonal engagement.

Compared with developmental participation, contributory participation showed a more complex pattern in its association with depressive symptoms. Contributory activities may be linked to mental health through social value, role fulfillment, and reciprocity, but their implications depend on the specific form and context of participation. In the baseline models, reemployment after retirement was associated with lower depressive symptoms, supporting continuity theory and the productive aging perspective, which emphasize that maintaining meaningful social roles and identities may contribute to psychological well-being [2, 22]. However, this association was no longer statistically significant in the LDV sensitivity analysis after controlling for depressive symptoms in 2018, suggesting that the result should be interpreted cautiously. This also contrasts with findings by [43]. One possible explanation is a bidirectional relationship between reemployment and depressive symptoms: older adults with fewer depressive symptoms may be more likely to re-enter the workforce, while reemployment may also provide role continuity and social engagement for some retirees.

Our study also finds that intergenerational childcare was associated with lower depressive symptoms, which differs from [21] findings and may be due to cross-cultural differences [20], also noted that the poorer health of grandparent caregivers was more likely to be due to pre-existing disadvantages than to caregiving per se. Moderate and voluntary grandparental caregiving may result in emotional rewards, a sense of purpose, and the fulfillment of generative needs [13], leading to lower depressive symptoms.

However, not all contributory participation is associated with favorable outcomes. Our findings show that intergenerational domestic support, such as daily chores like cooking and laundry, is significantly associated with higher depressive symptoms among older adults. This underscores the importance of the nature and intensity of contributory behavior [5]. Unlike grandchild caregiving, which often provides emotional returns, social recognition, and psychological gains [37], household labor is repetitive, lacks intrinsic motivation and emotional feedback, and can result in resource depletion [19] and autonomy frustration [33], particularly when perceived as obligatory. These effects may be linked to poorer psychological well-being and stand in contrast to the social recognition and psychological gains often associated with caregiving.

Moreover, our study finds no significant association between depressive symptoms and participation in community volunteering, electoral activities, or passive leisure. Passive leisure activities, due to their lack of cognitive, physical, or social interaction, may offer limited mental health benefits, aligning with prior findings [8]. Although some studies have found positive effects of volunteering [14, 29], our results reveal no significant overall association. Likewise, electoral participation did not show a significant overall association with depressive symptoms in the baseline models, although previous research suggests that the psychological implications of political participation may depend on contextual factors such as regional political competition and local governance environments [24]. The moderation analyses further suggest that the insignificant overall associations of community volunteering and electoral participation may stem from substantial heterogeneity across urban and rural contexts. Specifically, opposite subgroup-specific associations may cancel each other out in the pooled baseline models, underscoring the importance of examining subgroup heterogeneity.

Heterogeneity by urban–rural residence

Our results reveal that several forms of social participation show differential associations with depressive symptoms across urban and rural contexts. These differences are observed not only for contributory participation, such as intergenerational domestic support, community volunteering, intergenerational childcare, and electoral participation, but also for developmental participation, including learning participation, active leisure, and internet use. Notably, intergenerational domestic support is positively associated with depressive symptoms for both urban and rural older adults, but the adverse effect is significantly stronger in rural areas. This may be attributed to stronger traditional family obligations and social norms in rural regions, where older adults face greater informal pressure to undertake household duties. Moreover, rural older adults may perceive such support more as reciprocal intergenerational exchange than as altruistic emotional support; the lack of reciprocation could thus negatively impact mental well-being [5, 44].

In addition, community volunteering shows an opposite pattern across urban and rural areas: it is associated with lower depressive symptom levels among urban older adults, but higher depressive symptoms among rural older adults. This suggests a fundamental difference in the nature and experience of volunteering across settings. Urban volunteering is often interest-driven, voluntary, and better organized with formal recognition mechanisms, contributing to a sense of achievement and social inclusion [29]. In contrast, rural volunteering may rely more on informal mobilization based on interpersonal ties or village obligations [41], driven by communal responsibility and social expectations [31]. These activities, though fostering social exchange, may lack institutional support or emotional rewards, leaving rural older adults feeling burdened and unfulfilled, particularly when their efforts are not reciprocated.

Electoral participation presents another striking urban–rural contrast. It is negatively associated with depressive symptoms in rural areas but linked to higher depressive symptoms in urban settings. This may reflect differences in the structure and experience of elections. Rural elections often involve grassroots-level engagement and familiar interpersonal contexts, fostering a sense of agency and belonging [26, 27, 36], and providing channels for expressing local demands [4]. Urban elections, by contrast, tend to involve more complex processes and higher-level institutions, with older adults’ participation often limited to voting, lacking deeper engagement or tangible outcomes [17]. Moreover, the complex urban socio-political landscape may elicit feelings of helplessness or anxiety, adversely affecting mental health [38].

Urban–rural differences are also observed for several developmental activities. Learning participation is more strongly associated with lower depressive symptoms among urban older adults, which may reflect the greater availability, accessibility, and institutional support of later-life learning opportunities in urban settings. By contrast, active leisure and internet use show stronger negative associations with depressive symptoms among rural older adults. One possible explanation is that these activities may represent more substantial forms of resource expansion in rural contexts, where opportunities for organized leisure, digital connection, and diversified social interaction are relatively more limited. Thus, participation in these activities may carry greater marginal psychological significance for rural older adults.

Meanwhile, passive leisure and reemployment after retirement do not show significant urban–rural differences in their association with depressive symptoms. This suggests that urban–rural heterogeneity is not universal across all forms of social participation, but is concentrated in activities whose meaning, accessibility, or social organization is more strongly shaped by local residential context.

When these findings are contextualized within actual participation patterns, our results indicate that urban older adults are more likely to engage in learning, leisure, internet use, volunteering, and grandchild caregiving, consistent with more abundant resource availability in urban settings. Rural older adults, in contrast, participate more frequently in household labor, reemployment after retirement, and political activities, which reflect the social structure, economic needs, and local governance of rural communities. These differences not only stem from contextual constraints but may also shape the mental health associations of various forms of participation. Special attention should be given to rural older adults, who may have fewer opportunities for mentally beneficial developmental activities while bearing a heavier burden of negatively associated contributory activities.

Heterogeneity by gender

Gender-based heterogeneity analysis indicates that most forms of social participation do not show significant gender differences in their associations with depressive symptoms. However, a few activities do exhibit gender-moderated outcomes. Specifically, learning participation is associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms among women, while no significant effect is observed for men. This pattern differs from our initial expectation that learning-developmental participation might be more strongly associated with men’s mental health through role reconstruction after retirement. Gender role theory (Eagly, 2013) posits that women’s later-life experiences are often more strongly shaped by family and caregiving roles. For older women, learning participation may therefore provide especially valuable opportunities for self-development, emotional support, and social interaction beyond routine family roles, which may explain why women benefit more from learning participation in terms of mental health [35].

Importantly, reemployment after retirement also shows a gendered pattern in its association with depressive symptoms. Among men, reemployment is associated with lower depressive symptoms, whereas among women this negative association is much weaker and not clearly observed. This supports findings by [40], that older women face more emotional challenges post-retirement. Gender differences in role expectations and motivations for re-employment may explain this pattern. Men are more concerned with social status and may re-enter the workforce to maintain their identity [12, 23]. Women, on the other hand, may be driven more by economic pressures or caregiving needs, which can add stress and contribute to psychological distress [40].

Integrating our findings on gendered participation patterns, we identify a potential mismatch between older adults’ participation patterns and the activities from which they appear to benefit most. We find that older men appear to gravitate towards types of participation that are more strongly associated with lower depressive symptoms among men (reemployment). In contrast, older women may face a more constrained opportunity structure: they not only shoulder a greater burden of activities associated with higher depressive symptoms (intergenerational domestic support), but they also exhibit lower levels of participation in the very activities most beneficial to them (learning participation). This finding suggests how gender norms, mediated through social participation, may continue to shape gender inequality in mental health in later life.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this study is the first to classify older adults’ social participation into developmental and contributory categories. This innovative framework aligns with the concepts of active aging and productive aging, providing a more comprehensive representation of older adults’ social participation. By incorporating two broad domains encompassing nine types of activities, the study captures the major forms of social participation among older adults in the digital era. The findings suggest that both the types of social participation and individual characteristics jointly shape their associations with psychological well-being.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the measurement of social participation was constrained by the available CLASS items. Although this study included nine forms of participation, these measures may not fully capture the diversity, intensity, quality, and subjective meanings of older adults’ participation. In particular, internet use was measured only by overall frequency, without distinguishing specific purposes such as learning, entertainment, information seeking, or social communication. As a result, internet use in this study should be interpreted as a broad indicator of digital engagement rather than a specific type of social participation. Future research should use more detailed measures to distinguish the purposes, quality, and social meanings of different forms of participation, especially digital participation.

Second, the measurement of depressive symptoms was also limited by the available scale in CLASS. Although the nine-item CES-D scale is derived from a widely used depressive symptom measure and is commonly scored as a summed index, its internal consistency in the analytical sample was moderate (Cronbach’s α = 0.676). Therefore, the outcome should be interpreted as a general depressive symptom index rather than a clinical diagnosis of depression. Future research using longer or clinically validated mental health instruments could further validate these findings.

Third, sample selection remains a potential concern. This study focused on retired older adults and used complete-case analysis for the main models. Comparisons between included and excluded respondents indicated differences in several observable characteristics, suggesting that the final analytical sample may not fully represent all eligible respondents in the 2020 wave. Although we used inverse probability weighting as a sensitivity analysis to assess whether the results were affected by observable sample selectivity due to listwise deletion, this approach cannot eliminate selection bias related to unobserved factors, such as cognitive status, functional capacity, or unmeasured social resources. The findings should therefore be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Fourth, despite a series of sensitivity analyses, including full-model estimation, inverse probability weighting, province fixed effects with clustered standard errors, and lagged dependent variable models, the observational design does not allow strong causal inference. Omitted variable bias may remain because unmeasured factors, such as personality, cognitive functioning, functional limitations, social networks, or local service availability, may influence both social participation and depressive symptoms. Reverse causality may also remain, as older adults with fewer depressive symptoms may be more likely to participate in certain activities. Some associations, particularly those for learning participation, active leisure, and reemployment after retirement, were also sensitive to model specification and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Future research using longitudinal data and more detailed measures should further examine the mechanisms or pathways through which different forms of social participation are linked to mental health in later life.

Finally, the data are drawn from China, where family norms, intergenerational obligations, rural–urban structures, and later-life learning opportunities have distinctive institutional and cultural features. Future cross-national studies are needed to examine whether the developmental-contributory framework applies to other aging societies.

Conclusion

This study developed a contributory–developmental framework to distinguish different forms of social participation among older adults and showed that their associations with depressive symptoms vary by activity type and social context. The findings suggest that social participation should not be treated as a uniformly beneficial construct. Developmental activities such as learning, active leisure, and internet use, family-oriented contributions such as intergenerational childcare and domestic support, and public or productive activities such as volunteering, electoral participation, and reemployment after retirement may carry different psychological meanings.

These findings have several practical implications. In rural areas, expanding accessible developmental opportunities, including learning, leisure, and digital engagement, may be especially important, while support should also be provided to reduce burdensome family-based contributions. For older women, meaningful learning opportunities and participation spaces beyond routine family roles may be particularly valuable. More broadly, active ageing policies should move beyond simply encouraging more participation and instead support participation that is voluntary, meaningful, and matched to older adults’ resources, needs, and social contexts.

Future research should further examine the mechanisms linking different forms of social participation to mental health, using longitudinal data and more detailed measures of participation quality, internet-use purposes, and subjective meanings.
