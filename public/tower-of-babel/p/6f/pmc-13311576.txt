‘The evidence is out there…’: perspectives of healthcare providers on improving asthma and COPD prevention and care within a major health authority region of British Columbia, Canada
Authors: Tina Afshar, Aneisha Collins-Fairclough, Pat Camp, Karen Rideout, Erin Shellington, Nardia Strydom, J Mark FitzGerald, Sian Hoe Cheong, Mary-Frances Ceccato, John Fleetham, Carmen Rempel, Phalgun Joshi, Christopher Carlsten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAsthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) impose substantial morbidity and health system burden in Canada. Within Vancouver Coastal Health (VCH), a large and geographically diverse health authority in British Columbia, variation in service availability, access and care coordination creates barriers to prevention and high-quality chronic respiratory disease management. This project sought to document healthcare professionals’ (HCPs’) experiences and recommendations to inform a regional strategy to improve asthma and COPD care.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative quality improvement project using occupation-specific virtual focus groups (March to September 2021) with HCPs involved in asthma and COPD prevention and care across VCH. Purposive sampling targeted diversity in profession, practice setting and geography (urban, suburban, rural and remote). Results were analysed thematically in NVivo using iterative coding and team-based refinement.Results54 HCPs participated across 10 focus groups, including respiratory physicians (n=9), respiratory therapists (n=19), family physicians (n=11), nurse practitioners (n=2), nurses (n=7), pharmacists (n=6) and one registered dietitian (n=1). Themes aligned with a socio-ecological framework and spanned (1) patient-level barriers (financial insecurity, language barriers, comorbid mental health and substance use, medication beliefs and adherence, smoking and vaping), (2) health system barriers (limited access to spirometry and pulmonary rehabilitation, fragmented transitions and unlinked electronic records, workforce and resource constraints) and (3) environmental barriers (medication coverage and formulary restrictions, poor air quality as an exacerbation trigger). HCPs recommended expanding community-based services (including mobile diagnostics and rehabilitation), strengthening prevention and cessation supports, improving medication access and inhaler education, and building integrated pathways and information systems to support continuity across settings.ConclusionsFrontline HCP perspectives identify actionable, system-level opportunities to improve asthma and COPD prevention and care within a complex health authority. This work provides implementation-oriented insights to inform regional strategy and offers a replicable approach for engaging HCPs to strengthen chronic respiratory care.Keywords: Asthma, Chronic disease management, Attitude of Health Personnel, Focus Groups, Healthcare quality improvement

Introduction

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are common chronic respiratory conditions that contribute substantially to morbidity, reduced quality of life and healthcare utilisation. In Canada, asthma affects more than 4.6 million people and is projected to cost the Canadian economy approximately $4.2 billion by 2033.1 In British Columbia, asthma prevalence has increased over the past two decades, reaching approximately 12% in 2021/2022.2 The economic burden is also substantial at the provincial level; using linked administrative data, asthma-related direct healthcare costs in British Columbia were estimated at approximately $41.9 million annually (2006 CAD), rising to $56.1 million annually under broader asthma-related cost definitions.3

COPD prevalence estimates depend strongly on case definition. While diagnosed COPD based on self-report or physician claims is often reported at lower levels, spirometry-based population estimates suggest a substantially higher burden. In the Canadian Obstructive Lung Disease (COLD) study, the overall population prevalence of COPD defined by lower limit of normal criteria was 11.2% (95% CI 9.7 to 12.6), illustrating the extent to which diagnosed prevalence can underestimate disease burden.4 These considerations are directly relevant for regional planning in Vancouver Coastal Health (VCH), which serves over one million people, approximately 25% of British Columbia’s population.5 VCH includes dense urban centres as well as suburban, rural and remote coastal communities, which introduces distinctive challenges for equitable access and care delivery across a single health region.5

Common challenges affect the prevention and management of chronic respiratory diseases globally.6 In the VCH region, asthma and COPD burden is shaped by a growing and ageing population, but also by exposures and structural conditions that directly influence airway symptoms and exacerbations, including ambient air pollution and wildfire smoke, indoor air quality and housing-related factors such as ventilation quality and dampness/mould.7 8 Inequitable access to effective indoor filtration and clean-air spaces during smoke events may further widen disparities in respiratory risk and symptom control.9

At the health-system level, gaps in service delivery, care coordination and access to evidence-based respiratory services can contribute to avoidable emergency department visits, hospitalisations and poorer outcomes. 7 10 Within VCH specifically, geographic differences in availability and access to asthma and COPD care services have been documented, with additional challenges for rural and remote communities.11 10 Together, these environmental drivers and health-system gaps create practical barriers for clinicians attempting to deliver timely, patient-centred and prevention-oriented care. Systematically characterising these drivers and care gaps is an essential first step toward identifying and implementing robust solutions.12

The professional experiences and perspectives of local healthcare professionals (HCPs) regarding access to respiratory services, care pathways and referral processes, coordination across settings (primary care, specialty care, emergency care) and barriers to delivering guideline-based asthma and COPD care are critical for identifying actionable, context-relevant solutions. VCH leadership requested and supported this project to document HCP experiences and priorities to inform development of a regional strategy to improve asthma and COPD care. This work represents the foundational diagnostic phase of a broader regional quality improvement strategy, aimed at identifying where care processes break down, what resources are missing and which changes may be most feasible within current clinical workflows. It therefore provides an applied foundation for subsequent refinement of policies, care pathways and implementation priorities within VCH. This work captures frontline insights on where care processes break down, what resources are missing and which changes may be most feasible within current clinical workflows. Methods

Project objectives

The project aimed to achieve the following objectives:

Identify health-system and care delivery challenges that HCPs face in asthma and COPD prevention and management (for example, access to services, care pathways, coordination across settings and resource constraints).

Project design

This qualitative quality improvement project used focus groups (FG) to explore the perspectives of HCPs involved in asthma and COPD care. Occupation-specific groups enabled in-depth discussion of barriers, challenges and opportunities across the care continuum. A semistructured interview guide was used to prompt detailed responses, and data were analysed to identify themes across groups. Focus groups included participants from diverse roles, disciplines and settings within the VCH region to capture a broad range of viewpoints.

Patient involvement

Patient partners with lived experience of asthma/COPD were engaged through the Legacy for Airway Health Community Partner Committee (CPC) during the study design phase. CPC members provided input on the development and refinement of the focus group questions via virtual meetings and email-based review. In addition, a patient partner reviewed the draft manuscript and provided feedback to improve clarity, relevance and interpretation for audiences affected by asthma and COPD. Patient partners were not involved in recruiting healthcare professional participants, facilitating focus groups or conducting qualitative coding/analysis. Findings will be shared with VCH partners and CPC members as part of an integrated knowledge translation approach.

Recruitment

Purposive sampling was used to recruit HCPs actively involved in asthma and COPD care in the VCH region. Recruitment occurred through professional networks, key stakeholders and targeted invitations. Efforts were made to ensure diversity in profession, geography and practice setting.

Data collection

Focus groups were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, between March and September 2021, via virtual platforms. Each group included 4 to 9 participants from the same occupation who work in diverse geographic areas of the VCH region in the same discussion. Discussions were audio-recorded with participant consent.

Each focus group discussion lasted 60 to 90 min and was facilitated by an experienced moderator using a semistructured interview guide that included questions about barriers to delivering asthma and COPD care, priorities for improvement, recommendations to strengthen services across the care continuum (including access to diagnostics and treatment, care integration and referral pathways, communication and information sharing across electronic medical record (EMR) platforms, medication coverage and prescribing constraints, and equity-related needs of underserved groups), opportunities where additional evidence could inform practice and the impact of COVID-19 on access to, coordination and delivery of asthma and COPD services. The discussions were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcriptionist and uploaded into NVivo software for analysis.

Analysis

Each transcript was thematically analysed.13 One researcher (ACF) conducted in vivo coding and organised codes into preliminary categories. These were refined through iterative discussions with the research team to identify themes. Thematic saturation was assessed iteratively across focus groups and was judged to have been reached when later transcripts yielded no substantively new themes or categories. Focus group participants reviewed and provided feedback on the categories and themes via email or virtual discussion, which was incorporated into revisions. Recurring ideas across groups were consolidated under standardised theme names to ensure clarity, reduce redundancy and maintain consistency. In reporting results, themes were grouped into patient-level, health-system-level and environmental/policy-level domains using the socio-ecological model as a descriptive organising framework.14

Results

Participants

A total of 54 HCPs participated in 10 focus groups, including respiratory physicians (RPs, n=9), respiratory therapists (RTs, n=19), family physicians (FPs, n=11), nurse practitioners (NPs, n=2), registered nurses (RNs, n=7), pharmacists (n=6) and one registered dietitian (RD). The RN focus group had one RD due to small numbers for that group. Their roles spanned primary care, specialty services and community programmes, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of gaps and opportunities in asthma and COPD care across the VCH region.

Challenges identified by HCPs

Themes were organised using a socio-ecological lens to describe how patient-level, health-system and environmental factors jointly shape the quality of asthma and COPD care. This structure highlights the multilevel nature of barriers and identifies intervention opportunities across the care continuum.

Patients’ personal-level factors

HCPs identified patients’ personal situations that interfere with them seeking care and following through on care recommendations to consequently affect stable management of asthma and COPD. Personal-level factors include sociodemographic barriers such as financial insecurity, limited English language proficiency, comorbidities (primarily substance use and mental health disorders), medication use, and smoking and vaping (table 1).

Regional health-system-level factors

HCPs identified procedural, resource and organisational constraints that impact the delivery of care services in the VCH region. These factors limited access to medication and services across the asthma and COPD care pathway. Inadequate care coordination and continuity arose from gaps in the flow of patient information between the multidisciplinary providers involved in asthma and COPD care from hospital to community. This gap in information flow is a result of limited communication structures and unlinked EMRs being used by the different HCP groups and sometimes led to inconsistent messaging to patients about their condition and care. Limiting organisation factors such as constraints in resources to support care provision, unclear models for organising services such as spirometry or smoking cessation regionally, were noted to create confusion for patients navigating asthma and COPD services (table 2).

Environment-level factors

HCPs noted that medication coverage policies and formulary restrictions limited consistent optimisation of pharmacotherapy. In British Columbia, this included BC PharmaCare (the provincial public drug coverage programme) and the hospital formulary (the hospital’s approved medication list), which were described as barriers to timely access to recommended inhaled therapies for some patients. HCPs also emphasised that environmental conditions, particularly poor air quality, can exacerbate symptoms and contribute to worsened lung function (table 3).

HCP recommendations

HCPs identified several changes they believed would enhance the delivery of asthma and COPD care across the VCH region.

Accessible, preventive, and patient-centred services

RTs, RPs, NPs, and RNs recommended expanding community-based asthma and COPD services to improve access and better meet patient needs. Priorities included increasing access to mobile spirometry, expanding pulmonary rehabilitation in urban and rural settings, strengthening smoking and vaping prevention and cessation supports, and improving access to inhaled medications by reducing BC PharmaCare restrictions and special authority barriers. HCPs also emphasised the importance of practical supports for self-management, including spacer use, inhaler teaching, action plans and medication counselling. These recommendations were framed within a broader call for more patient-centred care, particularly for people facing mental health challenges, financial barriers or complex social circumstances.

I would love to see more pulmonary rehab programs and less special authority coverage and just more PharmaCare coverage for some of the COPD and asthma medications. – NP FG 07/20

I mean, a lot of our patients have anxiety and/or depression, and unless you came from that background, working with mental health patients all the time, we’re kind of just winging it in helping people. There’s a lot on COPD and education out there, but not so much on COPD and mental health, (…), anxiety and, you know, coping mechanisms and COPD. – RN FG 05/26

Integrated care pathways and coordination

HCPs recommended stronger interprofessional collaboration and more coordinated care pathways across hospital, community, primary care and specialty settings. Key priorities included improving hospital-to-community transitions, supporting clearer referral and follow-up pathways, integrating specialist expertise into primary care, and improving access to shared patient information such as spirometry results, medication records and discharge documentation. Providers also highlighted the value of better linkage with community and regional programmes, including smoking cessation and respiratory education services, so that patients could access more consistent support across settings. Clearer screening tools and discharge processes were also recommended to help identify patients at risk of falling through gaps in care.

Talking about how to quit smoking. At the moment, it’s all very random. It is difficult to give up smoking, and we know that smoking just perpetuates the whole thing (COPD), you know, cause increased decline, cause increased exacerbations, and they increase symptom load. And yet it doesn’t seem to be a kind of concerted effort in one place under one stop to tell them how to stop smoking, in terms of implementation. It’s kind of random. You do go to that clinic; you go to your GP and so on. I feel like a traffic warden, you know? Go there, go here. But we are not giving them something. – RP FG 06/08

For me, it would be (useful to have) better screening tools upon discharge in the hospital to capture these clients that are falling through the cracks, identifying who they are and connecting them with the right resources so that when they’re home, they have the tools to rehabilitate or maintain their condition so that things don’t go south and they don’t continue going back to hospital. – RN FG 06/08

Resources and infrastructure to support service delivery

Providers also emphasised the need for stronger system capacity to support high-quality asthma and COPD care. Recommendations included ongoing provider education, accessible clinical decision tools and greater investment in multidisciplinary models of care. In particular, RPs highlighted the need for better-resourced clinics with access to disciplines such as social work and psychology, especially for patients with more complex needs. Some participants also advocated for data-informed healthcare networks to support implementation of best practices and continuous improvement across the region.

For me, it’s better multidisciplinary clinics. We talk about this a lot with severe asthma, but I think it extends all the way down to regular, old asthma. It’s not just the lone physician; there (also needs to be) a social worker and a psychologist. It’s just so multidimensional that, like, I work in CF (cystic fibrosis), so I know it’s possible to have these clinics and to have them resourced properly. Not every clinic needs to have all of these things, but sort of, you know, at least some of the clinics being really well resourced to deal with, like, difficult asthma. – RP FG 06/08

Discussion

Converging insights from patients and healthcare providers

The findings of this project align closely with previously published work capturing patient perspectives on asthma and COPD care in the VCH region.15 While this current work centres the experiences and recommendations of HCPs, many of the identified challenges echo the key themes raised by patients in an earlier, unpublished journey mapping project (table 4). Taken together, the two studies offer a multidimensional understanding of the systemic and patient-level barriers that impede optimal care for chronic respiratory disease in this context.

Asthma and COPD care in the VCH region is supported by a multidisciplinary healthcare workforce, a culture of innovation and ongoing quality improvement initiatives, which together provide a strong foundation for strengthening chronic respiratory care.10 16 However, this project also revealed system-level challenges that limit effective service delivery. These include delayed access to diagnostics and medications, fragmented coordination across care settings and inadequate support for patients with complex medical and social needs.16 17 When combined with individual-level factors, such as comorbidities, financial barriers and geographic isolation, these issues contribute to persistent inequities in care and outcomes.18 Although some initiatives have been introduced to address these concerns, current efforts remain insufficient to meet the growing need for timely, patient-centred asthma and COPD care, particularly in underserved communities.19

The recommendations put forth by HCPs reflected foundational principles of high-performing health systems: services tailored to population needs, a strong emphasis on prevention, integrated care delivery across settings and disciplines, and sufficient resourcing.20 21 These principles align with international best practices and with evidence-supported chronic disease care models associated with improved outcomes in other jurisdictions.22 23 For example, tailored services, such as culturally appropriate care models or services adapted for people with comorbid mental health and substance use disorders, have shown improved engagement and outcomes in jurisdictions such as the UK.24 Similarly, prevention-oriented models, including risk factor screening and early intervention programmes, have successfully identified undiagnosed respiratory disease and exacerbation risk in the USA.25 26 Integrated care pathways, particularly those linking primary care with specialists and community-based services, have improved continuity of care, decreased hospitalisations and reduced duplications in the USA.27 Lastly, investments in staffing, infrastructure and decision support tools have been essential to the success of chronic disease strategies in global health systems.22 28 29 More broadly, these findings suggest that improving asthma and COPD outcomes requires not only high-quality clinical care at the point of encounter, but also regional system design that supports equitable access to diagnostics, coordinated transitions, shared information systems and multidisciplinary community-based care.

The patient-level challenges identified in this project underscore the need to design services that mitigate, rather than exacerbate, existing barriers. In addition to strengthening patient-centred care, adopting a proportionate universalism approach may help reduce inequities by tailoring service intensity to varying levels of need.30 For example, comorbid mental health conditions and substance use were identified as contributors to poorer outcomes, emphasising the importance of adapting asthma and COPD services to better meet the needs of historically underserved subgroups.18 31 Finally, although many upstream environmental drivers (including air quality) extend beyond direct health-system control, HCPs’ observations highlight opportunities for community-level adaptation and population-protective measures (for example, clean-air messaging, access to filtration and clean-air spaces during smoke events, and targeted supports for higher-risk patients) that could help prevent exacerbations and reduce disease burden.17 31 The broader implication is that health systems aiming to reduce respiratory inequities must address structural features of care delivery, not only individual patient behaviours or isolated service gaps.

This project provides the first extensive multidisciplinary exploration of HCP perspectives on asthma and COPD care within the VCH region. The occupation-specific format of our sessions encouraged interactive discussion, with participants often extending or challenging one another’s contributions, which we believe yielded a nuanced depiction of system gaps and opportunities. By identifying actionable, provider-informed recommendations within a real-world regional context, this work offers a potentially replicable approach for other jurisdictions seeking to strengthen chronic respiratory disease management through locally grounded, equity-focused strategies.23

Implications for practice

This project provides actionable guidance to improve asthma and COPD care by centering the experiences of frontline HCPs. For practice, the findings support three immediate priorities: expanding access to diagnostics and pulmonary rehabilitation, improving coordination across settings through shared information and clearer pathways, and strengthening self-management and psychosocial supports for patients with more complex needs. These findings directly informed the development of a new regional COPD strategy at VCH, with early initiatives already underway to improve access to spirometry and smoking cessation services in rural communities.19 As such, this work should be understood not as a stand-alone assessment, but as the foundational diagnostic phase of a broader regional quality improvement process. By adding the provider perspective to existing patient insights, the project offers a more complete understanding of barriers and opportunities, highlighting the need for equitable funding, professional support, culturally safe care and system-level reforms. Together, these insights create a strong mandate for more coordinated, patient-centred respiratory care.

Strengths and limitations

As a qualitative quality improvement study using focus groups, findings may have been influenced by social desirability bias, group dynamics or more vocal participants. However, discussions were interactive, with participants building on or challenging each other’s ideas, suggesting meaningful exchange rather than passive agreement. While efforts were made to include diverse roles and locations, some provider groups and rural perspectives may be under-represented. The findings reflect only healthcare professionals’ views; patients, caregivers and community voices were not included and would provide valuable complementary insight. Because the project focused on the Vancouver Coastal Health region, transferability to other settings may be limited.

This work was conducted as a leadership-supported quality improvement initiative with an explicit mandate to inform a regional strategy. This context is a strength, as it grounded discussions in operational realities and increased the likelihood that findings could translate into actionable changes. At the same time, the improvement-oriented framing may have influenced the content or tone of participant responses, including the emphasis placed on modifiable system levers and implementation feasibility. In a purely academic research context without an explicit pathway to change, participants may have prioritised different themes or communicated differently.

Conclusion

This project highlights substantial barriers faced by HCPs in delivering effective asthma and COPD care within the VCH region. Drawing on multidisciplinary perspectives, it shows how patient-level challenges, including medication adherence, sociodemographic barriers and limited health literacy, interact with system-level gaps such as fragmented care coordination, workforce constraints and limited access to diagnostics to contribute to suboptimal outcomes. Importantly, this interplay underscores the need to apply patient-oriented care principles not only in clinical encounters, but also in the design of care pathways and services, so that health-system structures actively reduce, rather than intensify, barriers experienced by patients. Participants also offered actionable, context-relevant recommendations that identify feasible improvements in care delivery, education and policy. Collectively, these findings reinforce the value of engaging frontline providers in health system improvement and offer a replicable approach for other jurisdictions seeking to strengthen chronic respiratory care in ways that are both effective and equity oriented.
