Radiomics-assisted unsupervised clustering and predictive modeling to identify optimal candidates for virtual navigation bronchoscopy localization and CT-guided localization in thoracoscopic lung resection
Authors: Yu Huang, Hongchun Xu, Feng Xiang, Longyu Jin, Yingji Chen, Wei Feng, Yuchao Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate localization of small pulmonary nodules during thoracoscopic lung resection remains challenging, and the selection of preoperative localization methods largely relies on surgeon experience. This study aimed to integrate radiomic features and clinical characteristics to develop a data-driven patient clustering approach and to explore the suitability of different preoperative localization methods across patient subgroups. Patients who underwent thoracoscopic wedge resection or segmentectomy with virtual navigation bronchoscopy (VNB) localization, computed tompgraphy (CT)-guided localization, or no localization were retrospectively included. Radiomic features were extracted from preoperative CT images and integrated with clinical and nodule characteristics. Unsupervised K-means clustering was applied. Within each cluster, perioperative outcomes were compared among localization methods. In addition, supervised learning models were constructed to predict cluster assignment and intraoperative blood loss. A total of 925 patients were included, and 3 stable clusters were identified. In Cluster 0, VNB localization was associated with lower intraoperative blood loss than CT-guided localization [wedge resection: 50 (20, 70) ml versus 70 (50, 100) ml, P = .004; segmentectomy: 50 (20, 100) ml versus 100 (50, 100) ml, P = .002]. In Cluster 2, CT-guided localization was associated with lower intraoperative blood loss than VNB localization [wedge resection: 50 (30, 90) ml versus 100 (50, 110) ml, P = .005; segmentectomy: 90 (50, 100) ml versus 105 (85, 170) ml, P = .011], along with a higher localization success rate. The supervised model demonstrated stable performance in predicting cluster assignment (macro-area under the curve = 0.863) and intraoperative blood loss (macro-area under the curve range: 0.813–0.850). VNB localization and CT-guided localization demonstrated distinct advantages across different clusters. These findings support a data-driven, individualized strategy for preoperative localization selection.

1. Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide, with a 5-year survival rate of only about 18%.[1] Patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer generally have a better prognosis after curative surgical resection. Although the risk of recurrence and metastasis still exists, it decreases significantly with lower tumor stage. Therefore, early detection and treatment of primary lung cancer are crucial for improving patient outcomes.[2,3] In recent years, the widespread use of low-dose computed tomography has markedly increased the detection rate of small pulmonary nodules, making early diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer possible.[4] Surgical resection remains the preferred treatment for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer.[5] However, for small nodules with limited solid components or pure ground-glass opacity, achieving precise intraoperative localization continues to be a major challenge for thoracic surgeons.

Currently, several preoperative localization techniques are used for pulmonary nodules, including computed tomography (CT)-guided percutaneous localization, bronchoscopic localization, ultrasonography, and infrared imaging.[6–11] Among them, CT-guided localization and bronchoscopic localization are the most widely used. A prospective study reported that CT-guided localization-assisted thoracoscopic lung resection achieved a success rate of up to 97%, improving surgical accuracy and patient management.[12] However, CT-guided localization has limitations. In a large population-based study involving more than 15,000 patients, the incidence of pneumothorax after puncture was about 15%, and localization was more difficult for nodules located beneath the scapula or near the mediastinum.[13] Bronchoscopic localization mainly includes electromagnetic navigation bronchoscopy (ENB) and virtual navigation bronchoscopy (VNB).[9,14,15] Studies have shown that compared with ENB, VNB offers higher specificity and sensitivity in diagnosing pulmonary nodules while reducing economic burden.[16] At present, ENB has been more extensively studied with larger sample sizes,[17–20] whereas VNB research remains relatively limited.[14,21] Moreover, most previous studies have focused on patients undergoing VNB alone,[21,22] with few investigating perioperative outcomes in patients who also underwent thoracoscopic lung resection.

In clinical practice, surgeons usually rely on personal experience to decide whether preoperative localization is necessary and which method to use. However, such experience-based decisions vary greatly among individuals and lack objective, quantitative criteria. Therefore, exploring the suitability and prognostic impact of different localization methods based on the imaging characteristics of pulmonary nodules may provide new insights for developing personalized preoperative localization strategies.

In recent years, radiomics based on CT imaging has developed rapidly in the field of lung cancer. It has been widely used to predict treatment response, risk stratification, and prognosis,[23,24] showing strong predictive performance in multiple studies.[25,26] Radiomics can extract a large number of high-dimensional quantitative features from CT images.[27,28] To address challenges such as high feature dimensionality and complex information, machine learning has been increasingly applied in medical image analysis. Among these methods, unsupervised learning models can automatically identify patient subgroups in multidimensional feature spaces, revealing potential heterogeneity and hidden feature patterns.[29]

However, there is still a lack of systematic studies comparing perioperative outcomes between CT-guided localization and VNB localization. This study aimed to integrate radiomic features, clinical characteristics, and unsupervised learning to develop a data-driven patient clustering approach for exploring the suitability of different preoperative localization methods across patient clusters. Furthermore, based on the clustering results, we constructed supervised predictive models to assist in identifying the optimal preoperative localization strategy for individual patients.[30]

2. Methods

2.1. Patient selection and clinical data extraction

This retrospective study analyzed patients who underwent thoracoscopic lung resection at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery of Xiangya Third Hospital, Central South University, between March 2019 and January 2025. The cohort included patients who received VNB localization, CT-guided localization, or no specific preoperative localization. According to intraoperative assessment, successful localization was defined as the presence of localization markers (either dye or a hook) on the pleural surface corresponding to the lung parenchyma within 1 cm of the nodule. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Xiangya Third Hospital, Central South University (approval number: Kuai 25083), and all patients provided written informed consent.

All patients who underwent thoracoscopic lung resection for a solitary pulmonary nodule were assessed using predefined exclusion criteria. Patients with multiple nodules requiring treatment of a single target lesion were also assessed, because only the target lesion required localization and analysis, whereas nontarget nodules did not require separate localization or specific intraoperative manipulation during the index operation. Patients were excluded if they: were younger than 18 years; were admitted for emergency surgery; had a nodule larger than 1.5 cm; had a history of ipsilateral thoracic or pulmonary surgery; had distant or regional lymph node metastasis; had received radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or neoadjuvant therapy; had severe pleural adhesions identified during surgery; underwent other localization methods; had incomplete clinical data; or underwent lobectomy.

Basic clinical information was collected, including age, sex, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, surgical procedure (wedge resection or segmentectomy), pathological stage, forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1), nodule density, size, location, distance from the pleura, and preoperative comorbidities.

Outcome variables included operative time (from skin incision to wound closure), specimen retrieval time (from skin incision to removal of the specimen), intraoperative blood loss (as documented in the operative and anesthesia records), postoperative length of stay, chest tube duration, chest drainage volume on postoperative day 1, intensive care unit stay, conversion to thoracotomy, discharge with a chest tube, postoperative pneumothorax or pleural effusion, pulmonary inflammation, postoperative fever, postoperative white blood cell (WBC) count, localization success rate, and other complications.

2.2. CT radiomic feature extraction and selection

All included patients underwent preoperative chest CT scanning at Xiangya Third Hospital, Central South University, within 2 weeks before surgery, using a slice thickness of 1 mm. Pulmonary nodules were confirmed by a radiologist with more than 10 years of experience and a thoracic surgeon. Based on the preoperative CT images, regions of interest (ROI) were segmented and annotated using 3-dimensional Slicer, and radiomic features were extracted from the ROI masks using the Radiomics module.[31] ROI segmentation was performed manually using a standardized segmentation protocol, with consistent window settings and slice-by-slice annotation criteria. Before feature extraction, all CT images were resampled to isotropic 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 voxels. Intensity normalization was then performed within the lung window range (−1000–400 Hounsfield units), followed by discretization using a fixed bin width of 25 Hounsfield units to ensure consistency in feature calculation.

2.3. Unsupervised clustering analysis

In the preprocessing stage, radiomic features with a variance lower than 0.01 were removed, followed by the elimination of highly correlated features with a pairwise correlation coefficient > 0.9. No CT-based disease severity ranking score was assigned; CT information entered the clustering model through quantitative radiomic features and predefined nodule characteristics. All radiomic features were standardized using z-score normalization and then combined with prespecified preoperative clinical characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, smoking history, FEV1, and preoperative comorbidities, as well as nodule features, including size, density, distance from the pleura, and location. Before merging, numerical variables were standardized using z-score normalization, and categorical variables were encoded using 1-hot encoding. To reduce dimensionality and noise, principal component analysis was applied, and principal components explaining 95% of the variance were retained for clustering. K-means clustering was then performed to identify potential patient subgroups. Candidate cluster numbers were evaluated using the elbow method based on the within-cluster sum of squares. Perioperative outcomes were not included in the clustering algorithm or used as optimization targets for determining the number of clusters. Outcome comparisons were performed only after the cluster labels had been fixed.

2.4. Statistical analysis

Within each surgical stratum (wedge resection and segmentectomy), 1:1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using a multinomial logistic regression model to compare patients who underwent different localization methods (no localization, VNB localization, and CT-guided localization) across clusters. Model covariates included age, sex, smoking history, BMI, FEV1, preoperative comorbidities, and nodule characteristics. After matching, major outcomes were compared between groups. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the t-test. Variables with a non-normal distribution were presented as median (Q1, Q3) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage [n (%)] and compared using the χ2 test. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Multiprocessor 18.0, and a 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

To assess the stability of the clustering results, a prospective internal validation cohort was collected from the same institution and analyzed using the same preprocessing procedures, parameter settings, and K-means clustering method as the retrospective cohort. Major outcomes were then compared between groups.

2.6. Supervised prediction model construction

To explore the predictability of the clusters, supervised prediction models were developed using clinical and imaging features. The dependent variable was the cluster assignment derived from unsupervised clustering, and independent variables included age, sex, BMI, and preoperative clinical, nodule, and imaging features that showed significant differences between clusters.

Before model construction, all variables were standardized. The data were randomly divided into a training set and a testing set at a 7:3 ratio. Random Forest, Logistic Regression, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) models were built in the training set and evaluated in the testing set. Major performance metrics included accuracy, F1-score, recall, and the area under the curve. In addition, 5-fold cross-validation was performed for the best-performing model, and hyperparameters were optimized using grid search. For Random Forest and XGBoost models, built-in feature importance values were extracted to assess the relative contribution of each variable.

To further explore the relationship between localization methods and intraoperative blood loss within different clusters, XGBoost models were developed separately for patients who underwent wedge resection or segmentectomy in each cluster. Features potentially associated with intraoperative blood loss were first screened using univariate analysis with a threshold of P < .20. Age, sex, BMI, coagulation disorders, hypertension history, and localization method were then included as independent variables in the models. Five-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate model performance.

3.2. Wedge resection

In Cluster 0, the VNB group had lower intraoperative blood loss and lower chest drainage volume on postoperative day 1 compared with the CT group (50 [20, 70] ml vs 70 [50, 100] ml, P = .004; 120 [75, 180] ml vs 155 [100, 250] ml, P = .020) and the no localization group (50 [20, 70] ml vs 50 [50, 100] ml, P = .025; 120 [75, 180] ml vs 163 [100, 250] ml, P = .037). The VNB group also had a lower incidence of postoperative pulmonary inflammation than the CT group (40 [82%] vs 41 [98%], P = .015]. In Cluster 1, the CT group had a lower incidence of elevated postoperative WBC count compared with the VNB group (11 [48%] vs 31 [78%], P = .016]. In Cluster 2, the CT group had lower intraoperative blood loss than the VNB group (50 [30, 90] ml vs 100 [50, 110] ml, P = .005) and the no localization group (50 [30, 90] ml vs 100 [50, 123] ml, P = .049). The no localization group had a longer chest tube duration than the VNB group (4 [3, 5] vs 3 [2, 4], P = .019) and the CT group (4 [3, 5] vs 3 [3, 4], P = .049). The CT group had a lower incidence of postoperative pleural effusion than the no localization group (36 [72%] vs 68 [89%], P = .011) and a higher localization success rate than the VNB group (46 [92%] vs 45 [76%], P = .028) (Fig. 2; Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content 2 and Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content 3).

3.3. Segmentectomy

In Cluster 0, the VNB group had lower intraoperative blood loss, lower chest drainage volume on postoperative day 1, and a lower incidence of elevated postoperative WBC count compared with the CT group (50 [20, 100] ml vs 100 [50, 100] ml, P = .002; 100 [85, 165] vs 155 [100, 250], P = .009; 13 [36%] vs 42 [67%], P = .003) and the no localization group (50 [20, 100] ml vs 100 [50, 120] ml, P = .012; 100 [85, 165] vs 150 [100, 200], P = .049; 13 [36%] vs 21 [60%], P = .044). In Cluster 2, the CT group had lower intraoperative blood loss than the VNB group (90 [50, 100] ml vs 105 [85, 170] ml, P = .011) and the no localization group (90 [50, 100] ml vs 100 [50, 160] ml, P = .018) (Fig. 2; Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content 4 and Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content 5).

3.4. Internal validation cohort

In the internal validation cohort, 64, 80, and 105 patients were assigned to Clusters 0, 1, and 2, respectively. In wedge resection, Cluster 0 showed differences in intraoperative blood loss among the 3 localization methods, with the VNB group having the lowest values (P = .035). Cluster 2 also showed differences, with the CT group having the lowest intraoperative blood loss (P = .031). In segmentectomy, Cluster 0 showed differences in intraoperative blood loss (P = .049), chest drainage volume on postoperative day 1 (P = .037), and the incidence of elevated postoperative WBC count (P = .025), all of which were lowest in the VNB group. In Cluster 2, the CT group had relatively lower intraoperative blood loss, although the difference was not statistically significant (Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content 6 and Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content 7).

Overall, the results showed that the VNB group had a more favorable perioperative profile in Cluster 0. In Cluster 2, the CT group demonstrated advantages in intraoperative blood loss, drainage-related outcomes, and localization success rate. In Cluster 1, no notable differences were observed among the 3 localization methods. Among the evaluated perioperative outcomes, including operative time, localization success rate, postoperative complications, and postoperative recovery indicators, intraoperative blood loss showed clear differences in Clusters 0 and 2 and held important clinical relevance. This measure reflects procedural safety and provides meaningful guidance for selecting a preoperative localization method. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on patients in Clusters 0 and 2 and on intraoperative blood loss to further explore the applicability of different localization methods across patient subgroups.

3.6. Differences in radiomic and clinical features across clusters

Analysis of radiomic features showed that patients in Cluster 0 had higher values of firstorder_10Percentile, firstorder_Kurtosis, glcm_ClusterShade, gldm_LargeDependenceHighGrayLevelEmphasis, and glrlm_LongRunHighGrayLevelEmphasis, and a lower value of firstorder_Range. These features indicated higher overall nodule density with a more uniform and continuous distribution of high-density regions. Patients in Cluster 2 had higher values of shape_MajorAxisLength, shape_MinorAxisLength, shape_Maximum2DDiameterColumn, shape_Maximum2DDiameterRow, and firstorder_Range, suggesting larger nodules with more complex morphology and more heterogeneous internal density. For preoperative clinical and nodule characteristics, patients in Cluster 0 had higher FEV1, a higher proportion of solid nodules, greater distance from the pleura, and a higher proportion of nodules located in the lower lung. Patients in Cluster 2 had a higher incidence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, larger nodule volume, a higher proportion of part-solid and ground-glass nodules, and a higher proportion of nodules located in the upper lung (Fig. 4).

3.7. Supervised prediction model analysis

3.7.1. Cluster prediction model

To facilitate the application of the unsupervised clustering results in individualized clinical decision-making, supervised prediction models were developed using cluster assignment as the outcome. The XGBoost model demonstrated the highest predictive performance (accuracy = 0.82; macro-area under the curve [AUC] = 0.863) (Fig. 5; Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content 9), outperforming logistic regression (accuracy = 0.72; macro-AUC = 0.784) and random forest (accuracy = 0.78; macro-AUC = 0.822) (Fig. S2, Supplemental Digital Content 10 and Table S8, Supplemental Digital Content 9). Five-fold cross-validation of the XGBoost model showed stable performance across folds (F1-score = 0.755; accuracy = 0.761; macro-AUC = 0.824) (Fig. 5; Table S9, Supplemental Digital Content 11). Feature importance analysis showed that the top ten features accounted for 82% of the cumulative contribution (Fig. S3, Supplemental Digital Content 12).

3.7.2. Intraoperative blood loss prediction model

To further assess the risk of intraoperative blood loss associated with different localization methods within each cluster, prediction models were developed using intraoperative blood loss as the outcome. Based on the distribution of blood loss, patients undergoing wedge resection were classified into 4 groups: 0 to 40 ml, 41 to 80 ml, 81 to 120 ml, and > 120 ml. Patients undergoing segmentectomy were classified into 0 to 50 ml, 51 to 100 ml, 101 to 150 ml, and > 150 ml. XGBoost models were then constructed for patients in Clusters 0 and 2 undergoing wedge resection and segmentectomy. Five-fold cross-validation showed stable performance across the 4 models (Cluster0-wedge: F1-score = 0.774; accuracy = 0.773; macro-AUC = 0.833), (Cluster2-wedge: F1-score = 0.739; accuracy = 0.741; macro-AUC = 0.813), (Cluster0-segmentectomy: F1-score = 0.717; accuracy = 0.731; macro-AUC = 0.850), (Cluster2-segmentectomy: F1-score = 0.764; accuracy = 0.769; macro-AUC = 0.830) (Fig. 6; Fig. S4, Supplemental Digital Content 13 and Table S10, Supplemental Digital Content 14). 4. Discussion

This study focused on the clinical challenge of localizing small, non-palpable pulmonary nodules during thoracoscopic surgery. Previous studies have shown that intraoperative localization relying solely on visual inspection or instrument sweeping achieves a success rate of only about 30%, and the failure rate may reach 50% or higher when the nodule diameter is ≤ 10 mm and the distance from the pleura is > 5 mm.[32–35] Therefore, accurate preoperative and intraoperative localization remains essential for improving surgical precision. In this study, radiomic features derived from CT imaging were integrated with preoperative clinical and nodule characteristics to identify patient subgroups using unsupervised clustering, followed by a comparison of perioperative outcomes among different localization methods in wedge resection and segmentectomy. The results showed that in Cluster 0, VNB localization was associated with lower intraoperative blood loss and favorable drainage and inflammation-related outcomes compared with CT-guided localization and no localization. In Cluster 2, CT-guided localization was associated with lower intraoperative blood loss, a lower incidence of postoperative pleural effusion, and a higher localization success rate. No notable differences were observed among localization methods in Cluster 1. In addition, supervised models showed stable performance, supporting an individualized decision-making approach that first predicts cluster assignment and then uses surgical procedure and predicted blood loss to select the localization method.

Previous studies comparing bronchoscopic localization and CT-guided localization in general populations have shown that both techniques achieve similar localization success rates, while CT-guided localization is associated with a higher incidence of puncture-related complications such as pneumothorax.[36–38] The present findings are consistent with previous studies in that both localization methods achieved high success rates, and VNB showed an advantage in reducing complication risk. Furthermore, this study demonstrated that the direction and magnitude of this advantage differed across patient clusters. In addition, intraoperative blood loss was used as the core outcome for comparison across different localization methods.[36,39] Although the absolute differences in blood loss were modest in some comparisons, lower blood loss may still have clinical relevance because it may reflect easier target identification, less parenchymal manipulation, and a smoother operative workflow during thoracoscopic resection of small pulmonary nodules.

Feature importance analysis showed that the models were primarily driven by physiological indicators, shape features, and texture features, with these variable types playing complementary roles in predicting different clusters. FEV1 had the highest weight and showed the highest mean value in Cluster 0, suggesting that patients with better lung function may have more favorable airway compliance under ventilation, making them more suitable for VNB localization. Furthermore, higher glcm_ClusterShade and firstorder_Kurtosis values and lower firstorder_Range in Cluster 0 indicated a more concentrated internal grayscale distribution and greater texture uniformity, which may improve the stability and reduce the localization error of VNB during target identification and segmentation. In Cluster 2, higher shape_Maximum2DDiameter and firstorder_Range values indicated larger nodule size and greater internal density variation, resulting in higher visibility and contrast on CT images. For such targets, CT can directly display the nodule boundary and position on a single slice, making needle trajectory planning more straightforward and favoring CT-guided localization. Other features, such as firstorder_10Percentile and distance from the pleura, also influenced the selection and safety of localization methods to varying degrees. Overall, these features collectively reflected airway reachability, nodule visibility, and imaging recognizability, providing a multifactor framework for interpreting the optimal localization method across different patient clusters.

In the wedge resection subgroup of Cluster 2, most VNB localization failures occurred in the right upper lobe, particularly in the apical segment. Clinical observations and previous studies suggest that the apical segmental bronchi have steep branching angles and complex trajectories, and the flexibility and small outer diameter of the ultrathin bronchoscope may limit its ability to navigate near sharp turns approaching 180°.[40–42] In addition, double-lumen endotracheal intubation may alter bronchial morphology and reduce airway elasticity, further decreasing distal reachability and increasing the risk of VNB localization failure. This chain of anatomical configuration, ventilation management, and instrument reachability may explain why CT-guided localization performed better in Cluster 2. Meanwhile, in Cluster 0 (characterized by more homogeneous nodule density distribution, higher FEV1 values, and greater distance from the pleura) airway-based VNB localization may allow a more reproducible and controllable navigation pathway, thereby reducing intraoperative blood loss and improving postoperative drainage.

This study suggests that preoperative localization should be selected in an individualized manner for new patients. If the model predicts Cluster 0 (characterized by higher and more homogeneous nodule density, better lung function, and greater distance from the pleura) and VNB localization is associated with the lowest intraoperative blood loss, VNB localization is recommended. If the model predicts Cluster 2 (characterized by larger nodule size, greater density heterogeneity, a higher proportion of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and a higher proportion of upper-lobe nodules) and CT-guided localization is associated with the lowest intraoperative blood loss, CT-guided localization is the more appropriate choice. In addition, CT-guided localization should be considered for lesions that are difficult to reach in the upper lobe or in patients whose bronchial morphology may be altered by double-lumen intubation, while VNB localization may be preferred in patients at high risk of pneumothorax or when puncture-related complications should be minimized.[39,42]

In terms of real-world feasibility, this workflow is based on routine preoperative thin-slice CT images and does not require additional imaging examinations. The main additional workload includes ROI segmentation, radiomic feature extraction, and model-based prediction. Once the pipeline is standardized, feature extraction and prediction can be completed rapidly, although manual ROI segmentation remains time-consuming and requires trained personnel. The cost-effectiveness and workflow efficiency of this approach were not formally evaluated and should be assessed in future prospective implementation studies.

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first study to integrate unsupervised clustering, radiomic features, preoperative clinical characteristics, and nodule features to compare different preoperative localization methods for pulmonary nodules. In addition, supervised models were used to predict cluster assignment and intraoperative blood loss, shifting the experience-based selection of localization methods toward a data-driven individualized decision-making process. Second, the sample size was adequate, models were constructed separately for different surgical procedures, confounding was controlled using PSM, and a prospective internal validation cohort was included to enhance cluster robustness. Third, based on previous studies and clinical observations, this study proposed a reasonable mechanism in which the anatomy of the right upper lobe apical segment combined with double-lumen intubation may limit distal navigation, supporting the relative advantage of CT-guided localization in Cluster 2.[40]

This study has several limitations. First, although a prospective internal validation cohort was included, the study was based on single-center data. Therefore, its generalizability to other institutions with different scanners and imaging protocols remains uncertain and requires further validation in larger multicenter cohorts. Second, the elbow plot allowed more than 1 plausible cluster solution. Although perioperative outcomes were not included in the clustering algorithm or used as optimization targets, the 3-cluster framework should be considered an exploratory and clinically interpretable classification, and its robustness requires further validation using external datasets and alternative clustering methods. Third, the localization method was not randomly assigned but was selected according to surgeon experience and nodule characteristics. Although PSM was used to balance measured baseline variables, residual confounding by indication and selection bias may still exist; therefore, the proposed data-driven framework should be interpreted as an adjunct to clinical judgment rather than a replacement for it. Fourth, formal interobserver or intraobserver reproducibility assessment of ROI segmentation was not performed, which may affect the stability of radiomic features. Fifth, multiple comparisons were performed across clusters, surgical procedures, localization methods, and perioperative outcomes without formal multiplicity adjustment; therefore, the P values should be interpreted as exploratory. Nevertheless, this study provides a new approach for selecting preoperative localization methods in thoracoscopic lung resection and has potential clinical application after further validation.
