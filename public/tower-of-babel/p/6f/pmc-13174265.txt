Functional chromatin signatures premark future lineage-specific enhancers
Authors: Julian Pulecio, Zakieh Tayyebi, Dingyu Liu, Wilfred Wong, Renhe Luo, Jeyaram R Damodaran, Samuel J Kaplan, Nan Hu, Hyein S Cho, Jielin Yan, Dylan Murphy, Robert W Rickert, Abhijit Shukla, Aaron Zhong, Denis Torre, Qianzi Li, Federico González, Dexin Yang, Wenbo Li, Ting Zhou, Effie Apostolou, Christina S Leslie, Danwei Huangfu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIt remains unknown whether early embryonic cells harbor a blueprint for future enhancers that regulate the expression of lineage-specific genes in adult tissues. Here, we demonstrate that embryonic stem cells (ESCs) have transcriptionally competent chromatin regions (CCRs) prepared to induce the expression of lineage genes prior to differentiation. CCRs represent activatable pre-enhancers within the topological chromatin domains of lineage genes, marked by chromatin signatures distinguishable from primed/poised enhancers, enabling their genome-wide identification. The pioneer transcription factor (TF) FOXA2 preferentially binds CCRs during early lineage specification, promoting their conversion into active enhancers. CCRs can be harnessed to boost the expression of master TFs and promote the direct reprogramming of ESCs into differentiated cells, showcasing their potential for practical applications. Our findings identify a mechanism by which ESCs rapidly establish enhancer activity during early lineage differentiation and expand our understanding of the epigenetic features supporting transcriptional regulation and cellular plasticity.Keywords: gene regulation, epigenetics, pluripotency, embryonic stem cells, lineage specification, chromatin, developmental competence, CRISPR

Introduction

Elucidating the molecular mechanisms that support the ability of embryonic stem cells (ESCs) to differentiate into any cell type has broad implications for our understanding of cellular plasticity in developmental cell-fate decision, tissue regeneration, and disease pathogenesis. The presence of bivalent domains at the promoters of lineage genes as well as the hyperdynamic binding of chromatin-associated proteins are some of the mechanisms that allow ESCs to swiftly activate or repress distinct lineage-specific gene-expression programs in response to external differentiation cues.1,2,3 However, it is unclear how ESCs can rapidly activate distal cis-regulatory regions to induce the expression of lineage genes. Previous observations suggest that chromatin regions that become active enhancers in differentiated cells are associated with specific chromatin premarks in ESCs before lineage specification.4,5,6,7,8,9,10 Yet, the functional significance of these chromatin premarks in ESCs remains an open question and, fundamentally, whether ESCs have an intrinsic roadmap for future enhancers is still unclear.

To investigate whether tissue-specific enhancers are pre-established during early embryonic development, we hypothesized the existence of functional pre-enhancers, which upon activation can instruct the transcriptional upregulation of their target genes. We postulated that the dead Cas9 (dCas9) protein could serve as a targetable pioneer transcription factor (TF),11 with the ability to bind to previously inaccessible chromatin regions through RNA-guided targeting12 without the constraint of TF-binding motifs. Previous studies have employed CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) systems to induce gene expression by targeting their promoter regions with the dCas9 protein coupled to potent transcriptional activators.13,14 Here, we repurpose a CRISPRa system to directly interrogate the ability of distal chromatin regions to activate the expression of lineage genes in ESCs. This strategy enabled us to uncover chromatin regions with the intrinsic potential to induce the expression of lineage genes in ESCs, characterize the epigenetic features underlying their transcriptional competence, and harness the potential of such regions to promote the differentiation of pluripotent stem cells.

Results

CRISPRa screens discover competent chromatin regions in ESCs

We employed CRISPRa as a functional assay to investigate distal (non-promoter) chromatin regions capable of activating lineage-specific gene expression in undifferentiated human ESCs (hESCs). Accordingly, we generated an inducible hESC line expressing the Synergistic Activation Mediator CRISPRa system (iSAM), which recruits VP64, p65, and HSF1 activators to any chromatin region of interest.15 To compare CRISPRa-identified regions with functional enhancers of lineage genes, we also generated a CRISPR-interference (CRISPRi) hESC line with an inducible dCas9-KRAB cassette. CRISPRa interrogations were performed without any differentiation cues to reveal chromatin potential at the ESC stage (sufficiency), while CRISPRi interrogations aimed to identify enhancers required for gene activation during differentiation (necessity).

We first designed screens to interrogate chromatin regions surrounding PDX1, a master TF that is inactive in hESCs but highly expressed in pancreatic progenitor (PP) cells.16 Two matching guide RNA (gRNA) libraries, compatible with either CRISPRa or CRISPRi, were created to tile ∼100 kb spanning the PDX1 locus (Figure 1A). After library infection and dCas9 activation, PDX1+ and PDX1− cells were isolated by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for gRNA enrichment analysis in both screens (Figures S1A and S1B). MAGeCK analysis17 of the CRISPRa screen identified 423 gRNAs enriched in the PDX1+ fraction. A sliding-window approach18 revealed six delimited regions associated with the induction of PDX1 expression in hESCs (Figure 1B and Table S1). Similarly, the CRISPRi screen identified ten PDX1 regulatory elements, many of which overlapped with the CRISPRa-identified regions (Figure 1B and Table S1). We validated ten regions, including five identified in both screens and five unique to each screen, and confirmed six regions capable of activating PDX1 expression in undifferentiated hESCs. Five of the six activatable pre-enhancers overlapped with CRISPRi-identified enhancer elements that are required for PDX1 activation during differentiation (Figures 1C, S1C–S1E and Table S1). We termed the CRISPRa-identified regions competent chromatin regions (CCRs), based on their demonstrated ability to induce lineage gene expression without differentiation cues.

Based on our PDX1 results, we sought to identify CCRs at a larger scale by interrogating regions that become accessible upon differentiation and likely function as enhancers. Focusing on four hallmark genes expressed in definitive endoderm (DE) cells (GATA6, EOMES, SOX17, and MIXL1), we designed a CRISPR gRNA library targeting 163 regions encompassing 12.8 Mb surrounding these four lineage gene loci (Figure 1D). A matching library was previously used in a CRISPRi screen for DE enhancer discovery,19 allowing downstream comparisons between CCRs and functional enhancers. After transducing iSAM hESCs with the gRNA library, we induced CRISPRa for 48 h and FACS-sorted cells expressing any of the four DE marker genes (Figures 1E and S2A). We identified 197 gRNAs significantly enriched in the marker-positive cells compared to the marker-negative cells (Table S2; Figures 1F and S2B). Further analysis identified 36 CCR hits linked to the activation of GATA6 (16 hits), SOX17 (8 hits), EOMES (7 hits), and MIXL1 (5 hits) (Table S3). We individually validated 15 CCR hits and confirmed that all were able to induce the expression of their associated genes upon CRISPRa-mediated activation (Figures 1G and S2C), supporting the robustness of the CRISPRa screens.

Our results indicate that hESCs have functional chromatin regions, which we termed CCRs, that can strongly induce lineage gene expression to protein levels comparable to those observed in differentiated cells (Figures 1H and S2D). In total, we identified 42 CCRs associated with five lineage genes at the ESC stage. Comparison with CRISPRi screens in differentiated cells (this study and previous work19) revealed that ∼62% of CCRs overlap with functional enhancers of these five lineage genes (Figure 1I and Table S3), and ∼86% overlap with H3K27ac-marked putative enhancers in differentiated cells (Figure S2E). In line with these results, we observed significantly increased levels of H3K27ac and chromatin accessibility at CCRs following ESC differentiation into DE (for GATA6, EOMES, SOX17, and MIXL1) and PP (for PDX1) cells (Figure S2F). These findings suggest that CCRs represent a functional blueprint for a subset of future enhancers, priming them for activation upon lineage differentiation.

CCRs reside within the same TADs as their linked genes

CCRs identify activatable pre-enhancers. To understand the mechanisms underlying this unique ability, we explored molecular features that support CCR transcriptional competence and distinguish them from the candidate pre-enhancer regions that were not able to induce gene expression in our assays (non-CCR regions) (Figure 2A and Table S3). Considering that ∼38% of CCRs are located more than 100 kb away from the transcription start site (TSS) of their associated lineage gene (Figure 2B), we sought to determine whether CCRs are constrained by their surrounding chromatin topology. Gene-regulatory regions, including enhancers, are typically located within topologically associated domains (TADs) containing their target genes.20 TADs are thought to facilitate promoter-enhancer interactions either through direct contact or by oligomerization of the TFs bound to these regions.21 We performed Hi-C assays and employed TopDom22 to identify TAD boundaries in hESCs. We found that all CCRs are exclusively located within the same TAD of their associated lineage gene, except for MIXL1, which was not initially assigned to a TAD. In contrast, ∼59% of the non-CCRs were found outside the TADs linked to the corresponding genes (Figures 2C, 2D, and S3A–S3C). Expanding the window size used to scan for chromatin domain boundaries identified a MIXL1-containing TAD, and we found all five MIXL1 CCRs also mapped within (Figure S3D and Table S4). This indicates that TADs demarcate the chromatin regions with the competence to activate the expression of lineage genes before differentiation. The restriction of CCRs within TADs may provide a mechanism for the confinement of future enhancer-gene links within these topological boundaries.

To further investigate the spatial connection of CCRs and their corresponding genes, we explored whether CCRs have increased chromatin interactions with their linked genes in hESCs, even before activation by CRISPRa targeting or exposure to differentiation signals. To overcome the limited resolution of the Hi-C data for identifying chromatin loops in bins smaller than 50 kb, we performed circular chromosome conformation capture coupled with sequencing (4C-seq, hereafter 4C) assays on hESCs using EOMES, PDX1, SOX17, and GATA6 as viewpoints (VPs) (Table S8). To determine the chromatin contacts with the VPs, we computed kernel density estimates of the 4C Z scores for CCRs, non-CCRs, and background regions by calculating the probability of observing the counts under an estimated background model. Non-CCRs exhibited a unimodal density, suggesting that the non-competent regions are neither preferentially enriched nor depleted of contacts with the corresponding gene. In comparison, CCRs tended to have a higher number of significant chromatin contacts with their corresponding VPs compared to background levels, but only a subset of CCRs showed a strong contact enrichment with the corresponding genes as indicated by the bimodal density estimate (Figures 2E, S3A–S3C, and S3E). Interestingly, CCRs found at distant locations tended to have a higher number of chromatin contacts with their associated genes (Figure S3F). In summary, our Hi-C and 4C results demonstrate that the activity of CCRs is constrained by the three-dimensional conformation of the chromatin. In addition, pre-existing chromatin interactions with their target genes in ESCs may contribute to the functionality of some CCRs, especially for those positioned relatively far from their target gene promoter.

CCRs are preferentially bound by pluripotent TFs and DNA demethylation factors

To investigate whether additional factors beyond the three-dimensional chromatin conformation could influence the transcriptional competence of CCRs in ESCs, we performed HOMER motif enrichment analysis23 comparing CCRs to random genomic regions. This revealed that CCRs are significantly enriched for POU, HMG(SOX), ZF (GATA), and FOX motifs (Figure 3A). When comparing CCRs and non-CCRs, either within the TADs containing our genes of interest or irrespective of their location, POU motifs showed the strongest enrichment in CCRs (Figures 3B, S4A, and S4B; Table S5).

The enrichment in POU motifs at the CCRs suggests binding by OCT4 (POU5F1) and related pluripotency factors. We compared the chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) binding levels of OCT4 in hESCs between CCRs and non-CCRs within gene-containing TADs, along with 24 additional factors including pluripotency TFs, histone marks, and chromatin modifiers (Table S6). The top factors enriched at CCRs were the pluripotent TFs OCT4 and NANOG and the DNA demethylation factors QSER124 and TET1 (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, effect size r > 0.2, p < 0.05) (Figure 3C and Table S6). A comparison between CCRs and non-CCRs without the TAD restriction showed similar results but with lower effect size or consistency for ChIP-seq data from different studies (Figure S4C and Table S6), which could be attributed to the likelihood that some non-CCRs outside the gene-containing TADs may function as CCRs for other genes not tested in our experiments. Previous studies have suggested that regions marked by H3K27me3, H3K4me1, or the histone variant H2A.Z are predisposed to acquire enhancer features during differentiation.6,8,10 We found that the ChIP signal of those marks tends to be elevated at the CCRs but is also elevated at the non-CCR regions (Figure 3D), suggesting that additional features are needed to predict whether a region can be directly activated in hESCs. ChIP-seq profiles together with meta-signal plots confirmed that the binding levels of most of the enriched factors in CCRs were indeed higher than those in non-CCRs inside gene-containing TADs (Figures 3E and S4D–S4F). Other pluripotent TFs such as SOX2 or KLF5, or histone marks associated with active enhancers such as H3K27ac, are not significantly higher in CCRs (Figure S4G). In summary, CCRs are enriched for POU sequence motifs and exhibit distinct chromatin features, with notably enriched binding of OCT4, NANOG, QSER1, and TET1.

ON and ONQT signatures enable genome-wide identification of CCRs

We sought to identify CCRs across the hESC genome by integrating CRISPRa screening with single-cell transcriptomics using a modified CRISPR droplet sequencing (CROP-seq) construct for gRNA expression25,26 (Figures 4A and 4B). We selected 70 TFs that are not active in hESCs but are expressed in differentiated tissues representing all three germ layers19,27 (Figures S5A and S5B; Table S7). Within the TADs containing these TFs, we identified non-coding regions (distal intergenic) bound by OCT4 and NANOG (ON) and regions co-bound by OCT4, NANOG, QSER1, and TET1 (ONQT). Additionally, we identified distal intergenic regions in the same TADs marked only by H3K27me3, as presumed poised enhancers,6,10 for comparison with ON or ONQT chromatin regions. We generated a gRNA library targeting 234 regions, evenly distributed across the three chromatin signatures (ON, ONQT, and H3K27me3-only). This large-scale library assesses 2,132 potential combinations of CCR-gene pairs associated with 317 genes (within the selected TADs) across 18 chromosomes (Figure 4A and Table S7). We included non-targeting and safe-targeting gRNAs as negative controls and gRNAs targeting the promoters of 35 lineage TFs as positive controls. To maximize the detection of CCR-gene pairs, we performed two independent CRISPRa screens aiming for a single and a multiplex CRISPR array (see STAR Methods). We used the SCEPTRE algorithm28 to identify CCR-gene activation pairs by comparing gene expression in cells carrying gRNAs targeting candidate regions and cells carrying negative control gRNAs (Figure S5C). The combined analysis of the single and multiplexed array revealed 131 CCR-gene pairs linking 89 newly discovered CCRs to the activation of 75 genes (Table S7).

We found that 90% of the discovered CCRs had the ONQT and ON signatures (Figures 4C and 4D). Remarkably, the probability of discovering CCR-gene activation pairs through ONQT or ON targeting was not significantly different from targeting gene promoters (positive controls), whereas targeting H3K27me3-only regions yielded significantly lower discovery rates (Figure 4E). CCRs associated with the ON signature can be found at significantly larger distances from their target genes than ONQT-CCRs, although overall their transcriptional potency remains similar (Figures 4F and 4G). We also observed a moderate correlation between CCR-TSS distance and CCR potency (Figure S5D). A large fraction of the interrogated regions and discovered CCRs with the ONQT signature overlaps with either H3K27me3 or H3K4me1 peaks, while the ON regions overlap primarily with H3K4me1 peaks, although 25% of them do not overlap with either mark (Figure 4H). In addition, BAF155 and BAF250, which were enriched in CCRs associated with DE and PP genes (Figure 3C), show higher binding in CCRs compared to non-CCRs (Figure S5E). These results indicate that CCRs can be identified by the ON and ONQT signatures, while additional chromatin features may support their transcriptional potential.

Coupling single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) with CRISPRa enabled us to further elucidate the mechanisms that govern gene expression upon CCR activation. Approximately 40% of CCRs can activate two or more genes within the same TAD (Figure 4I). CCRs can induce stronger expression changes in TFs compared to peripheral genes (non-TFs) (Figure 4J), which could be due to the lower basal expression levels of lineage TFs compared to peripheral genes.29 All CCRs selected for individual validation significantly increased the expression of their associated genes (GATA3, ETS1, TAL1, RUNX1, NEUROG2, and OLIG1) when activated at the ESC stage (Figures 4K–4M and S5F–S5H). Finally, we assessed whether the discovered CCRs become enhancers in differentiated cells and found that 71% of them overlap with predicted enhancers across diverse cell types from the GeneHancer database30 (Figure S5I).

We assessed the association between the histone marks linked to primed/poised enhancers, the ONQT/ON signatures, and activatable CCRs using the complete set of CCRs (n = 131) and non-CCRs (n = 340) discovered from all CRISPRa screens in this study. We found that regions marked by H3K27me3 together with the ONQT signature, or those marked by H3K4me1 together with ONQT or ON signatures, have a significantly higher overlap with CCRs compared to regions marked by H3K4me1 or H3K27me3 without ON/ONQT (Figure S5J). In agreement with this, we found that adding H3K4me1/H3K27me3 does not significantly improve CCR identification for either ONQT or ON regions (Figures S5K–S5L). We analyzed the relationship between the signal intensities of H3K27me3/H3K4me1 (poised/primed enhancers) and the ONQT/ON factors at CCRs and non-CCRs (Figure S6). We observed no correlation between OCT4 or NANOG and the H3K27me3 signal, while for H3K4me1 we found a moderate correlation, which is stronger in non-CCRs compared to CCRs (Spearman correlation |r| > 0.40, p < 0.01). In addition, we observed strong positive correlations between OCT4 and NANOG as well as between QSER1 and TET1 (Spearman correlation |r| > 0.7, p < 0.01). These findings are in line with previous reports showing that OCT4 and NANOG share similar binding profiles in ESCs31 and that QSER1 and TET1 function together to prevent DNA hypermethylation.24 In summary, histone marks linked to primed/poised enhancers are less informative for predicting CCR functional potential than the ON/ONQT signatures.

Our large-scale CRISPRa scRNA-seq screens further demonstrate that CCRs are functional pre-enhancer regions present across the hESC genome, identifiable by the ON and ONQT signatures. CCRs cannot be reliably identified by H3K4me1 or H3K27me3 marks, indicating that they are not equivalent to primed/poised enhancers and represent a distinguishable subset of chromatin regulatory regions characterized by their functional activatability. CCRs tend to promote the expression of lineage-specific TFs, suggesting a role in enabling hESCs to rapidly activate lineage specification programs.

ONQT signature facilitates pioneer TF binding to future enhancer elements

To understand how CCRs assist with the transition from ESCs to differentiated cells, we hypothesized that the chromatin landscape at CCRs may facilitate the binding of pioneer TFs to activate these regions during early lineage specification. To test this, we induced the expression of FOXA2, a well-known pioneer TF that plays critical roles in the development of endodermal organs.32,33,34 We expressed FOXA2 by targeting its promoter with our CRISPRa system while keeping hESCs in E8 maintenance culture conditions to mimic the earliest stages of lineage specification when pioneer TFs are first expressed. We tested three time points to identify the window that maximized FOXA2 induction while minimizing differentiation, using GATA6 expression as a readout of DE differentiation. We identified 24 h after inducing CRISPRa as an optimal time window for analysis, with ∼60% of hESCs expressing FOXA2 without activating GATA6 (Figure 5A).

FOXA2 ChIP-seq revealed that FOXA2 mainly binds to regulatory genomic regions (introns and distal intergenic regions), which are enriched in FOX motifs, as expected (Figures S7A and S7B). In addition to FOX motifs, we found that FOXA2-bound regions were also enriched in motifs associated with pluripotent factors SOX2, NANOG, and OCT4 (Figure 5B). To examine whether the chromatin features enriched in the CCRs are associated with regions bound by FOXA2 in hESCs, we analyzed the binding levels of the ONQT factors in distal intergenic or intronic FOXA2-bound regions that lack H3K27ac (to exclude active enhancers). We found significantly higher levels of the ONQT factors in FOXA2-bound regions compared to control regions with FOXA motifs but not bound by FOXA2 (Figure 5C). Next, we analyzed the FOXA2 ChIP-seq signal in subsets of its bound regions, categorized on the basis of the presence of ChIP-seq peaks corresponding to any of the ONQT combinations (Figure S7C). We found that the concomitant presence of all four ONQT factors is associated with significantly higher FOXA2 binding levels compared to subsets of ONQT factors or the absence of ONQT factors (FOXA2 alone) (Figure 5D).

To examine whether the FOXA2 binding enrichment was specifically associated with the ONQT regions, we analyzed the FOXA2 ChIP-seq signal at distal/intronic regulatory regions that overlap with histone marks previously associated with “pre-enhancers” (H3K27me3/H3K4me1/H2A.Z) or combinations of four factors substituting the pluripotent TFs (SOX2 + KLF5 + QT) or the DNA demethylation agents (ON + BAF155/SMAD2-3). FOXA2 binding levels were significantly higher at the ONQT regions compared to any of the other factor combinations, supporting the idea that this signature facilitates the binding of pioneer TFs (Figure S7D). Interestingly, FOXA2 binding levels were significantly higher at ONQT regions than at ON regions, suggesting that QSER1 and TET1 play a key role in facilitating pioneer TF binding at CCRs. Considering the substantial association between CCRs identified in hESCs and enhancers in differentiated cells, we examined the overlap between FOXA2-bound regions in hESCs with regions expected to become active enhancers in DE cells. We found that FOXA2-bound regions with the concomitant presence of all ONQT factors in hESCs had a significantly higher percentage of overlap with FOXA2-bound active enhancers in DE cells (marked by H3K27ac) compared to any of the other binding combinations (Figures 5E and S7E). In summary, our data suggest that the ONQT chromatin signature pre-establishes CCRs in hESCs, primes them for pioneer TF binding in the early phases of lineage specification, and helps create a conducive chromatin environment for the subsequent activation of lineage-specific enhancers.

QSER1 and TET1 safeguard CCRs from DNA hypermethylation and facilitate their activation

QSER1 and TET1 have been shown to cooperate to keep DNA methylation valleys lowly methylated in hESCs.24 We tested whether these DNA demethylation factors play a similar role at CCRs and whether their activity influences CCR’s regulatory potency (Figure 6A). We analyzed the DNA methylation levels at all CCRs discovered in our screens and compared them across hESC knockout (KO) lines for TET1, QSER1, double TET1/QSER1 KO (DKO), and wild-type (WT) control cells.24 We found that QSER1/TET1 DKO caused a significant increase in the percentage of CpG methylation at the CCRs when compared to WT controls (Wilcoxon test, p = 0.0016, r = 0.24) (Figure 6B). To uncover the functional consequences of having increased DNA methylation levels at the CCRs in TET1/QSER1 DKO hESCs, we transduced the iSAM lentiviral vectors into the DKO hESC line and its matching WT control line. After confirming that both lines had comparable expression levels of the targetable activators used in the SAM system (Figure S8A), we transduced the cells with gRNAs targeting validated CCRs for GATA6, SOX17, EOMES, and PDX1 and measured protein expression by flow cytometry after 7 days. DKO cells showed significantly lower levels of gene activation for each CCR interrogated when compared to WT controls, indicating that the absence of the DNA demethylation factors impairs the regulatory potency of CCRs (Figures 6C and S8B).

To determine whether TET1/QSER1 also affect CCR activation during hESC differentiation in the absence of CRISPRa, we differentiated DKO and WT hESCs toward DE and collected samples at early and late differentiation time points (48 and 72 h). DKO cells showed a small but significant reduction in CXCR4+/SOX17+ DE cells at 48 h, which recovered by 72 h (Figures 6D and S8C). To examine the underlying chromatin changes, we performed assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) and H3K27ac ChIP-seq 48 h into DE differentiation. Chromatin accessibility at CCRs discovered for GATA6, EOMES, SOX17, and MIXL1 was significantly reduced in DKO cells compared to WT (Figures 6E and 6F). We extended this analysis to all ONQT-marked candidate CCRs within the TADs of DE-upregulated genes and found a similar decrease in ATAC signal in DKO cells (Figure 6F and STAR Methods). However, the H3K27ac levels remained comparable between DKO and WT in both discovered and ONQT-marked candidate CCRs (Figures 6E, 6F, S8D, and S8E). In summary, our findings demonstrate that QSER1 and TET1 support the transcriptional competence of CCRs by protecting them from DNA hypermethylation and that their absence likely impairs CCR activation by affecting the recruitment of additional chromatin factors during early ESC differentiation.

Harnessing CCR activation to promote hESC differentiation

Direct reprogramming of hESCs by inducing master lineage TFs is a promising strategy to generate therapeutic cell types.35,36 However, CRISPRa targeting of TF promoters alone has shown limited reprogramming efficiency.37 To address this limitation, we explored whether concomitantly activating a CCR and the promoter of a lineage TF could enhance gene upregulation and improve ESC differentiation. We selected CCRs associated with GATA6 and PDX1 and used CRISPRa to co-target each CCR with its corresponding promoter in hESCs. Combined CCR and promoter activation significantly increased target protein expression in five out of six tested combinations compared with promoter activation alone (Figures 7A, 7B, and S9A).

Encouraged by these results, we explored the utility of CCR activation in driving hESC differentiation (Figure 7C). MYOD1, a master TF responsible for the myogenic lineage specification, has been shown to promote the conversion of human fibroblasts and pluripotent stem cells into skeletal muscle cells.35,38,39 We identified a candidate CCR ∼20 kb upstream of MYOD1 characterized by the ONQT signature (Figure 7D) and discovered that several gRNAs targeting this region were able to activate MYOD1 expression (Figures 7E and S9B; Table S10). One of the gRNAs (g6.1) induced MYOD1 to levels comparable to those achieved by promoter targeting alone. Furthermore, co-targeting of the MYOD1 CCR and promoter led to significantly higher MYOD1 activation than targeting either element alone (Figures 7F and S9C).

Next, we investigated whether MYOD1 activation via CCR and/or promoter targeting could drive hESC differentiation into skeletal muscle cells. We devised a differentiation protocol based on a previous report where MYOD1 is expressed in pluripotent stem cells to generate functional muscle cells40 (Figure 7G). MYOD1 expression was activated for 4 days by targeting its CCR, promoter, or both, followed by 4 days of guided differentiation. Successful differentiation of hESCs into skeletal muscle cells was confirmed by expression of the hallmark proteins TITIN, MHC, and ACTININ, as well as the presence of sarcomeric fibers by immunofluorescence (Figure 7H). Remarkably, muscle differentiation efficiency was significantly higher through the combined activation of MYOD1 promoter and CCR, as evidenced by an increased number of cells expressing ACTININ and MHC, along with elevated expression levels of additional skeletal muscle genes and a higher myogenic fusion index (Figures 7I and S9D–S9F). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that the ONQT signature discovered through a series of CRISPRa screens effectively identifies CCRs linked to lineage-specific TFs. Importantly, targeted CCR activation robustly induces lineage TF expression and drives hESC differentiation into specialized cell types.

Discussion

We systematically interrogated hESCs with CRISPRa tools, directly linking chromatin activation to gene expression, and found that many enhancer regions controlling lineage genes in differentiated cells are pre-established at the ESC stage with unique chromatin signatures. These regions, termed CCRs, are characterized by an intrinsic transcriptional competence that can be harnessed to activate their associated lineage genes to levels comparable to those observed in differentiated cells. Our findings uncover a mechanism that connects ESC chromatin features to the developmental plasticity required for the rapid activation of lineage-specific genes, before complex enhancer networks are fully established.

Our work builds upon foundational approaches indicating that certain chromatin regions in undifferentiated ESCs may be primed for later activation as enhancers in differentiated cells. For example, low DNA methylation levels and the histone variant H2A.Z are believed to facilitate lineage TF binding at pre-enhancer regions, while H3K27me3 is proposed to prevent premature activation.4,5,6,8,10 These studies led to the identification of primed and poised enhancers, which are associated with the histone marks H3K4me1 and H3K27me3.6,10 However, directly interrogating the transcriptional potential of the chromatin required the development of targetable epigenetic activators, such as the CRISPRa system. Through large-scale CRISPRa functional interrogation, we discovered that ESCs have activatable chromatin regions (CCRs) ready to induce the expression of lineage genes before differentiation. We found that H2A.Z and H3K27me3 were not specifically enriched in CCRs among the regions we interrogated. However, we found that CCRs are enriched for the ONQT or ON signatures and partially overlap with H3K4me1 or H3K27me3 peaks. This suggests that within the broader category of primed and poised enhancers, there exists a subset of activatable pre-enhancers ready to be activated in hESCs to drive lineage gene expression, adding an important functional layer to the chromatin-based framework.

CCRs show enriched binding of the pluripotent TFs OCT4 and NANOG. This agrees with previous observations indicating that chromatin regions with high pluripotent TF occupancy in murine ESCs tend to acquire active-enhancer histone marks upon differentiation.41 We extend these observations by showing that OCT4 and NANOG binding in hESCs primes non-coding regions to activate the expression of genes within the same TAD prior to differentiation. We further established that DNA demethylation factors TET1 and its partner QSER1 support the transcriptional competence of CCRs by protecting them from excessive DNA methylation and facilitating their conversion into active enhancers during lineage differentiation. Previous studies suggest that pioneer TF binding is the initial event that triggers the formation of an enhancer element during the earliest stages of differentiation.11,32,42 We found that regions premarked by the ONQT factors are preferentially bound by the pioneer TF FOXA2 during early lineage specification. Therefore, the enriched binding of pluripotent TFs combined with DNA demethylation factors serves a “prepioneering” role by facilitating the binding of pioneer lineage TFs. In this scenario, CCRs could represent a set of future core enhancers activated during lineage specification. This is supported by the substantial overlap between CCRs and enhancers identified by CRISPRi or marked by H3K27ac in DE and PP cells. The overlap with annotated enhancers in the GeneHancer database30 further suggests that some CCRs could become active enhancers in untested lineages expressing the same genes (e.g., EOMES in immune cells and GATA6 in cardiac cells). In addition, CRISPRa interrogation in ESCs can reveal weak or shadow enhancers competent to activate transcription yet may not show a detectable phenotype when individually repressed by CRISPRi in differentiated cells. The concept of pre-establishing core enhancers of lineage genes at the ESC stage is further supported by the fact that CCRs affect only genes within the TADs.43 By screening TADs across 18 chromosomes containing over 2,000 potential CCR-gene pairs, our results provide a foundation for genome-wide screens to identify additional features of activatable pre-enhancers and ultimately build predictive models.

Beyond these mechanistic insights, our work demonstrates that targeting CCRs, either alone or in combination with a promoter region, can drive the expression of lineage genes and promote hESC differentiation. This opens multiple avenues for research, including improving CRISPRa efficacy, designing more efficient differentiation protocols, and dissecting the roles of enhancer activation during lineage specification. An important future direction is to investigate whether CCR-like regions with transcriptional potential also exist in differentiated cells and adult tissues. A previous study on the CD69 locus in T cells discovered CRISPRa-responsive elements only within regions already marked by H3K27ac when using the dCas9-VP64 system.44 We speculate that employing stronger CRISPRa systems may enable the discovery of CCR-like regions in multipotent or terminally differentiated cells. This is supported by a recent report demonstrating the induction of β-like globin genes in HEK293 cells by targeting strong CRISPRa systems to a locus control region that functions as an enhancer in erythroid cells but lacks features associated with enhancers in HEK293 cells.45 Finally, exploring the involvement of CCRs in pathological conditions where pluripotent TFs are aberrantly expressed, such as cancer,46 could unveil how CCRs are hijacked to activate abnormal gene-expression programs. Uncovering CCR function in these contexts may provide opportunities for therapeutic intervention and precision medicine. Together, CCRs provide a functional framework through which ESCs preconfigure lineage gene regulation and a practical toolkit for programmable control of cell fate with potential applications spanning regenerative medicine, disease modeling, and therapeutic intervention.

Limitations of the study

The scale of our screens was limited by the sparsity of single-cell transcriptomic data and the sequencing costs associated with profiling large numbers of cells. Moreover, the identification of chromatin factors associated with CCRs was constrained by the availability of ChIP-seq datasets in hESCs. Future studies will explore additional factors beyond the ONQT signature that may contribute to CCR features and function. Despite these limitations, our findings provide a foundation for developing predictive models of enhancer function and for designing more comprehensive screens to systematically identify activatable pre-enhancers across the genome.

Experimental model and study participant details

Ethics statement

All experiments involving human embryonic stem cells were approved by the Tri-SCI Embryonic Stem Cell Research Oversight Committee (ESCRO).

hESCs culture

Experiments in this study were mainly performed using the hESC line H1 (NIHhESC-10-0043). hESCs were cultured in Essential 8 (E8) medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A1517001) on vitronectin (VTN) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A14700) pre-coated plates at 37°C with 5% CO2. Medium was changed every day. Cells were passaged every 4–5 days at a ratio 1:15-20 using 0.5 mM EDTA (KD Medical, RGE-3130) or TrypLE Select (Gibco, 12563029) to dissociate cells. 5 μM Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK) inhibitor (RI) Y-27632 (Selleck Chemicals, S1049) was added into the E8 media when passaging or thawing hESCs. Cells were tested every 6 months by Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) Antibody & Bioresource Core Facility to verify they were mycoplasma-free. 4C, ChIP-seq and PRO-seq experiments were also performed using the hESC line HUES8 (NIHhESC-09–0021).

Method details

CRISPRa and CRISPRi line generation

An H1 inducible Cas9 -PDX1-GFP line previously generated in our lab16 which has a Cas9 cassette located at the AAVS1 locus was used to generate the CRISPRa and CRISPRi hESC lines. First, we aimed to substitute the endogenous Cas9 cassette by targeting the Cas9 protein to the Cas9 DNA flanking regions either with a dCas9-VP64 donor sequence from Addgene plasmid 61425 or a dCas9-KRAB donor. The dCas9-KRAB donor was generated through two rounds of QuikChange II Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent), using Puro-Cas9 donor (Addgene plasmid 58409) as template and the following primer pairs: RuvC1-F:R; RuvC2-F:R (Table S8). The final donor plasmid was generated through Gibson cloning (NEB) of a synthetic FseI-HA-KRAB-AscI DNA fragment (GenScript) flanked by 50 nucleotide homology arms into Puro-dCas9 donor cut FseI-AscI and the plasmid backbone was further replaced with one containing a low copy number origin of replication. PCR-amplified donor sequences were independently cloned by In-fusion reaction (Takara) into the backbone of the Addgene plasmid 58409 which contains the homology arms (HA) to target the AAVS1 locus. The resulting plasmids contained the HA sequences flanking the donor sequences, to be recombined with the endogenous AAVS1 sequence following a previously reported strategy.55 In brief, iCAS9 -PDX1-GFP cells were seeded in VTN-coated plates using regular E8 medium and treated with doxycycline (Dox) 2 μg/mL for 2 days to activate Cas9 expression and for each final cell line 3 × 105 cells were transfected with 1 μg of the corresponding donor vector and 80ng of the targeting gRNAs (CR2R-CR2L gRNAs for the idCas9-VP64 line and CR1R-CR2L gRNAs for the idCas9-KRAB) (Table S8) using the Lipofectamine Stem Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) following manufacturer’s guidelines. Transfected cells were treated an additional day with Dox, and treated 4 days with G418 (500 μg/mL) to select the clones with the RTTA cassette), recovered one day in basal E8 and treated with Blasticidin (10μg/mL) for another 4 days (to select the clones with the dCas9-VP64 or dCas9-KRAB cassette). Surviving colonies were dissected into single cells using TrypLE Select Enzyme (1×) and 1000 cells were seeded into individual VTN-coated 10cm plates. Individual colonies were manually picked, grown for one week and tested for genotyping using the sequencing primers listed in Table S8. Clones with the correct sequences were further validated by treatment with Doxycycline over 3 days and analysis of the expression of the dCas9-KRAB or dCas9-VP64 cassettes by qPCR, Western blot and FACS. After several rounds of verifications, we obtained one positive clone for the dCas9-KRAB line and two clones for the dCas9-VP64 line. We picked one clone for the dCas9-VP64 line together with the dCas9-KRAB and both clones were karyotyped to verify absence of genetic abnormalities by the MSKCC Molecular Cytogenetics Core. To generate the iSAM line, we transduced the idCas9-VP64-PDX1-GFP hESC line with the Addgene plasmid 61426, containing the EF1-MS2-P65-HSF1-hygro cassette. After 4 days of selection with hygromycin (200 μg/mL), 1000 cells were seeded in 10cm plates and individual clones were picked and grown separately. Three rounds of clonal selection were performed, and 12 final clones were treated with doxycycline for 3 days and tested for MS2 and dCas9 protein expression by flow cytometry. The clone with the highest expression of both was chosen as the iSAM hESC line.

RT-qPCR

RNA was extracted using Quick-RNA MiniPrep kits (ZYMO research; R1055) following manufacturer’s guidelines. cDNA was generated with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems; 4368817) using 500 ng of total RNA per reaction. RT-qPCR reactions were performed using the SYBR Green Master mix (Applied Biosystems; A25742) on a QuantStudio 6 flex Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). GAPDH and R18S primers were used as housekeeping controls. qPCR primers are listed in Table S8.

gRNA libraries

For the gRNA library targeting the candidate enhancers surrounding GATA6, EOMES, SOX17 and MIXL1 was derived from a previously published library.19 In brief, regions 2mb upstream/downstream around the 4 TFs that showed any chromatin accessibility at the ESC or DE stage were selected for interrogation, removing promoters and exons. 163 regions were selected to design fully tiled gRNAs using CHOPCHOP.56 In addition, 1100 gRNAs targeting safe harbor loci and 3 gRNAs targeting the SOX17 promoter were included as negative and positive controls respectively. gRNA oligos were synthesized on-Chip (Agilent). For the PDX1 CRISPRa and CRISPRi screens, we designed a tiled library using the GuideScan package57 covering 50kb upstream from TSS (2998 gRNAs), PDX1 intronic regions (698 gRNAs) and 50kb downstream of 5′ end of the 2nd exon (3442 gRNAs), as positive controls we included gRNAs targeting exonic regions (99 gRNAs), and as negative controls non-targeting to human genome (200 gRNAs) and safe-harbours for each chromosome (200 gRNAs). PDX1 synthesized oligos were amplified and restriction cloned into lentiGuide-puro (Addgene; 52963) and lenti MS2 grna-Puro (Addgene; 73795), while the first library was only cloned into the lenti MS2 grna-Puro backbone by the MSKCC Gene Editing & Screening Core Facility. Cloned plasmid libraries were PCR amplified to incorporate adapters for NGS. Samples were purified and sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. FASTQ files were clipped by position and reads were mapped back to the reference library file to show relative abundance of reads per gRNA. Reads within each sample were normalized to total number of mapped reads and library size. The Overall Representation of the libraries was charted over a one-log fold change to evaluate if any gRNA was over- or under-represented in the final library.

Lentiviral gRNA libraries

gRNA libraries lentiviral particles generation was performed as previously described.48 In brief, a total of 9.45 μg of each library plasmids combined with 6.75 μg lentiviral packaging vector psPAX2 and 1.36 μg vesicular stomatitis virus G (VSV-G) envelope expressing plasmid pMD2.G (Addgene plasmids 12260 and 12259) were transfected with the JetPRIME reagent (Polyplus; 101000046 into 1 × 106 293T cells in a 10cm plate to produce the lentiviral particles. Fresh medium was changed 24h after transfection and viral supernatant was collected, spun at 1000 rpm for 5 min, filtered, and stored at −80°C, 72h after transfection.

CRISPRa screen for PDX1 CCRs

The PDX1 lentiviral library with the MS2-gRNA backbone was transduced into 12 million hESC iSAM cells distributed in 10 × 10 cm plates, to reach a MOI = 0.3. Cells were reverse transduced in the presence of RI (10 μM) and PS (6 μg/mL). Both transduced cell lines were kept under puromycin selection (1 μg/mL) for 4 days, and after one day of recovery with regular E8 media, were grown with Dox during four additional days. The presence of cells expressing PDX1 (reported by GFP) was verified by flow cytometry, after being stained with LIVE/DEAD reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L34955) for 15 min at RT. All the iSAM-hESCs expressing PDX1 (GFP+) cells were FACS-sorted using FACSaria sorters (BD Biosciences) by the MSKCC cytometry facility, a corresponding number of PDX1-GFP (−) cells were also sorted, pelleted down and kept at −80 for downstream gRNA sequencing. The screen was performed twice using independent biological replicates.

CRISPRi screen for PDX1 enhancers

The PDX1 lentiviral library with the regular lenti-gRNA backbone was reverse transduced into 12 million hESC (i)dCas9-KRAB hESCs distributed in 10 × 10 cm plates following the previous conditions (MOI = 0.3, PS = 6 μg/mL, RI = 10 μM). Cells were kept under puromycin selection for 4 days, and after one day of recovery with regular E8 media. 12 million selected cells were seeded into 6well plates (4 × 105 per well) in VTN-coated plates, grown for 48 h in E8 medium with Dox. Calculating the moving average

The moving average of log fold change values was calculated using the sliding window approach.18 After ordering the gRNAs by their genomic coordinates, the average LFC was computed over a window of N gRNAs, sliding by one at a time. Given replicates Ι and ΙΙ, let LFC¯iI and LFC¯iII denote the average LFC of the ith window in each replicate. With N ranging between 1 and 50, the window size resulting in the highest Pearson correlation coefficient between LFC¯Ι and LFC¯ΙΙ is selected. For visualization purposes, the LFC of a screen across the genome is represented by the mean between replicates as (LFC¯iI+LFC¯iII)/2.

Quantifying significance and reproducibility

The significance of window i in each replicate was quantified with the Z-score of its LFC as Zi=(LFC¯i−μ)/σ where the mean and standard deviation, respectively μ and σ, are calculated from LFC of individual gRNAs in that replicate. Reproducibility between the replicates was measured by the reverse of Irreproducible Discovery Rate (IDR).59 Using R function idr::est.IDR, the rate for window i can be estimated by IDRi between |ZiΙ| and |ZiΙΙ|, which represent its significance in replicates Ι and ΙΙ.

Identification of candidate regulatory regions for PDX1

The following analysis was performed on CRISPRa and CRISPRi screens, respectively, to discover the candidate PDX1 regulatory regions. First, any gRNA with < min_count (=50 for CRISPRa and 20 for CRISPRi) reads in either replicate was excluded. For each replicate, log fold change values between GFP+ and GFP− were calculated as described in “Read count normalization and computing fold changes”. To normalize read counts in this analysis, the size factors were calculated as the median of size factors for non-targeting gRNA controls (i.e., median{sg}g∈NT). To mitigate the noise of log fold change values, the moving average was computed for a sliding window of N (=30 for CRISPRa and 10 for CRISPRi) gRNAs (see ‘Calculating the moving average’). Then, the significance and reproducibility of each window was quantified as described in ‘Quantifying significance and reproducibility’. To calculate the Z-scores in this analysis, the mean and standard deviation were derived from log fold change values of non-targeting gRNA controls (i.e., LFCg for g∈NT). Significant windows were selected based on their length (i.e., span from the starting coordinate of the window’s first gRNA to the ending coordinate of its last), significance in each replicate, and reproducibility between the replicates: window i was discarded if lengthi>max_length or |ZiΙ| <min_score or |ZiΙΙ| <min_score or IDRi>max_IDR (max_length = 250bp, min_score = 0.25, max_IDR = 0.05 for CRISPRa and max_length = 200bp, min_score = 3, max_IDR = 0.001 for CRISPRi). Finally, we created the candidate regions by merging the genomic regions corresponding to the selected windows in an iterative process, as follows: out of all consecutive regions whose combination would span <max_span (=1000bp for CRISPRa and 2000bp for CRISPRi), the pair with the minimum distance is combined; if no such pair exists, stop and report the final set of genomic regions. The coordinates of the identified regions are summarized in Table S1. For comparative analyses purposes, the PDX1 non-CCRs were randomly chosen from the regions that did not meet the criteria for CRISPRa (i.e., window i for which |ZiΙ| <0.25 or |ZiΙΙ| <0.25 or IDRi > 0.05) and had an average length equal to the PDX1 CCRs, see Table S3. In Figure S1C and Table S1 we provide the LFC and the coordinates of the regions discovered (+50bp) in the CRISPRa and CRISPRi screens that were further validated.

CRISPRa screen analysis to identify CCRs associated with DE genes

To identify the CCRs associated with GATA6, EOMES, SOX17 and MIXL1, the gRNAs sequencing reads for the positive (i.e., cells expressing any of the 4 genes after CRISPRa interrogation) and the negative FACS sorted fraction were obtained by NGS, and the following analysis was performed for each gene respectively. First, off-target gRNAs were filtered out using CRISPOR,60 as previously reported.19 For each replicate, after adding pseudocount = 1000 to the read counts, log fold change values between the positive and the negative sorted fractions were calculated as described below (“Read count normalization and computing fold changes”). For this analysis, the log fold change values of individual gRNAs were used instead of the moving average; in other words, window i is the ith gRNA and the notation in ‘Calculating the moving average’ holds for N = 1. Then, the significance and reproducibility of each gRNA was quantified as described in ‘Quantifying significance and reproducibility’. Significant gRNAs were selected based on their significance in each replicate and reproducibility between the replicates: gRNA i is discarded if ZiΙ <1 or ZiΙΙ <1 or IDRi > 0.05. We defined a genomic region for each group of nearby significant gRNAs, extended until there were none closer than 100bp. Finally, we identified CCRs by extending the resulting regions by 150bp around the center and merging the overlapping regions. We identified non-CCRs by subtracting the CCRs from the differential ATAC peaks between the ESC and DE stages that were targeted by the screen: ATAC peaks and parts of the ATAC peaks that did not overlap CCRs and were longer than 150bp were considered non-CCRs. gRNA enrichment for each interrogated region and the final list of CCRs and non-CCRs are provided in Tables S2 and S3. 4C

For each primary digestion reaction, 10 million hESCs were transferred into 15 mL Falcon tubes and fixed with 10 mL of 1% formaldehyde (Thermo Scientific, 28908) in PBS without Ca+/Mg+ for 10 min at room temperature (RT) (on a rotation device). After quenching with freshly prepared ice-cold 1M glycine, tubes were centrifuged for 5 min 500g at 4°C. Cells were kept on ice, washed with PBS, centrifuged for 5 min 500g at 4°C, and pellets were frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C. Next, cells were vigorously resuspended in 1 mL of ice-cold lysis buffer (10 mM tris (pH 8), 10 mM NaCl, 0.2% NP-40, and 1 tablet of complete protease inhibitor (Roche), transferred to 9 mL of prechilled lysis buffer, and incubated for 15 min on ice. Following centrifugation at 500g for 5 min at 4°C, the pellet was resuspended in 500 μL 1.2× CutSmart buffer containing 0.5% SDS and incubated for 1 h at 37°C while shaking at 750 rpm. After, SDS quenching was performed by adding 75 μL 20% Triton X-100 and incubating for 1 h at 37°C while shaking at 750 rpm. 10 μL of the sample were taken as an undigested control. The remaining samples were treated with DpnII enzyme (NEB, R0543M) (30μl) in CutSmart buffer (NEB, B7204S) (25μl) and samples were incubated ON at 37°C under agitation (750rpm). After first digestion, 5μL of the sample were taken as a digested control and the efficiency of the chromatin digestion was verified by electrophoresis, detecting a smear between 0.2 and 2 kb in a 1.5% agarose gel. DpnII was deactivated by (65°C, 20 min, 750 rpm), and ligation of DNA ends between the cross-linked DNA fragments was performed in T4 ligation buffer (NEB, B0202), ATP (NEB, P0756S), 10% Triton X-100, 20mg/mL BSA and T4 DNA Ligase (NEB, M0202) ON at 16°C (gently rocking) followed by 30min at RT. Then, ligated samples were treated with proteinase K and reverse crosslinked overnight at 65C. Following RNase (Sigma-Aldrich, 10109142001) treatment, phenol/chloroform extraction and DNA precipitation, the pellets were dissolved in 10mM Tris pH 8. Then the efficiency of the DNA extraction was verified on a 1.5% agarose gel. For the second digestion, 5 μL of NlaIII enzyme diluted in CutSmart buffer were added to the DpnII-ligated 3C template and samples were incubated ON (37°C, 750rpm). NlaIII was inactivated at 65°C for 20 min, and a second ligation was performed by adding T4 ligation buffer, 10mM ATP, T4 DNA Ligase, and ddH2O, incubating ON at 16°C. Ligation product was extracted by phenol/chloroform. DNA concentration of each digested sample was calculated using the Qubit brDNA HS assay kit (Invitrogen). For library preparation, viewpoint primers were designed around promoters of EOMES, GATA6, SOX17 and PDX1, depending on the availability of the nearest restriction enzyme sites (see Table S8). Library preparation was then performed using 200 ng of 4C-template DNA with the Expand Long Range PCR kit (Millipore, 4829042001) under specific PCR conditions (94°C for 2 min, 16 cycles: 94°C for 10 s; 59°C for 1 min; 68°C for 3 min, final extension: 68°C for 5 min). Amplified DNA was pooled, and primers were removed using AMPure beads (Beckman Coulter, A63881). A second round of PCR was performed using the amplified DNA as a template and overlapping primers to add the P5/P7 sequencing primers and indexes (94°C for 2 min, 20 cycles: 94°C for 10 s; 60°C for 1 min; 68°C for 3 min and 68°C for 5 min). The samples quantity and purity were determined using a Qubit fluorometer. 4C PCR library efficiencies were confirmed by Agilent Bioanalyzer and libraries were sequenced on a HiSeq4000 in PE150 mode by the MSKCC Genomics Core Facility. Three independent assays per viewpoint were performed.

4C processing

4C processing was first carried out with pipe4C v1.0.69 Default settings were used for all analysis. After all reads were processed, the data was first re-binned into fixed bins of 2500 bp, and then a sliding window of 5 was used to aggregate counts. These smoothed counts were then used for subsequent analysis. A local model analogous to HiC-DC+ was used to identify local loop enrichments at an FDR of 0.1.22 In brief, HiC-DC+ assumes a Negative Binomial GLM parametrized with a fixed design spline for the distance term. To deal with the intrinsic variation of 4C data, we adopted a Generalized Additive Model for Location Shape and Scale70 that uses smooth spline terms for the distance dependence, and simultaneously models the dispersion parameter of the observations. These models were fit to 4C capture experiment to model local dependencies.

4C scoring

To score events observed within each replicate, we used a z-scoring procedure based on quantile residuals derived from the local model analogous to HiC-DC+. After scoring, these quantile residuals should follow an approximately normal distribution, and so, z¯=1n∑z∼N(0,1)) as well. These were then used to identify differences between the CCRs and non-CCRs. A kernel density estimator from the CRAN package sm 2.2–5.7.1 was used to estimate the KDE and its associated confidence band of equality for the Z-scores of the CCRs, non-CCRs and background regions.71

ChIP-seq assays

ChIP-seq was performed as previously described.48 Briefly, for each sample, around 30 million cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde and quenched with 0.125M glycine. Fixed cells were collected in cold PBS buffer and washed with the same. Then the cells were snap frozen and saved at −80°C. On the day of immunoprecipitation, cell pellet was thawed on ice, resuspended in 700 μL SDS buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA, 50 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8), and incubated for 10min on ice. Sonication was performed on a Branson Sonifier 150 set at 30% amplitude for 5min30s (10s on/off pulsing). Supernatant was pre-cleared with Dynabeads and then incubated overnight with antibodies (see Table S8) at 4°C.). On the next day, the ChIP samples were incubated with Dynabeads for 6 h at 4°C. Then the beads were pelleted and washed twice with low salt (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl), high salt (0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2 mM EDTA, 20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 500 mM NaCl), and TE buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8, 1 mM EDTA) respectively. The DNA was eluted from the beads by incubating in elution buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3) at 65°C for 15min and decrosslinked with 5M NaCl at 65°C overnight. A total of 10 μL 0.5 M EDTA, 20 μL 1 M Tris–HCl, pH 6.5, and 1 μL Proteinase K (20 mg/mL) were added to the de-crosslinked product and incubated for 1 h at 45°C. DNA was isolated by using QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen, 28104; QIAGEN). Then the sequencing library was generated by the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs; E7103S) and NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (New England Biolabs Index Primers Set 1; NEB, E7335S). Samples were pooled and submitted to MSKCC Integrated Genomics Operation core for quality control and sequencing on Illumina HiSeq 4000 or NovaSeq X platforms. Two independent ChIP-seq assays were performed for each condition.

ChIP-seq and ATAC-seq analyses

Paired-end reads were mapped to hg19 reference genome with bowtie264 using default parameters. Peaks were called using macs2 (Model-based Analysis of ChIP-Seq (MACS),54 with the options to filter duplicates using –keepdup auto with the permissive p-value threshold of 0.01. Peaks were further filtered down using irreproducible discovery ratio (IDR)59 filter of 0.01. Peaks were annotated by their nearest genes using R function ChIPseekerannotatePeak,72 with default dataset. The additional ChIP-seq data included to characterize the chromatin-associated factors is listed in Table S6.19,24,34,48,49,50,51 We downloaded datasets from the ENCODE portal47 (https://www.encodeproject.org/) with the following identifiers: ENCSR000ATQ, ENCSR000APX, ENCSR000AVB.

PRO-seq data generation and analysis

We followed a previous published protocol73 for PRO-seq. Briefly, around 5 million of hESCs were swelled in swelling buffer (10 mM Tris pH 7.5, 2 mM MgCl2, 3 mM CaCl2, 1× Proteinase Inhibitor Cocktail, 10 U/mL Superase-In) for 10min, then nuclei were extracted in swelling buffer added with 10% glycerol and 0.5% NP-40. The nuclei were resuspended in freezing buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.3, 40% glycerol, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1xPIC, 10U/100μL Superase-IN). Nuclear run-on (NRO) reaction takes place by mixing the nuclei in freezing buffer with equal volume of NRO buffer (10mM Tris pH 8.0, 5mM MgCl2, 300mM KCl, 1mM DTT, 100u/mL Superase.In, 1% sarkosyl, 250 μM ATP and GRP, 50 μM biotin-11-UTP and 50 μM biotin-11-CTP) at 37°C for 5min. The total RNAs from the nuclei were immediately extracted by TRIzol LS, and the NRO RNAs from this mixture were purified by streptavidin C1beads twice. The purified NRO RNAs were processed to sequencing library using two rounds of adaptor ligation following eCLIP-seq method with 10nt UMI.74 The libraries were sequenced using NextSeq 550 by the 40nt Pair-end model. We used Trim Galore (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore) to trim the adapters, and aligned all the clean reads to human reference genome hg19 using STAR 2.7.1.75 Then, we applied UMI-tools76 to remove the duplicated reads. The unique aligned reads were further converted to bigwig format normalized by FPKM using BamCoverage function in deepTools.77

HOMER motif enrichment analysis

Motif enrichment on a set of genomic regions was performed by HOMER v4.11.1 using the command findMotifsGenome.23 To compare one set of regions against another, we used the latter as background, instead of the random background regions HOMER uses by default.

Comparative analysis of chromatin features signal

The ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, or Pro-seq signals in a genomic region are estimated by the average score of all overlapping bins in the bigwig file, weighted by the width of their overlap with the given region. If there are no overlaps, we assume the score is zero. We compared the enrichment of signal in two groups of genomic regions by the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test,78 as signal intensities are often non-normally distributed. Besides the p-value, we include the effect size value (r) to quantify the magnitude of the difference between groups, considering r > 0.2 as a small to moderate effect.79

Meta-peak analysis

The score matrix of a bigwig file in a set of genomic regions was calculated by deepTools v3.5.1,80 using the command computeMatrix reference-point --referencePoint = center --binSize = 1 --averageTypeBins = mean. To summarize the enrichment of signal in the regions, we calculated the mean score per base pair (i.e., average of each column in the score matrix).

CRISPRa screen coupled with 10× Genomics

Region identification and gRNA library design

We tested whether the regions with enriched binding of the pluripotent TFs OCT4 and NANOG (ON) alone or together with the enriched binding of QSER1 and TET1 (ONQT) could predict the presence of CCRs associated with lineage genes at a large genome scale. We first identify lineage TFs that are lowly or not expressed in hESC according to our previous RNA-seq data19 and selected 70 TFs distributed across 18 chromosomes. We verified these TFs become active in fetal and adult tissues from the three germ layers. The data used to identify the gene expression levels of the lineage TFs in adult tissues were obtained from the GTEx Portal V10 release (https://www.gtexportal.org/). We determined the TADs called in hESCs where those TF loci are located and looked for regions containing the ON or ONQT signatures by overlapping called peaks from previous ChIP-seq experiments24,48 using the findOverlaps functions from the gRanges package in R. In addition, we sought for control regions associated with poised enhancers that contain H3K27me3 peaks24 but did not contain any of the ONQT factors (Table S9). The final selected regions were annotated with the annotatePeak function from the ChIPseeker package in R, and only the regions defined as interdistal or intronic with a total width less than 1kb were selected (candidate enhancer regions). A total of 80 ON, 78 ONQT and 76 H3K27me3 regions were selected for the library. We selected the gRNAs targeting our candidate regions through guidescan2 (https://guidescan.com/) and when possible, selected the top4 gRNAs per region. In addition, as positive controls to validate the functionality of our CRISPRa tool in the 10× genomics assays, we identified the promoter regions of 35 lineage TFs and designed 2 gRNAs per promoter based on their transcriptional starting site defined by CAGE-seq FANTOM5 database81 (https://fantom.gsc.riken.jp/5/). Finally, a second small gRNA library was designed with 15 gRNAs targeting safe-harbor regions and non-targeting sequences previously validated.19,82 All gRNA sequences and region coordinates are included in Table S7.

gRNA library cloning and transduction

sgRNA were synthesized on-Chip (Agilent) and cloned into the MS2-CROP backbone by the MSKCC Gene Editing & Screening Core Facility (CROP-sgRNA-MS2 was a gift from Wolf Reik - Addgene #153457). In brief, this backbone contains an U6 promoter that drives the expression of a single MS2-gRNA and EF-1α promoter that drives the transcription of a cassette that encloses mCherry-2a-Puromycin selection markers and the gRNA sequence at its 3′ end. This dual system allows the identification of the gRNA sequences in the transcriptome at a single-cell level. In parallel, oligos from the negative control gRNA library were synthesized (EtOn Bio) and cloned into the MS2-CROP backbone, to generate a small “control gRNA library” to facilitate the detection of cells transduced with control gRNAs for the downstream 10× experiments. gRNAs lentiviral libraries were prepared as described in “Lentiviral gRNA libraries” and titered at different concentrations that were subsequently measured, based on viral RNA levels detected in transduced cells. Libraries at MOI ∼1.0 (gRNAs/cell) and MOI ∼3 (gRNAs/cell) were selected to represent single and multiplexed conditions. hESC iSAM cells were independently transduced at high and low MOIs with the libraries of the candidate CCRs + promoters and the “control gRNA libraries” (4 independent transductions). After selection 4 days with puromycin, cells were treated with doxycycline for 3 additional days.

Single-cell 10× Genomics

After doxycycline-induced activation of the iSAM CRISPRa tool for 3 days, cells were washed with PBS and treated with TrypLE Select for 2 min, washed 2 times with PBS containing 0.04%BSA and filtered through CellTrics (20μm, Sysmex) to be sorted in a Flow Cytometer, using the mCherry live marker that distinguishes the transduced cell population expressing the cassette that contains the gRNA sequences (see Addgene #153457). mCherry+ hESCs transduced with the 4 different library conditions were FACS-sorted and 2 cellular mixes were prepared as it follows: 1.) 500000 cells at 97:3 ratio = low MOI candidate CCR-library transduced hESCs: low MOI negative control transduced hESCs. 2.) 100000 cells at 97:3 ratio = high MOI candidate CCR-library transduced hESCs: high MOI negative control transduced hESCs. Cellular suspensions were loaded on a Chromium Controller targeting 30000 cells per reaction. Seven reactions for the low MOI condition and one reaction for the high MOI condition were loaded. Single-cell 3′ transcriptome libraries were generated following the manufacturer’s instructions (10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1-Dual Index User Guide). gRNA libraries were generated by amplifying the fragment that contains the gRNA sequence from the MS2-CROP construct (see oligos in Table S8), and a second PCR to add index adaptors from the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3.1-Dual Index. Libraries were then quantified on the Agilent Bioanalyzer with a high-sensitivity chip (Agilent), and Kapa DNA quantification kit for Illumina platforms (Roche). The libraries were sequenced on a NovaX platform following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The total number of reads was adjusted to aim for ∼20000 average reads per cell. (∼7 × 109 total reads for the seven low MOI transcriptome reactions and 4.5 × 108 total reads for the high MOI transcriptome reaction).

scRNA-seq analysis

SCEPTRE analysis

FASTQ files were pre-processed via 10× Genomics CellRanger −8.0.0. The files were aligned to GRCh38 (refdata-gex-GRCh38-2024-A) and counted by ‘cellranger count’. For the single array samples, we combined the alignment results files from the seven 10× reactions into one, by employing ‘cellranger aggr --normalize = none’ to create an unified count matrix. We used the filtered CellRanger output matrices to create an SCEPTRE object as previously described.28 First, we imported the CellRanger data and defined the type of MOI (low or high for each independent experiment) and the gRNA target data frame, which included all the genes that are present in the same TAD of each interrogated candidate CCR region. To assign gRNAs we used the maximum method for the single array screen (low MOI) and the mixture method for the multiplexed array (high MOI), employing the default values. For the maximum method the umi_fraction_threshold = 0.8 and min_grna_n_umis_threshold = 5 identified 293.74 cells per gRNA. For the mixture method the n_em_rep = 5, n_nonzero_cells_cutoff = 10, backup_threshold = 5, identified 61.33 cells per gRNA.

The SCEPTRE analysis was configured with a right-tailed test and a negative control cell group. The model included covariates for gene and gRNA library complexity and mitochondrial percentage.(formula_object = log(response_n_nonzero) + log(response_n_umis) + log(grna_n_nonzero +1) + log(grna_n_umis +1) + response_p_mito) We used a skew-normal resampling approximation with a permutation-based mechanism. The analysis was run at scale using the NextFlow pipeline (https://timothy-barry.github.io/sceptre-book/at-scale.html) with standard QC parameters: response_n_nonzero_range = c(0.01, 0.99), response_n_umis_range = c(0.01, 0.99), and p_mito_threshold = 20%. A minimum of 7 nonzero cells was required for both treatment and control groups. Significant discovery pairs were defined as candidate CCR-gene pairs with a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p-value <0.1 (Table S7).

Additional analysis on 10× Genomics results

To identify the overlap between the interrogated regions, discovered CCRs and the histone marks reported as markers of poised and primed enhancers we employed the findOverlaps function from the gRanges package wih H3K27me3 and H3K4me1 previously called peaks24,34 (Table S9). We performed the expression analysis based on TF or peripheral gene labeling following the human transcription factor database system83 (https://humantfs.ccbr.utoronto.ca/). For the final analyses to identify type and number of genes activated by a single CCR we integrated the results between the single and multiplexed assays (An average LFC was calculated for the discovery pairs found in both type of screens). For analysis including all CCRs and non-CCRs discovered across the CRISPRa screens, Fisher’s exact test was employed to assess the statistical significance of the differences in overlap percentage between regions labeled as CCRs/non-CCRs, regions called as H3K4me1/H3K27me3 peaks and regions with called peaks for the ON or ONQT factors (Figures S5J–S5L).

FOXA2 expression in hESCs

2 × 105 iSAM hESCs were transduced with two lentiviral gRNAS targeting the promoter region of the endogenous FOXA2 locus (see Table S8), aiming a MOI = 3, following the lentiviral infection protocol described above. After one day of recovery cells were cultured in E8 medium and treated with puromycin for three days. Dox was added to E8 media for three days and protein levels of GATA6 and FOXA2 were measured every 24 h by flow cytometry analysis. To perform FOXA2 ChiP-seq experiments, iSAM hESCs transduced with the FOXA2 targeting lentiviral gRNAs were expanded for multiple passages in regular E8. Next, 2 × 107 lenti-gRNA transduced cells were seeded in 15cm VTN-coated plates in E8 with Doxycycline and 24 h later were collected to be crosslinked (see ChIP-seq assays).

FOXA2 binding analyses based on motif enrichment

Genome-wide motif positions for human genome hg19 were downloaded from HOMER23 (http://homer.ucsd.edu/homer/data/motifs/). Potential binding sites for FOXA1 and FOXA2 motifs in standard chromosomes (chr1, …, chr22, chrX, chrY) which were ≤20bp and annotated as “Intron” or “Distal Intergenic”, by R function ChIPseeker::annotatePeak,72 were selected and resized to 2Kbp around the center. Then, we filtered out regions that extended outside of chromosomes, overlapped with hg19 black-listed regions compiled by ArchR,84 or overlapped with FOXA2-bound regions (i.e., FOXA2 peaks detected upon activation at the ESC stage) which we also resized to 2Kbp around the center. From the top 5% of the remaining motif sites with the highest log odds scores, we created a non-overlapping set of regions by keeping the higher score out of each overlapping pair.

Signal comparative analysis in FOXA2 overlapping regions

Bed files for the chromatin regions were computed and subsets with any FOXA2 overlap were estimated using the package rtracklayer in R.85 ChIP-seq signal in the genomic regions of interest were estimated as the average score of all overlapping bins in the bigwig file.

DNA EPIC-methylation analysis

To quantify DNA methylation levels across defined genomic regions, BED files containing the genomic coordinates of all CCRs and all non-CCRs were used. Each region was extended by 500 bp upstream and downstream to capture adjacent CpG sites. Methylation data were obtained from EPIC-Methyl bigWig files previously generated.24 If there were no overlaps between the regions defined in the BED files and the bigWig bins, the percentage of methylation was assigned as unknown. The resulting average methylation percentages per region were obtained and compared for the WT, TET1 KO, QSER1 KO and TET1/QSER1 double KO conditions.

CRISPRa CCR targeting in QSER1/TET1-KO line

2 × 105 TET1/QSER1-DKO hESCs (DKO) and their corresponding WT matching line were transduced with the lentiviral supernatant generated from the Lenti-dCas9-VP64-Blast plasmid (addgene # 61425), after one day of recovery cells were selected under Blasticidin (10μg/mL) for 4 days. Next, cells were cultured seven days in E8 medium, and 1000 cells were seeded into VTN-coated 10cm plates. After 3 days of culture, 12 individual colonies were picked, grown for 5 days and the (d)Cas9 expression levels were checked by flow cytometry. DKO and WT colonies with comparable (d)Cas9 levels of expression were selected to be transduced with the lentiviral supernatant generated from a modified version of the Lenti-MS2-p65-HSF1-Hygro plasmid (addgene # 61426), where the Hygromycin cassette was replaced by the Zeocin selection cassette using In-Fusion cloning (Takara). After one day of recovery, cells were treated with Zeocin (200 μg/mL) for 4 days. Next, cells were cultured seven days in E8 medium, 1000 cells were seeded into VTN-coated 10cm plates and after 3 days of culture, 5 individual colonies were picked, grown for 10 days and the MS2 and p65 expression levels were checked by qPCR. WT and DKO colonies with comparable expression levels were selected to perform CCR interrogation experiments. The resulting WT and DKO SAM hESCs lines were transduced with Lenti-Puro gRNAs targeting various validated CCRs from the studied genes, following the protocol described above. After a recovery day and 3 days of treatment with Puromycin (1 μg/mL), cells were kept in E8 media for two additional days (in the same well) and fixed to measure the protein expression levels by flow cytometry analysis. n ≥ 3 independent biological replicates for each CCR validation.

ATAC-seq

Assay for Transposase-Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-Seq) was performed as previously described.86 200000 fresh cells were washed in cold PBS and lysed. The transposition reaction containing TDE1 Tagment DNA Enzyme (Illumina; 20034198) was incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The DNA was purified with the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN; 28004) and amplified for 5 cycles using NEBNext High-Fidelity 2× PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs; M0541L). After evaluation by real-time PCR, 3-14 additional PCR cycles were done. The final product was cleaned by AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter; A63882) at a 1× ratio, and size selection was performed at a 0.5× ratio. Libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq X platform in a PE100 run, using a 10B flow cell (200 Cycles) (Illumina). Two independent ATAC-seq assays were performed for each condition.

H3K27ac and ATAC-seq analyses on CCRs in QSER1/TET1 DKO vs. WT

H3K27ac-seq and ATAC-seq was performed in DKO and WT cells at the ESC and DE stages (48 h). A binding/signal analysis was performed with the genomic coordinates of the CCRs we found associated with the DE genes, EOMES, GATA6, MIXL1 and SOX17. To extend the number of potential CCRs evaluated, we fetched the Top 200 differentially upregulated genes between ESC and DE from previously published data19 and defined the TADs containing each of these genes. Then, we identified all the distal intergenic and intronic chromatin regions that concomitantly have enriched binding of OCT4-NANOG-QSER1 and TET1. These ONQT regions were assigned as candidate CCRs and the H3K27ac and ATAC average signals were obtained for the DKO and WT samples at the ESC and DE stages, as described in “Comparative analysis of chromatin features signal”.

MYOD1 CCR small-scale CRISPRa screen

We employed ChIP-seq bigwig files of the OCT4, NANOG, QSER1 and TET1 factors previously generated in hESCs24,48 (Table S9) to identify candidate CCRs with the ON or ONQT signature located at the TAD containing MYOD1. A region ∼20kb upstream with the ONQT signature was selected to test its capacity to induce MYOD1 expression when interrogated with our CRISPRa tool. We designed a tiled gRNA library containing 20 gRNAs targeting ∼1.1kb which encompasses the ONQT overlapping region (Table S8). We cloned individually each gRNA into an MS2-lenti gRNA backbone as described above and the resulting plasmids were combined to generate a small-scale lentiviral library. iSAM hESCs were transduced with the MYOD1 candidate CCR gRNA library, and after selection with puromycin, cells were treated with doxycycline for 3 days. After, cells were harvested, fixed and immuno-stained to identify MYOD1 expressing cells (Abcam # ab133627). After antibody incubation, cells were sorted by flow cytometry to isolate the MYOD1+ and MYOD1-fractions and their DNA was extracted as described in “gRNA sequencing”. Shallow CRISPR-sequencing was performed on a NovaX platform and enriched gRNAs in the MYOD1+ fraction were identified through the MAGECK pipeline17 (Table S10). The top individual gRNAs that were identified enriched in the MYOD1+ fraction were individually tested to validate the MAGECK results, following the protocol described in “Validation of individual CCRs by flow cytometry”.

Skeletal muscle differentiation through MYOD1 activation

Muscle differentiation from hESCs to muscle cells was performed inspired by a previously published protocol.40 hESCs were transduced with a single gRNA targeting either the newly discovered MYOD1 CCR, its promoter region or both the promoter + CCR. For double transductions, we employed two individual lenti-gRNA MS2 backbones with puromycin and zeocin (200 μg/mL) resistance cassettes. After infection and selection, 5 × 105 hESCs were cultured in a single well from a vitronectin-coated 24wp plate, 48 h later cells were transfected with siRNA targeting OCT4 (20pmol, s10873 ThermoFisher) and doxycycline was added to the E8 media. The day after, cells were rinsed with PBS and the media was replaced to a muscle differentiation media (Alpha-Mem, Knock-out Serum Replacement 5%, Na Piruvate 1mM, NEAA 0.1mM, Glutamine 2mM, Beta-Mercaptoethanol 0.1 mM, Pen/Strep 100×) with doxycycline for 3 additional days. After, doxycycline was removed from the media cocktail and the cells were cultured for 4 additional days, and then were collected for immunofluorescence, flow cytometry and qPCR mRNA quantification following the protocols described in “Validation of individual CCRs by flow cytometry”, “Validation of individual CCRs by Immunofluorescence” and “RT-qPCR” (Table S8). n = 3 independent differentiation replicates.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses

No statistical method was used to predetermine the sample size. All hESC differentiation experiments involving flow cytometry or immunostaining had at least 3 independent biological repeats and the exact number of biological repeats are provided in figure legends, with representative plots/images shown in the figures. CRISPR screens were performed with two biological repeats using custom made gRNA libraries to target candidate regulatory regions. All ATAC-seq, ChIP-seq experiments were performed in duplicates. Statistical tests are indicated in the main text and the figure legends. In conditions where the non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed we provide the p-value and the effect size value (r) to quantify the magnitude of the difference.
