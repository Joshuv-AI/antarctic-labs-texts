ZIF-8 nanoplatform coordinating Zn2+ overload and STING activation for enhanced prostate cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Xin'an Wang, Mingming Xu, Haoyu Wang, Yifan Ding, Yifan Liu, Weidong Zhou, Jun Xiang, Youyang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zinc homeostasis dysregulation is a significant feature in prostate cancer (PCa), characterized by a marked decrease in intracellular Zn2+ concentration. High levels of Zn2+ inhibit PCa cell proliferation, enhance drug sensitivity, and are closely linked to antitumor immune activation. Here, zeolitic imidazolate framework-8 (ZIF-8) was employed to encapsulate pyrithione (PT), a Zn2+ ionophore, and cyclic diguanylate (c-di-GMP), a STING agonist, followed by encapsulation with ovalbumin (OVA). In this nanoplatform (PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8), ZIF-8 degrades within the tumor microenvironment (TME) to release Zn2+. Upon cellular uptake, PT facilitates the intracellular transport of extracellular Zn2+. These Zn2+ may act synergistically with c-di-GMP to stimulate the cGAS-STING pathway, while OVA promoting antigen presentation, collectively activating and amplifying antitumor immunity. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 effectively activates STING signaling and antitumor immune responses, leading to tumor regression. Furthermore, combination therapy with an anti-PD-L1 antibody further enhanced the antitumor efficacy. This study proposes a Zn2+ overload and cGAS-STING pathway-based nanoplatform that activates robust antitumor immunity with a favorable safety profile, showing promise as a candidate strategy for PCa treatment.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) represents one of the most prevalent malignant tumors affecting men globally and links to elevated mortality rates (Feng et al., 2024). The chemoresistance of PCa and its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) pose significant challenges to clinical treatment (Chen et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2024). Although some advances have been made in immunotherapy and targeted therapy, the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) remains unsatisfactory in PCa. This is largely because PCa is typically a “cold” tumor, characterized by low antigenicity and limited mutations (Li et al., 2024), which hinders immune recognition and activation of antitumor immunity. Therefore, the development of novel agents capable of effectively activating antitumor immunity in PCa is of critical importance.

It is noteworthy that normal prostate epithelial cells exhibit elevated Zn2+ concentrations, whereas Zn2+ levels are markedly diminished in PCa cells (Wetherell et al., 2018; Barman et al., 2022). This phenomenon may be linked to metabolic reprogramming and the downregulation of zinc transporters (An et al., 2020). The decrease in Zn2+ concentration may impair its pro-apoptotic effects in tumor cells (Iitaka et al., 2001; Rudolf et al., 2005), thereby promoting the survival and proliferation of PCa cells. This suggests that elevating intracellular Zn2+ levels may induce apoptosis in PCa cells.

In recent years, the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway has attracted considerable attention as a central mediator of innate immune activation and antitumor immunity (Zhang et al., 2022a). STING activation triggers type I interferon (IFN-I) production, facilitates dendritic cell maturation, and augments cytotoxic T cell infiltration, thereby reversing the characteristic “cold” TME in PCa (Chen et al., 2025). However, PCa cells may intrinsically suppress STING signaling (Suter et al., 2021), leading to immune tolerance. Interestingly, Zn2+ has been shown to enhance intracellular cGAS enzymatic activity by facilitating cGAS-DNA phase separation, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway (Du and Chen, 2018). This activation mechanism is fundamentally distinct from conventional pathways. These findings suggest that elevating intracellular Zn2+ levels in combination with STING agonists may represent a promising strategy for PCa immunotherapy.

Conventional nanoplatforms generally either deliver STING agonists as a single agent or utilize Zn2+ as the sole activator of cGAS, thereby restricting the overall therapeutic outcome (Song et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2022b). Herein, we synthesized a nanoplatform based on ZIF-8 nanoparticles (Scheme 1). Briefly, ZIF-8 was sequentially loaded with pyrithione (PT, a Zn2+ ionophore) and cyclic diguanylate (c-di-GMP, a STING agonist), followed by encapsulation with ovalbumin (OVA). In the acidic TME, ZIF-8 degrades to release Zn2+. Due to the limited uptake of nanoparticles by PCa cells, the extracellularly released Zn2+ is transported intracellularly via PT, resulting in a rapid increase in intracellular Zn2+ levels. Intracellular Zn2+ overloading may act synergistically with c-di-GMP to potently activate the cGAS-STING pathway and stimulate antitumor immunity in PCa, as demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo. Additionally, OVA promoted antigen presentation. These responses led to significant tumor regression. In summary, this nanoplatform demonstrates potential as a promising therapeutic candidate for PCa treatment.

Materials and methods

Preparation of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8

First, a PT solution (1% DMSO, 1 mg/mL, 2.5 mL) was mixed with a zinc nitrate solution (0.4 M, 2.5 mL) under stirring for 5 min. The mixture was subsequently introduced gradually into a 2-MIM solution (1 M, 45 mL) under vigorous stirring and reacted for 30 min.The PT@ZIF-8 nanoparticles were gathered through centrifugation, cleaned, and subsequently redispersed. Subsequently, PT@ZIF-8 (2 mg/mL, 5 mL) was dispersed in a c-di-GMP solution (0.2 mg/mL, 15 mL) and mixed at ambient conditions for 2 h. The obtained nanoparticles (PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, 10 mg) were then dispersed in an OVA solution (0.5 mg/mL, 5 mL) and agitated at ambient conditions for 30 min. The final product, PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8, was collected by centrifugation, washed, and lyophilized to obtain dry nanoparticles. The supernatants from each step were collected, and the loading amounts of PT (measured at 275 nm), c-di-GMP (measured at 264 nm), and OVA were determined using UV–Vis spectroscopy and a BCA protein assay kit, respectively.

Characterization

The dimensions and structural characteristics of the nanoparticles were assessed through transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-2100F, JEOL) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Nova NanoSEM 450, FEI). The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential were ascertained via dynamic light scattering (DLS) utilizing a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS. X-ray diffraction (XRD) examination was executed employing a SmartLab diffractometer. Ultraviolet-visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy (Shimadzu, UV-3600i Plus) was employed to characterize the loading and release of components. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Bruker, VERTEX 70v) was utilized to identify functional groups on the nanoparticles. The zinc ion release was ascertained through inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, Shimadzu).

Zn2+, PT, c-di-GMP and OVA release

PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 underwent dispersion in PBS (pH 7.4 and pH 6.5) within dialysis bags. Suspensions were gathered at diverse time intervals and underwent ICP-MS analysis (for Zn2+ release), UV–Vis spectroscopy (for PT and c-di-GMP release), and BCA protein assay (for OVA release).

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were procured from Shanghai JieSiJie Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. All procedures were executed per the Animal Ethics Committee guidelines. The animal research protocol was sanctioned by the Animal Care Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji University (No. 2025-DW-(002)).

Cell culture

The mouse PCa cell line RM-1 was grown in RPMI-1640 medium comprising 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The human embryonic kidney cell line 293 T and the mouse fibroblast cell line L929 were cultivated in DMEM medium comprising 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

To acquire primary bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs), femurs and tibias were procured from 6 to 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Both ends of the bones were cut to expose the marrow cavity, which was repeatedly flushed with RPMI-1640 medium until the bones turned white. The flushed marrow was collected, dispersed by pipetting, and centrifuged (1000 rpm, 5 min). The cell pellet was resuspended and underwent incubation in red blood cell lysis buffer for 5 min to eliminate erythrocytes. After another centrifugation, the cells underwent two washing cycles with culture medium (RPMI-1640 + 15% FBS + 1% penicillin-streptomycin + 20 ng/mL GM-CSF) before being plated in 6-well plates. BMDCs were obtained after 7 days of culture.

Cell viability detection

The cytotoxicity of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 against the PCa cell line (RM-1) and normal cell lines (293 T and L929) was examined employing a CCK-8 assay. Briefly, cells from the three cell lines were plated in 96-well plates at 5000 cells per well and exposed to diverse concentrations of the nanoparticles for 24 h. Following the treatment, CCK-8 reagent was incorporated to each well per the supplier's protocol, and absorbance readings at 450 nm were obtained.

Intracellular Zn2+ detection

Intracellular Zn2+ concentrations were assessed utilizing the fluorescent probe Metal Fluor Zn-520. RM-1 cells were plated in 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well and exposed to ZnCl₂ or nanomaterials (equivalent to 50 ppm Zn, 12 h). Per the supplier's protocol, the cells were labeled with the fluorescent probe (5 μM) and DAPI. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to probenecid (1 mM) and visualized under a fluorescence microscope (MF53N, MShot). All fluorescence intensities were examined employing ImageJ software.

Cell apoptosis detection

RM-1 cells were placed into 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well. After incubation with PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (equivalent to 50 ppm Zn, 12 h), the cells were collected. The cell suspensions underwent sequential staining with Annexin V-FITC (5 μL) and PI (10 μL) staining solutions, followed by dark incubation for 20 min. Apoptotic cells were quantified utilizing a flow cytometer (Cytoflex).

Intracellular ROS detection

RM-1 cells were placed into 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well. After incubation with PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (equivalent to 50 ppm Zn, 12 h), the culture medium was eliminated, and the cells underwent two washes with PBS. A working solution of 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was subsequently introduced to each well, succeeded by incubation at 37 °C under dark conditions for 30 min. After incubation, the DCFH-DA-containing medium was removed, and cells underwent three washes with PBS. Subsequently, images were procured utilizing a fluorescence microscope (MF53N, MShot).

Mitochondrial membrane potential detection

RM-1 cells were plated in 24-well plates at 1 × 105 cells per well and exposed to PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (equivalent to 50 ppm Zn, 12 h). Following treatment, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells underwent two PBS washes. A JC-1 staining working solution was subsequently incorporated to each well and incubated following the protocol. After incubation, the cells received two PBS buffer washes, underwent fixation, and were stained with DAPI for nuclear labeling. Images were ultimately procured employing a fluorescence microscope (MF53N, MShot).

Cytoplasmic dsDNA detection

RM-1 cells were placed into confocal dishes and permitted to attach. After treatment with PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (corresponding to 50 ppm Zn, 12 h), the culture medium was substituted with a Mito-Tracker Red staining working solution and maintained at 37 °C for 30 min. The labeled cells were subsequently fixed and permeabilized, followed by treatment with a dsDNA monoclonal antibody at 4 °C overnight. Finally, FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG H&L was applied as the secondary antibody for 1 h, and images were acquired employing confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

Biosafety and distribution of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 in vivo

C57BL/6 mice were allocated into five groups and administered intravenously with PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, and PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (Zn, 5 mg/kg). Following 14 days, the mice were euthanized, and blood samples along with major organs were harvested for blood biochemical analysis and histopathological examination.

Cy5-labeled PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 was prepared following a method described previously (Yan et al., 2023). Briefly, during the synthesis of PT@ZIF-8, Cy5 (10 μg per mouse) was added to the mixture. Subsequent steps were consistent with the preparation procedure for PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8. Tumor-bearing mice received intravenous injections of free Cy5 or Cy5-labeled PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8. After 24 h post-injection, the mice underwent euthanization, and tumors along with major organs (heart, lungs, liver, spleen, and kidneys) were harvested for fluorescence intensity evaluation.

B3Z T cell hybridoma assays

To verify the processing and presentation of OVA, we performed B3Z T cell hybridoma assays. The specific experimental protocols were carried out analogously to those previously reported in the literature (Han et al., 2011).

Establishment of tumor-bearing mouse model and anti-tumor therapy

RM-1 cells (5 × 106) were subcutaneously administered to C57BL/6 mice for establishing a PCa mouse model as previously described (Wang et al., 2019). Upon the tumor volume reaching approximately 100 mm3, the tumor-bearing mice were allocated into five groups (n = 5) and received intravenous administration of PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (Zn equivalent: 5 mg/kg, administered every three days for 15 days). Tumor volume and body weight were documented every other day. Similarly, tumor-bearing mice were arbitrarily split into four groups (n = 5) and intravenously treated with PBS, anti-PD-L1 antibody (aPD-L1, 2 mg/kg), PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (Zn equivalent: 5 mg/kg), or a combination of aPD-L1 and PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8. All therapeutic interventions were delivered every three days over a complete 15-day period.

Histological staining

After various treatments, mice underwent euthanization, and tumor tissues underwent collection and weighing procedures. The obtained tumors underwent fixation and paraffin embedding for sectioning purposes. The resulting sections underwent hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, TUNEL staining, and immunofluorescence staining to assess histological alterations. Both H&E and TUNEL staining procedures per the supplier's protocols.

Enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

BMDCs were plated in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well. After different treatments, the culture supernatant was gathered for ELISA to assess TNF-α and IL-6 levels. Soluble proteins were extracted from tumor tissues subjected to various treatments using a homogenization method and subsequently analyzed by ELISA to quantify TNF-α, IL-6, IFN-β, and IFN-γ levels.

Flow cytometry

Briefly, BMDCs were placed into 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells per well. After respective treatments, the cells were collected. For tumor tissues, single-cell suspensions were generated from collected tumors through mechanical dissociation and filtration. The cells underwent subsequent staining with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies that target specific cell subsets. Finally, the percentages of CD11c+CD86+ and CD11c+CD40+ dendritic cells (DCs), CD3+CD4+ and CD3+CD8+ T cells, M1 macrophages (F4/80+CD86+), and M2 macrophages (F4/80+CD206+) were quantified using a flow cytometer (Cytoflex).

Western blotting

RM-1 cells were placed into 6-well culture plates and exposed to PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 (corresponding to 50 ppm Zn, 12 h). Complete protein was procured from the cells utilizing RIPA lysis buffer, and the protein concentration was ascertained employing a BCA protein assay kit. The procedures included protein denaturation, electrophoresis, membrane transfer, blocking, incubation with primary antibody overnight, and incubation with secondary antibody. Finally, the membrane underwent visualization utilizing an iBright imaging system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). β-actin was used as the loading control. For tumor tissues, proteins were extracted by grinding followed by the same Western blot protocol. The grayscale values of protein bands were quantified employing ImageJ software.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Tumor-bearing mice were arbitrarily distributed into two groups (n = 4) and received intravenous administration of PBS or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 following the identical treatment protocol outlined previously. Following treatment completion, tumor tissues were harvested, and total RNA extraction was performed utilizing TRIzol reagent. RNA sequencing was executed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Differential expression genes (DEGs) analysis was executed employing DESeq2, while Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were executed through DAVID.

Statistical analysis

All data were denoted as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The differences between groups were determined by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test (GraphPad Prism 9.0). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 and ****p < 0.0001 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8

Firstly, PT@ZIF-8 was prepared using a one-pot method by mixing a PT solution with a zinc nitrate solution, followed by addition to a 2-MIM solution under vigorous stirring. The obtained nanoparticles were then sequentially dispersed in c-di-GMP and OVA solutions, where they combined via electrostatic interactions to form PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8. As observed by SEM (Fig. 1A) and TEM (Fig. 1B), PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 exhibited uniform size (∼200 nm) and a polyhedral morphology similar to that of ZIF-8. The hydrodynamic diameter of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 was 449.5 ± 24.5 nm (Fig. 1E), exhibiting a zeta potential of − 10.62 mV (Fig. 1F). The stepwise increase in hydrodynamic diameter and the reversal of zeta potential provided preliminary evidence for the successful synthesis of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8. Elemental mapping confirmed the presence of Zn elements, N elements, O elements, and C elements in PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8 (Fig. 1C). This is consistent with the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data. Compared to ZIF-8, the intensity of the O 1 s photoelectron peak in PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8 is enhanced (Fig. 1G). Meanwhile, the appearance of S 2p and P 2p photoelectron peaks confirms the successful loading of PT and c-di-GMP on ZIF-8, respectively (Fig. 1G). X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis reveals that the crystalline structure is well preserved after drug loading (Fig. S2). Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) data also confirms successful loading of PT and c-di-GMP (Fig. 1D). The loading of PT, c-di-GMP, and OVA on ZIF-8 was quantified using UV–vis spectroscopy and a BCA assay (Fig. S1). The results demonstrated that 42.72% of PT, 29.75% of c-di-GMP, and 72.24% of OVA were encapsulated within ZIF-8, with corresponding loading rates of 1.45%, 8.19%, and 15.30%, respectively.Fig. 1Characterization of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8. It has been reported that ZIF-8 degrades in the acidic TME, releasing Zn2+ and its cargo. To validate this, the release profiles of Zn2+, PT, c-di-GMP, and OVA were evaluated at pH 6.5 and pH 7.4, respectively. The findings revealed that the cumulative release of these compounds was markedly elevated in PBS at pH 6.5 compared to pH 7.4. Specifically, at pH 6.5, 83.2% of Zn2+, 61.4% of PT, 41.73% of c-di-GMP, and 72.47% of OVA were released within 48 h (Fig. 1H). Given that ZIF-8 is known to undergo premature degradation in protein-rich environments, we further evaluated the release profiles of the aforementioned compounds under 10% FBS conditions to validate this characteristic. Compared with pH 7.4 alone, the cumulative release of these compounds was relatively increased in the presence of 10% FBS at the same pH. Specifically, under pH 7.4 with 10% FBS, the release rates within 48 h reached 20.95% for Zn2+ and 21.19% for c-di-GMP (Fig. 1H). Furthermore, compared with pH 7.4 alone, the particle size remained largely unchanged in the presence of 10% FBS at pH 7.4. This result indicates that serum proteins have a relatively minor impact on the colloidal stability of ZIF-8 (Fig. S2).

To determine whether OVA was indeed processed and presented, a B3Z T cell hybridoma cross-presentation assay was performed. The results demonstrated that the PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 treatment group significantly activated B3Z T cells compared with the control group (Fig. S3A).

Furthermore, PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 treatment markedly elevated the population of CD11c+CD86+ and CD11c+CD40+ cells (Fig. 2E and G-H, Fig. S3B), indicating that it promotes the maturation of BMDCs and enhances antigen presentation, which is crucial for antitumor immunotherapy.

To detect intracellular Zn2+ levels, fluorescence imaging of Zn2+ was performed. PT@ZIF-8 and PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 exhibited higher fluorescence intensity compared to ZIF-8 at equivalent concentrations (Fig. 2F and I), demonstrating the enhanced intracellular transport of Zn2+. Additionally, TUNEL staining revealed higher fluorescence intensity in RM-1 cells treated with PT@ZIF-8 and PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8, indicating enhanced apoptosis (Fig. S4). This effect may relate to intracellular Zn2+ accumulation and cGAS-STING pathway activation.

PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 activate cGAS-STING pathway

Previous research has shown that Zn2+ stimulates the cGAS-STING signaling pathway (Du and Chen, 2018; Sun et al., 2024). In this study, treatment with PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 induced intracellular Zn2+ overload in RM-1 cells. We measured reactive oxygen species (ROS) level in RM-1 cells using an ROS detection kit. The results showed that PT-mediated intracellular Zn2+ overload further increased ROS levels (Fig. 3A and E). Subsequently, elevated ROS resulted in diminished mitochondrial membrane potential, as demonstrated by reduced JC-1 aggregates and elevated JC-1 monomers (Fig. 3B and F). Damaged mitochondria tend to release double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) into the cytoplasm, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway. Fluorescence staining of dsDNA revealed that PT@ZIF-8 induced more dsDNA leakage compared to ZIF-8 alone, and RM-1 cells treated with PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity (Fig. 3C and G). The biocompatibility and therapeutic efficacy of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 in vivo

First, we evaluated the in vivo biocompatibility of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8. After intravenous injection of PBS, ZIF-8, PT@ZIF-8, PT-c-di-GMP@ZIF-8, or PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 into mice via the tail vein for 14 days, the animals underwent euthanization, and blood samples along with major organs (encompassing the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were harvested for blood biochemical analysis and histopathological examination. Notably, no marked alterations were detected in the histopathology of major organs (Fig. S5A) and blood biochemical parameters (Fig. S5B) of the mice. At 24 h post-injection, PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 was primarily distributed in the liver and kidneys, with additional accumulation in tumors (Fig. S6), presumably attributable to the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect and the accelerated degradation of the nanoparticles in the acidic TME. These results demonstrate that PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 exhibits favorable biocompatibility and is suitable for applications in vivo.

PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 activates the anti-tumor immune response in vivo

To further explore the antitumor mechanism of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8, RNA sequencing was executed on tumor tissues extracted from treated and untreated tumor-bearing mice. A sum of 7435 differentially expressed genes (DEGs, P < 0.05 & |log₂FC| ≥ 1) were detected, encompassing 4958 upregulated and 2477 downregulated genes (Fig. 4A). Consistent with the immune-activating effects of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 observed in vitro, genes linked to interferon and STING signaling pathways, including Ifnb1 (Liu et al., 2024), Cxcl10 (Bartolini et al., 2024), Ccl5 (Yan et al., 2025), Cxcl9 (Enbergs et al., 2024), Irf7 (Peng et al., 2024), Mx1 (Nouri et al., 2025), were markedly upregulated (Fig. 4B), providing strong evidence for cGAS-STING pathway activation. Additionally, inflammation-related genes (Tnf, Il6, Tlr1, Tlr3, Tlr5) (Guo et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2025), pro-apoptotic genes (Bcl2l11, Casp3, Bax, Casp8) (Huang et al., 2025; Cui et al., 2018) and immune response-promoting genes (Hlaa, Hlab, Tap1, Gzmb, Prf1) (Carré et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2023; Thompson and Cao, 2024; Guan et al., 2024) were upregulated. Discussion

PCa represents one of the most prevalent malignancies among men, distinguished by markedly diminished intracellular Zn2+ levels, an immunosuppressive TME, and a limited response to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) (Esteves et al., 2021; Jeong and Kwak, 2022). Therefore, developing effective immunotherapy strategies is of critical importance. Furthermore, ZIF-8-based nanoplatforms, which possess favorable stability and tend to degrade only under acidic conditions, have been widely employed for pH-responsive drug delivery (Wang et al., 2024a; Wang et al., 2024b). In this study, we designed a nanoplatform based on ZIF-8, which was sequentially loaded with PT and c-di-GMP, and finally encapsulated with OVA. This system synergistically enhances antitumor immunity through inducing intracellular Zn2+ overload, activating the cGAS-STING pathway, and promoting antigen presentation. The findings demonstrated that the nanoplatform activated the cGAS-STING signaling pathway, reversed the immunosuppressive TME, and promoted tumor regression in vivo, showing promising therapeutic efficacy.

PCa cells are known for dysregulated Zn2+ metabolism (Gmeiner et al., 2015). ZIF-8 degrades in the acidic TME to release Zn2+, and PT further enhances the intracellular transport of Zn2+, leading to Zn2+ overload in PCa cells. This overload not only directly induces apoptosis but also activates the cGAS-STING pathway to initiate antitumor immunity. Meanwhile, c-di-GMP, a STING agonist (Zhang et al., 2023), amplifies the downstream signaling, resulting in enhanced secretion of IFNs and maturation of DCs. OVA further facilitates antigen presentation (He et al., 2025). This multi-layered therapeutic strategy counteracts the “cold” tumor phenotype of PCa. This is supported by the upregulation of genes related to interferon and STING signaling pathways (e.g., Ifnb1, Cxcl10), inflammatory response (e.g., Tnf, Il6), and immune activation (e.g., Hlaa, Gzmb).

Compared to single STING agonists or Zn2+-based therapeutic strategies, our nanoplatform innovatively combines intracellular Zn2+ overload with STING agonism, markedly enhancing therapeutic efficacy (Song et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2022b). This approach overcomes limitations such as insufficient STING activation caused by inadequate intracellular Zn2+ accumulation. Furthermore, the significant upregulation of PD-L1 expression in tumors following PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 treatment indicates the presence of adaptive immune resistance. Combining PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 with an anti-PD-L1 antibody (aPD-L1) counteracts this effect and markedly enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration, highlighting its potential for clinical translation.

PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 exhibits excellent biocompatibility. No significant changes in blood biochemical parameters or histology of major organs were observed in mice 14 days after intravenous administration. The nanoparticles' pH-responsive degradation in the TME further enhances their accumulation at the tumor site, building upon the EPR effect. Notably, the nanoplatform promotes macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, aligning with the growing interest in multimodal immunotherapy.

Although our study demonstrates advances in PCa immunotherapy, several challenges remain and warrant further investigation. First, the heterogeneity of PCa subtypes may influence the efficacy of Zn2+ overload and STING agonist synergy, necessitating subtype-specific evaluations. Second, the immune memory effects of this therapy remain unexplored. Studying the long-term response of PT-c-di-GMP/OVA@ZIF-8 in recurrent or metastatic tumor models could provide deeper insights. Furthermore, the combination therapy group only assessed CD8+ T cell infiltration as the primary endpoint. Although this parameter is sufficient to demonstrate the therapeutic efficacy, future investigations could be extended to examine the roles of the combination regimen in modulating cytokine levels and DC maturation, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying synergistic mechanism. Additionally, optimizing the dosing regimen and conducting pharmacokinetic studies are critical for clinical application. Future studies could incorporate additional time points to further characterize the biodistribution profile of this nanomaterial, thereby providing a basis for its clinical application. Finally, combination strategies with other ICIs or targeted therapies may further improve therapeutic outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed a nanoplatform that triggers robust antitumor immunity through intracellular Zn2+ overload and STING activation, with negligible systemic toxicity. We demonstrated that Zn2+ overload in PCa cells effectively activates the cGAS-STING pathway and provides a combinatorial strategy to overcome adaptive immune resistance. Future investigations should concentrate on clinical validation of this approach to facilitate translation, as well as exploring novel Zn2+-based targets to develop more effective therapeutic regimens for PCa.
