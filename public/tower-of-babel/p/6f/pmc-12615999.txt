Evaluating sunn hemp as a dead-end trap crop for sustainable management of the legume pod borer (Lepidoptera: Crambidae)
Authors: Sopana Yule, Ramasamy Srinivasan, Stephen Tarmogin Omburo Othim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe legume pod borer, Maruca vitrata (F.) poses a significant threat to leguminous crops, necessitating sustainable management approaches to mitigate damage and maintain productivity. This study evaluated sunn hemp, Crotalaria juncea (L.) (Fabaceae), as a potential dead-end trap crop for managing M. vitrata infestations in cowpea, Vigna unguiculata subsp. sesquipedalis (L.) (Fabaceae), and lablab bean, Lablab purpureus (L.) (Fabaceae). Laboratory oviposition assays indicated that sunn hemp was significantly less preferred than cowpea in dual-choice conditions, whereas no significant preference was detected relative to lablab bean. Interestingly, sunn hemp received more eggs than cowpea under no-choice conditions but fewer than lablab bean. Larvae feeding on sunn hemp suffered complete mortality, failing to develop beyond the fourth instar. In contrast, cowpea and lablab beans equally supported complete larval and pupal development without significant differences in developmental parameters, pupal weight, or sex ratio. Field experiments demonstrated that sunn hemp effectively reduced pest infestation relative to untreated controls, showing comparable efficacy to chemical treatments. Marketable yield proportions differed significantly between the crops, with lablab bean consistently achieving higher marketable yields than cowpea. Sunn hemp significantly enhanced the marketable yield of lablab beans, reinforcing its value as a tool for ecological pest management. Overall, sunn hemp effectively disrupted M. vitrata populations, reduced pest pressure, and improved yield quality, thus highlighting its potential as a sustainable and ecologically beneficial component in integrated pest management programs for leguminous crops.

Introduction

The legume pod borer, Maruca vitrata (F.) (Lepidoptera: Crambidae), is a significant pest of leguminous crops across tropical and subtropical regions, causing severe yield losses due to larval feeding on flowers, pods, and developing seeds (Sharma 1998, Sharma et al. 1999, Ba et al. 2019, Srinivasan et al. 2021). Legume farmers in Asia and Africa have been reported to rely heavily on chemical insecticides, often applied indiscriminately, to produce blemish-free beans (Schreinemachers et al. 2017, Srinivasan et al. 2021). However, excessive pesticide use poses environmental risks, harms beneficial organisms, and contributes to the development of insecticide resistance, ultimately threatening sustainable crop production (Jackai and Adalla 1997, Ekesi 1999, Sreelakshmi et al. 2015).

Various pest management approaches have been tested against M. vitrata, including chemical control, host plant resistance, biological control, microbial pesticides, semiochemical-based strategies, and RNA interference (RNAi) technologies (Srinivasan et al. 2021). Among these, synthetic insecticides remain the most widely used. Host plant resistance and microbial pesticides, such as formulations of Bacillus thuringiensis (Berliner), have shown potential but are often limited by their constrained field efficacy or accessibility (Yule and Srinivasan 2014, Ahmed et al. 2020, Srinivasan et al. 2021). Biological control using parasitoids such as Apanteles taragamae Viereck (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), Phanerotoma syleptae Nixon (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), and Therophilus javanus Bhat and Gupta (Hymenoptera: Braconidae) has been explored with promising results in parts of West Africa (Tamò et al. 2017, Ba et al. 2019, Srinivasan et al. 2021). Still, adoption across regions has been slow due to challenges related to ecological adaptation, mass rearing, and release (Srinivasan et al. 2021). Likewise, deploying pheromone-based mating disruption and RNAi remains largely experimental or localized in practice. Given these limitations, especially in resource-constrained settings, there is a clear need for low-cost, ecologically sound strategies that are compatible with smallholder farming systems.

Trap cropping is one such strategy that holds promise. It involves planting a more attractive crop near the main crop to divert pest oviposition, thereby reducing infestation on the main crop (Shelton and Badenes-Perez 2006). Recent studies have reinforced the ecological value of trap cropping within diversified systems, demonstrating that it can reduce insect pest loads and promote the abundance of natural enemies (Jaworski et al. 2023). For smallholder farmers practicing mixed or intercropping systems, trap cropping offers a practical and scalable approach to pest management. While widely adopted in some temperate cereal and pulse systems (Cárcamo et al. 2024), its adoption remains limited in vegetable legumes. A further refinement, using dead-end trap crops—those that attract oviposition but fail to support larval development—adds another layer of pest suppression by disrupting the pest’s life cycle entirely (Hokkanen 1991, Cook et al. 2007). Dead-end trap cropping has gained attention in recent years as an ecologically safe tactic that leverages pest behavioral manipulation without the need for synthetic inputs (Jaworski et al. 2023, Cárcamo et al. 2024). While most well-known examples focus on Lepidoptera, such as Helicoverpa armigera on tomato systems using Solanum viarum (Srinivasan et al. 2005), its application against pod borers like M. vitrata remains limited.

Sunn hemp (Crotalaria juncea [L.]), a tropical legume primarily known for soil improvement and nematode suppression (Narberhaus et al. 2005, Wang et al. 2008), has shown promise as a potential dead-end trap crop, owing to its attractiveness for oviposition coupled with its unsuitability for larval development (Jackai and Singh 1983). Studies conducted 4 decades ago demonstrated high oviposition rates of M. vitrata on C. juncea relative to cowpea (Vigna unguiculata subsp. sesquipedalis [L.]) but with up to 80% larval mortality and the lowest larval growth index, reinforcing its potential as a dead-end trap crop (Jackai and Singh 1981, 1983).

Despite its promise, trap cropping for M. vitrata remains underexplored, particularly in vegetable legumes, despite offering a practical solution for smallholder farming systems where intercropping is common. Recent reviews emphasize the need to evaluate local models and deploy trap crops, such as C. juncea, in spatial arrangements that optimally intercept the pest (Srinivasan et al. 2021). Panwar et al. (2021) similarly highlighted the potential of trap cropping in vegetable systems, especially when integrated with other agroecological tools; however, they noted that it remains underutilized in tropical legume systems due to knowledge gaps in deployment protocols, crop pairing, and scaling pathways. Furthermore, successful management strategies must consider the wide host range and plasticity of M. vitrata, which feeds on over 70 Fabaceae species and can shift between wild and cultivated hosts to persist throughout the year (Arodokoun et al. 2003, Gowda et al. 2015). This makes dead-end trap cropping an even more appealing tactic when suitable non-crop hosts are scarce.

This study evaluated the effectiveness of sunn hemp as a dead-end trap crop for managing M. vitrata infestations in 2 economically important legume crops: cowpea and lablab bean (Lablab purpureus [L.]). We assessed host-plant preference, developmental performance, pest infestation levels, and crop yield through comprehensive laboratory assays and complementary field trials. We hypothesized that sunn hemp would attract oviposition but not support larval development, thereby lowering infestation pressure on adjacent crops. We aimed to gain empirical insights into the potential of sunn hemp as a viable component of integrated pest management programs to enhance legume crop productivity and promote environmentally sustainable pest control strategies.

Materials and Methods

Insect Culture Maintenance

Maruca vitrata larvae collected from a Sesbania tree at the World Vegetable Center (WorldVeg) research station (N 14°1′ 46.887″ E 99°57′ 51.104″) were reared under controlled laboratory conditions (27 ± 3 °C, 70 ± 5% RH, 12 L:12D) using fresh flowers of Sesbania, as these were readily available. The flowers were randomly collected from Sesbania trees growing at the WorldVeg research station and placed directly into plastic rearing containers (0.15 × 0.25 × 0.075 m). New flowers were provided daily for larval feeding to ensure freshness and prevent decay. Approximately 3 d later, the first larval instar hatched. Upon reaching the mature fifth larval instar (7 to 10 d), folded paper was introduced into the containers for pupation. Newly emerged adults were transferred to mating cages (0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 m) covered with 40-mesh netting and maintained for 48 h. A water-soaked sponge and honey solution were provided for adult feeding. Mated adults were then allowed to oviposit on lablab bean inflorescences, which are highly attractive to Maruca moths, and lay more eggs for 24 h. Newly hatched larvae were fed Sesbania flowers, as mentioned earlier. The Sesbania flower is used to reduce bias in host preference for Maruca colonies on sunn hemp, lablab bean, and cowpea in a two-choice oviposition preference assay. Colonies were maintained for 4 generations under these conditions before being used in the experiments.

No-Choice Oviposition Preference Assay

Sunn hemp (Chainat 72, Department of Lands, Thailand), cowpea (Rapepat variety, Paetong), and lablab bean (purple hyacinth bean, WorldVeg) were grown in a nursery (29 ± 3 °C, 75 ± 5% RH) until the flowering stage (45 DAS). Twenty 10-d seedlings of each crop were transplanted into 2 plant beds (10 plants per bed) with 30 cm distance between plants. Fertilizers with NPK ratios of 15-0-0, 15-15-15, and 12-24-12 were applied at 1, 3, and 5 wks after transplanting, respectively, and watering was done daily. For each test, 4 budding inflorescences (each with 5 flowers) from a single plant species were placed into individual insect cages (0.6 × 1 × 0.6 m) in the greenhouse. In each cage, 5 naïve-mated female adults (20 females per treatment) of M. vitrata were released at a distance of 0.5 m from the inflorescences for oviposition. Honey solution and water were provided as food sources. After 48 h, the number of eggs laid was recorded under a stereo microscope (Zaturn ZT-255). The experiment followed a Completely Randomized Design (CRD) with 4 replications per plant species. The experiment was repeated twice.

Two-Choice Oviposition Preference Assay

Dual-choice tests evaluated oviposition preference between sunn hemp and cowpea, and between sunn hemp and lablab bean. Fifteen inflorescences per crop were divided into 3 containers with 5 inflorescences per container (4 flowers per inflorescence). Three containers of each crop were placed in the same row, opposite the compared crop, in insect cages (0.6 × 1.6 × 0.6 m). Twenty mated female adults were released centrally between the 2 crops, at an equal distance (0.5 m) and allowed to oviposit for 48 h. Eggs were then counted from each flower bud. The experiment followed a CRD with 3 replications and was repeated twice.

Larval Survival and Development Assay

Second-instar larvae were individually transferred into rearing containers (5 cm diameter), each of which was provided with fresh flowers of sunn hemp, cowpea, or lablab bean daily. Second-instar larvae were used to ensure uniformity and viability, as the minute size of first-instar larvae makes it difficult to accurately assess health status, increasing the risk of including unhealthy individuals. Larvae were reared under controlled laboratory conditions (27 ± 3 °C, 70 ± 5% RH) until the adult stage. Mortality, larval development time, pupal duration, pupal weight, and sex ratios were recorded. Containers were arranged in a CRD, with 5 larvae per treatment, replicated 5 times, and the entire experiment was conducted twice for reproducibility.

Field Evaluation of Dead-End Trap Crop Effectiveness

Field trials evaluating the effectiveness of sunn hemp trap cropping compared to chemical treatments and an untreated control were conducted separately for cowpea and lablab beans. Sunn hemp was directly sown 1 and 3 wks before transplanting lablab and cowpeas, respectively, to synchronize flowering times. A second sowing of sunn hemp was conducted 2 wks after the first sowing of lablab beans, as lablab beans have an extended harvesting period compared to cowpea. Main crops (10-d-old seedlings) were transplanted at 0.5 × 1 m spacing with 20 plants per plot in a Randomized Complete Block Design with 4 replications per treatment. Sunn hemp was planted 1 m from the main crop, and treatments were spaced 5 m apart to avoid cross-treatment volatile contamination.

Three insecticides were applied sequentially in the chemical treatment plots at weekly intervals. The rotation began with abamectin (1.8%) at a rate of 20 ml per 20 liters of water (IRAC MoA Group 6: Chloride channel activator), followed by spinetoram (12%) at 10 ml per 20 liters (IRAC MoA Group 5: Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor allosteric modulators), and then chlorantraniliprole (5.17%) at 60 ml per 20 liters (IRAC MoA Group 28: Ryanodine receptor modulators). This rotation was designed to reduce the likelihood of resistance development and enhance control efficacy (Sparks et al. 2020, IRAC 2025). Insecticide applications commenced on 19 March 2024, for the cowpea trial and on 18 July 2024, for the lablab bean trial. Maruca vitrata adults (50 females each) were released at 3 locations per plot weekly for 2 consecutive weeks. During insect releases, a 2-m-high shade-net enclosure was installed around the experimental area for 2 wks. Larval infestation was monitored weekly from 10 randomly sampled inflorescences per plot, resulting in 40 inflorescences assessed per treatment, starting 1 wk after adult release. Mature pods were harvested weekly, and the weights of marketable and non-marketable pods were recorded separately.

Data Analysis

The data were analyzed to evaluate the oviposition preference, sex ratio, and developmental times of M. vitrata across 3 plant treatments: sunn hemp, cowpea, and lablab bean. Paired egg counts from dual-choice assays were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test to account for non-independence between treatments within replicates. In the no-choice analysis, a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) fitted using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015) with a Poisson distribution was used to model the number of eggs laid, accounting for trial-related variability (ie the time at which trials were conducted). A Poisson model was selected because the response variable, egg count, is discrete and non-negative, and preliminary residual diagnostics showed no evidence of overdispersion. Sex ratio data were analyzed using a mixed-effects logistic regression model with a binomial family, where the binary response variable (1 = male, 0 = female) represented the sex of each individual. Treatment (cowpea vs. lablab) was included as a fixed effect, while batch (ie time of experiment) was treated as a random intercept to account for variation across experimental runs. The impact of treatment on larval time, pupal time, and total development time was assessed using a linear mixed-effects model, with significance tested via t-tests with Satterthwaite’s method. Estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts were computed using the emmeans package (Lenth 2025). Odds ratios (OR) were calculated by exponentiating the model coefficients from the GLMM and logistic regression analyses, specifically for the no-choice oviposition counts and the sex ratio comparisons. In the field experiment, a Poisson regression model with a log link was used to examine the effects of crop and treatment on pest counts. At the same time, one-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the impact of treatments on yield and marketable yield for cowpea and lablab beans, with Tukey’s HSD test applied for mean separation when significant differences were found. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.4.2 (R Development Core Team 2024).

Results

In the no-choice test with sunn hemp as the baseline, the model revealed significant differences among treatments (χ2 = 583.68, df = 2, P < 0.001). The predicted mean number of eggs was highest on lablab bean (117.0 ± 5.4), followed by sunn hemp (38.6 ± 2.5), and lowest on cowpea (21.9 ± 1.8). Relative to sunn hemp, lablab received about 3.02 times as many eggs (P < 0.001). In comparison, cowpea received 0.57 times as many eggs as sunn hemp (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Additionally, cowpea was significantly less preferred than lablab (OR = 0.19; P < 0.001).

In the dual-choice test, the sunn hemp vs. lablab combination yielded a higher total (and mean) number of eggs laid (∼241 per replicate) compared to sunn hemp vs. cowpea (∼152 per replicate). Within each pairing, M. vitrata laid markedly fewer eggs on sunn hemp than on the other legume (cowpea or lablab), and the absolute totals for lablab were higher than those for cowpea. Results from the Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed a significant preference for lablab bean over sunn hemp (W = 0, P = 0.03). In contrast, the preference for cowpea over sunn hemp was marginally non-significant (W = 1, P = 0.06), despite a consistent trend toward lower oviposition on sunn hemp across replicates (Fig. 2). Cowpea had the highest variability in egg counts. In contrast, sunn hemp had the lowest, with most data points near zero. The lablab bean also showed a higher egg count than sunn hemp, although with a more concentrated distribution (Fig. 2).

When feeding on sunn hemp, M. vitrata was unable to complete its larval development. Over 70% of the larvae were unable to develop beyond the third instar stage. It was also observed that larval development was significantly delayed; for instance, it took an average of 3.5 days to complete the second instar on sunn hemp compared to 1.6 days on cowpea and 1.4 days on lablab beans. Since all the larvae died before the fifth instar, sunn hemp was excluded from subsequent analyses of larval, pupal, and total development time.

Among the 2 suitable host plants, the mean larval development time was slightly longer on cowpea (8 ± 0.1 days) than on lablab (7 ± 0.4 days). Still, this difference was not statistically significant (t = 1.46, df = 10, P = 0.146). Pupal time averaged 5 ± 0.2 days on both cowpea and lablab, again with no significant difference (t = –1.55, df = 10, P = 0.13). Similarly, the total development time from larva to adult was 13 ± 0.5 days on cowpea and 12. ± 0.2 days on lablab (t = 0.52, df = 10, P = 0.60). Pupal weights were nearly identical between treatments (0.04 ± 0.001 g), with no significant effect of host plant (t = 0.14, df = 10, P = 0.89) (Table 1).

Open Field Trial

The Poisson regression model showed that there were significantly fewer larvae (M. vitrata larvae) on the cowpea field (mean = 0.17 larvae/plot) compared to the lablab bean field (mean = 10.22 larvae/plot), with an OR of 0.02 and P < 0.001 (Fig. 4). Within the lablab field, the chemical treatment significantly reduced pest counts compared to the control. Specifically, the mean number of larvae per plot was 8.71 in the chemical treatment, 9.88 in the sunn hemp treatment, and 12.08 in the control treatment. Compared to the control, pest numbers were significantly reduced by 28% in the chemical treatment (OR = 0.72; 95% CI: [0.60, 0.87]; P < 0.001) and by 18% in the sunn hemp treatment (OR = 0.82; 95% CI: [0.69, 0.97]; P = 0.02). While the sunn hemp treatment had 13% more pests than the chemical treatment (OR = 1.13; 95% CI: [0.94, 1.37]), this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.19). In the cowpea field, larval infestation by M. vitrata was generally low across all treatments, with several plots recording no infestation. The mean number of larvae per plot was 0.38 ± 0.18 in the control treatment and 0.13 ± 0.13 in both the chemical and sunn hemp treatments. Given the consistently low pest counts and limited variation across treatments, statistical model estimates were unstable and thus not reliable.

Lablab beans consistently showed higher values for marketable yield across treatments (88.8% to 93.8%) than cowpeas (57.7% to 74.8%). There were no significant differences in yield for cowpea (F = 2.01; df = 2,8; P = 0.20) or lablab beans (F = 0.27; df = 2,9; P = 0.77) across the chemical, control, and sunn hemp treatments, with all treatments yielding similar results (Table 2). However, for marketable yield, chemical treatment significantly (F = 7.89; df = 2,9; P = 0.011) improved the proportion of marketable lablab beans (93.85 ± 0.81), followed by sunn hemp (90.06 ± 1.18) and control (88.82 ± 1.22). There were no significant differences (F = 2.45; df = 2,8; P = 0.15) in the proportion of marketable yield across treatments for cowpeas.

Discussion

The oviposition behavior of M. vitrata under both dual-choice and no-choice conditions reinforces the potential of sunn hemp as a trap crop but also underscores the influence of crop pairing on its effectiveness. In choice tests, females consistently laid fewer eggs on sunn hemp compared to lablab and cowpea, with a statistically significant preference for lablab and a marginal preference for cowpea. No-choice results further reinforced this trend, showing that lablab was the most preferred host for oviposition, followed by sunn hemp, while cowpea was the least preferred. This differs somewhat from earlier findings by Jackai and Singh (1981), who reported higher oviposition on Crotalaria than cowpea, suggesting that varietal differences, environmental context, or methodological factors may influence host preference. The discrepancies observed between choice and no-choice settings in this study indicate that oviposition decisions by M. vitrata females may be influenced by a combination of external cues, such as volatile profiles and physical traits, and internal thresholds for host acceptance ­(Srinivasan et al. 2021). The inconsistent ranking of cowpea suggests a potential role for plant-specific factors influencing attractiveness or suitability, possibly including variable production of inflorescence volatiles (Agunbiade et al. 2014).

While sunn hemp was not the most attractive oviposition site, its critical value lies in its ability to attract some oviposition and then disrupt the pest’s life cycle due to its unsuitability for larval development, the defining feature of a dead-end trap crop (Hokkanen 1991, Cook et al. 2007). These findings affirm the potential of sunn hemp as a dead-end trap crop capable of reducing oviposition pressure but also indicate that the specific legume crop with which it is paired may influence its performance as a dead-end trap crop. Notably, although sunn hemp was not the most preferred oviposition site in the dual-choice bioassays, its inclusion in open-field trials led to substantial reductions in M. vitrata infestation. This divergence underscores the importance of testing trap crops under real-world conditions, where visual and olfactory cues, spatial arrangements, and ecological interactions may amplify or modify behavioral responses. In the field, sunn hemp likely acts as both a physical and chemical decoy, intercepting gravid females and disrupting host-finding efficiency, which may not be fully captured in laboratory settings. Further studies are recommended to explore the relative attractiveness of other Crotalaria species or to enhance C. juncea with attractant volatiles for improved field performance.

Larval performance on host plants plays a pivotal role in determining the success of insect pests and guides the selection of suitable management strategies. The developmental assays in this study provided strong evidence supporting the role of sunn hemp as a dead-end trap crop for M. vitrata. Larvae feeding on sunn hemp experienced substantial developmental delays and suffered complete mortality before reaching pupation, with over 70% dying before the fourth instar stage. These outcomes likely stem from the plant’s poor nutritional quality or the presence of toxic compounds that disrupt digestion and development. This is in agreement with previous reports by Jackai and Singh (1983), who observed larval mortality of approximately 82.2% on C. juncea. However, the complete (100%) mortality observed in our study suggests that specific environmental or varietal factors, such as plant age, chemical profile, or local M. vitrata population differences, may further intensify the plant’s anti-herbivore effects. It is plausible that the C. juncea genotype or maturity stage used in our assays had elevated levels of defensive compounds, such as pyrrolizidine alkaloids, which may have enhanced larval toxicity. Secondary metabolites such as pyrrolizidine alkaloids, commonly found in Crotalaria species, have been implicated in deterring insect feeding and impairing larval development (Narberhaus et al. 2005). In contrast, cowpea and lablab bean supported full larval development with low mortality (∼8%) and no significant differences in developmental duration or pupal weight, suggesting both hosts provide adequate nutrition. These results were mirrored in the sex ratio analysis, which showed no significant difference in the proportion of male progeny between cowpea and lablab beans. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which indicate that sex ratios in lepidopteran pests are generally stable across host plants, as they are influenced more strongly by genetic and physiological mechanisms than by environmental or nutritional differences (Awmack and Leather 2002, Moreau et al. 2006). These findings highlight the comparable developmental outcomes of cowpea and lablab beans. Importantly, they also show that sunn hemp successfully attracts oviposition, albeit at a lower rate than lablab, but does not support larval development. This combination of high moth attraction for oviposition and complete developmental failure makes it a strong candidate for inclusion in integrated pest management programs targeting M. vitrata.

The open-field evaluation provided practical insights into the potential of sunn hemp as a trap crop for M. vitrata management compared to conventional chemical treatments. The significantly lower infestation observed on cowpea compared to lablab bean fields aligns with earlier findings indicating inherent differences in susceptibility among legume hosts under natural infestation conditions (Sharma et al. 1999). Chemical treatment effectively suppressed larval populations, reinforcing its established efficacy in pest control programs. Nevertheless, the comparable efficacy observed with sunn hemp, which achieved substantial pest reduction relative to the untreated control, underscores its value as an ecologically sustainable pest management tool. This observation is consistent with previous studies highlighting the role of trap crops in mitigating pest populations without the environmental drawbacks associated with chemical pesticides (Hokkanen 1991, Shelton and Badenes-Perez 2006, Reddy et al. 2019, Cárcamo et al. 2024). Recent reviews reinforce these benefits, showing that intercropping and volatilome manipulation in pulse systems can significantly disrupt pest host-finding behaviors (Lamzira et al. 2025) and emphasizing that traditional and cultural strategies remain central to integrated pest management in legumes such as pigeonpea (Volp et al. 2025). Although chemical treatments remain highly effective, sunn hemp may be included as a suitable trap crop in IPM programs for M. vitrata. Utilizing sunn hemp could reduce insecticide dependence, aligning with emerging trends towards ecologically sustainable crop protection, substantiated by both agroecological and biotechnological innovations, such as Genetically Modified cowpea expressing Cry1Ab (Nboyine et al. 2024).

While overall yields remained unaffected by treatment, the proportion of marketable yield differed notably within each crop and was influenced by pest management interventions. The chemical treatment significantly improved marketable yield proportions for lablab beans, followed closely by sunn hemp, highlighting the effectiveness of this intervention in reducing pest damage and preserving crop quality. This aligns with previous findings where trap cropping strategies, by mitigating direct pest damage, enhance marketable yields even without significantly affecting total production (Shelton and Badenes-Perez 2006, Parker et al. 2016, Cotes et al. 2018). In contrast, the lack of treatment effects on marketable yield proportions in cowpeas, combined with low pest pressure, suggests that intrinsic susceptibility or environmental factors beyond pest pressure may limit marketable yields in this crop. Thus, integrating sunn hemp into pest management programs appears particularly promising for crops like lablab bean, enhancing product quality sustainably and complementing or potentially reducing reliance on chemical treatments.
