Reprogramming natural killer cells in the tumor microenvironment: Challenges and therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Wenxue Ma, Sheldon Morris, Catriona Jamieson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Natural killer (NK) cells are central effectors of innate immunity, traditionally viewed as rapid responders capable of recognizing and eliminating malignant or virus-infected cells without prior sensitization. Their combined capacity to mediate direct cytotoxicity and secret cytokines has established NK cells as attractive and powerful effectors in immunotherapeutic strategies, including CAR-NK cell therapies and cytokine-based stimulation. However, recent discoveries challenge the notion of NK cells as uniformly antitumor. In certain tumor microenvironments (TME), NK cells undergo functional reprogramming into regulatory or dysfunctional phenotypes that can suppress CD8+ T cell activities and contribute to resistance against immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). These suppressive tumor-associated NK cells (TANKs) may limit antitumor immunity through cytokine competition, immune editing, and modulation of dendritic cell (DC) function. This review critically examines the dual roles of NK cells in cancer immunity, highlighting both their cytotoxic potential and their emerging immunoregulatory functions. We discuss how cytokine signaling, spatial distribution, and tumor-induced plasticity shape NK cell behavior, and evaluate the therapeutic implications of these findings. By addressing a critical knowledge gap in NK cell biology and integrating mechanistic insights with clinical perspectives, this review provides a framework for leveraging or reprogramming NK cells to enhance cancer immunotherapy outcomes.

Introduction

Natural killer (NK) cells are innate lymphocytes that play a pivotal role in immune surveillance, particularly in the recognition and elimination of malignant or virus-infected cells without prior antigen sensitization [1,2]. Their intrinsic cytotoxicity and cytokine production, particularly interferon-γ (IFN-γ), have historically positioned NK cells as a frontline defense against tumorigenesis [3,4].

In recent years, NK cells have been increasingly recognized as part of the broader innate lymphoid cell (ILC) family, which also includes ILC1, ILC2, and ILC3 subsets. All ILCs arise from common lymphoid progenitors and share certain developmental pathways [5,6]; however, NK cells are distinguished by their dependence on the transcription factor Eomesodermin (Eomes), their capacity to circulate, and their potent cytotoxic effector functions. Integrating NK cells into the ILC framework underscores both their shared developmental and functional plasticity with other ILC subsets and their unique molecular and functional signatures [7]. This broader perspective, highlighted in several recent reviews, provides an updated conceptual lens for understanding NK cell identity and behavior in cancer.

Over the past two decades, NK cells have attracted increasing attention in cancer immunotherapy due to their major histocompatibility complex (MHC)-unrestricted tumor killing capability and their ability to bridge innate and adaptive immunity [8]. A wide range of therapeutic strategies including adoptive NK cell transfer, chimeric antigen receptors (CAR)-NK cells, and cytokine-based stimulation (e.g., IL-2, IL-15) are currently under clinical investigation, showing notable efficacy in hematological malignancies and emerging potential in solid tumors [9,10].

However, recent studies challenge the conventional view of NK cells as uniformly antitumor. Within the suppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), intratumoral NK cells may lose cytotoxic capacity, acquire regulatory phenotypes, or even inhibit CD8+ T cell function [11,12]. Tumor-associated NK cells (TANKs), present in both the peripheral blood and tumors (tumor-infiltrating NK cells, TINKs), have been shown to compete with T cells for critical cytokines such as IL-15, thereby limiting cytotoxic lymphocyte expansion and reducing the efficacy of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) checkpoint blockade [11]. In addition, NK cells can impair DC-mediated antigen presentation, thereby limiting CD4+ T cell responses [13]. Collectively, these findings reveal that NK cells function not only as cytotoxic effectors but also as immunoregulator cells, adding an underappreciated layer of complexity to cancer immunity.

Despite these emerging insights, a critical gap remains in our understanding of the context-dependent dual roles of NK cells, particularly their capacity to either promote or suppress antitumor immunity [14]. Most existing reviews to date focus on the antitumor properties of NK cells or the development of NK-based therapies [15–17], but fewer address their paradoxical roles in immune suppression, tumor immune escape, and therapy resistance [3,18].

In this review, we critically examine the dual nature of NK cells in cancer, focusing on their interaction with cytokine networks, regulation of CD8+ T cell responses, and emerging functions as both therapeutic effectors and barriers. We highlight recent mechanistic insights that redefine NK cell biology, explore their clinical implications for immunotherapy, and propose strategies to reprogram NK cells to maximize therapeutic benefit. By addressing this overlooked dualism, this review offers a timely perspective for optimizing NK cell-directed approaches in the era of precision immunotherapy.

Canonical antitumor functions of NK cells

NK cells are traditionally regarded as innate immune effectors capable of eliminating malignant or virus-infected cells without prior sensitization [3,13]. Unlike T cells, NK cells do not require MHC-restricted antigen presentation, allowing them to respond rapidly against transformed or stressed cells [19]. Their antitumor effects arise through direct cytotoxicity and the secretion of immunomodulatory cytokines [20].

NK cells primarily eliminate tumor cells by releasing cytolytic granules containing perforin and granzymes [21]. Perforin forms pores in the target cell membrane, enabling granzymes entry and induction of apoptosis via both caspase-dependent and caspase-independent pathways [22]. In addition, NK cells also express death-inducing ligands such as Fas ligand (FasL) and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL), which engage their respective receptors on tumor cells to activate extrinsic apoptotic signaling [23,24].

In addition to direct killing, NK cells secrete proinflammatory cytokines, most notably IFN-γ and TNF-α [25]. IFN-γ enhances antigen presentation, promotes T helper type 1 (Th1)-polarized adaptive responses, and upregulates MHC class I expression on tumor cells, thereby sensitizing them to T cell-mediated killing [26,27]. TNF-α contributes to immune cell recruitment and, under certain conditions, can directly induce tumor necrosis [28,29].

NK cell activation is governed by a finely tuned balance between activating and inhibitory receptors [2,30]. Activating receptors such as natural killer group 2 member D (NKG2D) and NKp46 (also known as NCR1) recognize stress-induced ligands on tumor cells, whereas inhibitory receptors such as killer-cell immunoglobulin-like receptors (KIRs) and natural killer group 2 member A (NKG2A) bind to self-MHC class I molecules to prevent autoreactivity, a process termed “missing-self” recognition [31,32]. Antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), mediated by CD16 (FcγRIIIa), represents another critical pathway for NK-cell killing of antibody-coated tumor cells and underlies the clinical efficacy of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies [33,34]. At the molecular level, NK cell activation involves the integration of signaling cascades downstream of multiple receptor families. Engagement of NKG2D with stress-induced ligands activates the DAP10-PI3K-Grb2/Vav1 pathway, leading to cytoskeletal rearrangement and degranulation [2,35]. Natural cytotoxicity receptors (e.g., NKp30, NKp44, NKp46) signal through ITAM-containing adaptor proteins such as CD3ζ and FcRγ, recruiting Syk/ZAP70 kinases to initiate downstream MAPK and NF-κB pathways [36]. CD16 engagement during ADCC activates both Syk/ZAP70 and PLCγ-Ca2+ signaling, promoting robust cytotoxic responses and cytokine release. In parallel, inhibitory receptors (e.g., KIRs, NKG2A) contain ITIM motifs that recruit phosphatases (SHP-1/2) to dampen activating signals, thereby establishing a dynamic signaling balance [37]. These pathways are further modulated by transcription factors such as T-bet and Eomes, which coordinate cytotoxic gene programs, cytokine production, and NK cell maturation.

Preclinical studies consistently demonstrate NK cell-mediated tumor control, especially in hematologic malignancies [38,39]. Clinically, adoptive NK cell therapies and ADCC have shown efficacy in diseases such as acute myeloid leukemia (AML), multiple myeloma (MM), and non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) [20,40,41]. The therapeutic success of monoclonal antibodies such as rituximab and daratumumab is partly attributed to their capacity to harness NK cell-mediated ADCC [42,43]. Moreover, the development of CAR-NK cells underscores the translational promise of NK cell-based therapies, offering safety advantages and distinct effector functions compared with CAR-T cells [10,44,45].

Together, these canonical functions establish NK cells as indispensable effectors in cancer immunosurveillance and a cornerstone of innate immune-based cancer therapy [4,46]. Nevertheless, their roles are increasingly recognized as context-dependent, with growing evidence that NK cells can also contribute to immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance under specific conditions [2,11,47,48].

Emerging immunoregulatory and suppressive roles of TANKs

Although NK cells are conventionally recognized for their cytotoxic functions in tumor surveillance, recent findings reveal a more nuanced reality in which they can also acquire immunosuppressive phenotypes within the TME [3,14]. These so-called TANKs paradoxically hinder antitumor immunity, particularly by suppressing cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses that are essential for the success of immune checkpoint therapy [11].

A recent study by Song et al. [11] and Pozniak et al. [49] revealed that in melanoma with an immune-excluded phenotype, intratumoral cytotoxic NK cells can actually restrict CD8+ T cell infiltration and impair antitumor activity during immune checkpoint blockade (ICB). Using patient biopsies and mouse models, the authors showed that NK cells are recruited to immune-excluded tumor regions via the chemokine receptor CX3CR1, where they block CD8+ T cell entry and effector function. Notably, pharmacologic depletion of NK cells in these models exhibiting immune exclusion restored CD8+ T cell recruitment to the tumor core, enhanced effector activity, and improved responses to anti-PD-1 therapy [49]. These findings underscore a context-dependent immunosuppressive role of NK cells in limiting ICB efficacy.

To capture this phenomenon, we use the term NK cell-regulated CD8+ T cells (NKRT cells) to describe a subset of conventional T lymphocytes whose activation state and functional differentiation are dynamically modulated by NK cell-derived cytokines and direct cell-cell interactions [11]. More specifically, these NKRT cells represent a transitional CD8+ T cell population whose effector maturation is shaped by NK-mediated regulation and by competition for shared resources such as IL-15, a dependency that distinguishes them from other T cell subsets [50,51]. Together, these findings define a critical NK cell-regulated checkpoint in CD8+ T cell differentiation, reinforcing the suppressive role of TANKs in the TME.

Supporting this concept, transcriptomic analyses of human melanoma have revealed that intratumoral NK cells undergo extensive reprogramming, marked by elevated expression of cytokine receptors such as IL-2Rβ (CD122) and IL-15Rα (CD215), along with inhibitory receptors including NKG2A and TIGIT [52,53]. Functionally, these NK cells display reduced production of perforin and granzymes, but secrete immunomodulatory cytokines, suggesting a shift toward a regulatory-like or exhausted phenotype [2,3,54,55]. Single-cell RNA sequencing coupled with spatial profiling has further shown that such immunoregulatory NK populations preferentially localize within T cell-rich tumor regions, positioning them as key modulators of T cell responses in immune-inflamed tumors [56,57].

Together, these findings reshape our understanding of NK cell plasticity in cancer. Rather than serving exclusively as cytotoxic effectors, TANKs actively shape the immunological landscape of the TME by restricting cytokine availability and restraining CD8+ T cell function at critical developmental checkpoints [11,50]. This emerging paradigm highlights the need to reconsider NK cells not only as therapeutic agents but also as potential obstacles to effective immunotherapy.

Cytokine and growth factor regulation in NK cell function

Cytokines and growth factors are central regulators of NK cell activation, differentiation, and functional plasticity [2,13]. Within the TME, the cytokine milieu profoundly shapes NK cell behavior, promoting either cytotoxic responses or suppressive phenotypes that limit effective antitumor immunity [58,59].

Among the most critical cytokines for NK cell homeostasis and activation are interleukin-15 (IL-15) and interleukin-2 (IL-2) [60]. IL-15 is indispensable for NK cell development and survival, acting primarily through trans-presentation by IL-15Rα-expressing cells [61]. However, competition for IL-15 can yield immunoregulatory outcomes. Recent studies, including Song et al. [11] demonstrate that NK cells with high-affinity IL-15 receptor expression can outcompete CD8+ T cells, thereby restricting their expansion and maturation into effective cytotoxic lymphocytes [9,11]. This “cytokine sink” effect contributes to NK cell-mediated suppression of T cell immunity and resistance to checkpoint blockade [17,62].

At the signaling level, IL-15 engagement of IL-2Rβ and the common γ chain (γc) activates JAK1 and JAK3, leading to STAT5 phosphorylation and the induction of genes controlling survival (e.g., Bcl-2), proliferation, and effector functions [9,63]. IL-2 signals through the same receptor components, but high-affinity IL-2Rα expression on regulatory T cells provides them with a competitive advantage at low cytokine concentrations [64]. The balance of these signaling events determines whether NK cells receive sufficient cues to maintain cytotoxic function or are functionally outcompeted by other lymphocyte populations [65]. Furthermore, cytokine signaling is tightly integrated with metabolic checkpoints: IL-15-JAK-STAT5 signaling enhances mTOR activity, supporting NK cell biosynthetic and cytotoxic programs [66]. IL-2 exerts a similarly complex role in NK cell biology [3,9,67]. At low doses, IL-2 preferentially expands regulatory T (Treg) cells, whereas high doses promote NK cell activation [68,69]. TANKs may sustain their presence within the TME by exploiting limited cytokine resources, thereby exacerbating competition and restricting CD8+ T cell responses. Therapeutic administration of IL-2 or IL-15 superagonists is being explored to boost lymphocyte activity; however, without careful targeting, these interventions may also expand NK subsets with regulatory or dysfunctional properties. For example, low-dose IL-2 preferentially drives Treg expansion due to their high-affinity IL-2 receptor [70]. Similarly, IL-15 superagonists such as ALT-803/N-803 stimulate robust NK and CD8+ T cell proliferation but may also expand heterogeneous NK populations with divergent, sometimes suppressive, functions [71].

Beyond IL-2 and IL-15, transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) is a potent immunosuppressive cytokine that directly impairs NK cell cytotoxicity [72]. TGF-β suppresses NK effector function by downregulating activating receptors such as NKp30 and NKG2D, and thus reprogramming NK cells into type 1 innate lymphoid cell (ILC1)-like, non-cytotoxic, or tissue-resident states [14,48,73]. Elevated intratumoral TGF-β levels are associated with reduced NK cell function and infiltration, thereby reinforcing an immune-evasive TME [72,74].

At the transcriptional level, cytokine and growth factor signals converge on key transcription factors that orchestrate NK cell fate [13]. STAT5 is essential for IL-15-driven survival and cytotoxicity, whereas STAT3 can mediate suppressive effects downstream of IL-10 or chronic type I IFN exposure [75]. T-bet and Eomesodermin (Eomes) regulate terminal differentiation and effector function, with T-bet promoting cytotoxicity and IFN-γ production, and Eomes supporting long-term persistence and tissue residency [76]. TGF-β signaling through SMAD2/3 antagonizes these programs, driving the transcriptional reprogramming of NK cells toward less cytotoxic, ILC1-like phenotypes [77]. Understanding this transcriptional circuitry is crucial for designing cytokine-based interventions that sustain NK cell effector functions within the TME.

Other soluble cytokines and growth factors, such as IL-10, IL-21, and type I IFNs (IFN-α/β), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) also exert context-dependent effects on NK cells [78–80]. For example, IL-21 enhances NK cytotoxicity and memory-like responses under acute exposure but promotes dysfunction when chronic [81,82]. Likewise, VEGF indirectly suppresses NK function by remodeling stromal and vascular compartments, thereby reducing immune infiltration [83,84].

Altogether, the interplay between soluble mediators and NK subsets defines a critical axis of immune regulation in cancer [85,86]. Tumor-driven modulation of cytokine availability, receptor expression, and signaling thresholds can tip NK cell function toward either effector or suppressive states [2,3,87]. From a therapeutic perspective, strategies that selectively tune cytokine delivery such as IL-15 superagonists, IL-2 muteins, or TGF-β blockade hold promise to reprogram NK cells toward sustained antitumor activity while minimizing immunosuppressive effects [88,89].

NK cells as modulators of the TME

In addition to their direct cytotoxic functions, NK cells play a dynamic role in shaping the TME through complex interactions with immune and stromal cells [90,91]. Depending on their phenotypic state and spatial localization, these interactions can either enhance or suppress antitumor immunity [92,93]. Increasingly, NK cells are recognized not only as killers of tumor cells but also regulators of immune architecture within the TME [94,95].

A key example of this regulatory role is NK cell interaction with DCs. In early-stage tumors or inflamed tissues, NK cells promote DC maturation through IFN-γ and TNF-α secretion, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and CD8+ T cell priming [96,97]. In contrast, suppressive NK cell subsets within the TME can impair DC recruitment or skew differentiation toward tolerogenic phenotypes. These dual roles position NK cells as key upstream regulators of adaptive immunity [98,99].

NK cells also engage with tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and Tregs [100–102]. Through cytokine such as IL-10 and TGF-β, or inhibitory receptors including NKG2A, TIGIT, NK cells reinforce immunosuppressive feedback loops that sustain tumor immune evasion [12,48]. Under chronic stimulation, NK-derived IL-10 or TGF-β can polarize macrophages toward an M2-like phenotype and suppress effector T cell recruitment or activation [103–105].

Emerging evidence indicates that NK cells also contribute to immune editing, whereby immune pressure selects for tumor cell variants with reduced immunogenicity [106,107]. NK cytotoxicity eliminates susceptible tumor cells but spares variants lacking stress ligands (e.g., MICA/B, ULBP) or expressing inhibitory ligands (e.g., HLA-E, PD-L1) [48,108]. This selective pressure not only facilitates tumor escape but also complicates the efficacy of ICB [31].

The spatial distribution of NK cells within tumors critically influences their immunomodulatory capacity [55,109]. Spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed imaging have shown that NK cells often reside in perivascular or stromal regions rather than infiltrating tumor nests [2,48,110]. This restricted localization limits their access to tumor cells and immune partners, thereby reducing effector function [111]. In addition, NK cells in these niches are exposed to suppressive cues such as TGF-β and hypoxia, which reinforce dysfunctional or regulatory phenotypes [112,113]. In parallel, the TME subjects NK cells to metabolic stressors such as glucose deprivation and hypoxia, which impair mitochondrial function and reduce cytotoxic capacity [90,114]. These conditions also drive transcriptional reprogramming toward immune-tolerant states [115]. Thus, NK cell phenotype is regulated not only by cytokine and cellular interactions but also by metabolic and physical barriers imposed by the TME [92,116].

Collectively, these findings underscore the multifaceted roles of NK cells in orchestrating immune responses within the TME [117]. Far from being solely cytotoxic effectors, NK cells exhibit remarkable plasticity in the TME, where they can either promote antitumor immunity or under tumor-driven constraints, adopt suppressive phenotypes that sustain immune evasion and tumor progression [14,118–120].

Therapeutic implications and strategies to harness or reprogram NK cells

The growing recognition of NK cell plasticity in the TME presents both opportunities and challenges for cancer immunotherapy [121, 122]. While NK cells offer rapid, MHC-independent cytotoxicity and a lower risk of graft-versus-host disease (GVHD), the clinical translation of NK-based therapies particularly in solid tumors has been limited by poor in vivo persistence, restricted tumor infiltration, and suppressive reprogramming by the TME [94,123].

Current limitations in NK cell immunotherapy arise from several factors. In solid tumors, NK cells frequently acquire dysfunctional phenotypes driven by chronic antigen stimulation, hypoxia, and exposure to immunosuppressive cytokines such as TGF-β [124,125]. Furthermore, the lack of sustained cytokine support within the TME restricts NK cell survival and expansion [126,127]. As a result, clinical responses have been modest and remain largely confined to hematologic malignancies, highlighting the urgent need for strategies tailored to solid tumors [14, 128,129].

To overcome these barriers, ongoing efforts aim to reprogram suppressive NK subsets into functional effectors [20,130]. One approach is to block inhibitory receptors such as NKG2A, TIGIT, and KIRs, which are frequently upregulated on TANKs [131–133]. Monoclonal antibodies targeting these checkpoints including NKG2A (monalizumab), KIRs (lirilumab), and TIGIT (tiragolumab, vibostolimab) have demonstrated promise in preclinical studies and early-phase clinical trials, either as monotherapies or in combination with PD-1/PD-L1 blockade [133–137]. Another strategy focuses on cytokine modulation, using IL-15 superagonists or IL-2 variants engineered to avoid Treg activation while enhancing NK cell persistence and cytotoxicity [9,138]. Finally, metabolic reconditioning such as targeting hypoxia-inducible pathways or restoring mitochondrial fitness represents an emerging frontier to sustain NK cell activity within metabolically hostile TME [139–141].

The combination of NK-based strategies with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) holds significant potential, particularly in settings where NK cells suppress CD8+ T cell responses through cytokine competition [142,143]. Concurrently releasing both NK- and T cell-mediated inhibition could synergize to remodel the immune landscape, as evidenced by preclinical models, in which NK depletion enhanced anti-PD-1 efficacy [144,145].

Emerging NK cell platforms are also advancing the therapeutic frontier. CAR-NK cells combine antigen specificity with innate cytotoxic mechanisms and offer safety advantages over CAR-T cells [10,45,146, 147]. Bispecific and trispecific killer engagers (BiKEs/TriKEs) are antibody-based constructs that link NK cells to tumor antigens while delivering cytokine co-stimulation, such as IL-15, to boost persistence and effector function [148,149]. Likewise, IL-15-anchored immunocytokines provide localized NK stimulation within the TME, reducing systemic toxicity while amplifying antitumor activity [4,150].

Altogether, these innovative strategies aim to unlock the full therapeutic potential of NK cells, converting them from passive or suppressive actors into active drivers of antitumor immunity. Knowledge gaps and future directions

Despite growing recognition of the dual roles of NK cells in cancer, several critical gaps still hinder the integration into clinical immunotherapy strategies [151,152]. Addressing these gaps is essential to translate mechanistic insights into therapeutic innovations that more effectively harness or reprogram NK cells.

A persistent limitation is the absence of specific, and standardized phenotypic markers that reliably distinguish immunosuppressive NK subsets, particularly TANKs [153]. Although recent transcriptomic and proteomic studies have identified potential candidates, such as NKG2A, TIGIT, and high-affinity cytokine receptors their expression remains heterogeneous and frequently overlaps with conventional NK subsets [154]. This lack of clarity not only hampers diagnostic profiling but also complicates the rational design of targeted therapies. Moving forward, it will be essential to develop robust marker panels capable of consistently differentiating suppressive NK cells from cytotoxic counterparts across diverse tumor types and disease stages [155].

Another barrier is the limited availability of preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate NK-mediated immunoregulation in the TME [50]. Traditional murine systems fail to capture the complexity of human NK cell diversity or their complex interactions with T cells, stromal components, and cytokine gradients. Advanced platforms, including humanized mouse models, organotypic tumor spheroids, and patient-derived xenografts (PDX) with preserved immune niches are urgently needed to evaluate the context-dependent NK functions and to test NK-modulating therapies in vivo [2].

The dynamic and heterogeneous nature of intratumoral NK cells for longitudinal spatial and single cell profiling in patient samples [156]. Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), spatial transcriptomics, and multiplexed imaging technologies have begun to illuminate NK state evolution [157]. However, these approaches have not yet been widely applied in large, therapy-relevant cohorts. Longitudinal studies are especially critical to determine how NK cell phenotypes evolve in response to treatment and how they contribute to resistance or relapse, particularly in patients receiving ICIs or cytokine therapies [158].

Finally, the complexity of NK cell biology suggests that a personalized approach will be required for optimal therapeutic efficacy. Rather than uniformly activating or expanding NK cells, future strategies may need to selectively suppress regulatory NK subsets while enhancing effector populations [2,20,152]. Biomarker-guided interventions based on tumor cytokine signatures, NK receptor expression, or spatial localization could enable tailored interventions. Integrating such precision strategies with CAR-NK cells, BiKEs/TriKEs, and cytokine-based therapies has the potential to improve both safety and efficacy in solid tumors [44,159,160].

As the field advances, NK cells must be redefined not as static players, but as highly plastic, context-sensitive regulators of tumor-immune dynamics. Bridging mechanistic discoveries with clinical translation will require coordinated efforts across immunology, oncology, bioengineering, and systems biology [2,118,161].

Conclusions

NK cells are increasingly recognized as complex, and context-dependent players in cancer immunology [13]. While long celebrated for their rapid, MHC-independent cytotoxicity, recent evidence reveals that NK cells are not uniformly beneficial. Within the TME, they can acquire suppressive phenotypes that actively impair antitumor immunity, particularly by limiting CD8+ T cell responses and shaping the broader immune landscape [50].

This review has highlighted the dichotomous functions of NK cells, ranging from their canonical role in tumor surveillance to their emerging capacity to mediate immunoregulation and resistance to ICB. We examined molecular and environmental factors, including cytokine availability, metabolic constraints, and receptor-ligand interactions, that govern NK cell fate and function in tumors. Therapeutic innovations designed to reprogram suppressive NK subsets or to enhance NK cell activity are rapidly advancing, with innovations such as CAR-NK cells, BiKEs, TriKEs, and cytokine-engineered platforms [110,153].

Nevertheless, critical knowledge gaps remain. There is an urgent need to distinguish NK cell subsets (e.g., CD56brightCD16dim and CD56dimCD16bright NK cells) [110], alongside more reliable, predictive preclinical models, and deeper clinical profiling to guide precision NK-targeted interventions [153]. CD56dimCD16bright NK cells, the predominant peripheral blood population, are highly cytotoxic, whereas CD56brightCD16dim NK cells specialize in cytokine release and immune regulation [110,162]. Unlike adaptive lymphocytes, NK cells uniquely provide rapid effector responses without prior antigen priming or MHC restriction [163]. Harnessing this functional duality and plasticity may transform NK cells from unpredictable bystanders into precision-guided allies in cancer immunotherapy [164].

Looking ahead, single-cell and spatial technologies will be instrumental in uncovering novel NK cell states and context-dependent functions within the TME, paving the way for new biomarkers and therapeutic targets [165]. Advances in genetic engineering including CAR-NK and multispecific engagers are likely to expand NK-based therapies beyond hematologic malignancies to solid tumors [166]. Ultimately, integrating cytokine biology, checkpoint modulation, and adoptive NK therapies into rational combination regimens may establish NK cells as central players in next-generation immuno-oncology [20].
