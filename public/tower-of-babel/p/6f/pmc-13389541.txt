Experimental and numerical investigation of short carbon fiber reinforced PA66 spur gears for fatigue performance optimization
Authors: Dilip S Choudhari, Rahul G Deshmukh, Yogesh G Joshi, G D Gosavi, Sarang P Joshi, Mangesh D Shende, Arunkumar Bongale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing demand for lightweight and high performance mechanical components has accelerated the implementation of composite materials as alternatives to conventional metals. In this study, polyamide-66 spur gears reinforced with short carbon fibers (SCF) were investigated as lightweight alternatives to conventional metallic gears using an integrated numerical–experimental framework. Spur gears containing 0–60 wt% SCF were manufactured by injection molding and evaluated using FEA and fatigue testing on a custom gear test rig via Taguchi-based design of experiments. Static and dynamic FEA were employed to determine deformation, equivalent stress and fatigue life under operational torque and speed. Results show that increasing SCF content improves stiffness and fatigue resistance up to 40 wt%. The experimentally measured fatigue life increased from 2.63 × 10⁵ cycles for neat PA66 to 17.05 × 105 cycles for 40 wt% SCF/PA66, representing an improvement of approximately 6.5 times. Further increase in SCF content degrades the mechanical performance, which is attributed to increased brittleness. The 40 wt% SCF/PA66 composite showed minimum total deformation (0.1627 mm) and maximum simulated fatigue life (18.7 × 105 cycles), which closely matches experimental results (17.05 × 105 cycles). Taguchi optimization found material composition as the principal parameter governing fatigue life, with 40 wt% SCF at 1135 rpm producing the highest signal-to-noise ratio. The maximum operating temperature recorded at the gear tooth interface (82.6 °C) remained noticeably below the composite melting temperature (260.9 °C), which confirms thermal stability during operation. This study identifies 40 wt% SCF/PA66 as the optimal material composition and provides insight into the fatigue behaviour of fiber-reinforced polymer gears used in lightweight transmission systems.Keywords: Spur gear, Finite element analysis, Experimental, Gear test rig, Fatigue failure lifeSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Gears made of Carbon Fiber reinforced Polymer (CFRP) composite material are widely used in automotive industry, aircraft, transportation, sporting goods, industry, marine, and defense industries. Moreover, there are certain uses of these materials in a pharmaceutical machinery/equipment and food processing machinery. The widespread adoption of CFRP gears can majorly be related to their good mechanical properties that encompass low density, exceptional wear resistance, excellent modulus, decreased noise, and improved sound absorption1. Compared to long carbon fibers, short carbon fibers are much easier to disperse into the polymer matrix and to be compacted uniformly and their bonding with polyamide 66 (PA66) also is better. These properties allow the creation of lightweight gears that have the increased mechanical performance.

Substantial studies have been done pertaining to the gears produced out of CF reinforced with PA6, PA12, PA46 and PA66. These gears are commonly designed through the controlled injection molding. They are such processes that provide good compatibility and high fiber- matrix bonding to operate in lubricated and dry operation2–7. Dilip et al. studied the effect of an addendum modification on deformation, stress distribution and factor of safety of short carbon fiber (SCF) reinforced polyamide 66 (PA66) composite spur gears with different contents of fibers through FEA. The paper has established that addendum modification and SCF reinforcement are both expected to create an effect on the deformation behavior and strength of gears. When these parameters are optimally done, the overall performance of composite gears can be enhanced8. Kuram et al. studied the micro-machinability of glass-fiber reinforced (GFR) and Polyamide 6 (PA6) composites via micro-milling by inspecting cutting forces, tool wear, surface roughness, and burr formation. It was highlighted that the reinforcement improved the mechanical and thermal properties, but deteriorated machinability9. Luo et al. analyzed the long CF-reinforced PA6, emphasized the necessity of fiber sizing treatments to achieve desirable mechanical performance10.

Numerous research studies have also focused on enhancing the tribological behavior of polymer composites via filler integration. Mohan et al. reported that the inclusion of ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene particles and other fillers to polymer composites considerably enhanced wear resistance, reduced friction, and provided environmental friendly solutions for tribological applications11,12. Mao et al. found that for machine-cut acetal gears, the wear rate increases rapidly when the applied load exceeds a critical threshold for a given gear geometry, which highlights the sensitivity of polymer gears to loading environments13.

Progressions in computer aided engineering and numerical modeling have significantly contributed to the design and performance assessment of polymer gears. Hegazi et al. adopted a computer-aided reverse engineering technique to describe production and inspection requirements while sustaining geometric constraints14. S. Senthilvelan et al. extended previous method to injection molded short glass fiber reinforced PA66 spur gears using three-dimensional modeling and mold flow simulations15. Previous researchers reported that time-varying gear mesh stiffness can effectively represent the dynamic behavior of gear systems. Their results showed good agreement with finite element simulations. However, reduced mesh stiffness may lead to excessive tooth deflection16,17. Cerne et al. observed the methodical gear meshing by simulating a complete structural contact analysis using a FEM18, whereas Naveen et al. and Li et al. used innovative nonlinear FEM methods to examine deformation, stress distribution and gear tooth contact behavior19,20. Investigational estimation of polymer gears has also been widely reported. Dearn et al. presented an advanced testing technique for polymer gears that reduces testing time and cycle count while confirming reliable performance assessment21.

Numerous investigators developed in-house experiment set-ups capable of applying torque with controlled misalignment to estimate power transmission efficiency, frictional losses and surface wear under realistic operating environments22,23. Aljaz Pogacnik et al. reported that increasing tooth face width considerably improves gear life24, whereas Hayrettin Duzcukoglu et al. experimentally compared reinforced and unreinforced spur gears using a power-absorption test rig25. The latter were outlined by Ken Mao et al. who stated that glass fiber reinforced gears exhibit about 50% of increased load carrying capacity as compared to the non-reinforced polyoxymethylene gears26. Also, there were numerous works demonstrating that wear resistance, service life and load carrying capacity are high when the operating temperature of polyamide composition gears is reduced27,28.

Polymer gears have been widely researched on failure mechanisms to enhance durability and reliability. Bharti Sarita et al. showed that crowding fracturing in the surface of the gear root and then the debris detachment and cratering had been observed thus the need to pay attention to a longer endurance test29. Other research studies reported subsurface cracking in the pitch region and scuffing in the flank region under dry and lubricated conditions, respectively30,31. It has also been stated that self-lubricated polymer gears often fail initially by fatigue, followed by progressive wear. Here, the fatigue life can be measured directly, whereas wear evolution needs continuous monitoring32,33. Investigations on acetal gears shown that failures mainly occur due to root fractures and pitch-line surface wear under varying load conditions34,35. In addition, gear teeth have been shown to possess higher tensile strength than compressive strength, with failure normally originating at the tooth root on both tensile and compressive sides36–39.

More recently, Dilip et al. conducted thorough failure analysis of polymer composite gears using field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM), which provides high-resolution insights into surface morphology, fracture behavior, and microstructural damage40. Consequent SEM examinations of worn gear surfaces exposed matrix damage, fiber breakage, fiber pull-out, and the formation of matrix debris, offers a deeper understanding of wear and failure mechanisms in fiber-reinforced polymer gears41,42.

Previous investigations have primarily focused on either the tribological behavior, mechanical properties, or numerical modeling of polymer composite gears, often considering limited reinforcement compositions or relying only on simulation-based approaches. However, a systematic experimental–numerical investigation covering a wide range of SCF reinforcement levels in PA66 spur gears, together with fatigue life validation and microstructural failure analysis, remains largely unexplored. Therefore, the present study aims to bridge this research gap by combining finite element analysis, experimental fatigue testing, Taguchi optimization, and FESEM characterization to identify the optimal SCF/PA66 composition and to understand the associated fatigue failure mechanisms.

Novelty of research work

The novelty of present work lies in the broad examination of SCF/PA66 composites across the full reinforcement range of 0–60% SCF, an area not formerly explored for spur gear applications. This combined method fills an important research gap and supports the development of high performance composite spur gears for light and medium-duty applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

SCF was regarded as reinforcing phase because they are of high strength to weight ratio, stiffness and inherent brittle property, thereby making them to bear greater load in polymer composites. The SCF employed in this study had a mean diameter of 60 μm and a length on average of 6 mm. These fiber sizes are chosen due to the fact that short carbon fibres in the length of a number of millimeters and diameter of just a few micrometers are normally utilized in injection-molded thermoplastic composites. These dimensions are sufficiently balanced between reinforcement effectiveness, dispersion of the polymer structure and the producibility of intricate components like gears. Unrealistically long fibers could cause problems in the process, and the fibers could broke during molding, but on the other hand, very short fibers could increase the load transfer between the matrix and reinforcement. These fibers were supplied by Formosa Plastics, Taiwan, and are presented in Fig. 1a. The selected PA66 grade represents a commercially available engineering thermoplastic widely used in gear and tribological applications due to its favorable combination of strength, wear resistance, and thermal stability. The PA66 was provided in pellet form with an average diameter of 1.50 mm and a length of 2.00 mm, as shown in Fig. 1b. Owing to its desirable mechanical strength, thermal stability and tribological performance, PA66 is widely used in engineering components such as gears, bearings, cams, bushings, handles, sheaths, cables, and wires. The PA66 material used in the current work was supplied by Solvay Chemical, South Korea.

Experimental test rig

The experimental test rig was developed to estimate the performance and fatigue life of spur gears under both loaded and unloaded operating circumstances. The system is compact, user-friendly and explicitly designed for composite gear testing, which offers operational simplicity compared to conventional universal gear test rigs. The setup includes three main units. The first unit offers input power through a 2 HP AC motor. The second unit transmits the required torque and rotational speed to the spur gear pair, with provisions to vary both parameters according to the examination requirements. The third unit comprises of the gear testing assembly, where the composite spur gear pair is mounted and assessed under controlled loading conditions. Load measurement is accomplished using a carbon fiber Ferrodo belt mounted on a brake dynamometer drum positioned adjacent to the test gears. Two adjustable load cells are integrated to confirm accurate torque measurement. Under maximum speed conditions, the gear pair operates at 1135 rpm with a torque of 10.50 Nm. The actual and schematic views of the experimental gear test rig are presented in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively.

Experimental test procedure

After satisfactory design and fabrication, the number of spur gears is tested on the test rig. The life of spur gears is measured under different load conditions. The running timing of the gears is noted down. Each combination of %SCF in PA66 has a different load-taking capacity. These gears are run at a constant speed, and torque is applied till their failure. Experimentally, fatigue life in several cycles before failure of different %SCF/PA66 composite spur gears is recorded. The detailed experimental procedure is provided in Fig. 6.

To understand the plastic behavior and the deterioration of gears, the temperature of gears is measured and monitored during fatigue tests. Initially, the temperature is measured at the contact surfaces of the gear pair by an Infrared thermometer. The infrared thermometer was positioned at an approximate distance of 10–15 cm, emissivity of 0.95 with sampling frequency of 60 min from the gear tooth contact region to ensure consistent measurement without interference from the rotating components. Temperature readings were recorded at regular intervals during the fatigue tests to monitor the thermal behavior of the gears throughout the experiment. Meshing strategy and element selection

In the finite element model, the ANSYS SOLID186 hexahedral element was used. This higher-order 20-node element possesses three degrees of freedom at each node, which corresponds to translational displacements in the x, y, and z directions. The sweep meshing method was used to confirm structured element distribution throughout the gear geometry. An element size of 0.2 mm was defined in the contact face region, while a finer mesh size of 0.07 mm was applied near the fillet region to precisely capture stress gradients. The generated gear assembly mesh consisted of 36,379 elements and 172,102 nodes. The meshed model of the spur gear assembly is provided in Fig. 8. Mesh quality was evaluated and controlled using standard quality metrics, which includes parallel deviation, warping factor, skewness and aspect ratio, to ensure numerical stability and solution accuracy. To ascertain the accuracy of the numerical data, mesh convergence test was conducted by increasing and decreasing the meshes and testing the change of main response parameters, i.e. total deformation, von Mises stress and the predicted fatigue life of the critical part of the gear basically the tooth root. Three densities of the mesh were discussed by changing the size of elements on the points of contact and on the fillets. The findings have shown that a smaller size of the element around the fillet area less than 0.07 mm led to less than 23% difference in total deformation, equivalent stress and fatigue life prognosis than going one step further and additional refine the mesh. This agreed that the numerical solution was mesh independent. Thus, the final mesh structure was selected 36,379 elements and 172,102 nodes, which ensures the right balance of the computational efficiency and the accuracy of the solution (Table 3).

Design of experiments (Taguchi method)

To systematically analyze and optimize the fatigue performance of SCF/PA66 composite spur gears, a Design of Experiments (DoE) approach based on the Taguchi method was adopted. In the current study, the Taguchi method is used as a post-processing optimization framework to assess the effect of selected input parameters on the fatigue life obtained from finite element simulations.

Based on initial FEA and practical operating conditions of the spur gear pair, two control factors were selected for the DoE, namely material composition (%SCF in PA66) and operating speed. Each factor was considered at two levels, as these ranges were found to be adequate to capture the variation in fatigue behavior without introducing unnecessary computational complexity. The response parameter chosen for optimization is the fatigue life of the spur gear, which is expressed in terms of the number of cycles to failure. An L4 (2²) orthogonal array was selected for the current examination, as it allows effective estimation of the selected factors and their individual influences with a minimal number of simulation runs. The Taguchi technique allows separation of the effects of control factors from experimental noise and offers a structured approach to identify the most important parameters governing fatigue life.

The characteristic of quality of the response was determined in the terms of the larger-is-better criterion because the greater is the fatigue life the greater is the reliable functioning of spur gears. The fatigue life values obtained based on finite element simulations of each trial in the orthogonal array were found to be further used to calculate the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratios and average response values. Such analyses allow determining the best material composition and operating speed that can be used to achieve better fatigue performance. The complete outcome of the Taguchi analysis inclusive of S/N ratio consideration, main effects plot and identification of the best set of parameters are presented and discussed within the next Results and Discussion section.

Results and discussion

Results and discussion of FEA, and experimental performance testing of spur gears are carried out. The results obtained by these approaches are compared, discussed, and analyzed for validation. The results are found in close approximation among different approaches, and variations of results are analyzed with proper technical justification.

Static FEA analysis for total deformation and equivalent stresses

The total deformation contours obtained from finite element simulations of spur gears fabricated with varying SCF/PA66 compositions show a noticeable dependence of structural stiffness on fiber reinforcement. As the SCF content upsurges from 0% to 40%, a progressive decrease in total deformation is detected, which indicates enhanced load-carrying capability and improved resistance to elastic deflection under the applied torque. The minimum total deformation of 0.1627 mm is attained for the 40% SCF/PA66 composite gear. Beyond this reinforcement level, a slight rise or near-saturation in deformation is observed, which can be attributed to increased brittleness and reduced matrix continuity at higher fiber loadings41,42. The equivalent von Mises stress values for composite spur gears containing 0% to 40% SCF/PA66 ranges from 29.432 MPa to 69.81 MPa, with the maximum equivalent stress of 69.81 MPa noted for the 40% SCF/PA66 composition. For higher fiber contents up to 60% SCF/PA66, the equivalent stress decreases up to 49.393 MPa. Fatigue life analysis by dynamic FEA

The fatigue life contours obtained from the finite element simulations noticeably show the influence of SCF reinforcement on the durability of composite spur gears. As the SCF content upsurges from 00% to 40% within the PA66 matrix, a substantial improvement in fatigue life is observed. This improvement can be explained by a higher stiffness of the composite, better redistribution of stresses as well as an improvement in interfacial load transfer between the fibers and the matrix. Maximum life of fatigue is always predicted to occur in the 40% SCF/PA66 mixture. However, an appreciable decrease in the fatigue life is realized beyond this reinforcement level. The primary connection of this decrease is with heightened material brittleness and local concentrated stress, both bad influences on the crack initiation and resistance to propagation. The estimation of the fatigue life of spur gear pairs under the conditions of PA66 with SCF at 00% to 60% was done by using dynamic FEA. Experimental fatigue life analysis

The performance evaluation of the composite spur gears was done using a modification of weight% of SCF reinforcement in PA66 matrix. The constant torque, which is 8.250 Nm, was provided to the working pairs of composite gears that had a rotational rate of 1135 rpm. All gear pairs were also subjected to continuous running until they failed and the related run time was measured to identify the fatigue life. The fatigue life of the spur gear pairs obtained experimentally was 2.63 × 105 cycles in the case of 00% SCF/PA66 and 17.05 × 105 cycles in the case of 40% SCF/PA66. This corresponds to an improvement of approximately 6.5 times in fatigue life compared with neat PA66, clearly demonstrating the beneficial effect of short carbon fiber reinforcement on the durability of the composite spur gears. Beyond this composition, the fatigue life reduced to 8.57 × 10⁵ cycles for 60% SCF/PA66. The maximum fatigue life of 17.05 × 10⁵ cycles was therefore attained for the 40% SCF/PA66 composite, which indicates an optimal reinforcement level. Temperature measurement during fatigue test

The gear pairs fabricated with 00%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%, and 60% SCF/PA66 failed after 232, 368, 515, 814, 1502, 476, and 755 min of operation, respectively, under the specified trial conditions. Among these, the 40% SCF/PA66 composite gear showed the longest service life and was thus known as the optimal composition. This gear functioned at a rotational speed of 1135 rpm under a transmitted load of 110 N. The pinion, functioning as the driving member rotated in the clockwise direction, whereas the gear performed as the driven component in the gear system. The maximum operating temperature recorded during the investigational trial was 82.60 °C (Fig. 34). In contrast, the melting temperature of the same composite material, determined via Differential Scanning Calorimetry analysis was establish to be 260.95 °C (Fig. 35). The results noticeably show that the maximum operating temperature of the composite gears remained considerably lower than the melting temperature, which confirms that thermal softening or melting was not accountable for gear failure under the confirmed conditions.

Taguchi analysis and optimization

Taguchi optimization was carried out using an L4 (2²) orthogonal array (Table 6) to investigate the effect of composition and speed on the response characteristic (life). The Signal-to-Noise (S/N) ratio was evaluated using the larger-is-better criterion to identify the optimal factor levels. The response table for S/N ratios (Table 7) indicates that composition has the highest influence on the response, with a delta value of 5.0, followed by speed with a delta value of 0.5. This ranking confirms that composition is the most dominant parameter affecting life. Similarly, the response table for means (Table 8) shows a significantly higher delta value for composition (700,833) compared to speed (63,758), further validating the dominant effect of composition. The optimal condition corresponding to the maximum mean response is Level 2 of composition and Level 2 of speed. Overall, both S/N ratio and mean response analyses consistently indicate that composition plays a crucial role in improving life, whereas speed has a comparatively minor effect within the selected experimental range.

Surface morphology of spur gear

The fracture behavior of the failed spur gear was studied using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM). The failed gear specimens were examined after applying a thin gold coating to the fractured surface in order to improve image clarity and electrical conductivity. The investigation was performed on the optimal 40% SCF/PA66 composite specimen. Microstructural observations were obtained at magnifications of 130×, 250×, 500×, 1000×, 5000×, and 10,000× at the failure cross-section. The analysis was accompanied using an FEI Nova Nano SEM 450 FESEM system.

Prior to investigation, the spur gear sample was sectioned at the failure region to expose the fracture surface. The surface morphology of the fractured 40% SCF/PA66 spur gear is presented in Fig. 38. Thorough examination of the micrographs discloses strong interfacial adhesion between SCF and the PA66 matrix. A substantial number of carbon fibers exhibit fracture or damage and dispersed debris particles are detected within the PA66 matrix. The wide fragmentation of the PA66 matrix as evident in the FESEM images, seems to be the main source of the generated debris. The worn surface also shows matrix cracking oriented perpendicular to the sliding direction, along with noticeable plastic deformation of the matrix material. Under fatigue loading conditions, comparatively greater damage is observed in the PA66 matrix than in the reinforcing SCF. The worn surface morphology of the spur gear at various magnification levels is illustrated in Fig. 39. The SCF/PA66 composite with the highest fatigue life (17.05 × 106 cycles) the 40 wt% SCF/PA66 composite has relatively uniform distribution of fibers in the micrographs, as well as, good interfacial bonding between the fiber and the matrix. These microstructure properties facilitate easy transfer of loads between the matrix and reinforcing fibers thereby enhancement of resistance to crack initiation and propagation during cyclic loading. Conversely, composites that are low in SCF content (020 wt. percent) have fewer reinforcing fibers hence lower stiffness and a less delay period to initiation of fatigue damages. Higher reinforcement contents (50 to 60 wt% SCF) can cause the agglomeration of the fibers locally in the matrix and discontinuities that serve as stress concentration sources and also encourage precoccurring crack propagation, thus leading to the shortening of fatigue life.

Conclusion

This study presented a comprehensive numerical and experimental investigation of fiber-reinforced PA66 gears intended for lightweight gearbox applications. Spur gears with SCF contents ranging from 00 to 60 wt% were manufactured using injection molding and systematically evaluated through FEA, experimental fatigue testing on a custom-designed gear test rig, DoE using the Taguchi method, temperature monitoring and FESEM.

The statistic and dynamic FEA outcomes confirmed that deformation of gears, equal stress and life-span of fatigue is highly dependent on the level of carbon-fiber reinforcement. The addition of a reinforcement to the maximum SCF wt% of 40 wt% had a significant positive impact on gear stiffness and load carrying capacity, thus giving rise to the lowest total deformation (0.1627 mm) and longest simulated fatigue life (18.7 × 105 cycles). Above this reinforced level, mechanical performance was deteriorated by growing brittle nature and non-uniform distribution of stress that underscores the sacrifice of fracture protection and the simultaneous increase in stiffness with a higher content of fiber.

Numerical results were confirmed by experimental fatigue tests, and in the experimental conditions, the gears of 40 wt% SCF/PA66 had the highest fatigue life of 17.05 × 105 cycles. The near similarity of the predictions of FEA and the experimental results with the error of less than 9 per cent justify the credibility of the numerical model prepared. Taguchi based optimization also further came up with the material composition as the decisive one in controlling fatigue life which is only secondarily affected by the operating speed in the range of study range of operating speed. The maximum condition was found to be 40 wt% SCF with 1135 rpm.

Monitoring of the temperature of the gear tooth contacts during fatigue testing indicated that the highest gear tooth contact temperature (82.6 °C) was significantly lower than the melting temperature of the composite (260.9 °C) and was therefore thermally stable under operation. FESEM investigation of failed gears gave mechanistic understanding of fatigue damage and found dominant failure modes of fracture of fibers, matrix cracking, interfacial debonding and formation of debris especially at increased SCF contents.

It is necessary to add that optimal level of reinforcement determined during the current study is on the basis of tests managed in controlled laboratory conditions with consideration of dry operating, constant rotating speed, and constant level of torque. The tribological behavior and fatigue performance of the polymer composite gears are sensitive to environmental factors which include lubrication conditions, humidity, high temperature, and weight spectrum in the varied load. These may potentially change the optimum reinforcement level through influencing the property of matrices, fiber interaction and wear mechanisms. Hence, the inferences made under this study can be largely generalized in investigations of the operating circumstances.
