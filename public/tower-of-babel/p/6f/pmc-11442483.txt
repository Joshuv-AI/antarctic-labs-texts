Experimental Investigation and Machine Learning Prediction of Mechanical Properties of Rubberized Concrete for Sustainable Construction
Authors: T. Senthil Vadivel, Ardra Suseelan, K. Karthick, Mejdl Safran, Sultan Alfarhood
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Concrete is widely used in civil engineering applications and the natural aggregates which used in concrete are scarce, but its demand is increasing. The disposal of rubber tyres poses a significant environmental challenge, as their decomposition releases harmful chemicals into the soil and water bodies over many years. Decomposition of tyres should be done in a smart way and hence came the emergence of mixing recycled rubber crumbs into concrete as Rubberised Concrete (RC). This paper provides an in-depth analysis of the mechanical properties of concrete such as Compressive Strength (fck), Tensile Strength (ft), Flexural Strength (fcr) of 7, 14, 28 days in replacement of fine aggregate with fine rubber (FR), and Coarse Aggregate with Coarse Rubber (CR). The results indicate that RC is more suitable for structural applications, including Reinforced Concrete columns, beams, slabs, than conventional concrete. The primary objective of the article is to explore the potential use of recycled rubber crumbs in concrete, referred to as Rubberised Concrete (RC), and to analyze its mechanical properties such as compressive strength, tensile strength, and flexural strength over different curing periods. Additionally, machine learning (ML) based prediction model has been developed for various strength characteristics of concrete mixtures at 28 days. The hyperparameter optimization using Grid Search CV with fivefold cross-validation have been performed to obtain the best hyperparameters. The model’s performance is evaluated using metrics like MAE, MSE, RMSE, and R-squared values. Results reveal varying performances among different ML algorithms for predicting flexural, tensile, and compressive strengths.

Introduction

Waste disposal is one of the major issues facing countries today, with waste materials taking various forms, such as rubber, tires, plastics, and glass. Hundreds of millions of tires are produced by the automobile industry every year1. India is the world’s third-largest producer and fourth-largest consumer of natural rubber2. Chen et al.3 identified that in 2017, 3.4 million tons of used passenger car tires were generated, leading to a significant amount of waste. The disposal of these waste tires is costly and cannot simply be discarded into the environment, as it leads to increased environmental pollution and health issues4,5. These waste tires also serve as breeding grounds for mosquitoes, which spread numerous viral illnesses and pose a major health hazard6. However, current research has identified several appropriate and efficient methods for disposing of these non-rotting materials. According to Torgal et al.7, the total number of disposed waste tires is expected to reach 5 billion by 2023. To overcome this challenge, waste tires should be recycled or treated properly8.

Concrete is among the most widely used materials in the construction industry, second only to water9. With rapid industrialization and urbanization, construction activity is on the rise, leading to increased cement consumption. Concrete typically comprises cement, aggregates (both fine and coarse), water, and admixtures. However, the production of vast quantities of concrete depletes natural resources, directly or indirectly affecting the environment. The excessive extraction of river sand for use as fine aggregate contributes to water table depletion and poses risks such as deep riverbeds, leading to accidents and potential loss of human and animal lives (Vadivel et al.10). According to the Freedonia Group’s study in 2015, global consumption of natural aggregates reached approximately 49 billion tons, with a projected annual increase of 5%, expected to double within the next two decades11.

Urban areas, in particular, struggle with solid waste disposal due to rapid urbanization. Researchers have explored solid waste and byproducts as potential substitutes for concrete ingredients, including cement, fine, and coarse aggregates. Moreover, the cement industry faces significant challenges related to CO2 emissions, contributing to global temperature rise12. To mitigate environmental impacts, materials such as fly ash, silica fume, metakaolin, rice husk ash, and glass powder can be utilized as partial or full substitutes for cement, leading to the development of environmentally friendly concrete alternatives.

One alternative solution to address the environmental impact of traditional concrete is the adoption of Rubberised Concrete (RC), wherein aggregates are replaced with rubber—Fine Rubber (FR) for fine aggregates and Coarse Rubber (CR) for coarse aggregates. RC, known for its cost-effectiveness, utilizes a continuous shredding method to produce granulated rubber crumbs fine enough to replace aggregates as thin as sand13. Previous studies have shown that replacing fine aggregates with those extracted from solid waste improves properties compared to conventional concrete, enhancing thermal and sound insulation, ductility, toughness, and energy absorption while reducing brittleness14,15.

RC finds extensive applications in various civil engineering projects, including roads, railways, RC bumpers, and artificial reefs16. Notably, RC containing crumb rubber in lieu of natural aggregates exhibits excellent mechanical properties. Comparative studies under quasi-static tests have shown that an increase in strain rate correlates directly with enhancements in concrete’s mechanical characteristics17.

From previous literature studies18–23, Eltayeb et al.24, it has been identified that RC has lower compressive strength (fck), tensile strength (ft), and flexural strength (fcr) compared to traditional concrete. The use of RC in structural members such as beams, columns, and slabs is limited due to its lower strength compared to traditional concrete. Only 20% of the rubber content should be used to produce structural RC (Li et al.25). However, various research has been conducted that suggests these limitations can be overcome by pretreating rubber particles with additives such as silica fume (SF), fly ash (FA), granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS), and steel fibers (SFR) to produce high-strength RC16,19,22,26. Mudassir Iqbal et al. (2021) investigates the factors affecting the durability of GFRP bars in harsh alkaline environments, particularly in seawater and sea sand concrete. It introduces a new strategy for predicting tensile strength retention (TSR) using artificial intelligence techniques such as ANN, GEP, and ANFIS.

Adamu et al.27 explore the use of artificial neural networks (ANNs) to predict the mechanical properties of concrete incorporating waste and sustainable materials. The study uses fly ash (FA) as a partial cement replacement, crumb rubber (CR) as a fine aggregate substitute, and nano silica (NS) as an additive. The ANN effectively predicts key properties—compressive strength, tensile strength, modulus of elasticity, and flexural strength—with high accuracy. The low mean square error (MSE) and high coefficient of determination (R2) underscore the ANN model’s ability to capture complex data patterns, highlighting the role of machine learning in optimizing concrete compositions with sustainable materials.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the mechanical strength of rubberised concrete using various equipment and to determine the optimal crumb rubber content for achieving acceptable mechanical properties. In this research, crumb rubber and chiselled tire rubber were used as a replacement for fine and coarse aggregate in proportions ranging from 2 to 10%.

Previous studies have established that rubber tires possess low density, higher strength, and improved durability, making them suitable for reuse as rubber aggregates in concrete. However, the mechanical properties of materials can vary significantly under impact loading compared to static loading. Understanding the behavior of materials and structural components under impact loading can be challenging due to the complex dynamic response of concrete structures, which typical computational approaches and design tools may not adequately capture28. Numerous studies have been conducted to investigate the behavior of concrete and its composites when subjected to impact loads.

The objective of this study is to explore the use of waste rubber as a replacement for fine and coarse aggregates in concrete, with replacement levels ranging from 2 to 10%, and to examine the resulting mechanical properties. Additionally, the study investigates the application of machine learning techniques to predict concrete strength characteristics. This approach offers insights into optimizing material composition and improving construction practices for sustainability and resilience in the built environment. Concrete strength depends on factors like aggregate composition, curing conditions, and mix proportions (Xu Long et al.29,30). Machine learning models can effectively manage these complexities and reveal relationships between variables and strength. When combined with hyperparameter optimization, these models can refine concrete mix designs to improve strength and durability.

Shrinkage properties

The study by Kundan and Sharma33 suggested that adding rubber shreds to mortar can reduce shrinkage caused by plastic cracking. Furthermore, according to their research, the control specimen’s cracks developed on average to a size of 0.9 mm, but the specimen with 5% rubber shreds had cracks with a width of 0.4–0.6 mm. For the composites incorporated with 5% rubber shreds, the fracture start time is postponed.

In recent years, a large number of methods have been introduced in the disposal of these tires, which includes the use of tire-derived aggregate in construction projects34. Toutanji35 conducted experiments to investigate the effect of replacing coarse aggregate with rubber aggregate. Four different contents of rubber aggregate with a maximum size of 12.7 mm were used to replace the coarse aggregate at 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% by volume, and it was discovered that the incorporation of rubber aggregates in concrete produced a reduction in compressive strength of up to 75% and a significantly smaller reduction in flexural strength of up to 35%. A study reported that mortar specimens with rubber shreds were able to withstand additional load after the peak load6. The specimens were not separated into two pieces under the failure flexural load because of bridging of cracks by rubber shreds, but specimens made with granular rubber particles broke into two pieces at the failure load. This indicates that post-crack strength seemed to be enhanced when rubber shreds are used instead of granular rubber.

Segre and Joekes36 worked on the use of tire rubber particles as an addition to cement paste. In their work, the surface of powdered tire rubber (particles of maximum size 35 mesh, 500 μm) was modified to increase its adhesion to cement paste. Low-cost procedures and reagents were used in the surface treatment, among which sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution gave the best result. The particles were surface-treated with NaOH saturated aqueous solutions for 20 min before using them in concrete. The test results showed that the NaOH treatment enhances the adhesion of tire rubber particles to cement paste, and mechanical properties such as flexural strength and fracture energy were improved with the use of tire rubber particles as an addition instead of substitution for aggregate. A reduction in compressive strength (33%) was observed, which is lower than that reported in the literature. Vadivel and Thenmozhi39 delve into current practices regarding the repurposing of waste tire rubber and explore potential novel applications. The creation of fresh building materials from recycled waste tires holds significance for both the construction and rubber sectors. Extensive investigation has been undertaken to explore the utilization of waste tire rubber as reinforcement in under-reinforced beams. Fourteen beams were specifically crafted, incorporating two pieces of 15 mm × 25 mm scrap tire rubber as reinforcement in lieu of steel within the tension zone. Experiments were conducted to assess the flexural behaviour of these tire-reinforced concrete beams at various stages of aging. The findings revealed that all test specimens exhibited ductile behaviour, with testing procedures progressing smoothly and under controlled conditions. Through analysis of experimental data, the ultimate strength, flexural stiffness, and ductility were scrutinized and juxtaposed. Test results further suggested that the strength and stiffness of the beams are comparable to those of Reinforced Concrete beams. The study aimed to explore the most effective utilization of waste tire rubber crumbs as fine aggregate in concrete composites (Vadivel and Thenmozhi39. Ninety cubes, cylinders, and beam specimens were cast, incorporating replacements of fine aggregate with shredded rubber crumbs at proportions ranging from 2 to 10% by weight, and these were juxtaposed with 18 conventional specimens. Properties of both fresh and hardened concrete, including workability, compressive strength, tensile strength, and flexural strength, were identified. Ultimately, it was recommended that a 6% replacement of waste tire rubber aggregate with fine aggregate provides the most optimal and safest alternative in concrete composites. Additionally, the researcher expressed intent to investigate the Impact of Rubber as Aggregate in both Conventional and Geopolymer Concrete under Elevated Temperature.

Park et al.40 conducted a study which revealed that fine aggregate can be substituted with an equivalent volume of crumb rubber up to 5% in three varieties of fly ash-based geopolymer concrete, with a 95% confidence level using analysis of variance (ANOVA). The fundamental ingredients utilized for geopolymer in this investigation are fly ash, an alkaline liquid mixture of sodium hydroxide, sodium silicate, and crumb rubber. This study identifies various factors affecting strength, such as Compressive Strength, Aggregate Size, rubber content, and different types of fly ash utilized. The study concluded by highlighting the benefits of using rubberized geopolymer, which include reduced CO2 emissions and its effectiveness as a binder in concrete production.

Yanming Liu et al.41 review the preparation and mix design of planting concrete (PC), a sustainable type of concrete designed with specific considerations for voidage and alkalinity. The study highlights the impact of various factors on PC’s permeability, mechanical properties, durability, and plant compatibility. It emphasizes the importance of alkali reduction and vegetation compatibility in the mix design. Optimal conditions for PC include 25–30% voidage and a pH of 8–10 to ensure plant health and effective fertilizer retention.

Habib Akbarzadeh Bengar et al.42 focused on the mechanical behavior of Recycled Rubber Concrete (RRC) when exposed to high temperatures, addressing the environmental issue of non-biodegradable rubber waste by incorporating recycled rubber aggregate (RRA) into concrete. The study explored how RRC, with fine aggregates replaced by 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% of RRA, performed under temperatures of 200, 400, 600, and 800 ℃.

Mahdi Nematzadeh et al.43 explored the post-fire bond behavior of steel rebar embedded in high-strength concrete incorporating crumb rubber (CR) as a sustainable aggregate substitute. To enhance bond strength, steel fibers (SF) were also added to the concrete mix. A total of 108 specimens were tested under varying conditions, including CR content (0%, 5%, 10%), SF volume (0%, 0.5%, 1%), and exposure to high temperatures (200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C). The results showed that increasing CR content and temperature reduced bond strength, with the most significant decline observed at 600 °C.

Habib Akbarzadeh Bengar et al.44 investigates the compressive behavior of rubberized concrete confined by steel tubes (STCC) after exposure to high temperatures. It is observed that temperatures up to 400 °C have minimal impact on compressive strength, but at 800 °C, there is a significant decrease. Additionally, increasing the crumb rubber content and exposure temperature leads to higher strain at peak stress in the STCC.

Ghanbari et al.45 focuses on predicting the compressive strength of high-strength lightweight concrete made with oil palm shell (OPS) as a sustainable aggregate using machine learning methods. The researchers utilized data from 229 concrete samples and employed models such as gene expression programming (GEP), adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference system (ANFIS), artificial neural network (ANN), and multiple linear regression (MLR). The study found that all machine learning models predicted the compressive strength effectively, with GEP outperforming ANFIS and ANN. Additionally, Monte Carlo simulation (MCS) was used for uncertainty analysis, highlighting the potential of these models in sustainable concrete practices.

Abouzar Jafari et al.46 explores the use of artificial neural networks (ANNs) for predicting the fracture strength of brittle materials, specifically glasses and ceramics. Using a dataset of over 4,500 fracture surfaces, the ANN models were trained and validated, showing better performance than traditional empirical methods.

Ashrafian et al.47 investigates the post-fire behavior of concrete mixtures containing natural zeolite (NZ) as an eco-friendly alternative to ordinary Portland cement (OPC). Using a novel evolutionary-based machine learning approach, the research developed a model to assess the compressive strength of NZ concrete (NZC) after exposure to high temperatures.

Luhar et al.48 conducted a comparative study on the thermal resistance of fly ash-based geopolymer concrete and rubberized geopolymer concrete. This research proposed the substitution of natural sand with waste rubber fiber in the preparation of rubberized fly ash-based geopolymer concrete and suggested disposal methods for fly ash and waste rubber. Following thermal treatment for two hours at temperatures of 200 °C, 400 °C, 600 °C, and 800 °C, X-Ray Diffraction (XRD), Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), and thermogravimetric analysis (TGA-DTA) were utilized to investigate changes in weight, compressive strength, and density of rubberized fly ash-based geopolymer concrete.

Luhar et al.49 identified the substitution of sand with waste rubber tire fiber as cost-effective and sustainable. This study offers a comprehensive view of performance evaluation of strength characteristics such as Compressive Strength, Flexural Strength, Split Tensile Strength, Modulus of Elasticity, Pull of Strength, and durability parameters such as abrasion resistance of fly ash-based rubberized geopolymer concrete. Comparisons are made between fly ash-based rubberized geopolymer concrete and OPC rubberized geopolymer concrete, with the paper concluding that as the percentage of waste rubber fiber increases, the compressive strength decreases at all ages. Additionally, the compressive strength of OPC concrete is inferior to that of geopolymer concrete.

Bellum et al.50 conducted a study on the properties of fly ash and ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS) based geopolymer concrete containing graphite oxide. The paper focused on identifying the modulus of elasticity and compressive strength of geopolymer concrete. Microstructure characterization was carried out using a scanning electron microscope (SEM). The chloride iron resistance of graphene-modified geopolymer concrete was assessed through Rapid Chloride Permeability Test (RCPT).

The study’s findings indicate that adding 3% of graphene oxide with a 30% replacement of GGBFS increases the mixture’s modulus compressive strength, chloride ion permeability, and elasticity by 38.51%, 65.44%, and 28%, respectively. Singaravel et al.64 conducted a study on waste tire rubber to check the dynamic properties and structural integration of a 10% waste tire rubber replacement in high-performance concrete and identified it as a viable option for diverse construction applications. Mohamed et al.65 conducted an experimental study on Rubber Fiber-Reinforced Concrete (RFRC) and identified that replacing sand with crumb rubber helps in increasing impact energy. Nabilah et al.66 analyzed the bond behavior of RC in Concrete Filled Steel Tubes (CFST), showing that two-stage rubber treatment and metakaolin improve bond strength, making it suitable for construction. Amran et al.67 identified the clean production and impact of fly ash (FA) disposal on the environment and human health, as well as the factors affecting the durability of fly ash-based geopolymer concrete (FA-GPC). They identified fly ash as one of the most sustainable and eco-friendly construction materials for making greener concrete composites. This study also identified the advantages of FA-GPC, such as high early strength and improved durability against aggressive environments.

Nayak et al.68 conducted a study on fly ash admixed concrete and analyzed it in this publication. This study examines mechanical qualities, durability metrics, and microstructural details of fly ash concrete both while it is fresh and after it has hardened. Furthermore, summarized are the helpful case studies of fly ash concrete use provided by the American Coal Ash Association. The study concluded that FA can be used as an alternative to cement both environmentally and economically. FA makes concrete more workable and is used in sustainable development because waste resources may be utilized as efficiently as feasible.

The efficacy of geopolymer mortar was evaluated through altering the proportions of sodium silicate to sodium hydroxide concentration, alkaline activator to fly ash ratio, and sodium hydroxide concentration34. The study also identifies the properties of fresh and hardened fly ash-based geopolymer concrete. The study identified that the compressive strength values ranged from 3 to 40 MPa, and fly ash properties, the ratio of alkaline liquid to fly ash, the concentration, and the sodium silicate to sodium ratio are the main elements impacting the compressive strength values. The study’s findings indicate that resource recovery from garbage may be used to create sustainable building materials.

From the above all literature studies, It is observed that rubber wastes are lower in density, higher strength/ water ratio, and durability properties which can be used rubber wastes as aggregates in concrete. To obtain good strength, proper monitoring and care should be required while producing Tyre Rubber Aggregate Concrete (TRAC). The present study identifies the mechanical properties of concrete, replaced with crumb rubber as fine aggregate and chiselled rubber crumbs as coarse aggregates both fine and coarse aggregate varied between 2 to 10% of aggregates in steps of 2%. This present study uses a large number of mixtures with varying weight of rubber aggregates are prepared to check the mechanical properties of TRAC.

Materials

Coarse Aggregates, Fine Aggregates, Fine Rubber, Coarse Rubber, Cement, and Water were used in this study. Cement serves as the binding material, setting, hardening, and adhering to the other materials. The concrete used was made with Ordinary Portland Cement of grade 53, in accordance with IS: 12269-1987, and had a specific gravity of 3.14.

Fine Aggregate: Natural river sand passing through a 4.75 mm sieve, falling under grading zone II according to IS: 383-1970. It had a specific gravity of 2.63, a fineness modulus of 4.91, and a water absorption rate of 1%.

Coarse Aggregates: Crushed angular stone aggregate that passed through a 20 mm sieve and was retained on a 12 mm sieve, as per IS: 383-1970. Their specific gravity and fineness modulus were 2.61 and 7.42, respectively.

The Clear, clean water free from chemicals and impurities was used for both concreting and curing.

Rubber Aggregates: Rubber aggregates were collected from a tire recycling plant. Finely ground tire rubber was used as fine aggregate, while coarse rubber was obtained by crushing truck tire rubber. Recycled tire rubber with a particle size less than 1 mm is referred to as fine rubber. Sieve analysis was conducted to match the particle size of the rubber to sand. The rubber was used without surface treatment. Drinking water with a pH of 7.0 was used for mixing and curing, ensuring it was free from acids, organic materials, suspended particles, alkalis, and other contaminants.

Machine learning

A machine learning-based prediction model can be beneficial for predicting the compressive strength (28 Days fck), tensile strength (28 Days ft), and flexural strength (28 Days fcr) of concrete. Concrete strength is influenced by various factors such as the proportions of cement, sand, aggregates, water-cement ratio, and curing conditions. These relationships can be nonlinear and complex, making it difficult to capture using traditional analytical methods. Machine learning algorithms can automatically learn these complex relationships from data. Once trained, machine learning models can automate the prediction process, allowing for quick and efficient estimation of concrete strength without the need for manual calculations or extensive domain expertise. In this research, the XGBRegressor, CatBoost, ExtraTreesRegressor and BaggingRegressor have been implemented to predict the 28 days compressive strength, tensile strength, and flexural strength.

XG boost

XGBoost is known for its high performance and efficiency in handling large datasets (A I A Osman et al., 2021). It uses gradient boosting techniques, which sequentially builds an ensemble of weak learners (decision trees) to make predictions. XGBoost is highly customizable and can handle missing values and regularization effectively.

XGBoost can capture complex relationships between input features and target variables, making it suitable for predicting concrete strength, which often involves nonlinear dependencies on various factors like the composition of materials and curing conditions.

CatBoost

CatBoost is a gradient boosting algorithm that is specifically designed to handle categorical features efficiently (Yixiao Zhang et al., 2020). It can automatically handle categorical variables without requiring one-hot encoding or label encoding, which simplifies the preprocessing steps and reduces the risk of overfitting. CatBoost can be advantageous when dealing with datasets containing categorical variables related to concrete mixtures or other factors that influence strength properties. It can effectively capture interactions between categorical and numerical features, potentially improving prediction accuracy.

Extra trees regressor

ExtraTreesRegressor is an ensemble learning method that builds multiple decision trees and averages their predictions (Mohammed Majeed Hameed et al., 2021). They are less prone to overfitting, especially with noisy data. ExtraTreesRegressor can handle high-dimensional datasets with noisy features, making it suitable for predicting concrete strength where there may be a large number of input variables and potential sources of noise (e.g., variations in material properties, environmental factors). Its ability to reduce overfitting can lead to more robust predictions.

Bagging regressor

BaggingRegressor is an ensemble method that combines multiple base estimators (e.g., decision trees) trained on different random subsets of the training data69. It reduces variance by averaging the predictions of these base estimators, which can lead to improved generalization performance. BaggingRegressor can be effective when there is high variance in the dataset or when the underlying relationship between input features and target variables is complex. It can stabilize predictions and reduce the risk of overfitting, making it suitable for predicting concrete strength where there may be variability in material properties and environmental conditions.

Environmental impact and lifecycle assessment of RC

By using recycled rubber from used tires, rubberized concrete (RC) offers significant environmental advantages over regular concrete and helps address the growing problem of tire waste. This integration reduces the demand for virgin aggregates like sand and gravel, thereby lessening the environmental impact of their extraction. Additionally, the inclusion of recycled rubber helps lower the overall carbon footprint of the concrete manufacturing process. The calcination of limestone during cement production is responsible for approximately 8% of global CO2 emissions, which is a major contributor to the high emissions associated with traditional concrete production70. By replacing some of the traditional aggregates, rubber can reduce the amount of cement required, thereby decreasing CO2 emissions. Furthermore, RC enhances the flexibility and resistance to cracking in concrete, thereby extending its lifespan and reducing maintenance costs.

Despite these advantages, RC has certain drawbacks. Rubberised Concrete generally exhibits lower mechanical properties than regular concrete, particularly in terms of compressive strength. This limitation may restrict its use in applications that require high structural integrity. Additionally, further research is needed to ensure the long-term reliability and effectiveness of RC in various environmental conditions. While RC offers clear sustainability benefits, such as reduced waste and lower CO2 emissions, it is important to balance these advantages against the material’s performance characteristics and potential limitations in structural applications71.

Hyper parameter optimization with k-fold cross validation

The dataset has been split into an 80:20 ratio, with a total of 36 instances available. Of these, 29 data points were used for training, and 7 were used for testing. GridSearchCV was used with fivefold cross-validation to obtain the best hyperparameters for the model. GridSearchCV is a technique used in machine learning for hyperparameter tuning, which involves systematically searching through a specified set of hyperparameters for a model (Muhamad Malik Matin, I, 2023). It exhaustively tries all possible combinations of hyperparameters to identify the best ones that optimize the model’s performance according to a specified evaluation metric. GridSearchCV helps automate the process of hyperparameter tuning and can improve the performance of machine learning models by finding the optimal hyperparameters for a given dataset.

The technique known as fivefold cross-validation serves as a method for assessing the effectiveness of a machine learning model (B. Anandan and M. Manikandan, 2023). In this methodology, the dataset is divided into five equally sized segments or folds. The model undergoes training on four of these folds, constituting the training set, while the remaining fold serves as the validation set for evaluation. This process iterates five times, with each fold taking a turn as the validation set. Ultimately, the model’s overall performance is gauged by averaging its performance across all five folds. Employing fivefold cross-validation aids in evaluating a model’s ability to generalize and offers a more reliable estimation of its performance compared to a single train-test split.

The dataset being split into an 80:20 ratio, with a total of 36 instances available. Of these, 29 data points were used for training, and 7 were used for testing. The data were derived from a specific experimental setup. Data augmentation, which involves generating synthetic samples based on existing data to expand the dataset, was not implemented to develop the prediction model. The reason for this is that generating synthetic data might lead to unrealistic or non-representative samples that could distort the model’s predictions.

This relatively small dataset size can significantly affect the robustness and generalizability of the predictions. In machine learning, the amount of training data is crucial for developing models that perform well on unseen data.

To address these concerns and mitigate the limitations of the small dataset, several steps were taken. We employed fivefold cross-validation during the hyperparameter tuning process to ensure that the model’s performance was evaluated on multiple subsets of the data. This approach helps assess the model’s ability to generalize across different portions of the dataset and provides a more reliable estimate of its performance. GridSearchCV was used to systematically explore various hyperparameter combinations, optimizing the models to achieve the best performance within the constraints of the available data.

A variety of machine learning models (XGBRegressor, CatBoost, ExtraTreesRegressor, and BaggingRegressor) were chosen to compare their performance and select the most robust model for our dataset. This approach helps ensure that the best possible model is identified, even with limited data. Additionally, multiple performance metrics (MAE, MSE, RMSE, and R-squared) were used to evaluate and compare model performance. This comprehensive evaluation helps in understanding the strengths and weaknesses of each model and provides a balanced view of their predictive capabilities.

Conclusion

This experimental study involved the replacement of fine aggregate (FA) and coarse aggregate (CA) with fine rubber (FR) and coarse rubber (CR), respectively, and the investigation of their mechanical properties at 7 days, 21 days, and 28 days. The findings of the study indicate that the addition of rubberised particles has a notable impact on the mechanical properties of concrete. Adding rubber to concrete enhances its flexibility and ductility, thereby improving overall durability and resistance to cracking.

Rubberised concrete (RC) can be utilized in applications where impact resistance is crucial due to its reduced brittleness and increased energy absorption capacity. To assess the feasibility of using leftover tire rubber in concrete, experiments were conducted in which rubber aggregates partially replaced conventional aggregates. The test results revealed that replacing all types of rubber aggregates with 6% of the original material leads to a consistent and significant improvement in all necessary mechanical characteristics, which behaved extremely similar to conventional concrete. Proper mix design and optimization processes in preparing RC will result in properties equivalent to those of traditional concrete mixtures. The use of RC contributes to waste reduction and environmental sustainability. Additionally, the developed machine learning prediction model demonstrates promising results in predicting various strength characteristics of concrete mixtures at 28 days. The model’s performance varies depending on the type of strength prediction and the chosen machine learning algorithm. XGBRegressor and CatBoost exhibit superior performance for flexural strength prediction, while ExtraTreesRegressor and BaggingRegressor excel in predicting tensile strength. CatBoost outperforms other models for compressive strength prediction.

In the future, investigating higher grades of concrete with rubber replacement to evaluate performance under more demanding structural requirements, using additives to strengthen the bond between rubber aggregates and cement paste to potentially enhance mechanical properties. This study uses M20, M25, and M30 grade concrete with a replacement of FA and CA ranging from 2 to 10%. Future studies can be carried out by choosing higher grades of concrete, increasing the grade and replacement, and using additives to strengthen the bonding properies.
