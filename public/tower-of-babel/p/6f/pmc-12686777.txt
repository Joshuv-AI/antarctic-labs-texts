Designing effective clinical education spaces: A scoping review
Authors: Megan Phelps, Pippa Yeoman, Lynn V Monrouxe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionClinical education in health facilities is critical for health professional student learning. However, consideration of health professional students' learning when designing and building hospitals appears to be neglected. Evidence‐based health facility design may encourage student learning and add value to the investment in facilities. We ask, ‘What does the literature say about the consideration of health professional students’ education when designing health facilities?’MethodsWe developed a scoping review protocol following Arksey and O′Malley's Guidelines. Our search strategy employed the following concepts: Health Facilities, Facility design, Health professional students, Learning and teaching and health professional education. Forty articles were identified for inclusion. Databases comprised Medline, Art and Architecture Archive, Avery Index to Architecture and Periodicals (EBSCO), CINAHL, ERIC, Scopus, Proquest Central and Web of Science.ResultsArticles were published between 1963 and 2023. There was a range of publication types originating mostly from North America and the United Kingdom (UK). Only four articles describe original research. Medical students are the most reported individual student group. Links are made between principles of learning and the built environment (45% of articles). Recognised features supporting learning include floor space, various building elements and their attributes, environmental features and furniture, fittings and equipment. Evaluation is lacking, and building codes and standards are noted in only three publications.DiscussionThe review reveals a scarcity of published evidence documenting how health professional student learning is considered in health facility design. While design professionals may address these needs in practice, the lack of accessible literature limits evidence‐based approaches. Opportunities exist for educators and administrators to advocate for the inclusion of students and their learning needs in collaboration with colleagues from other disciplines such as architecture and design. Documenting and publishing these collaborations will strengthen the evidence base, ensuring learning is more intentionally integrated in clinical settings.

INTRODUCTION

The apprenticeship model of learning has long been recognised in medical education, 1 and appreciation for the range of spaces in which this learning happens can be found: ‘In addition to the bedside, hospital corridors just outside of patient rooms have traditionally been used for lengthy discussions among medical staff and students.’ 2,p.40 Hospital corridors may not be optimal places for learning. However, in the 60 years since Straus made this observation, it seems we have done little to attend to the needs of learners when designing health facilities.

Patient‐centred design is fundamental to the work of many healthcare architects and planners. 3 , 4 However, it appears those engaged in the design of teaching or university hospitals or academic medical centers (e.g., medical educators, architects, designers, planners and facility managers), often fail to notice that clinical areas within these developments are also significant sites of learning for future healthcare professionals. 5 , 6 As a result, little care is given to optimising these spaces to support learning: indeed, as noted above, space for learning in clinical areas is often left to chance. 7 , 8

Our review aims to identify the current evidence in the literature about designing for health professional student learning in health facilities. Developing a clear understanding of the current literature brings us one step closer to redressing inadequacies in the evidence base necessary to support health facility design for student learning, ensuring space for clinical learning is well designed.

Designing for learning

Active learning is a major contributor to effective learning in healthcare professions' education. 9 Research in education facilities, from schools to universities, demonstrates the impact of the built environment on learning. For example, didactic teaching is supported by a single teaching point that is the focus of attention for many individual students seated in raked lecture theatres, or in uniform rows oriented towards the teacher in the front of traditional flat floor classrooms. 10 By contrast, active learning is best supported in settings which accommodate learning in small groups with room to move between groups and writable surfaces often housed on the perimeter. 11 That is, spatial arrangements ought to attend to the types of activity they are intended to accommodate, and failing to attend to these relations does not result in a neutral outcome. We must acknowledge that clinical areas are having an impact on health professional students' learning, and how much better would outcomes be if they had been designed to support active learning in an authentic clinical setting.

Currently, the impact and quality of learning environments in clinical areas are being evaluated using checklists and surveys. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 However, these checklists focus on the human, with little to no mention of the physical environment. One example, the Johns Hopkins Learning Environment Scale, has seven subscales (community of peers, faculty relationships, academic climate, meaningful engagement, mentoring, inclusion and safety and physical space) and only two of the 28 items reference physical space (‘The preclinical SOM [School of Medicine] building has a significant effect on my perception of the learning environment’, ‘The work spaces where clinical teaching occurs contributes positively to my sense of the SOM learning environment’). 17 p 815 This places the emphasis on the social dimensions of space and safety, obscuring the positive potential of the physical or non‐human aspects 18 resulting in missed opportunities to gather data to enhance learning by modifying the built environment. Moreover, new considerations of virtual ‘space’, at its simplest reliable WiFi access, are extremely important and often under‐recognised for education in clinical and adjacent areas. 12 , 19

Clinical education

Clinical education may be formal or informal, taking place in traditional learning spaces (e.g. classrooms), or in clinical areas (e.g. wards, patients' rooms and the spaces in‐between, such as corridors). 20 Clinical education is not merely about learning the whats of being a clinician (e.g. the formal curriculum around differential diagnosis, treatment options, etc.) it also includes the hows as students observe their educators' and other clinicians' behaviours during patient encounters and other work: that is, the informal and hidden curricula activities of interprofessional work, including communicating with families and colleagues. 21 , 22 Through this process of clinical education in authentic settings students are also developing their professional identities. 21 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26

Economics and investment

Planning and building hospitals require a huge investment of funds and other resources. 27 Given the initial capital cost and ongoing expenditure on health facilities, it is important to glean all possible value from the resulting infrastructure. Enhanced health professional student learning provides important additional value to the health sector and the community, addressing issues such as continuing workforce shortages and poor staff retention. 28 Furthermore, lifelong learning has been recognised as a major goal of the World Health Organization as part of the WHO Academy Strategy 2024–2026,29, p. iv with this learning taking place across the careers of health professionals in health facilities around the world.

The governance and oversight of health facilities is under the control of many authorities (and individuals) throughout the life cycle of the building. 29 This creates multiple opportunities for interaction when information about enhancing learning could be shared and used. These occur before (e.g. functional briefs, concept planning, outline business cases, precinct planning, building regulations, safety regulations), during (e.g. detailed design, co‐design, collaborative design, project user groups, value engineering, safety precautions, accreditation standards) and after (e.g. refurbishment, repurposing, demolition, replacement) the construction. 30 , 31 The authorities include government and industry building regulators. 32 , 33 , 34 They also include accrediting organisations for health professional training such as medical schools and specialist colleges. 35 , 36 , 37 These authorities have opportunities to address and enhance, or ignore and obstruct, learning in health facilities.

Space

The term ‘space’ has many different meanings, both concrete and abstract, across academic disciplines. Examples of the use of the word ‘space’ include indicating activity types (e.g., formal or informal), describing navigation support (e.g., indoors, outdoors, in‐between), 38 and in debate about the constitution of space and the emergence of place in human‐geography. 39 This debate has made its way into health professional education, in part through exploring how social spaces such as lounges and tea rooms contribute to student wellbeing. 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 ‘Space’ is used differently in the conceptualisation of liminal spaces between healthcare and education professions. 44 We consider this range of conceptualisations and uses of the word ‘space’ in our search strategy.

METHODS

Aim

The aim of our scoping review is to identify and synthesise the literature around the current state of preparing health facilities to encompass and embody learning for health professional students. It contributes by mapping the evidence base for designing and building learning environments in clinical settings, without which we will be unable to build health facilities that are worthy of the title learning hospital.

Study design

Our scoping review design follows the work of Arksey and O'Malley and others. 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 We use the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist to guide our reporting of this review. 49 We registered our review protocol in July 2024 on the Open Science Framework. 50

Step 1: research question

Given the importance of clinical education and health facilities outlined above and the apparent lack of foundational information for evidence‐based health facility design we formulated the following overarching research question: What does the literature say about the consideration of health professional students' education when designing health facilities? We delineate our overarching research question into the following sub‐questions:

RQ1a: When and where were the articles published?

RQ1b: What is the country of origin of the articles?

RQ1c: What type of healthcare facility is the focus of the articles?

RQ1d: What article types are published?

RQ1e: What type of healthcare professional students are considered?

RQ1f: To what degree are stakeholders, including health professional students and educators, consulted/represented during health facility design?

RQ1g: What principles, theories or rationales are used when designing health facilities for student learning?

RQ1h: How do health facility design features support learning, and how is their effectiveness evaluated?

Step 2: identifying relevant publications

With the assistance of specialist research librarians across multiple sessions, four concepts were identified: Concept 1, Health Facilities; Concept 2, Facility design; Concept 3, Health professional students; Concept 4, Learning and teaching and health professional education. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and other search terms were identified for the four concepts and explored and adapted for each of the databases and search strategies used. For Concept 1 ‘Health facilities, including hospitals’, keywords such as ‘hospital*’, ‘health centre’, ‘health facility’ and ‘Academic Medical Center’ were used to capture the range of facilities with clinical areas. For Concept 2 ‘Facility Design’, terms such ‘architect*’, ‘space’, ’design’ and ‘wayfinding’ (‘the process of finding your way to a destination in a familiar or unfamiliar setting using any cues given by the environment’ 51,p.715 ) and Subject headings such as ‘evidence‐based facility design’ and ‘architecture’ were employed. Concept 3 ‘Health professional students', required a range of keywords and subject headings to include all professional groups. Keywords such as ‘Health education’ and ‘interprofessional’ and Subject headings such as ‘Education, professional’ were used to capture publications related to Concept 4 ‘Learning and teaching and health professional education’. Alternative spellings of terms were included. Repeated trials and revisions of search strategies were conducted.

The following key databases were searched in August to October 2023: Medline; Art and Architecture Archive; Avery Index to Architecture and Periodicals (EBSCO); CINAHL; ERIC; Scopus; Proquest Central; Web of Science. A Google Scholar simple search was used to search broadly for omissions. An example of the search details used in Medline, can be found in Appendix A [Supplementary Material].

Step 3: selecting the publications to be included

All articles discovered through database and citation searching were screened using the systematic review platform Covidence. This process was undertaken by two researchers (Megan Phelps (MP) and Lynn Monrouxe (LVM)). After duplicate removal, more than 5% of titles and abstracts were screened by LVM and MP. Conflicting votes were discussed and resolved. MP then screened the remaining titles and abstracts. Articles not available in English were excluded. Forty full‐text publications remained for inclusion (see Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram. [Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Step 4: charting the data

MP extracted descriptive data for included publications using Covidence. The final full‐text articles were imported to NVivo. 52 All three authors reviewed a subset of articles (n = 6), with each article being reviewed by two authors. Written reports on these articles were compared for commonalities and differences. MP used NVivo to create a codebook, which was then discussed and verified by LVM and Pippa Yeoman (PY). As additional articles were reviewed, the team further developed and specified codes.

Step 5: collating, summarising and reporting results

Descriptive data were summarised using tables and graphs. The NVivo codebook was used to review codes and themes. Researchers MP and PY further refined this. The team discussed and refined results.

RQ1c: what type of healthcare facility is the focus of the articles?

The publications relate to a range of health facility types. Twenty‐one articles discuss ‘teaching hospitals’, although the authors do not define the term (Table 2). 54 , 55 , 57 , 59 , 63 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 69 , 73 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 80 , 82 , 83 , 85 , 86 , 88 , 89 , 92 The National Library of Medicine (NLM) Medical Search Heading (MeSH) term ‘Hospitals, teaching’ includes ‘Hospitals, university’. The MeSH scope notes indicate these are ‘Hospitals engaged in educational and research programs, as well as providing medical care to the patients.’ ‘Academic medical centers’ are the subject of 10 articles. 2 , 56 , 58 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 70 , 74 , 79 , 90 This term, used predominantly in the U.S.A. to describe a complex of medical school, hospital, clinics, library and administrative facilities, was recognised as a MeSH term in 1980. A university dental service is the subject of four articles. 53 , 68 , 81 , 87 Three publications refer to a mixture of facility types. 72 , 75 , 84 One further publication describes the development of a health and research precinct which also includes industry facilities, such as for medical devices. 64

RQ1d: what article types are published?

There are a range of publication types, which we grouped into four categories: 1) peer‐reviewed journal article, 2) general commentary, 3) project description and 4) magazine article (Table 2).

Although 16 articles were published in peer‐reviewed journals, only four describe original research. 79 , 85 , 87 , 88 These four articles, although not primarily focused on the design of the built environment, include how physical spaces influence learning, teaching, patient quality and safety and healthcare practices through architectural changes, spatial design and location. The 15 general commentaries were published in the form of opinion pieces, 61 , 62 , 81 , 84 conference presentations, 64 , 71 , 75 , 80 , 92 editorials, 64 , 71 , 75 , 80 , 92 reviews, 73 and letters. 66

Four publications appeared in colour illustrated magazines. 53 , 68 , 83 , 86 Four further publications are project descriptions, detailing the planning, design and building of hospital projects, without formal evaluation or ‘lessons learned’. 57 , 59 , 63 , 65 This type of publication is referred to as a ‘case study’ in architectural work, employed differently to the term ‘case study’ in Medicine and Health research. 93

RQ1e: what type of healthcare professional students are considered?

Medical students are the most reported student group (Table 2: n = 12), 54 , 56 , 57 , 59 , 60 , 62 , 63 , 66 , 84 , 88 , 90 , 92 followed by dentistry students (n = 4), 53 , 68 , 81 , 87 and nursing students (n = 2). 67 , 85 In 18 publications multiple student types were included, for example, medical, nursing and allied health. 2 , 55 , 64 , 65 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 76 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 82 , 86 , 88 , 89 Student type was not specified in four publications. 58 , 75 , 77 , 83

RQ1f: to what degree are stakeholders, including health professional students and educators, consulted/represented during health facility design?

Stakeholders are referred to in 27 of the 40 articles: specifically, healthcare professions students and other learners (n = 22), 2 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 57 , 58 , 59 , 63 , 64 , 65 , 68 , 72 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 87 , 88 , 90 , 92 university representatives (n = 15), 2 , 55 , 57 , 58 , 64 , 68 , 72 , 77 , 80 , 82 , 84 , 87 , 88 , 89 , 92 architects and designers (n = 11), 2 , 57 , 58 , 60 , 64 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 77 , 80 , 92 clinicians (n = 11), 2 , 55 , 59 , 64 , 70 , 72 , 80 , 87 , 88 , 89 , 92 and patients and the community (n = 6). 2 , 55 , 70 , 78 , 88 , 92 Despite being the most commonly mentioned stakeholder group, students were actually consulted in just three publications: via surveys and interviews. 53 , 84 , 87 In one further publication they were observed in an ethnographic study. 79 Interestingly, publications suggest a shift in the design process over time with increasing numbers of stakeholders included, alongside an emphasis on the importance of co‐design, 94 , 95 , 96 in more recent studies.

RQ1g: what principles, theories or rationales are used when designing health facilities for student learning?

Student learning

Philosophies or understandings of learning are frequently mentioned across the articles (n = 30). 2 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 64 , 66 , 67 , 68 , 69 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 83 , 84 , 86 , 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 In 18 of these 30 articles the authors discuss student learning in the context of the built environment but do not connect the two. 2 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 56 , 60 , 61 , 68 , 73 , 74 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 80 , 81 , 83 , 86 , 90 Constructivist theory and active pedagogies are also referenced (n = 6), 55 , 56 , 61 , 72 , 73 , 76 including detailing the underlying constructivist educational rationale guiding education facility design. 76 In describing the role of 1) ‘material spatiality’ and 2) Actor Network Theory (ANT) for guiding design of spaces supporting competency‐based medical education, 78 Nordquist et al emphasise the importance of learning in clinical health facilities, in spaces that are often contested, such as patient rooms. However, the authors illustrate the use of these frameworks for two non‐clinical areas: a novel classroom (a ‘case‐study theatre’) in an education centre, and a university teaching laboratory. No formal evaluation of these examples is included.

Interprofessional practice

Interprofessional education is defined by the World Health Organisation as occurring when ‘two or more professions learn about, from and with each other to enable effective collaboration and improve health outcomes.’ 97 Page 13 References to interprofessional education and practice are found in 15 articles. 55 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 64 , 67 , 69 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 76 , 77 , 79 , 84 , 86 Five of these articles appear in the Journal of Interprofessional Care supplement. 55 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 76 However, despite the recognition of the potential for interprofessional practice in these 15 articles, there is a distinct absence of facility evaluation to determine whether the intention to encourage interprofessional education through facility design is achieved.

Lifelong learning

Lifelong learning for health professionals is a major goal of the World Health Organization. 98 Five articles state the importance of lifelong learning. 54 , 60 , 76 , 80 , 90 However, this is not linked to design features.

Professionalism

Three publications specifically reference the importance of design of facilities to the development of professionalism. 54 , 72 , 76

Student dignity

Student dignity was identified as foundational in three articles. 60 , 87 , 89 In one of these, the following observation was made: ‘Facilities can help encourage and facilitate the process by dignifying the learner's role in the hospital ‐ places to work, places to talk, places to deal with patients, places to relax over a cup of coffee, all bring dignity and ease the task of the learner’. 60,p. 663

RQ1h: how do health facility design features support learning, and how is their effectiveness evaluated?

Space considerations

The importance of adequate space for learning is specified in only nine articles. 55 , 56 , 60 , 67 , 68 , 72 , 76 , 77 , 87 Floor area, more specifically, is the measurement of ‘space’, demarcated by clear boundaries, such as the walls of patient rooms. It is an extremely important measure for architects and designers, dictating budgets and the use of ‘value engineering’ to optimise projects. 99 Floor area was identified as a factor in four references. 2 , 67 , 68 , 77 The design constraints and ‘competition’ around floor space imposed by factors such as budget prior to building, are compounded, once buildings are occupied and in use, by the appropriation of space (the potential for, or examples of, spaces for learning and teaching being taken over for use for other purposes). Appropriation of space was described in five articles. 60 , 63 , 78 , 79 , 87 The notion of physical ‘contested spaces’, areas whose ownership is disputed implicitly or explicitly, links to appropriation and was included in one article. 72

Secondary consideration of learning

Throughout the articles, the primary function of health facilities as care of the patient is expressed with designing for learning implicitly a secondary consideration. Learning and education as only a secondary function of a health facility was explicated in four articles. 53 , 76 , 78 , 86

Building codes and standards

Space in design and building may be formalised through building codes or standards such as the U.K. Health Building Notes 34 or the Australasian Health Facilities Guidelines. 32 Typically government‐created, they provide regulations or guidance about facilities. Only two references identified building codes in relation to teaching and learning; one references Italian government project guidelines, 86 the other U.K. Building Notes. 82

Building elements

Building elements make up health facilities. 100 The following elements were identified; clinical areas (n = 15), 2 , 53 , 55 , 60 , 62 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 84 , 86 , 87 , 90 outpatient or ambulatory areas (n = 4), 83 , 84 , 86 , 89 specialised units (e.g. intensive care unit)(n = 2) 82 , 85 individual study areas (n = 2), 87 , 90 laboratories (n = 7), 2 , 53 , 60 , 62 , 69 , 81 , 90 libraries (n = 12), 2 , 53 , 55 , 60 , 65 , 69 , 73 , 76 , 81 , 82 , 87 , 90 operating theatres (n = 6), 55 , 67 , 69 , 76 , 77 , 90 simulation areas (n = 9), 55 , 64 , 67 , 68 , 73 , 74 , 76 , 86 , 87 social areas (n‐12), 2 , 53 , 55 , 60 , 61 , 73 , 76 , 77 , 81 , 82 , 87 , 90 technology (n = 12), 55 , 60 , 61 , 63 , 64 , 68 , 71 , 74 , 77 , 82 , 88 , 90 in‐between or liminal spaces (n = 10), 53 , 55 , 56 , 60 , 69 , 70 , 73 , 74 , 77 , 83 traditional education spaces (n = 19), 2 , 55 , 59 , 60 , 63 , 65 , 68 , 69 , 73 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 88 , 89 , 90 staff accommodation, 57 and wayfinding (n = 2). Eight papers mentioned innovative or alternative use of elements (e.g. a ‘teddy bear’ hospital community project led by medical students in the hospital foyer 55 , 67 , 101 (n = 8). 2 , 55 , 56 , 68 , 74 , 76 , 77 , 90 All these elements were considered important by the authors for supporting learning.

Attributes of elements

Attributes of elements (descriptions of design attributes or features of elements or items that could be reused or specified for building projects) were also identified. These include ‘usual attributes’, environmental features that are ‘given’ or assumed as important currently by designers, such as noise (n = 2), 68 , 73 light (n‐3), 55 , 68 , 73 colour (n = 4), 55 , 59 , 68 , 73 and visibility considerations (n = 2). 63 , 79 External connections (n = 4) 55 , 61 , 69 , 73 , 90 and efficiency for health professional education (n = 5) 2 , 56 , 60 , 70 , 84 were also noted. Given building security is a significant current design issue, it was interesting that 24‐hour access was noted in two of the oldest (1963, 1965) publications (n = 2). 2 , 60 Privacy for learning was noted in five publications (n = 5). 2 , 53 , 55 , 59 , 90

Furniture, fittings and equipment

Furniture, Fittings and Equipment (FF&E) refers to movable furniture, fixtures, or other equipment with no permanent connection to the structure of a building. FF&E may include desks, chairs, computers, electronic equipment, tables, bookcases and partitions. Ten publications include reference to FF&E. 2 , 55 , 61 , 68 , 73 , 77 , 82 , 88 , 90 There is a shift from an emphasis in early papers on FF&E for individual study areas (n = 2) 2 , 90 to furnishings that facilitate small group teaching (n = 5) 55 , 61 , 73 , 77 , 88 in more recent work.

Evaluation and knowledge creation

The need to evaluate the built environment is recognised and possible methods described in 22 publications. 2 , 53 , 54 , 55 , 58 , 61 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 84 , 86 , 89 None of these provide post‐occupancy evaluation data specifically related to the success or otherwise of the designed built environment for learning.

Comparative workspaces

One publication draws on the learning and working paradigm shifts of changes in the corporate workplace and compares them to interprofessional clinical areas. 74 The authors illustrate the increase in collaborative work practices and the importance of information technology for innovation in the design of spaces by using three corporate site case studies on which they worked (Google, GlaxoSmithKline and SAP AppHaus).

DISCUSSION

Our scoping review, identifying 40 publications, reveals a striking lack of consideration in the published, retrievable literature for healthcare professions students and their learning in the design of health facilities over the past six decades. Despite the number of years, breadth of countries and range of publications, we did not identify articles with detailed data to demonstrate how buildings have supported student learning in health facilities. There are descriptions of projects and aspirations, completed or planned, without detailed evaluation of the impact of the ‘physical and virtual spaces’ of the learning environment 12 on learning. Unfortunately, in response to our research question, ‘What does the literature say about the consideration of health professional students’ education when designing health facilities?’, we found little we could use to add to the evidence base for future design.

Students and their learning are an important focus of articles in the earlier years, between 1963 and 1975, with authors acknowledging the importance of aligning pedagogical and philosophical approaches with health facility design. 54 , 58 , 60 , 62 , 66 , 69 , 70 , 75 , 81 , 90 , 92 Across all the articles we reviewed most are general guidelines for health facility design, 2 , 54 , 58 , 60 , 61 , 62 , 63 , 66 , 68 , 70 , 74 , 75 , 77 , 78 , 86 , 90 , 92 descriptions of planned or recently completed projects, 2 , 55 , 57 , 59 , 62 , 65 , 69 , 76 , 81 , 82 , 89 or observations of clinical settings where the built environment is not the primary concern. 79 , 85 , 88 Notably, many of these articles refer to traditional educational spaces such as lecture theatres and classrooms, rather than, or in addition to, the deliberate designing of learning spaces within clinical environments. 2 , 55 , 60 , 63 , 65 , 68 , 69 , 73 , 76 , 77 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 88 , 89 , 90 Again, more recently, there is a shift in the literature back to focussing on students, towards linking learning in active clinical settings through design, particularly in the context of interprofessional practice. 77 The bimodal distribution of publications may reflect investment in health infrastructure post‐World War II, and 50 years later as buildings come to the end of the life cycle. 102 The more recent emerging focus on designing clinical environments that support student learning and interprofessional collaboration highlights a potential turning point in the consideration of healthcare professions students in health facility design.

Through our review, we identified building elements and their attributes (what might be thought of as ‘building blocks’) that can be used to construct a learning hospital, one designed to encourage healthcare professions students learning. Notably, the review articles do not include post‐occupancy evaluations, which represent a crucial way that elements and attributes can be examined for their impact on student learning. Post‐occupancy evaluations of facilities in use may be part of a quality improvement process, well recognised in healthcare generally, for evaluating the built environment. 32 , 103

One of the elements identified is Technology. 55 , 60 , 61 , 63 , 64 , 68 , 71 , 74 , 77 , 82 , 88 , 90 The integration of technology and artificial intelligence will inevitably influence the process and products of architects and designers working on healthcare facilities. Indeed, the adoption of ambient artificial intelligence, for example, at least in higher budget projects, may impact not only patient care outcomes but healthcare professional and student learning, as well as providing information for facility design. 104 , 105 , 106 , 107 The continuing move to centralised electronic health records, for instance, means the technology designed into spaces will have an impact on student access and therefore learning. 108 , 109 In fact, if anything, technology has taken precedence over other aspects and attributes of the built environment to date, in theory 110 , 111 and in standards. 112 These technological innovations represent just one component of the broader ecosystem of elements needed to create effective learning environments in healthcare facilities. Like all aspects of the built environment, space considerations, elements and their attributes, alongside Furniture, Fittings and Equipment, technology requires systematic evaluation to determine its effectiveness in supporting learning.

Given the importance of workplace‐based learning for clinical education, and the increasing involvement of stakeholders in the lengthy design and construction process, it is surprising that students were consulted in only three articles. 53 , 86 , 87 Planning, design and construction of health facilities provides many opportunities for students to be involved alongside being represented by university faculty. Health professional educators have an opportunity to develop their expertise, to include students and to advocate on their behalf in this process towards encouraging better design and evaluation for learning.

The governance and oversight of facilities also provides opportunities for health professional educators, and architects and designers, to contribute to guidelines, regulations and standards. Only two articles mentioned codes and standards: the Italian government project guidelines and the U.K. Building Notes 82 , 86 Other standards could include those of education and training authorities (e.g. Royal Australasian College of Physicians, 35 Australian Medical Council 36 , 37 ) We can contribute to these tools with both government building regulators and health accrediting organisations, underscoring the importance of design of the built environment for clinical education and providing documentary and regulatory levers for change. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37

Our review demonstrates the implicit recognition that clinical education competes with the primary clinical function of health facilities, with education regarded as a ‘secondary’ function in four articles. 53 , 76 , 78 , 86 If learning is relegated to a secondary consideration in design, planning and management, there is continuing risk that the learning of health professional students – individuals and groups ‐ will be relegated in both location and importance. Furthermore, there is an acknowledgement that learning spaces may be ‘usurped for other purposes’, 60,p. 664 either ‘”value‐engineered” out of the construction program, or re‐purposed following construction’ 78,p. 2 as storage spaces, offices, or to meet a range of other non‐educational clinical functions. What we fail to apprehend every time educational spaces are appropriated is that the education and training of future health professional students suffer, and this is a global concern for the future workforce. 113

As with all research, our review faced some challenges. Challenges to the review include the search terms we used. For example, the word ‘architecture’ is used in many ways across areas that have nothing to do with buildings, expanding the number of articles for review. We limited selection to health professional students; extending the search across the vocational lifespan, through post‐graduate education and continuing professional development, may yield additional publications. 114 We also limited the review to English language only, potentially excluding useful work, due to language proficiencies across the research team. 115 Furthermore, the range of publication type, from magazine‐style trade publications to sixty‐year‐old commentaries, makes comparison difficult. However, despite our challenges, our review is strengthened by the wide‐ranging and detailed search strategy we used to identify a broad array of publications across different academic disciplines. Indeed, the various disciplines of the authors bring rich perspectives to our analysis.

Ultimately, our review points to the need for further integration of health professional student education needs and healthcare building design. To advance healthcare professions education in the clinical space, we must embark on a collaborative journey. This journey must bridge the gaps between educators, architects, designers and students. By establishing partnerships and interdisciplinary design teams, we can harness the collective expertise of these stakeholders to co‐design learning environments that truly support student learning. 116 Student‐centered design is paramount, and this can be achieved by implementing regular feedback mechanisms, co‐design workshops and participatory action research projects. By empowering students to take an active role in shaping their learning environments, we can unlock innovative solutions that cater to their unique needs and preferences when working in the clinical environment. Furthermore, part of this journey could be to develop training in healthcare for architects and in architecture for clinicians. 117 , 118

Our review also points towards the need for further research on the integration of education and healthcare building design. Starting points for research projects can include the identification of principles common to architecture and design, and healthcare. For example, the principle of dignity provides a shared language for better collaboration. Indeed, not only has dignity been identified in our review, 60 , 87 , 89 it also features in recent architectural writing. 119 , 120 Furthermore, ‘Design for Dignity’ is the first design principle of the Design Guide for Health: Spaces, Places and Precincts from the Government Architect NSW. 22 , 121 However, while emphasising the importance of facilities being welcoming and well maintained, the link between dignity and learning in the design of health spaces appears to be lacking. By contrast, this dignity‐learning link has been the focus of recent literature in healthcare professions education. 122 , 123 , 124 , 125

Furthermore, given the importance of many disciplines to the design process and the functioning of health facilities as complex environments, future research will benefit from being multidisciplinary. Together with the disciplines of architecture and design, healthcare educators and students can improve the contribution to evidence‐based practice, particularly related to learning, through involvement in research and activities such as rigorous post‐occupancy evaluation. 103 , 126 Given the cyclical design process 31 there are many points at which information may be gathered and research conducted. There are a range tools available for this, using an array of quantitative and qualitative methods. 127 Tools such as clinical learning environment surveys may be combined with ones that concentrate on learning spaces such as the Learning Space Rating System (LSRS). 114 , 128 This will provide information about the necessary elements, attributes and features, including Furniture, Fittings and Equipment (FF&E) for the learning hospital. As well as being a building element in the form of bricks and mortar ‘centres’ and ‘in situ’ considerations, simulation may be used as part of creating the architectural brief, testing design options, preparing for moving in (commissioning) and, once the dust has settled, post‐occupancy evaluation. 129 , 130 , 131 , 132 , 133

Ultimately, driving meaningful change requires a concerted advocacy effort. We must raise awareness about the critical importance of designing learning environments that support healthcare professions education. Together, we can create a future where healthcare professions education is transformed by intentional, student‐centered design.
