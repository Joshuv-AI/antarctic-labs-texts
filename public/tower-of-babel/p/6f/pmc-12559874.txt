Alveolar Epithelial Cell‐Derived Exosomes Regulate Autophagy Through miR‐450b‐5p/HDAC7 to Improve Pulmonary Fibrosis
Authors: Yong Zhou, Zhongkai Tong, Ying Zhou, Yuhan Zhu, Zanbo Chu, Zhaoxing Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIdiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive lung disease. In recent years, alveolar epithelial cell‐derived exosomes (AEc‐Exos) have been shown to influence the progression of pulmonary fibrosis. The main objective of this study was to investigate the specific molecular mechanism of the effect of AEc‐Exos on IPF. In this study, a cell fibrosis model was constructed using TGF‐β, and a mouse fibrosis model was constructed with bleomycin (BLM), treated with AEc‐Exos, which was interfered with by treatment with a miR‐450b‐5p inhibitor. Experimental analysis revealed that AEc‐Exos activated autophagy by inhibiting the expression of HDAC7 and attenuated TGF‐β‐induced pulmonary fibrosis and that inhibiting miR‐450b‐5p weakened the therapeutic effect of AEc‐Exos. starBase analysis revealed that HDAC7 was the target gene of miR‐450b‐5p, and experiments revealed that overexpression of HDAC7 weakened the therapeutic effect of AEc‐Exos on TGF‐β‐induced pulmonary fibrosis. In in vivo experiments, BLM induced pulmonary fibrosis and inhibited autophagy in mice, an effect that was weakened after the addition of AEc‐Exos, which partially reversed the effect of BLM after further inhibition of miR‐450b‐5p. In summary, this study revealed that AEc‐Exos significantly inhibited the development of pulmonary fibrosis by activating autophagy through the miR‐450b‐5p/HDAC7 molecular axis, which provides new ideas for the clinical treatment of pulmonary fibrosis.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic, progressive lung disease characterized by the formation of scar tissue, thickening of fibrosis, and loss of the ability to promote gas exchange and provide needed oxygen to cells as it progresses [1].

The pathogenesis of IPF involves complex interactions among cell types and signaling pathways, with dysregulated epithelial cells interacting with mesenchymal, immune, and endothelial cells through a variety of signaling mechanisms, triggering fibroblast and myofibroblast activation [2]. Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a key process in the development of pulmonary fibrosis [3]. Aging is the main risk factor for the development of chronic IPF [4]. At present, the drugs used for the treatment of IPF include pirfenidone and nintedanib; the main clinical treatment for IPF is lung transplantation, and there are few effective treatments for IPF [5]. Therefore, finding new effective methods for the treatment of IPF to improve the clinical treatment of IPF is highly important.

Exosomes are cell‐derived bilayer lipid vesicles ranging from 30 to 150 nm in diameter that are released upon fusion of the multivesicular body with the cell surface and can deliver a variety of functional biomolecules, such as DNA, microRNA, messenger RNA (mRNA), and long noncoding RNA (lncRNA), for cell–cell communication and to activate signaling pathways in target cells [6, 7]. In the process of pulmonary fibrosis, microRNAs, lncRNAs, and proteins in exosomes can promote or inhibit EMT, regulate the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts, promote fibroblast proliferation, and promote immune regulation and mitochondrial damage in the process of pulmonary fibrosis [8]. In addition, studies have reported that alveolar epithelial cell‐derived exosomes (AEc‐Exos) play important roles in several pulmonary fibrosis diseases. For example, AEc‐Exos promote alveolar macrophage activation and affect the progression of acute lung injury [9]. Exosomes from damaged alveolar epithelial type II cells promote the proliferation and migration of mesenchymal stem cells, exacerbating the progression of COPD [10]. However, the function and mechanism of AEc‐Exos in IPF regulation are currently unclear, and further study of the molecular mechanism of AEc‐Exos in IPF may be helpful in the development of new IPF therapeutics.

Autophagy, a pathway for preserving intracellular degradation through evolution, relies on the breakdown of cytoplasmic proteins or lysosomal organelles and plays a vital role in maintaining cellular equilibrium [11, 12]. Numerous research findings indicate a significant impact of autophagy on pulmonary fibrosis. For example, autophagy increases the expression levels of tumor necrosis factor and transforming growth factors, and modulating the inflammatory response can attenuate silica‐induced pulmonary fibrosis [13]. However, excessive macrophage autophagy exacerbates the pathogenesis of silicosis by promoting the proliferation and migration of lung fibroblasts. These studies suggest that the normal process of autophagy can attenuate pulmonary fibrosis. However, there have been no reports on the influence of pulmonary epithelial cell‐derived exosomes on pulmonary fibrosis through the regulation of autophagy.

Therefore, in this study, we analyzed the mechanism by which AEc‐Exos affect pulmonary fibrosis by regulating autophagy, constructing a fibrosis model to provide new ideas for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Treatment

Mouse lung epithelial cells (MLE‐12, #BFN607200387) were purchased from the ATCC cell bank. Mouse lung fibroblasts (MLg, #BES‐2915HC) were purchased from Shanghai Bolsen Biotechnology Co. Ltd. and inoculated in DMEM (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in a humidified incubator at 5% CO2 at 37°C. After 12 h of cell culture, 10 ng/mL human recombinant protein (TGF‐β) was added, and the mixture was incubated for 24 h to induce fibrosis. To reduce the level of autophagy in the cells, we treated the cells with an autophagy inhibitor (CQ) at a concentration of 10 μM for 72 h.

Cell Transfection

Two hundred nanomolar concentrations [14] of antagomir‐NC or antagomir‐450b‐5p were diluted in 50 μL of DMEM, mixed, and incubated with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX in 50 μL of DMEM for 5 min at room temperature, after which the MLg cells were treated. Two micrograms of OE‐HDAC7 were transfected into MLg cells using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) transfection reagent. After the cells were transfected for 24 h, they were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF‐β at 37°C for 24 h, and cells without TGF‐β were added as controls. The cells were then collected for further experiments. The transfection efficiency was evaluated by RT–qPCR and Western blotting.

Isolation and Characterization of Exosomes

Exosomes are prepared from the cell supernatant through differential centrifugation, ultracentrifugation, and filtration steps. Briefly, the cell culture supernatant was collected and centrifuged twice: 300 × g for 10 min (pelleting floating cells) and 3000 × g for 15 min (pelleting cell debris). Exosome particles were collected via a series of ultracentrifugation methods: 1610000 × g for 30 min and 100 000 × g for 70 min. The pellet was washed once with PBS and ultracentrifuged again at 100 000 × g for 70 min. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was used to observe the particle size of the exosomes, which were morphologically observed via transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and the exosomes containing labeled CD63 and CD81 were identified via Western blotting. Exosomes were labeled using the PKH26 Fluorescent Labeling Kit (Sigma–Aldrich) and incubated with MLg cells for 12 h, followed by fixation and imaging of exosome‐treated cells.

Animal Husbandry and Model Construction

Forty SPF‐grade C57BL/6 mice, 6–8 weeks old and weighing 18–20 g, were purchased from the Laboratory Animal Research Center of Kunming Medical University (Kunming, China). After being maintained at a constant temperature of 24°C for 1 week of feeding with free access to drinking water, the mice were randomly divided into four experimental groups (10 animals in each group). (I) The control group was administered 50 μL of 0.9% normal saline via intratracheal injection. (II) In the bleomycin (BLM) group, 5 mg/kg BLM was dissolved in 50 μL 0.9% normal saline and administered intratracheally once. (III) In the BLM + Exos group, BLM was injected intratracheally, and MLE‐12 Exos were injected intraperitoneally. (IV) In the BLM + Exos + 450b‐5p antagomir group, each mouse was injected intratracheally with 10 nmoL of antagomir‐450b‐5p [15], and 72 h later, intratracheal injection of BLM and intraperitoneal injection of Exos were performed. The group‐treated mice were sacrificed after 28 days of rearing, and lung tissue was rapidly collected, part of which was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and part of which was stored in a −80°C freezer for subsequent experiments.

Cell Viability Assay Using Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8)

Log‐phase MLg cells were collected, adjusted to 4 × 104/mL, and seeded at 100 μL/well in 96‐well plates. The cells were then treated with TGF‐β according to the group of cells. After treatment, 10 μL of CCK‐8 working solution (CA1210, Solaibao, Beijing) was added and incubated for 2 h, and cell viability was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Biotek, Vermont, USA).

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT–qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using miRNA TRIzol Reagent (LM1803, LMAI Bio, Shanghai, China). The concentrations of all the miRNA samples were determined spectrophotometrically. cDNA was extracted from total RNA (1 μg) using a Prime Script RT kit (Takara Bio, Dalian, China). RT–qPCR was performed on a Bio‐Rad iQ5 RT–PCR detection system (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq II Kit (Takara Bio, Dalian, China). All samples were analyzed in triplicate, the expression levels of relevant genes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and U6 was used as an internal control. Western Blot

Western blotting was performed according to the standard protocol, and MLg cells were collected and treated for 24 h. Proteins were extracted from lung tissue and cells using a protein extraction kit (Solarbio, China). Proteins were separated on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated overnight at 4°C with the appropriate primary antibodies. Next, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:20000, ab97051, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature and subsequently incubated with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Millipore, USA). Finally, the protein bands were semiquantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

The primary antibodies used were α‐SMA (1:1000, ab28052, Abcam, UK), CD63 (1:1000, ab134045, Abcam, UK), CD81 (1:700, ab109201, Abcam, UK), fibronectin (1:1000, ab268020, Abcam, UK), collagen I (1:1000, ab138492, Abcam, UK), collagen III (1:1000, ab184993, Abcam, UK), LC3 (1:4000, ab232940, Abcam, UK), p62 (1:1000, ab207305, Abcam, UK), Beclin‐1 (1:1000, ab210498, Abcam, UK), and β‐actin (1:1000, ab8226, Abcam, UK).

Immunofluorescence

MLg cells in each group were collected, and α‐SMA expression was detected by immunofluorescence. The cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed 3 times in PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with diluted primary anti‐α‐SMA (1:1000, ab28052, Abcam, UK) or anti‐LC3 (1:4000, ab232940, Abcam, UK) antibodies overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000, ab154207, Abcam, UK) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, 5 μg/mL DAPI was added, and the samples were stained for 5 min. The results were observed and photographed under a fluorescence microscope (400 857, Nikon, Japan).

Monodansylcadaverine (MDC) Staining

Autophagy was detected using an autophagy staining kit (C3018S, Beyotime). To prepare the MDC working solution, 9 mL of the cell suspension was added to the EP tube, and then 1 mL of MDC dye was added and mixed gently. MLg cells were cultured on small round slides coated with polylysine. The cells were incubated with the fluorescent dye MDC (20 μM) for 30 min. PBS was added, and the cells were centrifuged twice at 179 × g (3 min each). The supernatant was discarded after centrifugation. The cell samples were then fixed with 40 μL of formaldehyde for 3 min and incubated in 40 μL of MDC (0.1 mmol/L) for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were centrifuged again before the cell concentration was adjusted with 1% paraformaldehyde. The samples were mixed with glycerin and observed with a microscope. Three replicates were established for each group.

Dual‐Luciferase Gene Reporters

To confirm the binding between miR‐450b‐5p and HDAC7, a mirGLO‐HDAC7‐WT/MUT luciferase reporter vector was constructed by inserting an HDAC7 3′ UTR wild‐type (WT) or mutant (MUT) fragment sequence containing a binding site with miR‐450b‐5p into the pmirGLO luciferase Target Expression Vector (PuFei Bio, Shanghai, China). HDAC7‐WT or HDAC7‐MUT was cotransfected with the miR‐450b‐5p mimic or the miR‐NC mimic into 293 T cells with Lipofectamine 2000 transfection reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA), and luciferase activity was detected with a dual‐luciferase reporter test cassette (PuFei Biologics, Shanghai, China) for 48 h.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining and Masson Staining

HE and Masson staining were used to detect the degree of pulmonary fibrosis. Mouse lung tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at room temperature and dehydrated with gradient ethanol. The samples were paraffin embedded and cut into 5 μm thick sections. The sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (G1120, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and a Masson's Tricolor Staining Kit (G1340, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Images were obtained under a microscope (CKX53, OLYMPUS, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin‐embedded mouse lung tissue was deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0). The slides were incubated with α‐SMA (1:500, ab223068, Abcam, UK) overnight at 4°C and subjected to immunodetection with an HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody and a DAB chromogenic agent. The results were photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Statistical Analysis

All the data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software and are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). A t test was used for comparisons between two groups, one‐way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) and post hoc Tukey tests were used for comparisons between multiple groups, and when p < 0.05, the difference was considered significant. All the experiments were repeated 3 times.

Results

Alveolar Epithelial Cells Inhibit the Fibrosis of Pulmonary Fibroblasts

To explore the effect of alveolar epithelial cells on lung fibroblasts, MLE‐12 cells were cocultured with TGF‐β‐induced MLg cells, and MLg cell viability was determined via a CCK‐8 assay. The results showed that TGF‐β induction enhanced MLg cell viability, whereas the presence of MLE‐12 cells inhibited MLg viability (Figure 1A). Western blotting was used to analyze the expression levels of the pulmonary fibrosis markers fibronectin, collagen I, collagen III, and α‐SMA. The results revealed that the expression levels of fibronectin, collagen I, collagen III, and α‐SMA in MLg cells were increased after TGF‐β treatment. Coculture with MLE‐12 cells effectively inhibited the upregulation of these proteins in MLg cells (Figure 1B). Immunofluorescence assays of α‐SMA expression also revealed that TGF‐β induction increased the expression of α‐SMA in MLE‐12 cells, which was attenuated after coculture with MLE‐12 cells (Figure 1C). These results suggest that alveolar epithelial cells can significantly reduce the degree of fibrosis in lung fibroblasts.

Alveolar Epithelial Cell‐Derived Exosomes Inhibit Pulmonary Fibrosis

To further investigate whether alveolar epithelial cells inhibit pulmonary fibroblast fibrosis through exosomal pathways, we pretreated MLE‐12 cells with the exosome (Exos) secretion inhibitor GW4869 before co‐culture with fibroblasts. Western blot analysis demonstrated that compared to the TGF‐β + MLE‐12 group, the TGF‐β + MLE‐12 + GW4869 group showed significantly increased expression levels of fibrosis markers including Fibronectin, Collagen I, Collagen III, and α‐SMA (Figure 2A), confirming that MLE‐12 cells primarily exert their anti‐fibrotic effects through exosome secretion. To directly validate the functional characteristics of MLE‐12‐derived exosomes, we isolated and purified exosomes from MLE‐12 cells and co‐cultured them with pulmonary fibroblasts. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the lung epithelial cell‐derived exosomes were cup‐shaped vesicles that were round or oval in shape (Figure 2B). The results of the nanoparticle tracking analysis revealed that the diameter of the exosomes ranged from 90 to 160 nm (Figure 2C). Western blotting was used to detect the expression levels of the exosome marker proteins CD63 and CD81, and the results revealed that the expression levels of the exosome markers CD63 and CD81 were greater in the Exos group than in the MLE‐12 group (Figure 2D). These experimental results fully demonstrate that we successfully isolated high‐purity exosomes from MLE‐12 cells. Exos were then co‐cultured with lung fibroblasts, PKH67‐labeled exosomes were detected for exosome uptake, and MLg was incubated with PKH67‐labeled Exos, which revealed that PKH67‐labeled MLE‐12‐Exos (green) infiltrated MLg cells (Figure 2E). The viability of the MLg cells was subsequently detected, and the results indicated that Exos inhibited the viability of the cells (Figure 2F). The expression levels of fibrosis‐associated proteins in the cells were then determined, and the results revealed that Exos significantly inhibited the protein expression of fibronectin, collagen I, collagen III, and α‐SMA in cells and the fluorescence intensity of α‐SMA (Figure 2G,H). These results indicate that exosomes derived from alveolar epithelial cells can inhibit the occurrence of pulmonary fibrosis.

Exosome‐Induced Autophagy Inhibits Pulmonary Fibrosis

Autophagy is a process of cellular self‐renewal that underlies intracellular degradation and is necessary to maintain cellular homeostasis [16]. Studies have shown that lung diseases such as IPF, pulmonary hypertension, and cystic fibrosis are associated with autophagy [17]. This study further explored whether exosomes can mediate autophagy and affect the process of fibrosis. Western blotting was used to detect the expression levels of the autophagy‐related proteins LC3II/I, Beclin1, and p62, and the results revealed that TGF‐β induction significantly decreased the expression of the LC3II/I and Beclin1 proteins and upregulated the expression of p62 and that Exos alleviated the induction effect of TGF‐β (Figure 3A). The immunofluorescence results also confirmed that Exos alleviated the decrease in the LC3 fluorescence intensity of TGF‐β (Figure 3B). MDC staining assays for autophagy revealed that TGF‐β inhibited autophagy and that Exos were able to alleviate the effects of TGF‐β (Figure 3C). We then used an autophagy inhibitor (CQ) to block autophagy. The assay results revealed that Exos treatment inhibited cell viability, which was increased with the addition of CQ (Figure 3D). Western blotting and immunofluorescence revealed that Exos reduced the expression of fibronectin, collagen I, collagen III, and α‐SMA and inhibited fibrosis in cells, whereas CQ weakened the inhibitory effect of Exos on fibrosis (Figure 3E,F) These results suggest that Exos can increase the level of autophagy in cells and hinder the occurrence of pulmonary fibrosis.

Exosomes Inhibit Fibrosis by Regulating miR‐450b‐5p

In the development of pulmonary fibrosis, a variety of miRNAs are associated with pulmonary fibrosis and play important regulatory roles. To explore the mechanism by which AEc‐Exos regulate pulmonary fibrosis through related miRNAs, we detected six miRNAs associated with pulmonary fibrosis, namely, miR‐186 [18], miR‐205 [19], miR‐320a‐3p [20], miR‐326 [21], miR‐16 [22] and miR‐450b‐5p [23], via RT–qPCR. The RT–qPCR results revealed that miR‐450b‐5p was expressed at the lowest level in the TGF‐β‐induced fibroblasts (Figure 4A). To verify whether AEc‐Exo exerts its regulatory function via miR‐450b‐5p delivery, we co‐cultured exosomes with fibroblasts and re‐examined miRNA expression. The data revealed that exosome treatment specifically upregulated miR‐450b‐5p expression in fibroblasts, while other miRNAs remained unchanged. These findings confirm that AEc‐Exo primarily modulates fibroblast fibrogenesis through transferring miR‐450b‐5p (Figure 4B). Then, the miR‐450b‐5p antagonist (miR‐450b‐5p antagomir) was transferred into MLE‐12 cells, and RT–qPCR revealed that the expression of miR‐450b‐5p was significantly downregulated, indicating that the transfection was successful (Figure 4C). Next, MLE‐12 cell exosomes transfected with the miR‐450b‐5p antagomir (miR‐450b‐5p antagomir‐Exos) were extracted and treated with MLg cells. RT–qPCR was subsequently performed to detect the expression of miR‐450b‐5p in MLg cells, which revealed that Exos effectively promoted the expression of miR‐450b‐5p in MLg cells; however, compared with that in the TGF‐β + Exos group, the expression of miR‐450b‐5p in the TGF‐β + miR‐450b‐5p antagomir‐Exos group was downregulated (Figure 4D). The autophagy of the cells was then observed. Compared with those in the TGF‐β + Exos group, the expression levels of LC3II/I and Beclin1 decreased, and the expression of p62 increased, whereas the fluorescence intensity of LC3 and the content of autophagosomes decreased in the TGF‐β + miR‐450b‐5p antagomir‐Exos group (Figure 4E–G). The expression levels of cellular fibrosis‐related factors were further determined. Compared with those in the TGF‐β + Exos group, the protein expression levels of fibronectin, collagen I, collagen III, and α‐SMA were increased in the TGF‐β + miR‐450b‐5p antagomir‐Exos group (Figure 4H,I). These results suggest that exosomes inhibit fibrosis by regulating miR‐450b‐5p.

miR‐450b‐5p Targets HDAC7

Predictions from the starBase (https://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) database revealed that HDAC7 was a target gene for miR‐450b‐5p, with a targeted binding site between the two (Figure 5A). The results of the dual‐luciferase reporter assay verified that miR‐450b‐5p significantly inhibited the fluorescence intensity of HDAC7‐WT, whereas the fluorescence intensity of HDAC7‐MUT was not significantly different from that of the control group (Figure 5B). Meanwhile, RIP assay results showed that miR‐450b‐5p was enriched in the immunoprecipitation complexes of HDAC7 protein, confirming the binding between the two (Figure 5C). The RT–qPCR results revealed that the expression of miR‐450b‐5p was significantly upregulated after transfection with the miR‐450b‐5p mimic, indicating that the transfection was successful (Figure 5D). The Western blot results revealed that miR‐450b‐5p mimic transfection significantly inhibited the expression of HDAC7 (Figure 5E), indicating that miR‐450b‐5p targeted and negatively regulated HDAC7.

(C) RIP verification of the binding relationship between miR‐450b‐5p and HDAC7. (D) RT–qPCR was used to detect the transfection efficiency of the miR‐450b‐5p mimic. Overexpression of HDAC7 Attenuated the Effect of Exosomes

To further explore the regulatory effect of HDAC7 on cell fibrosis, we transfected MLE‐12 cells with OE‐HDAC7. First, the transfection efficiency was detected, which revealed that the expression of HDAC7 in the OE‐HDAC7 group was significantly upregulated, indicating that the transfection was successful (Figure 6A). Next, MLE‐12 cell exosomes transfected with OE‐HDAC7 were extracted and used to treat MLg cells, and the autophagy of the cells was observed. Compared with those in the TGF‐β + Exos group, the expression levels of the autophagy‐related proteins LC3II/I and Beclin1 were downregulated, the expression of p62 was upregulated, the fluorescence level of LC3 was decreased, and the number of autophagosomes was reduced in the TGF‐β + OE‐HDAC7‐Exos group (Figure 6B–D). The fibrosis of the cells was further observed. Compared with those in the TGF‐β + Exos group, the expression levels of the fibrosis‐related proteins fibronectin, collagen I, collagen III, and α‐SMA were increased, and the fluorescence of α‐SMA was increased in the TGF‐β + OE‐HDAC7‐Exos group (Figure 6E,F). These results indicated that the overexpression of HDAC7 could attenuate the effects of MLE‐12 cell exosomes on the autophagy and fibrosis of MLg cells.

Exosomes Alleviate Pulmonary Fibrosis in Mice by Regulating miR‐450b‐5p

To further confirm that AEc‐Exos alleviate lung fibrosis in mice by regulating miR‐450b‐5p, we performed animal experiments. The expression of miR‐450b‐5p was first detected. Compared with that in the control group, the expression of miR‐450b‐5p in the BLM model group was significantly downregulated, and the expression of miR‐450b‐5p was restored to a certain extent after Exos treatment; however, the expression of miR‐450b‐5p was inhibited by Exos + miR‐450b‐5p antagomir treatment (Figure 7A). Western blot analysis revealed that the expression of HDAC7 could be inhibited by Exos treatment, but compared with Exos treatment alone, Exos + miR‐450b‐5p antagomir treatment promoted the expression of HDAC7 to a certain extent (Figure 7B). The results of HE and Masson staining revealed that the alveolar structure in the control group was intact, with no inflammatory infiltration foci and no widening of the interstitium. Compared with those in the control group, the alveolar structure of the lung tissue in the BLM group was damaged, the infiltration of inflammatory cells was increased, and severe fibrosis was observed. In the Exos treatment group, the inflammatory infiltration and damaged structures in the lung tissue were improved, and collagen deposition decreased. However, treatment with Exos + miR‐450b‐5p antagomir did not improve these effects (Figure 7C). Detection of the expression levels of fibrosis‐ and autophagy‐related proteins revealed that, compared with that in the control group, fibrosis was increased in the BLM group (as indicated by upregulated expression levels of fibronectin, collagen I, collagen III, and α‐SMA), and autophagy was inhibited (as indicated by downregulated expression levels of LC3II/I and Beclin1 and upregulated expression of p62). Treatment with Exos reversed these observations to a certain extent, but treatment with Exos + miR‐450b‐5p antagomir limited the function of Exos alone (Figure 7D–G). These results suggest that AEc‐Exos alleviate pulmonary fibrosis in mice by regulating miR‐450b‐5p.

Discussion

IPF is a progressive, fatal interstitial lung disease characterized by structural destruction of lung tissue, progressive fibrosis, and irreversible decline in lung function, eventually leading to respiratory failure [24, 25]. Preclinical treatment options are extremely limited, and aberrant activation of lung fibroblasts is thought to be a central part of the fibrosis process [26]. These activated fibroblasts not only overproliferate but also disrupt the normal structure of lung tissue by secreting large amounts of extracellular matrix components, such as type I collagen and fibronectin [27, 28]. In this study, we systematically evaluated the regulatory effect of AEc‐Exos on the expression levels of fibrosis markers (α‐SMA, collagen I/III, and fibronectin) in a TGF‐β‐induced fibroblast activation model and revealed the molecular mechanism of the miR‐450b‐5p/HDAC7/autophagy axis in this process.

As important mediators of cell–cell communication, exosomes play dual regulatory roles in lung diseases [29, 30]. Previous studies have shown that exosomes can promote both alveolar development and angiogenesis [31] and regulate fibrosis through the delivery of specific miRNAs. Our study revealed that alveolar epithelial cell‐derived Exos significantly inhibited fibroblast activation, an effect echoing the recently reported antifibrotic effects of mesenchymal stem cell exosomes [32, 33]. This study further confirmed that it significantly improved both the TGF‐β‐ and bleomycin‐induced fibrosis models.

At the mechanistic level, miRNAs carried by exosomes constitute the material basis of their biological effects. Through systematic analysis, we found that miR‐450b‐5p was significantly downregulated in fibrotic samples, providing an interesting contrast to previously reported applications for the diagnosis of lung cancer [34]. Experiments have confirmed that Exos inhibit fibrosis by delivering miR‐450b‐5p. However, the pathogenesis of IPF involves a complex miRNA regulatory network. In addition to miR‐450b‐5p, other exosomal miRNAs may also be involved in the regulation of pulmonary fibrosis. Although we have examined several miRNAs associated with pulmonary fibrosis and reported that miR‐450b‐5p is the most differentially expressed molecule, the potential contributions of other miRNAs need to be ruled out through loss‐of‐function experiments in the future. Future studies can identify other molecules that may synergize with or antagonize miR‐450b‐5p through exosomal miRNA sequencing or functional screening to more fully elucidate the therapeutic effects of exosomes. In addition, exosomes are multicomponent carriers, and in addition to miRNAs, other proteins (e.g., TGF‐β‐binding proteins) and lipid mediators (e.g., sphingosine‐1‐phosphate) may be synergistically involved in regulation. In the future, it will be necessary to exclude confounding effects through the selective exclusion of exosome components (e.g., RNase treatment) or single‐miRNA backfill experiments.

In this study, it was found that miR‐450b‐5p has an inhibitory effect on fibrosis, which is different from the profibrotic effects of miR‐590 [35] and miR‐125a‐5p [36] reported in previous studies. Furthermore, through the dual luciferase gene reporter assay, this study also confirmed that miR‐450b‐5p exerts its protective effect by uniquely regulating the target protein HDAC7. As a key molecule in epigenetic regulation, the profibrotic effect of HDAC7 was fully verified in this study. We found that HDAC7 overexpression not only counteracted the protective effect of Exos but also directly inhibited autophagy (i.e., by attenuating the expression of the LC3II/I and Beclin1 proteins and upregulating the expression of p62) and promoted collagen deposition. These findings expand the current understanding of the HDAC family [37, 38], suggesting that specific targeting of HDAC7 may be a new strategy to intervene in fibrosis. Notably, the increase in p62 may reflect its accumulation due to the obstruction of autophagic flow; however, the increase in p62, a selective autophagic aptamer, may have a compensatory protective effect, maintaining cellular homeostasis by enhancing the clearance efficiency of misfolded proteins. In the future, the specific role of p62 accumulation in this model can be clarified by observing the effects of p62 knockout on the fibrosis process. Of particular concern is the dual nature of autophagy regulation in IPF therapy: moderate activation of autophagy can alleviate epithelial–mesenchymal transition [39], but overactivation may lead to an imbalance in cellular homeostasis. In this study, precise intervention at the level of autophagy was achieved by precisely regulating the miR‐450b‐5p–HDAC7 axis, which was both mutually corroborated and innovative with respect to the mechanism of action of drugs such as etoposide [40] and molecules such as IL‐37 [41]. Although we have demonstrated that miR‐450b‐5p activates autophagy and inhibits fibrosis by targeting HDAC7, how HDAC7 inhibition precisely regulates autophagy still needs further elucidation. As a member of the histone deacetylase family, HDAC7 may affect the expression of autophagy‐related genes such as those in the ATG family or the mTOR pathway through epigenetic modifications. In addition, whether HDAC7 regulates autophagy by interacting with other transcription factors, such as FOXO3 or TFEB, still needs to be experimentally verified. Future research will focus on the downstream signaling network of HDAC7 to reveal a more comprehensive molecular mechanism.

In summary, this study demonstrates that alveolar epithelial cell‐derived exosomes attenuate pulmonary fibrosis progression by modulating the miR‐450b‐5p/HDAC7 signaling axis to activate autophagy, offering novel insights into the autophagy–fibrosis interplay and potential therapeutic targets for exosome‐based precision medicine. Moving forward, future investigations will focus on refining disease models by integrating silica‐induced progressive fibrosis and TERT‐mutated aged mouse models to better recapitulate the chronic progression of human IPF. Additionally, therapeutic potential will be further explored through fibrosis reversal experiments, optimized dosing regimens (including dose–response and frequency assessments), and comprehensive safety evaluations using miRNA target prediction combined with histopathological analysis. These efforts will strengthen the translational relevance of our findings and facilitate the development of safer, more effective exosome‐based therapies.
