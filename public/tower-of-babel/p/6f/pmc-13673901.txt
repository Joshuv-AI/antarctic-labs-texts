Explaining changes in socioeconomic inequality in catastrophic health expenditure in China: a mobility-determinant decomposition
Authors: Jingxian Wu, Yaping Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCatastrophic health expenditure (CHE) remains a central challenge to achieving financial protection and equity in health systems. While extensive evidence shows that CHE remains disproportionately concentrated among socioeconomically disadvantaged households, most studies adopt a static perspective and provide limited insights into how CHE inequality evolves over time and through which mechanisms. This study aims to examine changes in socioeconomic inequality in CHE in China and disentangle the underlying channels from a household mobility perspective.MethodsUsing nationally representative longitudinal data from the 2010 and 2020 waves of the China Family Panel Studies, socioeconomic-related CHE inequality was measured using Erreygers’ corrected concentration index, with household per capita wealth as the ranking variable. An integrated mobility–determinant decomposition framework was employed to quantify the contributions of both mobility processes and socioeconomic, demographic, and health system-related factors to changes in CHE inequality. Robustness analyses were performed using alternative CHE definitions, different specifications of household socioeconomic status, and an alternative decomposition approach.ResultsCHE remained disproportionately concentrated among lower-wealth households in both waves, although the magnitude of inequality declined significantly between 2010 and 2020. This reduction was primarily driven by progressive contribution of wealth-related CHE mobility, indicating larger improvements in financial protection among initially poorer households, while the regressive contribution of CHE-related wealth mobility partially offset these gains. Lower wealth quintiles, urban residence, and social health insurance coverage were associated with the reduction in CHE inequality, whereas higher wealth quintiles and household ageing were associated with attenuating the inequality reduction, reflecting heterogenous contributions through distinct mobility channels. Robustness checks confirmed the stability of the main findings.ConclusionsSocioeconomic inequality in CHE in China has narrowed over the past decade, primarily due to progressive improvements in financial protection. However, persistent wealth stratification and population ageing continue to constrain further reductions in CHE inequality. Monitoring progress toward universal health coverage requires attention not only to aggregate CHE incidence but also to the underlying mobility processes and structural determinants that influence distributional change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-026-00825-w.

Introduction

Catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) remains a central challenge to achieving financial protection and equity in health systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where health financing continues to rely heavily on out-of-pocket (OOP) payments [1]. In 2019, more than one billion people worldwide were estimated to have experienced CHE [2], highlighting its persistent threat to household welfare and economic security. Beyond its immediate financial consequences, CHE can exacerbate socioeconomic disparities by disproportionately burdening households with limited economic resources and increasing their risk of long-term financial distress and impoverishment [3]. As countries advance toward universal health coverage (UHC), the overall decline in CHE incidence is often used as a primary indicator of progress in financial protection [4]. However, a reduction in aggregate incidence does not necessarily imply a reduction in socioeconomic inequality. Without examining the socioeconomic distribution of CHE and how this inequality evolves over time, it remains unclear whether progress toward UHC reflects genuine gains in financial protection for disadvantaged households or merely aggregate improvements that may mask persistent underlying disparities.

A substantial body of empirical research documents that CHE is disproportionately concentrated among households with lower socioeconomic status (SES) [5–13]. More recently, attention has shifted from static assessments toward examining trends in socioeconomic-related inequality in CHE, most commonly using repeated cross-sectional survey data [14–17]. These studies typically compute concentration indices for each survey wave and compare inequality levels across time. Some further apply regression-based decomposition methods to attribute observed changes in CHE inequality to variations in measurable determinants [11, 14, 15, 18–21]. While such approaches provide valuable evidence on aggregate distributional changes in CHE status and their correlates, they implicitly assume a relatively stable socioeconomic ordering and cannot reveal the household-level processes through which these changes occur. In reality, however, households may move up or down the socioeconomic distribution over time [22], while their exposure to CHE may change in response to evolving health needs, economic opportunities, and social protection policies [23]. Consequently, the same observed changes in CHE inequality may arise from distinct processes, including accumulation or dissipation of financial hardship, socioeconomic re-ranking of households, and their underlying determinants. Repeated cross-sectional analyses, even when combined with decomposition techniques, fail to disentangle these mechanisms because they do not link changes in SES and CHE status within the same households over time, thereby limiting their ability to inform policies aimed at sustainably reducing inequality in financial protection.

To address these limitations, health economists have increasingly drawn on the concept of mobility—originally developed in the income inequality literature [24]—to examine the dynamics of socioeconomic inequality in health-related outcomes using longitudinal data. Early work by Jones and López Nicolás introduced measures of health-related income mobility, demonstrating that changes in individuals’ economic positions can substantially alter assessments of socioeconomic health inequality [25]. Building on this foundation, Allanson et al. [26] proposed a formal longitudinal decomposition framework that distinguishes two complementary mobility processes: SES-related health mobility and health-related SES mobility. The former captures changes in individuals’ health outcomes while holding their socioeconomic rank constant, indicating whether health improvements or deteriorations are systematically concentrated among those initially better-off or worse-off. The latter reflects changes in socioeconomic rankings conditional on health outcomes, revealing whether individuals experiencing changes in health outcomes tend to move up or down the socioeconomic distribution over time. By separating inequality changes arising from shifts in health status from those driven by socioeconomic re-ranking, this mobility-based decomposition framework provides a structured account of the dynamic sources of socioeconomic health inequality. Subsequent applications to health outcomes [27, 28], healthcare utilization [29, 30], and health expenditures [31] further highlight the value of this approach in uncovering individual-level dynamic processes in inequality evolution that remain obscured in repeated cross-sectional analyses. However, applications to financial protection outcomes such as CHE remain limited, despite the fact that households’ exposure to catastrophic payments is likely to be closely connected with socioeconomic mobility processes.

Conceptually, CHE differs from many health indicators in that it represents an economic shock from illness that may both reflect and influence household socioeconomic position. Severe health shocks are also accompanied by increased OOP spending and can contribute to asset depletion or indebtedness, while changes in income or financial protection policies may alter exposure to CHE. This bidirectional relationship suggests that SES-related CHE mobility and CHE-related SES mobility may jointly shape the evolution of socioeconomic-related CHE inequality. A mobility-based perspective therefore allows a more comprehensive understanding of whether observed changes in CHE inequality reflect shifts in financial protection across socioeconomic groups or changes in the socioeconomic composition of households experiencing CHE.

More recently, methodological advances have sought to integrate mobility-based decomposition with determinant-based approaches to better characterize how socioeconomic health inequality changes over time. By incorporating socioeconomic, demographic, and health system-related factors within a longitudinal mobility framework, this integrated approach allows researchers to identify not only whether inequality changes are driven by the two distinct mobility processes, but also how observed determinants are associated with these processes [32, 33]. Hashmi et al. [34], for instance, combine Allanson et al.’s mobility framework [26] with Wagstaff et al.’s regression-based decomposition approaches [35] to examine how key determinants are associated with changes in health inequality through different mobility channels. However, evidence remains limited regarding how socioeconomic inequality in CHE evolves over time and through which mobility channels these changes occur.

China provides a particularly informative context for addressing these gaps. Since 2009, China’s health system has undergone substantial structural transformation, including expansions in social health insurance coverage and increased public investment in basic healthcare [36], alongside broader socioeconomic and demographic changes such as rapid income growth, persistent economic inequality [37, 38], population ageing, and an increasing burden of chronic diseases [39]. These concurrent developments suggest that both households’ socioeconomic positions and their exposure to CHE may have evolved substantially over time, making China a useful setting to study the dynamic evolution of CHE inequality within a context of rapid socioeconomic and institutional change.

In this study, we aim to examine changes in socioeconomic inequality in CHE in China using longitudinal household data from the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS). Socioeconomic CHE inequality is measured using Erreygers’ concentration index, with household per capita wealth serving as the socioeconomic ranking variable. Building on the integrated mobility-determinant decomposition framework proposed by Hashmi et al. (2024) [34], we quantify the contributions of household mobility processes and socioeconomic, demographic, and health system-related factors to changes in CHE inequality between 2010 and 2020. Specifically, we address three research questions: (i) how did socioeconomic inequality in CHE change over this period in terms of direction and magnitude; (ii) to what extent did wealth-related CHE mobility and CHE-related wealth mobility contribute to the observed changes in CHE inequality; and (iii) through which mobility channels were key determinants associated with these changes?

This study contributes to the literature in two ways. First, moving beyond static or repeated cross-sectional assessments, we adopt a household-level mobility perspective to examine the evolution of socioeconomic-related CHE inequality. This framework allows us to distinguish whether changes in inequality are driven by shifts in CHE exposure among different socioeconomic groups or by re-ranking within the wealth distribution, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of the mechanisms underlying socioeconomically distributional change in CHE status. Second, by integrating longitudinal mobility analysis with determinant-based decomposition, this study identifies the specific mobility channels through which socioeconomic, demographic, and health system-related factors are associated with changes in CHE inequality. This approach provides a structured explanation of distributional change and offers evidence relevant to understanding equity-oriented progress toward UHC in China and comparable settings.

Method

Data source

Data for this study were drawn from the CFPS, a nationally representative longitudinal household survey of Chinese communities, families, and individuals. The CFPS covers 25 out of the 31 provinces or municipalities in mainland China, representing approximately 95% of the national population [40]. Administered by the Institute of Social Science Survey at Peking University, the CFPS employs a multistage, implicitly stratified, population-proportional-to-size sampling design to ensure national representativeness [40]. The baseline survey was conducted in 2010, and all original household members and their biological or adopted children have been followed in biennial waves. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews by trained interviewers using standardized questionnaires, covering detailed information on demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, health status, family relationships, and migration histories. Ethical approval for the CFPS was granted by the Peking University Biomedical Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-14010), and informed consent was obtained from all respondents. Further details on survey design and data quality are available elsewhere [40].

For the present study, we used the 2010 baseline and 2020 follow-up wave to construct a balanced household panel for the longitudinal decomposition of socioeconomic-related CHE inequality. Although a more recent wave is available, key socioeconomic and health expenditure indicators in the 2022 survey may be affected by disruptions associated with the COVID-19 pandemic. To preserve comparability and avoid contamination of long-term trend estimates, our analysis was therefore restricted to the 2010–2020 period. The baseline sample comprised 14,797 households. After excluding households with unmatched household-head identifiers, missing key variables, or incomplete follow-up information, the final analytic sample consisted of 6,599 households successfully tracked in 2020. Explanatory variables

The explanatory variables were selected as potential determinants of CHE based on previous studies [5, 14, 18–21] and Andersen’s behavioral model of health services utilization [51, 52]. Originally developed to explain patterns of healthcare use, the Andersen model has been increasingly applied in financial protection research [53, 54], as CHE results from the complex relationship between health needs, healthcare utilization, and household economic resources. Following this framework, the determinants of CHE were categorized into predisposing, enabling, and need-related factors. Apart from household-level factors, key characteristics of the household head, who often plays a central role in healthcare decision-making and household financial management, were included in the analysis [53, 55]. Following previous studies [56], the household head was identified using the survey question: “When your family encounters important matters and decisions have to be made, who has the final say?”. This definition was consistently applied across survey waves to ensure comparability of head-level characteristics over time. In cases where the reported household head changed between waves, covariates were updated accordingly to reflect the characteristics of the current decision-making head at each observation point.

Predisposing factors capture demographic and socio-structural characteristics that influence a household’s propensity to utilize healthcare services and [52], consequently, its risk of incurring CHE. These factors included household size (1 member, 2–4 members, ≥ 5 members), the presence of at least one member aged 60 years or older (yes = 1, no = 0), and the presence of at least one member aged 5 years or younger (yes = 1, no = 0). Characteristics of the household head were also incorporated, including sex (male, female), age (16–44 years, 45–59 years, ≥ 60 years), marital status (unmarried, married, divorced/widowed), educational attainment (primary school and below, junior secondary school, senior secondary school, university and above), and occupation (not employed, formal employment, informal employment, farmer). Enabling factors refer to the economic and structural resources that facilitate access to healthcare and determine a household’s capacity to bear OOP payments [52]. These factors primarily included household wealth status and health insurance coverage. Household wealth was prioritized over income due to its greater stability and more reliable measurement, particularly in rural China [57, 58]. Household wealth was constructed by aggregating land, housing, financial and production assets, and durable goods, net of liabilities [58]. It was measured as household per capita wealth for ranking purpose when estimating the Erreygers’ Concentration Index (ECI), and was further categorized into wealth quintiles (quintile 1 = poorest, quintile 5 = wealthiest) when used as an explanatory variable. Health insurance coverage was measured in accordance with the institutional arrangements of China’s fragmented health insurance system [36], with household-level insurance coverage measures constructed, given that medical expenditures and financial risk are typically pooled and shared within households. Two categorical variables were created to measure the household-level coverage by social health insurance and supplementary health insurance, respectively. Social health insurance coverage was categorized as: (i) fully uninsured households (no household member enrolled in any social health insurance scheme); (ii) households with at least one member covered by employment-based Urban Employees’ Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI); and (iii) households covered exclusively by non-employment-based Urban and Rural Residents’ Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI). Supplementary health insurance coverage was measured as: (i) fully uninsured households (no household member covered by any supplementary health insurance program); and (ii) household with at least one member covered by supplementary health insurance programs, either voluntarily purchased by the household or provided through employers. This classification is consistent with commonly used approaches in health financing studies that distinguish between formal and informal sector coverage as well as public–private insurance combinations, particularly in fragmented insurance systems [59].2 To account for structural disparities in healthcare access, geographic region (eastern, central, western, northeastern China) [60] and place of residence (urban, rural) were also included.

Need-related factors reflect underlying health conditions that drive healthcare utilization [52] and directly influence a household’s likelihood of incurring CHE [53, 54]. These factors included the number of household members diagnosed with chronic diseases over the past six months (none, one, two or more), and whether any household member experienced hospitalization in the past year (yes = 1, no = 0).

All monetary variables, including health spending, household consumption, and wealth, were deflated to constant 2010 prices using the Consumer Price Index and converted to U.S. dollars based on the 2010 average exchange rate [61] to ensure comparability across survey waves.

Measurement of socioeconomic inequality in catastrophic health expenditure

Socioeconomic inequality in CHE was assessed using the Concentration Index (CI), a widely used measure that quantifies the covariance between a health outcome variable and the fractional rank of SES [35]. While the standard CI is appropriate for continuous variable, its application to binary outcomes such as CHE is problematic. In particular, it violates the mirror property—where inequality in attainments does not correspond symmetrically to inequality in shortfalls—and fails to satisfy cardinal invariance when the outcome is bounded [62]. These limitations have motivated the development of corrected concentration indices specifically designed for variables with fixed upper and lower limits, among which the adjustments proposed by Wagstaff [62] and Erreygers [63] are the most commonly applied.

For binary outcomes, the interpretation of both relative and absolute inequality is constrained because proportionate or additive changes cannot be defined symmetrically at the boundaries of the outcome space [64]. Unlike the Wagstaff’s adjusted CI, which preserves a purely relative interpretation, the ECI provides a coherent framework that incorporates both quasi-relative and quasi-absolute features [64]. Importantly, the ECI satisfies all key axioms required for meaningful measurement of inequality in bounded variables, including the mirror, transfer, cardinal invariance, and level-independence properties [64]. Given these desirable characteristics and its widespread use in the CHE literature [13, 15], we therefore employed the ECI to measure socioeconomic inequality in CHE, using household per capita wealth as the SES ranking variable.

To characterize changes in socioeconomic-related CHE inequality over time, we adopted the longitudinal decomposition framework proposed by Hashmi et al. [34], which integrates the mobility-based approach of Allanson et al. [26] with the determinant-based decomposition method of Wagstaff et al. [65]. Within this framework, the change in socioeconomic CHE inequality between the initial period t=1 (2010) and the final period t=T (2020), measured by the difference in the ECI, can be decomposed into components attributable to mobility processes and their underlying determinants.

Unlike traditional decomposition approaches such as Oaxaca-type decompositions, which attribute changes in inequality to variations in covariates and their coefficients [19, 70], the present framework decomposes the observed changes in inequality into components associated with differences in distributional structure and rank-dependent associations across periods. By embedding the decomposition within a rank-based inequality measure, our approach distinguishes between changes in CHE inequality driven by household wealth re-ranking and those arising from differential changes in CHE status across the wealth distribution. This allows observed determinants to be related not only to the magnitude of inequality change, but also to the specific mobility channels through which they operate—an aspect that is not captured by conventional decomposition methods.

All analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, Texas, USA). Standard errors for inequality and mobility estimates were obtained using a bootstrap procedure with 1,000 replications [71], and statistical significance was assessed at the 5% level.

Table 3 further shows that the contributions of key determinants to changes in CHE inequality differed across the two mobility indices. Lower household wealth quintiles were positively associated with the reduction in CHE inequality primarily through their positive contributions to wealth-related CHE mobility, which outweighed their negative contributions to CHE-related wealth mobility. In contrast, higher household wealth quintiles were negatively associated with the reduction in CHE inequality due to their negative contributions to both mobility indices. Urban residence was positively related to the reduction in CHE inequality mainly through its positive contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility. UEBMI coverage was also positively linked with the reduction in CHE inequality, as its positive contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility offset its negative contribution to CHE-related wealth mobility. By contrast, ageing household was negatively associated with the reduction in CHE inequality, because its negative contributions to CHE-related wealth mobility outweighed its positive contributions to wealth-related CHE mobility.

Robustness checks

Alternative measure of CHE based on CTP

The longitudinal decomposition was re-estimated using the 40% threshold under the normative subsistence spending approach proposed by the WHO Regional Office for Europe [45]. Detailed results are reported in Table S3 in the Supplementary file. The results are broadly consistent with the baseline analysis in Table 3, with wealth-related CHE mobility continuing to represent the main contributor to changes in predicted socioeconomic-related CHE inequality. The direction and relative ranking of key determinant-specific contributions were also largely unchanged. In particular, household wealth quintiles, urban residence, age of the household head, presence of older household members, and UEBMI coverage remained the main contributors to the decline in CHE inequality, operating through mobility channels consistent with those observed in the baseline analysis. Overall, these findings suggest that the baseline results are not sensitive to the alternative definition of CHE based on CTP.

Alternative threshold for defining CHE

To further assess robustness, a rank-dependent threshold that assigns greater weight to financial protection among poorer households was applied [48]. Results are reported in Table S3 in the Supplementary file. Wealth-related CHE mobility again emerged as the dominant contributor to changes in CHE inequality, while the key determinants identified in the baseline specification continued to play important roles. Specifically, reductions in CHE inequality are primarily associated with lower household wealth quintiles, urban residence, and UEBMI coverage through mobility channels consistent with the baseline results. In contrast, higher household wealth quintiles, older household head age, and the presence of older household members are associated with smaller reductions in CHE inequality, operating through the same mobility mechanisms as in the main analysis. These results confirm the robustness of the baseline findings to alternative threshold specifications.

Alternative specification of socioeconomic status

To address the potential concern that household per capita wealth was used both as the ranking variable in the ECI and, in grouped form, as an explanatory variable in the decomposition, two robustness checks using alternative socioeconomic status specifications were conducted.

First, household per capita wealth was retained as the ranking variable in the ECI, while household income quintiles were used as an alternative grouping variable in the decomposition. As shown in Table S4 in the Supplementary file, household income quintiles, urban residence, presence of older household members, age of the household head, and UEBMI coverage continue to play important roles in explaining changes in CHE inequality, with contribution patterns broadly similar to those in the baseline results.

Second, household per capita income was used as the ranking variable in the ECI, while wealth quintiles were retained in the decomposition. The results, reported in Tables S5 and S6 in the Supplementary file, are broadly consistent with the baseline findings. Wealth-related CHE mobility remains the dominant contributor to changes in predicted CHE inequality, and the overall pattern of determinant contributions remains stable. Lower wealth quintiles, urban residence, and UEBMI coverage are associated with larger reductions in CHE inequality through mobility channels, whereas higher wealth quintiles, older household head age, and the presence of older household members are associated with smaller reductions. Overall, the main findings are robust to alternative specifications of socioeconomic status.

Comparison with the Oaxaca-type decomposition

To benchmark the integrated longitudinal decomposition against a commonly used alternative approach, an Oaxaca-type decomposition of changes in CHE inequality was conducted [70]. Detailed results are provided in Table S7 in the Supplementary file. The decomposition yields determinant-level contribution patterns that are broadly consistent in direction and relative ordering with those obtained from the integrated mobility-based framework. Key determinants identified in the baseline analysis—including household wealth quintiles, urban residence, UEBMI coverage, age of the household head, and presence of older household members—remain qualitatively consistent across approaches, with no changes in the direction of their contributions. In addition, the Oaxaca-type decomposition was applied to the unbalanced panel sample. Results are reported in Table S8 in the Supplementary file. The contribution patterns from the unbalanced sample are largely consistent with those from the balanced panel in the main analysis, suggesting that the main findings are not sensitive to sample selection. Overall, these comparisons indicate that the observed decomposition patterns are stable across alternative methodological specifications.

Potential impact of the COVID-19 pandemic

The 2020 wave of CFPS was conducted between July and December 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although all monetary components used in the CHE calculation refer to expenditures incurred in the previous calendar year and are therefore less likely to be directly affected by contemporaneous pandemic-related disruptions, some non-monetary socioeconomic characteristics may still have been influenced by the pandemic context. To assess potential sensitivity to COVID-19, we compared the main 2010–2020 results with a pre-pandemic period covering 2010–2018. Detailed results are provided in Tables S9 and S10 in the Supplementary file.

The 2010–2018 results are broadly consistent with the main findings. The absolute value of the ECI for CHE decreased from 0.1231 in 2010 to 0.1032 in 2018, corresponding to a statistically significant reduction of 0.0199 (p < 0.05). Wealth-related CHE mobility remains the dominant contributor to changes in predicted socioeconomic-related CHE inequality, and the main determinant patterns, including household wealth quintiles and the presence of older household members, are consistent with those observed in the full-period analysis. Some differences in determinant contributions are also observed between the two periods. Superficially, in the 2010–2018 decomposition, urban residence and UEBMI coverage are no longer among the leading contributors, while other determinants such as household size and educational attainment of the household head show relatively larger contributions. By contrast, formal employment of the household head is associated with offsetting contributions within the mobility channels. However, these differences reflect only variations in decomposition patterns across periods rather than structural changes in the decomposition framework.

Discussion

Despite growing attention to financial protection under UHC, relatively little is known about how socioeconomic-related inequality in CHE evolves over time and through which mechanisms such changes occur. Using nationally representative longitudinal data from the 2010 and 2020 waves of the CFPS, this study examines the changes in socioeconomic CHE inequality through an integrated mobility-determinant decomposition framework. CHE remained disproportionately concentrated among lower-wealth households over the study period, with the magnitude of this inequality declining over time. This declining pattern aligns with earlier evidence from China [18] and parallels trends reported in other low- and middle-income settings [16, 72]. The 2020 ECI (−0.1034), however, indicates that a non-negligible degree of socioeconomic inequality in CHE persists in China. Its magnitude appears larger than that reported in several other low- and middle-income settings, such as Cambodia [15], despite recent improvements. Importantly, the decomposition results indicate that the observed reduction in CHE inequality was primarily driven by disproportionate improvements in financial protection among initially poorer households, reflected in a progressive contribution of wealth-related CHE mobility. At the same time, CHE-related wealth mobility exhibited an offsetting regressive contribution, reflecting the role of movement in households’ relative wealth positions in shaping the inequality change. When mobility processes and determinants were jointly considered, lower household wealth quintiles, urban residence, and UEBMI coverage were positively associated with the decline in CHE inequality, whereas higher household wealth quintiles and household ageing were negatively associated, indicating heterogeneity in the underlying mobility channels. Robustness analyses using alternative CHE definitions, alternative specifications of SES, and an alternative decomposition approach yielded broadly consistent results. To the best of our knowledge, this study is among the first to systematically decompose the changes in socioeconomic-related CHE inequality in China, highlighting the importance of integrating household-level mobility processes with structural determinants when evaluating distributional progress in financial protection towards UHC.

The predominance of a progressive contribution from wealth-related CHE mobility to the decline of CHE inequality is consistent with the observed reduction of approximately six percentage points in overall CHE incidence, suggesting that improvements in financial protection disproportionately benefited initially poorer households. These changes coincided with a period of expanded financial protection following China’s New Medical Reform in 2009, including broader insurance coverage, enhanced benefit generosity [67], and the introduction of Critical Illness Insurance and medical assistance programs [73]. These institutional developments provide a broader context for strengthened protection against high OOP medical expenditures, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged households, and are consistent with a more progressive distribution of financial protection gains. Nevertheless, the persistence of a regressive contribution from CHE-related wealth mobility suggests that catastrophic expenditure events remained associated with adverse changes in household wealth positions for some affected households, reflecting continuing wealth stratification and unequal resilience to financial shocks in China. Overall, although this channel tended to exert upward pressure on CHE inequality, its influence may have been outweighed by broader and more progressive improvements in financial protection over the study period.

Further decomposition highlights household wealth as a dominant determinant of changes in socioeconomic-related CHE inequality, operating through distinct mobility channels across wealth quintiles. Lower wealth quintiles were positively associated with the decline in CHE inequality, as their positive contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility exceeded the offsetting contribution from CHE-related wealth mobility. This pattern is consistent with the earlier finding that reductions in CHE incidence were disproportionately concentrated among initially poorer households [11]. At the same time, the negative contribution of lower wealth groups to CHE-related wealth mobility indicates that those who incurred CHE were more likely to suffer adverse wealth consequence. This pattern is consistent with persistent illness-induced financial vulnerability among socioeconomically disadvantaged households in China [74]. Nonetheless, the distributional gains associated with declining CHE incidence among poorer households were sufficiently large to outweigh these adverse wealth-related contributions, resulting in a net reduction in CHE inequality.

In contrast, higher household wealth quintiles were negatively associated with the reduction in CHE inequality through both mobility channels. Their negative contributions to wealth-related CHE mobility may reflect relatively smaller distributional gains from declining CHE incidence, given their already low baseline exposure [18]. At the same time, their negative contributions to CHE-related wealth mobility suggest a stronger capacity to maintain relative wealth positions in the presence of health-related financial shocks. The observed pattern is consistent with increasing wealth concentration among higher-wealth households in China over the past decade [75], which may be associated with greater stability in their position within the wealth distribution. Together, these results suggest that higher-wealth households are associated with both lower exposure to catastrophic expenditures and weaker redistribution effects through CHE events, thereby contributing negatively to the reduction in CHE inequality.

Place of residence also emerged as an important structural factor associated with changes in socioeconomic CHE inequality. Specifically, urban residence was positively related to the reduction in CHE inequality primarily through its positive contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility, suggesting relatively greater improvements in CHE risk among lower-wealth urban households compared with their rural counterparts. One plausible explanation for this pattern relates to differences in effective financial protection across residential settings. Urban households may have been more likely to benefit from stronger risk pooling and more effective coverage during the reform period, including better access to higher-quality health services, more convenient reimbursement arrangements, and broader availability of insured medicines and services [76]. These institutional advantages may be associated with lower OOP burdens, particularly for lower-wealth households for whom even moderate health shocks can result in catastrophic spending. In addition, rapid urbanization provides a broader contextual background for these findings. In our study, 5.38% of households classified as rural in 2010 transitioned to urban residence by 2020. Such rural-to-urban transitions may be associated with improved economic opportunities, expanded social security coverage, and greater access to healthcare services, which may in turn reduce the risk of exceeding catastrophic thresholds [15, 77]. By contrast, poorer rural households may continue to face barriers in accessing timely care and adequate financial protection [78], which may limit the extent of their improvements in CHE risk.

Social health insurance coverage also played a distinct institutional role in shaping changes in socioeconomic-related CHE inequality. In particular, coverage under UEBMI, a relatively more generous scheme for formal employees, was positively associated with the reduction in CHE inequality. This pattern was primarily driven by its positive contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility, which outweighed its modest negative contribution to CHE-related wealth mobility. UEBMI is a mandatory scheme financed through contributions from both employers and employees. It is characterized by higher reimbursement rates, broader benefit packages, and more integrated pooling arrangements, compared to the resident-based URRBMI scheme. These institutional features are associated with lower OOP burdens and CHE risk [79, 80], which is consistent with a stronger progressive contribution of wealth-related CHE mobility to overall inequality reduction. At the same time, its limited negative contribution to CHE-related wealth mobility indicates that UEBMI coverage was not strongly linked to changes in households’ relative wealth positions following health shocks. Overall, UEBMI coverage tends to be associated with a net equalizing contribution to CHE inequality.

Meanwhile, URRBMI coverage also demonstrated an inequality-reducing tendency, mainly through its positive contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility. This pattern is consistent with expanded financial protection for rural and low-income populations following reforms aimed at integrating urban and rural resident insurance schemes and broadening benefit coverage [11, 80, 81]. However, the overall contribution of URRBMI was relatively smaller, which is consistent with its weaker pooling capacity and lower benefit generosity compared with the UEBMI.4 In China, URRBMI is primarily financed through government subsidies and individual contributions, resulting in comparatively lower financing levels. Its benefit structure is characterized by lower reimbursement rates, higher cost-sharing requirements, and lower coverage ceilings [82], possibly limiting its capacity to protect households against large medical expenditures, particularly for households facing severe health shocks. As a result, URRBMI is associated with a more modest contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility. Overall, these differences suggestions heterogenous associations between insurance schemes and CHE inequality changes, highlighting the importance of reducing fragmentation within China’s health insurance system to improve equity in financial protection.

Additionally, given the ten-year interval of longitudinal panel data (2010–2020) underlying our decomposition framework, household ageing is also an important factor to consider in interpreting long-term changes in CHE inequality, even though its contribution is relatively small in magnitude. In our study, household ageing, captured by the presence of an older household head or at least one older household member, was associated with a positive contribution to wealth-related CHE mobility, suggesting some reduction in CHE risk among households with older members. However, this was offset by a stronger negative contribution to CHE-related wealth mobility, resulting in an overall inequality-increasing pattern. This asymmetric pattern suggests that, although CHE incidence may have declined for some ageing households during the study period, the economic consequences of CHE events remained more pronounced for this group in terms of relative wealth movements. This pattern may reflect the higher healthcare needs and weaker income-generating capacity of older households, as well as their more limited ability to recover wealth positions following health shocks [18, 83]. This finding reflects the persistent vulnerability associated with population ageing in China and highlights the importance of complementing health insurance expansion with broader social protection mechanisms to mitigate the longer-term economic consequences of health shocks among older households.

Finally, the comparison between the 2010–2018 and 2010–2020 results provide additional context for interpreting potential influences of the COVID-19 pandemic during the 2020 wave. Overall, the 2010–2018 pre-pandemic results exhibit broadly similar patterns to those observed in the full sample period, particularly in terms of the dominant role of wealth-related CHE mobility and the main determinant contributions. Some differences are observed across the two periods, with urban residence and UEBMI coverage appearing relatively more important in the 2010–2020 results, while some household socioeconomic characteristics are less prominent. These differences may reflect contextual changes during the pandemic period, including disruptions in healthcare utilization and broader economic shocks [84, 85], which could have altered the relative importance of different channels of financial protection. These comparisons suggest that the main findings are not driven by the pandemic-affected wave, and that reducing CHE inequality requires continued attention to vulnerable households, strengthening of financial protection mechanisms, and improved resilience to health and economic shocks.

Policy implications

These findings carry several policy implications for advancing equitable financial protection and achieving UHC in China. First, the persistent pro-poor concentration of CHE, despite its gradual decline over time, indicates that inequality in financial risk exposure remains an important policy challenge. Further progress requires not only reducing the overall incidence of CHE, but also ensuring a more balanced distribution of financial protection across socioeconomic groups. Strengthening equity-oriented monitoring of CHE inequality, alongside average financial protection indicators, may help better align policy evaluation with the distributive goals of UHC.

Second, the predominance of progressive contribution from wealth-related CHE mobility to the inequality decline suggests that improvements in financial protection during the study period disproportionately benefited poorer households. Sustaining and deepening this progress requires continued prioritization of benefit generosity and effective coverage for socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, including higher reimbursement rates, expanded coverage of high-cost services, and targeted medical assistance for low-wealth populations.

Third, the persistence of regressive CHE-related wealth mobility indicates that when catastrophic expenditures occur, they remain associated with adverse changes in household wealth positions, particularly among households with limited financial buffers and older members. This highlights the need to complement insurance-based protection with broader social protection instruments, including income support, long-term care insurance, and asset-protection policies, in order to mitigate the longer-term economic consequences of health shocks.

Fourth, the heterogeneous roles of social health insurance schemes and urban–rural residence highlight the importance of reducing institutional fragmentation within China’s health insurance system. Greater integration of insurance arrangements and improved portability of benefits may help ensure a more equal distribution of financial protection gains. Continued efforts should focus on reducing out-of-pocket burdens for enrollees in less generous resident-based schemes, while commercial health insurance may serve as a supplementary mechanism for covering high-cost drugs and catastrophic risks.

Finally, the inequality-increasing association of household ageing is particularly relevant in the context of China’s ongoing demographic transition. As population ageing accelerates, a growing share of households is likely to face chronic illness, functional decline, and repeated medical expenditures. Policy responses should therefore explicitly integrate ageing into financial risk protection strategies, including strengthened chronic disease management, expanded coverage for long-term and high-cost care, integrated community- and home-based care services, and improved pension adequacy for older residents. Targeted support should be prioritized for low-income households with older members to prevent widening socioeconomic disparities in financial risk exposure.

Limitations of the study

This study advances the understanding of changes in socioeconomic-related CHE inequality in China using nationally representative longitudinal data, providing evidence on the role of mobility processes and determinants in shaping changes in the socioeconomic distribution of financial protection. Nonetheless, several limitations should be acknowledged.

First, several measurement-related limitations should be noted. Due to data constraints, CHE was based on self-reported household healthcare expenditures, which may be subject to recall bias and reporting error. In addition, non-medical and indirect costs associated with healthcare use, such as transportation, accommodation, time costs, and income loss, were not fully captured. Households with unmet healthcare needs, particularly those that incurred forgone or delayed care due to financial constraints, were not classified as incurring CHE. This may lead to an underestimation of the pro-poor concentration of CHE, given that unmet need is more prevalent among poorer households [86]. Household wealth was measured using available asset-based indicators, which, although widely used in inequality research [13, 15, 16], may not fully capture other dimensions of economic vulnerability, such as liquidity constraints, indebtedness, or income volatility.

In addition, household headship was defined according to the household decision maker, which may change over time due to ageing, migration, or household reconstitution, implying that head-level covariates may not consistently refer to the same individual across waves. Healthcare needs and health insurance coverage were measured at the household level, which may mask important within-household heterogeneity, including differences in demographic composition, health status, and insurance-related protection and reimbursement capacity across individuals within the same household. Future studies linking household survey data with administrative records may help address this concern. It should also be noted that although observed determinants were incorporated in the decomposition, unobserved factors—such as health-seeking preferences, risk attitudes, informal support networks, and local variation in healthcare prices—may also shape changes in CHE inequality. These influences were absorbed in the residual component and warrant further investigation, particularly through mixed-method or regionally disaggregated analyses.

Second, the decomposition was primarily based on two waves of panel data observed a decade apart. While this design enables assessment of long-term changes in CHE inequality, it limits the ability to capture intermediate transitions or short-term mobility patterns that may have occurred between 2010 and 2020. Moreover, the use of a balanced panel may exclude households experiencing high mobility or structural vulnerability, including those lost to follow-up due to migration, refusal, unsuccessful contact, migration, or household reconstitution (e.g., splitting or dissolution). Although inverse probability weighting was applied to reduce attrition bias, residual selection effects cannot be fully ruled out. Future studies using higher-frequency panel data would allow a more granular analysis of mobility patterns.

Finally, the decomposition framework should be interpreted as a descriptive analysis of changes in the socioeconomic distribution of CHE rather than a causal identification strategy. The estimated contributions reflect associations between mobility processes, observed determinants, and changes in CHE inequality. Accordingly, the comparison involving the COVID-19 period is also descriptive and should not be interpreted causally. Future studies using quasi-experimental or policy evaluation designs could further examine the causal effects of specific health financing reforms. In addition, it should be noted that household SES is used both as the ranking variable in the concentration index and as a grouped explanatory factor in the determinant decomposition. While this reflects a standard approach in analyses of socioeconomic-related inequality [13, 16, 65], it implies that part of the explanatory contribution of wealth quintiles may be driven by their shared dependence on the same underlying socioeconomic dimension used to define inequality. Therefore, the contributions of wealth groups should be interpreted as capturing heterogeneity within the socioeconomic gradient rather than as fully independent explanatory effects.

Conclusion

This study provides a longitudinal decomposition of changes in socioeconomic-related CHE inequality in China by integrating a mobility framework with determinant-based decomposition. Although CHE remained disproportionately concentrated among poorer households, socioeconomic inequality in CHE declined between 2010 and 2020. Our findings indicate that this reduction was driven primarily by the progressive contribution of wealth-related CHE mobility, reflecting disproportionate improvements in financial protection among initially disadvantaged households, while regressive contribution from CHE-related wealth mobility partially offset these gains. By distinguishing between these mobility processes and identifying the determinants operating through distinct channels, the analysis reveals that the reduction in measured inequality does not arise from a single mechanism but from the interaction between changes in exposure to catastrophic expenditure and shifts in households’ relative socioeconomic positions. These findings highlight that monitoring progress towards financial protection and health equity should move beyond aggregate indicators and explicitly incorporate the mobility processes through which jointly shape inequality over time. Advancing UHC requires simultaneously reducing the overall risk of CHE and narrowing socioeconomic disparities in financial protection, with particular attention to poorer households. At the same time, mitigating the longer-term wealth consequences of health shocks remains critical to prevent adverse socioeconomic re-ranking and to sustain equitable progress toward UHC. Future research using higher-frequency longitudinal data and multidimensional measures of economic vulnerability may further deepen understanding of how CHE reshapes socioeconomic inequality through mobility processes over time.
