Carboxymethyl cellulose-ferulic acid nanocomposite enhances salinity tolerance and phytochemical accumulation in Dracocephalum moldavica via coordinated redox and osmotic regulation
Authors: Soghra Sarvi, Seyed Mehdi Razavi, Ahlam Khalofah, Meisam Zargar, Abazar Ghorbani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalinity is a significant constraint on medicinal and aromatic plants that limits their growth and photosynthetic activity, increases oxidative stress, and disrupts water balance.ResultsThe present paper prepared a carboxymethyl cellulose-ferulic acid nanocomposite (FA-NC), which is a bio-based nano-formulation to enhance the performance of Dracocephalum moldavica in the presence of salinity stress, and the effect was directly compared to free FA. Carboxymethyl cellulose was successfully used to prepare the FA-NC, which was verified by conventional physicochemical characterization. Salinity stress significantly reduced growth, fresh and dry biomass, stomatal conductance, chlorophyll pigments, and photochemical efficiency, and enhanced membrane damage and oxidative stress indicators. Both FA and FA-NC reduced these negative effects, but FA-NC was always stronger in protection compared to free FA. FA-NC was more effective in enhancing leaf hydration and membrane stability, and more powerful in minimizing oxidative damage (reducing H2O2 and MDA). This increased stress alleviation was accompanied by an improved antioxidant capacity (increased CAT, APX, and GPO activities and low protease activity). FA-NC also enhanced the accumulation of proline, glycine betaine, soluble sugars, and free amino acids that assist in the enhancement of osmotic adjustment in exposure to salt. Notably, FA-NC had a greater effect on the accumulation of major secondary metabolites, such as phenolics, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and tannins, than free FA, which is beneficial in maintaining medicinal quality during stress. PCA combined responses and emphasized the greater change in the FA-NC-treated plants towards desirable associations of traits when compared to free FA.ConclusionIn general, FA-NC nano-formulation is a sustainable and more efficient substitute for free FA to improve salinity tolerance and phytochemical potential in D. moldavica.

Introduction

Dracocephalum moldavica (Moldavian balm) is a balm of the Lamiaceae family that is also a medicinal and aromatic plant. It is appreciated due to its wide phytochemical profile, such as flavonoids, phenolic compounds, essential oils, and terpenoids, which make it anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective, and enhance its value in the pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, and cosmetic industries [1, 2]. This plant was chosen as a model plant due to its medicinal value, abundance of bioactive secondary metabolites, and salinity stress sensitivity, which can adversely impact plant growth and change its phytochemical profile [3]. Moreover, D. moldavica is extensively grown in Iran, particularly in the Azerbaijan region, so it is a valuable plant species to investigate the mechanisms of plant adaptation to salinity stress [4]. It has aerial components with high concentrations of valuable compounds, which include hydroxycinnamic acids and flavonoids like caffeic acid, ferulic acid, luteolin-7-O-glucoside, rosmarinic acid, rutin, gallic acid, chlorogenic acid, and apigenin [5]. Nevertheless, environmental conditions have a strong influence on its essential oils, secondary metabolites, and overall bioactivity, which explains the necessity of measures to retain or improve the phytochemical activity of the plant under stress [6, 7].

Salinity is one of the most harmful types of abiotic stressors, and over a fifth of the arable land is under its influence [8, 9]. The over-accumulation of Na and Cl in salty areas disrupts the osmotic pressure, the nutritional food chain, and the metabolism in the cells [10]. Additionally, the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) induced by salt leads to lipid peroxidation and oxidation of proteins and nucleic acids, which may exceed normal antioxidant defense mechanisms of the plant when faced with high or excessive salt levels [11]. These physiological impairments eventually undermine the production of secondary metabolites that form the main part of the medicinal usefulness of the plant. Salt stress alters plant metabolic pathways and stimulates the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, leading to an increase in compounds such as phenolics and flavonoids [12]. Numerous studies have shown that these metabolites accumulate significantly under saline conditions across various plant species and function as typical stress-responsive compounds [13, 14]. This pattern indicates that the enhancement of secondary metabolite production is a common adaptive strategy employed by plants to cope with salinity stress [12].

Nanotechnology innovations have the potential to provide solutions to salt-induced damage by facilitating the creation of biocompatible, biodegradable, and functionally tunable nanomaterials that can enable plants to be more resilient to stress [15, 16]. Cellulose-based nanocomposites have received specific focus among these due to their structural stability, mechanical strength, and the capacity to enhance the solubility and controlled release of bioactive compounds in agricultural systems [17, 18]. Ferulic acid (FA), a derivative of hydroxycinnamic acid found in large amounts in the plant cell wall, is a powerful antioxidant able to scavenge ROS and stimulate the activity of antioxidant enzymes, which in turn allows plants to adjust to stress, but has low solubility and stability in water [19, 20]. In this regard, FA-based nanocomposites are a new approach to the sustainability of protecting medicinal plants against salinity. Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC), which has been functionalized with CH2COOH- polar functional groups, is a perfect scaffold to enhance the dispersibility, stability, controlled release, bioavailability, and bioactivity of FA when subjected to stress [17]. However, the impact of CMC-FA nanocomposites on the physiological and biochemical processes of D. moldavica under salinity stress and the biological activity of CMC-FA nanocomposites in contrast to the non-nano counterpart of FA has not been comprehensively studied yet, which restricts our current knowledge on how nano-enabled bioactive carriers modulate physiological and biochemical pathways in.

To determine the ability of a new CMC-FA nanocomposite (FA-NC) to enhance the stress resilience of D. moldavica, a new kind of nanocomposite was developed and evaluated in this study. We compared and contrasted the stress impacts of nano and non-nano forms of FA on the main physiological characteristics and the concentration of secondary metabolites, such as anthocyanins, tannins, phenolic compounds, and the stimulation of the antioxidant defense system. This paper presents FA-NC as a novel bioactive nanocomposite, in addition to shedding mechanistic light on how engineered nano-carriers can be used to improve the performance of plants in unfavorable environmental conditions. The results further our knowledge in nano-enabled biostimulants and help grow sustainable approaches to enhance the productivity and phytochemical content of medicinal plants grown on saline soils.

Materials and methods

Preparation of FA-NC

The CMC polymer matrix was prepared based on the protocol reported previously by Ranjbar et al. [21]. Simultaneously, a FA stock solution (2 mg/mL in ethanol) was prepared and added dropwise to the CMC solution at 65 °C, and stirred at a steady pace. Sonication was continued until the mixture was uniformly incorporated into the polymer to ensure that FA was incorporated throughout the polymer. Then, the suspension was centrifuged after 20 min at 6000 rpm, and the supernatant was dried in Petri dishes at room temperature to achieve the solid FA-NC. In order to verify the successful synthesis as well as to analyze the physicochemical characteristics of FA-NC, important parameters such as particle size distribution, thermal stability, surface morphology, and the composition of functional groups were evaluated in a systematic manner. All these analyses confirmed the integrity, stability, and efficient interaction between FA and the CMC matrix. In order to eliminate the potential confounding effect of ethanol, equal amounts of this solvent were applied to all treatments, including the control groups (as a mock treatment).

Plant growth and treatment application

D. moldavica seeds (obtained from the Research Institute of Forests and Rangelands, Tehran, Iran) were sterilized with a 1% solution of sodium hypochlorite for 5 min and then thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and planted in pots with a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of perlite and vermiculite. The experiment involved growing plants in controlled conditions and irrigating them with half-strength Hoagland nutrient solution. The treatments were initiated at the 8-leaf stage and were organized in a fully randomized study that had five replicates per treatment. The treatments were: control, salinity stress (150 mM NaCl), FA (0.001 mg/mL), FA-NC (0.001 mg/mL), salinity + FA, and salinity + FA-NC. The entire treatment was done through the addition of the nutrient solution with the respective compounds. Plants were harvested after 28 days of treatment, undergoing physiological and biochemical analysis. To identify the dry weight, the samples were dried in the oven at a temperature of 75 °C over a period of 48 h.

Photosynthetic and gas exchange parameters

In the case of photosynthetic pigments, fresh leaf samples were homogenized in 80% acetone, and absorbance was measured spectrophotometrically at 663, 645, and 470 nm [22]. A portable chlorophyll fluorometer was used to measure Fv/Fm after 30 min dark adaptation of the leaves, and the SPAD value was measured non-destructively with the use of a SPAD chlorophyll meter on fully expanded leaves. The stomatal conductance (gs) was measured by a portable leaf porometer (Walz IRGA) under constant environmental conditions.

Leaf water status and membrane integrity

For determination of leaf relative water content (RWC), the samples of fresh leaves were weighed as fresh (FW), rehydrated in dark conditions in distilled water (24 h to obtain turgid weight, TW), and dried at 75 °C (48 h, DW). RWC was determined by taking [(FW-DW)/(TW-DW)] x 100. Equal discs of leaves were wetted using deionized water and allowed to incubate in distilled water at room temperature over a period of 24 h, and initial electrical conductivity (EC1) was measured. Total electrolytes were then released by autoclaving the samples, and the final conductivity (EC2) was then measured. Electrolyte leakage (EL) was calculated as a percentage of EC1 to EC2.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)

The extent of lipid peroxidation was determined by MDA content with the help of the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction. The tissue of the fresh leaves was homogenized in trichloroacetic acid (TCA), and the homogenate was mixed with TBA reagent. The solution was boiled in a bath of boiling water, quickly cooled, and centrifuged. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 532 nm, and non-specific turbidity was compensated for by obtaining absorbance at 600 nm [23]. The level of H2O2 was measured by a colorimetric procedure. The leaf sample was extracted with cold TCA, and the supernatant was reacted with potassium iodide. The absorbance was measured at 390 nm [24].

Protein content and antioxidant enzymes

Fresh leaf tissues were ground in liquid nitrogen and homogenized in a cold phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.0). The homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were subjected to protein and enzyme assays. The content of total soluble protein was determined by referring to the concentration of bovine serum albumin, and absorbance was measured at 595 nm [25].

The activity of ascorbate peroxidase (APX) was measured on the basis of absorbance reduction following the oxidation of ascorbate [26]. Catalase (CAT) activity was measured by monitoring the decomposition rate of hydrogen peroxide spectrophotometrically [27]. The activity of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) was measured by the use of catechol as the substrate. This reaction was incubated at 25 °C, and the reaction was stopped using sulfuric acid and read at 495 nm [28]. The guaiacol peroxidase (GPO) activity was determined by the increase in absorbance due to the oxidation of guaiacol [29]. Casein was hydrolyzed and used as a substrate to determine the protease activity. The mixture was incubated at 45 °C for 1 h, and the reaction was terminated by adding TCA, and absorbance was recorded at 280 nm [30].

Compatible solutes

The total free amino acids were identified using ninhydrin, where the free amino acids reacted with ninhydrin to produce a colored product. The absorbance at 570 nm was taken, and the concentrations were then calculated using a glycine standard curve [31]. The amount of total soluble sugars (TSS) was measured using the phenol sulfuric acid method, which is based on the dehydration of sugars to furfural derivatives and the subsequent color change, which was measured at 490 nm [32]. Proline was estimated using the acid-ninhydrin assay method, wherein proline reacts with ninhydrin to produce a colour in acidic conditions. At 520 nm, absorbance was taken [33]. The amount of glycine betaine was calculated according to the periodide method, which relies on the development of a betaine-periodide complex at 365 nm [34].

Phenolic compounds

The content of anthocyanin was assessed through the acidified methanol technique, where the pigments were extracted and measured spectrophotometrically on the basis of their absorbance at 530 nm with correction at 657 nm to remove the effect of chlorophylls [35]. The Folin-Denis method was used to estimate the tannin content, which is based on the reduction of phosphotungstomolybdic acid by tannins, and the absorbance was measured at 760 nm [36]. The Folin-Ciocalteu reagent was used to identify the total phenolic content; in this process, the phenolic compounds will reduce the reagent to form a blue complex. The absorbance was taken at 765 nm, and the concentration of phenol was presented as gallic acid equivalents [37]. The total flavonoid content was determined by the use of the aluminum chloride colorimetric assay, whereby flavonoids form a stable complex with AlCl3. The absorbance was determined at 415 nm, and the flavonoid content was determined with the help of a quercetin standard [38].

Statistical analysis

All the experimental results were reported in the form of mean ± SD of at least five biological replicates. The SPSS software was used to conduct statistical analyses. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test the difference among the treatments, and mean comparisons were done with the help of the Duncan multiple range test at a confidence level of 95% (p < 0.05). The measured parameters were analyzed using the principal component analysis (PCA) of the data in Origin software.

Results

Characterization of FA-NCs

Pure FA had the typical broad O-H stretching band with clear aromatic bands and a carbonyl vibration linked to the carboxylic group. The O-H stretching area was also shifted and broadened in the FA-NCs and was located at approximately 3649 –3103 cm− 1, suggesting changes in the hydrogen bonding between FA and the hydroxyl/carboxymethyl groups of CMC. It was found that a good absorption at 1731–1735 cm− 1, corresponding to the -O = C=O stretching, was stronger, indicating a much more constrained/polar carbonyl in the polymer framework. Bands that were intensified at the 1166 –1065 cm− 1 region were ascribed to C-O-C/C-O vibrations of the polysaccharide backbone, and aromatic out-of-plane vibrations proved the existence of FA moieties in the composite (Fig. 1A, B).

TGA demonstrated that pure FA undergoes a minor mass loss at low temperatures (moisture/volatiles), to a significant decomposition step within the range of temperatures of about 200 to 260 °C, and a slow progressive degradation at higher temperatures, with insignificant residue remaining. Conversely, the FA-NCs exhibit a higher low-temperature loss and a more multi-step degradation profile with a larger residual. The higher residue indicates the higher char formation that could enhance thermal resistance at high temperature and, in general, the successful incorporation of FA into the CMC matrix and altered degradation routes as a result of intermolecular interactions (Fig. 1C, D).

DLS established the size of the FA-NCs as within the range of nanoscale (Fig. 2A), with a mean size of the particles as 87.3 ± 16.6 nm and a zeta potential of -24.9 mV, which is a moderate level of stability of the dispersion as the particles have a negative charge (Fig. 2B). SEM micrographs also revealed a fairly homogenous morphology with dispersed nanoscale features, which favored the creation of a nanostructured composite with reasonable uniform dispersion (Fig. 2C).

Growth responses

Salinity had a significant impact on root and shoot length, with decreases of about 43 and 37%, respectively, compared to the control. FA and FA-NC stimulated shoot length by almost 38 and 48%, respectively, compared to salinity stress (Table 1). Salinity had a high effect on the biomass of roots, with a reduction of about 69% in root fresh weight and about 53% in root dry weight relative to the control. When FA was applied to salinity, there was a great increase in root fresh weight of close to 88% and root dry weight of close to 85% in comparison with salt-stressed plants. FA-NC was effective in promoting root biomass during salinity, with the root fresh weight increasing about 75% and the root dry weight returning to a statistically similar level as the control (Table 1). Salinity stress also significantly reduced shoot fresh and dry weights with a reduction of about 44 and 48%, respectively. During salinity, FA enhanced the fresh and dry weight of the shoot by an average of 48 and 41%, respectively. Nonetheless, FA-NC improved the shoot fresh weight by almost 56% and the shoot dry weight by about 83% compared to salinity alone (Table 1).

Photosynthetic pigments and gas exchange

Salinity stress significantly decreased the chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoid contents with respect to the control. Stomatal conductance was reduced by approximately 37% during salinity. In salty conditions, FA enhanced photosynthetic pigments by almost 42, 19, and 92%, respectively, over salinity conditions, and stomatal conductance rose by about 21%. The response was more pronounced with FA-NC, which increased chlorophyll a by 83, chlorophyll b by 48, carotenoids by 128, and stomatal conductance by approximately 29% over salt-stressed plants (Table 2).

SPAD and Fv/FM index

Salinity greatly decreased the SPAD values and Fv/Fm by about 15 and 42%, respectively, as compared to the control. The effects of FA during salinity were a rise in SPAD and Fv/Fm by approximately 10 and 47%, respectively. A larger improvement was observed with FA-NC, where SPAD was improved by almost 12%, and Fv/Fm was improved by about 56% over salinity alone (Table 2).

Water status and oxidative damage

RWC was reduced by about 37% at salinity relative to non-stressed plants, whereas EL rose by almost 109%, which demonstrates severe cellular dehydration and membrane destruction. Applications of FA yielded about 21% higher RWC and about 35% less EL under saline conditions than salinity alone. The greater effect was noted in the case of FA-NC, which raised the RWC by approximately 29% and reduced EL by approximately 43% (Fig. 3A, B). Salinity also contributed to forming an apparent concentration of the oxidative stress markers, as evidenced by increased levels of H2O2 and MDA. Both parameters were highest in the presence of salinity only, and the use of FA significantly lowered the H2O2 and MDA contents. It is noteworthy that FA-NC led to a more significant decrease in H2O2 and MDA compared to free FA, which means that it was more effective in mitigating oxidative damage (Fig. 3C, D).

Antioxidant enzymes and protein content

CAT activity rose by an average of 49%, and APX activity rose by an average of 48% during salinity in comparison with non-stressed plants. During saline conditions, FA also enhanced the CAT and APX activity by almost 33 and 56%, respectively, over salinity. The greatest induction was noted in plants treated with FA-NC, in which CAT and APX activities improved by about 49 and 53% (Fig. 4A, B). Another effect of salinity stress on PPO activity was also observed with an increase of about 25% relative to the control. FA under salinity was found to have significantly increased the PPO activity, and the highest PPO activity was found to be caused by FA-NCs, meaning the nanocomposite form was more stimulatory (Fig. 4C). In the case of GPO, salinity had a significant effect on activity (44%) as compared to the control. This was greatly enhanced by both FA and FA-NCs under salinity, but the effect of FA-NCs was significantly stronger, with a 62% increase in GPO activity over salinity alone (Fig. 4D). Protease activity, in contrast, reacted in a different way. Salinity caused the sharp rise of the protease activity up to a maximum value under salt stress alone. FA considerably decreased protease activity in salinity, whereas FA-NCs did so to a larger extent, demonstrating that it is more effective in preventing the occurrence of stress-induced proteolysis (Fig. 4E). The overall content of soluble proteins declined by about 32% when stressed by salinity as compared to plants that were not. FA and FA-NCs enhanced protein content greatly under salinity, but FA-NCs produced slightly greater protein content than the free FA (Fig. 4F).

Compatible solutes

Salinity had a significant effect on proline accumulation, with its accumulation increasing by around 118% relative to the non-stressed plants. FA and FA-NC also increased the proline content by approximately 59 and 52%, respectively, compared to salinity alone (Fig. 5A). The same effect was recorded in the case of glycine betaine, in which salinity raised its proportion by about 82% compared to the control. During salinity, FA led to an additional increase of approximately 35%, and FA-NCs to the greatest response, which increased approximately 48%, over salt stress alone (Fig. 5B). The accumulation of soluble sugars was also favored by salinity stress, with a growth of about 67% as opposed to non-stressed plants. FA and FA-NCs had a significant difference as the former raised the soluble sugar content by about 51 and 72% over salinity alone, respectively (Fig. 5C). Salinity stress in total free amino acids resulted in almost a 55% increment compared to the control. The total free amino acid levels under salinity were further increased by both FA and FA-NCs, and the greatest magnitude was under FA-NCs, which increased by an average of 42% relative to salinity alone (Fig. 5D).

Phenolic compounds

The anthocyanin accumulation increased by around 30% compared to non-stressed plants. FA also enhanced it in saline conditions by approximately 33%, whereas FA-NCs evoked the greatest response, boosting the levels of anthocyanin by approximately 50% compared to salinity alone (Fig. 6A). The same trend was observed in tannin content. Salinity amplified tannin concentration by a factor of about 47% in comparison to the control. FA and FA-NCs were found to increase tannin accumulation in salinity significantly, with an increase of approximately 21% and 24%, respectively, over salt-stressed plants, with no significant difference between the two treatments (Fig. 6B). Salinity stress significantly stimulated total phenolic content by about 65% in contrast to non-stressed plants. In saline conditions, FA induced a significant increase in phenolic compounds (67%), whereas FA-NCs enhanced a high increase in total phenols by about 88% compared to salinity alone (Fig. 6C). In the case of flavonoids, salinity enhanced their levels up to approximately 70% compared to the control. FA also increased flavonoid concentration by approximately 47%, whereas FA-NCs produced the greatest effect, with an increase of 59%, at salinity (Fig. 6D).

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA indicated the distinct separation of treatments in terms of the measured physiological and biochemical traits. The two main components were used to explain 98.19% of the total variance; PC1 (73.92%) explained the main variation, and PC2 (24.27%) explained secondary differentiation. Salinity-stressed plants along PC1 were clearly differentiated and tightly linked with parameters that are stress-related, including H2O2, MDA, EL, and protease activity, meaning that the oxidative stress is severe, and membranes are unstable. Control, FA, and FA-NC treatments were, on the other hand, lumped together on the opposite side of PC1 and positively correlated with growth characteristics, photosynthetic parameters, RWC, and protein content. Under salinity conditions, S + FA and S + FA-NC were clearly separated from salinity alone and exhibited a strong positive relationship with antioxidant enzymes, osmolytes, and secondary metabolites (phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins). It is worth noting that S + FA-NC had a greater change to favorable characteristics compared to S + FA, which demonstrates that the nanocomposite was more effective in alleviating salt-induced stress. Among different physiological and growth parameters, root dry weight was more affected by FA-NC treatment; MDA was regarded as the most influential stress marker under salinity stress (Fig. 7).

Discussion

CMC was used as a biocompatible carrier to synthesize the FA-NCs to achieve a nano-formulation with better physicochemical properties compared to free FA, which is consistent with recent publications on CMC-based nanocomposites of other phenolics/flavonoids to improve stability, delivery, and stress-mitigation efficiency in plants [39, 40]. Based on previous reports that CMC-based hydrogels, membranes, and nanocomposite systems can function as controlled-release matrices for polyphenols and other bioactive compounds [17], it is plausible that embedding FA into a CMC-based nanocomposite may improve its stability and modulate its release to plant tissues. However, in the present study, we did not directly measure FA release kinetics or nanocomposite persistence in the substrate, and this assumption should therefore be regarded as a working hypothesis that requires direct verification in future experiments.

In our work, FTIR has directly demonstrated the successful incorporation of FA into the CMC matrix by broadening/shifting the OH band and modifying the C = O-associated absorption, which indicates that there is a strong intermolecular interaction between FA and CMC, similar to interactions with the other biopolymer-based nanocomposites that are designed to enhance stability and functionality [41, 42]. The negative zeta potential and the nanoscale size of the particles (87 nm) also suggest a dispersed nano-system with moderate colloidal stability similar to those reported in effective plant-oriented nanocomposites [21]. Moreover, TGA exhibited a multi-step degradation pattern and increased residue of FA-NCs compared to FA, which is also associated with the incorporation of the active compound into a polymeric scaffold and can be used to explain enhanced resistance at high temperatures, which is also the case with related CMC-based phenolic nanocomposites [40, 43]. On the whole, all these complementary findings prove the effective production of FA-NCs and justify the general idea that the nano-form can be more effective than the free compound because of the better dispersion/stability and the increased delivery of the bioactive agent.

D. moldavica L. has been reported to be sensitive to salinity stress during the germination stage, as salinity can significantly reduce germination indices and negatively affect seed germination [3]. The data indicate a dose-dependent effect of salinity, meaning that as the NaCl concentration increases, stress intensity increases and growth decreases [44, 45]. In line with the normal salinity syndrome displayed by medicinal/aromatic plants, the salinity treatment in our experiment was linked with reduced growth qualities and a debilitated photosynthetic mechanism. The mechanistic explanation of these outcomes is that the integration of osmotic limitation (impaired tissue hydration and cell growth), ion imbalance (especially K/Na homeostasis disruption), and oxidative stress that promotes pigment loss and destroys photosystems and electron transport accelerates them [46]. Although salt stress reduced root length, the application of FA and its nanocomposite significantly increased root fresh and dry weights, without a significant change in root length. According to physiological studies, under salinity stress, root elongation and biomass accumulation can become uncoupled; reduced cell division together with cell swelling, radial expansion of roots, and osmotic adjustment may increase root weight and tissue density even when root elongation remains limited [47]. In addition, FA is a key phenolic component of plant cell walls that can form cross-links with polysaccharides and lignin, leading to strengthened and thickened cell walls and increased tissue density, which may contribute to higher root dry weight without necessarily increasing root length [48, 49]. Consequently, according to this paradigm, elicitor-based priming using phenolic antioxidants typically prevents salt-induced photosynthetic down-regulation by maintaining chlorophyll pools and photochemical efficiency and reducing oxidative damage. To illustrate, foliar FA enhanced the performance of photosynthesis in short-term salt-stressed Chinese cabbage, and it was found that salt impairment was closely related to dysfunction of photosystems (especially PSI) and that FA increased phenolic metabolism and antioxidant capacity [20]. Similarly, a protective effect of FA during dehydration stress has been mechanistically explained by increased antioxidant enzyme activities and decreased ROS/MDA levels, along with enhanced RWC and the osmolyte/soluble sugar conditions that are directly applicable to sustaining leaf functionality and carbon assimilation during saline osmotic stress [50]. Notably, our comparison pattern of free FA and its nanocomposite agrees with several reports that indicate nano-enabled phenolic elicitors perform better than free ones under salinity. Caffeic acid nanocomposites were more effective in improving biomass, chlorophyll content, and photosynthetic efficiency, inhibiting oxidative markers and enhancing osmotic adjustment as opposed to free caffeic acid in Melissa officinalis [21]. A nanocomposite of cinnamic acid using CMC enhanced photosynthetic/physiological characteristics and stress tolerance index of peppermint in comparison to the free compound [43]. Other elicitor systems have also been recorded to have similar benefits over salinity as this nanocomposite. As an illustration, catechin nanocomposites enhanced photosynthetic pigments, growth, and ion balance and minimized oxidative stress in stevia [41]. In our study, FA-NC caused a 128% increase in carotenoids compared with salt-stressed plants, whereas chlorophyll b increased by only 48%. This disproportionate rise is consistent with the biochemical roles of carotenoids as the major lipid-soluble, non-enzymatic antioxidants in chloroplasts [51]. Carotenoids possess long conjugated double-bond systems that enable efficient scavenging of peroxyl radicals and singlet oxygen, forming resonance-stabilized intermediates without undergoing degradation [51, 52]. They are also integral components of thylakoid pigment-protein complexes and are central to photoprotection by quenching ¹O₂ and preventing lipid peroxidation of thylakoid membranes [52, 53]. Under salt stress, chloroplasts experience excess excitation energy at PSII, and the carotenoid-dependent xanthophyll cycle dissipates this energy through non-photochemical quenching, thereby limiting ROS formation [53]. Because this mechanism is carotenoid-based, FA-NC is expected to strongly enhance carotenoid accumulation or stability. In contrast, chlorophyll b functions mainly in light-harvesting antenna complexes and contributes less directly to ROS detoxification, while its biosynthesis is more tightly constrained under salinity. Therefore, FA-NC may primarily prevent further chlorophyll degradation rather than induce large over-accumulation. Consequently, chlorophyll b shows only a modest increase, whereas carotenoids rise sharply, reflecting the plant’s prioritization of photoprotection and ROS scavenging over expansion of light-harvesting capacity under salt stress. CeO2-SA nanocomposites enhanced pigments and antioxidant potential, and reduced H2O2/MDA in spearmint in relation to salinity alone [54]. These findings confirm the explanation that nano-formulation enhances elicitor efficacy through increased stability, increased bioavailability, and longer/targeted delivery to those tissues where pigment and photosystem protection is most needed [55]. Thus, we have mechanistic evidence that FA-NC responded more strongly in our experiment due to the fact that the redox homeostasis and photosynthetic integrity were reinforced more efficiently in the presence of salinity, and, most importantly, this study is the first evidence of plant usage of a FA-NC during salty conditions.

The steep increase in EL and the high levels of H2O2 and MDA in our plants in the presence of salinity are clear indications of increased oxidative stress, destabilization of the membranes, and lipid peroxidation induced by stress. Such reactions are usually initiated by the osmotic/ionic imbalances that interfere with the electron transport chain and enhance the formation of ROS in chloroplasts, mitochondria, and apoplast [46]. The trend is consistent with the existing literature in medicinal/vegetable crops where salinity augmented oxidative biomarkers and destabilized cellular structure [56, 57]. In plants, the mitigation of such ROS pressure depends on an integrated antioxidant defense system where CAT is used to rapidly break down H2O2, APX is used to offer high-affinity H2O2 scavenging activity in the AsA-GSH cycle, and the class III peroxidases, including GPO, use phenolic substrates to detoxify H2O2, which reinforced barriers and contributed to redox buffering in response to stress [58]. Salinity alone in our study was already sufficient to upregulate CAT and APX, but the markers of oxidative damage were maximal in salt stress, which showed that the basal induction was not enough to restore redox homeostasis completely. This enzymatic protection was additionally enhanced by both FA and FA-NCs, which were accompanied by reduced EL, H2O2, and MDA and reflected a better regulation of ROS-induced membrane damage. Mechanistically, these effects are consistent with the evidence that FA activates antioxidant machinery (both activity and transcription-level) and reduces the accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxidation and enhances cellular water/solute status during different stresses [50, 59], and also promotes AsA-GSH cycling and general radical-scavenging capacity during stress [60]. The fact that salinity and FA (and more so FA–NCs) stimulated and inhibited stress-induced proteolysis and soluble protein recovery, respectively, also supports our interpretation that enhanced redox regulation minimized oxidative protein damage and the resulting requirement of increased protein turnover. The enhanced efficacy of FA-NCs compared to free FA is also in line with the increasing body of nano-elicitor literature indicating that bioactive-loaded NCs are more effective at reinstating antioxidant defenses than free counterparts, probably because of enhanced stability, increased tissue availability/uptake, and extended release [39, 42].

The reported enhancement of CAT and APX activity and the inhibition of stress-induced proteolysis are not contradictory but reflect two mechanistically linked aspects of the antioxidant defense network. CAT and APX are well-known enzymatic antioxidants involved in ROS detoxification and redox homeostasis [61]. Phenolic compounds, including FA, also act synergistically with antioxidant enzymes to limit ROS overproduction [61]. Because CAT and APX efficiently remove H2O2 through the AsA-GSH cycle, their upregulation reduces oxidative injury to macromolecules, particularly proteins. Importantly, abiotic stresses such as salinity generate secondary oxidative stress, leading to the overproduction of oxidized and damaged proteins that subsequently require proteolytic processing [62, 63]. Stress-induced ROS accumulation drives protein oxidation and activates proteolytic pathways responsible for the removal of damaged proteins as part of the cellular stress response [64, 65]. Therefore, when FA and FA-NC treatments enhance antioxidant capacity and suppress ROS accumulation, they indirectly lower the generation of oxidized protein substrates and consequently diminish stress-induced proteolysis. Thus, FA and FA-NC function primarily as stimulators of protective antioxidant enzymes while secondarily reducing proteolysis by limiting oxidative protein damage. These two observations are fully consistent and represent consecutive steps within the same ROS-dependent stress-response cascade.

Our findings are consistent with a mechanistic theory where FA is a redox-active priming signal and antioxidant substrate, which enhances enzymatic ROS detoxification and membrane defense. Meanwhile, nano-formulation improves the efficiency and persistence of FA to cellular targets, thus producing a stronger attenuation of oxidative injury under salinity, which is especially significant considering that FA is capable of eliciting stress-like responses at the wrong dosage or context, which highlights the importance of formulation and effective bioavailability in producing beneficial responses [66, 67].

The impact of salinity on plant water relationships, as evidenced by a significant depletion of RWC in this study, can be explained by the rapid osmotic phase of salt stress. The osmotic phase results in reduced water uptake and increased dehydration, and is usually followed by a compatible solute phase, which helps to compensate for the negative effects of osmotic stress by increasing compatible solute concentrations and maintaining turgor pressure, integrity, and stability, respectively [68]. As expected, the osmotic phase was coupled with significant enhancements of osmolyte concentration, including proline, glycine betaine, soluble sugar, and total free amino acids, which were triggered by salinity treatment. The significant induction of comparable increases in the concentration of osmolytes, along with concurrent depletion of water status, has been shown in medicinal plants [40]. However, it is interesting to note that FA, when applied under saline stress, increased RWC and further induced significant potentiation of the concentration of the dominant osmolytes, which may indicate that the FA acted as a metabolic elicitor involved in the metabolic regulation of osmotic homeostasis. The evidence of the efficacy of pretreatment with FA in enhancing RWC and soliciting accumulation of proline and soluble sugars during dehydration and heat stress supports this opinion [50, 59]. Mechanistic work also reveals that FA is able to control osmotic adjustment and soluble protein/proline accumulation by stress signaling pathways, such as MAPK-dependent control during chilling stress [69]. Even though there have been reports that effective priming can lower excessive accumulation of proline in short-term salinity, this is probably due to variation in the duration/intensity of stress conditions, and whether proline is an active osmoprotectant or a symptom of injury according to the experimental conditions in question [70]. It is worth noting that FA-NCs had the most potent regeneration of the leaf water status and an overall higher stimulation of glycine betaine, soluble sugars, and total free amino acids than free FA. This is in line with the reports that NC delivery of bioactive phenolics more efficiently increases osmotic adjustment (higher RWC and compatible solutes) under drought/salinity than the free compounds, which is likely because it improves stability, uptake, and sustained release that prolongs metabolic reprogramming to osmoprotection [39, 43]. Our results favour a mechanistic model where FA, and more effectively FA-NCs, enhance salinity tolerance by enhancing water status in plants by enhancing osmotic adjustment, which maintains cellular turgor and protects metabolic activity under low water conditions.

Exogenous FA or FA-NC application is expected to enhance specific branches of secondary metabolism primarily through activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway, which is a central route for the biosynthesis of phenolic acids and flavonoids. Under salt stress, FA reduces oxidative damage and ROS accumulation [71], thereby relieving redox inhibition of key entry-point enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL). In addition, FA has been shown to upregulate antioxidant-related gene expression (SOD, CAT, APX) in fruit crops [72], and this ROS-mediated signaling is closely linked to stimulation of phenylpropanoid flux. Consistently, foliar FA treatment in Chinese cabbage significantly increased the accumulation of FA, sinapic acid, kaempferol, and quercetin-compounds derived from the phenylpropanoid and downstream flavonoid biosynthetic pathways [20]. Therefore, the enhanced levels of phenolic compounds observed in FA-NC-treated salt-stressed plants are most plausibly explained by FA-mediated activation of the phenylpropanoid core pathway and its flavonoid branch, both of which are well-documented to respond strongly to oxidative cues and to contribute to improved photoprotection and antioxidant capacity.

The effect of salinity in our experiment caused the phenylpropanoid-derived antioxidant pool, as indicated by an increase in anthocyanins, tannins, total phenolics, and flavonoids, which is an adaptive response normally triggered by salt-elicited ROS and metabolic imbalance, inducing secondary metabolism to strengthen redox buffering, membrane protection, and stress signaling [46]. The additional enhancement of these phenolic fractions by FA suggests the priming action on phenolic biosynthesis and phenolic-dependent defense. This can be mechanistically plausible since FA is an effective antioxidant as well as a phenylpropanoid intermediate that can regulate phenylpropanoid pathway activity and other oxidative enzymes. Evidence has been provided that exogenous FA is capable of stimulating defense-related enzymes (e.g., PAL and PPO) and stress-related signaling pathways and increasing phenolic-associated resistance mechanisms [73]. Similarly, previous studies conducted under salinity indicated that FA treatment stimulated polyphenolic metabolites and enhanced stress performance, which was accompanied by a concerted rise in antioxidant capacity and production of secondary metabolites [20]. The differential response of secondary metabolites observed in the present study may be attributed to the selective regulation of biosynthetic pathways under external stimuli. Environmental stresses do not affect all metabolites uniformly and may stimulate the production of some compounds while others remain unchanged. For example, salinity stress altered the essential oil composition of D. moldavica, where some compounds increased, others decreased, and some were not detected [74]. Moreover, transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses have shown that salt and alkali stresses activate specific metabolic pathways such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and the TCA cycle, leading to differential metabolite accumulation [75]. Therefore, the unchanged tannin content observed in the present study may be related to the different regulation and sensitivity of the tannin biosynthetic pathway compared with other secondary metabolites [74, 75].

It is important to note that the accumulation of anthocyanins, total phenols, and flavonoids was greater with FA-NCs than with free FA under salinity, and nano-formulation enhanced functional delivery of the elicitor and prolonged the stimulation of the phenylpropanoid metabolism. This trend is similar to other bioactive NCs in the herbs in salinity, where NC forms were more effective than the free compound in preserving or stimulating antioxidant defenses, osmoregulation, and improving bioactive phytochemicals in the presence of high salt. Together, these findings help to endorse a mechanistic explanation that suggests that FA increases salinity tolerance, in part, by increasing phenolic-based antioxidative and protective chemistry. It is important to note that the NC form seems to obtain a better response in terms of greater stability and bioavailability and more enduring interaction with cellular targets, thus causing a greater phenylpropanoid response and, therefore, more effective buffering of the oxidative pressure caused by salt.

Conclusion

In general, the paper presents a new CMC-based FA-NC as a promising nano-enabled biostimulant of D. moldavica in salty conditions, and offers mechanistic data to support the fact that the nano-preparation is superior to free FA. Physicochemical characterization indicated that a stable nanoscale composite had been successfully prepared, and the biological results repeatedly demonstrated that FA-NC had a stronger mitigatory effect on salt injury based on the preservation of photosynthetic capacity and growth, enhancement of water status and membrane integrity of the leaf, reduced oxidative damage through an enhanced antioxidant response, and strengthening of osmotic adjustment and secondary metabolism. The PCA also justified this combined mechanism of action by locating FA-NC-treated plants nearer to clusters of beneficial traits and farther away from stress-marker associations, and signified a coordinated recovery of redox homeostasis and metabolic resilience. Applied-wise, these results indicate that FA-NC is a potentially effective, environmentally-friendly approach to increase productivity and phytochemical potential of medicinal plants on salt-impacted soils. Future research should prove the effectiveness of FA-NC in greenhouse-to-field conditions, determine the optimal dose and routing, and kinetics of release, as well as its long-term environmental safety (interaction with microbiome) and scalability of practical agricultural use.
