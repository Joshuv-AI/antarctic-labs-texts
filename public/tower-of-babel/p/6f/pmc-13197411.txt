A “turn-off” photoacoustic contrast for urokinase-type plasminogen activator activity
Authors: Ananya Sharma, Suvam Kumar Panda, Thuria Hasan, Sneha Ravanan, Sanhita Sinharay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ability to distinguish cancerous lesions based on aggressiveness using noninvasive molecular imaging techniques enables more precise and accurate diagnosis. In colorectal cancer screening, current approaches such as blood or fecal tests and endoscopic examination are widely used. However, reliably differentiating malignant adenomatous lesions from benign lesions, particularly those ≤5 mm in size, remains a significant clinical challenge. We synthesized, optimized, and validated a small-molecule near-infrared (NIR) activated photoacoustic dye conjugated to a tripeptide substrate specific for urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA), a protease that is upregulated in colorectal cancer tissues. The probe was designed to produce an “ON-OFF” photoacoustic signal upon activation by uPA. Specificity of the probe towards aggressiveness was evaluated using two colorectal cancer cell lines with differential uPA and cathepsin B expression. The uPA-responsive photoacoustic probe demonstrated high sensitivity and a clear “ON-OFF” activation signal in response to uPA activity. It showed strong specificity between colorectal cancer cell lines with different levels of uPA expression, confirming its selective activation. Noninvasive monitoring of extracellular uPA activity using photoacoustic imaging shows promise as a predictive screening approach for distinguishing malignant and premalignant colorectal lesions, particularly those that are small and difficult to classify using current screening methods.

Introduction

Globally, colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the deadliest cancers and is the third most-common type of cancer among both sexes, with approximately 900,000 deaths reported annually1. Histological evidence demonstrates colon cancer as a slow-evolving cancer that develops from adenomatous polyps through an adenoma-cancer sequence2. Numerous studies have documented how effective removal of adenomatous polyps through colonoscopic polypectomy correlated to the reduced incidence of CRC3,4. However, even with current gold-standard colonoscopy techniques, a substantial portion of polyps (~21%) are still missed during screening, especially flat polyps and adenomas smaller than 5 mm5,6. More importantly, polyps are characterized as adenomatous, hamartomatous, inflammatory, benign, or malignant, and there is no clear consensus on whether all need to be excised due to a lack of complete molecular characterization of each kind. Therefore, improving colon polyp detection through molecular markers has a critical impact, as these markers can assess malignancy beyond mere anatomical differences7. Recently, scientific and clinical communities evidenced the use of chromoendoscopy-assisted colonoscopy to enhance the detection of colonic lesions. In this approach, dyes are applied to the colon mucosa via an endoscopic catheter to improve lesion visualization8. However, the resulting color change is not based on molecular recognition. To address this need, incorporating a truly molecular imaging biomarker could greatly enhance the specificity of detecting precancerous malignant adenomatous polyps for early-stage colorectal cancer detection.

Previously, several proteolytic enzymes have been implicated in gastrointestinal tissue remodeling and angiogenic processes9. Urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA), a protease, along with the cell surface receptor of urokinase plasminogen activator (uPAR), plays an integral role in the plasminogen-plasmin system10. In breast and colorectal cancer (CRC), this system is heavily implicated with poor prognosis, and correlation of high levels of uPA with the enhanced tumor cell migration and higher risk of recurrence11,12. Interestingly, in a recent genomic profiling of CRC patients, prognostic driver genes were identified, and they represented mainly the EGFR, KRAS, BRAF or TGFβ pathways1. uPA has crucial mutual interactions and positive feedback loops with the TGFβ pathway13 and mutated KRAS induces uPAR expression through the AP-1 transcription factor14. In clinical studies involving CRC patients, the detection of uPA is reported to be a more significant prognostic marker than the commonly used tumor markers such as the gastrointestinal CA-19-915,16. In other clinical studies involving CRC, high uPA levels correlated with tumor budding, which is strongly associated with distant metastasis17.

Serum and plasma levels of uPA in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients are typically measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), mRNA quantification, or immunohistochemical staining. However, these approaches are limited by labor-intensive sample preparation and variability introduced by different extraction protocols9. More importantly, they report expression levels of uPA or its receptor uPAR, which do not necessarily reflect the amount of active enzyme because expression may include both active uPA and its inactive zymogen form18. In contrast, a uPA-responsive imaging probe would generate a measurable signal only in the presence of the active enzyme within the extracellular environment. Despite this advantage, there are currently few effective small-molecule imaging probes capable of noninvasively detecting active uPA in vivo.

Clinical molecular imaging modalities such as PET, MRI, and fluorescence imaging each have inherent constraints: MRI suffers from low sensitivity requiring millimolar concentrations of contrast agents; PET offers limited spatial resolution and involves radioactivity and high cost; and fluorescence imaging is limited by shallow tissue penetration and autofluorescence. These limitations highlight the need for newer, clinically translatable hybrid imaging strategies18–21. Photoacoustic (PA) imaging, a relatively recent technique, provides high spatial and temporal resolution without ionizing radiation, achieves greater tissue penetration (~5 cm) than optical imaging, and offers ultrasound-like spatial resolution. These attributes support its strong potential for clinical adoption, including PA tomography and photoacoustic endoscopy22,23. Small-molecule targeted contrast agents are particularly advantageous for molecular diagnostics because they offer high specificity and faster clearance than nanoparticle-based agents24. Such probes can be envisioned by selecting a tunable dye scaffold and linking it directly or through a linker to a targeting moiety such as a peptide25 or an antibody that responds selectively to the biomarker of interest26.

Recently, two photoacoustic probes have been reported for detecting uPA activity in invasive breast cancer models: one is an activatable polymeric reporter producing dual fluorescence and PA signals, and the other is a small-molecule PA probe incorporating a self-assembly motif27,28. All of these designs, including ours, utilize the uPA-specific GGR tripeptide. However, unlike previous probes in which the N-terminal glycine is protected with a Cbz (carboxy-benzoyl) group, our probe employs the deprotected tripeptide, resulting in improved aqueous solubility and a smaller molecular weight (~400 Da lower) characteristic of small-molecule behavior27. The key distinction is the response mechanism: our probe produces a decrease (“turn-off”) in PA signal upon uPA activation, whereas earlier probes primarily function as “turn-on” NIR-PA or PA-only agents. In this work, we present this small-molecule, uPA-responsive, “turn-off” PA probe and evaluate the uPA-responsiveness and mechanism with noninvasive PA imaging for future translation.

Results

A small molecule tripeptide conjugated, water soluble, uPA-activatable PA probe

We designed a small-molecule uPA-responsive photoacoustic probe, GGR-IR780, for efficient, sensitive, and specific detection of extracellular uPA activity (Scheme 1). The probe was developed on an NIR dye scaffold of IR780-Iodide and conjugated to a tripeptide Gly-Gly-Arg (GGR) through a para-aminophenol linker moiety. We hypothesized that the linker would stabilize the final probe with the dye conjugated to the peptide. We developed this probe in 7 steps, and the products were characterized with 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy and mass spectrometry (Figs. S1–S23). The final compound, GGR-IR780, was purified over high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), using a C-18 column (Rt=12.2 min) and validated with MALDI mass spectrometry as well as 2D TOCSY NMR studies (Figs. We then proceeded to test the photophysical behavior of this hypothesized uPA-responsive agent. The agent was soluble in water, establishing its good biocompatibility. From UV–Visible absorbance spectrum in both DMSO and water, the probe displayed a sharp absorption band in the NIR range with a λmax of 783 nm (DMSO) and 766 nm (Water). Interestingly, the molar extinction coefficients calculated were similar for both media, ƐDMSO = 1,35,748 M−1cm−1 and Ɛwater = 92,481 M−1cm−1, suggesting minimal H-aggregate or higher order aggregate formation in aqueous media (Figs. 1A, B and S24). From fluorescence emission studies on GGR-IR780, we observed maximum fluorescence intensity at λex = 700 nm (excitation wavelength) and λem = 812 nm (emission wavelength) and a Stokes shift, Δλ of 30 nm (Figs. 1C and S26). We finally assessed the photoacoustic properties of GGR-IR780 in the spectral range of 660–960 nm (iTHERA™ inVision 256-TF, Germany). The probe displayed excellent PA sensitivity in TRIS (pH=7.4) at concentrations as low as 50 µM, and we observed a linear increase in photoacoustic intensity with increasing concentrations up to 200 µM of the agent (Fig. 1D). It is important to note that tissue autofluorescence is minimal in the NIR-I window (700–900 nm), and our agents’ 700/812 nm excitation/emission profile lies within this low-background range, circumventing a significant translational challenge. Since cyanine fluorophores have also demonstrated susceptibility to photobleaching, especially at higher concentrations29, we investigated the probe stability in solution with absorbance studies, at pH 7.4, and compared it with the free IR780-iodide (Figs. 1E and S25). Although free IR-780 iodide suffered from limited photostability, as indicated by a time-dependent reduction in absorbance over 4 h, GGR-IR-780 demonstrated markedly enhanced stability under comparable conditions. Similarly, using dynamic photoacoustic imaging, we studied stability of GGR-IR780 over time with a 250 µM solution of the agent in DMSO at intervals of 10 min for 1 h, followed by 30 min intervals upto 4 h, and observed excellent stability without any significant change in the maximum PA intensity even at such high concentration (Fig. S27).

The usual tissue-mimicking agar-intralipid mold incorporated a primary cylindrical insert of 6 mm inner diameter (ID), within which a secondary capillary insert of 2.5 mm ID was positioned. In the control condition, both inserts were filled with water, and as expected, minimal photoacoustic signal was observed in either region (Fig. S37D). In contrast, when the outer 6 mm insert was filled with water and the inner 2.5 mm capillary was filled with the GGR-IR780 probe solution, a distinct and localized photoacoustic signal was detected exclusively from the 2.5 mm capillary insert (Fig. S37E). The signal intensity from the capillary insert was substantially above background and exhibited the characteristic PA spectral profile of the GGR-IR 780 probe (Fig. S37E). These results confirm that the uPA-responsive probe enables clear and spatially confined detection of targets with diameters as small as 2.5 mm, supporting the feasibility of identifying sub-5-mm lesions using our imaging strategy.

Loss of photoacoustic signal upon response to urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA) activity

To study the responsiveness of GGR-IR780 towards uPA activity, we monitored the change in both the NIR fluorescence and PA signal with a solution of GGR-IR780 (TRIS buffer) incubated at 37 °C with 10 enzyme units (EU). In both cases, we observed a prominent signal loss as a response to enzyme activity, suggesting fluorescence or PA “turn-off” upon uPA activity. Interestingly, the photoacoustic signal loss was 57% at 4 h (Figs. 2B and S29), while fluorescence signal demonstrated a 54% signal loss only after 24 h incubation (Figs. 2A and S28) with the same enzyme units, suggesting higher sensitivity and temporal resolution of PA than NIR fluorescence for detecting uPA responsiveness with the developed probe. To quantitatively assess the sensitivity of the uPA-responsive photoacoustic probe, we evaluated the response of GGR-IR780 as the slope of the normalized photoacoustic signal (PA/PA₀) versus enzyme concentration and compared it with the normalized absorbance response (A/A₀) of a commercially available colorimetric uPA substrate (ECM600, Sigma-Aldrich), where PA represents the photoacoustic intensity at a given enzyme concentration, PA₀ is the baseline signal in the absence of enzyme, A is the absorbance of the colorimetric probe at a given concentration, and A₀ is the absorbance of the same colorimetric probe without enzyme. The slope of the PA/PA₀ calibration curve for GGR-IR780 was −0.0092 U/µL, indicating high sensitivity toward uPA-mediated activation (Fig. S33). The negative sign of the slope was indicative of the decrease in PA signal as opposed to the regular increase in signal upon enzyme activation in other probes. A linear correlation between PA signal intensity and enzyme concentration was observed within the range of 2.5–20 EU, and the limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 20.96 U/µL. To assess the specificity of the probe for uPA activity and to validate that the “turn-off” property or loss in PA signal was indeed due to enzyme activity, we acquired longitudinal photoacoustic spectra for GGR-IR780 phantom (TRIS buffer, 100 µM), incubated with a cocktail of uPA enzyme (10 EU) and uPA inhibitor (10 mM, amiloride hydrochloride) at 37 °C, for 4 h30. We observed almost complete PA signal retention with the uPA inhibitor (Figs. 2C and S30) and therefore confirmed that the signal loss observed upon response to uPA was indeed due to high uPA enzyme activity. Additionally, we tested the probe against cathepsin B and MMP-2 enzymes, which are also upregulated in colorectal cancer31,32. Although no decrease in PA signal was observed when the probe was incubated with MMP-2, we observed a significant decrease in PA signal upon incubation with cathepsin B, although the decrease in PA signal upon uPA activity was considerably higher. The GGR-IR780 probe, therefore had moderately high but not complete specificity for uPA. These photoacoustic imaging results upon incubation with the uPA enzyme and uPA inhibitor as well as with other proteases overexpressed in aggressive colorectal cancer, suggested that we developed a probe that functions as a “turn-off” probe, where signal loss is predictive of high enzyme activity as the biomarker.

We then mechanistically investigated the loss of PA signal upon incubation with the enzyme, by following the enzyme reaction (10 EU) on GGR-IR780 with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, C-18 column). We observed ~45% decrease in the parent peak corresponding to GGR-IR780 (Rt = 12.2 min) and the appearance of a distinct new peak (Rt =10.5 min) at 1 h post incubation (Fig. 3A, B). A complete loss of the parent peak was, however, not observed even with longer incubation periods (5 h) with the same enzyme unit and probe concentration. Upon comparing the MALDI mass spectra of GGR-IR780 with that of the new product (Rt = 10.5 min), we observed that the molecular ion peak at m/z 882.52, corresponding to [M]⁺, was entirely absent in the product (Fig. 3D, E). Once the enzyme cleaves the peptide GGR, we expect the dye, now only conjugated with the linker with the free-amine (-NH2), annotated as “M” (Fig. 3E). In the MALDI-mass spectrum of the product peak, we observed peaks for both the ammonium (m/z = 631.1) and the ACN (m/z = 653.1) adducts, thereby confirming the cleavage of the

peptide as expected, however we also confirmed a new peak at a higher mass of m/z = 1013.36, which hinted at possible dimerization of the dye fragment without the phenolic linker (Fig. 3D, E). This possible dimerization could explain the loss of the PA signal upon enzyme response.

To further quantify how the probe changes structure in response to enzyme activity, we performed time variable 1D NMR spectroscopy (600 MHz, Bruker). Previously, we had characterized the protons in GGR-IR780 using CD3OH as the deuterated solvent, with this choice of deuterated solvent to maximize our chances of observing the faster exchanging N-H protons.

In this experiment, we prepared a 1 mM solution of GGR-IR780 in H₂O containing ~10% D₂O, since enzyme reactions generally require an aqueous environment. After acquiring the initial water-suppressed 1D NMR spectrum (Fig. 4, bottom panel), we added 10 EU of enzyme to the solution and recorded 1D NMR spectra every 30 min, and then every hour from 6 to 12 h. Although the N-H proton signals were difficult to observe, notable spectral changes appeared in the olefinic–aromatic region (δ 6.0–9.83 ppm), indicating peptide bond cleavage and alterations to the dye structure (Fig. 4, top panel). The broad singlet at δ 6.0 ppm shifted downfield by 0.6 ppm (to δ 6.6 ppm), and the multiplet cluster between δ 7.0 and 7.7 ppm integrated to 7 protons instead of 12, consistent with partial breakdown of aromatic and olefinic ring systems. The three most downfield-shifted protons (δ 9.8, 8.4, and 8.3 ppm) remained unchanged in both spectra, suggesting that the indole ring containing these protons remains intact during enzymatic activity. These downfield-shifted protons should belong to the indole ring that carries the positive charge.

In the aliphatic region, we observed changes in peptide-associated peaks and differences in proton integration, supporting the presence of two distinct species in solution. These observations are consistent with our previous MALDI and PA spectral data showing the formation of a reaction product during enzymatic processing of GGR-IR780.

PA imaging in colorectal cancer cell lines with GGR-IR780 can distinguish between aggressive vs. non-aggressive cancer cells

For this study, we selected two human colorectal adenocarcinoma cell lines, HCT-116 and Caco-2, which, despite originating from colorectal tumors, display markedly different biological behavior. Caco-2 cells are reported to be resistant to rapamycin, whereas HCT-116 cells are rapamycin-sensitive. Moreover, HCT-116 cells exhibit substantially higher tumorigenicity and invasive capacity compared with Caco-2 cells. As a result, Caco-2 cells arerepresentative of a colorectal cancer model with low metastatic potential, while HCT-116 cells are considered more aggressive and highly metastatic39,40. This distinction is reflected in reports of wound-healing assays where Caco-2 cells show limited migration and fail to close the wound even after 72 h, whereas HCT-116 cells demonstrate rapid wound closure, indicative of enhanced motility41. These phenotypic differences extend to the urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) system. Caco-2 cells express very low levels of the uPA receptor (uPAR), typically fewer than 1000 copies per cell, and are therefore frequently used as a low-uPAR-expressing control model42. In contrast, HCT-116 cells display elevated invasive behavior associated with significantly higher uPA expression at both transcript and protein levels43.

To determine whether these differences extend to uPA expression, we first performed Western blot analysis from protein extracts of cell lysates. We found that HCT-116 cells produced significantly higher levels of uPA compared to Caco-2 cells (Fig. 6A, B). We then performed a colorimetric uPA assay with cell lysates of both these cell lines to evaluate differences in uPA activity in the two cell lines. This commercial colorimetric kit provided a lyophilized power of uPA from human urine as a positive control. We observed activity within 20 min for both cell lines, although significantly higher activity for HCT-116 compared to that of Caco-2 (Fig. 6C). To assess the biocompatibility, we performed cytotoxicity studies with GGR-IR780 in HCT-116 cells, using Sulforhodamine B assay, and established that the agent had minimal toxicity below 100 µM (Fig. S36). Finally, we performed longitudinal photoacoustic imaging of GGR-IR780 incubated with cell lysates from both cell lines to evaluate the sensitivity of detecting uPA activity and specificity in differentiating between aggressive and non-aggressive colorectal cancer cells with photoacoustic imaging. We observed a 56% decrease in PA intensity after 3 h for HCT-116 cells and only a 33% decrease for Caco-2 cells, demonstrating an almost twofold decrease in PA intensity for aggressive colorectal tumor cells compared to the nonaggressive one (Fig. 6D–F). Interestingly, we observed that within the in vitro setting, maximum enzyme-activity was observed within the first 30 min of incubation and the seemingly low catalytic efficiency calculated in the solution studies was not a concerning deterrent within cell lysates (Fig. 6D–F). While the GGR-IR780 probe does not exhibit complete specificity for uPA activity, as indicated by our earlier specificity experiments, the observed 56% reduction in PA signal in HCT-116 lysates may partly reflect the high cathepsin B activity present in this cell line. Importantly, this does not undermine the overall utility or validity of the probe. Both previous reports and our western blot analysis demonstrate that cathepsin B is significantly overexpressed in the aggressive colorectal cancer cell line HCT-116 compared with the non-aggressive Caco-2 cell line (Fig. S35A, B). Consequently, even if a portion of the PA signal decrease arises from cathepsin B activity, this does not compromise the probe’s diagnostic relevance. Instead, the elevated cathepsin B levels observed in aggressive colorectal cancer further support the probe’s ability to preferentially generate a signal in more aggressive colorectal tumor phenotypes. Therefore, the partial contribution of cathepsin B activity does not detract from, and may in fact reinforce, the probe’s effectiveness for distinguishing aggressive colorectal cancer. These results proved that the GGR-IR780 probe could be activated by uPA in the extracellular environment within a short duration and could differentiate between low and high uPA activity, correlating to benign nonaggressive and malignant aggressive cancer tissues.

Discussion

Among enzymes, protease activity plays a central role in tumor biology, and this is validated with the evolution of enzyme-sensors and activatable probes that detect enzyme activity as a measure of malignancy or track early response to therapies. Among all noninvasive imaging techniques, optical or fluorescence imaging has leveraged this biomarker maximally and developed numerous optical probes that generate an optical or near-infrared fluorescent (NIRF) signal upon activation by proteases. These imaging agents developed for targeting cathepsins, gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), ß-galactosidase, MMPs, prostate-specific antigen (PSA) for cancers as well as against HIV-protease and Herpes simplex virus (HSV) protease for the detection of infectious pathology44–48. Photoacoustic imaging, a hybrid emerging technology that combines rich optical contrast with acoustic resolution and significantly better tissue penetration ability (~5 cm) compared to fluorescence imaging, has built on the same premise and developed PA probes for detecting cathepsins, MMP-2 and others49–52. The majority of the protease-detecting PA probes are polymeric and therefore lack the specificity of small molecule contrast agents24. In this study, we developed a water-soluble, small molecule (<1000 Da) PA probe, incorporating a dye conjugated to uPA-targeting tripeptide through a short aminophenol linker unit. We chose a NIR-dye IR780, such that it would absorb in the far NIR window at ~780 nm, thereby enabling greater tissue penetration. In the linker, the phenolic part (-OH) is conjugated to the IR780, and the amino part enables peptide coupling. The final probe, GGR-IR780 retains the NIR-absorbance, but shows improved stability compared to the free IR780-iodide (Fig. 1E).

Another major difference in the behavior of our uPA responsive probe from most other activatable probes developed is the response mechanism, where in this case the signal is quenched, leading it to behave as a “turn-off” or quenched probe, rather than the usual “turn-on” activatable probes. We approached the cause of this “turn-off” signal through various experiments. Our HPLC analysis revealed the appearance of a new peak at approximately Rt = 10.5 min, indicating the formation of a new compound that is slightly more polar than the parent molecule (Fig. 3B). Upon uPA-mediated peptide cleavage, the free amino group on the linker produces a more polar compound, as shown in the inset of Fig. 3E. However, the complete disappearance of the PA signal suggests a degradation of the IRdye chain, resulting in a loss of conjugation within the dye scaffold. Although conclusive evidence is lacking, proton NMR data from the enzyme-incubated probe (Fig. 4, top panel) partially support the loss of aromatic rings and the presence of multiple species following enzymatic activity. Notably, heptamethine cyanine dyes containing a linker with a terminal free amino group may be inherently unstable. The nitrogen’s lone pair electrons can delocalize into the ring system, triggering a cascade that disrupts the conjugation of the heptamethine cyanine scaffold or promotes partial dimer formation after linker cleavage, leading to the observed m/z 1013 peak (Fig. 3E).

Very few studies have investigated the in vitro enzyme kinetics parameters for protease-activatable NIRF and PA probes developed, so an extensive comparison is not feasible43. From our enzyme kinetics studies, the substrate GGR-IR780 demonstrated high binding affinity towards uPA, but the catalytic efficiency seemed only moderate due to a low turnover number. However, when compared to a series of other activatable probes, with different dye constructs, such as fluorescein, ATT0655, DY681 and Cy5, the catalytic efficiency of our construct is in a similar range for that of the reported probes53,54. In the in vitro studies in cell lysates, we observed that the maximum PA signal loss in the HCT116 lysates occurred within the first 30 min, which suggests efficient enzyme activity; however, complete loss of signal is not achieved, which is a limitation of the current probe.

Our comprehensive investigations conducted in solution and in vitro demonstrate that the GGR-IR780 probe exhibits robust responsiveness and functions effectively as a “turn-off” photoacoustic contrast agent for the assessment of elevated urokinase activity. However, these findings currently establish the probe primarily as a proof-of-concept, and several factors that may influence its performance in vivo remain to be systematically evaluated. In particular, further in vivo studies are required to investigate key parameters such as the probe’s ability to permeate cellular membranes, its biodistribution across target and non-target tissues following systemic administration, and its pharmacokinetic behavior, including circulation time and clearance pathways. Additionally, physiological variables such as tissue microenvironmental pH and redox conditions may affect probe stability and activation, and therefore warrant careful examination prior to translational application. From an imaging perspective, in vivo signal detection may also be influenced by factors including endogenous tissue background signals, optical scattering, and limited imaging depth, which could impact overall sensitivity and contrast. Consequently, future studies will focus on a comprehensive evaluation of pharmacokinetics, metabolic stability, and in vivo imaging performance in relevant animal models in order to further validate the translational potential of this probe

Among activatable ON-OFF probes, those that generate a signal in response to biomarker or enzyme recognition (“turn-on”) have clear benefits. However, a turn-off probe that begins with a strong initial photoacoustic (PA) signal can first indicate its localization in tissue and then enable monitoring of the progressive loss of signal over time. This dynamic decrease can directly reflect the level of enzyme activity, which in turn may correlate with lesion aggressiveness. We envision that such a uPA-responsive turn-off PA probe could initially aid in the diagnosis of all lesions and polyps (strong initial PA signal) and then further be used to distinguish aggressive colorectal lesions or precancerous malignant polyps from non-aggressive lesions or benign polyps, through ~50% signal loss in aggressive lesions, thereby improving the current challenge of specificity of molecular diagnostics in colorectal cancer.

Methods

General information

All commercially available reagents were used without further purification. The reagents and solvents were purchased from Sisco Research Laboratories Pvt. Ltd (SRL), India, Sigma Aldrich India, ChemScene LLC, and Merck, Germany. To monitor the progress of the reactions, analytical thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analyzes were performed using aluminum sheets precoated with silica gel (60 F254, Merck, Germany), and the components were observed under 265 nm UV light. PA spectra were recorded with the following acquisition parameters: 660–960 nm with a step size of 2 nm using 3 average frames per wavelength for 4 h at 37 °C. The plot of mean pixel intensity in the region of interest (ROI) at 780 nm vs. wavelength (nm) displayed a retention of PA signal upon exposure of the probe, GGR-IR780 to E-I cocktail, validating the enzyme specificity of the proposed probe.

The specificity of our probe towards uPA was investigated by also incubating the probe with other cancer cell-overexpressing proteases, such as cathepsin-B (Sigma catalogue no: 219364-M, extracted from human liver) and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP-2, Human Recombinant Protein, PeproTech, 420-02, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Enzyme solutions were made using the commercially recommended buffer, and 50 µM of GGR-IR780 was incubated with each for 37 °C for 4 h. Post-incubation as previously described, photoacoustic intensity of the solutions was recorded and for N = 3 biological repeats. HPLC assay to monitor enzyme responsiveness of GGR-IR780: The same 5 mM stock of GGR-IR780 was used to prepare a 500 µM solution of the probe (Tris). 10 enzyme units (EU) of uPA were added to 100 µL of 500 µM sample and incubated at 37 °C in a water bath. First, (i) 20 µL of 500 µM GGR-IR780 was injected (analytical HPLC, C-18, 5 µm), followed by the injection of (ii) 20 µL of 500 µM GGR-IR780 incubated with 100 EU of uPA at 37 °C for 5 h. The gradient used during the run is mentioned in Table S1. Post HPLC runs, the peaks at the retention time of 12.2 min for (i) and 10.5 min for (ii) were collected and submitted for MALDI-TOF mass spectra. The reduction in intensity of the parent peak at 12.2 min and the appearance of a new peak at 10.5 min validate the formation of a new compound post-enzyme reaction on the probe.

1D Proton NMR to monitor enzyme reaction: A 500 µL 1 mM solution of GGR-IR780 was prepared in H2O, with 10% D2O, and 1D proton NMR was acquired at room temperature, after shimming and water suppression on a 600 MHz Bruker Avance spectrometer (ns=16). Then the sample was taken out from the spectrometer, and 10 enzyme units (EU) (1 µL of 10000 EU stock dissolved in water) was added to the NMR tube, and mixed well. The NMR tube was then inserted back in the spectrometer, and the temperature was allowed to reach 37 °C over 5 min. Then the same 1D proton NMR with water suppression was set (ns=16).

Michaelis–Menten (MM) enzyme kinetics studies with photoacoustic imaging: GGR-IR780 solutions (each 200 µL) with concentrations of 35, 50, 65, 80, and 100 µM were prepared by serial dilution of a 5 mM stock solution using tris buffer (pH = 7.4). Then 10 enzyme units (EU) (10 µL of the enzyme stock) were added to the samples. Samples were placed in an agar phantom maintained at a temperature of 37 °C in the MSOT scanner, and PA spectra were collected for 240 min after the enzyme was added to the samples. The experiment was performed as three individual repeats, and then analysis was performed using the viewMSOT4.0 software. In vitro studies with two human colorectal cancer cell lines

Cell culture: Colorectal cancer cell lines, HCT-116 and Caco-2, were obtained as gifts from Dr. Kesavardana Sannula’s (Department of Biochemistry) and Dr. Sandhya S. Visweswariah’s lab (Department of Developmental Biology and Genetics), respectively, at Indian Institute of Science, Bengaluru. Both HCT-116 and Caco-2 cells were cultured in DMEM media (HiMedia) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco) and 1% Penicillin-streptomycin antibiotics (HiMedia). Cells were grown at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator, followed by lysate preparation.

Lysate preparation and protein estimation: Cultured cells were lysed using 10X assay buffer (90091 Merck Millipore) to perform the enzyme activity assay. Estimation of protein concentration was performed using the Bradford assay, where absorbance was taken at 595 nm using a multimode plate reader (BioTek Synergy H1, Agilent Technologies) in a Corning® 96-well plate (black) with a clear bottom.

Estimation of uPA activity in cell lysates through colorimetric assay: The cell lysates collected from HCT-116 and Caco-2 cell lines were used to compare the uPA activity. The assay was performed using the uPA Activity Assay Kit (ECM600 Sigma-Aldrich). The assay procedure recommended in the kit was employed to estimate the enzyme activity. The standard curves (Fig. S30) were used as a reference to calculate the enzyme activity in the unknown lysates.

Expression profiles of uPA in HCT-116 and Caco-2 cells using Western Blot: HCT-116 and Caco-2 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.6, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS) containing a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (PPC1010, Sigma-Aldrich). A standard Bradford assay was performed to estimate the total protein concentration of the lysates. Proteins were separated on 10% acrylamide/bisacrylamide gels and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Blocking was performed using 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h on the rocker. Membranes were incubated with primary antibody (Dilution of 1:1000) and peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Dilution of 1:2000). The following antibodies were used: uPA (E2M6I) Rabbit mAb (#15800), Cathepsin B (D1C7Y) Rabbit mAb (#31718S),β-Actin (13E5) Rabbit mAb (#4970), and Anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked Antibody (#7074) from Cell Signaling Technology (CST). Proteins were visualized with ChemiDoc XRS+ system (Biorad, USA) using Clarity ECL substrate (Biorad, USA) with exposure times of 30 s for uPA and 3 s for β-Actin. Quantification was done using β-Actin (housekeeping protein) as the loading control. Densitometry was employed as the normalization method, and Image Lab was used as an imaging software to measure the signal intensity (density) for each lane of the target protein band and the housekeeping protein. We then normalized the protein expression by taking the ratio of the target protein signal intensity to the loading control signal intensity for each sample lane.

Cytotoxicity assays: Cytotoxicity studies were performed with a standard Sulforhodamine B (SRB) assay. Briefly, 20,000 HCT-116 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate, and after 48 h, different concentrations of GGR-IR780 in 1X PBS (0.1–300 μM) were added to the plate in triplicate. 4 h post incubation with GGR-IR780, cells were fixed with 30% trichloroacetic acid, washed with 1% acetic acid, and dried overnight. SRB dye was then added the following day, following the assay protocol, and absorbance was recorded at 510 nm to quantify the cell viability. The assay was performed for 3 biological repeats. 1 μM Doxorubicin was used as a positive control, and 2% DMSO was used as a negative control. The % cell viability was quantified from the normalised absorbance values reported against each concentration.

Photoacoustic imaging (PAI) with cell lysates: As mentioned in the previous section, cell lysates were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer and were used for the assay. A solution of 400 µM GGR-IR780 in TRIS buffer was taken in two microcentrifuge tubes (150 μL in each) and 50 μL of the HCT-116 and Caco-2 cell lysates were added, respectively. The final concentration of GGR-IR780 in both samples was calculated to be 300 µM. The samples were sealed into capillaries for PA imaging, and the data were acquired at 10, 30, 60, 120, and 180 min. PA signals were detected in the wavelength range of 660–960 nm with a step size of 2 nm. The PA images of cell suspensions were obtained at a single wavelength of 800 nm. The samples were maintained at 37 °C in the water chamber to ensure optimal enzyme activity throughout the study.

Statistics and reproducibility

Experiments in solutions were performed in triplicate for enzyme kinetics studies and in vitro experiments. Unpaired t-tests were performed to determine the statistical difference for in vitro experiments, with **p < 0.05. Data is presented as mean ± SD.
