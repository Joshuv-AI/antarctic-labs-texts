Systemic amyloid A amyloidosis secondary to presumed chronic vertebral osteomyelitis and pressure ulcers in a mandrill (Mandrillus sphinx)
Authors: Akiho ISHIHARA, Keisuke KAWASE, Haruhisa MASATOU, Shinya IIDA, Kota KADOWAKI, Hina YAMASHITA, Tomokazu NAGASHIMA, Natsumi KOBAYASHI, Tomoaki MURAKAMI, Honoka KAWAMURA, Yukino MACHIDA, Junichi KAMIIE, Daisuke HASEGAWA, Masaki MICHISHITA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Systemic amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis involves extracellular deposition of fibrils derived from serum amyloid A (SAA) and is typically associated with chronic inflammation. A 16-year-old male mandrill (Mandrillus sphinx) developed lameness and presumed chronic vertebral osteomyelitis, progressed to recumbency, and died. Histopathology revealed Congo red–positive deposits with apple-green birefringence under polarized light in multiple organs, including the liver, spleen, kidneys, heart, and intestines. Immunohistochemistry confirmed SAA immunoreactivity, and mass spectrometry identified SAA-2 as the predominant amyloid protein, establishing systemic AA amyloidosis. No infectious agents were detected in examined tissues; however, vertebral osteomyelitis and pressure ulcers were considered likely sources of persistent inflammation, which may have contributed to the development of systemic AA amyloidosis. This represents the first reported case of AA amyloidosis in a mandrill, in which amyloid A was identified and confirmed by both immunohistochemistry and mass spectrometry.

Systemic amyloidosis is a disorder characterized by the extracellular deposition of insoluble amyloid fibrils derived from misfolded proteins in multiple organs [16]. In veterinary medicine, amyloid A (AA) amyloidosis is the most common form of systemic amyloidosis, typically associated with chronic inflammatory conditions leading to excessive production of serum amyloid A (SAA) [10, 16]. The liver, kidneys, and spleen are the most frequently affected organs, and deposition often results in progressive dysfunction of multiple organs, including renal or hepatic failure [23]. In recent years, amyloid characterization has relied on both conventional histopathological techniques and mass spectrometry (MS)-based approaches, enabling the precise identification of amyloid precursor proteins [11].

Systemic amyloidosis in non-human primates has been reported in Celebes crested macaques [6], chimpanzees [8], common marmosets [15], rhesus monkeys [3, 5], pig-tailed macaques [9, 21] and Bornean orangutans [9], with most cases being AA amyloidosis. In mandrills, amyloid deposits have been reported in only a case, involving the heart and vasculature, secondary to persistent chronic inflammation associated with diffuse granulomatous enteritis and mesenteric lymphadenitis caused by Mycobacterium avium subsp. Paratuberculosis [23]. Additionally, these deposits are seen in multicentric granulomatous interstitial pneumonia without mycobacterial infection, multifocal tongue candidiasis [23], and islet amyloid polypeptide deposition in spontaneously occurring diabetes mellitus [19]. Thus, reports of systemic amyloidosis or amyloid deposition in mandrills are limited, and no documented cases have been identified SAA as the amyloid precursor protein using immunohistochemistry (IHC) and MS. Herein, we report the morphological and immunohistochemical characteristics of systemic AA amyloidosis in a mandrill, as well as the molecular features of the amyloid revealed by MS.

A 16-year-old male mandrill (Mandrillus sphinx) housed at a zoological park presented with lameness of the left hind limb and was radiographically diagnosed with osteoarthritis. After 15 months, the animal became unable to stand and developed decubital ulcers on both gluteal regions and the thoracic skin. Hematological and serum biochemical analyses revealed no remarkable abnormalities. One month later, computed tomography revealed periosteal new bone formation on the vertebral bodies from T6 to T10 and T13 to L2, predominantly on the ventral aspects. In this case, amyloid deposition was observed in the liver, spleen, renal medulla, heart, and gastrointestinal mucosa. The distribution of amyloid resembled that of systemic amyloidosis reported in non-human primates, in which deposition occurs in various organs, including the liver, gastrointestinal tract, and spleen [1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 15]. In nonhuman primates, amyloidosis derived from transthyretin (ATTR) has been reported, particularly in vervet monkeys as a senile systemic form [4, 18]. In contrast, the present case demonstrated immunohistochemical positivity for serum amyloid A (SAA), and LC-MS/MS analysis confirmed SAA as the major amyloid component, supporting a diagnosis of systemic AA amyloidosis. Moreover, the distribution pattern and clinical background of persistent inflammation were consistent with AA amyloidosis, and there were no findings suggestive of other amyloid types, including ATTR. These results highlight the importance of combining IHC and proteomic analysis for precise amyloid typing in nonhuman primates.

At necropsy, accumulation of pericardial effusion containing clots was observed, suggesting cardiac tamponade as a possible immediate cause of death. However, histopathological examination failed to identify a definitive underlying lesion responsible for the pericardial effusion. Given the presence of systemic amyloid deposition, including involvement of multiple organs, it is possible that amyloidosis contributed to the overall deterioration of the clinical condition. Although leukopenia was noted shortly before death, the absence of bone marrow examination and limited clinicopathological data precluded evaluation of sepsis or other systemic conditions. Therefore, the cause of death in this case was considered to be most consistent with suspected cardiac tamponade, with systemic amyloidosis as a potential contributing factor. No amyloid deposition was observed in the renal glomeruli, whereas deposition was limited to the medullary interstitium. Although hypoalbuminemia in AA amyloidosis is commonly associated with renal loss due to glomerular involvement [13], the absence of glomerular deposition in this case suggests that protein loss from the kidney was likely minimal. In contrast, severe hepatic amyloid deposition together with decreased ALT and AST activities indicates that impaired hepatic protein synthesis may have played a major role in the development of hypoalbuminemia.

Reported predisposing factors for systemic amyloidosis in non-human primates include chronic enteritis [2, 21], chronic arthritis [3, 5], chronic cutaneous ulcers or decubital lesions [14], intestinal parasitic infections, tuberculosis, or non-tuberculous mycobacteriosis [2, 6, 8], persistent chronic inflammation associated with elevated SAA [20], familial predisposition, often in the context of group housing and chronic inflammation [15], and possible seeding or transmission, in the context of high SAA concentrations and chronic inflammation [16]. In the present case, the animal was housed individually. No evidence of mycobacterial or fungal infection was identified in the examined tissues; however, these infections cannot be completely excluded because the suspected osteomyelitis lesion was not evaluated histologically or by culture. Histopathological examination of the perineal skin and thoracic vertebrae, as well as longitudinal monitoring of SAA, could not be performed. Secondary AA amyloidosis associated with chronic osteomyelitis has been reported in animals [22], supporting the likelihood that chronic vertebral osteomyelitis in this mandrill served as a persistent inflammatory trigger. Although 16 years of age is not exceptionally old for captive mandrills, this case developed systemic amyloidosis leading to death. This finding emphasizes that careful monitoring and management of chronic inflammatory conditions are essential to reduce the risk of amyloidosis, even in individuals that are not extremely aged.

In conclusion, this report describes a case of AA amyloidosis in a mandrill, characterized by detailed clinicopathological findings and precise identification of the amyloid precursor protein using IHC and LC-MS/MS. This case highlights the diagnostic value of integrating histopathology with advanced proteomic analysis and provides novel insight into amyloidosis in mandrill.
