Cellular replisomes are powered by flex-fuel motors for unwinding DNA
Authors: Fahad Rashid, Sushil Pangeni, Chuan Liu, Harish Kumar, Gyeongtae Sun Moon, Qianyun Yan, Taekjip Ha, James M Berger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA replication relies on hexameric, ring-shaped helicases to unwind parental DNA for supporting fork progression over tens of thousands of base pairs. Oddly, biochemical studies have suggested that, on their own, replicative helicases are rather limited motors that struggle to couple rapid movement to nucleotide turnover (typically believed to be solely ATP). Here, single-molecule studies reveal that when properly loaded, the Escherichia coli (E. coli) replicative helicase, DnaB, is a tremendously fast single-stranded DNA translocase that moves up to three times more rapidly than the replisome (3 knt/s). Translocation is highly processive, resistant to pulling force/high salt, and can displace short, 3’-tailed DNA duplexes without apparent changes in speed. Surprisingly, we find that the loader for DnaB, DnaC, can use any rNTP or dATP for depositing DnaB onto ssDNA and that the helicase itself also must hydrolyze nucleotide for stable loading. DnaB translocation also turns out to be supported by any r/dNTP, a property shown to extend to the eukaryotic CMG replicative helicase. Overall, the DNA unwinding engines that support cellular replisomes are highly indiscriminate of their fuel source, a feature that may be of utility during times when cells encounter nucleotide pool stress but have committed to DNA synthesis.Subject terms: DNA, Biophysics, Replisome, Enzymes

Introduction

DNA replication is essential to cellular proliferation. Because genetic information is encoded in long, intertwined nucleic acid strands, cells have evolved molecular machineries—termed replisomes—that can unwind and synthesize DNA in a fast and processive manner. Replisomes consist of multisubunit complexes that are tasked with separating parental DNA strands and catalyzing the formation of complementary daughter copies1,2. How disparate enzymatic and scaffolding functions are brought together by replisomes to support the timely and accurate duplication of genetic information is a significant area of fundamental inquiry with important implications for both understanding human disease and driving therapeutic development.

Within cellular replisomes, duplex DNA unwinding is carried out by ring-shaped hexameric helicases3. Two major families of cellular replicative helicases, bacterial and archaeal/eukaryotic, appear to have evolved independently4,5; however, both encircle and track along single-stranded DNA (ssDNA)6. Due to their toroidal nature, specific recruitment and loading proteins are often used to overcome the topological challenges associated with shepherding replicative helicases onto origin DNA. In eukaryotes and many prokaryotic species, helicase loading factors consume ATP to support their function7. The mechanisms by which helicase loaders appropriately channel ATP binding vs. turnover into timely and productive loading events generally remain poorly understood at a molecular level. In bacterial replication systems, it is also unclear whether loading factors must fully dissociate from their clients before helicase movement can ensue.

Once loaded, replicative helicases consume chemical energy to support unidirectional movement and strand separation. It has long been accepted that ATP is the sole fuel source for the cellular versions of these enzymes8–11. However, ATPase rates measured for replicative helicases in vitro are typically slower than the rate which cellular replisomes move (~3.5 ATP/s vs. ~100 bp/s in eukaryotes and ~40 ATP/s vs. ~1000 bp/s in bacteria)12,13, and studies of replicative helicases in isolation have revealed them to be puzzlingly weak DNA unwindases (e.g., the bacterial replicative helicase, DnaB, takes minutes to unwind short (20–40 bp) DNA forks14,15). In some instances, the binding of partner proteins (e.g., DnaG, a primase, to DnaB in bacteria or Mcm10 to the CMG helicase in eukaryotes) can stimulate helicase activity13,16–18, but not to levels that match known replication speeds in cells. Interestingly, replicative helicases typically interact physically with their cognate DNA polymerases and, in instances where it has been well-studied (e.g., the phage T7 replication system), the act of deoxyribonucleotide incorporation can greatly accelerate DNA unwinding rates19,20. Whether this stimulation arises primarily because all replicative helicases need the motor activity of DNA polymerases to help power their own movement, or whether some replicative helicases innately possess robust motor qualities that have yet to be uncovered is unknown.

Here, we set out to better define the role of nucleotide in supporting the loading and translocation of E. coli DnaB and its intrinsic properties as a motor. Using single-molecule confocal fluorescence microscopy combined with dual optical traps, we establish that DnaC can unexpectedly use any ribonucleotide, as well as dATP, to load DnaB onto ssDNA, and that nucleotide hydrolysis is required both for DnaC dissociation and the initiation of DnaB movement. During translocation, DnaC can rebind to DnaB to slow movement and promote helicase unloading; however, when free of DnaC, DnaB can move along ssDNA at tremendous rates that exceed the speed of the full E. coli replisome. DnaB proved to be a highly processive and robust translocase, showing tolerance to salt and force challenges and unwinding short DNA duplexes without apparent pauses. Surprisingly, we find that DnaB is even more agnostic to the type of chemical fuel it uses, with both dNTPs and rNTPs proving capable of supporting rapid translocation, a nucleotide promiscuity we show to extend to the eukaryotic CMG helicase as well. These findings reveal that cellular replicative helicases have evolved a capacity to utilise the entire pool of nucleoside triphosphates when called upon to support DNA synthesis.

Results

DnaB loading by full-length DnaC strictly requires rNTPs (or dATP) but not the DnaC AAA+ ATPase domain

To better understand the molecular mechanisms of both replicative helicase loading and translocation in bacteria, we established a single molecule assay that monitors both events using E. coli DnaB and DnaC. We covalently attached a Cy5 fluorophore to the helicase through a surface-exposed point mutation, D290C, which does not affect helicase function (Supplementary Fig. 1A). A 48 knt ssDNA substrate was then generated by force stretching dsDNA lambda DNA tethered between two 4.3 µm beads (in a coupled optical trap/confocal fluorescence microscopy system, the LUMICKS C-trap) and used to capture individual helicases and image their behaviour over time (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Figs. 1B–E). DnaB loading was evidenced by the appearance of single, fluorescent foci between the beads, and was seen only when both nucleotide (ATP) and DnaC were present in the C-trap loading channel (Fig. 1B). Once loaded, helicases were seen to translocate in the reaction channel, moving smoothly over time with little discernable pausing (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 2A, Supplementary movie 1). All helicases loaded onto a given tether moved in a single direction (presumably 5′→3′, the established polarity for DnaB11), consistent with powered rather than diffusive movement.

To assess the specific role of ATP in supporting DnaB loading by DnaC, the recruitment of DnaB to ssDNA was monitored in the presence of each of one of four rNTPs (ATP, GTP, CTP, and UTP) or dNTPs (dATP, dCTP, dGTP, dTTP). Unexpectedly, all four rNTPs were found to support loading, as well as dATP (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Figs. 2B–E), but not other dNTPs tested (Supplementary Figs. 2F–H). As a control, when ATP-loaded DnaB molecules were moved to a channel with ADP or no nucleotide, translocation ceased (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Fig. 2I); however, translocation resumed when the same loaded DnaB was moved back to the ATP channel (Supplementary Fig. 2J), consistent with the known dependency of translocation on nucleotide hydrolysis. Loading (but not translocation) could be seen when ATPγS or AMPPNP was used in the loading channel (Fig. 1E, Supplementary Fig. 2K), confirming prior reports that DnaC does not need to hydrolyze nucleotide to guide DnaB onto ssDNA14,21. We also tested a fragment of DnaC comprising just its N-terminal helicase-binding domain (DnaCNTD) that had been shown to promote DnaB loading in bulk studies14,22; this truncation mutant also supported productive DnaB loading in the single molecule assay (Fig. 1F). Thus, the DnaC N-terminal domain is sufficient to load DnaB onto ssDNA, whereas full-length DnaC additionally requires nucleotide binding by its AAA+ catalytic region (any rNTP or by dATP) but not nucleotide hydrolysis.

DnaC represses DnaB translocation and can promote helicase unloading

With only DnaB fluorescently labeled, the fate of DnaC during DnaB loading and translocation remained unclear. Previous studies have suggested the ejection of DnaC from DnaB is inefficient, and that the helicase cannot move until DnaC has dissociated23,24. To further resolve this relationship, we labeled DnaC with Cy3 using a C-terminal YbbR tag and imaged ssDNA loading/translocation reactions by monitoring both Cy3 and Cy5 emission. The initial experimental approach involved first loading DnaB6Cy5•DnaC6Cy3 complexes onto ssDNA in buffer containing ATP and then moving the molecules into the reaction channel; however, only translocating DnaB molecules were seen without any DnaC signal during these experiments (Supplementary Figs. 3A–B). Reasoning that DnaC might have dissociated from DnaB immediately after loading or while moving the tether from the loading to the reaction channel, we modified the experimental setup to pre-form DnaB6Cy5•DnaC6Cy3 complexes with ATPγS, with the goal of capturing a pre-translocation state where DnaC might still be bound (Fig. 2A). In this setup, clear signals for both DnaB and DnaC were observed (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Next, the loaded DnaBC was moved to the reaction channel containing buffer with ATP. We observed that when DnaC was evident, it remained bound to DnaB for a short period (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 3D), presumably while ATPγS was exchanged for ATP, before departing from DnaB. Notably, the loss of the Cy3 signal (indicating DnaC dissociation) was quickly followed by the onset of DnaB translocation, with an average delay time of less than a second (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 3D–E). Thus, the ability of DnaC to dissociate from DnaB is dependent on ATP hydrolysis, and DnaB in turn requires that DnaC leave before initiating translocation on ssDNA.

In several instances, it was seen that overlapping, paused DnaBC foci would disappear in synchrony (Fig. 2D–E). Because each loaded DnaBC complex should have up to ~12 dye labels (6 Cy3 and 6 Cy5), making complete photo-bleaching in a single step unlikely, this behaviour instead suggested that DnaC could unload DnaB from ssDNA before the bound ATPγS could be replaced with ATP. To test this hypothesis, labeled DnaB was first deposited onto ssDNA with label-free DnaC and ATP in the loading channel and then moved to a reaction channel that also contained different concentrations of unlabeled DnaC (0–10 µM) (Supplementary Fig. 3F). When no DnaC was present or when it was present at low concentrations, robust and highly processive DnaB movement was seen (Supplementary Fig. 3G). By contrast, at high DnaC concentrations, little helicase translocation was observed and the helicase signal frequently disappeared before moving (Supplementary Fig. 3H). Interestingly, at intermediate levels of DnaC, processivity was decreased from that seen when DnaC was omitted and the rate of DnaB movement now could be seen to shift between different speeds multiple times until the helicase signal would either disappear or run into the bead (Fig. 2F). This response suggests that once mobile, DnaB may occasionally reassociate with free DnaC, which causes the motor to slow until the loader either dissociates again (restoring full speed) or unloads the helicase; the midpoint of the transition from highly processive to dissociative movement by DnaB occurred at ~1 µM DnaC (Supplementary Fig. 3I).

The slower translocation rates observed at intermediate DnaC concentrations indicate that DnaB can accommodate the binding of one or a few copies of the loader while still retaining some capacity to move. Since DnaC is known to repress DnaB’s ATPase activity23,25, we set out to more directly explore the role of ATP hydrolysis by DnaB in supporting helicase loading and movement. Using the optical trap, a Cy5-labeled, ATPase defective Walker B mutant of DnaB (DnaBEA) was loaded onto single-stranded DNA in presence of wild-type, Cy3-labeled DnaC. Stable co-localisation was seen for both DnaBEA and DnaC signals (Fig. 2G). Notably, DnaBEA remained immobile when ATP was present in the reaction channel, consistent with a need for nucleotide hydrolysis in supporting helicase translocation. Surprisingly, however, DnaC also remained bound to DnaBEA and did not dissociate unless the helicase signal also disappeared. This correlation indicates that the catalytic activity of DnaC is coupled to the hydrolysis state of DnaB and that nucleotide hydrolysis by DnaB is in turn necessary for triggering DnaC release.

DnaB movement on ssDNA is fast, highly processive, and both salt and tension tolerant

While assessing helicase loading, we were struck by the rapid and uniform pace of DnaB’s movement once DnaC was no longer present. ssDNA tracking by a DnaB-family helicase has been previously measured at a rate of ~300 nt/s for the bacteriophage T7 gp4 protein (which lacks an N-terminal dimerization and assembly domain found in cellular DnaBs)20. In examining our single molecule traces, DnaB could be seen to translocate nearly 5x faster than gp4 at an average speed of 1400 nt/s, with the rates of individual helicases following a Gaussian distribution about the mean (Fig. 3A). By comparison, when DnaC was present, the multiple movement phases observed for DnaB varied between the average speed seen in the absence of the loader and rates that are 2× (~800 nts/s) or 10× slower (~110 nt/s) (Fig. 3B). Upon raising the temperature of the assay, the speed of DnaB increased, reaching an average rate of 2600 nt/s at 37 °C (the nominal growth temperature of E. coli) and 2800 nt/s at 45 °C (Fig. 3C, Supplementary Figs. 4A, B). Regardless of the temperature used, the processivity of DnaB remained extremely high with virtually no particles dissociating before they collided with the 3′-tethered bead. To confirm that fast DnaB movement depends on consistent nucleotide turnover and does not involve a diffusive component, we also monitored translocation in the presence of 1 mM ATP and differing amounts of ATPγS. At high ATPγS concentrations (100 µM), extremely slow or no movement was seen (Supplementary Fig. 4C). However, as ATPγS levels were dropped (e.g., to 5 µM), movement became apparent, interspersed with pauses (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Unlike the biphasic rate switching observed with DnaC, little to no discernable movement was seen during pausing with ATPγS, indicating that the nucleotide completely halted helicase progression. Thus, the rapid translocation observed for DnaB depends upon the motor’s ability to catalyze ATP hydrolysis.

Because the speed and processivity of DnaB proved much greater than anticipated from prior studies, we elected to challenge the helicase with different experimental conditions to assess the motor’s robustness. Like many other hexameric helicases, DnaB associates with its ssDNA substrate in part by forming salt-bridges between the substrate's phosphodiester backbone and basic amino acids in the pore of the motor (R387 and R403 in E. coli DnaB)26. To disrupt ion-pair mediated DnaB-DNA interactions, the salt concentration in the optical trap reaction channel was adjusted between 100, 300, and 500 mM NaCl. Remarkably, the translocation rate of DnaB stayed relatively constant and even appeared to slightly increase as the salt concentration increased (Fig. 3D). Next, we tested the ability of the helicase to work against tension in the translocation strand. The force applied to the tether was examined at 3, 5, 10, and 20 pN. For these conditions, the translocation rate remained relatively constant and unaffected, except at 5 pN, where the rate appeared to increase by ~50% on average compared to the other forces (at forces >20 pN, helicases were seen to dissociate after a short period of binding) (Fig. 3E). The modest increase in translocation velocity at 5 pN may result from partial suppression of transient secondary structure in the ssDNA substrate, thereby slightly improving productive engagement of DnaB with the tracking strand. By contrast, higher forces over 20 pN may prevent DnaB from molding the ssDNA bound within its central channel into a curved spiral (with approximate A-form geometry), thereby destabilizing the hexamer and promoting dissociation. Overall, DnaB is a markedly salt- and tension-resistant motor.

DnaB remains relatively fast while unwinding DNA but loses processivity

Although DnaB translocates along ssDNA, its primary role is to do so under the load of unwinding a complementary strand from its template. Prior bulk biochemical studies using model DNA fork substrates have reported unwinding by DnaB to be relatively slow, ~35 bp/s27, whereas a single molecule study using magnetic tweezers reported that DnaB could unwind a DNA duplex at a rate of ~100 bp/s, but also provided the DNA was stretched by ~20 pN of tension, a fairly high level of pulling force28. To examine unwinding further, we probed whether a fast-moving DnaB on ssDNA slows when encountering and displacing either a 30 nt or 60 nt Cy3-labeled annealed DNA oligo with a 24 nt 3′ noncomplementary overhang. Analysis of the resulting traces showed that once loaded and moving, DnaB frequently displaced the oligo (as observed by an abrupt disappearance of Cy3 signal when Cy5 signal reaches the oligo location) without any apparent change in rate either during or following the encounter (Fig. 4A–C).

While assessing the rate of DnaB during unwinding, we noticed two other outcomes in addition to oligo displacement after a DnaB encounter. In one, DnaB could be seen to bypass the oligo without removing it from the tether (Fig. 4D). In the other, the DnaB signal disappeared while the oligo again remained annealed (Fig. 4E). The overall observed frequency for the three events was 85% unwinding, 5% bypass, and 10% DnaB dissociation for the 30 bp substrate and 60% unwinding, 20% bypass, and 20% DnaB dissociation for the 60 bp substrate (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these findings establish that DnaB can rapidly unwind a duplex when a 3′ overhang is present to act as a steric block. However, the data also show that DnaB is at times also capable of translocating over a 3′-overhang duplex and that this substrate can at times induce the isolated helicase to fall off the tether.

DnaB can unexpectedly utilise dNTPs or rNTPs for driving translocation

While assessing DnaB loading by DnaC, we ran a control assay in which the loader was replaced with just the DnaC NTD and ATP was replaced by a dNTP mix. We were surprised to see that DnaB was not only loaded but also translocated along ssDNA (Fig. 5A). To verify that this activity wasn’t a result of an impurity from the DnaC NTD prep, we expressed, purified, and examined a different DnaB loading factor, the P protein, from phage λ. As with the DnaC NTD, dNTPs alone were sufficient for both loading and translocation when P was employed as well (Supplementary Fig. 5A). To further validate this activity, we used full-length DnaC to first load DnaB onto ssDNA in presence of ATP and then subsequently moved the DnaB-bound tether to a channel containing dNTPs (Supplementary Fig. 5B). DnaB again showed normal translocation in the dNTP-containing channel, thus demonstrating that its translocation can be powered by nucleotides other than ATP (Fig. 5B).

Since the ability of DnaB to use dNTPs as a cofactor for driving translocation was unexpected, we elected to characterize and compare the relative effectiveness of ATP to other nucleotides (Fig. 5C). For the rNTPs that were assessed (GTP, CTP, UTP), all supported movement at an average rate similar to that seen for ATP. When dNTPs were individually tested (dCTP, dGTP, dTTP, dATP), the average rate observed was approximately half that seen for rATP; the lone exception was dTTP, which supported a mean translocation speed comparable to that of ATP. A comparison of mean translocation rates at room temperature (20 °C) as a function of different ATP or dTTP concentrations revealed a maximal translocation rate of 1575 nt/s and 1560 nt/s for the two nucleotides, respectively, and a Km of ~350 µM and ~700 µM (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Thus, although dTTP may not be bound as effectively as ATP (higher Km), it nonetheless appears to be utilised with comparable efficiency in terms of supporting movement. One potential concern to this study is that trace ATP contamination in the dNTP preparations could account for the observed activity; however, commercial dNTPs are typically supplied at ≥99% purity, and contaminants would need to be present at unusually high levels to explain our results. Based on the ATP concentration-dependence of translocation (Supplementary Fig. 5C), ATP (or NTP) contamination would need to exceed ~20%–30% of the total dNTP concentration to support the rates observed, a level far above that expected from the vendor specifications. We nonetheless performed several controls to address this possibility. First, DnaB failed to load in the presence of individual dNTPs (Supplementary Figs. 2F–H), arguing against ATP contamination, since even low levels of contaminating ATP would be expected to support loading. Second, we analyzed the individual nucleotide stocks by ion-exchange chromatography (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Although not all dNTP/rNTP species could be fully resolved from one another, the nucleotides that could be cleanly assessed, including dGTP and dTTP, showed no detectable ATP contaminating peak.

Having established that dNTPs alone are sufficient to promote the movement of DnaB along ssDNA, we next sought to determine whether they would also support helicase activity in the context of a replication fork. To address this question, we employed a rolling circle replication assay using magnetic beads to monitor leading-strand replication (Fig. 5D). Because the loading of the β clamp (a ring-shaped polymerase processivity factor) requires ATP, this protein as well as the clamp loader complex (subunits τ3δδ’χψ) and the DNA polymerase III holoenzyme (subunits αεθ) were loaded onto the substrate along with DnaB in the presence of DnaC and ATP. After washing the beads multiple times with an ATP-free buffer, replication was initiated by adding either dNTPs alone or dNTPs in combination with ATP, along with SSB. A control reaction with ATP alone was also included. Results from this experiment showed that the inclusion of both ATP and dNTPs supported strand synthesis, as indicated by the appearance of a high-molecular-weight band near the well of the gel (Fig. 5E), whereas ATP alone did not yield any replication product. However, the presence of just dNTPs also enabled leading strand synthesis, with an efficiency over time similar to that seen when a comparable amount of ATP was present (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Thus, dNTPs alone are sufficient for driving the movement and activity of a bacterial DNA replication fork once it is assembled.

Of the dNTPs examined, the higher effectiveness of dTTP in promoting ssDNA translocation was intriguing, as it is the primary nucleotide used to support DNA unwinding by T7 gp429. This congruence led us to inspect the catalytic centers of gp4 and DnaB for features that might explain their relative specificity and promiscuity, respectively. A structure of hexameric gp4 in complex with dTTP and ssDNA shows that the helicase binds nucleotide in a confined pocket that allows the catalytic center to sterically discriminate thymine from other nucleobases and deoxyribose from ribose (Fig. 6A)30,31. By comparison, the active site of hexamerized DnaB is more spacious and makes no specific contacts to the bound base and only one (a hydrogen bond with non-ideal geometry) to the 2′-OH (Fig. 6B)26. To confirm that dTTP was being recognized in the same manner as ATP, we determined a 3.5 Å resolution cryo-EM structure of DnaB with dTDP•AlFx and a 37 nt ssDNA substrate (Supplementary Figs. 6A–F). The structure is similar in overall configuration to prior models of the helicase (except that the N-terminal domains appear more conformationally dynamic) and shows a pose for thymine nucleoside that is similar to that of ATP (Fig. 6C). Thus, cellular DnaB family proteins have evolved a non-discriminatory active site that can utilise any nucleotide for supporting translocation and fork progression.

Processive movement by eukaryotic CMG is also supported by d/rNTPs

Given the ability of the bacterial replicative helicase to utilise any of the predominant ribo- and deoxyribo-nucleoside triphosphates found in cells, we next asked whether this promiscuity might be shared with its eukaryotic counterpart. Although the AAA+ ATPase active site present in MCMs bind nucleotide in a manner distinct from that of their RecA counterparts in DnaB-like helicases, there is again a lack of specific nucleobase or 2′-OH interactions in this region (Fig. 6D)32. To determine whether this spacious structural configuration translates into a relaxed functional requirement, we expressed, purified, and tested the ability of D. melanogaster CMG to translocate along DNA in the presence of Mcm10 (a processivity factor16) and dNTPs vs. ATP. CMG was labeled using N-hydroxysuccinimide chemistry to attach Cy3 to protein N-termini and then used in the same optical trap ssDNA translocation assay as employed for DnaB (Supplementary Fig. 7A). These experiments showed that in the presence of ATP, CMG translocated with an average speed of ~15 nt/s (Fig. 6E), a relatively slow rate but one in agreement with previously published findings33,34. The same experiment was then conducted using dNTPs and CMG was again observed to translocate at a similar average speed as with ATP and with a comparable rate distribution (Fig. 6F, Supplementary Fig. 7B). To test whether dNTPs could be used to support DNA unwinding as well as ssDNA translocation, we conducted a fluorescence-based bulk biochemical assay using a DNA fork. In this reaction, CMG promoted comparable levels of strand displacement regardless of whether ATP or dNTPs were present (Fig. 6G). Further testing with individual nucleotides showed that CMG can support unwinding with any of the rNTPs or dNTPs tested, with a modest preference for ATP and dATP (Supplementary Fig. 7C). This promiscuity was corroborated by single-molecule optical-trap measurements, where CMG also translocated on ssDNA in the presence of alternative nucleotides (e.g., CTP, dCTP, and dGTP) (Supplementary Fig. 7D). Interestingly, CMG required either a low level of ATP to be present (0.1 mM, which did not support activity in itself), or a brief exposure to ATP before other nucleotides could be used to drive DNA unwinding or translocation, respectively. This behaviour suggests that CMG undergoes some type of remodeling or activation step that is ATP-dependent prior to movement, a dependency also noted by Spinks et al.35. Nonetheless, nucleotide promiscuity appears to be a shared feature of cellular replicative helicases.

Discussion

In the present work, we set out to better understand the molecular mechanisms of replicative helicase loading and translocation and the role of nucleotide in supporting these reactions. Using E. coli DnaB and DnaC as a model system and single molecule fluorescence imaging on mechanically tethered ssDNA, we first examined the role of nucleotide hydrolysis. A non-hydrolyzable analog, ATPγS, permits DnaC to load DnaB onto ssDNA, a finding in agreement with prior structural and mutagenesis studies of the protein (Fig. 1E)14,21,26. However, an inability to hydrolyze nucleotide prevents DnaC from dissociating from DnaB, blocks helicase movement, and promotes the dissociation of DnaB from DNA (Fig. 2D). Thus, DnaC requires ATP hydrolysis to both productively place DnaB around ssDNA and to leave once this task has been completed, although the loader can reassociate to slow or even unload DnaB (Fig. 2F, Supplementary Fig. 3I).

In comparison to loading, the translocation of DnaB is readily paused by ATPγS. Interestingly, no rate changes are seen before a pause or after translocation resumes. The pauses themselves appear to reflect a complete or near-complete cessation of movement (Supplementary Fig. 4D); however, helicase processivity appears unaffected by the stuttering progression. Given the low concentrations of ATPγS needed to induce DnaB pausing in the presence of ATP (fewer than 1 molecule in 50), this response suggests that the failure of just a single active site to hydrolyze ATP may be sufficient to halt movement. The sensitivity of DnaB to ATPγS comports with studies of other RecA-family hexameric helicases such as the Rho transcription termination factor and the phage T7 gp4 helicase, which have found that the active sites of these enzymes must all be hydrolysis-competent to support translocation36–38. This high degree of turnover-coupled movement differs from many AAA+ family hexameric translocases such as the ClpX protein unfoldase or the MCM2-7 helicase, which can functionally tolerate one or more inactive subunits12,39.

While conducting the single molecule studies, we were surprised to discover that DnaB is a far more robust translocase than anticipated. Once loaded onto ssDNA (and once DnaC dissociates), DnaB is extremely fast and processive, moving at speeds up to twofold higher than that measured for DNA replication in cells (>3000 nt/s) while virtually never falling off its substrate (Fig. 3A, C). Once moving, DnaB can also resist increases in salt concentration (up to 500 mM NaCl) and DNA stretching forces (up to 20 pN) with little to no effect on translocation speed (Fig. 3D–E). The helicase even proved capable of unwinding 30 or 60 bp DNA duplexes without any apparent slowing (Fig. 4A–C), although encounters with these substrates did appear to lower enzyme processivity, as evidenced by occasional dissociation events localised at the site of the duplex (Fig. 4D–F).

Despite the fast translocation seen here for DnaB, it is highly likely that the helicase moves more slowly when unwinding DNA. Given the sizes of the oligo substrates used in our experiments, this deceleration may be too short lived to be observable on the time scales accessible to the optical trap; efforts to more fully measure how DnaB translocation properties respond to unwinding long (kilobase) stretches of dsDNA, as well as to bypass or displace other nucleic acid structures or roadblocks are ongoing (e.g., blunt-ended DNA or RNA duplexes or DNA-bound proteins). Nonetheless, prior bulk biochemical studies of DnaB had found that the enzyme typically unwinds only tens of bases of DNA over the course of several minutes13,27,40, while a previous single molecule analysis using magnetic tweezers reported that DnaB could move roughly tenfold more quickly while unwinding DNA but also paused frequently, giving rise to slow progression overall28. In single molecule studies of T7 gp4, the helicase was found to be capable of moving along ssDNA on the order of ~300 nt/s but slowed ~1.5–10 fold during unwinding, depending on the applied force20. By way of comparison, the data presented here demonstrate DnaB is a fairly force- and salt-resistant motor that does not require an associated polymerase to move along ssDNA at very high speeds or to quickly unwind short stretches of DNA that are in its path. Both properties are rather unexpected given that moderate ionic strength is typically carefully maintained in most cells (excluding some extremophiles) and that the external tension on DNA in vivo is expected to be relatively low.

What might account for the properties seen here for DnaB as compared to prior studies? We speculate that this is due at least in part to a non-catalytic, N-terminal ‘collar’ domain found in cellular DnaB family enzymes (and in some phage homologs as well). Structural and biochemical studies have shown that the collar acts as a stabilizing scaffold for the DnaB ring13,26,41,42. The collar consists of two helical hairpin domains (each capped with a small globular fold) that come together to form a tight dimer that then associates with two other dimers in the hexamer. The interfaces between dimers in this assembly can rearrange or be selectively split apart in response to binding a nucleic acid substrate or DnaC42–46, but the collar generally appears to play a role in helping to maintain DnaB’s topological encirclement of ssDNA. In bulk solution studies, it has been observed that DnaC stimulates the ability of DnaB to unwind a model DNA fork substrate as compared to measurements conducted with the helicase alone22,25,40. This stimulation has been attributed to the ability of DnaC to remodel the DnaB collar from an inactive closed-ring state into an active cracked-ring form that is also seen when ssDNA is properly bound in the helicase’s translocation pore13,22; unwinding studies that assessed DnaB alone may not have fully accessed this activated state. However, it has also been reported that the N-terminal domain of DnaC stimulates unwinding in bulk measurements more readily than the full-length loader14. The findings here suggest that rate differences with these prior studies are likely in part due to a need for DnaC to be initially present to remodel/activate DnaB, coupled with a requirement that the loader then be removed to prevent DnaB rebinding and helicase inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 3I). The inability to decouple DnaC activities during and subsequent to DnaB loading and activation in bulk solution experiments may explain the increased activity evident in our single molecule data performed under microfluidic control. For its part, T7 gp4 lacks the N-terminal collar, which may account for its distinct rate and processivity properties.

In the optical trap translocation assays, it is notable that DnaC could directly modulate DnaB’s speed (Fig. 2F). At low concentrations, the loader induced a roughly 10× slowing of helicase movement. At higher concentrations, DnaC promoted DnaB stalling and even unloading (Supplementary Fig. 3I). It is likely that DnaB slowing is induced by the isolated binding of just a small number of DnaC molecules (possibly just one or two), whereas the association of more loaders allows for cooperative, nucleotide-mediated inter-DnaC interactions that impede helicase movement and destabilise the DnaB ring to facilitate its dissociation from ssDNA. The effects of DnaC on DnaB, from remodeling the regulatory N-terminal collar to altering helicase speed and processivity, may be of utility in replication restart pathways at sites of fork damage where DnaB has stalled and DnaC is known to act (reviewed in47), although additional studies are necessary to test this idea.

Perhaps the most unexpected finding to come from these studies is the discovery that DnaC and DnaB are not strict ATPases, as has been long believed, but rather can utilise a broad range of ribonucleoside and deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates. For DnaB, consumption of dNTPs is only ~2-fold less efficient than rNTPs (dTTP being an exception) but still enables rapid helicase movement (Fig. 5B–C), whereas DnaC cannot use dCTP, dGTP or dTTP at all (but can use dATP, Supplementary Figs. 2E–H). The chemical promiscuity of DnaB appears to derive from its relatively open active site, which can sterically accommodate a range of pyrimidine and purine bases and is generally agnostic to the presence of a ribose 2′-hydroxyl (Fig. 6B). This accommodating nature is carried over to the function of DnaB within the replisome, which is found to be capable of synthesizing comparable amounts of DNA in vitro regardless of whether ATP is present along with the dNTP substrates necessary to support polymerase activity (Fig. 5E, Supplementary Fig. 5E). Although a recent, non-peer reviewed report had suggested that the E. coli replisome can function without ATP (with DnaB serving as a catalytically inactive plow pushed by DNA polymerase)48, our findings indicate that DnaB can circumvent a need for ATP when dNTPs are available as a motive source, a conclusion reached independently by the Spenkelink, Dixon, and van Oijen groups35. Notably, we find that a relatively open active site and a capacity to use dNTPs for translocating along ssDNA and unwinding dsDNA is also shared with the evolutionarily distinct eukaryotic CMG helicase (Fig. 6C–G). In vivo, ATP is typically present at low millimolar concentrations, whereas cellular dNTP pools are lower, generally present at 50-100 µM49. An approximately twofold higher Km was measured for dTTP than for ATP, even though the maximal translocation speeds are similar under saturating conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5C). Together, these observations suggest that during unperturbed replication, ATP (or other rNTPs) likely serves as the primary fuel for DnaB and CMG. The flex-fuel property of cellular replicative helicases would thus likely be of utility during times when cells have committed to DNA replication but encounter nucleotide pool stress and still need to complete chromosomal synthesis (e.g., depending on the cellular state, ATP concentrations in E. coli can drop below 50 µM50). Future efforts will help clarify when such situations arise and their potential impact on genomic integrity and may highlight new avenues for disrupting replisomal function to selectively impede cell proliferation.

Single molecule force spectroscopy

A LUMICKS C-trap, a commercial optical trap with combined confocal microscopy, was used for all single molecule measurements. The system has a microfluidic setup with five experimental channels, each with a height of 100 µm. Re-usable glass slides provided by the company are extensively passivated before the experiment to create a uniform surface. Passivation was performed sequentially with 0.5 mL of each component in each syringe. First, a 1 mg/mL BSA solution (Millipore Sigma) was prepared by dissolving 50 mg of BSA in 50 mL of nuclease free water (0.1% BSA). A 30 min of passivation cycle with BSA was then run which includes an automated cycle that ran at 1 bar for 5 min, then at 0.4 bar for 25 min. Next, excess and unbound BSA was washed by flushing with ddH2O for 10 min using an automated cycle that ran for 5 min at 1 bar and 5 min at 0.4 bar. Next, to create more uniformly passivated surface, 0.5% w/v or 0.5 mg/mL Pluronic® F-127 (Millipore Sigma) was flushed though the flow cell for 30 min; 250 mg of Pluronic was dissolved in 50 mL of nuclease free water to make a 0.5% or 5 mg/mL stock that was subsequently diluted to 0.05 % or 0.5 mg/mL with nuclease free water. Of note, Pluronic can be heated to 40 °C for ~30 min to dissolve quickly, or should be left to rotate at room temperature overnight. The automated Pluronic passivation cycle was also run for 30 mins using the same parameters as for BSA. Finally, a final 30-min wash was performed with equilibrium buffer, 1× PBS. 10× PBS buffer was purchased from Invitrogen by ThermoFisher Scientific and contains 1.37 M NaCl, 27 mM KCl, 100 mM Na2HPO4, and 18 mM KH2PO4. 1× PBS was made with nuclease free water purchased from ThermoFisher scientific. Buffers were filtered with 0.2 µm filters (Millipore sigma).

After the passivation, a four-channel experiment was generally carried out. Streptavidin-coated polystyrene particle beads of average size 4.34 µm [0.5% w/v] (Spherotech Inc.) were diluted 1:250 in 1X PBS. 50-100 ng of λ DNA (see method below) was added to 0.5 mL of 1× PBS. DNA was captured between two streptavidin beads and mechanically denatured by moving one bead to the overstretched region to create ssDNA. ssDNA generation was confirmed by fitting the force-distance (FD) curve to the Freely Jointed Chain model (FJC) (contour length 48.5 kbp/27.160 µm; persistence length 0.9 nm; stretch modulus 1000 pN) in real-time. The experimental buffer used was 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 mM MgCl2, and ATP (ATP concentration was varied for ATP-dependent experiments). The reaction buffer was prepared by adding oxygen scavenging condition that contained 0.8% (w/v) dextrose (Millipore Sigma), 165 U/mL glucose oxidase (Millipore Sigma), 2170 U/mL catalase (Millipore Sigma), and 1–3 mM Trolox (Millipore Sigma) in experimental buffer to increase the fluorescence lifetime of the fluorophores. Channels 3, 4, and 5 contained reaction buffer. Imaging settings were 0.1–0.5 ms exposure time (per pixel), with a constant pixel size of 100 nm. Excitation wavelengths were Blue (488 nm) green (561 nm) and red (638 nm). Laser power was selected to maintain 5 μW power at the objective. A general experimental setup contained 3%–5% of red laser power and 20%–30% of green laser power unless otherwise specified.

After an experimental session was over, a thorough cleaning of the flow cell was performed to make the flow cell reusable. First, 10 min of cleaning with ddH2O was performed to rinse out free nucleic acids and proteins. Next, 2.5% bleach was used to clean the passivation agents. Bleach was purchased from Hill & Markes that contains 7.5% sodium hypochlorite as an active bleach, amongst other reagents. This is considered 100% and diluted to 2.5%. Next, the cell was washed with water for 10 min. Next, a 30 min neutralisation cycle was performed with 1x sodium thiosulfate solution (Millipore Sigma; 10× sodium thiosulfate corresponds to 100 mM sodium thiosulfate in nuclease free water). A final cleaning step was performed using the nuclease free water for 30 min. Syringes are left in nuclease free water until the subsequent experiment, which would start with passivation as stated above.

Lambda

(λ) DNA preparation

λ DNA (48.5 kb with 12-nucleotide 5′ overhangs) was purchased from NEB. A series of annealing and ligation steps were performed to generate a construct with biotin modifications on the same strand, as previously described52. In brief, three oligonucleotides—Oligo 1, Oligo 2 and Oligo 3 (Supplementary Table 2) were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies Inc. (IDT). Oligo 1 and Oligo 3 were then phosphorylated to prepare them for ligation. Phosphorylation was performed using T4 PNK (NEB) at 37 °C for 1 h. Next, Oligo 1 and Oligo 3 were annealed to the 5′ overhangs by heating to 65 °C, followed by slow cooling. Immediately after annealing, ligation was performed using T4 DNA ligase (NEB) at room temperature for 20 min, resulting in biotinylation of one 3′ end of the DNA. Subsequently, Oligo 2 was annealed to the previously ligated product by heating to 45 °C, followed by slow cooling. Another ligation step was performed at room temperature for 20 min. To remove excess primers and proteins, drop dialysis was performed using 0.025 µm filters (Millipore Sigma). A membrane was carefully suspended over ~20–25 mL of dialysis buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 0.1 mM EDTA) in a Petri dish. A drop of DNA (100–150 µL) was placed onto the membrane using a large-orifice pipette tip, ensuring that the final volume remained within this range to prevent the membrane from dipping into the buffer. Dialysis was carried out at room temperature for at least 1.5 h. Afterward, the solution was carefully collected using large-orifice pipette tips. DNA can be stored at 4 °C; however, based on experience, the DNA constructs remain optimal for ssDNA generation for up to two weeks before nicking becomes evident (if only dsDNA is required, the construct remains stable for 2–3 months). For ssDNA generation, trapped dsDNA was moved to channel 3 (reaction buffer containing 100 mM NaCl and 10 mM MgCl₂) and stretched to around the 65–75 pN transition by periodically relaxing and extending the tether by 1–2 µm to reduce the chance of snapping until the non-biotinylated strand was released from the bound strand based on a force-extension curve. Thereafter, ssDNA tethers were held at 5 pN unless otherwise stated. Supplementary Table 2 lists all oligonucleotides used for generating biotinylated lambda DNA and for DNA unwinding assays.

Data analysis

All single molecule data were analysed using custom scripts and Lumicks Pylake python package. Analysis scripts have been deposited in GitHub (https://github.com/spangeni/DnaB-project). In brief, translocation traces were tracked using kymowidgetgreedy function in pylake details of which can be found here (https://lumicks-pylake.readthedocs.io/en/stable/). Pixel threshold and track width was maintained at 3 and 2 respectively. After the tracks were obtained, they were fitted with linear regression to find the slope of the tracks which is the translocation rate of the molecules along the DNA. Plots were generated using Python, Origin Pro 2024b version and GraphPad Prism.

Reconstitution of GsDnaB6-ssDNA-dTDP/AlFx complex

To reconstitute the bacterial DnaB complex with ssDNA and a deoxythymidine nucleotide for cryoEM, GsDnaB6 (2 µM) was mixed with 37nt ssDNA (0.5 µM) (/5deSBioTEG/GACTGAACATTTGGTACATAGCTGTCTCTTGATCAGA) and TDP (5 mM) in reconstitution buffer (30 mM HEPES (pH = 7.5), 12.5 mM Mg(OAc)2, 0.5 mM TCEP, 0.5 mM spermine) and allowed to sit at room temperature for ~1 h (all concentrations represent final concentrations, DNA oligos were ordered from IDT with desthiobiotin at the 5′ end). A stock NaF/AlCl3 solution was then added to the reaction for a final concentration of 5 mM NaF and 1 mM AlCl3 and allowed to sit at room temperature for ~1 h. Next, 150 µL High-Capacity streptavidin agarose resin (Thermo Scientific) was added into the reaction, gently mixed and left to slowly rotate overnight at 4 °C. The following morning, the reaction was centrifuged at 500 × g and the supernatant aspirated with a pipette. The resin was then washed three times with reconstitution buffer containing 2.25 mM dTDP/AlFX, after which 800 µL of reaction buffer containing 1.75 mM dTDP/AlFx and 25 mM biotin was added, and the reaction was left to slowly rotate overnight at 4 °C to elute the complex. The reaction was then centrifuged at 5000 × g and the supernatant recovered by aspiration with a pipette. The supernatant was concentrated to ~75 µL using 30 kDa cutoff Millipore concentrator (Amicon). The concentration of GsDnaB6-ssDNA-dTDP/AlFx complex after these steps was typically ~3 µM.

Bulk unwinding assay

Forked DNA substrates were prepared by annealing a Cy3-labeled strand (20 nM; Cy3-TACGTAACGAGCCTGC(dT)25) with a 1.2-fold molar excess of a complementary quencher strand ((dT)25-GCAGGCTCGTTACGTA-BHQ2) in annealing buffer (100 mM potassium acetate, 30 mM HEPES pH 7.5). Annealing was performed by heating and gradual cooling to room temperature. For DnaB unwinding reactions, helicase and loader were premixed on ice, after which the fork substrate was added. The mixture was then pre-warmed to 37 °C. In parallel, the ssDNA capture oligonucleotide was mixed with the indicated rNTP/dNTP and pre-warmed to 37 °C. Reactions were initiated by adding the pre-warmed nucleotide/capture-strand mixture to the pre-warmed helicase reaction, mixing gently by pipetting, and immediately dispensing three 20 µL technical replicates into a 384-well black plate. Fluorescence was monitored at 37 °C on a CLARIOstar microplate reader (BMG Labtech) as an increase in Cy3 signal over time resulting from strand separation and capture. Unless otherwise indicated, the final reaction conditions were: 200 nM DnaB hexamer (± loader monomer), 10 nM fork substrate, 20 nM capture strand, and 2 mM rNTP/dNTP in assay buffer containing 20 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 5 mM magnesium acetate, 150 mM potassium glutamate, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.2 mg/mL BSA.

For CMG-based unwinding assays, CMG (50 nM) and Mcm10 (100 nM) along with with 10 nM fork substrate were preincubated in 50 mM HEPES-KOH (pH 7.5), 5 mM magnesium acetate, 200 mM potassium glutamate, 5% (v/v) glycerol, and 0.2 mg/mL BSA at 37 °C in the presence of 0.1 mM ATP. Reactions were initiated by the addition of 2 mM of the indicated nucleotide(s) and monitored as described for DnaB.

CryoEM sample preparation

For making cryoEM grids, 3 µL of the reconstituted DnaB/ssDNA/dTDP•AlFx complex was applied to C-flat holey carbon 1.2/1.3 Cu grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) pretreated with a PELCO easiGlow Glow Discharge Cleaning System (15 mA, 40 s, negative polarity). The sample was then blotted manually using filter paper (Whatman). Manual application followed by manual blotting was repeated two more times, after which sample was applied one more time but then blotted using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) at 8 °C and 100% humidity (blot force 5 and blot time 4 s) before plunge freezing into cryocooled liquid ethane.

CryoEM data acquisition

Grids were imaged on a G3i Titan Krios microscope (Thermo Fisher) at 300 kV and equipped with a Falcon 4i detector and Selectris Energy Filter operating in nanoprobe and EF-TEM (Energy Filtered Transmission Electron Microscopy) mode (Johns Hopkins University Beckman Center for CryoEM). Raw movies in EER format were collected at 130,000x magnification in counting mode with a pixel size of 0.97 Å/pixel. Total dose was 40 e − / Å2. Data were collected in fast mode using EPU. A total of 10,634 movies were collected for the GsDnaB6-ssDNA-dTDP/AlFx sample.

CryoEM data processing

All data processing was performed using CryoSPARC (v.4.0). Movies (EER format) were imported into CryoSPARC, and patch motion correction and patch CTF estimation were used to preprocess the data. Particles were picked by Blob Picking and extracted with a box size of 360 pixels and Fourier cropped to 120 pixels. After several rounds of 2D classification, 521,705 particles were selected for 3D heterogeneous refinement. Particles of the best class in the heterogeneous refinement were extracted again with a box size of 360 pixels. 154,856 remaining particles were further refined by homogeneous refinement, nonuniform refinement and local refinement to generate a final reconstructed map at 3.48 Å resolution. Most of the density for the NTD of DnaB was missing in the final reconstruction, likely due to dynamic motion of the region. EMReady46 was used to postprocess the map to reveal additional detail and aid with model building.

Model building

A crystal structure of the GsDnaB hexamer in complex with GDP-AlFx and ssDNA (PDB Code: 4ESV42,) was used as an initial model for building. The NTD region of the model was removed due to a lack of interpretable density, after which nucleotide GDP was replaced with dTDP. The model was built into the cryoEM map using ISOLDE (v1.4b2)53 in UCSF ChimeraX (v1.4). Then the model was inspected and revised in Coot (v.0.9.6)54. A final round of real-space refinement of the model against the map was performed in Phenix (v.1.20.1)55. Information for all deposited maps and models in this work is summarised in Supplementary Table 1.

Rolling circle assay

A 1.5 kb plasmid was used to generate a Tailed Form II (TFII) DNA substrate containing a 40-nt 5′ single-stranded overhang with a biotin modification at its 5′ end. Briefly, a 60-nt single-stranded DNA fragment was excised from the plasmid using the nicking endonuclease Nt.BbvCI. A synthetic 60-nt oligonucleotide—bearing a 5′ biotin, a 30-nt poly(T) sequence, and a 30-nt region complementary to the excised plasmid sequence—was then annealed to the plasmid and ligated using T4 DNA ligase (NEB). The plasmid was then purified using Micro-spin S-400 columns (Cytiva). The final DNA substrate contained a 30-nt single-stranded gap at the replication fork on the leading-strand template arm. The substrate (a 3 nM concentration) was mixed with and immobilized on 10 µL of magnetic streptavidin beads (Dynabeads™ MyOne™ Streptavidin T1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in 30 µL of TE-500 buffer. The beads were shaken at 1200 RPM for 1 h, followed by extensive washing with replication buffer (50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10 mM Mg(OAc)₂, 50 mM KOAc, 4 mM DTT) using a magnetic bead separator and manual pipetting. Next, 360 nM DnaB, 360 nM DnaC, 100 nM Pol III* holoenzyme, and 200 nM β-clamp were added in 30 µL of replication buffer containing ATP. The beads were incubated at 37 °C for 10 min with shaking at 1000 RPM to prevent beads from settling. After 10 min, excess proteins and ATP were removed by washing the beads with replication buffer using a magnetic bead separator. To initiate replication, 30 µL of 50 nM SSB and dNTPs—alone or in combination with ATP—were added (dNTP concentrations were varied between 0 and 4 mM) in replication buffer. The reaction was incubated for 30 min (or the indicated time), after which the DNA products were heat denatured and supernatant containing DNA was separated from beads using a magnetic bead separator. DNA products were analysed on 1% denaturing agarose gels run at 23 V for 16 h in denaturing running buffer (50 mM NaOH and 2 mM EDTA). DNA synthesis was visualized by staining gels with SYBR Gold (vendor) before imaging on GelDoc Imaging system (Bio-Rad).
