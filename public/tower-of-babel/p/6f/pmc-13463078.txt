Microfluidic sperm selection versus density gradient centrifugation in ICSI cycles with abnormal semen parameters: a randomized sibling-oocyte study
Authors: Chatsaran Thanapongpibul, Pornsri Niransuk, Supitcha Sassanarakkit, Nitchanan Suksawat, Chonthicha Satirapod
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microfluidic sperm selection (MSS) is a centrifugation-free alternative to density gradient centrifugation (DGC) for sperm preparation in intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI). This prospective, randomized sibling-oocyte study included 55 ICSI cycles from 55 couples treated between February 2025 and February 2026. Eligible couples had abnormal semen parameters according to World Health Organization 2021 criteria. Within each cycle, sibling metaphase II oocytes were randomly allocated to ICSI using sperm prepared by MSS or DGC. The primary outcome was blastocyst formation per two-pronuclear embryo (2PN). Secondary outcomes included fertilization, cleavage, good-quality blastocyst rate, and euploidy rate. Embryo-level outcomes were analyzed using a mixed-effects binomial regression model. MSS was associated with a higher fertilization rate than DGC (82.3% vs. 74.7%; OR 1.60, 95% CI 1.07–2.39; p = 0.022). Cleavage, blastocyst formation (61.8% vs. 65.2%; p = 0.494), and good-quality blastocyst rates were similar between groups. Among 47 cycles undergoing preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy, euploidy rates per biopsied blastocyst did not differ significantly between the MSS and DGC groups. Overall, MSS was associated with higher fertilization, while subsequent embryologic outcomes were comparable between groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55209-1.

Introduction

Infertility, defined as the failure to achieve pregnancy after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse, affects approximately 15% of reproductive-age couples worldwide1. Male factor infertility contributes to approximately 40–50% of infertility cases and remains a major determinant of assisted reproductive technology (ART) outcomes2. The evaluation of male infertility typically includes a detailed reproductive history and at least one semen analysis according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, which assesses sperm concentration, motility, and morphology3,4. However, conventional semen analysis does not directly assess sperm DNA integrity, which may play an important role in determining embryo developmental potential4.

Sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) is one of the most extensively studied indicators of sperm genomic integrity. SDF refers to single- and double-strand DNA breaks that may arise during spermatogenesis or post-testicular maturation, often due to defective apoptosis, impaired DNA repair, or oxidative stress. Although oocytes possess intrinsic DNA repair mechanisms, excessive sperm DNA damage may exceed this repair capacity and negatively affect embryonic development5. Because major embryonic genome activation (EGA) in humans occurs between the 4- and 8-cell stages, the effects of paternal genomic defects may become more apparent during subsequent preimplantation development6,7. Consistent with this concept, a large systematic review and meta-analysis reported that elevated SDF is associated with reduced implantation and pregnancy rates, and multiple studies have demonstrated adverse associations between increased SDF and laboratory outcomes, including embryo quality and blastocyst formation8.

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) has largely replaced conventional in vitro fertilization for the treatment of male factor infertility because it can overcome several sperm-related barriers to fertilization9. However, because ICSI bypasses physiological sperm selection, laboratory sperm preparation becomes an important determinant of which spermatozoa are ultimately selected for injection. Density gradient centrifugation (DGC) remains one of the most widely used sperm preparation techniques in ART laboratories, enriching motile sperm while removing debris and non-viable cells10. Nevertheless, centrifugation during sperm processing may increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and oxidative stress, potentially contributing to sperm DNA damage11. To address these limitations, Microfluidic sperm selection (MSS) has emerged as an alternative sperm preparation technique that avoids centrifugation. By allowing motile sperm to migrate through microscale channels, microfluidic systems aim to reduce mechanical stress and may enrich sperm populations with more favorable functional and genomic characteristics12,13. From an operational perspective, MSS may also offer a simpler and more standardized workflow by eliminating multi-step gradient preparation, repeated centrifugation, and extensive sample handling12,13. Meta-analyses suggest that MSS may reduce SDF and improve reproductive outcomes compared with conventional methods. However, heterogeneity across studies remains substantial14,15.

Importantly, abnormal semen parameters including oligozoospermia, asthenozoospermia, and teratozoospermia have been consistently associated with higher SDF levels16. Consequently, men presenting with these abnormal parameters have a higher baseline prevalence of SDF and may particularly benefit from sperm preparation techniques that better preserve genomic integrity16. However, evidence directly comparing MSS with DGC in this specific population remains limited and inconsistent17,18. In this prospective, randomized sibling-oocyte study, we compared embryologic outcomes after MSS and DGC sperm preparation in couples undergoing ICSI with abnormal semen parameters. We hypothesized that MSS may affect sperm selection, thereby influencing fertilization and downstream embryologic outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, oocyte-level randomized sibling-oocyte study was conducted at the Center of Excellence in Assisted Reproductive Technology, Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University. Infertile couples undergoing ICSI between February 2025 and February 2026 were recruited and assessed for eligibility.

Eligible participants were infertile couples undergoing ICSI in whom the male partner had abnormal semen parameters according to the WHO 2021 (6th edition) criteria, based on the most recent diagnostic semen analysis performed before the treatment cycle. Abnormal semen parameters were defined as values below the WHO 2021 lower reference limits for sperm concentration, total or progressive motility, and/or morphology. Additional inclusion criteria were female age ≤ 42 years and the presence of at least four dominant follicles on the trigger day. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) retrieval of fewer than four metaphase II (MII) oocytes; (2) semen volume < 1.4 mL on the day of oocyte retrieval, or (3) pre-preparation total motile sperm count (TMSC) < 5 million. Semen volume < 1.4 mL and pre-preparation TMSC < 5 million were used as exclusion criteria because the sibling-oocyte design required the same semen specimen to be divided into two aliquots for parallel MSS and DGC processing, followed by ICSI. These thresholds were therefore intended to ensure sufficient sample volume and motile sperm recovery for both preparation methods.

The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine Ramathibodi Hospital, Mahidol University (Approval No. MURA2025/109). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study was registered at the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (TCTR20250130004, registered on 30 January 2025).

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of blastocyst formation rate. Based on Guler et al.17, the mean blastulation rates per cycle were assumed to be 0.59 for the MSS group and 0.47 for the DGC group, corresponding to a mean paired difference of 0.12 and an assumed within-pair standard deviation of 0.30. Although Guler et al. reported Day 5 blastocyst development, the present study defined blastocyst formation as development by Day 5 or Day 6, consistent with routine blastocyst assessment in our laboratory. Because no prior sibling-oocyte study using the same Day 5 or Day 6 endpoint definition was available, these estimates were used as the closest available pragmatic planning assumptions. Using a two-sided paired t-test with a significance level of α = 0.05 and 80% statistical power, the required sample size was calculated to be 52 paired cycles using Stata version 18 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). To account for an anticipated 5% data loss or cycle cancellation, the final target sample size was set at 55 paired ICSI cycles.

Ovarian stimulation and oocyte retrieval

Controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) was performed using individualized protocols at Ramathibodi Hospital, including gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist, GnRH agonist, or progestin-primed ovarian stimulation (PPOS) regimens. Follicular development was supported with recombinant follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) or highly purified human menopausal gonadotropin (hMG), with doses adjusted according to ovarian response monitored by transvaginal ultrasonography (TVUS).

Final oocyte maturation was triggered when at least three leading follicles reached ≥ 17 mm in diameter, using human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), a GnRH agonist, or a dual trigger. Oocyte retrieval was performed 34–36 h after triggering under TVUS-guided follicular aspiration.

Retrieved cumulus–oocyte complexes (COCs) were collected in FertiCult® Flushing medium (FertiPro, Beernem, Belgium) and transported to the embryology laboratory. Approximately 2 h after retrieval, oocytes were denuded enzymatically and mechanically to assess nuclear maturity. Only MII oocytes, identified by the presence of the first polar body, were included for subsequent randomization.

Oocyte allocation and randomization

After denudation and confirmation of nuclear maturity, all eligible MII oocytes from each treatment cycle were pooled before allocation. Only maturity status was assessed to identify eligible MII oocytes; no detailed morphological assessment was performed before allocation. A cycle-specific random allocation sequence was then generated to assign MII oocytes in a 1:1 ratio to either the MSS or DGC group. Oocytes were assigned sequentially according to the generated allocation sequence, without selection based on oocyte morphology. The assigned sperm preparation method was applied subsequently during ICSI.

In cycles with an odd number of MII oocytes, the final unpaired oocyte was assigned according to a prespecified alternating rule between treatment groups to maintain near-equal allocation across cycles.

Because randomization occurred after confirmation of maturity and required immediate implementation within the embryology laboratory workflow, allocation concealment was not performed. However, pooling all eligible MII oocytes before allocation and using a cycle-specific random allocation sequence without morphology-based selection were intended to minimize subjective assignment and potential allocation bias.

Semen collection and preparation

Semen samples were obtained by masturbation on the day of oocyte retrieval after 2–7 days of sexual abstinence. After liquefaction at 37 °C for 20–30 min, sperm concentration and motility were assessed according to the WHO 2021 recommendations using a Makler counting chamber. Following the initial assessment, each sample was divided into two portions: an 850 µL aliquot was allocated for MSS, while the remaining volume was processed using DGC. Detailed sperm morphology was not systematically reassessed on the day of oocyte retrieval or after MSS or DGC preparation because formal morphology assessment requires fixation and staining of a sperm aliquot, which would reduce the number of viable spermatozoa available for ICSI. During ICSI, spermatozoa were selected according to routine laboratory practice based on motility and gross morphology under the ICSI microscope.

Microfluidic sperm selection

For the MSS group, an 850 µL aliquot of liquefied semen was loaded into the inlet port of a sperm separation device (ZyMōt® Multi, 850 µL; ZyMōt Fertility Inc., USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The outlet port was first primed with approximately 50 µL of sperm washing medium (FertiCult®; FertiPro, Beernem, Belgium) until the medium reached the separation membrane. Subsequently, an additional 700 µL of washing medium was gently added to cover the membrane surface.

The device was incubated horizontally at 37 °C for 30 min, allowing motile spermatozoa to migrate through the membrane into the upper collection chamber. After incubation, the separated motile fraction (up to 500 µL) was gently aspirated and used for ICSI.

Density gradient centrifugation

For the DGC group, semen samples were processed using a two-layer density gradient consisting of 1 mL of 45% and 1 mL of 90% Sil-Select Plus™ density gradient medium (FertiPro, Beernem, Belgium). Samples were centrifuged at 1250 rpm (~ 140×g; rotor radius 8 cm) for 10 min. The resulting sperm pellet was washed twice with FertiCult® medium (FertiPro, Beernem, Belgium) by centrifugation at 1000 rpm (~ 89×g; rotor radius 8 cm) for 6 min per wash. The final 0.4 mL sperm suspension was subsequently used for ICSI.

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection and embryo culture

ICSI was performed on all MII oocytes using spermatozoa prepared according to the randomized allocation.

Due to the distinct technical nature of the sperm preparation methods, embryologists were not blinded to the treatment allocation during the ICSI procedure. However, to minimize inter-operator variability, all oocytes within a single treatment cycle were injected by the same embryologist.

Following ICSI, oocytes were cultured in Global® Total® LP medium (CooperSurgical, Inc., Trumbull, CT, USA) under mineral oil in a conventional incubator maintained at 37 °C with 6% CO₂ and 5% O₂.

Fertilization was assessed 16–18 h post-injection by the presence of two pronuclei (2PN).

Embryo assessment

Embryo development was evaluated on Day 3 for cleavage-stage embryos and on Days 5–6 for blastocyst development. Blastocysts were graded according to the Gardner and Schoolcraft grading system, based on the degree of blastocyst expansion and the quality of the inner cell mass (ICM) and trophectoderm (TE).

Good-quality blastocysts were defined as those graded ≥3BB on Day 5 or Day 6.

Embryo assessment was performed within the routine laboratory workflow, and formal blinding of embryo assessors to the sperm preparation method was not implemented.

Embryo biopsy and preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy

For cycles undergoing preimplantation genetic testing for aneuploidy (PGT-A), TE biopsy was performed on Day 5 or Day 6 blastocysts. Genetic analysis was performed using next-generation sequencing (NGS) to assess the copy number status of all 24 chromosomes.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was the blastocyst formation rate, defined as the proportion of 2PN that developed into blastocysts on Day 5 or Day 6.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study population were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on the data distribution. In contrast, categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages.

Sperm parameters obtained after MSS and DGC were compared using paired statistical tests, as both preparation methods were applied to sibling samples derived from the same semen specimen. Continuous variables were compared using either the paired t-test or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, depending on the data distribution.

Embryo developmental outcomes were analyzed using generalized linear mixed models (GLMM) with binomial distribution and a logit link function. Outcomes were modeled as binomial trials, with the number of events (e.g., 2PN, blastocysts, good-quality blastocysts, or euploid embryos) specified relative to their corresponding denominators (e.g., injected oocytes, 2PN, or biopsied blastocysts). To account for the paired sibling-oocyte design and clustering of embryos originating from the same cycle, cycle identification was included as a random intercept. Results from the regression analyses were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The sample size calculation was performed at the cycle level because each treatment cycle represented one paired experimental unit in the sibling-oocyte design. The calculation was based on the best available cycle-level blastocyst proportion data from a prior sibling-oocyte study, as prior estimates required for embryo-level mixed-effects sample size calculation, such as within-cycle correlation, random-effect variance, and cluster size distribution, were not available. Therefore, the calculated sample size represents the planned number of paired treatment cycles to be enrolled, rather than an embryo-level sample size based on statistically independent embryos.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted at the cycle level to evaluate the robustness of embryo-level findings. For each cycle, outcome proportions were calculated separately for each sperm preparation method and compared using paired statistical tests (paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test) depending on data distribution. These analyses were intended to assess whether the conclusions from the embryo-level GLMMs were consistent when each treatment cycle was treated as the unit of analysis. An additional sensitivity analysis of fertilization was performed by excluding degenerated oocytes from the denominator and modeling 2PN formation per non-degenerated injected MII oocyte using the same mixed-effects binomial approach as the primary fertilization analysis.

A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 18. No missing data were observed for the primary outcome.

Results

Seventeen cycles were excluded based on the predefined eligibility criteria: fewer than four metaphase II (MII) oocytes retrieved (n = 5), semen volume < 1.4 mL on the day of oocyte retrieval (n = 6), and pre-preparation total motile sperm count (TMSC) < 5 million (n = 6). A total of 55 ICSI cycles from 55 couples were included in the final analysis. The mean female age was 37.5 ± 3.3 years, and the median duration of infertility was 3 years (IQR 2–4). Primary infertility was present in 65.5% of couples, and the median antral follicle count (AFC) was 10 (IQR 8–14). The majority of male partners exhibited abnormal semen parameters, with teratozoospermia being the most prevalent finding (80.0%).

A GnRH antagonist protocol was used in nearly all cycles (98.2%), with a mean stimulation duration of 9.5 ± 1.3 days and a mean total gonadotropin dose of 2461 ± 841 IU. A median of 13 (IQR 9–16) oocytes were retrieved per cycle, of which 9 (IQR 7–13) were mature (MII).

Within each cycle, sibling mature oocytes were randomly allocated to fertilization using sperm prepared by MSS or DGC. PGT-A was performed in 47 cycles (85.5%).

The fertilization rate (2PN/MII) was higher in the MSS group than in the DGC group (82.3% vs. 74.7%). Subsequent embryo developmental outcomes were similar between groups. Cleavage rates did not differ significantly between the MSS and DGC groups (97.6% vs. 96.9%, p = 0.651), and blastocyst formation rates were also comparable (61.8% vs. 65.2%, p = 0.494).

Similarly, the good-quality blastocyst rate per 2PN was 31.3% in the MSS group and 34.4% in the DGC group (p = 0.56). When calculated per blastocyst, the good-quality blastocyst rate was also comparable between groups (50.7% vs. 52.7%, p = 0.93).

Sensitivity analysis

Cycle-level sensitivity analyses restricted to complete paired observations are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, the cycle-level analyses were consistent with the findings of the primary embryo-level models for the main embryologic outcomes. The fertilization rate was higher in the MSS group than in the DGC group (median 83.3% vs. 75.0%, p = 0.015), whereas cleavage rate, blastocyst formation rate, and good-quality blastocyst outcomes did not differ significantly between groups.

For euploidy outcomes in the PGT-A subgroup, statistically significant differences were observed in the cycle-level sensitivity analyses when calculated per biopsied blastocyst (median 33.3% vs. 0%, p = 0.009) and per fertilized embryo (median 20.0% vs. 0%, p = 0.035). However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as the primary embryo-level mixed-effects analyses did not demonstrate statistically significant differences in euploidy outcomes.

An additional sensitivity analysis was performed to assess whether the higher fertilization rate observed with MSS remained after excluding degenerated oocytes from the denominator. The fertilization rate per non-degenerated injected MII oocyte remained higher in the MSS group than in the DGC group (92.1% vs. 86.5%), and this association remained statistically significant in the mixed-effects model (OR 1.85, 95% CI 1.04–3.29, p = 0.037; Supplementary Table S3).

Adverse events

No adverse events related to the interventions were observed during the study.

Discussion

This randomized sibling-oocyte study evaluated whether MSS improves fertilization and embryo development compared with DGC in ICSI cycles involving men with abnormal semen parameters. MSS was associated with a significantly higher fertilization rate, whereas subsequent embryologic outcomes, including cleavage, the primary outcome of blastocyst formation, and blastocyst quality, did not differ significantly between the two methods. Among cycles undergoing PGT-A, euploidy rates did not differ significantly between the MSS and DGC groups in the primary mixed-effects analysis. Overall, these findings indicate that MSS was associated with higher fertilization, without evidence of a significant difference in downstream embryologic outcomes.

Mechanistically, the significantly higher fertilization rate observed with MSS in our study may be attributed to its potential to enrich for sperm with better functional integrity and greater oocyte activation capacity. Phospholipase C zeta (PLCζ) is a sperm-specific cytosolic protein introduced into the oocyte at fertilization, where it initiates oocyte activation by triggering intracellular calcium (Ca²⁺) release from endoplasmic reticulum stores19. Since this process is required for normal fertilization, defects in sperm factors involved in oocyte activation may compromise fertilization potential19. In ICSI candidates, sperm DNA fragmentation has been shown to correlate negatively with the proportion of PLCζ-positive sperm, whereas the proportion of PLCζ-positive sperm correlates positively with fertilization rate20. This suggests a potential link between impaired sperm genomic integrity and reduced oocyte activation capacity20. MSS has been reported to reduce SDF relative to conventional sperm preparation methods such as DGC or swim-up, and a recent systematic review and meta-analysis confirmed a significant reduction in SDF with MSS14. Taken together, these findings support the hypothesis that MSS may improve fertilization by selecting sperm with better genomic and functional integrity, thereby supporting PLCζ-related oocyte activation.

Despite a higher fertilization rate with MSS, subsequent embryo developmental outcomes, including cleavage, blastocyst formation, and good-quality blastocyst rates, were comparable between MSS and DGC. This pattern may reflect a balance between the extent of paternal DNA injury (including SDF) and the oocyte’s repair capacity, given that mature spermatozoa are largely devoid of functional DNA repair machinery and therefore depend on oocyte-mediated repair after fertilization, which is reactivated post-fertilization through multiple repair pathways5,21. In this context, our use of a low-g, short-duration centrifugation approach for DGC may also have minimized processing-related oxidative stress, as both higher g-force and prolonged centrifugation duration can increase ROS generation in semen22. This may have limited any processing-induced DNA damage that the oocyte would need to repair. However, because PLCζ, SDF, and ROS were not directly measured in our study, these proposed mechanistic interpretations remain inferential.

Among studies most comparable to our abnormal-semen-parameter population, Guler et al.17evaluated a sibling-oocyte cohort of patients with astheno-teratozoospermia and found similar fertilization and Day 3 embryo outcomes between groups. At Day 5, MSS was associated with a higher proportion of excellent blastocysts, while total blastocyst development showed only a borderline difference17. In the present study, these blastocyst-stage differences were not observed. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. Compared with Guler et al., our study included a larger sample and a broader population with abnormal semen parameters rather than a narrowly defined astheno-teratozoospermia subgroup, which may have affected the magnitude and pattern of observed treatment effects. In addition, Guler et al. used a more intensive centrifugation protocol, with higher g-force and longer centrifugation duration than in our study17. Given that prolonged centrifugation and, under longer exposure, higher centrifugal force have been associated with greater ROS generation in human semen, differences in DGC processing intensity may have contributed to the more apparent downstream differences observed in their study22. Differences in statistical approach may also have contributed, as our analyses more explicitly accounted for the sibling-oocyte design and clustering of embryos within cycles.

Comparable morphology-based outcomes do not necessarily exclude differences in chromosomal outcomes; however, the present study does not provide conclusive evidence that MSS improves euploidy. In the PGT-A subgroup, euploidy rates did not differ significantly between MSS and DGC in the primary mixed-effects analysis (OR 1.88, 95% CI 0.98–3.58; p = 0.056). Although cycle-level sensitivity analyses showed significant results for euploidy outcomes, these findings were inconsistent with the primary embryo-level analysis and should be considered exploratory, particularly given the limited PGT-A subgroup and the presence of many zero-event cycles. Therefore, the euploidy findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of a treatment effect.

Our findings are consistent with those of Godiwala et al., who conducted a prospective randomized sibling-oocyte trial comparing microfluidic sperm processing with DGC in a general infertility population undergoing PGT-A. They reported no statistically significant difference in blastocyst euploidy rates per biopsied embryo between the microfluidics and DGC groups (53.0% vs. 45.7%)23. Taken together, these findings suggest that the effect of MSS on embryo euploidy remains unclear.

Across the broader literature, the apparent benefit of MSS may depend on patient selection. In relatively unselected infertility populations, downstream embryologic or chromosomal advantages have not been clearly demonstrated. Quinn et al. reported comparable cleavage-stage and blastocyst-stage embryo quality between microfluidic sperm preparation and DGC in a pragmatic randomized controlled trial that did not specifically target male-factor infertility or elevated SDF24. A similar lack of clear chromosomal benefit was observed in the general PGT-A population studied by Godiwala et al.23.

In contrast, studies conducted in more selected populations have reported signals of potential benefit. Guler et al. observed a higher proportion of excellent-quality Day 5 blastocysts with MSS in patients with astheno-teratozoospermia17. Kocur et al. reported higher euploidy rates in subsequent MSS cycles among couples selected for prior high embryo aneuploidy and elevated sperm chromatin fragmentation, although the historical comparison design limits causal inference18. In the present study, which focused on men with abnormal semen parameters, MSS was associated with a higher fertilization rate, although downstream embryo developmental and euploidy outcomes did not differ significantly in the primary analyses. Taken together, these findings suggest that the potential benefits of MSS may be more apparent in selected populations with underlying male-factor or sperm genomic concerns, but this hypothesis requires confirmation in well-designed prospective randomized studies.

From a laboratory workflow perspective, MSS may be simpler because it avoids gradient preparation, centrifugation, pellet recovery, and repeated washing steps required for DGC. In our laboratory workflow, MSS required approximately 35–40 min, including device loading, incubation, and sperm collection, whereas DGC required approximately 45–60 min, including gradient preparation, centrifugation, pellet recovery, and two washing steps. However, the actual time saving may vary across laboratories depending on local protocols, sample characteristics, and staffing workflows.

This study has several strengths. The randomized sibling-oocyte design minimizes confounding from female factors and cycle-level laboratory conditions, enabling a within-cycle comparison of MSS versus DGC under highly controlled circumstances. In addition, the use of mixed-effects modeling appropriately accounted for clustering at the cycle level, which is critical when analyzing embryo-level outcomes in a sibling-oocyte design.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, mechanistic sperm measures, including SDF, oxidative stress/ROS markers, and PLCζ expression, were not assessed on the day of oocyte retrieval, limiting causal inference regarding the biological pathway underlying the higher fertilization rate observed with MSS. These mechanistic endpoints were not incorporated because the study was designed primarily to compare clinical embryology outcomes between MSS and DGC within a sibling-oocyte model, rather than to evaluate biological mechanisms. Further prospective studies incorporating these mechanistic endpoints are needed to clarify whether improvements in sperm genomic integrity, oxidative status, or oocyte activation capacity mediate the observed fertilization difference. Second, our cohort was predominantly enriched for teratozoospermia, which may limit generalizability to other abnormal semen phenotypes (e.g., primarily oligozoospermia or asthenozoospermia) and may partially explain heterogeneity when comparing our findings with prior studies that enrolled broader or different male-factor profiles. Finally, euploidy was a secondary outcome, and the study was not powered to detect differences in euploidy within the PGT-A subgroup because the sample size calculation was based on the primary outcome of blastocyst formation. In addition, the study was not designed to determine whether MSS confers clinically meaningful improvements in implantation, ongoing pregnancy, or live birth. Future adequately powered trials should evaluate these clinical endpoints and identify which male-factor subgroups are most likely to benefit from MSS.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this randomized sibling-oocyte study showed that MSS was associated with a significantly higher fertilization rate than DGC in ICSI cycles involving abnormal semen parameters, while downstream embryologic outcomes were comparable, and euploidy differences were not statistically significant in the primary analysis. In addition to this early developmental advantage, MSS offers a simpler, centrifugation-free laboratory workflow. Collectively, these findings suggest that microfluidic selection may be particularly useful in selected male-factor populations. Future adequately powered studies are needed to determine whether these advantages translate into improved clinical outcomes, including live birth.
