Unravelling the reaction kinetics of ethyl propiolate with OH radicals
Authors: Manas Ranjan Dash, Lokesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ethyl propiolate (EP) is released into the atmosphere primarily due to anthropogenic activities, particularly through its widespread use in laboratory and industrial syntheses of organic compounds. In this study, the kinetics of the gas-phase reaction between EP and OH radicals were investigated experimentally at 298 K and 700 mbar of N2 using a relative rate technique. The measured rate constant was determined to be (2.34 ± 0.54) × 10−13 cm3 per molecule per s. Theoretical calculations were performed to explore the temperature-dependent kinetics over the range of 200–800 K by employing the variational transition state theory (VTST) with high-level single-point energies (SPE). All possible addition and abstraction pathways were explored for two lowest-energy conformers, syn–gauche (R1) and syn–anti (R2). Quantum mechanical tunneling effects were accounted for using both Wigner and Eckart corrections. The computed rate constants exhibited a positive temperature dependence and were fitted to three-parameter Arrhenius expressions (cm3 per molecule per s): kVTST/Wig = 4.1 × 10−28T5.2 exp[1230/T] and kVTST/Eck = 1.3 × 10−27T5.0 exp[1020/T]. Kinetic analysis indicated that hydrogen abstraction from the secondary carbon (–CH2–) dominated at lower temperatures, while at higher temperatures, OH addition to the terminal carbon and abstraction from the primary carbon (–CH3) became competitive. Potential energy surface and thermochemical analyses supported these findings. Atmospheric assessment showed that OH-initiated oxidation was the primary global loss pathway for EP. The calculated global warming potentials (GWPs) indicated a stronger climate impact at shorter time horizons, which decreased at longer times due to the short atmospheric lifetime of EP.

Introduction

Esters constitute an important class of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that are widely distributed in the atmosphere due to both natural processes and anthropogenic activities. In addition to direct emissions, esters can also be formed in situ through the atmospheric oxidation of ethers.1 Once released, these compounds undergo degradation via reactions with key atmospheric oxidants, such as hydroxyl (OH) radicals,2 nitrate (NO3) radicals,3 and chlorine (Cl) atoms,4 thereby contributing to complex oxidation cycles and secondary VOC formation. Among all oxidation processes, OH-initiated oxidation is the dominant atmospheric sink under most conditions. Ethyl propiolate has a low vapor pressure, and its vapor can cause eye irritation, skin corrosion, and respiratory problems. It is readily miscible in water, alcohol and most organic solvents. Therefore, this compound is extensively used in organic synthesis, 6–10 particularly in cycloaddition reactions that afford heterocyclic compounds, such as pyrazoles. Its structural features include an acetylenic (–CC–) moiety conjugated with an electron-withdrawing ester group, which influences its reactivity toward radical species. Despite its importance in synthetic chemistry, limited information is available regarding its physicochemical and atmospheric properties. Earlier spectroscopic investigations revealed the presence of conformational isomerism in ethyl propiolate.11 The low-energy conformers are characterized by a cis arrangement of the ester group and differences in the orientation of the ethyl substituent (syn–anti and syn–gauche).12 These conformational features are consistent with those observed in related carboxylic esters.13,14

Ethyl propiolate is predominantly emitted into the atmosphere from anthropogenic activities, including industrial operations, solvent evaporation, and laboratory usage, with minor contributions potentially arising from natural sources, such as oceanic emissions and the decomposition of organic matter, as observed for structurally related oxygenated volatile organic compounds.15 Emissions may occur during the manufacture, handling, and application of alkynes and acrylate derivatives through processes such as solvent volatilization, fugitive releases, and incomplete combustion. Furthermore, structurally analogous volatile alkynes and esters have been detected in urban and industrial atmospheres, particularly in the vicinity of chemical manufacturing and petrochemical facilities, indicating the potential presence of ethyl propiolate in such environments. Due to its volatility and reactivity, ethyl propiolate can participate in atmospheric oxidation processes even at trace concentrations, influencing radical chemistry and air quality, particularly in urban and industrial regions. Its degradation in the atmosphere is expected to proceed via reactions with OH radicals, Cl atoms, and NO3 radicals, as well as via photolysis under solar radiation.

Despite its potential atmospheric relevance, experimental and theoretical kinetic data on the reactions of ethyl propiolate with atmospheric oxidants remain limited. In particular, no comprehensive studies have been reported on its reaction kinetics with OH radicals. Recently, the temperature-dependent kinetic studies of ethyl propiolate with Cl atoms have been carried out using high-level ab initio methods.16 Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the detailed kinetics of the reaction of ethyl propiolate with OH radicals through a combined experimental and theoretical approach. High-level quantum chemical calculations are employed to characterize the potential energy surface, evaluate thermochemical parameters, and determine branching ratios. Furthermore, atmospheric implications, including lifetimes and global warming potentials (GWPs), are assessed to better understand the environmental fate of this compound. These results provide essential kinetic data for improving atmospheric models and assessing the impact of ester-based VOCs in the troposphere.

Methodologies

Experimental section

The rate constant for the reaction of ethyl propiolate with OH radicals was determined at 298 K and a total pressure of 600–700 mbar using the relative rate method. Experiments were conducted in a cylindrical quartz reaction cell (∼300 cm3) sealed at both ends with KBr windows (25 mm diameter, 5 mm thickness; uncoated, Edmund Optics). The reactor was custom-fabricated to fit directly into the sample compartment of an FT-IR spectrometer (Shimadzu). Gas mixtures containing ethyl propiolate, ethane (used as the reference compound with a well-established rate coefficient for reaction with OH), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), and nitrogen as the bath gas were prepared using a vacuum manifold system and introduced into the reactor. The cell was equipped with two high-vacuum stopcocks, and the manifold assembly consisted of Ultra-Torr and Swagelok fittings to ensure leak-tight operation. OH radicals were generated in situ by the photolysis of H2O2 at 254 nm using two UV lamps (Philips TUV8W; λmax = 254 nm) positioned adjacent to the reactor.17–21H2O2 + hν (∼254 nm) → 2OH

Prior to irradiation, control experiments were performed to verify system stability. Ethyl propiolate and ethane, each diluted in N2, were individually monitored by FT-IR to confirm spectral integrity and concentration stability. The complete reaction mixture was then held in the dark for approximately 30 minutes, during which infrared spectra were recorded over the 400–4000 cm−1 range. No significant loss of either organic component was observed, indicating negligible dark reactions and wall losses under the experimental conditions. To evaluate the possibility of direct photolysis, a mixture of ethyl propiolate, ethane, and nitrogen was irradiated in the absence of hydrogen peroxide. No measurable decrease in the concentrations of either the reactant or the reference compound was observed, confirming that direct photolysis was negligible under the experimental conditions. Subsequently, reaction mixtures containing ethyl propiolate, ethane, hydrogen peroxide, and nitrogen were irradiated for time intervals ranging from 1 to 5 minutes in a stepwise manner. After each irradiation period, the concentrations of ethyl propiolate and the reference compound were quantified using FT-IR. Ethane was monitored via its characteristic C–H bending absorption band in the 725–925 cm−1 region. Computational section

All electronic structure calculations were carried out using the Gaussian 16 package.22 The structure of reactants, transition states (TSs), and products were optimized, and their vibrational frequencies were computed using the M06-2X functional in combination with the 6-311G++(d,p) Pople basis set.23,24 This level of theory with basis set is widely regarded as reliable for predicting molecular structures, reaction kinetics, and thermochemical properties.16,17,25–30 Optimized structures were viewed using the Gauss View 06. To improve numerical precision and ensure stable convergence, an ultrafine integration grid (Integral = UltraFine), tight self-consistent field convergence (SCF = Tight), and strict geometry optimization criteria (Opt = Tight) were applied throughout. To obtain more accurate energy barriers, single-point energy calculations were further refined at the coupled-cluster single, double, and triple excitation [CCSD(T)] levels with the 6-311++G(d,p) basis set. In addition, to assess the basis-set dependence of the calculated barrier heights and to compare with the Pople-type basis set results, higher-level CCSD(T,FULL)//M06-2X calculations employing Dunning's correlation-consistent polarized valence triple-zeta (cc-pVTZ) basis set were performed for all reaction pathways. Furthermore, for the most kinetically favorable pathways involving the lowest-energy transition states, additional CCSD(T,FULL)//M06-2X/Aug-cc-pVTZ calculations were carried out for comparison purposes. Owing to the significantly high computational cost associated with the Aug-cc-pVTZ basis set at the CCSD(T,FULL) level for the present system, these calculations were restricted to the lowest-energy reaction channels. Transition states were characterized by the presence of a single imaginary frequency, while all other stationary points exhibited no imaginary frequencies, confirming them as true minima. The theoretical rate constants were determined using variational transition state theory (VTST), incorporating tunnelling corrections based on both Wigner and Eckart approaches.31 The nature of all stationary points was confirmed using vibrational frequency analysis. For each reaction channel, intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) calculations were performed, starting from the optimized transition states, to obtain the minimum energy path connecting reactants and products. Along each reaction path, a set of discrete points (21 IRC points per channel, including the transition state) was generated. All these structures were individually considered, and their corresponding Gibbs free energies (ΔG) were evaluated. These reaction path data were subsequently used for variational transition state theory (VTST) calculations. In this approach, the sequence of IRC points defines a set of generalized dividing surfaces along the reaction coordinate. The free energy profile constructed from these points allows the identification of the location of the maximum Gibbs free energy, which corresponds to the variational transition state. This position may differ slightly from the saddle point obtained from standard transition state optimization. In contrast to conventional transition state theory (TST), which relies on a single transition state structure, the present VTST treatment incorporates multiple reaction path points and explicitly accounts for the variation of free energy along the reaction coordinate. Furthermore, both potential energy and free energy profiles were analysed to highlight the variational effect on the location of the transition state at the given temperature.

Electronic structures and energetics

A recent theoretical study indicates that ethyl propiolate exists in four conformers: two low-energy cis forms (I and II) and two high-energy trans forms (III and IV).33 In each pair, the ethyl group adopts either an anti or a gauche conformation. Their calculation shows that the cis conformers (I and II) are significantly populated, while the trans forms are present in negligible amounts.33 The small energy difference (0.2–2.4 kJ mol−1) is consistent with early findings by Charles and co-workers, who reported an energy difference of ∼1 kJ mol−1.11 In this study, only the two lowest-energy cis conformers were considered, where R2 (syn–anti) corresponded to conformer I, and R1 (syn–gauche) corresponded to conformer II. Their energy difference (∼1.2 kJ mol−1) agreed with Lopes et al.33 The higher-energy trans conformers were not kinetically relevant. Fig. 2 presents the reaction schemes, along with the optimized geometries, of the reactant conformers, transition states (TSs), and products calculated at the M06-2X/6-311++G(d,p) level of theory. For the R1 conformer, OH addition at the non-terminal carbon of the –CC– bond via TS1 and TS3 formed the five-membered hydrogen-bonded secondary radical (P1), whereas terminal carbon addition through TS2 and TS4 produced the six-membered hydrogen-bonded tertiary radicals, P2 and P3, respectively. The hydrogen-bond distances in TS2 and TS4 were 2.65 and 2.66 Å, respectively, while P2 exhibited the strongest hydrogen bond (1.95 Å) between the hydroxyl hydrogen and the carbonyl oxygen, making it the most stable adduct (Fig. 2). The PES analysis (Fig. 3) indicated that terminal addition pathways were energetically more favorable than non-terminal addition because of strong intramolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions. Similarly, in the R2 conformer, non-terminal OH addition via TS11 formed the secondary radical (P7), whereas terminal addition through TS12 and TS13 yielded the tertiary radicals (P8 and P9) (Fig. 2). Overall, hydrogen bonding played a decisive role in stabilizing the transition states and intermediates in both R1 and R2 systems. Hydrogen abstraction reactions proceeded through ten distinguishable pathways, comprising six pathways for R1 + OH and four for R2 + OH, due to differences in hydrogen equivalence between the two conformers. These pathways were categorized into abstraction from the acetylenic –CC–H, methylene –CH2–, and methyl –CH3 groups (Fig. 2). The abstraction of the terminal acetylenic hydrogen via TS5 (R1) and TS14 (R2) formed ethynyl radicals (P5/P8) with H2O. However, these channels possessed high activation barriers (∼85 kJ mol−1) and were endothermic, indicating negligible kinetic importance. Among all abstraction pathways, methylene hydrogen abstraction was the most favorable. In R2, equivalent –CH2– hydrogens were abstracted via the seven-membered hydrogen-bonded transition state (TS15), forming P11 + H2O with strong stabilization (−73.5 kJ mol−1). In R1, non-equivalent –CH2– hydrogens reacted through TS6 and TS7, involving five- and six-membered hydrogen-bonded cyclic structures, respectively, to form P6 + H2O (Fig. 2). The lower barrier of TS15 (2.2 kJ mol−1) compared with those of TS6 (13.8 kJ mol−1) and TS7 (4.9 kJ mol−1) highlighted the strong stabilizing effect of intramolecular hydrogen bonding. Therefore, a symmetry factor (σ = 2) was applied for TS15 in the rate calculations. Hydrogen abstraction from the –CH3 group formed primary radicals through TS8–TS10 in R1 and TS16–TS17 in R2. Although hydrogen-bonding interactions were also present in these transition states, their activation barriers were substantially higher than those for methylene abstraction, rendering the methyl abstraction channels kinetically less important. Structural analysis further showed that abstraction from –CH2– and –CH3 groups proceeded through reactant-like early transition states, whereas abstraction from the terminal –CC–H bond proceeded via product-like late transition states. As discussed in the Computational section, higher-level CCSD (T,FULL)//M06-2X calculations employing the triple-zeta Dunning correlation-consistent family basis sets (cc-pVTZ and Aug-cc-pVTZ) were performed to assess basis-set effects and compare with the Pople-type basis set results. A comparison between frozen-core and full-electron calculations is provided in Table S1 of the SI. The obtained results indicated that the inclusion of full electron correlation led to minor changes in the absolute activation barriers and reaction energies. However, the overall energetic trends and mechanistic conclusions remained unchanged. Importantly, the lowest-energy transition states continued to be TS7 and TS15 at all levels of theory. For example, the barrier height of TS15 changed from 2.2 kJ mol−1 at the CCSD(T)//M06-2X/6-311++G(d,p) level to 3.0 kJ mol−1 at the CCSD (T,FULL)//M06-2X/cc-pVTZ level, while TS7 changed from 4.9 to 4.8 kJ mol−1. Further refinement using the Aug-cc-pVTZ basis set afforded barrier heights of 4.3 kJ mol−1 for TS7 and 2.4 kJ mol−1 for TS15. These small differences confirmed that the frozen-core approximation did not significantly affect the qualitative energetic ordering of the reaction pathways for the present system. The optimized geometrical parameters of the reactants, transition states, and products calculated at the M06-2X functional with 6-311++G (d,p), cc-pVTZ and Aug-cc-pVTZ basis sets are provided in the SI (Table S2). Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations for all stationary points were also performed at the same levels of theory, and are summarized in Table S3 of the SI. Each transition state was confirmed by the presence of a single imaginary frequency. To verify the reliability of the single-reference electronic structure description, T1 diagnostic values were computed at all levels. All values were found to be ≤0.03, indicating that the systems were adequately described using the single-reference method.

Thermochemical parameters

The standard thermodynamic parameters, including standard enthalpy (ΔH0), standard Gibbs free energy (ΔG0), standard entropy (ΔS0), and their corresponding activation quantities, such as enthalpy of activation (ΔH‡), Gibbs free energy of activation (ΔG‡), and entropy of activation (ΔS‡), were calculated for all reaction species at 298 K and 1 atm. The results are summarized in Table 2. Overall, the OH addition pathways were both highly exothermic and exergonic, indicating strong thermodynamic favorability. Hydrogen abstraction from the secondary –CH2– and primary –CH3 groups was also thermodynamically favorable, as these channels were exothermic and exergonic. In contrast, the abstraction of the terminal alkyne hydrogen (–CC–H) was endothermic and endergonic, making it the least thermodynamically favorable pathway among all considered channels. The calculated activation parameters showed that the standard enthalpy of activation (ΔH‡) and Gibbs free energy of activation (ΔG‡) were positive for all transition states, while the entropy of activation (ΔS‡) was negative, indicating a decrease in entropy due to the formation of more ordered transition-state structures. Similarly, the addition reactions exhibited negative ΔS0 values, reflecting increased structural organization during the reaction process. Their negative ΔG0 values confirmed that these reactions were spontaneous. In contrast, the hydrogen abstraction pathways exhibited comparatively more favorable entropy changes, supporting their feasibility from an entropic perspective. Overall, the thermodynamic analysis suggested that OH addition pathways were the most favorable, followed by hydrogen abstraction from the secondary and primary carbon atoms, while the abstraction of the terminal alkyne hydrogen was the least favorable. In addition, we performed a detailed temperature-dependent free energy analysis for all reaction channels over the range of 200–800 K and incorporated the corresponding free energy profiles in the SI (Fig. S1A–Q, Tables S4A and 4B). The calculated data revealed that all transition states exhibited a systematic increase in activation free energy with increasing temperature. For example, ΔG‡ for TS7 increased from 18.6 kJ mol−1 at 200 K to 97.5 kJ mol−1 at 800 K, while TS15 increased from 17.0 to 96.2 kJ mol−1 over the same temperature range. Similar trends were observed for all other TS structures. This behaviour indicated a negative activation entropy, suggesting that the transition states were more ordered than the reactant configurations. The thermodynamic profiles further demonstrated that no crossover among the transition-state free energies occurred throughout the investigated temperature range. Consequently, the reaction mechanism remained unchanged with temperature, and the reactions proceeded through the same kinetically preferred pathways over the entire temperature interval studied. Among all pathways, TS15 and TS7 consistently possessed the lowest activation free energies, indicating that these channels were kinetically the most favorable. In contrast, TS5 and TS14 exhibited substantially large activation barriers (>90 kJ mol−1 at 200 K), making these pathways kinetically less accessible. The product free energies also provided important thermodynamic insight. All the products obtained from the addition and abstraction channels, except abstraction from terminal alkyne hydrogen (P4/P10 + H2O), remained negative throughout the temperature range, confirming that these reaction channels were thermodynamically favourable. Conversely, P4 + H2O and P10 + H2O possessed positive Gibbs free energies across all temperatures, indicating thermodynamically unfavorable product formation despite the existence of kinetically accessible pathways. To examine the temperature dependence of the variational transition state, Gibbs free energy profiles were also analysed along the IRC-derived reaction coordinate for the reaction channel involving the lowest-energy transition state (TS15) over the temperature range of 200–800 K (Fig. 4). The results showed that as the temperature increased, the position of the maximum free-energy progressively shifted toward the product side. This behaviour indicated that the entropic contribution (−TΔS) became increasingly important relative to the enthalpic contribution (ΔH) at elevated temperatures.

Kinetics

To the best of our knowledge, no kinetic data for the reaction between ethyl propiolate and OH radicals have been previously reported. Therefore, this study presents the first experimental kinetic investigation of this reaction carried out at room temperature using the relative rate method. In addition, theoretical rate constants were calculated over a temperature range of 200–800 K with intervals of 25 K. The rate constants were determined using variational transition state theory (VTST) with tunneling corrections based on the models proposed by Eugene Wigner and Carl Eckart. The Wigner correction accounts for quantum mechanical tunneling using only the imaginary frequency of the transition state, whereas the Eckart method provides a more realistic description by considering the shape of the potential energy barrier and incorporating both forward and backward barrier heights along the reaction coordinate. The rate constants obtained using Variational Transition State Theory (VTST) and VTST with Wigner's and Eckart's tunneling corrections are summarized in Table 3. As discussed in the energetic analysis, the energy difference between the two conformers was approximately 1.2 kJ mol−1. Therefore, the overall rate constant (kTotal) was calculated as a Boltzmann-weighted average of the rate constants for the individual conformers using the following expression: ktotal = WR1 × kR1 + WR2 × kR2, where WR1 and WR2 represent the Boltzmann population weights of conformers R1 and R2, respectively, and kR1 and kR2 are the total rate constants associated with each conformer. The rate constant for each conformer was obtained by summing the contributions from all OH addition and hydrogen abstraction pathways. The calculated Boltzmann weight factors (WR1 and WR2) were 0.39 and 0.61 at 298 K, 0.33 and 0.67 at 200 K, and 0.46 and 0.54 at 800 K. The individual rate constants for all reaction channels across the temperature range of 200–800 K are provided in the SI (Tables S5A–F). The calculated rate constants over the investigated temperature range showed a positive temperature dependence. The monotonic increase in rate constants with temperature indicated thermally activated reaction behavior consistent with the variational transition-state theory. Although ΔG‡ increased with temperature, the rate constants also increased, because the temperature-dependent pre-factor (kBT/h) in the Eyring equation dominated the overall kinetics. Thus, the reactions became kinetically fast at elevated temperatures, despite the entropically less favorable transition states. At 298 K, the theoretical rate constants for the reaction of ethyl propiolate with OH radicals, obtained using the Wigner and Eckart tunneling corrections, were 1.56 × 10−13 and 1.12 × 10−13 cm3 per molecule per s, respectively. The close agreement between these values indicated that the choice of tunneling correction method has a minor influence on the calculated rate coefficients at room temperature. However, the differences between the Wigner and Eckart corrections became more significant at low temperatures (e.g., around 200 K), reflecting the sensitivity of tunneling contributions to the shape and width of the potential energy barrier. In the present study, the unsymmetrical Eckart correction was employed because it provided a more realistic description of asymmetric potential energy barriers than the simpler Wigner approximation, particularly in the low-temperature regime. Nevertheless, more sophisticated multidimensional tunneling treatments were beyond the scope of the present work and may be considered in future investigations to further improve the accuracy of low-temperature kinetic predictions. An experimental rate constant was determined at 298 K under the conditions accessible to the present setup. Since measurements over a wide temperature range are currently unavailable, the calculated temperature-dependent rate constants should be regarded as theoretical predictions requiring future experimental validation. From the experimental perspective, uncertainties may arise from variations in experimental conditions and limitations associated with the measurement techniques used to determine the rate constant. In the present study, the relative rate method was employed, which strongly depended on the accuracy of the reference reaction rate constant. Any uncertainty in the reference rate constant, as well as potential errors in measuring the concentrations of the reactants during the experiments, can significantly affect the derived rate constant. From the theoretical standpoint, uncertainties mainly originate from the calculated energies of transition states and products at the chosen level of theory. Furthermore, the theoretical pre-exponential factor depends on the accurate estimation of partition functions for both reactants and transition states, which are derived from vibrational frequencies calculated at the selected theoretical level. The calculated room temperature rate constants for the reactions of the two conformers with OH radicals were determined to be kR1 = 2.57 × 10−14 and kR2 = 8.62 × 10−14 cm3 per molecule per s for the syn–gauche (R1) and syn–anti (R2) –conformers, respectively. These results indicated that the syn–anti conformer (R2) contributed approximately thrice as much to the overall rate constant as the syn–gauche conformer (R1).

Although no experimental or theoretical kinetic data are currently available in the literature for the reaction of ethyl propiolate with OH radicals for direct comparison with the present results, a related study has reported rate constants for a structurally similar compound, ethyl acrylate (CH2CHCOOCH2CH3), reacting with OH radicals. Ethyl acrylate has a closely related molecular structure, differing from ethyl propiolate only by the presence of a CC double bond instead of a CC triple bond. Teruel et al.34 experimentally measured the kinetics of the reaction between ethyl acrylate and OH radicals. They reported a rate constant of (1.67 ± 0.17) × 10−11 cm3 per molecule per s at room temperature. In comparison, the measured rate constant for the ethyl propiolate + OH reaction obtained in the present study was (2.34 ± 0.54) × 10−13 cm3 per molecule per s, which was approximately two orders of magnitude lower than that reported for the ethyl acrylate system. This difference could be attributed to the lower reactivity of the CC triple bond toward OH radicals compared with that of the CC double bond. A similar trend in reactivity is also reflected in the IUPAC-recommended rate constants for the reactions of OH radicals with ethylene and acetylene.35 The reported rate constant for the ethylene + OH reaction was 9.0 × 10−12 cm3 per molecule per s,35 whereas for the acetylene + OH reaction, it was 9.0 × 10−13 cm3 per molecule per s,35 showing roughly an order-of-magnitude lower reactivity for the triple-bond system. The comparatively lower reactivity of alkynes toward OH radicals, relative to alkenes, was consistent with established chemical principles. The presence of a carbon–carbon triple bond in alkynes leads to the reduced accessibility of π-electrons and typically results in higher activation energy barriers for radical addition. Consequently, this contributes to the lower observed rate constants. These observations emphasize the significant role of π-system characteristics in controlling the reactivity of unsaturated compounds toward radical species. Furthermore, Dash et al.16 recently investigated the reaction of ethyl propiolate with Cl atoms using high-level DFT and ab initio methods. Their calculated rate constant was 3.9 × 10−11 cm3 per molecule per s, which was approximately two orders of magnitude higher than the value obtained in the present study for the reaction with OH radicals. Consistent with this observation, the IUPAC recommended data35 indicate that the rate constant for the Cl + acetylene reaction (2.3 × 10−10 cm3 per molecule per s) is also significantly higher than that for the OH + acetylene reaction. In addition, comparable reactivity trends have been reported for propargyl alcohol (HCCCH2OH), where the rate constants for reactions with OH radicals and Cl atoms are 9.2 × 10−12 (ref. 36) and 3.3 × 10−10 cm3 per molecule per s,37 respectively. Overall, these comparisons reveal consistent reactivity patterns among structurally related unsaturated compounds and demonstrate that Cl atoms generally react more rapidly than OH radicals, particularly with alkyne-containing molecules. As presented in Table 3, the influence of quantum tunneling on the calculated rate constants was evaluated using the kVTST/Wigner/kVTST and kVTST/Eckart/kVTST ratios. The results indicated that tunneling effects were more pronounced at low temperatures but became negligible at high temperatures. Across the studied temperature range, these ratios were 3.1 and 1.8 at 200 K, 1.9 and 1.4 at 298 K, and approximately 1.1 and 1.1 at 800 K. This trend showed that tunneling contributions decreased significantly with increasing temperature. Also, for both OH addition and hydrogen abstraction pathways, tunneling played a noticeable role at low temperatures, whereas its effect became insignificant at elevated temperatures.

Branching ratios

The temperature dependence of the branching ratios for individual reaction pathways was evaluated over the studied temperature range and is illustrated in Fig. 6A–C. As discussed earlier, the reaction proceeded through ten distinct pathways for conformer R1 and seven pathways for conformer R2. Among these, seven pathways corresponded to OH addition across the –CC– bond, while the remaining ten involved hydrogen abstraction from primary, secondary, and terminal hydrogen atoms. The branching ratio for each reaction channel was determined using the following expression:2where ki represents the rate constant of the individual reaction channel and kTotal is the overall rate constant obtained by summing the contributions of all channels. The results showed that the reaction of OH radicals with the syn–anti conformer (R2) contributed more significantly to the overall rate constant than the syn–gauche conformer (R1), particularly at relatively low temperatures (Fig. 6A). This behavior could be attributed to the relatively low activation barriers associated with the dominant reaction pathways of the R2 conformer. However, as the temperature increased, the contribution of R1 gradually became more significant due to the enhanced accessibility of its higher-energy transition states. Consequently, the R1 contribution increased with temperature and began to dominate above approximately 600 K. For example, at 200 K, the branching ratios for R1 and R2 were 11.9% and 88.1%, respectively. These values shifted to 22.9% and 77.1% at 298 K and further to 54.1% and 45.9% at 800 K, respectively. This temperature-dependent behavior was consistent with the calculated activation barriers for the respective transition states involved in each conformer. Fig. 6B further illustrates that hydrogen abstraction is the major reaction pathway over the entire temperature range investigated, while OH addition across the triple bond offers comparatively minor contributions. At low temperatures, the contribution from addition pathways was negligible due to their relatively high activation barriers. However, as the temperature increased, the addition channels became increasingly competitive. For instance, at 200 K, the contributions from the addition and abstraction pathways were 1.4% and 98.6%, respectively. These values changed to 10.3% and 89.7% at 298 K and to 43.1% and 56.9% at 800 K, respectively. This trend indicated that hydrogen abstraction was kinetically favored at low temperatures, whereas addition reactions became more significant at elevated temperatures. Fig. 6C presents the individual contributions of terminal and non-terminal addition pathways, as well as the different hydrogen abstraction channels, to the overall rate constant. Among the addition pathways, OH addition to the terminal carbon atom of the alkyne was consistently favored over addition to the non-terminal carbon throughout the studied temperature range, although the overall contributions of the addition channels remained minor at low temperatures. Nevertheless, the contribution from non-terminal addition increased gradually with increasing temperature. For example, at 200 K, the contributions from terminal and non-terminal additions were 1.2% and 0.1%, respectively; these values changed to 8.9% and 1.3% at 298 K and to 33.3% and 9.8% at 800 K, respectively. This regioselectivity could be rationalized by considering the strong electron-withdrawing character of the ester substituent (–COOC2H5), which exerted both –M (mesomeric) and –I (inductive) effects. When the OH radical was added to the terminal carbon of the triple bond, the resulting radical center was formed on the adjacent non-terminal carbon that bore the ester substituent. This radical was stabilized through resonance delocalization with the carbonyl group of the ester, resulting in a more stable intermediate. In contrast, addition at the non-terminal carbon generated a radical center at the terminal carbon, where such conjugative stabilization was absent. Consequently, terminal addition was energetically more favorable. A similar trend was observed for hydrogen abstraction pathways. At low temperatures, hydrogen abstraction from the secondary –CH2– group was the dominant channel. For example, at 200 K, the contributions from secondary and primary hydrogen abstraction were 97.6% and 1.0%, respectively. These values changed to 86.6% and 3.2% at 298 K and to 32.6% and 24.3% at 800 K, respectively. The increasing contribution of primary abstraction at high temperatures reflected the increasing accessibility of its associated transition states. Overall, the observed temperature-dependent variations in the contributions of individual reaction pathways were consistent with the calculated activation barriers for the corresponding transition states.

Atmospheric implications

The atmospheric degradation of ethyl propiolate primarily occurs through reactions with major tropospheric oxidants, including OH radicals, NO3 radicals, O3, and potentially Cl atoms. However, the reactions with NO3 radicals and O3 are expected to proceed much more slowly than those with OH radicals and Cl atoms. Consequently, the contributions of NO3 and O3-initiated reactions to the atmospheric removal of ethyl propiolate are likely to be negligible. The atmospheric lifetimes (τ) of ethyl propiolate with respect to reactions with OH radicals and Cl atoms were estimated using the following expression:3τ = (kx [X])−1where kx represents the rate constant for the reaction of ethyl propiolate with the oxidant (X) (OH or Cl), and [X] denotes the global average atmospheric concentration of the corresponding oxidant. Using a typical global average OH concentration of (106) radicals cm−3 (ref. 2 and 38) and the rate constants obtained from both experimental and theoretical calculations at 298 K, the OH-initiated atmospheric lifetime of ethyl propiolate was estimated to be on the order of several days. Similarly, the Cl-initiated atmospheric lifetime was estimated using reported rate constants16 at 298 K and a global average Cl atom concentration of (103) atoms cm−3,4,39 resulting in lifetimes also on the order of several hundred days. However, in coastal and marine boundary layer regions, where peak Cl atom concentrations can reach approximately (105) atoms cm−3,5 the Cl-driven atmospheric lifetime of ethyl propiolate decreases significantly to about three days. These results indicated that the calculated OH-driven lifetime was somewhat sensitive to the choice of rate constants, with experimentally derived values leading to shorter lifetimes compared to theoretical predictions. Nevertheless, the overall conclusions remained consistent. Both theoretical and experimental results suggest that the reaction with OH radicals is the dominant global atmospheric sink for ethyl propiolate, while reactions with Cl atoms may become important only in coastal and marine environments, where Cl concentrations are elevated.

To evaluate the influence of atmospheric variables, such as temperature, altitude, and oxidant concentration, the atmospheric lifetimes of ethyl propiolate were further estimated under different tropospheric conditions, and the results are summarized in Tables S4 and S5. Using the calculated rate constants, the OH-controlled lifetimes were determined for typical tropospheric OH concentrations, ranging from 9.0 × 105 to 1.5 × 107 radicals per cm3 (ref. 2, 40 and 41) at altitudes between 0 and 10 km, corresponding to temperatures from 298.15 to 223.29 K. As shown in Table S6, the predicted OH-driven atmospheric lifetime of ethyl propiolate varies from 6.9 to 157.4 days across this temperature and altitude range. Similarly, using previously reported rate constants,16 the Cl-controlled atmospheric lifetimes were estimated for Cl atom concentrations between 1.0 × 103 and 1.0 × 106 atoms per cm3 within the same altitude range (0–10 km). The resulting Cl-controlled lifetimes ranged from about 0.2 to 294.6 days (Table S7), depending on the Cl atom concentration and atmospheric conditions. At a fixed altitude, increasing the concentrations of OH radicals or Cl atoms led to a progressive decrease in the atmospheric lifetime of ethyl propiolate, reflecting the strong influence of these oxidants on its atmospheric removal. In addition, the OH-controlled lifetime increased with altitude in the troposphere due to the combined effects of low temperatures and reduced OH concentrations at high elevations. In contrast, the Cl-controlled lifetime decreased under conditions where Cl atom concentrations became elevated, indicating that chlorine-initiated oxidation may enhance the degradation of ethyl propiolate in regions with significant Cl reactivity. Overall, these results highlight that OH radicals dominate the global atmospheric degradation of ethyl propiolate, while Cl atoms may play a more localized role under specific environmental conditions.

Owing to its measurable tropospheric lifetime, ethyl propiolate has the potential to contribute to radiative forcing and, consequently, to climate change. The global warming potential (GWP)42–45 of ethyl propiolate was evaluated relative to CO2, using its atmospheric lifetime and infrared spectroscopic properties, specifically the vibrational frequencies (νk) and corresponding absorption intensities (Ak) of each normal mode.45 These parameters were obtained from the optimized molecular structure. The total radiative efficiency of ethyl propiolate was estimated to be 0.1 W per m2 per ppbv. The Absolute Global Warming Potential (AGWP) values for CO2 were taken as 2.29 × 10−14, 8.06 × 10−14, and 2.69 × 10−13 W per m2 per years (kgCO2) for time horizons of 20, 100, and 500 years, respectively.43 Using these reference values, GWPs of ethyl propiolate were calculated for different atmospheric conditions and are presented in Table 4. The results indicated that the climatic impact of ethyl propiolate was more pronounced over short time horizons, as reflected by the high GWP values at 20 years, while substantially low values were obtained for long time horizons (100 and 500 years). This behavior was consistent with its relatively short atmospheric lifetime. Under globally averaged OH and Cl concentrations, ethyl propiolate exhibited moderate short-term climate forcing. However, under elevated chlorine atom concentrations typical of marine boundary layer conditions, its atmospheric lifetime decreased significantly, resulting in markedly reduced GWP values. Overall, these findings suggest that ethyl propiolate contributes to radiative forcing in the near term, but its long-term climate impact is limited due to rapid atmospheric degradation, especially in chlorine-rich environments.

Conclusion

In this study, we reported the first measurement of the kinetics involved in the reaction between ethyl propiolate and OH radicals using a relative rate technique at room temperature. Also, we investigated the theoretical exploration of the temperature-dependent kinetics of ethyl propiolate with OH radicals using high-level DFT and ab initio methods over the 200–800 K range. The calculated rate constants exhibited a slight positive temperature dependence. Among the two conformers, the syn–anti conformer (R2) predominantly governed the overall reactivity at low temperatures, whereas the contribution of the syn–gauche conformer (R1) increased progressively with temperature. Both kinetic and thermodynamic analyses revealed that hydrogen abstraction from the secondary carbon site was the most favorable pathway under ambient conditions. However, with increasing temperature, the contributions from other reaction channels became increasingly significant. OH radical addition to the terminal carbon of the triple bond was consistently more favorable than the addition to the non-terminal carbon. In addition, hydrogen abstraction from the primary carbon became moderately competitive at elevated temperatures. All reaction pathways were exothermic, except for the terminal hydrogen abstraction, which contributed negligibly to the overall rate. The estimated atmospheric lifetimes indicated that OH-initiated oxidation was the dominant global sink for ethyl propiolate, while reactions with Cl atoms may become competitive in marine boundary layer regions. The calculated GWP values suggested that the climatic impact of ethyl propiolate was more significant over short time horizons, with substantially low values at long timescales, due to its relatively rapid atmospheric degradation, particularly in chlorine-rich environments. Overall, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of the reaction kinetics and atmospheric implications of ethyl propiolate. These findings are expected to support future experimental and theoretical investigations and improve the accuracy of atmospheric models in predicting the fate and distribution of this compound.
