Statin Use and Accessibility for Patients Living With HIV on a Global Scale
Authors: Verity N Ramirez, Cynthia Taub, Waseem Sous, Medson Boti, Wen-Liang Song, Elizabeth Asiago-Reddy
Published: 2024

Globally, there are 39 million people living with HIV (PLH), with 1.3 million new cases of HIV in 2022.1 More than 75% of PLH are now receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART).1 ART use alongside widespread prevention and screening efforts has led to a resultant decline in incident cases and a decrease in mortality from HIV.1,2 However, there remains a disproportionate risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) among PLH globally.2,3 HIV is listed as a risk-enhancer in the 2019 American College of Cardiology prevention guidelines because PLH have a two times greater risk of CVD 2, 3, 4 with an earlier age of presentation.2 The recently published REPRIEVE (Randomized Trial to Prevent Vascular Events in HIV) was a primary prevention trial, which demonstrated that PLH aged 40 to 75 years with low-to-intermediate CVD risk who are suppressed on ART had significantly reduced major adverse cardiac event (MACE) risk when randomized to pitavastatin 4 mg daily vs placebo.2 Subsequently, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) issued a recommendation that adults 40 to 75 years with HIV and a 5% to 20% CVD risk be started on a moderate intensity statin;5 the panel also favors statin use in such patients even with CVD risk <5%. Given the global scale of the HIV epidemic, significant benefits could be anticipated for millions of PLH, if statins were made available to eligible patients.2

Chronic inflammation plays a role in atherosclerosis and CVD pathogenesis.2, 3, 4 The chronic immune dysregulation and inflammation in HIV enhances a person’s CVD risk beyond traditional risk factors or what would be estimated by risk prediction models.3,4 Alterations in CD4+ count and CD8+ T cell activation seen in HIV are associated with coronary heart disease.3 These changes in the adaptive immune system cause inflammation within atheromatous plaques.3 An increase in M1-type macrophage differentiation in HIV can cause accelerated cholesterol endothelial deposition, plaque formation, and instability.3 Statins, a mainstay of CVD risk reduction, likely have anti-inflammatory pleiotropic effects as their observed benefits in clinical trials are larger than what would be predicted by low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) lowering alone.2,6 The recent randomized controlled trial, REPRIEVE, showed a decreased risk of MACE with pitavastatin 4 mg daily in low to moderate CVD risk individuals. Treatment with pitavastatin demonstrated a risk reduction larger than would be predicted by only LDL-C lowering.2 With systemic inflammation being a central pathophysiologic mediator in HIV and atherosclerosis, the lipid-lowering effects and anti-inflammatory properties of statins have an important role in preventing CVD in most adults with HIV.2,3,6

Beyond chronic inflammation in HIV contributing to atherosclerosis, side effects of ART regimens can lead to dyslipidemia, weight gain, and metabolic syndrome.3,6 These side effects profiles are common with many nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors, and protease inhibitors.3,6 Integrase inhibitors, while more lipid-neutral than many other HIV drug classes, have been associated with significant weight gain and its attendant health effects, including possible increased risk of diabetes.3 As HIV infection and ART influence cardiovascular risk and cardiometabolic risk factors, statins are an important medication that should be used in PLH to counter these effects.2,3,5,6

PLH in the United States have experienced underprescription of statins even prior to the results of the REPRIEVE trial.6 Globally, it is likely that the accessibility and utilization of statins among PLH remain suboptimal. Nonetheless, management of dyslipidemia is recognized as a key tenant of HIV management in some low- and middle-income countries. For example, HIV guidelines in Kenya and Tanzania recommend screening for dyslipidemia with initial treatment of lifestyle modifications, followed by ART switch, and lastly the use of statins if dyslipidemia persists.7,8 The 39 million PLH are aging and facing increased threats from concomitant traditional CVD risk factors, such as obesity, diabetes, and kidney disease. This increases the need for aggressive primary CVD prevention in PLH and the need to identify barriers to statin use and accessibility.

The current underutilization of statins requires universal attention.6 The multinational REPRIEVE trial suggests that even more low- to moderate-risk adults with HIV would benefit from statin therapy than are recommended by current cardiovascular prevention and HIV guidelines.2,4,7,8 Of major importance is the fact that PLH are largely already engaged in their medical care,1 enhancing the feasibility of incorporating statins as a regular part of this care. The focus now needs to be on accessibility and standardization of statins to address the CVD gap.6 Given the global burden of CVD and HIV infection, the following are recommendations to decrease incident CVD in PLH:

1.The REPRIEVE trial used the branded statin pitavastatin calcium.2 While branded medication may not be cost-effective or feasible globally, atorvastatin and rosuvastatin are available generically and can be coprescribed at appropriate dosages to avoid drug–drug interactions with ART.6 Furthermore, many HIV medications are also generic and manufactured throughout the world. The possibility exists to coformulate HIV mediations to include statin in a combination pill or polypill. A polypill strategy has been shown to be beneficial in cardiovascular prevention and would simplify medicines, improve compliance, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness, particularly in underserved populations.9 This strategy would also help limit contraindicated statin use among patients on ART.6

2.Defining the role of nonstatin LDL-C-lowering drugs in PLH is necessary. Ezetimibe is affordable and well-tolerated, and has less drug–drug interactions with ART compared to statins. A short 12-week trial of ezetimibe and rosuvastatin 10 mg was more effective than rosuvastatin 20 mg alone in reducing apolipoprotein B, total cholesterol, and LDL-C in PLH on ART.10 This also makes ezetimibe a potential polypill candidate.10 The emergence of very long-lasting small interfering RNA drugs, such as inclisiran, which can be dosed as infrequently as every 6 months, is also promising in PLH. Although these long-lasting drugs may not be cost-effective now, as technology matures and costs decrease, they could significantly lower the health care burden for PLH globally. These nonstatin LDL-C lowering drugs should have at least the same efficacy, secondary to LDL-C lowering, in reducing cardiovascular events in PLH compared to other populations. However, they might not be as effective as statins because statins also have pleiotropic effects, such as anti-inflammation. Nevertheless, further research on the use and cost-effectiveness of nonstatin therapies in PLH is needed, particularly as the use of statins in PLH becomes more utilized.5,10

3.Statin use globally is likely underutilized in PLH, as statin use in the United States is also suboptimal in this population.6 Given the expanded U.S. HHS guidance to include statin use for low to moderate CVD-risk adults with HIV,5 impediments to statin prescription should be explored. In low- and middle-income countries, there can be limited accessibility to laboratory monitoring. Liberalizing guidelines globally to recommend statin use for all low to moderate at-risk adults regardless of baseline lipids could ultimately be cost-saving. To identify barriers to statin use and accessibility, further research and cost-effectiveness analyses are needed.

4.The U.S. HHS recommendations for statin initiation in most adults in adults with HIV are a promising step toward addressing the CVD disparity gap. Biological women with HIV have a higher risk of CVD compared to men with HIV, likely related to differences in sex hormones and inflammation on myocardial and vascular stiffness and dysfunction.3 Non-Hispanic Black people, particularly women, are at an increased risk of cerebrovascular disease in the context of HIV. Given the disproportionate burden of CVD in women and people of color, these groups stand to benefit most from the newer recommendations for statins in HIV.3 However, many of these individuals live globally, outside of the United States.1 Partnering with local, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), such as Partners In Health in Malawi with a long-standing equity-based track record, can help expand the reach of statin use and accessibility worldwide. Novel care models for implementing statin guidelines that utilize NGOs currently providing ART are vital to ensuring that women of color globally do not get left behind in an increasing CVD disparity gap.

5.Current models such as the pooled cohort equation are inadequate to estimate risk in the HIV population.2,5 The median CVD risk estimate in REPRIEVE for participants was 4.5%. Statins are an effective means of reducing MACE risk associated with HIV, even if baseline CVD risk scores do not indicate a need for statins.2 The recent U.S. HHS recommendations are a decisive step for addressing cardiovascular disease prevention in PLH on a population level. Following the lead of the U.S. HHS recommendations,5 statin use in low- to intermediate-risk adults aged 40 to 75 years should be incorporated into cardiovascular prevention and HIV treatment guidelines globally.

The global burden of HIV and its associated cardiovascular risk necessitates a comprehensive approach to managing and mitigating these risks. The REPRIEVE trial has underscored the importance of statin therapy in reducing cardiovascular events in PLH.2 However, significant gaps remain in the utilization of statins, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.7,8 Addressing these gaps through the implementation of global guidelines, leveraging generic medications, utilizing NGOs, and exploring polypill strategies can potentially lead to substantial improvements in cardiovascular outcomes for PLH. Continued research and adaptation of treatment protocols will be crucial in achieving these goals and ensuring that all PLH receive the cardiovascular care they need.
