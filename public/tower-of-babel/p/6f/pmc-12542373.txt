Clinical features of MPP with or without viruses among hospitalized children in 2023, Wenzhou, Zhejiang, China
Authors: Liya Chen, Haiyan Li, Peipei Zhong, Weikun Zheng, Hu Zhang, Suhua Li, Haifan Shi, Yiping Chen, Qi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundM. pneumoniae (MP) is a common cause of childhood pneumonia, but there is limited understanding of whether virus co-infections affect clinical severity of M. pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of MPP at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University in 2023 to compare clinical characteristics and outcomes between MP with and without viral co-infection in children, including length of stay, febrile/ cough /wheeze duration, treatment, severity of MPP, and serum biomarkers. We also collected MP targeted next generation sequencing (tNGS) data from the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) samples and analyzed MP whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from a public database to investigate MP genome, typing, and drug resistance.ResultThe annually seasonal analysis showed that a higher prevalence of MP occurred from summer to winter. Viral pathogens in our study were highly detected in either autumn or winter. Virus co-infection cases showed a peak in November. HRV was the most frequent detected virus in our study. There was no significant different of gender in all the MPP patients. Compared with single MP infection, children with virus co-infection were younger. They had a shorter febrile duration, longer cough duration, and less proportion of severe MPP cases. Laboratory biomarkers (e.g. CRP, WBC, D-D, PCT) were also significant different between MP mono and viral co-infection groups. 74.07% the samples from BALF performed an A2063G mutation with macrolide-resistant MP (MRMP). By the analysis of phylogenetic tree and the Multi-Locus Sequence Typing (MLST), we suggested that the predominant strains of MP in our study remains ST3 and MRMP strains were independently selected from various genetic backgrounds of MP due to macrolide usage.ConclusionThese findings illustrated that viral co-infection aggravated MP associated bronchi and bronchioles damage, but did not increased severity of MPP, suggesting a potential attenuation of disease severity.Trial registrationMedical Research Registration and Filing Information System.2024-K-274-02.Registered 26 August 2024. https://www.medicalresearch.org.cn/clinicalResearch.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-025-02128-y.Keywords: M. pneumoniae pneumonia, Virus, Co-infection, Drug resistance-associated mutation

Introduction

Childhood pneumonia is a major clinical and global public health problem. It is estimated that 740,180 children under the age of 5 died due to pneumonia in 2019 (WHO), accounting for 14% of all deaths of children under 5 years of age (Available from: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/pneumonia.) M. pneumoniae (MP) is one of the most common cause of morbidity and mortality in children with community-acquired pneumonia (CAP). The epidemiological study from north China reported that MP pneumonia (MPP) has accounted for 20–40% of pediatric CAP cases in the past few years [1]. MPP is usually mild and self-limiting, but still about 12% of them will progress to severe MPP, leading to airway remodeling and even bronchiolitis/bronchitis obliterans [2, 3]. The majority of pediatric MPP are school-age children. However, there has increasing data showed that younger children are also affected and more likely to develop severe disease [4]. For children under 5 years of age, viruses are the main pathogens causing respiratory infections, such as human adenovirus (HADV), influenza (Inf), human rhinovirus (HRV), and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), which are spread through droplet transmission and contribute to the outbreaks of respiratory epidemics in schools, kindergartens, and other densely populated places [5, 6]. In addition, new pathogens such as human metapneumovirus (HMPV) and human bocavirus (Boca) have been identified in pediatric respiratory infections over the decades [7]. In the clinic, different pathogens may cause similar symptoms. MP often occurs as co-infection with viruses, with an incidence of approximately 8% [8]. It is challenging to differentiate between specific types of infection, which can lead to misdiagnosis and the inappropriate use of antibiotics. Additionally, the respiratory pathogens exhibit a variety of epidemic characteristics across different regions, population demographics, and seasons [9]. Therefore, it is significant to investigate the clinical features of respiratory tract infections in children. However, few studies have focused on the influence of respiratory virus co-infection on MPP, and it is uncertain whether co-infection increases the clinical severity of MPP.

In this study, we collected data from a cohort of hospitalized pediatric patients and conducted a systematic investigation of the prevalence of MPP to obtain a comprehensive insight into the characteristics of MPP with or without viral coinfection in Wenzhou.

Method

Study population and definition of infection

This retrospective cohort study of patients (0–18 years) was conducted in the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University from January 1, 2023 to December 31, 2023. As this was a retrospective study, all eligible cases during the study period were included without prior sample size calculation.During the study period, a total of hospitalized children (n = 14907) were diagnosed with CAP according to the following criteria: fever and/or respiratory symptoms, e.g. cough, sputum production, and sore throat; the presence of wheezing or/and crackles on auscultation; infiltrative inflammatory manifestations on a chest radiograph or computerized tomography (CT). We excluded patients if they had immunodeficiency, chronic lung diseases, congenital anomaly, genetic disorder, autoimmune diseases, and congenital heart diseases. Children with active asthma experiencing acute exacerbations at the time of admission were excluded to avoid confounding effects on respiratory symptoms. Children with active asthma experiencing acute exacerbations at the time of admission were excluded to avoid confounding effects on respiratory symptoms.

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analyses were used for determination of respiratory viruses and MP to identify the etiologies of CAP. Pathogen was defined as positive PCR of throat swab and/or other respiratory specimens (sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)). In addition, bacterial culture was also performed. Evidence of bacterial infection (e.g. Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae, M. catarrhalis, S. aureus, Group A streptococcus) was confirmed by the positive culture of blood, BALF, sputum, and nasopharyngeal swab. Patients with confirmed bacterial and virus-only infections were excluded from the study. The enrolled patients were classified into MP infection group, MP + Virus group, and MP + Mixed viruses coinfection group. This study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University.

Bronchial damage was inferred based on clinical symptoms (e.g., wheezing, prolonged cough), radiological findings (airway wall thickening or atelectasis), and bronchoscopy results where available. Additionally, levels of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-8 and TNF-α were used as surrogate markers of airway inflammation.Severe pneumonia was defined as the presence of one or more of the following manifestations (Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia in children (2023 edition)): (1) Radiography: infiltration of 2/3 of one lung, multilobar infiltration, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, atelectasis, lung necrosis or lung abscesses; (2) Hypoxemia: cyanosis; marked increase in respiratory rate; marked chest wall retractions, tracheal tugging or nasal flaring; O2 saturation less than 92%; (3) Extrapulmonary complications.

Data collection

Information on clinical, laboratory, and radiologic findings were obtained retrospectively from patient’s electronic medical record. The data of the following parameters were recorded: (1) demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, and duration of symptoms prior to admission, hospital length of stay (LoS), treatment; (2) results of viral diagnostic tests performed in nasopharyngeal aspirates; (3) results of blood tests, including white blood cell (WBC) count, percentage of neutrophils, serum C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, LDH, D-D, PCT, IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IFN-γ, IFN-α, and TNF-α.

Analysis of antimicrobial resistance genes and multi-locus sequence typing (MLST)

BALF samples were measured for drug resistance analysis by using targeted next generation sequencing (tNGS). Complete genomic data for MP strains were collected from NCBI (see supplementary data), including strains from Beijing (n = 19), Japan (n = 72), South Korea (n = 30), Taiwan (n = 96), and four Zhejiang samples. A BLASTn (version 2.9.0+) alignment [10]was performed against the 23 S rRNA gene, comparing these strains with four samples from Zhejiang. This analysis provided locus information at positions 2063, 2064, 2067, and 2617. Subsequently, a heatmap was generated using the R package pheatmap (version 1.0.12) to visualize the data. Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was conducted using the pubmlst platform [11], and sequence analysis results for each strain were obtained. The results were subsequently visualized using GrapeTree software [12].

Whole-genome SNP phylogenetic analysis

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis was conducted using the Snippy v4.6.0 pipeline (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy), with MP strain NCTC10119 (GenBank accession number GCA_900660465.1) as the reference genome. A total of 291 MP whole-genome (WGS) datasets from NCBI, alongside four Zhejiang sequencing samples, were included in the analysis. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using Mafft [13], and phylogenetic tree was constructed by iQtree2, employing the TVM + F + ASC + R4 model. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized with the iTol web tool.

Statistical analysis

The Quantile-Quantile plot and Skewness & Kurtosis test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables [14, 15]. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All data analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Study population

During the study period from Jan, 2023 to Dec, 2023, a total of 14,907 patients with CAP were initially included in this study, following the exclusion criteria, 6,167 pediatric patients were ultimately enrolled. Of these, 4347 were confirmed as MP mono infection (MP group), 1508 were identified as MP and a single virus infection (MP + Virus group), 269 were diagnose with MP and a mixed viral infection (MP + Mixed viruses group). These groups accounted for 70.49%, 24.45%, and 5.06% of all cases, respectively (Fig. 1B). Their demographic data are shown in Table 1. The age of MP group, MP + Virus group, and MP + Mixed viruses group was 6.4 ± 2.89, 4.95 ± 2.80, and 3.97 ± 2.65 (± SD, years). Significant differences were observed among the groups, indicating that the children of MP with viral infection were younger than those with MP mono-infection. There was no significant difference of gender among these groups (Table 1).

Seasonality and pathogens co-detected with MP

Figure 1A and D showed the total monthly number of cases of pneumonia hospitalizations and pathogens distribution, respectively. The number of cases of MP mono infection per month exhibited a notable increase from May, reaching a peak in July and August, and subsequently declining after September. MPP with viral co-infection cases continued to increase almost year-round, peaking in the winter months. The epidemics of MPP with or without virus persisted for approximately 8 months (Fig. 1A). HRV was the most frequently detected virus, accounting for 46% of cases of MP/viral co-infection cases (Fig. 1C). MP + HRV-positive cases began to increase in May and peaked in November (Fig. 1D). Among the patients with MP/viral co-infection, the positive rates of HAVD, HPIV, Inf, HRSV, HPMV, and Boca were 17%, 11%, 9%, 8%, 5%, and 4%, respectively (Fig. 1C). MP/HAVD and MP/HPIV cases began to rise from June, reaching a peak in December and July, respectively. The onset of the influenza season was observed in October, with the highest number of MP + Inf cases occurring in December. HRSV cases was raised dramatically from June, peaked in September, and then declined in October and December. However, the detection of the other respiratory pathogens remained at a relatively low level during the same period (Fig. 1D). The monthly prevalence of pathogens exhibited a similar pattern in the MP + Virus group as observed among the entire case population (Fig. 1E). Figure 1F showed mixed virus species with MP co-infection, exhibiting a similar proportion to that observed in all cases.

Clinical characteristics and laboratory data of MPP with or without viral co-infection

Table 1 showed the clinical manifestations for all the groups, all the patients enrolled in this study had fever and cough. The febrile duration before administration of each group was 5.84 ± 2.83 days for the MP group, 5.53 ± 3.66 days for the MP + Virus group, and 5.06 ± 4.27 days for the MP + Mixed viruses group. MP mono-infection patients exhibited a significant longer febrile duration in comparison to the other study groups. However, the trend of cough duration before administration was opposite. The cough duration in the MP group (6.23 ± 3.10 days) was significantly shorter than in the MP-viral co-infection group (6.76 ± 3.97 days for MP + Virus; 8.03 ± 5.21 days for MP + Mixed virus).

Only 2.6% of children infected with MP alone had wheezing symptoms, whereas more cases of wheezing were observed in patients with co-infection of MP and viruses (8.3% in the MP + Virus group; 10.3% in the MP + Mixed Viruses group). There were no significant differences in the duration of wheezing observed across these groups. The incidence of severe pneumonia was significantly higher in MP mono cohort compared to those with the MP/viral co-infection (19.7% vs. 16.2% vs. 8.9%). Bronchoscopy was utilized as a therapeutic intervention for the treatment of severe pneumonia. It was most frequently used in the MP group (17.1%) and least used in the MP + Mixed virus group (4.1%). The frequency trend was consistent with the severity of pneumonia in these three groups. The Azithromycin treatment in the three groups was 3.26 ± 1.39, 3,15 ± 1.41, and 3.08 ± 1.26 days, respectively, with no significant intergroup difference. The LoS in all the groups showed no significant difference.

Table 2 showed laboratory data of three groups. Of the three groups, the MP group exhibited the highest level of CRP, while the MP + Mixed viruses group showed the lowest CRP level. The serum WBC counts of the children in the MP group were lower than those in the virus co-infection groups. Conversely, the neutrophil levels were noted to be higher than those in the other groups. The Hb concentrations observed in all groups were found to be approximately similar, however, a slight statistical elevation was noted in the MP group in comparison to the viral co-infection group. The platelet count, PCT and LDH levels were higher in children in the MP + Mixed virus group compared to the MP mono. The D-D levels were higher in the MP group than in the other groups.

Clinical features of MPP with different viral co-infection

In order to elucidate the clinical manifestations of MPP co-infected with different viruses, we analyzed the data from the patients of MP infected with pathogens such as HRV, HADV, HPIV, Inf, and RSV (Table 3). There were no significant gender differences in all these cases, and viral co-infections with MP occurred in younger children, except for influenza. The duration of fever was shorter in the MP + HRV and MP + RSV groups than in the MP group, whereas it was longer in the MP + Inf than in the MP mono group. Cough duration was significantly longer in each viral co-infection than in the MP mono-infection group. The proportion of cases presenting with wheezing was higher for HRV, HPIV, and RSV than for MP. In addition, the duration of wheezing was significantly longer in the MP + RSV group than in the MP group, but there was no difference in the wheezing duration between the other virus groups and the MP group. The proportion of severe pneumonia and bronchoscopy treatment was significantly lower in the MP + RSV group than in the MP group. The administration of Azithromycin and LoS in all cases demonstrated no statistically significant difference.

Serum cytokine levels in children with MPP with or without viral co-infection

Serum samples from all the children were determined for cytokine levels, including IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IFN-γ, IFN-α, and TNF-α (Figs. 2 and 3). We observed that IL-6 level in the MP + Mixed viruses group was significantly decreased compared to the MP and MP + Virus group (14.8 ± 8.16 vs. 22.6 ± 18.7 vs. 21.3 ± 17.0pg/mL, *,**p < 0.01). MP group showed an increased level of IL-8 expression (28.1(18.4,39.4) vs. 23.1(14.9,38.6) vs. 30.0(12.0,30.3)pg/mL, *,***p < 0.01) and decreased IL-10 level (12.1 ± 7.94 vs. 15.4 ± 12.0 vs. 15.6 ± 10.9pg/mL, *,***p < 0.01) than the other two groups. There were no significant differences of the other cytokine levels among these groups. MP + HRV co-infection showed a decreased IL-8 level compared to MP group (19.21(12.9,32.6) vs. 28.1(18.4,39.4) pg/mL, ###p < 0.01). IL-10 levels in MP with HADV (21.0 ± 15.4 pg/mL) or Inf group (19.9 ± 16.4 pg/mL,) were elevated than MP mono infection (12.1 ± 7.94 pg/mL, ###p < 0.01). MP + HADV showed a significantly higher IFN-γ level than MP (10.0(5.84,18.2) vs. 6.89(4.8,10.2) pg/mL, ###p < 0.01). The TNF-α level was increased in the MP + Inf group (3.30 ± 1.21 U/mL) but decreased in the MP + RSV group(2.99 ± 0.96 U/mL), comparing with MP group (4.08 ± 1.70 U/mL, ###p < 0.01).

The macrolide resistance-associated mutation analysis of MP

A total of 635 BALF specimens were positive for MP DNA detection, 459 samples were measured for drug resistance analysis by using tNGS. Among these, 340 cases (340/459, 74.07%) harbored an A2063G mutation with macrolide-resistant MP (MRMP), 119 samples (119/459, 25.93%) were infected with sensitive MP (Table 4).

In order to investigate the change in drug resistance-associated mutation of MP over recent years, WGS analysis of 23 MP strains from different regions in mainland of China was conducted (Fig. 4). The macrolide resistance in MP is closely associated with mutations in the V domain of the 23 S rRNA gene. WGS of the total 23 MP strains revealed that the resistance was specifically linked to mutations at the 2063 binding site for macrolide antibiotics, including A2063G and A2063T. Among the 4 strains from Zhejiang, 2 showed mutations at the 2063 site, while in Beijing, 18 out of 19 strains had mutations at this same site. No mutations were found at the 2064, 2067, or 2617 loci in any of the isolates.

Multi-Locus sequence typing MLST) analysis

We collected MP strains from Beijing (n = 19), Japan (n = 72), South Korea (n = 30), and Taiwan (n = 96) from the NCBI database and conducted whole-genome multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) analysis (Fig. 5). These strains included globally prevalent sequence type (ST) 3; ST17 from Taiwan, South Korea, and Japan; ST1 from Beijing and Zhejiang; ST30 from Beijing; ST2 from Japan and Taiwan; ST7, ST16, ST47, and ST8 from Japan; ST14 from Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan; ST33 from South Korea; as well as 6 new sequence types. All 4 strains from Zhejiang were classified as the globally prevalent ST3.

Whole genome SNP phylogenetic analysis

To gain a deeper understanding of MP genotypes, we performed whole-genome SNP phylogenetic analysis on 221 MP isolates using the M129-B7 genome as a reference. The phylogenetic tree was primarily divided into four clades (Fig. 6). Clade 1: Predominantly non-Chinese regions (including Seoul, Japan, and Taiwan), with ST14 and ST2. Among 34 strains, 5 strains had mutations at the 2063 site of the 23 S rRNA gene. Clade 2: Mainly from Japan, with ST7. Among 23 strains, 4 strains had mutations at the 2063 and 2617 sites of the 23 S rRNA gene. Clade 3: Included strains from all regions, with ST17 being the most common, followed by ST3 and ST1. Of the 59 strains, 33 strains had mutations at the 2063 site of the 23 S rRNA gene. Clade 4: Included strains from all regions, with ST3. All 105 strains in this clade had mutations at the 2063 site of the 23 S rRNA gene. The two Zhejiang strains with mutations at the 2063 site were located in Clade 4, which was characterized by all strains having mutations at this site. The two Zhejiang strains without mutations at the 2063 site were located in a small branch within Clade 3, where no mutations were observed.

Discussion

MP is a common pathogen of respiratory infection, and it is responsible for 20–40% of CAP [1]. In addition to the respiratory infection, MP may also cause complications involving in multiple systems such as nervous system, cardiovascular system, and digestive system, making MP infection a public health problem to be concerned about [16]. MPP is the major form of MP infection, and the co-infection rate of MP with other pathogens was high, especially with viruses [17, 18]. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study of hospitalized children with MPP, with or without respiratory viruses between January and December in 2023, and investigated the seasonal patterns as well as clinical features of pneumonia infected with MP alone or with viral co-infection. Moreover, we collected MP tngs data from BALF samples and analyzed MP WGS data from a public database for investigation of MP genome, typing, and drug resistance.

MP infection occur in different seasons, and may increase in summer, autumn, and winter seasons [19, 20]. This study showed that MP infection in Wenzhou occurred throughout the year and revealed a higher prevalence between June and December, ranging from summer to winter, showing a consistent seasonal distribution with the previous studies. Virus co-infection cases consecutively increased throughout the entire year with different intensities, showing a peak in November. Almost viral pathogens (e.g. HADV, HRSV, Inf, HPIV) in our study were highly detected in either autumn or winter, excepted for HRV. HRV was the most frequent detected virus in our study, which was consistent with the results of other studies [21]. Additionally, HRV infection began from May, which was earlier than other virus onsets. Berginc et al. reported the circulation of HRV with clear seasonal cycles, resulting in two seasonal waves with peaks in spring and autumn [22], but our study showed a peak of HRV in winter. We suggested that it was possibly due to regional or environmental factors.

Hwang et al. indicated that the rates of viral infections and co-infections were higher in younger patients in pediatrics [23]. And it has been reported that children with viral co-infection are more common in children aged under 3 years of age with CAP [24]. In our study, we also found that MP-viral co-infection (e.g. HRV, HADV, HPIV, RSV) occurred in younger children compared to MP mono infection, the children of MP with mixed viruses were the youngest among all the groups (Tables 1 and 3). PCT has been widely used in identifying infectious diseases and it is showed that serum PCT is a risk factor for prolonged fever in children with MP [25]. PCT levels in MP + Mixed viruses were higher than in MP mono or single virus co-infection, but no significant difference was observed between MP and MP + Virus (Table 1). However, our data showed febrile duration of MPP was shortened with increasing viral species co-infection (MP + Mixed viruses < MP + Virus < MP mono) (Table 1).

We suggested that this was due to the imbalance of systemic pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine levels in these groups. IL-6, IL-8, IFN-γ and TNF-α, the well-known inflammatory mediators derived from macrophages, are considered to be the neutrophil chemoattractant involved in fever [26–30]. They participate in neutrophil recruitment and promote inflammation at the site of infection. Our study found that the MP mono group had a higher level of IL-6, IL-8, and neutrophil percentage compared to the MP-viral or mixed virus groups. Additionally, MP mono group showed a lower level of IL-10 (Fig. 2), which is known as an anti-inflammatory cytokine [31]. These altered cytokine levels explained the shorter febrile duration in MP than in viral co-infection, especially in HRV and RSV co-infection. This was also evidenced by reduced levels of IL-8 and TNF-a in MP + HRV and MP + RSV, respectively (Fig. 3). On the contrary, Inf co-infection extended febrile duration. Even though TNF-a and IL-10 levels were both elevated in MP + Inf children, the combined effect of these cytokines resulted in pro-inflammation and lead to a prolonged fever duration. HADV co-infection caused a slightly longer fever duration but no statistically significant difference (Table 3), which may be attributed to the balance of pro-inflammatory (e.g. IFN-y) and anti-inflammatory (e.g. IL-10) mediator secretions.

We also observed increased levels of CRP and D-D in the MP mono group compared with the viral co-infected groups (Table 2). CRP is an acute-phase reactant protein that is primarily induced by the IL-6 action on the gene responsible for CRP transcription during the acute phase of an infectious process [32]. D − dimer is a degradation product of cross-linked fibrin and its elevation indicates the presence of thrombosis and secondary fibrinolysis in the blood, which may reflect the coagulation function and fiber activity of the body [33]. Recent studies revealed it increased in the severe CAP [34]. It had shown that serum biomarkers such as CRP, IL-6, and D-D were predictive of severe MPP in children [35]. Studies indicated that LDH level (>410 U/L) was a predictor in children with severe MPP [36]. As this value was beyond LDH average value from all the groups in our study (Table 2), we suggested that slightly increased LDH level in viral co-infection was not strongly corelated to severe pneumonia. Taken together, these data suggested that single MP caused pneumonia might be severer than viral co-infected pneumonia.

Indeed, we found that the proportion of severe pneumonia cases was significantly higher in children with MP mono infection than in children with MP + Virus or MP + Mixed viruses (Table 1). The ratio of cases requiring bronchoscopy showed a similar trend in the three groups. These findings suggested that viral co-infection attenuated MPP which seemed to be unreasonable and confused us. We hypothesized that the presence of virus may influence MP pathogenicity through interfering with its replication. Viral co-infection occurred in younger children, which was confirmed by our data. Wujun Jiang et al. determined that high bacterial load of MP was mainly in the absence of other respiratory viral or bacterial infections, whereas low bacterial load of MP was detected more often in viral co-infection, suggesting that the co-infection of respiratory viruses may reduce MP colonization [37]. Furthermore, we also assumed that a proper immunoregulation was evoked in response to the viral and MP infection. Regulatory T (Treg) cells mitigate inflammation through IL-10 [38]. IL-10 plays a role in alleviating lung injury in a viral-bacterial coinfection model [39]. Increased levels of IL-10 was found in the coinfection with virus and MP [40]. Our data showed a elevated level of IL-10 in MP/viral co-infection (Fig. 2), This may explain the milder clinical manifestations of MPP in viral co-infection in our study. It was particularly supported by the comparison of MP mono with RSV co-infection, represented by a lower proportion of severe MPP and bronchoscope treatment in MP + RSV (Table 3). For other viruses such as HRV, HADV, HPIV, and Inf, there was no difference of viral co-infection in MPP severity. Generally, our data showed that viral co-infection may not exacerbate MPP.

Notably, MP with viral co-infection had a prolonged cough duration compared with MP mono (Table 2). This finding suggested that the combination of virus and MP caused a severer airway damage. Most virus infections are self-limiting, but viral particles associated proteins, lipids, or small molecules trigger inflammatory cytokines release, which may contribute to airway hyperreactivity [41].

In our study, more wheezing cases were observed in patients co-infected with HPIV, HRV and RSV (Table 3). HPIV infection has been reported as a cause of chronic wheezing, and children with HPIV-induced bronchiolitis may go on to develop chronic asthma due to virus-initiated immune reprogramming [42].

HRV and RSV infections were often found to be associated with wheezing [43, 44]. Our data showed that RSV co-infection had a significant longer wheezing duration than MP mono. Evidence showed that cytokines such as IL-3, IL-12p40, IL-25 and IL-33 may be involved in the promotion of type 2 inflammation after HRV or RSV infection, which was correlated with wheezing [45, 46]. Generally, virus induces damage to airway epithelium and aberrant inflammatory responses, which may play roles in MP infection. The increase of pathogen load in children with MP can cause the overactivation of inflammatory response [47]. Dysregulation of the immune effectors may contribute to co-infection of virus and it possibly promote MP sterile sites within the airway [48]. Taken together, these findings suggested that virus co-infection exacerbated bronchial and bronchioles damage, and directly or indirectly induced airway hypersensitivity, resulting in persistent cough in MPP.

MPP cases in 2023 were much higher than in 2022 globally [49, 50]. We wondered whether new mutations of MP had occurred during the epidemic. Our data showed that most MP-positive samples harbored A2063G mutation (Table 4). Dekyi et al. also reported that predominance of the same mutant strains in the MP epidemic in children in Wuhan, China [51].

The A2063G mutation in the 23 S rRNA gene effectively blocks the action of macrolide antibiotics, such as azithromycin, without significantly affecting the survival or replication ability of MP [52]. In China, the widespread clinical use of macrolide antibiotics provides strong selective pressure favoring strains with the A2063G mutation [53]. The dominance of the A2063G mutation reflects its stability and advantage under high selective pressure from macrolide use. Under such intense selection pressure, the A2063G mutant strain dominates, reducing the likelihood of new mutations emerging. MP has a relatively small genome with a low mutation rate. This genomic characteristic may limit the emergence of new resistance-associated mutations. New mutations may impose higher biological costs, such as reduced growth rates or weakened adaptation to the host. As a result, these mutations fail to gain an evolutionary advantage. The A2063G mutation may represent an optimal resistance mutation.

To better understand the genome of local and global MP strains, we constructed a whole-genome SNP phylogenetic tree and analyzed the MLST based on the data from NCBI. In the analysis of phylogenetic tree, we found that Clade 4 consisted mainly of strains from Taiwan, while Clade 2 consisted mainly of strains from Japan. MRMP strains were found in Clade 3–4, and some unclassified branches (Fig. 6). This suggested that MRMP strains were independently selected from various genetic backgrounds of MP due to macrolide usage, rather than through clonal spread of a single MRMP strain.

In countries with high rates of macrolide resistance, clonal expansion of ST3 was most common [54]. ST3 has been considered a globally successful clone and was prevalent globally [55]. Chen et al. had reported that ST3 was the predominant MP strain, and it had been circulating in China since 2022 [56]. We found 4 strains from Zhejiang were classified as the ST3 (Fig. 5). Strains with similar origins (e.g., similar isolation dates or geographical locations) tend to cluster together. Here, we suspected that the predominant strains of MP in our study remains ST3, continued molecular surveillance is necessary to monitor those trends.

Conclusion

Our study analyzed the clinic features of MPP with or without viral co-infection from Jan to Dec, 2023 in Wenzhou. Most of the MP strains in our research carried the 2063G mutation, which is associated with macrolide resistance. MP infection occurred throughout the year and achieved a peak in winter, almost viral pathogens were detected in autumn and winter. MP/Viral co-infection was observed in younger children than MP mono infection. HRV was the most frequent detected virus in our study. MP mono infection caused longer fever duration and higher percentage of severe pneumonia cases by comparison to viral co-infection. However, more wheezing cases and prolonged cough periods were observed in viral co-infection (e.g. HPIV, HRV, and RSV). These findings illustrated that viral co-infection may not exacerbate severity of MPP, but aggravated MP associated bronchi and bronchioles damage.Effective clinical management requires not only antimicrobial therapy, but also functional approaches (e.g., controlling airway hyperreactivity and monitoring lung function), which are crucial for preventing chronic airway remodeling and asthma development. Although viral co-infection was associated with prolonged cough and wheezing, it appeared to attenuate the severity of MPP compared to MP mono-infection. These findings underscore the complex interplay between pathogens and highlight the need for context-specific clinical management strategies.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center, retrospective study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, viral load and MP bacterial burden were not quantitatively assessed, which would have provided further insight into the interplay between co-infecting pathogens. Third, due to the retrospective design, some clinical parameters (e.g., duration of wheezing post-discharge) could not be fully captured. Finally, the findings rely on hospital-based data and may not reflect community-acquired infection patterns. Future multicenter, prospective studies are warranted to validate these results.
