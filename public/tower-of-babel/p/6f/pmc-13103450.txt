A fluorescent probe under the microscope showing dual recognition of B-DNA and G-quadruplex DNA
Authors: Lorenzo Gramolini, Richard López-Corbalán, Marco Marazzi, Cristina García-Iriepa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The use of small molecules as fluorescent DNA markers has been extensively explored since the mid-1960s, revealing limitations in designing selective probes due to the largely missing relationship between probe structure and DNA conformation. Here, we present a multistep computational protocol applied to a recently proposed fluorescent marker, QCy(MeBT)₃, capable of simultaneously recognizing both B-DNA and G-quadruplex DNA through distinct emission signatures. Our protocol identifies the conformations that enable selective probe–DNA binding and predicts a remarkably high affinity. Moreover, these same conformations are responsible for the different absorption and fluorescence shifts observed experimentally upon binding to B-DNA or G-quadruplex. Overall, we establish a fundamental principle for predicting the performance of fluorescent probes in complex biological environments: the properties of specific molecular conformations determine their photobiophysical fate upon binding to a given DNA sequence, and thus their ability to recognize a particular DNA topology.

Introduction

DNA recognition and selectivity are fundamental for understanding the structural diversity of nucleic acids and their role in regulating biological function1. Precise molecular recognition enables the development of precise probes and therapeutic agents that can distinguish between different DNA conformations2. This capability is critical not only for elucidating genome organization and dynamics3 but also for advancing biomedical applications including diagnostics, drug delivery, and cancer therapy4. Different DNA conformations can be selectively targeted depending on the biological function of interest. In addition to double-stranded DNA—which includes the B, A, and Z forms (with B-DNA being the most common)—non-canonical structures are also relevant. Among these, G-quadruplex DNA (G4), formed by stacked guanine tetrads and commonly found in telomeres and gene promoters, has attracted significant attention due to its high relevance in potential anticancer therapies5,6.

The use of small molecules for DNA recognition and selectivity has been a strategy attempted for many years, dating back to the 1950s, when actinomycin, mitomycin, and other intercalators, were introduced as chemotherapeutic agents7. Since then, the field of DNA-binding small molecules is still considerably active, with substantial progress over the decades8,9. Usually, these molecules are categorized by their DNA-binding modes, such as major and minor groove binders, intercalators, or operating by top (also called end) stacking. Nevertheless, this poses the attention solely on the type of binding mode, lacking information about the probe’s structure. Common structural categories include planar molecules, tweezer-shaped molecules, and star-shaped molecules. Structure-function relationships indicate that groove binders have usually curved shapes matching the DNA groove contour, intercalators include planar aromatic rings, tweezer-shaped molecules often show induced-fit binding, and star-shaped molecules can allow multi-site DNA recognition2,10.

Small molecules can enable various detection methods, with emissive spectroscopic signatures—such as fluorescence and phosphorescence—being commonly used. This requires the molecular probe to be a chromophore with an absorption wavelength ideally falling within the so-called therapeutic window (650–950 nm) to ensure optimal tissue penetration. This, in turn, would lead to emission at longer wavelengths in the near-infrared region, which is typically required for in vivo detection, due to reduced light absorption and autofluorescence by tissues11,12. Regarding the type of emission, fluorescence is usually preferred over phosphorescence because, when properly tuned, it provides a more intense signal, a particularly desirable feature when strong emission is needed to overcome low signal-to-noise ratios in living tissue13.

Fluorescent molecules can therefore probe DNA structures by altering their emission spectrum upon forming a molecule–DNA complex, either enhancing or quenching the fluorescence intensity relative to the free probe in solution. Additionally, a bathochromic (red-) or hypsochromic (blue-) shift of the emission peak could also occur. Most known DNA probes fall into the so-called switch-on (or light-up/turn-on/fluorogenic) category, meaning that fluorescence is enhanced upon binding. This is due to important benefits compared to switch-off probes: an improved signal-to-noise ratio, no need for washing steps in detection assays, and live-cell imaging14. Relevant examples of switch-on probes are molecular beacon probes15,16, requiring hybridization to a specific DNA target; cyanine-based intercalators, as YOYO-117–19 and TOTO-120, but also groove binders as QCy–DT. The latter is the abbreviation of quinone cyanine-dithiazole and has shown a remarkable enhancement of fluorescence in the presence of A/T (adenine/thymine)-rich double-stranded B-DNA21. Interestingly, an evolution of QCy–DT, including one more thiazole group bound to the quinone core (Fig. 1), named QCy(MeBT)₃ (MeBT: N-methylbenzothiazolium), resulted in the first experimentally reported switch-on fluorescent probe capable of distinguishing between two distinct DNA conformations—double-stranded B-DNA and G4—by emitting different fluorescence colors22. This provides a compelling tool for advancing the elucidation of DNA structural patterns. Nevertheless, the mechanism of action of QCy(MeBT)₃ remains largely unknown, lacking a detailed mechanistic, structural, and electronic explanation. In this work, we aim to address this gap by applying a purposefully designed multistep simulation approach that integrates multiple levels of theory and computational techniques (Scheme 1): at first, we hypothesized that the star-shaped architecture of QCy(MeBT)₃ does not merely provide three conjugated branches but rather introduces conformational flexibility within each branch. Given that the probe–DNA binding modes were initially proposed considering only one possible QCy(MeBT)₃ conformation for the spectra simulation, we performed separate molecular docking of each chemically relevant probe’s conformation, to obtain plausible initial complex structures for subsequent MD simulations on both ground and excited electronic states. The structural insights derived from these simulations were then used to evaluate binding energies and, by applying a hybrid quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) approach, to calculate the absorption and fluorescence spectra (see “Methods” section and Supporting Information (SI), §1). These computed spectra can be directly compared to the available experimental data22, that is, the spectroscopic signatures of the QCy(MeBT)₃ probe.

Our results reveal a subtle interplay between the conformational landscape of the QCy(MeBT)₃ probe and its selective recognition of a specific DNA form. Indeed, conformational interconversion of the parent QCy–DT was shown to determine an induced-fit mechanism, already suggesting the relevance of the inner flexibility of this type of molecule, even when including only two branches23. Ultimately, our results indicate the structural and electronic factors that lead to distinct absorption and emission spectra when the probe is free in solution or complexed with either B-DNA or G4.

Results and discussion

In Fig. 1 it is shown the chemical structure of QCy(MeBT)₃ including the four different torsional isomers that each of the three branches can adopt: A, B, C, D. This should result in 43 (i.e., 64) molecular conformations, but the symmetry axis offered by the quinone ring along the C–O bond determines that the number of chemically equivalent conformations is 32 (i.e., the half). The full conformation of a molecule is described by a three-letter code indicating the isomer adopted at each branch, in numerical order. For example, compound ACB corresponds to QCy(MeBT)₃ with isomer A at branch 1, isomer C at branch 2, and isomer B at branch 3 (all structures are depicted in SI, §2).

QCy(MeBT)₃ is initially released in a water environment, before reaching contact with DNA. Among the 32 conformers available, after implicit solvation calculations, ADD results in the most thermodynamically stable and accounts for ~80% of the relative population (see SI, §3, Fig. S7A). To have a kinetic picture of QCy(MeBT)₃ before reaching DNA, a simulation of 300 ns of the probe alone in water was performed for each conformer, showing how most of the thermodynamically less stable conformers end up rotating to be stabilized into ADD (see SI, §3, Fig. S7B), reinforcing the idea that this specific conformer is the most probable form of the probe in water. Although with different probabilities, all QCy(MeBT)₃ conformers can, in principle, bind to DNA (G4 or B-DNA), forming an initial complex, as indicated by our docking calculations. One could reasonably think that the conformational interchange experienced by the probe in water should be (at least partially) retained once complexed with DNA. Nevertheless, we observe by molecular dynamics that such torsional flexibility is not hampered by the interacting nucleic acid, with most of the initial conformers switching to different ones, by single or multiple steps (Fig. 2A). It has to be noted that, even during the 4 ns equilibration (i.e., prior to production run), conformational exchanges may happen, especially in B-DNA, highlighting how such torsions are essentially energetically free also when the complex is stably formed. Indeed, no unbinding was experienced in any case. This raises the question of which probe conformations are preferred by G4 versus B-DNA, potentially explaining their differential selectivity. Indeed, a large portion (69%) of the overall population binds to G4 through only two conformations, ABD and DAB, while two different conformations, ADB and CDB, are almost uniquely responsible for binding to B-DNA (92% of the population), as shown by the histograms of Fig. 2B. Concerning the recognition mechanism, we notice that the most representative conformer in water (ADD) is not preferred by any of the two targets, since it is not found even as a minor binder. This suggests the following recognition mechanism: QCy(MeBT)₃ in water stays indeed most of its time as ADD, isomerizing to any of the other available conformations for short times. Once G4 and B-DNA are added to the water environment, the affinity toward the respective nucleic acid of some specific probe conformations (G4: ABD, DAB; B-DNA: ADB, CDB), will make possible the fast generation of the probe/DNA complex. The higher the affinity, the more the equilibrium between unbound and bound states will be displaced toward the bound state, stabilizing the complex formation.

The affinity toward both DNA targets was then evaluated in terms of the interaction energy, resulting in almost the same narrow range for G4 and B-DNA: ca. from −20 to −22 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 2B). This agrees with the available experimental data, showing similar values of apparent association constant (KA,app) for both DNA complexes: 1.1 × 107 M−1 and 0.9 × 107 M−1 for the G4 (MycG4) and B-DNA complexes, respectively22. Moreover, such values are indicative of high binding affinity. Indeed, other G4 binders known to act as remarkable G4 stabilizers, Phen-DC3 or Phen-QE8, show KA,app values in similar experimental conditions of 0.6 × 107 M−1 and 0.2 × 107 M−1, respectively24,25. Hence, QCy(MeBT)₃ could act not only as a probe (confirming the kinetic nature of the recognition mechanism) but also as a prominent DNA structural stabilizer, particularly relevant for telomeric G4 sequences in cancer cells or non-telomeric G4 sequences in gene promoters. However, care should be taken when comparing KA,app values obtained from different experimental protocols.

Another relevant aspect is the characterization of the probe/DNA binding mode. In principle, the conformational landscape offered by QCy(MeBT)₃ could determine a variety of binding modes. Contrarily to this hypothesis, a single binding mode can be defined for each of the nucleic acids (Fig. 3A and SI, §3, Fig. S8): top- (or end-) stacking for G4, and minor groove binding for B-DNA. More in detail, the quinone core and two branches are responsible for stacking with the top G-tetrad and a thymine from the loop (Fig. S8A, B), and essentially the same moiety of the probe binds also to the minor groove of B-DNA (Fig. S8C, D). This means that in B-DNA, one branch of the probe is hanging in solution, not participating in the binding process. This corresponds to the same binding mode found experimentally for its analog with only two branches (QCy–DT, mentioned in the introduction; PDB code: 6IJV), with all solution NMR structures being consistent with the CDB conformation of QCy(MeBT)₃ bound to B-DNA23. Also, when forming the QCy(MeBT)₃/G4 complex, one branch of the probe does not have a prominent binding role, although it can induce occasional weak interactions with the loop, depending on the specific probe conformation. Such similarity in the interaction contact offered by QCy(MeBT)₃ when binding to both nucleic acids can also explain the similar interaction energy values, and therefore affinities, previously discussed. Nevertheless, an overall key difference between the two types of complexes lies in the type of interaction: while B-DNA binding is mainly driven by the formation of electrostatic interactions, G4 binding requires also Van der Waals interactions, almost on the same energy scale of the electrostatic interactions (Fig. 3B). Molecularly, this can be readily explained by our simulations: on the one hand, the positive charges of the thiazole moieties are attracted to the negatively charged phosphate backbone of B-DNA; on the other hand, the probe’s aromatic groups enable stable π–π interactions with the top G-tetrad, while also contributing a partial electrostatic component similar to that in B-DNA. Concerning the photophysics of the probe/DNA complex, absorption and emission spectra were simulated considering the Boltzmann population of all probes’ conformations. A direct comparison of the normalized simulated and experimental absorption spectra is shown in Fig. 4A for the probe alone in water, in interaction with B-DNA or G4. The results indicate that our protocol can reproduce the ordering in peak intensity, with the QCy(MeBT)₃/B-DNA complex showing the highest intensity, followed by QCy(MeBT)₃ in water and finally by the QCy(MeBT)₃/G4 complex, although in this last case, a direct comparison is not possible, since no experimental absorption spectrum is available regarding the simulated G4 sequence22. For the former two cases, for which a direct comparison is possible, a quantitative agreement is found: the peak maximum of the simulated spectra is shifted by just 9 nm (∼0.04 eV) for the QCy(MeBT)₃/B-DNA complex and 3 nm (∼0.01 eV) for QCy(MeBT)₃ in water. Given the π-conjugation of the probe and its almost planarity, all electronic transitions to lower excited states (S0 → S1,2,3) are of bright π,π* nature, while higher lying excited states (S0 → S4,5) can involve dark n,π* transitions from the lone pairs of the quinone oxygen (SI, §3, Fig. S11). A deconvolution of the simulated absorption spectra in terms of most representative conformers highlights that the probe conformations, which were found to be the most representative in terms of nucleic acid recognition and affinity (G4: ABD, DAB; B-DNA: ADB, CDB), are also the ones mainly participating in the overall spectrum shape (SI, §3, Fig. S10). Interestingly, we can also interpret the absorption process in connection with the possible QCy(MeBT)₃ resonant structures (Fig. 4B): the experimental probe design focused on a donor moiety (quinone) which could allow charge transfer to each of the positively charged branches, acting as acceptors. Our calculations point toward a somewhat different picture, characterized by a limited charge transfer character, since the quinone oxygen is only partially negatively charged on the ground state, allowing mainly delocalization over only two distinct branches when populating S1. Finally, the emission properties were simulated and compared with the experimental data available, providing two different sets: excitation wavelength in the blue range (470 nm, Fig. 5A) and in the yellow region (570 nm, Fig. 5B). In this case, the agreement of the simulated spectra with the experiment is only qualitative, as it is known that emission properties often entail larger uncertainties than absorption spectra, due to the increased complexity associated with excited-state vibrational effects and geometry relaxation, even at high levels of theory28–32. Despite this, our simulations successfully reproduce the main features of both emission spectra: the emission of the complex formed with G4 is red-shifted compared to B-DNA when exciting at both 470 and 570 nm, although to a lower extent compared to the experiment. With this respect, we would like to indicate the similarity in the maximum of the experimental emission wavelength of the non-telomeric G4 (MycG4, that we have simulated, present in the promoter region of the c-MYC oncogene)33 with the human telomeric G4 (hTelo_G4)34, as depicted in Fig. 5. Interestingly, our protocol can discern the main difference between the two spectra: when exciting at 470 nm, the emission intensity of the QCy(MeBT)₃/B-DNA complex is higher than that one of the QCy(MeBT)₃/G4 complex, while the opposite is detected when exciting at 570 nm. This is due to the fact that absorption is directly related to emission: if a certain probe’s conformation absorbs more at 470 nm than at 570 nm, then its emission intensity will also be higher in the first case. Examining the contribution of the most representative probe’s conformations to the fluorescence spectra (SI, §3, Fig. S12), it can be noted that the QCy(MeBT)₃/B-DNA complex maintains almost the same intensity contribution, with ADB leading and CDB following, irrespective of the excitation wavelength. Instead, the QCy(MeBT)₃/G4 complex undergoes a switch in intensity among the two most relevant QCy(MeBT)₃ conformations: ABD and DAB. All in all, this means that the main probe’s conformations not only dictate the recognition process toward G4 or B-DNA, but also display distinct fluorescence intensities, which depend on their absorption at a given excitation wavelength.

The last fluorescence property to be discussed is the emission wavelength, and especially the red shift observed for the QCy(MeBT)₃/G4 complex compared to the QCy(MeBT)₃/B-DNA complex, leading to its characteristic dual-color fluorescence. In the following, we show how this shift can be rationalized solely on the basis of the QCy(MeBT)₃ fluorescence in water, without the need for the surrounding nucleic acid, hence again pointing toward the importance of the properties of each probe’s conformation, instead of the effects due to the binding with a specific DNA structure. Especially, in Fig. 6 it is represented the maximum fluorescence wavelength for each of the QCy(MeBT)₃ conformers in water. Among them, on one hand, the three most representative B-DNA binders—ADB, CDB, and CDD (highlighted in green in Fig. 6) show fluorescence between 607 and 616 nm, falling in the yellow color range, as expected experimentally22. On the other hand, the six most representative G4 binders (highlighted in red in Fig. 6) clearly point toward a red shift, with ca. 53% of the relative population in the range 662–671 nm, corresponding to red emission. Since environmental electrostatic effects are generally known to play a major role in solvatochromism35,36, we disentangled their contribution from intrinsic conformational effects by computing the emission spectra of a conformer (CDB) primarily responsible for the blue-shifted emission when bound to B-DNA, both in the presence and absence of the electrostatic environment. Analogously, emission spectra were computed for a conformer (DAB) mainly responsible for the red-shifted emission when bound to G4. The results (Fig. S13) clearly indicate that the observed emission colors originate predominantly from intrinsic conformational properties rather than from specific DNA interactions.

Concerning the electronic nature of the S₁ state responsible for fluorescence, we analyzed in detail the emission properties of all the main conformers identified (Fig. 2B). We find that the S₁ state is predominantly characterized by a HOMO → LUMO transition, similarly to what was observed for absorption, with only minor contributions from the HOMO → LUMO + 1 transition (corresponding to S₂ in absorption). This indicates the absence of significant S₁/S₂ mixing prior to fluorescence emission (Fig. S14).

Conclusions

In this study, we have built a computational protocol through a multistep approach to rationalize the mechanisms underlying the experimental findings of the first dual-color switch-on fluorescent probe able to bind at the same time two different DNA structures: G-quadruplexes (G4) and double-helix B-DNA. As a guideline for designing small organic molecules that act as selective fluorescent bio-probes, we highlight the fundamental importance of recognizing the possible binding modes associated with each probe conformation. This understanding can significantly aid in predicting probe selectivity toward specific DNA structures, following a lock-and-key recognition mechanism. In particular, we have identified which QCy(MeBT)₃ conformations are responsible for binding to G4 and which to B-DNA. In spite of the torsional flexibility offered by the probe, we can safely assign a distinct and stable binding mode pattern when forming a complex with G4 or B-DNA. This makes it possible to reliably calculate the interaction energy of QCy(MeBT)₃ toward both targets, finding out (after comparison with available experimental data) a remarkably high affinity, comparable with some of the most known G4 stabilizers. This could be taken into consideration for future theranostic applications, since the stabilization of telomeric G4 sequences hindering the telomerase activity could be coupled to its switch-on fluorescence properties.

Regarding solely the diagnostic application (i.e., its function as a probe), we have elucidated the basis of the absorption and emission once the ligand–DNA complexes are formed. We have shown how our protocol quantitatively reproduces the absorption spectra; moreover, underlying how the initial experimental design concept of a probe with a donor core and three acceptor branches cannot be accomplished by QCy(MeBT)₃, since a limited charge transfer from the quinone to only two branches is observed. Future investigations could therefore rely on a priori computational approaches to clarify the nature of the absorption process, considering that a red-shifted absorption usually results in a red-shifted emission, thus optimizing the design of a fluorescent probe. Moreover, we have demonstrated how the absorption intensity at a certain excitation wavelength experimentally selected can result in a different fluorescence intensity, and that this strongly depends on the specific probe’s conformation. Even more interestingly, we have shown that the fluorescence wavelengths of the different QCy(MeBT)₃ conformations in water alone account for the observed red-shift upon binding G4 instead of B-DNA. Consequently, the emission color of the two complexes depends primarily on the probe’s conformation that previously dictated recognition and binding to each biological target, rather than on the specific type of interaction between the probe and G4 or B-DNA. This rationalization, in principle, paves the way for a priori design of fluorescent probes capable of recognizing multiple targets or, conversely, for locking a specific conformation tailored to recognize a single DNA structure. The latter design could be implemented by following the same strategy experimentally applied to other well-known chromophores (stilbene37,38, azobenzene39,40, and pyrene41,42), namely the introduction of bridges (alkyl chains, polyether macrocycles, or diazamacrocycles)43, each covalently linking two arms of QCy(MeBT)₃.

Methods

The proposed computational protocol is based on a multistep approach composed of distinct stages and levels of theory:

Quantum chemistry: geometry optimization and fluorescence spectra in water

All possible QCy(MeBT)₃ conformations, avoiding artificial steric clashes, were manually generated rotating around the bonds depicted in Scheme 1. The ground state (S0) geometry of each conformer was optimized using Density Functional Theory (DFT)44 at the PBE0/6-31 + G*45–49 level of theory in an implicit water solvent using the Integral Equation Formalism variant of the Polarizable Continuum Method (IEFPCM)50 as implemented in Gaussian1651. The choice of the functional was based on previous studies21,22 of DNA-interacting molecules of this type, including the specific molecule studied here. The basis set was shown to produce results with negligible difference compared to those obtained using the basis set from the aforementioned works (6-311++G**) at a lower computational cost (see SI, §1.8). The same functional and basis set were used to optimize the geometry of each conformer in the first excited state (S1) by Time-Dependent DFT52 (TDDFT) formalism. All S0 and S1 optimized geometries were confirmed to be minima by frequency calculations, not resulting in any imaginary frequency. The relative population of each QCy(MeBT)₃ conformer in solution was calculated considering S0 Gibbs energies via a Boltzmann distribution analysis (see SI, §1.2). Starting from the S1 optimized geometry, the corrected Linear Response (cLR)53,54 formalism was used to calculate the emission wavelength. This correction adds a State Specific (SS) energy term to the standard LR formalism, accounting for the solvent’s reaction to the S1 electronic density.

Molecular docking

Blind molecular docking was conducted to obtain initial configurations for subsequent Molecular Dynamics (MD) studies. The setup of the initial B-DNA and G4 structures is explained in SI, §1.1. All the results were obtained with the AutoDock4 (AD4) package through the AutoDockTools4 interface55. Both DNA and ligand were assigned AD4 atom types and Gasteiger charges56, with non-polar hydrogens merged into heavy atoms, according to previous studies on DNA binders23,57–60. Each S0 optimized conformer was docked separately to both B-DNA and G4 structures using a rigid docking approach, with all dihedral angles kept fixed. The pose with the highest binding affinity was selected, yielding one QCy(MeBT)3/B-DNA and one QCy(MeBT)3/G4 complex for each ligand conformation. This procedure guarantees the preservation of each specific QCy(MeBT)3 conformation during the docking process. All docking parameters are given in SI, §1.3.

Ligand force field parametrization

The complete set of parameters (charges, stretching, bending, dihedrals, improper dihedral angles, and non-bonded interactions) was calculated to build unique ground state and excited state QCy(MeBT)3 force fields, each of them capable of spanning all the ligand’s conformations.

Atomic partial charges for each conformer were determined by fitting to the electrostatic potential using the Merz-Singh-Kollman scheme61,62, in accordance with previous studies63–65. The same quantum chemistry level of theory and solvent model used for geometry optimization were applied in this step, extracting S0 and S1 charges. Subsequently, charge values were averaged over all the ligand conformations, and standard deviations were computed for each atomic charge to evaluate the transferability of the derived force field across the different conformers.

Bond and angle force constants, along with their equilibrium values, were parametrized by fitting to quantum mechanical calculations at the aforementioned level by using the modified Seminario66 method, which, similar to the original Seminario approach67, utilizes projections of the Hessian matrix to derive force constants, however, offering a significant improvement in the calculation of angle force constants. As described for charges, also stretching and bending parameters were calculated for each of the ligand’s conformations and then averaged, including standard deviation analysis. To reduce the number of parameters, atoms with similar local chemical environments and partial charges were assigned to the same atom type, resulting in atoms’ groups characterized by remarkable homogeneous parameters: the ground state QCy(MeBT)3 force field offers stretching force constants and equilibrium bond lengths differing by < 3% and 1%, respectively, while bending force constants and equilibrium angles vary by less than 5%. For the excited state, additional atom types are required to enable a force field similar in quality to the ground state one: each group of heavy (non-hydrogen) atoms oscillates by < 5% in stretching force constants, < 1% in equilibrium bond lengths, < 7% in bending force constants, and < 5% in equilibrium angles.

All dihedral angles involved in rings, -NMe groups, and –C=C– bonds of each QCy(MeBT)3 branch were standardly parametrized using the Generalized AMBER68 Force Field (GAFF)69, as assigned through antechamber70 and parmchk2 programs. Especially, –C=C– bonds were assigned as conventional double bonds due to a congruent 1.37 Å optimized bond length, and significant steric hindrance upon rotation. The two remaining single bonds preceding and following the –C=C– moieties were parametrized through a previously established protocol63,71,72: a quantum chemistry relaxed scan of 360° was performed around each bond of interest, at the same level described above. Given the prohibitive computational cost of scanning all dihedral angles across all ligand’s conformations, a subset of representative conformers was selected, including only those with a rotational free environment. A single-point molecular mechanics calculation was then performed on top of each optimized scan geometry, using the force field incorporating the GAFF parameters for the dihedrals, which were not reparametrized. Finally, the difference in energy between DFT and force field (FF) contributions was calculated, and the resulting data points were fitted to the functional form of the dihedral potential used in the AMBER force field, employing the curve_fit() function from SciPy’s73 optimize module (see Scheme 1 and SI, Fig. S4). This approach thus allows an independent parametrization of a dihedral angle, supported by the observation that, during the rotation of a given bond, the other dihedral angles (not yet parameterized) remain essentially unchanged. Improper dihedral angles and non-bonded interactions were parametrized using GAFF. All force field parameters and their characterization are given in SI (§1.4), including, for validation purposes, a comparison with the S0 and S1 force fields obtained using GAFF274, a widely used and well-established small-molecule force field.

Molecular dynamics and binding energy estimation

As resulted from molecular docking, the best QCy(MeBT)3/B-DNA and QCy(MeBT)3/G4 structures were considered as initial geometries for running 300 ns MD in S0 for each QCy(MeBT)3 conformer. For comparison purposes, MD simulations were also run starting from the S0 (DFT) optimized geometry of each QCy(MeBT)3 conformer in explicit water. All calculations were performed using the GPU version of Amber2268. In all cases, the system was solvated with TIP3P75 water molecules within a truncated octahedral box, ensuring a solvent shell of at least 12 Å from any solute molecule. Due to the charge of the system, K+ ions were added to neutralize the complexes, while Cl− ions were added to neutralize QCy(MeBT)3 in explicit water. Both B-DNA and G4 were described using the OL1576 force field, whereas the ligand was described through the ab-initio parametrized force field for S0 described above. First, each binding pose was minimized for 2000 steps using the steepest-descent algorithm and then a maximum of 18,000 steps using the conjugate gradient algorithm. Then, it was progressively heated for 2 ns from 100 K to 298 K in the NVT ensemble using a 1 fs timestep controlling the temperature by means of the Langevin thermostat77 with a 1 ps-1 collision frequency. The bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm78. Afterwards, the density was equilibrated with an isotropic position scaling through a 2 ns (1 fs timestep) simulation in the NPT ensemble, using periodic boundary conditions. The temperature was controlled as in the heating process, and the pressure was kept at 1 bar by the Monte Carlo barostat79. Additional details are given in SI (§1.5 and Data S3–S6). Given the flexibility of the built ligand’s force field, any conformational transition could be allowed during MDs. These transitions were identified through a combination of visual inspection using VMD80 and analysis of the Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) relative to reference conformers. Each trajectory was then partitioned into segments according to the conformation adopted over time: when a change in conformation was detected, a new segment was defined, resulting in the subdivision of the original trajectory into distinct conformational states. This process was applied consistently across all simulations. For each segment lasting at least 70 ns, MMPBSA81–84 (Molecular Mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann Surface Area) calculations were performed. In the case of ligand/DNA complexes, this enabled the estimation of the binding Gibbs energy associated with each conformation and its specific binding mode, which could vary even for the same conformation across different MD simulations, thus allowing comparative trends among different conformers. The binding Gibbs energy of each segment was then used to build a Boltzmann distribution, necessary to estimate the relative population of each distinct QCy(MeBT)3 conformation–DNA binding mode pair, considering that a single conformation can be associated with multiple binding poses (see SI, §1.6). The simulations of the ligand in explicit water allowed to assess the relative stability of each conformation in the unbound state. Considering the large set of MD simulations performed, the use of the MMPBSA method to estimate binding Gibbs free energies represents a compromise between accuracy and computational cost, in comparison with the less accurate MMGBSA approach85 and the more rigorous but computationally prohibitive alchemical free energy methods86.

Hybrid quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics: absorption and fluorescence spectra when bound to DNA

The MD segments exhibiting the highest binding affinity were selected and, if a selected conformation–binding mode pair was described by a segment of < 300 ns, the corresponding MD simulation was extended, ensuring that no conformational change occurs for a total of 300 ns, thus consistently representing all selected segments. Additionally, for each chosen segment, a further 300 ns MD was carried out on S1 (again checking that no change in conformation occurs) using the force field built for the excited state. From each of these S0 and S1 extended trajectories, 100 equally spaced snapshots were extracted for single-point Quantum Mechanics/Molecular Mechanics (QM/MM) calculations, using Cobramm 2.087 as an interface.

QCy(MeBT)3 was treated at the QM TD-DFT level, while the surrounding DNA and solvent environment were described using MM. For the snapshots extracted from S0 simulations, the lowest five excited states (Sn, n = 1–5) were computed, and Gaussian convolution of the resulting vertical transitions (S0→Sn) across all snapshots was used to generate the simulated absorption spectra for each conformation–binding mode pair. An analogous procedure was applied to the S1 simulations, yielding the corresponding S1 → S0 emission spectra.

To obtain an absorption spectrum directly comparable with experimental results, a weighted sum of all the absorption spectra was performed, using as weights the relative populations of each conformation–binding mode pair. For the emission spectrum, an additional weight was added, i.e., the absorption oscillator strength of each conformation–binding mode pair corresponding to the excitation wavelengths used experimentally to obtain the fluorescence spectra (see SI, §1.7).

Statistical significance and error analysis

Each bond and angle force constant of the generated ligand force field was statistically evaluated in terms of standard deviation, percentage difference between the highest and lowest parameter values, and coefficient of variation (see SI, §1.4.2 and §1.4.3).

Error bars for the MMPBSA binding interaction energies (Fig. 2B) were calculated using a two-tailed statistical t-test. The resulting standard error of the mean was multiplied by two to obtain a confidence level greater than 95%. In the same Fig. 2B, error bars for the relative Boltzmann populations are not reported, as the aim of this analysis is not to establish a precise quantitative ranking of all conformations, but rather to identify the dominant binding modes of QCy(MeBT)₃ with B-DNA and G4.

Standard deviations for all contributions to the linear interaction energy analysis are reported for interactions between the ligand and selected nucleotides exhibiting the most relevant average interaction energies, corresponding to the minima observed in Fig. 3B (see SI, §3, Data S1 and S2).
