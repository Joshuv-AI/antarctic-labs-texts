Comparing Carotid Artery Velocities with Current ASCVD Risk Stratification: A Novel Approach to Simpler Risk Assessment
Authors: Yueh-Chien Lu, Po-Ju Chen, Sheng-Nan Lu, Fu-Wen Liang, Hung-Yi Chuang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo explore the potential of a novel approach to simplify risk assessment by comparing carotid artery velocities with current atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk stratification method using nonlinear measurements.MethodsIn this prospective study conducted at a medical center in southern Taiwan from January 1, 2020, to December 31, 2021, 1636 participants aged 40–75 years without prior ASCVD events were enrolled. Carotid flow velocity was obtained through duplex ultrasonography. ASCVD risk was categorized into two groups according to the 2022 USPSTF guidelines for primary prevention. We analyzed associations between flow indices and ASCVD risk using logistic regression and generalized additive models (GAMs).ResultsThe end diastolic velocity (EDV) of common carotid artery (CCA) and the peak systolic velocity (PSV) of internal carotid artery (ICA) were inversely and nonlinearly associated with cardiovascular event risk. Multivariate logistic regression analysis with ROC curves revealed that the optimal speed for the EDV of CCA was approximately 23.75 cm/s, and the optimal PSV and EDV of ICA were approximately 81.75 cm/s and 26.75 cm/s, respectively. The GAMs showed U-shaped relationships between elevated ASCVD risk and blood flow velocity in the carotid arteries, with inflection points of approximately 82 cm/s in the PSV of ICA and near 25 cm/s in the EDV of CCA. Both methods revealed similar results.ConclusionsThe EDVs and PSVs of the CCA and ICA are associated with the development of cardiovascular events. Optimal velocity ranges were identified; however, further hemodynamic investigations are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-024-00308-3.

Introduction

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is a major cause of death worldwide, and myocardial infarction and stroke compose 85% of cardiovascular events [1, 2]. In 2020, Taiwan faced a significant CVD burden, with 70,618 deaths attributed to heart and cerebrovascular diseases, accounting for 40.3% of total deaths [3]. Precise estimation of the risk for the first cardiovascular event is important and desirable for prevention and treatment. Since the 1961 Framingham Heart Study first identified ‘risk factors’ for CHD [4], a number of region-specific risk models have emerged to estimate the risk for CVD in asymptomatic individuals.

In addition to the typical risk factors for cardiovascular diseases in various calculators, carotid artery intima-media thickness (CIMT) and carotid plaque are associated with the atherosclerotic process [5, 6]. They have been proposed as potential tools for ASCVD risk stratification [7, 8]. Carotid duplex ultrasound (CDUS), a noninvasive and radiation-free tool, not only measures CIMT and plaque but also provides valuable information about carotid artery velocity. Peak systolic velocity (PSV) and end-diastolic velocity (EDV) are commonly utilized parameters that are derived from CDUS [9, 10]. From a fluid mechanics standpoint, there is a correlation between increased carotid artery velocities and vascular stenosis [11, 12], which could lead to the development and progression of ASCVD. Conversely, decreased carotid flow velocities, particularly a reduction in EDV, may indicate compromised blood flow and vascular dysfunction, contributing to the risk of cardiovascular events [13, 14]. And compared to other measurable blood flow indicators, the significance of PSV and EDV lies in their ability to provide clear, quantifiable, and reproducible measures that directly correlate with the degree of stenosis.

Carotid ultrasound is currently used as a tool to assess vascular stenosis but is not considered essential for the assessment of cardiovascular disease or in decision-making frameworks for primary prevention. We hypothesize that abnormalities in carotid artery flow velocity are specifically related to ASCVD and that this relationship goes beyond the currently recognized phenomenon of co-stenosis leading to increased flow velocity. This study aimed to examine how the velocity parameters of the carotid artery are connected to the 10-year risk of cardiovascular disease. To achieve this goal, we utilized a generalized additive model (GAM) to explore the possibility of a nonlinear relationship and a possible threshold.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study included participants aged 40–75 years who underwent health examinations at a medical center in southern Taiwan between January 1, 2020, and December 31, 2021. The participants did not exhibit symptoms and were free of baseline ASCVD, including acute coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, stable or unstable angina, coronary artery disease (with or without recanalization), recanalization of other arteries, stroke, or peripheral arterial disease caused by atherosclerosis at the time of recruitment. Currently taking anticoagulant treatment but had not disclosed their history of cardiovascular disease were also excluded. A total of 1636 participants who met the Pool Cohort Equation (ACC/AHA, 2013) criteria were enroll. Included in the analysis were those with systolic blood pressure (SBP) within 90–200 mmHg, total cholesterol within 130–320 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) within 20–100 mg/dL, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) less than 190 mg/dL. The inclusion criteria for blood pressure and lipid profile status for the PCE can be found using the online ACC/AHA ASCVD Risk Estimator (https://tools.acc.org/ldl/ascvd_risk_estimator/index.html#!/calulate/estimator/). Our research was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Medical Foundation (No. 202200168B0).

Demographic information, medical history, current cigarette smoking status, alcohol consumption status, and educational level were collected using a questionnaire. Educational levels were categorized into three categories: elementary or below, high school or equivalent, and bachelor’s degree or higher. Participants who consumed alcohol frequently (> 3 days/week) were categorized as drinkers. Cigarette smoking is classified as a binary factor: an individual is either a current smoker or a ‘non-current smoker,’ defined as someone who has not smoked cigarettes in the past 30 days.

Measurement of 10-year ASCVD Risk and Classification

We used the Pooled Cohort Equations CV Risk Calculator (ACC/AHA, 2013) to estimate the 10-year cardiovascular disease risk. The predictors included SBP, lipid profiles, smoking status, diabetes status, and hypertension medication use. Participants were divided into four groups based on risk reduction recommendations from the 2018 Cholesterol Clinical Practice Guidelines [15] and the 2019 ACC/AHA Guidelines on the Risk Management of Cardiovascular Disease [16]: low risk (< 5%), borderline risk (5% to < 7.5%), intermediate risk (7.5% to < 20%) and high risk (≥ 20%). The 2019 ACC/AHA guidelines recommend statin therapy for individuals with LDL-C levels ≥ 190 mg/dL, those diagnosed with diabetes, and individuals with an estimated 10-year ASCVD risk of 7.5% or higher.

In contrast, the 2022 United States Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) suggests that health care professionals recommend cholesterol management for individuals with a 10% or greater risk of CVD and one or more risk factors, such as dyslipidemia, diabetes, hypertension, or smoking [17]. The expected benefit of statin therapy for persons with a 10-year CVD risk of 10% or greater exceeds the expected benefit for persons with a 10-year CVD risk of 7.5% to less than 10%. So that we classified participants into 2 classifications based on the USPSTF criteria for lipid-lowering drugs: high-risk for treatment, low-risk not requiring immediate intervention or needing further discussion.

Carotid Ultrasonography Measurement

An ultrasound instrument (Philips HD15®) with a high-resolution linear broadband array transducer was used to examine the bilateral carotid arteries. Participants were supine, and the exams were conducted by experienced neurologists following the standardized protocol [18]. The inspection included two parts: the use of grayscale B-mode to distinguish the intima-media thickness in the common carotid artery (CIMT), which is a 1-centimeter-long straight section of the main carotid artery proximal to the bifurcation [19], and assessing the presence of plaque, defined as a maximum IMT > 1.5 mm, according to the American Society of Echocardiography and the Mannheim consensus [20, 21]. The blood flow velocity in the common carotid artery (CCA), internal carotid artery (ICA), and external carotid artery (ECA) was measured by spectral pulse-wave Doppler. The maximal blood flow velocity in the pulsatile vascular system (i.e., PSV) and the minimal blood flow velocity (i.e., EDV) were recorded. The means values obtained from the right and left sides were used in our analysis. Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated to summarize the study population characteristics. Continuous variables are shown as the mean ± SD, while categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to assess the relationships between different carotid artery flow indices. Collinearity diagnostics were performed before regression analysis to ensure precision and reliability. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to investigate the associations between estimated ASCVD risk and blood flow velocity in the carotid arteries. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

To investigate the association between carotid artery velocity and categorical cardiovascular disease risk (binary outcome), we employed a generalized additive model (GAM). This model utilized a smooth function to additively incorporate variables, enabling the establishment of connections between the predictors and the response variable. The effective degree of freedom (EDF) value was used to assess whether the relationships were linear or nonlinear. When the EDF is 1, the smoothing function operates akin to a linear regression model, signifying a straightforward linear relationship between the predictor variable and the response variable. We performed statistical analyses using SPSS software, Windows version 20.0 (Chicago, IL, USA), and constructed the GAM by utilizing R 4.2.0 software with the mgcv package [22].

Results

Participant Characteristics

Among the 1,636 participants without prior stroke or heart events, the mean age was 55.8 years (SD = 8.4) years, and 60.9% were male. Those at a higher 10-year risk for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease were predominantly male and older, with greater alcohol consumption, current-smoking status, lower education, and elevated blood pressure and blood sugar levels. However, there was no significant difference in parental history of cardiovascular events (Table 1).

Correlation and Collinearity Diagnostics between Carotid Artery Velocity and Resistance Indices

First, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess the relationships between the CIMT and each velocity measurement in the CCA, ICA and ECA. Significant but relatively weak correlations were observed (r ≤.3), except for those between CIMT and PSV in the ECA, which were not significantly correlated with each other. Notably, the PSV and EDV in the CCA, ICA, and ECA were positively correlated with MV. Positive associations were found between the PI and the RI in all three arteries, indicating a close relationship between these resistance indices. Given that MV is a composite measure dependent on both PSV and EDV, its inclusion might introduce redundancy in the model, potentially inflating multicollinearity. Similarly, PI is strongly associated with RI, and including both indices could lead to overlapping information. To ensure reliable estimates and minimize potential issues with high standard errors, we excluded MV and the PI from further analysis, focusing solely on PSV, EDV, and the RI.

A lower correlation coefficient indicates a lower likelihood of collinearity (but does not guarantee its absence) among independent variables. To maintain the integrity of the regression analysis, we performed collinearity diagnostics for PSV, EDV, and the RI to identify collinearity. Given our focus on blood flow velocities, and since MV, PI and RI, which depend on PSV and EDV, have limited effectiveness in multivariate, particularly nonlinear models. Therefore, we excluded the RI from the analysis. Table S1 presents the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance values, which demonstrates that multicollinearity is more pronounced when considering PSV, EDV, and RI together across the carotid arteries. Following RI exclusion, the variance inflation factors were calculated, ranging from 1.67 for ECA-EDV to 2.68 for ICA-PSV. Based on Pearson correlation and collinearity analyses, PSV and EDV were identified as reliable independent variables for exploring 10-year ASCVD risk. Please refer to Table S2 in the supplemental materials for more information.

Multicollinearity among Risk Factors Included the Pooled Cohort Equation and Variables of those Not Enrolled

In our analysis, we employed the Pooled Cohort Equation to estimate the 10-year cardiovascular disease risk, considering continuous variables such as age, SBP, total cholesterol, and HDL-C. To avoid any potential collinearity issues between these parameters and carotid flow velocities or other CVD risks, we removed them as predictors from our regression models. Additionally, we observed significant correlation coefficients exceeding 0.7 between total cholesterol and LDL-C, between SBP and DBP, and between fasting glucose and glycohemoglobin (A1C) (Supplemental Materials, Table S3). Therefore, we decided to exclude LDL-C, DBP, and fasting glucose from the subsequent regression analysis.

Multiple Logistic Regression Analysis between Carotid Artery Velocity and ASCVD Risk

Discussion

Our study examined linear and nonlinear links between velocity in the carotid arteries and ASCVD risk based on recent primary prevention guidelines using color Doppler ultrasonography. First, we found significant associations of the EDV in the CCA, the EDV in the ICA, and the PSV in the ICA with ASCVD risk. We observed that the EDV in the ICA showed a linear relationship with ASCVD risk and acted as a potential negative predictor. Second, we identified the following critical thresholds: an EDV in the CCA ≤ 23.75 cm/s, a PSV in the ICA ≤ 81.75 cm/s, and an EDV in the ICA ≤ 26.75 cm/s. Primary prevention strategies for cardiovascular disease could be considered when vascular flow velocities fall below these thresholds. Lipid-lowering drug treatment can be prescribed to patients who meet the current treatment guidelines. For those who do not yet meet these guidelines, our findings provide direction for future studies with randomized controlled trials on statin use.

The optimal cutoff values derived from logistic regressions with ROC curve analysis were also like those derived from nonlinear relationships, for which we identified inflection points in the GAM analysis.

Besides RI and PI, which are derived from flow velocities, was discussed in our study. Time-Averaged Maximum Velocity (TAMV) is useful in specific contexts, such as transcranial Doppler, but is less commonly used for stenosis assessment than PSV and EDV. Volume Flow Rate (VFR) requires precise measurement of vessel diameter and does not directly quantify stenosis. Both Acceleration Time (AT) and Acceleration Index (AI), useful in assessing cardiac function, lack standardized reference guidelines for assessment, with measurement techniques varying among researchers. Spectral Broadening (SB) suggests flow disturbances, but incorrect acquisition techniques can lead to misleading results [23].

CIMT, a surrogate marker for atherosclerosis, is positively associated with stroke and coronary heart disease [7, 21, 24]. Approximately 28% of individuals aged 30 to 79 years worldwide exhibit CIMT [25]. In our study, individuals in the higher cardiovascular risk group had a greater CIMT. However, after adjustment for CIMT, this did not impact the association between EDV and cardiovascular disease risk. Consequently, CIMT and velocity from common and internal carotid arteries were independently associated with ASCVD risk in our research.

Unlike a prior Taiwanese study that included subjects with a history of CVD but adjusted for various variables, our study exclusively focused on individuals without prior cardiovascular events [13]. While the previous study lacked cardiac function and structure data, potentially limiting the ability to account for coronary heart disease (CHD)-related confounding factors, our study, while also lacking such parameters, included a research design centered on utilizing a cardiovascular disease risk formula to categorize subjects into distinct risk groups for the next 10 years. This approach obviated the need for including parameters of cardiac function and structure, as we followed an established formula.

Similarly, another study with 1,119 Korean individuals reported an association between the average EDV of the CCA and future cardiovascular events, with a hazard ratio of 0.95 per cm/s (95% confidence interval: 0.91–0.99) [14]. However, our study differed in the source of the subjects. Unlike the Korean study, which included participants undergoing carotid ultrasound exams in a tertiary hospital, possibly leading to a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risks and medication use, our study focused on a general population. Furthermore, unlike the reliance on clinical history and medical records in previous studies, which may have resulted in missing data and subjects being lost to follow-up, our study was prospective and was specifically designed to predict future cardiovascular risk, thereby addressing concerns regarding missing data.

The potential mechanisms for carotid flow velocity and the risk of developing cardiovascular disease may be that a reduced velocity in the carotid artery indicates decreased shear stress, which may increase the likelihood of atherosclerosis [26]. Vessel wall shear stress should be determined by blood viscosity and blood flow velocity and, as a result, should be highly correlated with blood flow velocity. The sites in the arterial wall where the wall shear stress is lower should be more prone to developing atherogenesis [21]. Our results also revealed a weak and inverse correlation between the CIMT and each velocity measurement in the CCA and ICA. Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory arterial disease. Endothelial dysfunction and inflammation are the keys to its onset and progression [27, 28]. From the perspectives of hemodynamics and molecular biology, shear stress from blood flow regulates endothelial cells to adopt functions and gene transcription programs that contribute to the process of atherosclerosis [29].

The CCA bifurcates into the ICA and ECA in the neck area. While previous studies have focused mainly on the ICA because of its direct association with cerebrovascular events, our research revealed that the EDV in both the CCA and ICA is linked to the combination of risk factors commonly used to predict the chance of developing cardiovascular disease. Additionally, it exhibited both nonlinear and linear associations with the need for lipid-lowering medication.

According to our logistic regressions, PSV in the ICA was negatively associated with the need for lipid-lowering medication in patients with elevated CVD risk. This seems to contradict the established belief that elevated PSV in the ICA may indicate ICA stenosis and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease. The threshold for moderate stenosis, defined as greater than 50% in the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial (NASCET), is more than 120 cm/s [30]. The utilization of a nonlinear correlation in the GAM analysis provides a reasonable explanation. The fitted GAM curve showed U-shaped relationships with an inflection point for the PSV in the ICA at approximately 80–100 cm/s, and the ROC analysis suggested an optimal velocity of 81.75 cm/s.

This study has several strengths. First, thorough multicollinearity management was conducted before constructing the logistic model, a crucial step to avoid instability in parameter estimates and unwarranted large standard errors resulting from high predictor variable correlation. Second, the model was appropriately adjusted for numerical risk factors linked to cardiovascular diseases. Third, and most importantly, the application of GAM analysis enabled flexible modeling of nonlinear relationships between carotid flow velocities and estimated ASCVD risks. Visualizing the fitted curve provided a clearer understanding of the relationship, which is advantageous for clinical physicians in determining statin usage.

This study has certain limitations, as it was still a cross-sectional observational study, which restricted the ability to establish causality and only allowed conclusions about associations to be drawn. The consistency of the examination may be affected by carotid ultrasound being a one-time measurement performed by different operators, although this was mitigated by ensuring that well-trained neurologists conducted the measurements. Moreover, the absence of information on exercise habits, a potential ASCVD risk modulator, is noteworthy, but exercise measurement lacks consistency and is not a typical or key feature of commonly used risk calculators.

The lack of data on lipid-lowering medication use in our study may explain the reverse trend where higher total cholesterol was associated with lower CVD risk. However, this did not impact the use of the Pooled Cohort Equation (PCE) to predict the risk of cardiovascular disease over the next 10 years when we used multivariate logistic regression and the GAM to adjust for these confounders.

Conclusion

Our study suggests varying predictive values of carotid artery velocity for the estimated 10-year ASCVD risk. The EDV in the CCA and the PSV in the ICA showed a nonlinear concave pattern, whereas the EDV in the ICA demonstrated a linear predictive effect. Multivariate logistic regression analysis with an ROC curve revealed that the optimal EDV in the CCA was approximately 23.75 cm/s, and in the ICA, the optimal PSV was 81.75 cm/s, and the optimal EDV was 26.75 cm/s. In addition, we found a U-shaped pattern of relationships with elevated ASCVD risk and velocity in the carotid arteries, with inflection points of approximately 82 cm/s in the PSV of the ICA and near 25 cm/s in the EDV of the CCA. Both methods revealed similar results. Our finding may offer a promising and non-invasive avenue for improving risk assessment and may provide timely interventions to prevent ASCVD events.
