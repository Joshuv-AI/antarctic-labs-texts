Hepatitis B, C, and E viruses in solid organ transplantation: donor and recipient perspectives
Authors: Jehanzaeb Khan, Jade Kozuch, Saima Aslam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolid organ transplantation (SOT) presents a unique intersection of life-saving opportunity and infectious risk. This review examines the implications of hepatitis B, C, and E from both donor and recipient perspectives and provides updated evidence-based management strategies. Advances in antiviral therapies have enabled the safe utilization of HBV- and HCV- infected donors, which can expand the donor pool without compromising recipient outcomes. For HBV, nucleos(t)ide analogs provide effective prophylaxis and treatment, and emerging data support using HBsAg+ donors in HBV-naive recipients with proper management. In the case of HCV, direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) have revolutionized transplant medicine, allowing the routine use of HCV-viremic donors. Both preemptive and prophylactic treatment strategies are discussed, with growing support for short-course DAA regimens in cardiothoracic transplant. HEV, though less commonly screened, poses a risk for chronic infection in immunocompromised hosts and can lead to graft dysfunction or rejection. Ribavirin remains the mainstay of treatment, and the potential for donor-derived HEV transmission highlights the importance of surveillance and, where available, screening protocols. This review proposes practical algorithms for pre- and post-transplant management and outlines future directions in vaccine development, viral screening, and antiviral therapies. A tailored, virus-specific approach informed by both donor and recipient virologic status is essential to optimize patient and graft outcomes while ensuring safe expansion of the donor organ pool.Keywords: Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, Hepatitis E, Solid Organ Transplant

Introduction

Solid organ transplantation (SOT) is a life-saving intervention for patients with end-stage organ failure. However, the risk of viral infection and transmission, particularly with hepatitis B virus (HBV), hepatitis C virus (HCV), and hepatitis E virus (HEV), remains a challenge in the transplantation process. These viruses vary in prevalence, transmission risk, clinical implications, and management in both donors and recipients. With recent advances in antiviral therapy, especially for HBV and HCV, the landscape of transplantation is evolving, making it feasible to consider organs from viremic donors under certain circumstances. This review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the implications of HBV, HCV, and HEV in the context of SOT, from both donor and candidate perspectives, and to review management recommendations.

Hepatitis B virus

HBV is a DNA virus of the Hepadnaviridae family and is associated with both acute and chronic liver disease. The global burden of HBV remains substantial despite the availability of highly effective vaccines. HBV is a major cause of chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) worldwide. According to WHO 2024 estimates, ∼254 million people worldwide are chronically infected with HBV, denoted by HBV surface antigen (HBsAg) positivity, which is ∼3.8% of the global population.1 Highest endemicity areas (>8% HBsAg+ prevalence) include sub-Saharan Africa, East Asia and Pacific Islands where perinatal and early childhood transmission remain the predominant modes of spread. The US and Europe are low endemicity areas (<2% HBsAg prevalence) where HBV is mainly spread through injection drug use, unprotected sexual contact especially in men who have sex with men, as well as immigration from high endemicity areas.1

Diagnosis

HBV diagnostic testing includes a combination of serologic and virologic assays that are widely available. Anti-HBV serologies consist of anti core antibody (anti-HBc) and quantitative surface antibody (anti-HBs) and virologic assays consist of quantitative surface antigen (HBsAg) or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of HBV DNA as well as qualitative nucleic acid amplification test (NAT). Transplant-related epidemiology

The rate of seropositivity and active HBV infection in donors and candidates varies geographically. In one study from Spain, 55/392 (14%) SOT recipients including 113 lung transplant recipients, were anti-HBc-positive.2 In this study, only 4% of lung donors were positive for anti-HBcAb. In HBV-endemic areas like Taiwan, one study noted approximately 8% of 412 heart transplant recipients were HBsAg-positive at the time of transplantation.3 Recent OPTN analysis of 54,307 cardiothoracic transplants from the US found that 5.3% of all donors were anti-HBc-positive, 0.3% were either HBsAg-positive or HBV NAT-positive. In this study, there were 53 heart or lung transplants from HBsAg-positive donors to HBV-naiive recipients no deleterious impact on patient and graft survival.4 Among all heart or lung recipients, 0.1% were HBV NAT-positive.

Treatment

HBV infection is not curable at this time as the virus establishes persistence within hepatocytes through the formation of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) or integrated DNA. Firstline agents recommended for HBV infection include entecavir and tenofovir (has 2 formulations – tenofovir alafenamide TAF and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate TDF). These drugs potently suppress HBV replication by inhibiting the viral reverse transcriptase/polymerase, leading to reduced serum HBV DNA and improved clinical outcomes.5 In general, these antivirals are well-tolerated, though TDF is associated with nephrotoxicity so not commonly used in SOT recipients. Tenofovir is preferred if there is any concern for prior HBV therapy due to minimum resistance concerns or when human immunodeficiency virus co-infection is present. Entecavir is orally dosed 0.5–1mg daily and TAF is 25 mg orally once daily.6, 7

Vaccination

There are multiple highly effective vaccines against HBV. All licensed HBV vaccines are recombinant subunit vaccines that contain HBsAg. They are non-infectious and do not contain live virus. HBV vaccines lead to the production of anti-HBs antibodies, which neutralize the virus upon exposure, preventing viral entry into hepatocytes. Generally, a protective anti-HBs titer is defined as ≥10 mIU/mL, though this has been recently called into question.8 Several vaccines are currently available, including monovalent formulations (Engerix-B, Recombivax HB, Heplisav-B, PreHevbrio) and a combination vaccine with hepatitis A (Twinrix). Almost all vaccines lead to 90–95% seroprotection after 3 doses (0, 1, and 6 months) in immunocompetent individuals. The Heplisav-B vaccine is associated with 95% seroprotection with 2 doses 0 and 1 month apart and if available is preferable for SOT candidates. In a large multicenter study of >900 cardiothoracic transplant recipients, patients receiving Heplisav-B were more likely to complete the series than Recombivax HB (81% vs. 60%, p < 0.001) and Heplisav-B had a higher seroprotection rate than Recombivax HB (75% vs. 64%, p = 0.023).9

Donor and Candidate/Recipient Testing

The HBV status of both donor and recipient is tested via HBsAg, anti-HBs, core antibody (anti-HBc), and nucleic acid amplification test (NAT) or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of HBV DNA. The risk of transmission from donor to recipient varies based on presence or absence of detectable virus via a virologic test (HBsAg, NAT, PCR) in the donor as detailed below. Risk of active disease in a recipient with chronic HBV infection is based on type of immunosuppression received.

a.HBsAg+ or NAT+ donors:Due to very high risk of transmission, traditionally such donors were used for candidates that already had HBV infection, mostly in the context of liver transplantation. Hepatitis B Immunoglobulin (HBIG) is used at time of transplant followed by lifelong antiviral therapy with overall excellent outcomes.10Studies from Taiwan, which is a high endemicity area, demonstrate excellent outcomes of heart transplant recipients that receive organs from HBsAg-positive donors regardless of the underlying HBV status of the recipient; HBIG was used in HBV naiive recipients.3, 11 Recent cohort studies and registry analyses also demonstrate that, with appropriate antiviral prophylaxis and close monitoring, transplantation of HBV NAT-positive or HBsAg-positive organs into HBV-naive recipients can be performed safely, with low rates of chronic infection and comparable graft and patient survival to standard-risk transplants.4, 12, 13, 14, 15 We recommend developing protocols for the safe use of HBsAg/ NAT-positive donors with consultation from transplant infectious diseases and/or hepatology specialists.

Hepatitis C virus (HCV)

HCV is an RNA virus of the Flaviviridae family, with six main genotypes. Globally, an estimated 57–58 million people were living with chronic HCV infection in 2020 (viremic prevalence 0.7–0.8%), with over 70% residing in low- and middle-income countries.28 Geographic prevalence and transmission routes vary. In high-income countries, injection drug use is the predominant transmission route; in low- and middle-income countries, unsafe health-care practices (unsafe injections, re-use of unsterilized needles) remain a major source. Other risks include mother to child transmission as well as unsafe sexual practices.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is via serology and nucleic acid testing. A positive HCV antibody result with a negative nucleic acid test reflects previous exposure to the virus without ongoing infection. Anti-HCV antibodies remain detectable long term after infection, whereas an undetectable HCV RNA level confirms there is no current viral replication. HCV-infected donors can be viremic, i.e. NAT positive or those that are seropositive only (i.e. antibody positive) but NAT negative. The risk of transmission of the former is almost 100% whereas the risk of transmission of the latter is negligible in the setting of non-liver transplantation.

Transplant-related epidemiology

HCV-positive donor utilization in cardiothoracic transplantation has increased dramatically over the past decade in the United States, rising from 0.6% of heart transplants in 2016 to 11.5% in 2019 and reaching 12.9% by 2021—representing over a 100-fold increase.29 This expansion has been driven by the opioid epidemic increasing HCV-viremic donors and the availability of highly effective direct-acting antivirals (DAAs).30 Lung transplants from HCV-positive has similar increased significantly over this time period though utilization remains lower than heart transplant, representing 4.4% of all lung transplants performed in the United States from 2019–2020.31 International data are limited, as the dramatic increase in HCV-viremic donors is primarily a US phenomenon driven by the domestic opioid epidemic.

Treatment

Historically, treatment of HCV relied on interferon-based regimens, which were poorly tolerated and achieved low cure rates. In the context of SOT, the use of HCV-infected organs was reported to have higher rates of graft failure, cirrhosis, and other complications prior to the era of current therapies.32, 33 Therefore, organs from HCV-infected donors used to be considered suboptimal, and were usually reserved for recipients with existing HCV infection. The advent of direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapies has transformed HCV treatment, leading to high cure rates and low rates of side effects.34 At the same time, the opioid epidemic in the United States led to a sharp rise in the number of HCV-infected organ donors.35 Since 2017, these developments contributed to a substantial increase in the use of HCV-infected donor organs, expanding the available donor pool.

The first available DAA regimens targeted specific genotypes of HCV, but newer regimens are pan-genotypic. Pan-genotypic regimens include sofosbuvir/velpatasvir (SOF/VEL) and glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (GLE/ PIB), which are now both considered preferred first-line treatment regimens.34 A third pan-genotypic regimen, sofosbuvir/velpatasvir/voxilaprevir (SOF/VEL/VOX), is a triple combination used for minority of patients that have failed first-line agents, and more complex regimens are available for rare virologic failures to the above DAAs.36 DAAs are well tolerated and have minimal drug interactions with immunosuppressives. Sofosbuvir-based regimens can rarely cause symptomatic bradycardia, especially when co-administered with amiodarone, though have been used safely in cardiothoracic transplant recipients.37

Vaccination

There is no licensed vaccine against HCV infection at this time.

Donor and candidate/recipient testing

The HCV status of both donor and recipient is tested via antibody testing as well as NAT or PCR testing. The risk of transmission from donor to recipient varies based on presence or absence of detectable virus via a virologic test in the donor as discussed below. Unlike HBV infection, as HCV is curable with DAA (defined as sustained virological response at 12 weeks from end of DAA treatment,SVR12), infection status is considered active (viremic) or resolved.

a.HCV viremic donors:

HCV viremic donors:

Currently, HCV-viremic donors (NAT-positive) are used as standard clinical care with one of two management approaches:

i.Pre-emptive treatment of HCV NAT-positive donor:This is the classic approach and is widely used with excellent outcomes. After a recipient is transplanted with an HCV-viremic organ, the infection is allowed to develop, and the recipient is then preemptively treated with an 8- to 12-week course of DAA, prior to development of liver disease and usually started within a month or so of transplant. Clinical cure rates in this setting are > 95% and severe adverse effects are rarely described.38 Heart transplantation from HCV-viremic donors to uninfected recipients using this approach was initiated around 2017 and has demonstrated high cure rates and no adverse outcomes attributable to HCV transmission or treatment.39 Multiple studies, including a subsequent meta-analysis have demonstrated a 100% cure rate with conventional durations of DAA therapy and no serious HCV-related adverse event.40, 41, 42, 43 Longer term data do not support an increased risk of coronary artery vasculopathy, rejection, or mortality.44, 45, 46, 47, 48 The data regarding the use of HCV-viremic lung transplantation is similarly supportive of this practice. The long-term safety of this approach has been corroborated in larger studies, and registry analysis.37, 42, 45, 49, 50 Although HCV-positive donors more often had substance use and overdose-related death, lung allograft quality and recipient survival were comparable to HCV-negative donors.51 Risk for rejection and chronic lung allograft dysfunction was shown to be similar to HCV-uninfected organs as well.52, 53

Hepatitis E virus (HEV)

HEV is an RNA virus from the Herpesviridae family with 8 genotypes, but the majority of infections are due to genotypes 1 and 2 which are associated with waterborne outbreaks related to fecal-oral spread in developing countries. Genotypes 3 and 4 are less common, usually zoonotic through consumption of raw or undercooked meat, and prevalent in developed regions; genotype 8 causes sporadic illness associated with camel exposure. HEV is often self-limiting but can cause chronic infection and cirrhosis in immunocompromised individuals, including SOT recipients, as well as increased acute liver failure and mortality in pregnant women and infants.65 HEV infection can be associated with extrahepatic manifestations, including renal and neurological clinical signs and symptoms.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is generally made via serology though PCR testing is available as well. Commercial serologic assays can test for both anti-HEV IgM (usually present for 3–12 month after infection onset) and IgG (generally remains positive for many years).66 Chronic HEV infection is defined as the presence of persistently positive serum or plasma HEV RNA for 3 or more months.67

Transplant-related epidemiology

A recent meta-analysis, including more than 4500 SOT recipients, found that the pooled estimated prevalence of HEV infection was 20.2%. Prevalence of HEV infection among SOT recipients was significantly higher in middle-income countries compared to high-income countries.68 Among 381 immunocompromised individuals in Spain, including 131 SOT recipients, anti-HEV IgG was found in 94 (25.6%) of patients and HEV-RNA was positive in 6, yielding a rate of chronic HEV of 5.8% in this study. In this population, therapy with mTOR inhibitors was independently associated with risk of chronic HEV.69 In a retrospective single center study from the US, 12/311 SOT recipients were found to have evidence of HEV infection (3.9% seropositivity); none of whom had chronic infection. In this study, posttransplant de novo HEV infection was associated with an increased risk for graft rejection (odds ratio, 14.2; P =.03).70 A French case series of 14 liver or kidney transplant recipients with acute HEV infection found that 8 progressed to chronic infection, and those patients had a significantly shorter time from transplantation to infection (37.5 vs 78.5 months; p=0.03).71 A subsequent study from the same French group found that chronic HEV hepatitis developed in 56/86 acutely infected patients (65.9%) and was independently associated with tacrolimus use (vs cyclosporine A; OR 1.87, 95% CI 1.49–1.97; p=0.004). Among those with chronic infection, 18 (32.1%) cleared HEV after immunosuppression reduction.72

Treatment

Management strategies focus on reducing immunosuppression when feasible. For those who do not clear the virus spontaneously, ribavirin monotherapy for 3 months is recommended as first-line treatment, achieving sustained virologic response in approximately 80% of cases. Ribavirin and interferon-alpha are most widely used agents for the treatment of chronic HEV infections in immunocompetent individuals.73 Interferon alpha is generally contraindicated in SOT recipients due to rejection risk associated with enhanced immune response; it has rarely been used as a salvage therapy in liver recipients.74

A retrospective multicenter European study of 255 SOT recipients confirmed that ribavirin is highly efficient for treating chronic HEV infection in SOT recipients. Ribavirin was given at the median dose of 600 (range, 29–1200) mg/day (mean, 8.6 ± 3.6 mg/kg/day) for a median duration of 3 (range, 0.25–18) months. After a first course of ribavirin, the sustained virological response rate was 81.2%. It increased to 89.8% when patients were offered a second course of ribavirin, if needed. Pretreatment HEV polymerase mutations and de novo mutations under ribavirin did not have a negative impact on HEV clearance.75

It is important to note that ribavirin can have significant adverse effects and drug interactions in SOT recipients. It requires adjustment for renal dysfunction and carries risk of hemolytic anemia, psychiatric disturbance, hepatotoxicity, and teratogenicity among others. Baseline laboratory testing should include complete blood cell count (CBC) and pregnancy test. Anemia typically occurs within 1–2 weeks of initiation so CBC should be monitored closely throughout treatment. Myocardial infarction resulting from ribavirin-induced anemia is a serious risk which should be thoughtfully considered in heart transplant recipients, and all patients with history of cardiac disease should have an electrocardiogram prior to treatment. In lung transplant recipients, ribavirin-induced dyspnea and cough can be miscategorized as rejection or infection. Additionally, severe pancytopenia can occur when ribavirin is used in combination with azathioprine due to accumulation of myelotoxic metabolites.76

Vaccination

The recombinant HEV 239 vaccine (Hecolin) has demonstrated high efficacy in preventing HEV after 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months) in immunocompetent individuals. Studies show 100% (95% CI: 72.1–100) efficacy within 12 months after the third dose.77 Extended follow-up studies indicate that the vaccine's efficacy remains high, with one study reporting 86.8% efficacy over 10 years.78 It is currently licensed in China and Pakistan, though has been used by the WHO in outbreak situations in Africa.

Donor and candidate/recipient testing

Universal HEV screening of deceased organ donors using PCR assay was implemented by the UK national organ procurement organization in October 2017 as HEV is the most common cause of acute hepatitis in that country. Donor screening is done post-transplant and presence of HEV RNA in the donor is used to inform recipient management. Incidence of HEV infection of 0.94 per 1000 organ donors is approximately four times higher than that seen in the blood donor population.79 In this study, 8 donors with detectable HEV RNA donated to 6 liver recipients and 14 kidney recipients. All 6 liver and 9/14 kidney recipients developed post-transplant infection with demonstrable HEV RNA in plasma (all recipients had negative serologies prior to transplant). Most were treated with a single course of ribavirin successfully with only one liver recipient that needed re-treatment. Another study from a hyperendemic region in Italy noted that 21% of 300 SOT candidates were positive when screened with a combination of HEV IgM, IgG, and PCR; among these 5 patients had positive PCR.80 At this time, it seems best to screen for HEV in the donor and recipient population based on geographic endemic prevalence rather than a blanket universal screening process.

Conclusion

Hepatitis B, C, and E viruses present unique challenges and opportunities in organ transplantation. The landscape of viral hepatitis in SOT continues to evolve, underscoring the need for targeted innovation in screening, treatment, and prevention strategies. For hepatitis B, ongoing research offers promise for future curative regimens, which could alter post-transplant management paradigms and donor selection criteria.5 The safe use of HBsAg+ or NAT+ donors in HBV-naive recipients, though supported by emerging data, warrants further study in larger, multicenter cohorts to assess long-term outcomes and refine prophylaxis strategies. In HCV, the success of short-course DAA prophylaxis has opened the door to more cost-effective transplantation paradigm compared to the standard test and treat approach. For hepatitis E, increased recognition of chronic infection and its impact on graft survival calls for broader implementation of donor and recipient screening in endemic regions. Vaccination against both HBV and HEV represents a promising prophylactic tool, though availability remains geographically limited for HEV vaccine. Expansion of vaccine access and evaluation of immunogenicity in immunocompromised populations, including transplant candidates, is critical. Cross-disciplinary efforts integrating transplant infectious disease, hepatology, and public health will be essential to drive policy change and advance clinical care for viral hepatitis in transplantation.
