Insights for Tailoring Exercise Recommendations in Home-based Cardiac Rehabilitation: Patients’ Perspectives
Authors: Jinsung Jeon, Eung Ju Kim
Published: 2024

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), including myocardial infarction (MI) and heart failure, represent a significant threat and burden in the global health domain, with their prevalence and mortality rates increasing progressively.1) Cardiac rehabilitation (CR), also known as cardiovascular rehabilitation, encompasses a multidisciplinary, systematic, and individualized methodology for delivering evidence-based secondary prevention therapies to individuals with CVD.2) Although numerous studies have demonstrated that CR is an effective intervention for the prevention of CVD, the persistently low participation rates in CR programs remain a significant obstacle to overcome.3)

Traditionally, CR has been conducted through center-based CR (CBCR) in hospital settings. The issues with CBCR include not only low patient participation rates but also a low implementation rate of CR due to manpower and facility constraints. According to a 2021 survey conducted in South Korea, only 28% of hospitals managing acute MI had implemented CR programs.4) In these circumstances, low participation rates in CBCR are particularly attributed to the geographical distance to the center and the incompatibility with work and social life.5) To overcome these limitations, there has been an increasing demand for various forms of CR as alternatives to CBCR. Proposed alternatives include home-based CR (HBCR) programs with professional support and telerehabilitation utilizing telehealth technology.6)

HBCR can be defined as comprehensive services that involve medical evaluation, prescribed exercise, cardiovascular risk factor modification, patient education, and behavioral activation, predominantly or entirely administered outside of the traditional CBCR context.7) A recent systematic review on the effectiveness of HBCR in patients with CVD demonstrated that HBCR showed comparable outcomes to CBCR in terms of clinical events such as mortality and rehospitalization, exercise capacity, and health-related quality of life.8) In addition, HBCR offers advantages over CBCR in terms of convenience and flexibility, potentially overcoming physical limitations to enhance patient participation and adherence. However, unlike CBCR, standardized intervention methods for HBCR have not yet been developed. It is known that individual patient preferences, health status, cultural background, and socioeconomic status influence the successful implementation of HBCR.7)

In this issue, Lee et al.9) investigated the physical activity levels, exercise preferences, and barriers to exercise among Korean patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and MI to provide basic resources for effective HBCR programs. According to the study, 43.4% of participants reported engaging in leisure-type physical activities, while only 26.5% of the total participants met the public health guidelines for moderate to vigorous physical activity. The most preferred form of exercise was walking, favored by 65.6% of participants, followed by resistance training at 35.4%. Additionally, 43.9% of respondents preferred exercising alone, while only 6.9% preferred exercising with a professional. These findings underscore the importance of tailoring exercise recommendations to individual patients, considering factors such as intensity, duration, type of exercise, and their preferences. Regarding barriers to exercise participation, the most frequently reported issues were fatigue, poor health, and low fitness levels, followed by lack of interest and fear of side effects. Therefore, it is essential to develop HBCR programs focused on enhancing interest in exercise and ensuring safety through monitoring for these patients.

In a previous study conducted on patients with coronary heart disease, personalized CR programs that reflect patients’ needs and preferences have demonstrated not only reduced hospital stay and readmission rates compared to standardized CR programs but also higher participation rates.10) Therefore, understanding patients’ preferences and barriers is essential for developing effective HBCR programs. In this regard, the study by Lee et al.9) provides important foundational data for the future implementation of HBCR. Based on these findings, subsequent studies are needed to validate the clinical effectiveness of individualized HBCR programs.
