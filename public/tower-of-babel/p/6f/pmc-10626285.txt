Guarding skin under PPE: Mechanistic insights and technological innovations
Authors: Junru Lu, Guan Jiang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the presence of diseases transmitted through respiratory droplets and direct contact, healthcare workers (HCWs) necessitate the use of personal protective equipment (PPE). For optimal safety, PPE should securely conform to the skin during extended wear. However, conventional PPE often lacks adequate air permeability and hygroscopicity, trapping heat and moisture emitted by the body within the enclosure. Such a hot and humid internal environment can induce skin damage, such as erythema, rash, pruritus, and itching among others, leading to microbial growth on the skin surface, the production of inflammatory mediators at the wound site and an increased risk of infection. This review strives to comprehensively elucidate the fundamental mechanisms triggering adverse skin reactions and their resultant manifestations. Furthermore, we explore recent advancements aimed at inhibiting these mechanisms to effectively mitigate the occurrence of skin lesions.

INTRODUCTION

Since the dawn of the 21st century, the emergence of respiratory viruses, primarily transmitted through respiratory droplets and direct contact, has wrought havoc upon humanity. These include the notorious Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (SARS), the enigmatic Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS), and the most recent pandemic, Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19). This modern‐day narrative of viral challenges serves as a testament to our ever‐evolving understanding of infectious diseases.

Healthcare workers (HCWs) face a significant and unexpected challenge as they combat infectious diseases. Their daily tasks, including vaccination and nucleic acid testing, require close contact with infected individuals. Consequently, Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) is vital to protect HCWs' health and lives. This includes disposable medical protective clothing for those on the epidemic frontlines and isolation gowns for outpatient nurses, volunteers, and community workers in close contact with infected individuals. PPE also plays a crucial role in safeguarding workers in maritime and firefighting industries.

Despite its excellent antibacterial and liquid‐repelling properties, PPE is often worn for prolonged periods, snugly against the skin to provide effective protection. In this scenario, PPE can exert pressure, friction, and shearing forces on the skin, resulting in an elevation of skin temperature. 1 Moreover, the perspiration of HCWs cannot easily evaporate, leaving their skin consistently moist. This high humidity, high‐temperature environment poses a risk of skin infections, triggering the production of inflammatory mediators, neutrophil and monocyte infiltration, and increased reactive oxygen species. 1 Additionally, these conditions can foster the growth of molds, fungi, bacteria, and viruses, altering the skin's microbiome and potentially causing respiratory discomfort, allergies, and illnesses. 2 , 3

Traditional PPE falls short of meeting the needs of HCWs, highlighting a growing dilemma between skin comfort and personal safety. To address these issues and better equip ourselves for future infectious diseases, numerous enhancements to PPE have been suggested. These include innovative solutions like reversible hydration composite films and air‐cooling garments equipped with electrical fans. 4 , 5 The majority of these improvements underscore the necessity of prioritizing new materials and comfort‐oriented design principles. Additionally, there's a strong call for the establishment of objective comfort evaluation criteria to guide the design and manufacturing of PPE.

MECHANISMS

The skin, our body's largest organ, serves as the primary interface with the external world, offering a crucial protective shield against external influences. When PPE is worn, virtually every facet of the skin's surface is impacted. These subtle alterations encompass changes in the skin's microbiota, as well as its physical and physiological characteristics. Skin microbiota

Microorganisms inhabit various ecological niches on the skin's surface, collectively forming a complex ecosystem known as skin microecology. Consequently, the interplay between microbial communities, hosts, and external stimuli establishes a delicate balance within the skin's microecology, serving as the initial biological defense barrier. However, when this equilibrium or the integrity of the skin barrier is disrupted, it can give rise to a range of inflammatory and dermatological conditions, including but not limited to psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, acne, and chronic wounds. 6 , 7

A research conducted by Lin et al. 3 delved into the impact of PPE usage on the skin's microbiome and skin condition among ten HCWs using VISIA analysis. The study encompassed 60 skin samples taken from the volunteers' faces, hands, and backs, both before and after donning PPE. Before PPE usage, the prevalent bacteria on the face, hands, and back consisted of Propionibacterium spp. (34.04%), Corynebacterium spp. (13.12%), and Staphylococcus spp. (38.07%). Before PPE usage, the prevalent bacteria on the face, hands, and back consisted of Staphylococcus spp. (31.23%), Xanthomonas spp. (26.21%), and Cutibacterium spp. (42.59%). Interestingly, PPE did not influence the composition of fungal communities across the three skin sites. Furthermore, the study revealed a statistically significant decrease in α bacterial and fungal diversity indices on the face, hands, and back after wearing PPE, indicating reduced diversity—a known risk factor for skin diseases. 3 Notably, the composition of bacterial communities on the hands after PPE usage exhibits variations in β bacterial diversity, possibly attributable to the consistently lower hydration levels on the hands compared to other sites. This may be explained by the instability of microbial communities in dry areas over time. 8

Based on experimental findings, 3 there is evidence suggesting an increase in the relative abundance of pathogenic bacteria following the use of PPE, notably species like Anaerococcus vaginalis and Elizabethkingia meningoseptica. Many of these pathogens thrive in oxygen‐deprived and moist conditions. Moreover, the wearing of PPE‐induced tissue hypoxia contributes to the reproduction of Parabacteroides spp., which is implicated in skin and soft tissue infections. 3 , 8

Physical property

Prolonged employment of PPE, especially tight‐fitting or sealing equipment, can cause changes in the physical properties of the skin. Such intimate contact between the skin and the PPE will lead to pressure, friction and shear forces, which are always seen as major contributors to stress‐related skin injuries (pressure ulcers). 1 , 9 Compared to normal loads, shear loads acting on the skin will cause damage at significantly lower pressure. 1 Shear stresses occur in contact between skin and PPE due to three primary mechanisms: (i) Static friction can prevent sliding when PPE is tangentially deformed or tangential loads (gravity) actuated; (ii) Dynamic friction (local relative motion) due to macroscopic sliding (such as speaking) causes PPE to rub against the skin; (iii) PPE materials expand in a direction perpendicular to the load.

The manifestations of these loads on the skin range from indentation at the skin‐PPE interface to a larger area of deep tissue abrasion and deformation. If not well managed, skin injuries will not only develop into erythema and mild irritations, but also cause open wounds, providing a potential skin pathway for infection and inflammation. 10 , 11 At the cellular level, excessive loads can lead to capillary occlusion and limited lymphatic flow. The resulting cellular distortion and reduced blood perfusions, thereby lowering percutaneous oxygen levels, could trigger apoptosis or necrosis. 1 , 9 In addition, Oomens et al. observed higher shear stresses at bony prominence (nasal bridge, zygomatic bone, and frontal bone). 12 This may explain why there is a high incidence of skin adverse reactions in these parts of the face.

To understand how mechanical load is influenced by PPE, Rikeen et al. 13 used strain energy density (SED) in the skin as a quantitative measure representative of local tissue failure. In this study, it was found that reducing the contact area (load distribution over a smaller area) caused a strong increase in SED throughout the skin. When the SED value of the upper dermis against the skin surface increases, it may indicate a risk of superficial skin lesions such as abrasions, surface ruptures and tears, and delamination at the dermis‐epidermis junction. When the SED value increases deeper in the skin, it may indicate the possibility of deep tissue damage such as bruising, complete skin rupture, and stratification of the skin from the underlying tissue. Moreover, the high interfacial friction coefficient at the skin‐PPE interface leads to increased levels of SED near the skin surface. 13 High friction is associated with the development of superficial tissue damage, while deep tissue damage appears to be associated with direct application of pressure. 14 , 15 , 16

Hydration levels, sebum secretion and other physiological properties

Any alterations in epidermal lipids, whether it is a reduction in quantity or a shift in composition, can directly impact the skin's barrier structure. This, in turn, leads to an increase in transepidermal water loss (TEWL), 17 which signifies the diffusion of moisture through the outermost layer of skin, known as the stratum corneum (SC). Furthermore, the effectiveness of the skin's pigmentation barrier is intricately linked to its response to ultraviolet (UV) light exposure. 18 Observable changes in skin color, such as erythema and pigmented spots, are the most evident manifestations of these effects. Any irregularities in these components have the potential to disrupt the skin's protective function, playing a role, to varying degrees, in the development and progression of various clinical skin conditions.

According to a study conducted by Hye et al. 19 analyzing skin physiology properties, it was found that compared to PPE‐uncovered areas, hydration levels, TEWL, skin erythema, and temperature significantly increased in covered areas. Skin erythema and high temperature may indication of inflammation and skin permeability. 20 Wearing PPE, increasing temperature and humidity, causes the skin to behave like diapered skin, resulting in disruption of the skin barrier. 21 Moreover, a higher water content in the SC promotes skin absorption of chemicals. 22 Therefore, wearing PPE will make the skin more susceptible to various allergens or chemical stimuli, causing the development of contact dermatitis. In another study, Seher et al. 23 also found that hydration levels are viewed as the main cause of PPE‐related skin changes. Being dehydrated while working will increase the risk of skin adverse reactions, which will cause the appearance of pressure ulcers.

Kim et al. 24 studied the effects of exposure to hot (summer) environments on skin properties. Such an environment is somewhat similar to that in which PPE is worn. According to this experiment, the summer environment enhances sweat production, sebum secretion and greasiness, as well as increases skin pH. Compared with the PPE‐uncovered area, sebum secretion in the PPE‐covered area increased significantly. 19 Cunliffe et al. found that with a local temperature increase of 1°C, sebum secretion increased by 10%. 25 Moreover, Cunliffe et al. 26 also reported that keratin hydration inhibited the sebum outflow (occlusion with surgical tape) and thus increased sebum secretion rates. Wearing PPE makes HCWs in these conditions (high temperature and occlusion), causing a significant increase in sebum secretion. In general, excessive sebum secretion will cause acne, pore enlargement, and seborrheic dermatitis. 27 Furthermore, a variant of acne mechanica, maskne, may be present in the O region with the employment of PPE. 28

The acidic environment of the skin is significant for the stable state of the epidermal permeability barrier, the recovery of the destroyed barrier, and the non‐specific antibacterial defense of the skin. 29 , 30 Wearing PPE increases skin pH over time. 19 Changes in pH may be associated with skin diseases. 31 When wearing PPE, HCWs are constantly exposed to oral fluids and sweat, which carry dangerous viral and bacterial agents. Thus, high skin pH will impair the ability of antimicrobial defense and healing, causing irritation, infection, and various adverse skin reactions related to PPE.

Skin adverse reactions

While the materials used for PPE may differ across industries, the development of cutaneous manifestations from a singular skin site typically exhibit similar characteristics. Intimate contact between PPE and the skin leads to permeability, persistent friction, and elevated levels of hydration, as previously noted. Furthermore, disinfectants such as alcohol‐based hand sanitizers dissolve intercellular lipid components, resulting in the disruption of the epidermal barrier. 32 , 33 These factors may exacerbate preexisting skin conditions and trigger specific skin manifestations. This section delves into numerous factors that compromise personal skin health, categorized as internal and external factors. Additionally, we delineate the attributes of skin adverse reactions caused by these factors.

EXOGENOUS FACTORS

Microclimate

Acne and seborrheic dermatitis

Prolonged use and frequent adjustment of masks may inadvertently heighten the likelihood of skin exposure to bacteria residing on hands or mask surfaces. Furthermore, extended facial coverage by masks can trap significant amounts of exhaled water vapor, creating a relatively enclosed and humid environment that fosters the proliferation of facial bacteria. This, in turn, can disrupt the skin's natural barrier functions, including the pH balance, 19 due to increased sebum and sweat secretion. Hence, the conducive milieu fosters the rapid proliferation of pathogenic microorganisms, notably Propionibacterium acnes, and the robust secretion of sebaceous glands, culminating in the onset of acne and seborrheic dermatitis. 34 The facial, chest, and back regions are the primary sites of acne manifestation, characterized by the presence of blackheads, whiteheads, inflammatory papules, and pustules. Notably, prolonged night shifts and sleep deprivation among young medical practitioners and nurses can predispose them to endocrine dysregulation and cutaneous afflictions.

Eczema

Frequent hand washing and disinfection can lead to dry and itchy skin, as the lipid membrane on the skin surface loses its protective function, causing damage to the skin barrier and an increase in TEWL. 17 This condition is characterized by chapped hands and desquamation, and in severe cases, nail shedding and paronychia may occur. The development of eczema is often the result of a complex interplay between internal and external factors. Based on a research report, it has been found that over half (52%) of medical personnel who experience hand eczema engage in hand washing practices exceeding 10 times per day. 35 Erythema squamous has been identified as the most prevalent type of eczema, with the back of the hand being the most commonly affected area. 36

Skin maceration

To ensure complete protection of the wearer from hazardous substances in the surroundings, gloves and protective boots are predominantly fabricated from waterproof materials with limited air permeability, such as rubber and plastic. HCWs are required to wear rubber gloves and shoes for extended periods, which can lead to difficulties in perspiration evaporation due to high temperatures and humidity. This results in sweat permeating the cuticle of the epidermis, leading to cellular swelling. Especially, the palms and soles of the feet exhibit symptoms of thickening, softening, whitening, wrinkling, and even erosion. It is noteworthy that the vulva, inner thigh, and perianal region may become contaminated with fecal matter and subjected to repetitive friction, potentially resulting in cutaneous inflammation and subsequent infection. 37

Prickly heat

Due to the elevated levels of temperature and humidity within the surrounding environment, the process of excessive sweating becomes hindered in its ability to evaporate. The sweat permeates the cuticle of the epidermis, resulting in the occlusion of the sweat gland catheter. This, in turn, causes the sweat to accumulate within the catheter, leading to an increase in pressure and eventual rupture of the tube. The overflow of sweat into the surrounding tissues subsequently triggers the formation of local papules, papulovesicles, and small blisters. When the symptoms appear as superficial small blisters ranging in size from the tip of a needle to that of a needle, the wall is thin and easy to break, and small scales are left after drying, that is, white miliaria; And if there are a lot of needle‐sized papules and papulovesicles, accompanied by a surrounding blush, it is red miliaria; If there is a needle‐sized pustule at the top of the prickly heat, it is purulent miliaria. Prickly heat typically occurs in regions such as the armpit, elbow socket, neck, trunk, and beneath the breasts of women.

Irritant contact dermatitis

Irritant contact dermatitis (ICD) can arise from both physical and chemical factors, with common physical factors including irritants such as rubber. 38 One of the most prevalent causes of ICD is long‐term exposure to natural chemicals present in urine and feces, which typically manifests in the vulva and perianal area. Additionally, ICD may occur around the mouth due to food scraps and saliva. The clinical features of ICD and ACD (allergic contact dermatitis) are comparable, with symptoms such as erythema, wheal, exudation, scabbing, hypertrophy, and desquamation often presenting challenges in differentiation. 39 Despite similarities, distinctions persist between ICD and ACD 39 : (i) Causes of symptoms: ICD results from an inflammatory response to diverse physical and chemical stimuli, whereas ACD stems from an immune‐mediated response to allergens; (ii) Time of symptoms onset: ICD elicits symptoms within minutes to hours following exposure to irritants, whereas ACD manifests as a delayed hypersensitivity reaction mediated by T cells; (iii) Conditions of skin damage: Skin damage is a common manifestation of ICD, which typically affects the epidermal layer responsible for safeguarding the body against external insults. Examples of skin damage associated with ICD include lacerations, burns, and epidermal peeling. Conversely, ACD does not necessarily require skin damage to elicit a reaction; (iv) Sites of reactions onset: ICD is typically localized to the site of contact or injury, whereas ACD involves immune cell participation and may extend beyond the area of allergen exposure.

Physical injury

PPE worn by HCWs for extended periods of time must be in close proximity to the skin surface, thereby compressing and immobilizing the skin for several hours, leading to mechanical pressure‐induced damage. 40 , 41 The factors contributing to such injuries include compromised local blood circulation, tissue hypoxia, skin evaporation, and the accumulation of excessive water vapor. These factors weaken and saturate the skin, reducing the ability of SC to withstand external pressure and shear forces, resulting in erythema, unevenness, and flattening of the skin contact area. 42 Frequently encountered anatomical regions include the cheekbones, cheeks, areas behind the ears, nasal region, the edge of goggles, along the cap, and the groin region in contact with the “diaper” and the belt tightening portion of PPE. Common clinical presentations include indentation, abrasion, and purpura, among others. 43 In severe cases, stress urticaria and contact urticaria may manifest. 44

Secondary bacterial and fungal infections

Tinea corporis

The microclimatic conditions characterized by elevated temperature and humidity in the local environment of tinea corporis foster an environment conducive to the proliferation and propagation of fungi on the surface of the skin. These fungi infiltrate the stratum corneum, hair, and dermis of human skin, thereby inducing infectious skin diseases. Based on the affected area, these diseases can be classified as tinea cruris, tinea pedis, tinea manus, and onychomycosis. The prevalent superficial fungal infection is characterized by annular erythema with distinct demarcation and elevation, accompanied by the presence of papules, pustules, and scales. The areas most commonly affected include the hands, feet, armpits, and groin.

Folliculitis

The skin is endowed with a plethora of hair follicles, sebaceous glands, and sweat glands that are capable of producing copious amounts of oil and sweat. The permeability of the skin varies across different regions of the body, with some areas exhibiting poor permeability, such as the head and axillae. Consequently, the confluence of sweat and sebum on the skin surface of the head can obstruct pores, thereby facilitating bacterial colonization and increasing the likelihood of folliculitis. The exacerbation of this inflammatory response can culminate in the development of skin lesions, including pustules, boils, seborrheic dermatitis, and secondary fungal infections.

ENDOGENOUS FACTORS

Nervous tension and lowered immunity

Herpes

Various factors such as sleep deprivation and psychological stress can significantly diminish the body's immune response, rendering it susceptible to infection by the herpes simplex virus. This viral infection typically manifests as localized clusters of blisters on the lips and face, with a high likelihood of recurrence during periods of compromised immunity.

Dyshidrosis

Dyshidrosis is a type of hypersensitivity reaction that resembles eczema and is distinguished by the presence of numerous small blisters that appear symmetrically on the hands and feet. These blisters are situated in the deep layer of the epidermis, slightly elevated above the skin surface, and are often accompanied by sensations of itching, tingling, or burning.

Anaphylaxis

Allergic contact dermatitis

In the context of daily life, the act of hand washing, disinfection, and wearing masks does not typically result in severe allergic reactions. However, individuals who work on the front‐line are required to frequently engage in disinfectant contact, wear masks and PPE that possess inadequate air permeability, and may come into contact with irritating materials such as rubber. 45 These activities may lead to the development of ACD, erythema, papules, exudation, or erosion. ACD is a form of hypersensitivity reaction that is generally influenced by individual susceptibility, the allergenicity of contacts, and the local environment (e.g., high temperature and excessive sweating). 46 Mild symptoms are characterized by the manifestation of local erythema, dense papules, or edema. In severe cases, erythema and swelling become more pronounced, leading to the development of papules, blisters, and even bullae. The rupture of blisters results in erosion and scabbing. Skin lesions are typically confined to the contact areas and exhibit a distinct boundary. The majority of individuals exhibit similar symptoms in various parts of the body that come into contact with protective equipment, including itching, redness, dryness, burning sensation, and in prolonged cases, hyperpigmentation and lichenification. 47

Neurodermatitis

Neurodermatitis, a dermatological condition characterized by thick and rough skin, bleeding, and open sores, is frequently induced by skin irritants. It is often concomitant with other skin disorders, including dry skin, atopic dermatitis, or psoriasis. Additionally, stress and anxiety have been identified as potential risk factors.

Disorder of skin resident flora

Extended utilization of disinfectants can have an impact on the microbial population and alter the immune microenvironment present on the skin surface, ultimately resulting in the development of eczema, fungal and bacterial infections, and allergic dermatitis. 48 Please refer to the aforementioned section for specific alterations in skin surface flora.

Exacerbation of preexisting skin disorders

The exacerbation of erythema, blisters, or ulcers, accompanied by pain and potential secondary infection, may occur due to the friction between PPE and the skin. The presence of risk factors such as a history of acne vulgaris, seborrheic dermatitis, oily skin, rosacea, psoriasis (rare), intertrigo, and dermatophytosis may further aggravate the underlying skin condition when wearing masks. 44 , 49

In essence, the interaction of internal and external factors can create a synergistic impact rather than a sole dominance of one factor. Additionally, the type of skin issues arising from protective attire differs by industry, with materials being stiffer for explosion‐proof and stab‐proof gear, while industrial and medical protective wear requires airtight sealing. Hence, a holistic assessment and protection approach should consider the collective impact of these elements on skin health.

Material innovation

Antimicrobial fiber material

Advanced fiber technology has led to the development of antimicrobial materials, which play a vital role in reducing bacterial growth and infection risks associated with prolonged PPE usage. 50 Recent research indicates the utilization of silver nanoparticles embedded in textiles, exhibiting exceptional antimicrobial properties. 51 , 52 These materials not only protect against pathogens but also contribute to reducing skin irritation and discomfort.

Coating technology

To enhance the comfort of PPE, specialized coatings are applied to reduce friction and irritation on the skin. 53 Cutting‐edge developments in this field have introduced hydrophobic coatings that repel moisture, preventing skin maceration. 54 Such advancements contribute significantly to ensuring HCWs remain comfortable and safe during extended PPE use.

Smart materials

The integration of intelligent technology into protective materials has seen remarkable progress. Shape‐memory polymers, superhydrophobic materials, and photocatalytic materials have emerged as key innovations. 55 , 56 , 57 Shape‐memory polymers adapt to body contours, minimizing friction and pressure points. Superhydrophobic materials repel liquids, reducing the risk of skin moisture accumulation. Photocatalytic materials help break down organic contaminants, maintaining a clean and sterile environment within the PPE. These smart materials represent a promising frontier in skin protection.

Nanomaterials

Nanotechnology has revolutionized the development of fiber materials with desirable properties. Nanosilver, nanocarbon, and nano‐zinc oxide have gained attention due to their high strength, abrasion resistance, breathability, low permeability, and static electricity control. 58 , 59 , 60 Nanosilver, in particular, exhibits strong antimicrobial properties, further enhancing the protective capabilities of PPE. 51 These nanomaterials are at the forefront of skin protection technology, offering a new level of safety and comfort.

Design innovation

Ergonomic design

Modern PPE is now designed with meticulous attention to ergonomics. Utilizing techniques like 3D printing and customized designs, 61 PPE is tailored to fit and accommodate the body shape and movements of HCWs. This approach not only ensures a better fit but also minimizes friction and pressure points, reducing the risk of skin injuries associated with ill‐fitting gear.

Ventilation system

Incorporating ventilation systems into PPE has become essential. These systems promote air circulation, improve moisture and heat dissipation, and minimize skin discomfort. Recent innovations have introduced lightweight and efficient ventilation designs, 62 ensuring that HCWs remain comfortable and cool even during extended periods of PPE use.

Compact structure

Advancements in material science and engineering have led to the development of lightweight and compact PPE. 63 This design philosophy reduces the pressure and burden on the skin caused by the PPE. By minimizing the weight and bulk of the gear, healthcare professionals can work effectively while mitigating the risk of skin damage.

Smart technology

Microclimate system

Smart temperature sensors and control technology have given rise to microclimate systems within PPE. These systems actively regulate temperature and humidity, providing a comfortable environment for HCWs. 64 By utilizing micro‐pumps, micro‐valves, and micro‐sensors, 65 the microclimate system maintains optimal conditions both inside and outside the PPE, ultimately enhancing skin protection.

Communication function (Health tech)

Incorporating health technology, PPE now features physiological monitoring systems equipped with biofeedback and sensing technologies. 66 , 67 These systems actively monitor physiological indicators such as heart rate, blood pressure, and skin condition. Real‐time data collection and analysis provide alerts and guidance, enabling healthcare professionals to take proactive measures to reduce the risk of skin damage and ensure their overall well‐being.

SUMMARY

This comprehensive review delves into the intricate mechanisms behind skin reactions triggered by the use of PPE. It covers an array of factors, including the skin's resident flora, physical forces such as shear and friction, pressure, hydration levels, sebum production, and surface pH. Moreover, it thoroughly examines the diverse skin conditions that can manifest as a result of distinct mechanisms, accounting for both external and internal factors.

Furthermore, this review provides an insight into the cutting‐edge advancements in averting adverse skin reactions associated with PPE usage. It is imperative to recognize the potential detrimental effects of prolonged PPE wear, not only among HCWs but also firefighters and individuals in their daily routines. Understanding the occupational and lifestyle‐related skin issues stemming from PPE usage and identifying skin ailments that can be prevented or treated through appropriate measures hold paramount significance in mitigating long‐term skin complications and upholding safety protocols.

The landscape of skin protection against PPE‐related injuries has seen remarkable evolution, with innovations in materials, designs, and the integration of smart technologies propelling progress. In our enduring battle against healthcare challenges, these breakthroughs offer a beacon of hope for HCWs who rely on PPE for their safety. By remaining at the forefront of scientific and technological advancements, we can ensure that those on the frontlines receive the utmost protection while fulfilling their critical responsibilities.
