The gut as a central hub for multi-organ crosstalk in aging
Authors: Xianhong Zhang, Rongqing Li, Yue Gao, Wenjia Zhang, Ting Ni, Yongbin Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe progression from aberrant DNA epigenetic modifications at the molecular level to the functional decline of cells, and subsequently to the deterioration of tissues and organs, represents a central process that shapes organismal health and aging. Aging of individual organs and the diseases associated with this process, including the decline of intestinal, skeletal muscle, cerebral, cardiac, hepatic, pulmonary and renal function, constitute key manifestations of systemic ageing and major contributors to reduced lifespan. The maintenance of organ and whole-body energy metabolism depends fundamentally on the digestion and absorption of nutrients following their entry into the intestinal tract. In recent years, the gut microbiota has emerged as a critical regulator of physiological and pathological communication among the intestine, muscle, brain, heart, liver, lungs and kidneys. It is now recognized as an important determinant of both health maintenance and disease progression. This Review presents the first comprehensive synthesis of the interactions between the gut microbiota and multi-organ ageing. It systematically examines how intestinal ageing and microbiota dysbiosis, acting through inflammatory responses and perturbed epigenetic regulation, contribute to complex networks of communication among multiple organs. Particular emphasis is placed on the mechanisms through which the gut microbiota influences the ageing trajectories of distal organs such as skeletal muscle, the central nervous system, the heart, liver, lungs and kidneys, primarily through the modulation of microbial metabolites and immune responses. Furthermore, we propose emerging intervention strategies targeting the gut or its microbiota that hold promise for mitigating ageing-associated disorders. We also discuss the potential applications of personalized interventions and precision nutrition in delaying ageing and preventing related diseases. By integrating the most recent advances in the field, this review broadens current understanding of the mechanisms underlying gut–organ communication in ageing and provides new conceptual and translational perspectives for future clinical intervention.Graphical abstract Keywords: Aging, Organ aging, Gut microbiota, Inter-organ communication, Age-related diseases

Introduction

Human aging has emerged as a major societal challenge closely linked to the steady rise in life expectancy [1]. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the regulation of aging, as it profoundly affects the quality of life in older adults. Since the beginning of the twenty-first century, the rapid expansion of the aging population has emerged as a major public health challenge in China. The number of older adults is increasing at an exponential rate, and this trend is expected to continue for several decades. At present, individuals aged 60 years or older account for 18.1% of the national population, reaching 254 million, a figure that surpasses the combined populations of Japan and Germany. By 2050, the proportion of the population aged 60 years and above is projected to approach 35%. Such a substantial demographic shift is anticipated to exert profound and long-lasting impacts on China’s socioeconomic development [2]. At the individual level, aging is a complex process characterized by a time-dependent decline in physiological reserves, which is accompanied by the gradual loss of function across multiple organs, including the brain, liver, lung and kidney [3]. Such functional deterioration shortens healthspan and increases susceptibility to a wide spectrum of age-related chronic disorders, including intestinal diseases, neurodegenerative disorders (NDDs), hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, chronic kidney disease and cancer [4]. This progressive decline in physiological capacity poses a serious threat to the physical and mental health of the elderly, reducing both lifespan and quality of life. Consequently, preventing age-related diseases, preserving functional capacity, and extending healthspan have become urgent public health priorities. Although aging is largely irreversible, a growing body of evidence suggests that it is a plastic and modifiable process [5]. For example, investments in healthy behaviors—such as balanced diets, regular exercise and preventive healthcare—can markedly increase the likelihood of achieving healthy aging [6]. These health-promoting practices not only help establish a resilient gut microbiome but also reduce the risk of chronic diseases. Excitingly, emerging evidence identifies the gut microbiota as a key determinant of host homeostasis and a central driver of many age-associated changes, with significant influence on human longevity [7, 8]. Of particular interest, the gut and its microbial community act as critical mediators of inter-organ communication, coordinating interactions between distant organs such as the brain, liver, lung and kidney, and thereby shaping the development of aging-related disorders. For instance, gut microbes can communicate with the brain through neural, immune and endocrine pathways along the gut–brain axis, modulating behavior and higher cognitive functions [9] Disruption of the gut–liver axis contributes to the pathogenesis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [10]. While gut–lung crosstalk, through microbiota dysbiosis and immune-inflammatory responses, directly or indirectly influences host immune regulation in pulmonary diseases [11].

All complex organisms coexist with intricate microbial ecosystems, and humans are no exception. The gut microbiota represents an integral component of the human body, having established a symbiotic relationship with its host through long-term coevolution. The term microbiome—a combination of “microbiota” and “genome”—refers to the collective genetic material of microorganisms inhabiting a given environment [12]. The composition of the human gut microbiome changes dynamically across the lifespan. From birth onward, it is shaped by delivery mode, diet, environmental exposures, geography, medication use and the aging process itself. Alterations in this microbial community have been linked to the deterioration of tissue and organ function (including the brain, skeletal muscle, immune system and vasculature) and to increased risk of age-associated diseases [13].

Metabolic alteration is a key hallmark of organismal aging [14]. Virtually all living organisms require energy to sustain normal physiological function, and the gut—responsible for absorbing carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins and minerals from food—serves as the starting point of systemic metabolism. Aging is accompanied by a gradual decline in gut function, which promotes a state of “inflammaging,” compromises intestinal integrity and drives shifts in gut microbial composition [15]. Intriguingly, changes in the gut microbiota and its metabolites can act as signaling cues that directly affect gut physiology and indirectly influence distant organs such as the liver, brain, adipose tissue and skeletal muscle, thereby shaping whole-body health. Accumulating evidence indicates that reduced diversity of core microbial taxa is associated with human aging and accelerated age-related phenotypes, including frailty and cognitive decline [16, 17]. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota can also elicit aberrant immune responses, increasing susceptibility to inflammatory bowel disease and metabolic disorders such as diabetes [18, 19]. Notably, specific taxa—including Butyricimonas, Akkermansia and Odoribacter—have been linked to healthy aging and exceptional longevity. Emerging clinical and preclinical studies suggest that interventions such as fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), dietary modulation and structured exercise programs can partially restore a youthful microbiome in older individuals [20]. These findings imply that age-related alterations of the gut microbiota are closely associated with phenotypes such as frailty, reduced physical activity, cognitive impairment and bone loss, and the intestine can act as an initiator of systemic ageing through alterations in its microbiota and barrier integrity, which influence host immunity, metabolic pathways and endocrine regulation. It can also function as a responder to organismal ageing by reacting to physiological dysregulation that accompanies age-related decline.

Aging and age-related diseases

Aging is characterized by a gradual loss of physiological function. In mammals, it manifests heterogeneously across organ systems, driving progressive deterioration that ultimately culminates in tissue dysfunction. This heterogeneity is evident not only among individuals but also across different organs within the same individual, with each aging at distinct rates [21]. Experimental evidence indicates that functional decline is primarily driven by cellular and organ senescence. Identifying robust biomarkers of cellular and organ aging—those with specificity, systemic relevance and clinical applicability—has therefore become a priority for tracking physiological aging, predicting pathological conditions and evaluating age-related changes. Importantly, tissue and organ deterioration mediated by cellular senescence is a fundamental component of organismal aging, encompassing virtually every organ system and involving multiple interconnected biological processes. Because changes in one organ can trigger compensatory mechanisms or systemic feedback throughout the body, a multidimensional panel of biomarkers is needed to capture these complex age-dependent changes [22]. Recent studies have proposed thirteen molecular, cellular and systemic hallmarks of aging, including genomic instability, telomere attrition, epigenetic alterations, loss of proteostasis, deregulated nutrient sensing, mitochondrial dysfunction, cellular senescence, stem-cell exhaustion, altered intercellular communication, impaired macroautophagy, chronic inflammation and dysbiosis [23]. Among these, cellular senescence—defined by cell-cycle arrest, a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), metabolic dysregulation and macromolecular damage—is a key driver of organismal aging [24]. It is well established that declining immune competence in older adults increases susceptibility to severe and life-threatening infections [25]. Comparative transcriptomic analyses of young and aged tissues in mice, rats and humans reveal that age-associated gene-expression changes are dominated by the strong induction of inflammatory and immune-response genes [26]. Elevated inflammatory signaling, combined with a higher burden of comorbidities and immune dysfunction, further heightens vulnerability to infection in the elderly [27]. High levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines shape a chronic low-grade inflammatory milieu—a phenomenon termed “inflammaging”—although the upstream sources of this inflammation remain incompletely defined. Multiple contributors have been identified, including cellular senescence, cell debris, immunosenescence, gut microbiota dysbiosis, obesity and alterations in sex hormones [21]. These findings underscore the persistent accumulation of chronic inflammation during aging and reveal how a network of internal and external factors promoting SASP accelerates both organ-specific and organismal aging. The aging of individual organs—including the gut, skin, muscle, heart, liver, lung and brain—leads to structural and functional deterioration that increases the risk of chronic disorders such as neurodegenerative disease, diabetes, hypertension and cancer [28].

Gut microbiota and aging

Physiological succession of the gut microbiota

The microbiota is defined as the community of microorganisms that inhabit a host or its surfaces, whereas the microbiome refers to the collective genomes of these microorganisms [29]. Humans have co-evolved with trillions of microbes for millennia, forming an integrated “superorganism” in which the microbiota participates in and influences a wide array of physiological and pathophysiological processes across the host lifespan [30]. In humans, the gut microbiota is highly dynamic throughout life, undergoing age-related ecological succession to establish a relatively stable adult configuration. Microbial colonization begins at birth, when pioneer species from the maternal vaginal canal, skin, feces and breast milk seed the infant gut [31]. Vaginal and milk-derived lactic acid–metabolizing bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, are prominent early colonizers [32, 33]. Breastfeeding further shapes microbial succession, as human milk oligosaccharides and glycoproteins promote the growth of beneficial taxa, including Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides [34]. During the first three years of life, the infant gut microbiota undergoes rapid compositional shifts, with the most pronounced changes occurring during weaning, when the diet transitions from milk to solid foods. By 3–5 years of age, the microbiota progressively differentiates toward an adult-like state, achieving approximately 40–60% similarity to the adult configuration [35]. With the introduction of solid foods, microbial phylogenetic diversity expands, and the community becomes increasingly complex and functionally specialized, culminating in the establishment of an adult-type microbiota dominated by Bacteroides and Prevotella rather than the Bifidobacterium-rich infant profile [36]. Based on the concept of the “core microbiome,” adult gut microbiota can be categorized into three enterotypes characterized by the dominance of Bacteroides, Prevotella or Ruminococcus, each representing a resilient and relatively stable ecological state [37]. Along the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, aerobic and anaerobic bacteria exhibit distinct spatial distributions: the proximal GI tract contains a higher proportion of aerobic and facultative anaerobic species, whereas the distal small intestine and colon are almost entirely dominated by strict anaerobes [38]. Although the adult gut microbiota is relatively stable, it remains responsive to external influences such as antibiotics, probiotics and dietary changes. In most cases, microbial resilience enables recovery of the original or an alternative stable state. However, advancing age brings physiological changes that can disrupt this equilibrium. Reduced dietary fiber intake, long-term hospitalization, and the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or antibiotics are among the factors that reshape microbial composition and activity in older adults [39]. Increasing evidence indicates that these microbial alterations are particularly relevant to aging, as the gut microbiota modulates innate immunity, metabolic homeostasis and cognitive function—processes closely linked to frailty [40]. Large-scale, carefully controlled studies that account for diet, lifestyle and medication use have revealed age-associated shifts in the abundance of specific taxa [41]. Compared with younger adults, frail elderly individuals typically exhibit altered ratios of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes, with higher proportions of Bacteroidetes and lower proportions of Firmicutes, a pattern reflected in a reduced Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio that is often considered a marker of aging [42]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the timing of adult gut microbiota destabilization or age-related dysbiosis varies among individuals and may correlate more closely with biological (physiological) age than with chronological age (Fig. 1). Such alterations in microbial populations and their functional capacities signify a loss of microbial “youthfulness” and contribute to chronic low-grade inflammation and innate immune activation—processes that drive immunosenescence, degenerative disorders and unhealthy aging.

Dysbiosis of the aging gut microbiota

Gut microbial dysbiosis

A highly consistent observation across studies of age-related disorders is a decline in gut microbial diversity [43, 44]. Early investigations in fruit flies [45], mice [46], rats [47] and humans [48] all demonstrated age-associated alterations in gut microbiota composition. With advancing age, shifts in community structure are strongly linked to health status, as revealed by analyses of elderly individuals transitioning into long-term care—an event frequently accompanied by physiological decline. In the very old (≥ 90 years), microbiota composition appears to depend more on biological health than on chronological age, with exceptionally healthy elders exhibiting distinctive microbial signatures [49]. Remarkably, centenarians (> 100 years) harbor unique microbial profiles characterized by greater diversity and enrichment of taxa associated with health, including Christensenellaceae and Akkermansia [50]. Similar findings have been reported in centenarians and “supercentenarians,” whose microbiota display higher diversity and abundance of beneficial families such as Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae and Akkermansia, even though Akkermansia abundance generally declines with aging and frailty [51]. Similar studies have shown that the transition from adulthood to old age is accompanied by marked shifts in the gut microbiota. In individuals over 65 years, compared with younger adults, microbial diversity is reduced, inter-individual variability increases, and the abundance of Bifidobacterium declines. Concurrently, the relative proportions of Firmicutes, Faecalibacterium, Clostridium cluster XIV, Eubacterium rectale, Bacteroidetes, and Enterobacteriaceae are altered, reflecting a broad restructuring of the gut ecosystem [52]. In some studies, exceptionally long-lived individuals exhibit greater microbial diversity than younger adults, suggesting the presence of “healthy-aging” taxa whose loss may constitute a taxonomic milestone preceding physiological decline and age-related disease. Analyses of 25 studies assessing age-related changes in elderly gut microbiota identified a third cluster of commensal biomarkers—including Akkermansia, Barnesiella, Butyricimonas, Christensenellaceae, Odoribacter, and Oscillospira—which tend to increase with age in healthy individuals but decline under age-associated pathological conditions. These taxa may serve as candidate biomarkers for healthy versus unhealthy aging [53]. Notably, depletion of Akkermansia has been linked to cognitive decline [54], higher mortality in centenarians [55], and progeroid syndromes [56]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the composition of a “healthy” versus frailty-associated gut microbiota correlates with physical frailty, cognitive status, sarcopenia, systemic inflammation, and overall health. A diverse and balanced microbiota may therefore support healthy aging. However, the causal relationship between gut microbial diversity and individual aging remains to be conclusively demonstrated and warrants further longitudinal and mechanistic studies. Excitingly, recent findings from the Rugao Longitudinal Ageing Study (RLAS), which included 1,821 individuals aged 62–96 years, provide compelling evidence linking frailty and gut microbiota composition [57]. Participants were stratified by Frailty Index (FI) scores to assess the severity of frailty, and fecal and plasma samples were collected for fecal metagenomics and plasma nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) metabolomics analyses. Participants were also followed longitudinally to monitor two-year survival outcomes. The study revealed that multiple host factors significantly influenced gut microbial composition, with frailty severity exerting the strongest effect. These observations underscore a close association between individual aging and gut microbiota dynamics. Although chronological age contributes to changes in the gastrointestinal microbiome, lifestyle and the aging environment exert substantial modulatory effects. Dietary patterns and living conditions, which reflect social, regional, and socioeconomic factors, can drive shifts in microbial composition [58, 59]. For example, over a one-year follow-up, older adults residing in long-term care facilities exhibited reduced microbial diversity and increased abundance of potential pathogens compared with community-dwelling peers. Concurrently, the abundance of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)–producing commensals such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii declined, while pathobionts including Veillonella parvula increased [60, 61]. Notably, these age-associated microbial differences are not necessarily caused by aging per se. Instead, they likely reflect overall health decline, including malnutrition and increased exposure to medications such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and antibiotics. Long-term care residents are often hospitalized and frequently treated with antibiotics, which can have profound and lasting impacts on the gut microbiota, particularly by reducing microbial diversity and enriching pathogenic and antibiotic-resistant species [62] (Fig. 2).

Gut microbiota dysbiosis and aging-associated inflammation

The human gut microbiota constitutes a complex, finely tuned symbiotic ecosystem that is influenced by diet, metabolism, and immune function. Host–microbiota interactions are highly interdependent, with bidirectional communication that shapes both microbial composition and host physiology. The adaptive and plastic nature of the gut microbiota allows it to modulate host health by influencing immune and metabolic pathways. For instance, commensal microbes maintain host homeostasis by supporting digestion and nutrient absorption, synthesizing vitamins, regulating immunity, and protecting against pathogens [63]. However, aging-associated remodeling of the gut microbiota can promote the release of pro-inflammatory mediators and alter host circadian metabolic programming. Dysbiosis often manifests as an expansion of pro-inflammatory taxa and a reduction in beneficial microbes, which can trigger local and systemic inflammatory responses, contributing to chronic low-grade inflammation [64, 65]. These microbial shifts commonly involve an increase in proteolytic bacteria and a decrease in saccharolytic bacteria. The resulting combination of increased gut permeability, impaired gastrointestinal integrity, and altered microbiota-derived metabolites is linked to age-related functional decline and the pathogenesis of frailty, cachexia, cancer, and metabolic disorders [66, 67]. As originally proposed by Franceschi et al. in 2000, “inflammaging” is an age-associated pro-inflammatory state accompanied by a reduced capacity to respond to diverse stressors [68]. Importantly, chronic low-grade inflammation in aging represents a persistent, systemic, unresolved inflammatory state and forms part of immunosenescence, with chronic activation of the innate immune system as a hallmark feature [69, 70]. Although epithelial cells are not classically considered innate immune cells, intestinal epithelial cells express a broad repertoire of innate immune receptors. The expression and signaling of these receptors in response to microbial recognition are critical for gut homeostasis, as epithelial-specific defects can compromise barrier integrity, disrupt spatial segregation between commensals and the mucosal layer, and predispose tissues to spontaneous inflammation. For example, intestinal epithelial cells sense microbial components and metabolites to regulate antimicrobial programs and metabolic homeostasis. This process involves key mediators of Toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling, including MyD88, TNF receptor–associated factor 6 (TRAF6), and NF-κB essential modulator (NEMO), as well as orchestrators of cell death such as receptor-interacting serine/threonine-protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), FAS-associated death domain protein (FADD), and caspase-8 [71–74]. Intriguingly, microbial metabolites derived from dysbiotic communities can act as persistent stimuli for chronic immune activation, sustaining unresolved inflammation. For instance, increased levels of Bacteroides fragilis in aging microbiota can modulate systemic immune responses by reducing responsiveness to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), accompanied by upregulation of aging-related markers such as p16, SAMHD1, and NF-κB activation [75]. Collectively, these findings indicate a close association between aging-related gut microbiota, their metabolites, and systemic inflammation. Key inflammatory biomarkers in aging include white blood cell counts (WBC), lymphocyte subsets, C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukins (IL-6, IL-10), and TNF-α. Early studies reported that older adults with frailty or pre-frailty exhibit significantly elevated levels of CRP, TNF-α, IL-6, leukocytes, and fibrinogen [76], underscoring the link between dysbiotic microbiota and age-associated inflammatory burden.

Notably, one of the primary modes by which the gut microbiota interacts with the host is through microbial metabolites—small molecules produced either as intermediates or end products of microbial metabolism. These metabolites can originate from bacterial processing of dietary substrates, modifications of host-derived molecules, or direct microbial synthesis. Microbial metabolites act as critical signaling molecules that influence immune maturation, immune homeostasis, host energy metabolism, and the maintenance of mucosal integrity. This underscores the capacity of the gut microbiota to directly modulate the balance between pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses in the intestine. Early studies have shown that age-associated increases in intestinal epithelial permeability allow microbial products and toxins to translocate into the circulation, triggering systemic inflammation [77]. Beyond this, microbial metabolites play pivotal regulatory roles in intestinal inflammation. For example, aging-related gut barrier dysfunction can activate the ALPK1–TIFA–NF-κB pathway through the microbial metabolite ADP-heptose, specifically driving the expansion of pre-leukemic cells harboring Dnmt3a mutations and promoting systemic inflammation [78]. Similarly, the gut microbiota-derived indole-3-propionic acid (IPA) maintains mucosal homeostasis and suppresses mucosal inflammation by promoting Hsp70-mediated apoptosis of Th1/Th17 cells, a mechanism implicated in the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [79]. These findings highlight IPA as a critical modulator of intestinal immune responses and mucosal stability. Other microbial metabolites, such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), can influence host metabolic state, immune function, and inflammatory tone, thereby contributing to the development of atherosclerosis [80]. Likewise, imidazole propionate (ImP), another gut microbiota-derived metabolite, correlates with atherosclerotic severity and can independently induce vascular lesions in mice via the ImP–I1R axis in myeloid cells. Inhibition of this pathway has been shown to markedly delay or even reverse disease progression [81]. Conversely, certain microbial metabolites exhibit anti-inflammatory properties. For instance, nordihydroguaiaretic acid (NDGA) levels decline in the feces of older adults. Experimental administration of NDGA alleviates colitis by suppressing GSDMD/NR4A1/NLRP3 axis-mediated macrophage pyroptosis [82]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that gut microbial metabolites are central regulators of the balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory signaling in the intestine. During aging, shifts in gut microbial composition directly alter metabolite profiles, and disruption of the dynamic equilibrium between anti-inflammatory and pro-inflammatory metabolites can precipitate intestinal and systemic inflammation, ultimately contributing to the onset of multiple age-associated diseases (Table 1).

Gut microbial metabolites and aging-related inflammation

Gut microbiota and aging-associated epigenetic alterations

It is well established that aberrant epigenetic modifications constitute a major driver of aging [23]. Recent studies have demonstrated that DNA repair processes promote ageing at physiological, cognitive, and molecular levels, including erosion of the epigenetic landscape, altered cell differentiation, cellular senescence, and the progression of the DNA methylation clock. Loss of epigenetic information has been shown to accelerate ageing in mammals [98]. Similarly, experimental reprogramming of epigenetic marks has revealed that cellular responses to double-strand DNA breaks induce changes in the epigenome, thereby promoting the accumulation of ageing-associated biomarkers, whereas epigenetic remodeling can reverse these alterations [99]. Indeed, epigenetic modifications alone are sufficient to drive ageing, and aberrations in DNA/RNA methylation, histone modifications, nucleosome positioning, non-coding RNA regulation, and three-dimensional chromatin architecture profoundly affect chromatin structure, gene expression, and fundamental cellular functions, establishing them as key determinants of the ageing process [100]. Notably, diverse histone post-translational modifications (PTMs), including DNA/RNA methylation, histone methylation, acetylation, succinylation, crotonylation, and lactylation, are dependent on the activity of epigenetic enzymes. Several metabolites, such as acetyl-CoA, NAD + (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide), and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), serve as substrates or cofactors, providing methyl or acetyl groups for these enzymatic reactions. This suggests that these critical metabolites can dynamically regulate the modifications of DNA, histones, and other proteins, thereby modulating the epigenome during aging.

It is noteworthy that epigenetic regulation has increasingly been recognized as a powerful mechanism through which the microbiota influences host physiology, acting via multiple potential pathways. These include: (1) microbial biosynthesis or metabolism altering the availability of chemical donors for DNA or histone modifications; (2) regulation of the expression and/or activity of epigenetic enzymes; and (3) activation of intrinsic host cellular processes that guide epigenetic pathways. Indeed, the gut microbiota can produce a variety of small bioactive molecules, including SCFAs and other microbial by-products such as β-hydroxybutyrate, folate, and other B vitamins, which serve as epigenetic substrates, cofactors, or modulators of epigenetic enzyme activity. Notably, folate is an essential nutrient synthesized by many symbiotic microbes, including probiotic species such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, which participate in one-carbon metabolism to generate SAM, the principal methyl donor for DNA and histone methylation [101]. Consequently, age-associated alterations in gut microbial composition can influence SAM availability and thereby modulate host DNA or histone methylation. The microbiota exerts broad effects on mammalian DNA methylomes across multiple cell types and tissues under both homeostatic and disease conditions. Transcriptomic and whole-genome DNA methylation analyses comparing germ-free (GF) and conventionally raised (CNV) mice have demonstrated that microbial exposure induces DNA demethylation and upregulates antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory genes [102]. In humans, DNA methylation in colonic biopsies correlates with microbial composition, inflammatory status, and disease classification in patients with ulcerative colitis and Crohn’s disease [103]. Beyond adult mammals, recent studies have highlighted the significance of microbiota-induced DNA methylation during early life. The neonatal gut exhibits unique sensitivity to initial postnatal microbial colonization, with microbial complexity and density expanding into adulthood. This critical early window is essential for postnatal tissue development and the establishment of normal immune responses [104]. Similarly, in vitro studies of human fetal and mature intestinal epithelial cell lines exposed to commensal or pathogenic bacteria have shown differential DNA methylation, with fetal cells demonstrating greater sensitivity to pathogen-induced modifications than adult cells [105]. These findings underscore the importance of microbiota–epigenome interactions during early life, and suggest that the timing, density, and diversity of microbial colonization may influence these interactions into adulthood. In addition to DNA methylation, microbiota-induced histone modifications that alter chromatin accessibility have been reported in multiple tissues and cell populations. SCFAs are key inhibitors of host histone deacetylases (HDACs) [106], influencing histone de-butyrylation and acetylation, and also serving as donor substrates for histone acetyltransferases (HATs) [107]. Intriguingly, the gut microbiota has been shown to globally alter histone H3 and H4 acetylation and methylation across various tissues, including the colon, liver, and adipose tissue, in a diet-dependent manner, and supplementation of SCFAs in GF mice partially restores microbiota-sensitive histone modifications and gene expression [108]. Furthermore, reductions in beneficial metabolites such as SCFAs have been associated with age-related pathophysiological changes and cognitive decline [109]. Collectively, these studies suggest that epigenetic modifications, including histone modifications, may play a central role in mediating host–microbiota interactions, and that dysbiosis of the gut microbiota could contribute to ageing and age-associated diseases via regulation of epigenetic states.

Intestinal aging and barrier dysfunction

The intestine is both the primary digestive organ and the largest immune organ in the human body, playing central roles in nutrient absorption, immune regulation, and hosting a complex and dynamic microbiota. Importantly, the gut functions as a critical physical and chemical barrier against environmental insults while orchestrating the absorption of nutrients essential for systemic homeostasis. It integrates a wide array of signals, including those derived from microbial metabolites, symbiotic microbiota, immune responses, and aging-associated stressors, thereby influencing numerous physiological and pathological processes. Under conditions of homeostasis, the intestine forms a dynamic interface with its microenvironment, including commensal microbes, serving as a protective barrier against external challenges. Early studies have demonstrated that during aging, the combined influence of intrinsic and extrinsic factors leads to functional deterioration of the intestinal tissue, contributing to the onset of age-related diseases [110, 111]. As the intestine is responsible for the absorption of diverse nutrients, its functional decline can disrupt nutrient uptake, alter systemic metabolic states, and ultimately compromise the performance of distal organs. During aging, intestinal digestive and absorptive capacities progressively decline, which is accompanied by profound alterations in the gut microbial community. Notably, the abundance of mucin-degrading Akkermansia muciniphila and SCFA-producing bacteria decreases, whereas pro-inflammatory microbial populations expand [112]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the intestine, as a highly complex organ composed of diverse epithelial and immune cell populations, is particularly susceptible to external and internal signals. Consequently, homeostatic disruption and functional decline of the gut are tightly linked to intestinal aging and the development of gut-associated diseases.

In aging, the intestine is subjected to continuous challenges, necessitating a high turnover rate to replace damaged epithelial cells and maintain its protective functions. The adult intestinal epithelium comprises differentiated cells forming the villi and proliferative cells residing in the crypts. At the base of the crypts, LGR5-expressing intestinal stem cells (ISCs), enriched in leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein-coupled receptors, can differentiate into diverse epithelial cell types—including enterocytes, Paneth cells, goblet cells, microfold cells, and tuft cells—thereby regenerating the epithelium [113, 114]. These differentiated epithelial cells organize into a structured intestinal architecture to preserve homeostasis and execute essential functions, including nutrient absorption, secretion, barrier integrity, and antimicrobial defense. Goblet cells secrete mucus, while Paneth cells release antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) into the luminal mucus layer to regulate microbial homeostasis. At the cellular level, the physiological decline associated with intestinal aging is closely linked to ISC dysfunction. Aging leads to the accumulation of cellular and DNA damage in ISCs [115], resulting in functional consequences such as impaired stem cell proliferation and differentiation, ISC exhaustion, cellular senescence, altered immune and inflammatory responses, and compromised epithelial barrier function. Increasing evidence suggests that intestinal regenerative capacity profoundly influences organismal lifespan [116]. For example, in Drosophila, mild reductions in ISC proliferation are associated with lifespan extension, whereas severe inhibition of ISC proliferation disrupts epithelial regeneration and shortens lifespan [117]. Remarkably, early-life damage or intermittent fasting can induce a mosaic pattern in the intestinal epithelium, alleviating age-associated ISC hyperproliferation, enhancing intercellular junctions, and extending lifespan in flies [118]. Similarly, multiple studies have identified the intestine as a key target organ for longevity-associated genetic interventions [119, 120]. A hallmark of epithelial dysfunction is the inability to maintain selective permeability, a phenomenon commonly observed during aging. Although the impact of intestinal barrier disruption on systemic aging and lifespan has been characterized primarily in Drosophila, age-associated barrier impairments have also been reported in older mammals, including humans. For instance, colonic biopsies from aged baboons exhibit significantly higher intestinal permeability—as measured by translocation of horseradish peroxidase across the epithelium—compared with young counterparts [121]. These findings highlight intestinal barrier dysfunction as a conserved feature of aging. Recent studies indicate that serum zonulin levels, an indirect marker of gut permeability, are elevated in healthy older adults relative to healthy younger individuals [122], and colonic tissue from healthy aged individuals displays alterations in tight junction protein expression [123]. Furthermore, many markers of barrier dysfunction are increased in older adults with cardiometabolic disease compared with age-matched healthy controls [124]. Collectively, these observations suggest that intestinal epithelial permeability closely reflects the structural and functional integrity of intercellular junctions, and that impaired gut barrier integrity represents an evolutionarily conserved pathophysiological feature of aging.

Gut itself aging diseases

Inflammatory bowel disease

Gut aging is associated with a decline in beneficial intestinal microbes, which normally contribute to mucosal barrier integrity and pathogen control through mucus production and lipid metabolite synthesis. Age-related intestinal dysfunction leads to increased gut permeability, which in turn drives alterations in the gut microbiota that are closely linked to intestinal diseases. To some extent, dysbiosis and microbial metabolite changes during aging can trigger chronic inflammation, encompassing both systemic and gut-specific inflammatory responses. IBD, a leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea, is characterized by chronic gastrointestinal inflammation and manifests clinically as persistent abdominal pain, diarrhea, and hematochezia [125, 126]. IBD encompasses Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), and its pathogenesis is influenced by multiple factors, including genetic susceptibility, immune dysregulation, and gut microbiota composition [127, 128]. Among these, the diversity and composition of the gut microbiota play a critical role in IBD development [129]. For example, compared with healthy controls, patients with IBD show decreased levels of beneficial taxa such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia intestinalis, and Bacteroides species, alongside increased relative abundance and growth of pathogenic bacteria, including Bacteroides fragilis [130, 131]. Direct clinical evidence further supports the link between microbial composition and disease activity. In a study of 85 Crohn’s disease patients treated with ustekinumab, fecal metagenomic analyses revealed that individuals achieving clinical remission exhibited increased microbial diversity and enrichment of beneficial taxa such as F. prausnitzii and Dysosmobacter welbionis, which correlated inversely with inflammatory markers. Mechanistically, F. prausnitzii can drive L-ornithine production, which interacts with Th17 cell-specific EGR1 protein to suppress IL-12RB1 transcription, thereby inhibiting the IL-23/IL-12RB1/TYK2/STAT3 signaling cascade and reducing Th17-mediated pro-inflammatory activity [132]. These findings indicate that IBD is characterized by gut microbial dysbiosis, resulting in an imbalance between beneficial and pathogenic taxa and disruption of the intestinal microbial barrier. IBD pathogenesis is also closely linked to epithelial cell metabolism. NOD-like receptor family member X1 (NLRX1), a mitochondria-associated Nod-like receptor, exhibits anti-inflammatory effects in colitis. In NLRX1 knockout mice, colitis-associated pathogens such as Veillonella spp. and Clostridium species are enriched, whereas glutamine supplementation mitigates inflammation and reshapes the gut microbiota [133]. Glutamine metabolism, which is influenced by the gut microbiota (e.g., Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron), thus provides a mechanistic link between age-related microbial alterations and IBD development [134]. Other microbial metabolites, such as N-acylethanolamines, have been associated with IBD, correlating with Proteobacteria enrichment and Bacteroides depletion; targeting these metabolites may offer therapeutic potential [135]. Immune dysregulation in aging further modulates IBD risk. Enhanced antibody and T cell responses to microbial antigens are common in IBD patients [136]. Age-associated immune senescence and chronic low-grade inflammation influence disease onset, with multiple immune cell types and inflammatory mediators participating. T cell subset differentiation, shaped by microbial interactions, is particularly important: excessive Th17 activity and impaired Treg function contribute to uncontrolled mucosal immune responses, culminating in epithelial injury [137]. For instance, segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB) colonization in the murine small intestine promotes IL-17 and IL-22 secretion from lamina propria Th17 cells, exacerbating gut inflammation [138]. IL-22 plays a protective role in acute colitis, but in concert with IL-17A, it mediates chronic colitis pathogenesis [139]. Collectively, these studies indicate that age-related gut microbial dysbiosis, cytokine imbalance, and mucosal barrier disruption synergize to drive mucosal inflammation and IBD progression. Moreover, genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, and age-associated organ functional decline can further perturb host–microbe interactions, highlighting the complex interplay between gut aging, immune regulation, and chronic intestinal disease.

Colorectal cancer

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents a major global health challenge, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed and the second deadliest malignancy worldwide [140]. Consistent with age-associated shifts in gut microbiota, early studies reported enrichment of Lactobacillales in tumor tissues, accompanied by a reduction in Faecalibacterium. Within the mucosa-adherent microbiota, taxa such as Clostridium, Porphyromonas, Actinomyces, and Mogibacterium are enriched in CRC patients, whereas SCFA-producing bacteria, including Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, and Blautia, are depleted [141]. These findings suggest a close link between gut microbial alterations and CRC pathogenesis. Mechanistically, age-associated gut dysbiosis may contribute to CRC through multiple pathways, including promotion of inflammation, pathogenic bacterial adherence, tumor initiation, and chemotherapy resistance. Aging is accompanied by impaired gut function, which is prevalent in populations over 40 years old. Gut aging involves morphological changes, slowed peristalsis, reduced absorptive capacity, impaired epithelial regeneration, and ecological imbalance. Advanced proteomic analyses of human CRC samples reveal age-dependent epigenetic modifications, altered differentiation patterns of ISCs, dysfunction of the enteric nervous system, and pivotal roles of the gut microbiota in regulating healthy aging [142]. Collectively, these observations underscore the intimate relationship between gut aging and CRC development. Metabolomics studies further illustrate the mechanistic link. Lipidomic profiling of tumor tissue, serum, and feces from CRC patients identified significant elevation of C26 ceramide, an ultra-long-chain ceramide, which correlated positively with clinical indicators of tumor progression. Riboflavin produced by gut microbes inhibits ceramide synthase CERS3, thereby reducing C26 ceramide levels, attenuating its activation of the EGFR signaling pathway, and suppressing CRC proliferation. This highlights that microbe-derived riboflavin metabolism can modulate ceramide dysregulation and downstream oncogenic signaling in CRC. Multi-cohort metagenomic analyses have shown that the abundance of Clostridium butyricum is significantly reduced in the feces of CRC patients compared with healthy individuals. Moreover, responders to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy exhibit higher C. butyricum abundance than non-responders. A newly isolated strain, C. butyricum Hkyucb11, derived from healthy adult feces, inhibited proliferation of CRC cell lines (HT29, RKO, DLD1) in vitro, induced apoptosis and G1/S cell cycle arrest, and showed no toxicity to normal colonic epithelial cells. The antitumor effect of C. butyricum relies on the specific interaction between its surface protein SecD and GRP78, a receptor overexpressed on CRC cells but minimally present on normal epithelial cells, enabling targeted modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment and potentiation of immunotherapy [143]. Aging-related shifts in the gut microbiota may therefore influence riboflavin metabolism and CRC progression. Gut microbiota and microbial metabolites also regulate chemotherapy resistance and immunotherapy outcomes in CRC. Chemotherapy resistance is a major cause of treatment failure. Bacteroides fragilis has been shown to promote chemoresistance, which can be reversed by phage VA7 specifically targeting and eliminating B. fragilis, restoring chemosensitivity [144]. Fecal metagenomic analyses of immunotherapy cohorts identified Alistipes indistinctus as a beneficial commensal associated with favorable clinical outcomes. Preclinical studies demonstrated that live A. indistinctus administration enhances immunotherapy efficacy and prolongs survival in mice, without inducing systemic toxicity, confirming its safety and effectiveness [145]. Collectively, these studies highlight the central regulatory role of the gut microbiota and microbial metabolites in CRC development. In elderly individuals, gut dysbiosis may exacerbate chronic inflammation, promote pathogenic bacterial adhesion, induce tumor initiation, and contribute to chemoresistance, emphasizing the interplay between gut aging and colorectal carcinogenesis.

Gut microbiota–mediated aging-associated diseases

The dynamic shifts of the gut microbiota from birth through old age profoundly influence host health and disease throughout the lifespan. Importantly, the intestinal system establishes functional connections with other organs and tissues, including the brain, liver, lungs, heart, skin, and skeletal muscle, many of which are mediated by the activity and signaling of the gut microbiota (Table 2). This underscores that the gut microbiota and its metabolites are central to maintaining host homeostasis, and that dysregulation can precipitate disease. Indeed, bidirectional interactions between the gut and extraintestinal tissues often undergo alterations prior to the onset of ageing-associated diseases. Therefore, elucidating the dynamic changes of the gut microbiota during human ageing is critical for understanding the signaling crosstalk and mechanistic interactions along the gut–extraintestinal axes, thereby providing insights into both healthy and pathological aging.

Gut–muscle axis aging

Skeletal muscle aging and gut microbiota alterations

Skeletal muscle aging is a hallmark of physical frailty, characterized by the loss of muscle mass and function, which increases the risk of adverse outcomes such as falls, fractures, disability, and mortality. Skeletal muscle accounts for approximately 40–50% of total body mass and serves as a major reservoir of amino acids for protein storage [146]. It exhibits remarkable plasticity, adapting throughout life to diverse stimuli, including neural activation, mechanical load, growth factors, and nutritional status [147]. As a central organ for locomotion and systemic metabolism, skeletal muscle undergoes physiological aging, forming the basis for sarcopenia and muscle atrophy. Structurally, skeletal muscle aging encompasses age-related morphological alterations and functional decline, including both intrinsic biological aging and pathological aging [148]. Pathological features include reductions in muscle mass, decreased myofiber cross-sectional area, microstructural abnormalities of myofibers, lipid infiltration, fibrosis, and fiber-type shifts [149, 150]. Functional consequences involve impaired contractility and regenerative capacity, reduced myokine secretion, and metabolic dysregulation [151]. The Aging Biomarkers Consortium of China (ABC) has established expert consensus on skeletal muscle aging biomarkers by integrating insights from the scientific literature and clinical practice. This framework systematically categorizes biomarkers across three dimensions: functional (e.g., physical activity, muscle contractility, grip strength), structural (e.g., fiber number, muscle composition), and humoral (e.g., circulating blood markers and hormones). Within each domain, specific biomarkers were recommended to assess clinically relevant aspects of muscle aging, including those that accurately reflect “biological muscle age” in terms of both structure and function [152]. Notably, recent studies using young and aged nonhuman primate models (cynomolgus monkeys) have revealed profound histological and molecular features of skeletal muscle aging. Aged muscle exhibited reduced myofiber cross-sectional area, increased expression of the aging marker Fbx32, higher incidence of centrally nucleated fibers, fast-to-slow fiber-type transitions, enhanced fibrotic and lipid deposition, degeneration of neuromuscular junctions, and reductions in both skeletal muscle stem cells and vascular endothelial cells [153].

Skeletal muscle, akin to the gut microbiota, functions as a signaling organ through the secretion of various myokines. Under homeostatic conditions, muscle protein undergoes a dynamic equilibrium between synthesis and degradation. The balance between muscle protein synthesis (MPS) and muscle protein breakdown (MPB) is critical for the maintenance of muscle mass and functional capacity [154]. The gut microbiota acts as a “metabolic organ” capable of modulating host metabolism, and a diverse, intact microbial community contributes to systemic homeostasis. However, dysbiosis and the consequent loss of microbiota-derived metabolites can profoundly disrupt skeletal muscle metabolism. Mechanistically, aging of the gut–muscle axis involves inflammation, immunosenescence, anabolic resistance, and increased oxidative stress [155]. Early studies have demonstrated that in elderly individuals, the relative abundances of Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium negatively correlate with muscle strength, whereas Eggerthella and Holdemanella positively correlate with frailty [156]. More recently, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have been used to explore the genetic correlation and potential causal relationships between gut microbes and frailty, revealing a suggestive genetic association between Christensenellaceae R-7 and frailty. Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses further identified 12 gut microbial genera with potential causal effects on frailty [157]. Elucidating the molecular and cellular biomarkers of skeletal muscle aging, alongside age-related alterations in gut microbiota and their metabolites, is essential to uncovering the mechanisms by which the gut–muscle axis regulates muscle atrophy and aging-associated muscle disorders. Such insights may provide novel targets to preserve muscle function and mitigate frailty, ultimately improving healthspan and quality of life in older adults.

Mechanisms of gut–muscle axis signaling

The gut microbiota, often referred to as a “central metabolic organ,” has emerged as a key regulator of host metabolism and immune homeostasis. Increasing evidence indicates that the gut microbiota modulates skeletal muscle metabolism via the gut–muscle axis, influencing metabolite production, hormone levels, and immune balance, thereby affecting muscle function. Dysbiosis and the loss of microbial diversity compromise intestinal barrier integrity, allowing harmful microbial products, such as LPS, to enter the circulation. These circulating factors trigger systemic inflammation, perturb metabolic homeostasis, and contribute to muscle dysfunction and atrophy [158]. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) are central regulators of cellular metabolism, autophagy, inflammation, insulin sensitivity, and skeletal muscle physiology. Notably, AMPK and PGC-1α signaling are implicated in gut microbiota–muscle communication [159], with AMPK specifically regulating muscle fiber size [160]. In GFmice, activation of AMPK signaling suggests that the AMPK/FOXO3/Atrogin-1/MuRF-1 pathway may mediate gut microbiota–dependent regulation of skeletal muscle [161]. Thus, AMPK and PGC-1α pathways integrate signals related to autophagy and inflammation to influence muscle mass and function. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, a well-characterized mediator of insulin resistance [162], converges on mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), a downstream effector. mTOR promotes protein synthesis through phosphorylation and inactivation of eukaryotic initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1) and phosphorylation and activation of ribosomal S6 kinase 1 (S6K1). Gut microbiota-derived insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) can activate PI3K/AKT signaling to stimulate mTOR activity, thereby promoting protein synthesis [163]. Conversely, excessive inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS) can inhibit mTOR signaling via AMPK activation [164], highlighting a mechanism by which microbiota-mediated inflammatory responses influence gut–muscle communication through mTOR. Gut microbes also produce bile acids (BAs) that interact with host BAs receptors, including the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), to regulate glucose and lipid metabolism [165]. FXR activation in the ileum induces fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19; FGF15 in rodents), which promotes muscle protein synthesis. Dysbiosis has been shown to impair BA/FXR/FGF15/19/ERK signaling, limiting protein synthesis and contributing to muscle atrophy [166]. Mitochondrial dysfunction in skeletal muscle further exacerbates the decline in muscle mass and function [167]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the gut microbiota communicates with skeletal muscle through multiple mechanisms, including regulation of inflammatory and immune responses, protein synthesis, energy and lipid metabolism, neuromuscular junction integrity, oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, endocrine signaling, and insulin sensitivity. Disruption of these pathways in aging may contribute to sarcopenia and frailty, highlighting the gut–muscle axis as a potential therapeutic target.

Gut–muscle axis-associated diseases

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an antibody-mediated autoimmune disorder characterized by fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness and fatigue. Pathogenic antibodies targeting the acetylcholine receptor (AChR), muscle-specific kinase (MuSK), and low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 4 (LRP4) disrupt post-synaptic neuromuscular junction (NMJ) transmission [168]. Early studies revealed pronounced gut microbiota dysbiosis in MG patients compared with healthy controls (HCs), with significant alterations in microbial metabolites involved in amino acid, lipid, and nucleotide metabolism [169–171]. Specifically, MG patients exhibited reduced abundance of Firmicutes, Clostridia, Bacillus, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, while Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Streptococcus, and Parabacteroides were enriched. Notably, the abundance of Clostridia in HCs was approximately threefold higher than in MG patients [172]. MG pathogenesis is closely linked to elevated circulating AChR antibodies [173], which are influenced by imbalances among Th1, B, and Foxp3 + regulatory T (Treg) cells [174, 175]. Importantly, MG patients exhibit a notable reduction in Foxp3 + CD4 + Treg cell frequency, a critical factor in disease onset. Evidence indicates that perturbations in gut microbial composition may underlie the Treg deficiency, potentially via microbial metabolite–mediated epigenetic modulation of Treg differentiation [176]. Moreover, gut microbiota and their metabolites can disrupt intestinal barrier integrity and skew the Th17/Treg balance, further contributing to MG pathophysiology [177]. Notably, preclinical studies using female Lewis rats as a model of MG, together with GF mice colonized with gut microbiota from patients with MG, have provided important mechanistic insights. The first investigation demonstrated that therapeutic probiotics, including Lactobacillus crispatus LMG P-23257, Lactobacillus rhamnosus ATCC 53103, Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB12, and B. animalis subsp. lactis LMG S-28195, effectively alleviated MG symptoms [178]. Moreover, blood-based analyses revealed strong associations between microbial dysbiosis and inflammatory biomarkers, suggesting that disruptions of the gut microbiota may promote chronic inflammation and compromise systemic immune responses. Importantly, clinical intervention studies have also shown therapeutic benefits. In patients with MG, administration of a composite Guangqi decoction significantly improved clinical symptoms. Microbiome profiling further revealed distinct compositional shifts compared with untreated controls, characterized by reduced abundance of Blautia and Bacteroides, and increased levels of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus at the genus level [179]. Collectively, these studies highlight the profound impact of microbial composition, diversity, and metabolite profiles on MG.

Sarcopenia, a prevalent chronic muscle disorder, is characterized by reduced muscle fiber cross-sectional area, decreased muscle mass and protein content, impaired force generation, and fiber-type shifts [180]. This condition arises from an imbalance between protein synthesis and degradation, with excessive proteolysis and muscle wasting posing significant morbidity and mortality risks. Maintaining optimal muscle health is therefore essential for preventing metabolic dysregulation and promoting healthy aging. Early studies demonstrated that restoring gut microbiota in mice treated with antidepressants improved running endurance and ex vivo fatigue resistance of extensor digitorum longus muscles, alongside increases in muscle mass and muscle mass-to-body weight ratio [181]. Supplementation with specific probiotic strains, such as Lactobacillus casei or Bifidobacterium longum, similarly enhanced muscle mass-to-body weight ratio in aged mice without affecting overall body weight [182]. Moreover, short-term severe physical inactivity induces muscle atrophy, with significant reductions in leg lean mass (-2.5%) as measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), accompanied by notable changes in operational taxonomic units (OTUs) associated with Clostridiales and the Lachnospiraceae family [183]. This indicates a relationship between muscle atrophy and gut microbial composition, with specific microbial taxa (e.g., Lachnospiraceae) potentially playing pivotal roles in the low-activity–gut microbiota–muscle axis. Gut microbiota can also regulate key metabolites such as betaine, influencing N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification and expression of slow-twitch muscle fiber marker Myh7, thereby modulating fiber-type composition [184]. Collectively, these findings underscore a comprehensive and systemic role of the gut microbiota in skeletal muscle regulation, potentially involving complex signaling network interactions. Increasing evidence from preclinical studies further implicates gut microbiota and microbiota-derived metabolites—including SCFA and bile acids—in skeletal muscle metabolism. Gut microbiota-dependent SCFA maintain muscle homeostasis by promoting mitochondrial function, whereas SCFA depletion can induce a pro-inflammatory gut environment, impairing muscle function [185]. Bile acids, synthesized in the liver and further metabolized by gut microbes, modulate host skeletal muscle function via nuclear receptor signaling, including the FXR [186]. These observations suggest that age-related gut microbiota alterations may promote sarcopenia through disruption of the bile acid–FXR pathway. Consistently, studies in older men show that changes in intestinal FXR expression correlate with reduced muscle mass, grip strength, and microbial diversity, indicating that gut dysbiosis and consequent FXR–FGF15/19 signaling dysregulation may underlie age-related muscle atrophy [187]. In a double-blind clinical study, administration of Lactobacillus plantarum TWK10, a probiotic strain isolated from fermented cabbage, led to increased muscle mass and strength in the left hand and lower limbs after six weeks of supplementation, with more pronounced improvements observed when the intervention was extended to 18 weeks [188]. Similarly, another clinical trial reported that TWK10 supplementation was associated with gains in muscle mass and strength in specific muscle groups. An eight-week TWK10 intervention produced beneficial effects on the gut microbiota, characterized by an enrichment of SCFA-producing bacteria accompanied by elevated SCFA levels [189]. These beneficial outcomes may be attributable, at least in part, to the suppression of age-associated pathogenic bacteria.

Gut–brain axis aging

Brain aging and gut microbiota alterations

The brain serves as the body’s central command center, integrating sensory, motor, and autonomic functions. During aging, the brain undergoes biochemical, cellular, structural, and functional changes, some of which serve as potential biomarkers for monitoring and evaluating brain aging. Human-based postmortem studies in elderly individuals without diagnosed neurological diseases consistently report age-associated neuropathological features, including amyloid plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, Lewy bodies, TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43) inclusions, synaptic dystrophy, neuronal loss, and widespread brain volume reduction [190]. While the precise causes of these age-related changes and their links to neurodegenerative diseases remain unclear, these observations suggest partial overlap between physiological aging and neurodegeneration.

With advances in studying brain aging, perspectives on the microstructural basis of observed macrostructural changes have evolved. One key debate centers on neuronal loss. Early studies reported substantial age-associated neuronal loss, ranging from 10 to 60%, depending on the study and the neuronal population examined, with cortical, hippocampal, and cerebellar Purkinje neurons considered particularly vulnerable [191–193]. However, more recent findings indicate that certain brain regions, such as cranial nerve nuclei, do not exhibit significant neuronal loss during aging [194], highlighting the complexity of neuronal decline in age-related functional deterioration. In contrast, neuronal size exhibits a more consistent pattern; moderate reductions in neuronal cell body size, particularly in the cortex, are observed with aging. Neuronal size reflects dendritic and axonal arborization, with larger cell bodies required to support increased energy demands and protein synthesis [195, 196]. Neuroimaging studies further confirm pronounced morphological changes in aging brains, including reductions in brain volume, gray and white matter lesions, neuronal atrophy, dendritic degeneration, demyelination, metabolic deficits, and microglial activation [197]. Pathological accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ), tau, and α-synuclein in the brain serves as hallmark features of neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases [198]. Collectively, these studies underscore the close relationship between brain aging and organismal aging, with age-associated cerebral decline contributing to neuronal loss, brain volume reduction, and eventual neurodegenerative disease.

Importantly, gut microbiota not only shape local immunity but also influence brain aging and the risk of neurodegenerative disorders. Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), play key roles in immune surveillance and neuroprotection [199]. Gut microbes interact with the brain and modulate microglial homeostasis. In aging brains, microbiota contribute to increased oxidative stress in cerebellar microglia, partially through direct mitochondrial damage [200]. During aging, an increased abundance of Ruminococcaceae produces metabolites such as indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), which regulate the expression of pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic factor S100A8, promoting microglial apoptosis and ultimately contributing to cognitive decline [201]. These findings highlight the influence of gut microbiota and their metabolites on brain aging.

Evidence suggests that the gut microbiota may even precede detectable brain aging. Gut microbial composition correlates with cognitive function and depressive symptoms and may serve as a predictive biomarker for future cognitive decline and mood alterations [202]. Remarkably, FMT from young to aged mice reverses age-associated immune alterations, restores brain vitality, and re-establishes metabolic and gene expression profiles akin to young brains. Behavioral assays of learning, memory, and anxiety further demonstrate improved performance in aged mice receiving young donor microbiota [203]. These results directly confirm that transplantation of youthful gut microbiota can counteract age-related brain changes and ameliorate features of brain aging. Collectively, these studies support the concept that gut microbiota and their metabolites modulate brain aging via the gut–brain axis and play critical roles in mediating the progression of neurodegenerative diseases.

Mechanisms of gut–brain axis signaling

The gut–brain axis refers to the bidirectional communication network between the gastrointestinal tract and the brain, mediated by metabolic, endocrine, neural, and immune pathways. This complex system is regulated by the gut microbiota, gut-derived hormones, the enteric nervous system (ENS), the CNS, and nutrient signals. Key components include the vagus nerve, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, bacterial metabolites, immune mediators, and enteroendocrine signaling. Stress triggers the principal neuroendocrine response via activation of the HPA axis, beginning with stimulation of parvocellular neurons in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN), followed by the release of neuropeptides such as corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and vasopressin (VP). These signals stimulate pituitary secretion of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which in turn drives glucocorticoid release from the adrenal cortex, a process essential for stress adaptation [204, 205]. Activation of the vagal pathway can modulate chronic stress and influence intestinal stem cell function [206]. Increased gut permeability and alterations in microbial composition are associated with elevated cortisol levels, which exacerbate inflammation and contribute to CNS disorders and cognitive impairment [207]. Certain gut microbes can directly stimulate ENS afferent neurons, transmitting signals to the brain via the vagus nerve. For example, Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species enhance HPA axis function and modulate stress-related behavior, whereas GF mice exhibit HPA axis dysregulation with elevated plasma corticosterone and ACTH levels [208]. These findings suggest a complex, bidirectional communication between gut microbiota and the brain, which may influence age-related cerebral decline. Increasing evidence supports a tight gut–brain interaction, in which microbial metabolites exert neuromodulatory effects. The hippocampus is a pivotal structure in both the healthy brain and in neuropsychiatric disorders, and alterations in the gut microbiota exert substantial effects on hippocampus-dependent learning and memory. Emerging evidence indicates that the composition of the gut microbial community correlates with hippocampal function in older adults [209],, whereas antibiotic-induced dysbiosis has been shown to impair hippocampal performance [210]. The gut microbiota also regulates neurodevelopment through its cell-wall constituents and by modulating systemic cytokine levels. For example, peptidoglycan fragments derived from bacterial cell walls can cross the blood–brain barrier and activate pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) in the brain. Recognition of peptidoglycan in the developing prefrontal cortex, striatum and cerebellum may influence synaptogenesis [211]. Microbiota-derived neuroactive compounds can influence behavior, and in some autism mouse models, specific metabolites ameliorate behavioral deficits while modulating neuronal excitability [212]. Furthermore, fear extinction learning in mice requires the presence of a functional gut microbiota [213]. Similarly, the gut-dependent production of endocannabinoid metabolites stimulates TRPV1-expressing sensory neurons, thereby elevating dopaminergic activity in the ventral striatum during physical exercise [214]. Collectively, these findings underscore the substantial regulatory influence of gut microbes and their metabolites on brain function.

The gut–brain axis not only modulates local intestinal physiology but also monitors microbiota composition and dietary inputs. However, the specific signals that convey microbial status to the CNS, ultimately influencing gut physiology, remain incompletely understood. The autonomic nervous system (ANS), comprising sympathetic and parasympathetic branches, innervates smooth muscle and glands to regulate visceral function. Within the gastrointestinal tract, the ENS—often referred to as the “second brain”—serves as the primary intrinsic neural network. Using chemogenetics, neuronal tracing, lesion studies, and transcriptomic analyses, researchers have identified specific vagal afferents that drive gut sympathetic activity. Notably, projections from nodose ganglia to distinct brain regions provide evidence for a gut–brain–gut circuit, through which gut microbes and their metabolites modulate sympathetic neuron activation, while specific brainstem nuclei integrate gut-specific stimuli [215]. Dysbiosis-driven neurodegenerative mechanisms can be summarized in three steps. First, microbial imbalance reduces beneficial SCFAproduction, increases intestinal permeability, and allows toxins such as LPS and pathogenic bacteria to enter systemic circulation, triggering inflammation. Second, systemic inflammation compromises the blood–brain barrier (BBB), permitting neurotoxic molecules, including LPS and amyloid precursor proteins, to infiltrate the CNS. Third, neuroinflammation and neuronal damage ensue: neurotoxic molecules activate central immune cells, induce sustained neuroinflammation, promote aberrant β-amyloid deposition and tau hyperphosphorylation, and ultimately drive neuronal degeneration and cognitive decline [216]. Collectively, these observations highlight that gut–brain axis interactions are mediated by a complex network involving gut microbiota, enteroendocrine signals, ENS, CNS, and microbial metabolites, forming the mechanistic basis for gut-mediated modulation of brain aging and neurodegeneration.

Gut–brain axis–associated diseases

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathology is thought to progress from normal cognition to preclinical disease and eventually to symptomatic AD, characterized by progressive cognitive decline and selective loss of specific neuronal populations and synapses. The most prominent pathological hallmarks of AD are amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles [217]. Amyloid plaques result from extracellular accumulation of abnormally folded Aβpeptides, typically 40 or 42 amino acids in length (Aβ40 and Aβ42), which are cleavage products of amyloid precursor protein (APP). Neurofibrillary tangles consist of paired helical filaments composed of hyperphosphorylated tau, a microtubule-stabilizing protein [218]. Notably, symptomatic AD patients exhibit distinct alterations in gut microbiota composition compared to cognitively normal controls. Deep fecal metagenomic sequencing of Chinese cohorts across multiple AD stages revealed widespread dysbiosis during disease progression, with specific bacterial taxa associated with neuroinflammation and neurotransmitter dysregulation, key pathological events in AD [219]. Similar observations were reported in Puerto Rican cohorts, where AD patients exhibited decreased Bacteroides and Faecalibacterium, alongside enrichment of proinflammatory taxa such as Eggerthella, particularly in APOE ε4 carriers. Reduced microbial diversity correlated with cognitive impairment severity, and APOE ε4 carriers showed diminished abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria, suggesting that gut dysbiosis may promote AD pathology via the gut–brain axis [220]. Further evidence indicates that gut microbiota alterations occur in preclinical AD. In a study of 164 cognitively normal individuals, changes in gut microbial composition were associated with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarkers of Aβ and tau pathology, but not with neurodegenerative biomarkers, suggesting early microbial shifts in the disease course. Specific bacterial taxa were identified as predictive of preclinical AD, improving machine learning classifier performance in distinguishing individuals at risk [221]. In elderly AD participants, gut microbiota structural changes—such as reduced Firmicutes, increased Bacteroidetes, and decreased Bifidobacterium—may increase intestinal permeability, induce systemic inflammation, and elevate AD risk [222]. Metabolic profiling revealed strong correlations between dysregulated microbial metabolites—including conjugated bile acids, branched-chain amino acids, and glutamate—and cognitive decline, clinical stage, and brain Aβ deposition, highlighting the critical role of metabolite-mediated nitrogen metabolism in AD pathogenesis [223]. Experimental evidence supports the functional contribution of gut microbiota to AD pathology. Germ-free AD mice exhibit reduced Aβ plaques, neurofibrillary tangles, and improved cognition, whereas microbiota transplantation from AD patients exacerbates pathology through pathways such as C/EBPβ/AEP signaling and polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism, promoting neuroinflammation [224]. Notably, clinical trials assessing the efficacy of probiotics for AD are also underway. In one study, 30 patients with AD received 200 mL of probiotic-enriched milk daily (2 × 10⁹ CFU/g each) for 12 weeks. Compared with controls, participants showed improvements in MMSE scores, increased insulin sensitivity, reduced serum levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), and overall better metabolic regulation after the intervention [225]. These findings suggest that probiotic supplementation may enhance both cognitive performance and metabolic function in individuals with AD. Similarly, in another trial in which 25 patients with AD were administered 3 × 10⁹ CFU of probiotics daily for 12 weeks, investigators observed increases in TYM scores and improvements in cognitive function [226]. Collectively, these studies provide preliminary evidence that interventions targeting the gut or gut microbiota hold substantial translational potential for aging-related diseases.

Parkinson’s disease (PD), the second most common neurodegenerative disorder after AD, predominantly affects individuals aged ≥ 65 and is closely linked to aging. The disease is characterized by α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregation and dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra, resulting in motor (bradykinesia, rigidity, postural instability, resting tremor) and non-motor symptoms (constipation, anxiety, sleep disturbances, autonomic dysfunction) that severely impact quality of life [227]. Early studies identified reduced abundance of Prevotella in PD patient fecal samples, negatively correlating with disease severity [228]. Moreover, Akkermansia, a mucin-degrading bacterium, is enriched, while SCFAs producers such as Roseburia and Faecalibacterium are depleted in PD patients [229]. Similar trends were observed in rotenone-induced PD mouse models, with increased Desulfovibrio and Akkermansia abundances [230]. In addition, an Italian cohort study analyzed fecal samples from 80 individuals with PD and 72 healthy controls, accounting for potential confounders such as age, sex and weight loss. Compared with controls, patients with PD exhibited significantly reduced abundances of Helicobacteriaceae, Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcaceae, alongside a marked increase in Lactobacillaceae. Alterations in the levels of members of the Prevotellaceae and Lachnospiraceae families were further associated with greater disease severity and worsening motor dysfunction [231]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the gut microbiota of patients with PD, as well as that of PD animal models, differs markedly in composition from that of healthy individuals, and that such microbial alterations are closely associated with PD pathogenesis. Notably, a study employing 16S rRNA gene sequencing assessed fecal microbiota profiles from 197 patients with PD and 130 controls. After adjusting for confounders such as age, sex, diet and medication use, the authors identified seven family-level, eight genus-level and thirteen OTU-level taxa associated with PD. A significant reduction was observed in SCFA-producing bacteria, including members of the Lachnospiraceae family [232]. However, the investigators cautioned that SCFA depletion may represent a consequence of PD rather than its cause or a definitive biomarker, and therefore should not be considered a PD-specific signature. Importantly, a number of FDA-defined phase clinical trials have been completed or are ongoing. For example, a phase III, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial evaluated 55 patients with idiopathic PD who received either a multi-strain probiotic formulation or placebo twice daily for eight weeks (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04451096). Improvements in bowel movement frequency and intestinal transit time were observed in PD patients with constipation [233]. Together, these studies suggest that interventions targeting the gut microbiota may hold considerable therapeutic potential for PD. Well-designed, placebo-controlled randomized trials with larger cohorts, longer follow-up periods and detailed characterization of microbial transplants will be essential for evaluating safety, adverse effects and underlying mechanisms.

Gut–heart axis aging

Cardiac aging and gut microbiota alterations

Biological aging encompasses a gradual decline in cellular and organ structure and function. Cardiac aging manifests as diastolic dysfunction, left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy, increased susceptibility to atrial fibrillation, and heightened infection risk due to immunosenescence. While some of these changes are disease-related, many represent intrinsic, age-associated alterations. Structural, functional, and molecular changes in the aging heart may render older individuals more vulnerable to therapy-related complications [234]. Autopsy and magnetic resonance imaging studies consistently indicate reduced cardiac mass in elderly individuals, with women maintaining cardiac mass in the absence of hypertension [235]. Age-related cardiac decline accelerates sharply after 50 years, mirroring the decline observed in other organ systems [236]. Aging hearts exhibit accumulation of ROS and lipids, accompanied by hypertrophy and diffuse fibrosis. Collagen deposition increases myocardial stiffness and reduces compliance, impairing diastolic function and predisposing to heart failure [237]. Progressive cardiomyocyte hypertrophy is another hallmark, reflecting a compensatory response to cell loss and heightened functional demand, but ultimately reducing cardiac reserve [238].

Clinical and preclinical studies increasingly implicate the gut microbiota in myocardial fibrosis and heart failure. Gut microbial alterations have been observed in heart failure patients, including bacterial overgrowth and enhanced mucosal biofilm formation [239]. More recent analyses detected higher abundances of pathogens such as Candida, Campylobacter, Shigella, and Yersinia in fecal samples from patients, correlating with disease severity [240]. More recent analyses detected higher abundances of pathogens such as Candida, Campylobacter, Shigella, and Yersinia in fecal samples from patients, correlating with disease severity [241], suggesting that gut microbial dysbiosis is closely linked to cardiac aging. 16S rRNA gene sequencing is now the standard approach for profiling gut bacterial populations. In transverse aortic constriction–induced cardiac remodeling models, 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples revealed reduced microbial production of tryptophan and SCFAs in wild-type mice [242]. Clinically, Clostridioides difficile infection is significantly increased in heart failure patients at discharge, after adjusting for patient and hospital characteristics [243]. Genes involved in LPS synthesis and in generating the pro-atherogenic metabolite TMAO are enriched in the gut microbiota of heart failure patients. In elderly patients with congestive heart failure, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii—a major butyrate producer—is decreased, highlighting its regulatory role in anti-inflammatory function [244]. Collectively, clinical and preclinical evidence indicates that gut microbial alterations significantly influence heart failure progression: beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium are depleted, whereas potentially pathogenic taxa, including Clostridium and Bacteroides, are enriched. Impaired intestinal barrier function facilitates bacterial translocation and abnormal metabolite production, amplifying inflammation and promoting adverse cardiac remodeling.

Mechanisms of the gut–heart axis signaling

Notably, an early hypothesis—the “gut leakage” hypothesis—proposed that heart failure (HF) is accompanied by intestinal wall edema and impaired barrier integrity, which facilitate the translocation of gut microbiota and their components into the host circulation, thereby triggering endotoxemia and exacerbating systemic inflammation [245]. Accumulating evidence suggests that bacterial translocation during HF is promoted by one or more mechanisms, including gastrointestinal structural and functional alterations secondary to visceral congestion and abnormalities in host immune defenses. A stable gut microbiota supports the production of SCFAs, BAs, and other metabolites that contribute to cardiac homeostasis, forming a regulatory network often referred to as the “microbiota–gut–heart axis” (MGHA). Importantly, gut microorganisms can also exert direct effects on the failing heart. Increased intestinal permeability and disruption of the gut barrier are frequently accompanied by local inflammation and enhanced microbial translocation, allowing pathogenic taxa and their toxins to enter the bloodstream, which in turn sustains chronic systemic inflammation and promotes the progression of chronic HF [246]. This mechanism may underlie, at least in part, the heightened inflammatory state observed in patients with HF.

The gut microbiota can also influence the failing heart indirectly through the production of diverse metabolites. The substrates for microbial metabolism are primarily derived from dietary components that escape host digestion and from mucins secreted by intestinal epithelial cells. Microbial activity generates a wide spectrum of metabolites, encompassing both deleterious and beneficial compounds, such as lipopolysaccharides, peptidoglycans, trimethylamine (TMA), BAs, and SCFAs [247]. Among these, the TMAO pathway—originating from dietary choline—has received particular attention. In a mouse model of doxorubicin-induced cardiac fibrosis, a TMAO-enriched diet aggravated cardiac dysfunction and fibrosis. Mechanistically, TMAO promotes collagen deposition, fibrosis, and pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in a dose-dependent manner by activating the transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1)/Smad3 signaling cascade [248]. TMAO has also been shown to enhance the production of inflammatory cytokines and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species, thereby eliciting inflammatory responses and directly damaging cardiomyocytes, suggesting its potential as a promising biomarker of cardiovascular risk [249]. SCFAs, including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are produced by colonic bacteria or yeasts through the anaerobic fermentation of dietary fibers. Key SCFA-producing taxa include Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, members of the phylum Firmicutes, Ruminococcus, Lactobacillus, Clostridium, and Streptococcus [250]. Bacteria that generate acetate have been identified as potential protective agents against hypertension, maladaptive cardiac hypertrophy, and fibrotic remodeling [251]. SCFAs also serve as alternative oxidative substrates for ATP generation, thereby compensating for the impaired oxidation of long-chain fatty acids in the hypertrophic heart [252]. These findings underscore the critical role of SCFAs in the prevention and management of heart failure. BAs, traditionally recognized as emulsifiers that facilitate intestinal absorption of fats and fat-soluble vitamins, also contribute to gut–heart communication. Primary BAs, such as cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), are synthesized in the liver from cholesterol. In a recent small cohort study comparing patients with heart failure and sex-matched healthy controls, the ratio of primary to secondary BAs was significantly reduced in patients with heart failure. Moreover, both the total concentration and compositional profile of circulating BAs were markedly altered, with plasma BAs levels being significantly lower than those in healthy controls [253]. Such BAs pattern changes have been associated with decreased overall survival in patients with heart failure. Notably, the gut–heart axis is modulated by a variety of host factors, including sex, age, and lifestyle, which shape not only the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota but also the production of microbial metabolites, immune responses, and inflammatory pathways. These host–microbe interactions are critical determinants of myocardial injury risk and recovery following cardiac events.

Gut–heart axis–associated diseases

CVD encompasses a broad spectrum of disorders affecting the heart and vasculature, including coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, rheumatic heart disease, and other related conditions. With the rapid advancement of metabolomics, microbiomics, and metagenomics, the role of the gut microbiota (GM) in CVD pathogenesis has gained increasing attention. Recent studies have revealed that a younger “microbial age,” characterized by a reduced abundance of Prevotella species, can mitigate the risk of CVD in elderly individuals with metabolically unfavorable profiles, independent of pharmacological treatment, dietary habits, education level, sex, age, or lifestyle. Age-related features of the gut microbiome include a marked decline in Bacteroides species, increased uniqueness and richness of facultative anaerobes (notably members of the Enterobacteriaceae family and the Streptococcus genus), and an associated enrichment of pro-inflammatory pathways. These microbial aging signatures are thought to contribute to diminished immunity, impaired digestion, and overall physiological decline [254]. Moreover, multiple lines of evidence indicate that the gut microbiota and its metabolites—particularly TMAO and phenylacetylglutamine (PAG)—are strongly linked to CVD risk. The pro-atherogenic effects of TMAO have been associated with reduced reverse cholesterol transport, altered tissue-level cholesterol and sterol metabolism, and modifications in the composition, pool size, and transport of BAs within the liver and intestine [255]. BAs receptors, such as the FXR and muscarinic receptors, are expressed in cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and vascular smooth muscle cells, implicating BAs signaling in cardiovascular pathophysiology. Indeed, exposure to supraphysiological BAs concentrations, such as those encountered during cholestasis, can provoke pro-arrhythmic responses in the heart, while taurine-conjugated bile salts disrupt Ca2⁺ homeostasis, leading to asynchronous cardiomyocyte contractions [256]. Notably, shotgun metagenomics combined with liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry has recently identified Oscillibacter species as potential CVD-protective taxa, capable of encoding cholesterol-metabolizing enzymes that facilitate intestinal cholesterol degradation and thereby lower circulating cholesterol levels and CVD risk [257].

HF is defined as a clinical syndrome characterized by reduced cardiac output and/or elevated ventricular filling pressures, leading to systemic neurohormonal dysregulation and multi-organ involvement. Numerous studies have demonstrated profound alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota in patients with HF. In particular, significant shifts in the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio have been observed in the gut microbiota of certain HF cohorts, suggesting that the gut microbial profile represents a promising emerging biomarker for HF [258]. Consistently, researchers have reported that patients with New York Heart Association (NYHA) class IV HF exhibit markedly reduced gut microbial diversity, increased endotoxemia, heightened inflammation, and oxidative stress, whereas gut taxa with potential anti-inflammatory properties are enriched in patients with milder disease (NYHA class I–II) [259]. The abundance of Escherichia coli and Shigella spp. positively correlates with harmful microbial metabolites such as TMAO and indoxyl sulfate (IS), indicating that gut-derived metabolites may actively contribute to HF pathogenesis [260]. In elderly HF patients, plasma levels of the tryptophan-derived metabolite IPA are significantly reduced, and this decline is associated with worsening cardiac function. Supplementation with IPA has been shown to attenuate aging-related myocardial fibrosis. Mechanistically, IPA regulates the expression of palmitoyl-protein thioesterase 1 (PPT1)—a lysosomal enzyme involved in autophagy—thereby modulating the PI3K–AKT–mTOR signaling pathway to restore autophagic activity in senescent macrophages and suppress inflammatory responses and fibrosis. IPA also influences PPT1 expression through modulation of the cGAS–STING pathway [261]. Notably, several preclinical findings have highlighted the potential efficacy of probiotic-based therapies in HF and begun to elucidate their underlying mechanisms. For example, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot trial in patients with HF demonstrated that a 3-month administration of Saccharomyces boulardii improved left atrial pump function, reduced left atrial diameter, and lowered total cholesterol and uric acid levels [262]. In another randomized triple-blind controlled study, consumption of probiotic yogurt was shown to alleviate systemic inflammation in patients with congestive HF, potentially through increased circulating levels of soluble TWEAK (sTWEAK) [263].

Hypertension is a major chronic disorder that markedly increases morbidity and mortality from CVD. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, hypertension ranks as the leading modifiable risk factor for the global CVD burden [264]. Hypertension represents a major worldwide health challenge driven by the interplay of genetic and environmental factors, among which high salt intake is a key environmental determinant. To identify metabolites that influence blood pressure, untargeted serum metabolomics was performed in mice fed a high-salt diet (HSD). This analysis revealed 46 significantly altered metabolites compared with controls, among which α-lipomycin—a gut microbiota–derived metabolite produced by Streptomyces aureofaciens Tü117—was markedly upregulated in both serum and feces of HSD-fed mice. Mechanistically, α-lipomycin induces vascular dysfunction and elevates blood pressure by antagonizing endothelial TRPV4 channels [265]. High salt intake also remodels the gut microbiota. In mice, salt loading selectively depletes Lactobacillus murinus, which in turn promotes T helper 17 (TH17) cell activation, thereby driving hypertension and CVD [266]. In humans, combined metagenomic and metabolomic analyses of 41 healthy controls, 56 individuals with prehypertension, and 99 patients with primary hypertension demonstrated a significant reduction in gut microbial richness and diversity in hypertensive patients, accompanied by compositional changes such as increased abundance of Prevotella and Klebsiella species. Moreover, fecal microbiota transplantation from hypertensive patients to germ-free mice elevated the blood pressure of recipient mice, providing direct evidence that gut microbes can influence blood pressure regulation [267]. Intriguingly, a recent study of 241 individuals in Hong Kong revealed a sex-specific association between gut dysbiosis and blood pressure: ecological imbalance of the gut microbiota was strongly correlated with 24-h ambulatory blood pressure in women, but not in men [268]. Collectively, these findings highlight a close relationship between gut microbial dysbiosis and hypertension, and suggest that distinct microbial taxa may exert differential effects on blood pressure. Microbial metabolites have also been implicated as key modulators of blood pressure. These include SCFAs, TMAO, BAs, hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), and serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT). SCFAs, for instance, lower blood pressure by activating G protein–coupled receptors GPR41/GPR43 to relax mesenteric arteries [269]. A recent randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial demonstrated that oral supplementation with acetate and butyrate significantly reduced 24-h systolic blood pressure in untreated patients with hypertension, with an average decrease of 4.1 mmHg (1 mmHg = 0.133 kPa) [270]. These findings suggest that reductions in SCFAs constitute a key mechanism underlying the development of hypertension, as SCFAs can directly influence blood-pressure homeostasis through modulation of immune and vascular function.

Gut–liver axis aging

Hepatic aging and gut microbiota alterations

Similar to other organs, the liver undergoes progressive structural and functional degeneration with advancing age. Physiological hallmarks of hepatic aging include reductions in liver volume, blood perfusion, and functional capacity [271]. Early histological studies described characteristic browning and atrophy of the aged liver, largely attributed to the accumulation of lipofuscin within hepatocytes [272]. Age-related hepatic changes not only compromise metabolic function but also impair regenerative capacity and immune responsiveness, predisposing individuals to chronic progressive liver diseases such as fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma. Notably, recent studies using liver injury models and hepatocyte-specific senescence mouse models revealed that hepatic aging can drive aging in extrahepatic organs, with the liver-associated TGFβ signaling pathway acting as a key mediator of systemic senescence. Pharmacological inhibition of TGFβ effectively blocked the transmission of senescence to other organs and prevented liver-aging–induced renal dysfunction [273]. These findings suggest that hepatic senescence can initiate and propagate organ-specific aging throughout the body. An intact intestinal barrier is essential for nutrient absorption while simultaneously confining trillions of gut microbes to the lumen to prevent systemic dissemination. However, intestinal permeability increases with age [274],, and microbial products and metabolites play pivotal roles in maintaining both gut barrier integrity and liver health [275]. In preclinical models, gut dysbiosis has been shown to modulate liver disease progression, and fecal microbiota transplantation can transmit liver pathology [276]. The portal vein delivers not only absorbed nutrients such as carbohydrates, lipids, and amino acids but also microbially derived metabolites directly to the liver, the first organ to encounter these compounds. These metabolites exert critical effects on hepatic immune and metabolic functions. For example, bacterial sphingolipids derived from the commensal Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron have been shown to reach the liver and reduce lipid accumulation by enhancing β-oxidation in mice, whereas germ-free mice lacking a microbiota are more susceptible to hepatotoxic insults [277]. Emerging evidence further highlights the therapeutic potential of modulating the gut–liver axis during aging. For instance, ginsenoside Rg2 alleviates age-related liver injury in senescence-accelerated mice (SAMP8) by suppressing pyroptosis and apoptosis through caspase-8 inhibition, while concurrently reshaping the gut microbiota to reduce hepatic inflammation [278]. Collectively, these findings indicate that age-associated alterations in the gut microbiota critically influence liver function and may serve as regulatory targets for mitigating hepatic aging.

Mechanisms of the gut–liver axis signaling

The gut–liver axis refers to the functional and structural crosstalk between the intestine and the liver, which enables bidirectional exchange of nutrients, microbial products, and signaling molecules to maintain systemic homeostasis. This axis is orchestrated by the gut, the gut microbiota, and the hepatic portal system, allowing continuous communication between the intestinal lumen and the liver. Among its key components, the gut microbiota and their metabolites play pivotal roles: a balanced microbial community and an intact intestinal barrier are essential for preserving immune homeostasis along the gut–liver axis. Anatomically, the liver and gut are connected via the portal vein and the biliary tract. Under physiological conditions, only small amounts of dietary antigens, bacterial DNA, and endotoxins traverse the intestinal mucosal barrier and reach the liver through the portal circulation, where the hepatic immune system eliminates these potentially harmful molecules. The portal vein provides approximately 70% of the liver’s blood supply, rendering the liver uniquely exposed and sensitive to gut-derived bacteria, bacterial products, endotoxins, and pro-inflammatory microbial factors [279]. The gut microbiota initiates the primary metabolism of dietary nutrients, thereby directly shaping hepatic nutrient absorption and metabolic responses. Although gut–liver interactions were traditionally viewed as a unidirectional pathway from the intestine to the liver, it is now recognized that the liver communicates reciprocally with the gut through the biliary system. BAs metabolism exemplifies this two-way regulation. The liver synthesizes and secretes bile—a complex fluid enriched in immunoglobulin A (IgA), bicarbonate, antimicrobial peptides, and bile acids—while the gut microbiota further modifies BAs through biotransformation. This coordinated hepatic synthesis and microbial conversion maintains BAs homeostasis, controls bacterial overgrowth, and regulates host lipid, glucose, and energy metabolism. BAs facilitate the digestion and absorption of lipids and fat-soluble vitamins and exert potent effects on gut microbial composition. The enterohepatic circulation of BAs contributes to mucosal colonization resistance and orchestrates local and systemic immune responses. Under pathological conditions, increased intestinal permeability and microbial dysbiosis disrupt the gut–liver axis, leading to bacterial overgrowth, pathological bacterial translocation, and endotoxemia. These changes activate hepatic Toll-like receptors, trigger chronic inflammation, and drive progressive liver injury, including fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [280]. Notably, crosstalk between BAs and the gut microbiota occurs at multiple levels and is bidirectional: the microbiota regulates BAs metabolism, whereas BAs, in turn, shape the microbial community structure [281, 282]. Although BAs were once regarded merely as recirculating digestive agents, it is now evident that secondary BAs serve as signaling molecules that activate FXR signaling in intestinal epithelial cells, thereby influencing host metabolic and immune pathways.

Gut–liver axis–associated diseases

NAFLD is the most prevalent chronic liver disorder worldwide, with a reported incidence ranging from 6 to 35% in adults [283]. Long-standing nonalcoholic fatty liver (NAFL) and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) are major drivers of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [284]. In patients with NAFLD, gut bacteria can translocate to the liver via the portal vein, triggering aberrant immune activation and hepatic inflammation [285]. Importantly, the interaction between the gut and liver is bidirectional: pro-inflammatory cytokines produced in the diseased liver can disrupt the intestinal epithelial barrier, impair tight-junction integrity, and reinforce a vicious gut–liver cycle during NAFLD progression [286]. Accumulating evidence indicates that gut microbiota dysbiosis and metabolic alterations are closely linked to NAFLD pathogenesis. NAFLD patients exhibit increased intestinal permeability and distinct microbial signatures compared with healthy controls, highlighting a causal relationship between microbial imbalance and liver injury [287, 288]. A recent interdisciplinary study combining clinical medicine, systems biology, and experimental research demonstrated that gut microbial composition and functional profiles diverge from healthy individuals even before ultrasonographic detection of NAFLD. Based on these findings, a predictive microbiome-based model was developed that accurately estimated the four-year risk of NAFLD onset in otherwise healthy subjects [289]. These results strongly support the concept that early shifts in gut microbial ecology contribute to disease susceptibility. Perturbations of a healthy gut microbiome—including the loss of beneficial taxa, altered microbial abundance, and expansion of pathobionts—can arise from environmental stress, immune dysregulation, changes in bile acid composition, gastric pH, or impaired gut motility [290]. In a biopsy-confirmed NAFLD cohort, 16S rRNA sequencing revealed significant increases in Bacteroides and Ruminococcus and a marked reduction in Prevotella compared with patients without NASH [291]. Multi-omics analyses have further uncovered interactions between the gut microbiota and hepatic regeneration in mouse models of metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), identifying Akkermansia muciniphila as a key species capable of promoting liver regeneration through regulation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [292]. These findings underscore the role of specific microbes in sustaining liver function via gut–liver crosstalk. NASH represents the critical inflammatory stage of NAFLD, where mitochondrial dysfunction and gut microbiota perturbations exhibit a bidirectional relationship. Mitochondrial impairment and associated ROS accumulation can disrupt gut barrier integrity and activate immune responses, whereas the gut microbiota itself can modulate mitochondrial function. A recent study reported a distinct microbial signature in lean NASH patients, characterized by a specific reduction in the genus Dorea, suggesting that enhanced mitochondrial activity may reshape the gut microbiome to attenuate NASH progression [293]. Dietary interventions also influence gut–liver communication. Soluble dietary fiber inulin enriches Parabacteroides distasonis, which produces pentadecanoic acid to restore gut barrier integrity and suppress pro-inflammatory signaling, thereby alleviating diet-induced NASH in mice [294]. Excitingly, several preclinical and early clinical investigations have demonstrated promising effects of probiotic-based interventions in NAFLD. In an early randomized controlled trial (RCT), 44 biopsy-proven NAFLD patients were enrolled to evaluate the efficacy of VSL#3—a formulation composed of eight probiotic strains, including Streptococcus thermophilus, Bifidobacterium spp., Lactobacillus acidophilus, L. plantarum, L. paracasei and L. delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus. This double-blind, 4-month trial revealed that only 9% of children receiving VSL#3 exhibited moderate-to-severe hepatic steatosis upon histological evaluation, compared with 93% in the placebo group [295]. These findings suggest a potential beneficial role of VSL#3 in ameliorating NAFLD; however, conclusions should be interpreted cautiously because the study population comprised only children, limiting generalizability to adults. The impact of probiotics on NAFLD has also been assessed in a 12-week, double-blind, randomized clinical trial involving 65 Korean patients with NAFLD. The probiotic mixture consisted of six bacterial strains (Lactobacillus acidophilus, L. rhamnosus, L. paracasei, Pediococcus pentosaceus, Bifidobacterium lactis and Bacillus coagulans). Compared with the control group, patients receiving probiotics exhibited significantly lower levels of cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL and TNF-α, whereas glucose, insulin, ALT and AST levels remained unchanged. Beyond reductions in body weight and total fat mass, MRI-PDFF measurements showed a decrease in hepatic fat fraction following 12 weeks of probiotic administration, indicating a potential therapeutic effect of probiotics in NAFLD [296]. Nonetheless, it is important to recognize that a 12-week intervention is relatively short for evaluating treatments targeting a chronic condition such as NAFLD, and the robustness of these conclusions requires cautious interpretation. Collectively, these studies highlight the pivotal regulatory role of the gut microbiome in maintaining liver function and shaping the course of NAFLD and NASH.

Acute liver failure (ALF) is a catastrophic hepatic injury that not only triggers the accumulation of senescent cells and impairs liver regenerative capacity but also precipitates systemic complications leading to multi-organ failure [297]. Emerging evidence indicates that maternal gut inflammation profoundly influences offspring susceptibility to ALF by reshaping the neonatal gut microbiome and its metabolite profile. In particular, Lactobacillus reuteri and its indole derivative IAA battenuate ALF severity by modulating oxidative stress and ferroptosis through the IL-22–STAT3 signaling axis [298]. ALF arises from diverse etiologies, among which acetaminophen (APAP) overdose remains the most common cause. Hepatic metabolism of APAP generates the toxic intermediate N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), which induces oxidative stress and hepatocyte death. Recent work has revealed that dietary magnesium—a widely used nutritional supplement—confers hepatoprotection by remodeling the metabolic output of Bifidobacterium. Magnesium supplementation enhances the production of indole-3-carboxylic acid (I3C), a metabolite consistently enriched in both Bifidobacterium culture supernatants and magnesium-treated fecal samples, identifying I3C as a key bioactive mediator of magnesium-induced liver protection [299]. Moreover, the gut microbiota–bile acid–Mas receptor signaling axis contributes to ALF progression through a previously unrecognized mechanism. This pathway stimulates endothelial release of sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), which drives neutrophil extracellular trap (NET) formation, thereby accelerating hepatic injury [300]. Together, these findings underscore the pivotal role of gut microbes and their metabolites in regulating ALF pathogenesis, highlighting the gut–liver axis as a critical target for future diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

The most common types of primary liver cancer are HCC and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), both of which are influenced by the gut microbiota in terms of initiation, progression, and therapeutic response. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiome contributes to HCC development through multiple mechanisms: it compromises the integrity of the gut vascular barrier (GVB), alters the tumor microenvironment (TME), and even affects the composition of the intratumoral microbiota. The tumor-resident microbiome subsequently exhibits distinctive features that promote HCC progression by inducing DNA damage, modulating oncogenic signaling pathways, remodeling the TME, and facilitating metastasis.Although specific findings vary across studies, a consistent trend in HCC patients is an increased abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria in the gut [301]. Compared with other liver diseases, Ruminococcus is markedly enriched in HCC patients [302]., indicating subtle differences in microbial distributions across HCC subtypes. Additionally, Enterococcus, including Enterobacter ludwigii, is significantly elevated in HCC, sometimes up to 100-fold compared with controls [303]. Conversely, beneficial taxa, such as Bifidobacterium (Actinobacteria), Akkermansia, Prevotella_2, Subdoligranulum [304], and Faecalibacterium, are generally depleted in HCC patients [305]. Microbial dysbiosis increases gut permeability, allowing SCFAs, BAs, bacterial components, choline, and endogenous ethanol to translocate to the liver, thereby imposing metabolic stress and pro-inflammatory stimuli that accelerate HCC progression [306]. Furthermore, live pathogens, Gram-negative microbial products, and pro-inflammatory luminal metabolites can reach the liver via the portal circulation, reshaping the hepatic microenvironment [307]. Environmental factors such as dietary or genetic obesity can alter gut microbiota composition, elevating deoxycholic acid (DCA)—a bacterial metabolite known to induce DNA damage. DCA recirculation activates SASP in hepatic stellate cells, leading to secretion of pro-inflammatory and tumor-promoting factors, which facilitate HCC development in chemically challenged mice [308]. Inhibition of DCA production or reduction of gut bacteria effectively prevents HCC in obese mouse models [309]. PRRs sense gut microbes through pathogen- or microbe-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/MAMPs) and subsequently trigger biological responses [310]. Among PRRs, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) is a critical immunomodulator that contributes to HCC progression, with overexpression observed in HCC tissues [311]. Notably, gut dysbiosis and TLR4 signaling are not essential for HCC initiation but are required for tumor progression [312]. Thus, gut microbes mediate complex signaling cascades that drive HCC development. Additional downstream pathways exhibit intricate or even dual effects. For example, certain non-pathogenic Escherichia coli strains stimulate NF-κB–mediated transcription of pro-inflammatory genes in activated B cells, while simultaneously upregulating negative feedback regulators within nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain (NOD) signaling, thereby tempering inflammation [313]. Collectively, these studies indicate that selective gut microbes may serve as potential biomarkers for HCC. Dysbiosis disrupts GVB integrity and further influences the tumor-associated microenvironment, while complex PAMP–PRR (TLR4)–related signaling networks contribute to HCC progression. Understanding how these factors interact and influence disease trajectory remains a critical area for future investigation.

The gut–lung axis aging

Lung aging and gut microbiota alterations

The lung, as the largest organ in terms of surface area, is primarily responsible for gas exchange with the external environment, and the maintenance of pulmonary homeostasis is essential for life and overall health. Increasing evidence indicates that lung function undergoes progressive decline with age, characterized by physical, mechanical, and structural alterations that compromise gas exchange [314]. The lung is a complex multicellular organ, comprising alveolar epithelial cells, vascular endothelial cells, airway epithelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, platelets, and neutrophils. Notably, cellular and molecular regulatory processes in the lung change over the human lifespan, and a detailed understanding of age-associated intrinsic alterations is critical for developing effective clinical interventions. According to the European Respiratory Society (ERS), pulmonary function is considered a predictor of healthy aging [315]. Radiological characterization of “normal” lung aging is essential for distinguishing physiological changes from clinically significant disease. Compared with younger individuals, older adults exhibit higher prevalence of pulmonary and airway cysts, reticular patterns, air trapping, bronchiectasis, and bronchial wall thickening. Age-related thoracic imaging features also include vascular tortuosity and calcification, increased mediastinal fat, breast protrusions, and musculoskeletal changes such as costal osteophytes and rib cartilage calcification. Histological analyses demonstrate that pulmonary aging involves mechanical, structural, and physiological alterations that compromise lung function [316]. Although the number of alveoli, alveolar ducts, and capillary segments remains relatively constant in adulthood, alveolar size and alveolar-capillary surface area significantly increase with age [317]. Age-associated decline in respiratory muscle and diaphragmatic strength further impairs pulmonary function [318]. Importantly, aging increases susceptibility to a spectrum of respiratory diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), interstitial pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and pneumonia [319]. Some age-associated pulmonary pathologies exhibit causal links with telomere dysfunction and the accumulation of senescent cells. For example, telomere attrition is a recognized risk factor for IPF [320], while telomerase mutations predispose individuals to emphysema in COPD patients [321]. These observations suggest that factors driving cellular senescence not only accelerate pulmonary aging but also contribute to the onset of age-related lung diseases.

Alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota can disrupt host–microbe crosstalk, with profound consequences for immune responses and disease susceptibility. In comparison, research on the pulmonary microbiome remains at an early stage. The lower respiratory tract has long been considered “sterile,” primarily due to the inability to culture lung-resident microbes using conventional microbiological methods in healthy individuals. More challenging is the identification and differentiation of true resident lung microbiota from transient bacterial populations, given technical limitations such as sampling approaches, oropharyngeal contamination during collection, and low microbial biomass. The nasal microbiota appears more similar to skin communities and contributes minimally to lung colonization [322]. With aging, microbial density within lung tissue remains relatively low, suggesting that even sparse lung microbiota may play critical roles in maintaining pulmonary health [323]. Certain bacterial taxa are shared between the oral cavity and the lungs, albeit at differing abundances, indicating that pulmonary communities are partially seeded through microaspiration from the oral microbiome. Consequently, the overall bacterial community in the lung resembles that of the oral cavity, with Streptococcus, Prevotella, and Veillonella among the most prevalent genera [324, 325]. In healthy individuals, the dominant phyla in the lung are Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes, whereas the oropharynx is enriched in Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes. In contrast, the nasal microbiota more closely resembles skin communities, with Firmicutes and Actinobacteria predominating [326, 327]. Ecological determinants of the pulmonary microbiome—including immigration, elimination, and regional growth conditions—undergo dynamic changes during acute and chronic respiratory diseases, such as COPD, which often reflects a pre-malignant inflammatory state, and lung cancer [328]. Disruption of gut–lung crosstalk has been associated with increased susceptibility to airway diseases and infections, including allergic responses [329]. For example, patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) or IBD exhibit higher prevalence of pulmonary disorders [330, 331]. Experimental studies in mice indicate that the gut microbiota modulates both innate and adaptive immune responses, conferring protection against bacterial and viral lung infections [332]. Collectively, these findings highlight a substantial bidirectional interaction between the gut and the lungs, which is critical for maintaining systemic homeostasis and “educating” the host immune system. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms by which the gut microbiota influences pulmonary health and disease remain incompletely understood and warrant further investigation.

Mechanisms of gut–lung axis signaling

The gut microbiota influences distant organs, including the lungs, through multiple mechanisms, encompassing the regulation of microbial metabolites—such as SCFAs, tryptophan and its derivatives, polyamines (PAs), and secondary BAs—as well as the modulation of immune cell trafficking and neuro-immune signaling. The concept of a gut–lung axis was first supported in the early 1990s, when studies implicated translocation of bacterial products from the intestinal lumen into the bloodstream in the pathogenesis of ARDS during septic shock [333]. Subsequent studies using bone marrow transplantation models demonstrated that limiting gut-derived endotoxins, particularly LPS, reduced susceptibility to idiopathic pneumonia, providing further evidence for an inflammatory gut–lung axis [334]. Epithelial cells in both the gut and the lung serve critical barrier functions, encompassing physical, chemical, and physiological protection, which deteriorate with aging. In elderly individuals or aged animal models, increased epithelial permeability is observed in both organs [335]. The mucosal surfaces of the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts are continuously exposed to diverse microbial populations, and microbes originating from the gut can reach the lung via microaspiration. These epithelial structures, constantly interacting with potentially pathogenic exogenous particles and microbes, regulate permeability through tight junctions. Several factors mediate gut–lung communication, including systemic dissemination of bacterial components and metabolic byproducts, among which SCFAs represent prominent immunoregulatory metabolites. SCFAs exert direct effects on host immunity primarily via G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), which are differentially expressed across multiple cell types and tissues. Their activity is determined not only by receptor availability and affinity but also by the expression of transporters and downstream signaling molecules in distinct cell populations [336]. Along the gut–lung axis, SCFAs can activate myeloid cells in the bone marrow, which subsequently migrate to the lung to establish an anti-inflammatory environment [337]. Although potential confounding effects of oral gavage and antibiotic treatment exist, these findings provide partial confirmation of gut–lung signaling. The gut microbiota additionally influences pulmonary immunity by modulating immune system activation. Notably, the commensal protozoan Tritrichomonas musculis (T. mu) can activate intestinal immune cells, profoundly impacting the pulmonary immune landscape. T. mu presence exacerbates allergic diseases such as asthma while enhancing immune defense against Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection [338]. Mechanistically, gut–lung communication can be divided into direct and indirect pathways. Direct effects include the presence of peptidoglycan and LPS, which enhance host immune responses. Indirect effects encompass: (a) the entry of microbial metabolites, such as SCFAs, into peripheral circulation to influence immune cell development; (b) migration of bone marrow–derived immune cells to the lung, triggering local immune responses; (c) trafficking of gut-resident immune cells—including ILC2s, ILC3s, and Th17 cells—via the bloodstream to modulate pulmonary immunity; and (d) microbial metabolites, such as deaminated tyrosine, enhancing type I interferon responses to protect the host from influenza virus infection [339]. Collectively, these findings underscore a complex and multi-layered network through which the gut microbiota communicates with the lungs, integrating metabolic, cellular, and immune signals to maintain pulmonary homeostasis and modulate disease susceptibility.

Although most evidence indicates that the primary direction of communication occurs from the gut to the lungs, bidirectional crosstalk is also plausible. Chronic pulmonary diseases, including asthma, COPD, and cystic fibrosis (CF), are characterized not only by dysbiosis of the airway microbiota but also by compositional perturbations in the gastrointestinal tract [340, 341]. Furthermore, respiratory influenza infection in mice can indirectly induce intestinal immune impairment and alter gut microbial communities. The resulting dysbiosis promotes inflammation through expansion of Enterobacteriaceae and reductions in Lactobacillus and Lactococcus species [342]. Similarly, in murine models, LPS-induced acute lung injury increases bacterial loads in the bloodstream and cecum, while total 16S rRNA levels in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) slightly decrease, accompanied by shifts in the abundance of specific bacterial taxa [343]. These findings suggest that alterations in the pulmonary microbiota can influence gut function, supporting the existence of reverse signaling along the gut–lung axis.

Gut–lung axis–associated diseases

Bronchial asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by airway and lung remodeling and inflammation, and its pathogenesis is closely linked to the gut microbiota [344]. Increasing experimental and clinical evidence indicates that the gut microbiota is associated with both the incidence and severity of asthma [345]. During the first few years of life, the infant gut microbiome is highly dynamic and undergoes continuous development. Disruptions in early-life gut microbial communities have been associated with an increased risk of asthma. Although the overall composition of the gut microbiota in high-risk infants may remain largely unchanged, subtle transient shifts in specific taxa can be detected during the first months of life. Notably, elevated asthma risk is associated with increased abundance of Bacteroides fragilis and total anaerobes, reduced microbial diversity, decreased Escherichia coli, and diminished relative abundances of Faecalibacterium, Ruminococcus, Roseburia, and Veillonella species [345–347]. Early studies demonstrated that an immature gut microbial composition at one year of age predicts asthma and allergic sensitization at later stages, allowing asthma risk to be forecasted at five years based on microbial signatures observed at one year [348]. Comprehensive metagenomic analysis of the gut microbiota and antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) in 662 one-year-old children from the Danish COPSAC2010 birth cohort revealed a bimodal distribution of ARG abundance, driven largely by Escherichia coli. ARGs were unevenly distributed across 208 bacterial species and seven phyla, highlighting E. coli as a major reservoir. ARG composition was significantly influenced by environmental factors, including antibiotic usage, frequency and timing of exposure, maternal antibiotic use during late pregnancy, birth order, housing type, and geographic region. Over time, the impact of antibiotic exposure on ARGs diminishes. Importantly, high ARG abundance correlates with lower gut microbial maturity and a microbial composition associated with asthma risk [349]. Early-life breastfeeding also exerts a direct effect on the gut microbiota and respiratory health. Analysis of over 2,000 children in the Canadian CHILD Cohort Study demonstrated that exclusive breastfeeding during the first year promotes orderly microbial maturation, thereby reducing asthma risk and supporting respiratory health [350]. Collectively, these findings suggest that disruptions of the gut microbiota during early life contribute to increased asthma susceptibility.

Globally, lung cancer accounts for approximately 2.5 million new cases annually and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality, posing a significant threat to human health [351]. Lung cancer comprises small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Using next-generation sequencing, Botticelli and colleagues demonstrated that NSCLC patients exhibit increased abundances of Rikenellaceae, Prevotella, Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Bacteroides plebeius, Oscillospira, and Enterobacteriaceae in fecal samples compared with healthy controls. Conversely, the abundances of Bromobacter, Dialister, and Sutterella were reduced in NSCLC patients [352, 353]. In human studies examining responses to anti-PD1 therapy, baseline gut microbiota compositions differed between responsive and non-responsive NSCLC patients. Responders displayed higher abundances of Enterococcus, Collinsella aerofaciens, Bifidobacterium adolescentis, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Veillonella sp., Parabacteroides merdae, and Lactobacillus longum compared to non-responders. Furthermore, selective colonization of certain species increased the frequency of regulatory T cells in the peripheral colon, while other species decreased it [354]. Notably, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are now standard-of-care treatments for many cancers, including NSCLC. Baseline gut microbiota in advanced lung cancer patients deriving long-term benefit from ICIs differs from that in resistant patients or those experiencing severe immune-related adverse events (irAEs). irAE-associated microbial features include the presence of potentially autoimmunity-related species, such as Prevotella copri, and enrichment of epitopes associated with systemic lupus erythematosus [355]. Collectively, these studies indicate that gut microbiota dysbiosis is closely associated with lung cancer progression, and certain gut microbes may serve as predictive biomarkers for therapeutic efficacy and adverse effects in lung cancer patients.

COPD is a progressive respiratory disorder characterized by persistent airflow limitation, primarily driven by long-term exposure to cigarette smoke, air pollution, occupational dust, and recurrent respiratory infections. These factors promote chronic airway inflammation, structural remodeling, and gradual decline in lung function. Among these, cigarette smoking is a major etiological factor in COPD pathogenesis. Tobacco smoke not only exacerbates pulmonary inflammation but also induces ecological dysbiosis within the gut microenvironment. For instance, smoke exposure can promote biofilm formation by specific bacterial genera, enhancing their adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells and increasing their abundance, such as Streptococcus species [356]. In murine models, chronic smoke exposure increases gut permeability even before the onset of emphysematous lung lesions [357]. Similar to smokers, patients with COPD exhibit alterations in both pulmonary and intestinal microbiota. Smoking disrupts the balance of the lung microbiome, favoring pathogenic over commensal bacteria, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection and exacerbating COPD progression [358]. Notably, dysbiosis in the gut microbiota is associated with changes in the pulmonary microbial composition, and reciprocally, lung microbiota alterations can influence gut homeostasis. In stable COPD patients, beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus are significantly reduced in fecal samples [359]. Analyses of gut microbiota across different GOLD stages of COPD reveal correlations between disease severity and microbial patterns. In GOLD stage 1 patients, Bacteroides abundance is higher, whereas Tyzzerella 4 and Microbacterium are reduced. In GOLD stages 2–4, the abundances of Ruminococcaceae and Lachnoclostridium decrease, while in GOLD stages 3–4, Clostridium and Aerococcus are relatively enriched [360]. Collectively, these findings indicate pronounced gut microbial dysbiosis in smokers, patients with stable COPD, and those experiencing acute exacerbations (AECOPD). Altered microbial communities and their metabolites may, in turn, modulate lung function and influence disease progression, highlighting a bidirectional gut–lung axis in COPD pathophysiology. Notably, prebiotic fibres can beneficially modulate the gut microbiota by promoting the production of health-associated metabolites. An exploratory analysis within the double-blind, placebo-controlled NUTRECOVER trial was conducted to obtain preliminary insights into the effects of nutritional intervention. Baseline cross-sectional comparisons included 32 patients with COPD and 32 age-matched healthy controls. Patients were then randomized to receive either a multi-nutrient supplement containing prebiotic fibres, vitamin D, tryptophan and n-3 long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (n = 16), or a placebo (n = 16), for three months. Compared with healthy controls, individuals with COPD exhibited higher abundances of Lactobacillus and lower abundances of Akkermansia, accompanied by reduced α-diversity within the gut microbiota. After three months of intervention, however, no significant effects were observed on gut microbial composition or systemic inflammation [361]. These findings suggest that although prebiotic fibres have the potential to influence the microbiota through the enhancement of beneficial metabolites, further investigation is required to determine whether such modulation can meaningfully alter the course of COPD.

The gut–kidney axis aging

Kidney aging and gut microbiota alterations

The kidney, a pivotal organ for blood filtration and metabolic waste excretion, undergoes progressive structural and functional changes throughout the human lifespan. With advancing age, characteristic histopathological alterations emerge, including glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, arterial stiffening, and interstitial fibrosis [362]. These age-related modifications are strongly associated with a gradual decline in renal function, typically manifested as a reduction in glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and an increased susceptibility to acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [363]. Morphometric analyses reveal a measurable, age-dependent decrease in renal parenchymal volume, with divergent trajectories between the renal cortex and medulla. From approximately 50 years of age onward, cortical volume shows an accelerated decline, whereas medullary volume displays a sex-specific pattern—continuing to increase in men but beginning to decrease in women [364]. Comparative studies of young adult (18–29 years) and elderly (70–75 years) kidney donors demonstrate a striking 48% reduction in nephron number across the adult lifespan, paralleling the well-documented age-related fall in GFR. Concomitantly, globally sclerotic glomeruli increase by approximately 15%, while cortical mass decreases by 16% [365]. Notably, compensatory hyperfiltration at the single-nephron level—reflected by an increased single-nephron GFR—has been identified as a prominent marker of heightened CKD risk [366]. Histological assessment of cortical glomerulosclerosis suggests that superficial glomerular lesions are most strongly age-associated, whereas pathologies such as hypertension, proteinuria, interstitial fibrosis, and low GFR exhibit a more diffuse, cortex-wide pattern of scarring [367]. Moreover, senescent renal epithelial cells are increasingly recognized as key drivers of renal fibrosis. In a recent analysis of paired urine and renal biopsy samples from 51 CKD patients enrolled in the Edinburgh seNSOR biobank, the proportion of senescent renal epithelial cells was positively correlated with patient age and the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR), but inversely correlated with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [368]. Importantly, the urinary clusterin-to-creatinine ratio (uCCR) emerged as a sensitive biomarker reflecting epithelial senescence and predicting disease progression in human kidneys. Collectively, these findings underscore that ageing-related renal senescence disrupts systemic metabolic homeostasis and serves as a critical pathogenic nexus linking organismal ageing to the development of kidney-related disorders.

Growing evidence indicates a pivotal connection between renal ageing and the gut microbiota. The intestinal microbiome has emerged as a key determinant of kidney senescence, with the rate of renal ageing showing an inverse relationship to the ageing trajectory of the gut microbial community. This negative correlation may reflect an age-related expansion of potentially pathogenic taxa, leading to the accumulation of toxic metabolites that contribute to renal dysfunction—a process that may be mitigated by reshaping microbial diversity and intestinal ecology [369]. Remarkably, centenarians exhibit a comparatively “younger” gut microbiome than nonagenarians, and the influence of gut microbes on circulating serum metabolites becomes progressively stronger with advancing age. These findings suggest that the gut microbiota exerts an increasingly important role in shaping systemic biological processes across the human lifespan. Many age-associated bacterial taxa—particularly Escherichia coli and pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae—are strongly correlated with serum metabolites, including bile acids and biomarkers of renal impairment such as eGFR, creatinine (CREA), urea (UREA), and uric acid (UA). The relationships among renal function, serum metabolites, and the gut microbiome collectively point to a mechanistic role for gut microbes in the pathogenesis of renal ageing [370]. A systematic review of adults with various kidney disorders reported that the relative abundance of Pseudomonas (formerly classified within Proteobacteria), unclassified Enterobacteriaceae, unclassified Enterococcaceae, and Streptococcus was higher in patients compared with healthy controls, whereas Bacillus (formerly within Firmicutes), unclassified Prevotellaceae, and Prevotella were significantly depleted [371]. Fecal metagenomic analyses of individuals with end-stage renal disease further revealed enrichment of microbial functional pathways associated with aromatic amino acid catabolism, accompanied by elevated levels of uremic toxin precursors (e.g., indole and p-cresol) and their sulfate-conjugated derivatives (indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate) [372]. Recent population-based studies reinforce a substantive bidirectional interaction between gut microbial composition and renal function [373]. Although most existing investigations rely on small cohorts and amplicon-based sequencing, their results consistently implicate gut dysbiosis in the development of renal dysfunction [374, 375]. Furthermore, microbial fermentation products linked to increased intestinal permeability and systemic inflammation have been associated with nephrotoxicity [376]. Collectively, these findings indicate that age-related alterations in the gut microbiota are closely linked to the accumulation of circulating markers of renal impairment. The buildup of such metabolites, together with declining kidney function, may accelerate organismal ageing itself. However, as in most microbiome research, current insights are derived primarily from fecal analyses, which capture only a fraction of the intestinal ecosystem [377]. Thus, while compelling evidence supports a functional gut–kidney connection, the precise molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets underlying microbiota-driven renal pathology—including acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, and nephrolithiasis—remain to be fully elucidated.

Signaling mechanisms of the gut–kidney axis

In 2011, scientists first proposed a functional link between the kidney and the intestine, describing the impact of the gut on CKD—a condition now frequently conceptualized as a prototypical gut–kidney axis disorder [378]. The gut and kidney engage in bidirectional crosstalk, whereby pathophysiological changes in either organ can reciprocally influence the other, ultimately driving disease progression [379]. Alterations in gastrointestinal or renal function can affect their counterpart through multiple pathways, including energy and nutrient metabolism, immune and inflammatory signaling, mucosal barrier integrity, and microbiome composition, forming a complex inter-organ communication network. Renal impairment promotes the release of nitrogenous metabolites that can traverse the intestinal barrier and foster the growth of pathogenic gut bacteria. Metabolic waste products that cannot be effectively excreted by the kidney readily enter the gut lumen, exacerbating intestinal dysbiosis [380]. Conversely, gut microbes and their metabolites can modulate renal disease progression through several well-characterized mechanisms. For example, (1) SCFAs promote immunosuppression and regulatory T-cell differentiation, participating in the immune regulation of kidney-related disorders [381]. (2) Uremic solutes such as IS and p-cresyl sulfate (PCS) aggravate renal dysfunction by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) [382]. (3) TMAO, a microbial metabolite, is a recognized risk factor for CKD. In addition to TMAO, gut-derived bile acids, SCFAs, neurotransmitters, uremic toxins (UTs), and inflammatory mediators can all impair renal function. The kidney, in turn, contributes to gut homeostasis by excreting gut-derived toxins and synthesizing hormones that regulate intestinal physiology [383]. Disruption of this equilibrium creates a vicious cycle: impaired renal clearance increases systemic toxin accumulation, which damages the intestinal barrier and triggers inflammation, while gut dysbiosis enhances UTs production, further accelerating renal injury. From the gut-to-kidney perspective, microbial imbalance leads to increased production of nephrotoxic compounds and reduced abundance of SCFA-producing taxa. This imbalance elevates circulating TMAO and lowers protective butyrate levels, directly promoting renal fibrosis and inflammation. From the kidney-to-gut perspective, impaired renal function reduces the excretion of urea and oxalate, while ageing-associated increases in intestinal permeability allow these metabolites to enter the gut lumen. The resulting barrier disruption facilitates the reabsorption of toxins such as indole, p-cresol, and oxalate into the bloodstream, thereby amplifying renal injury [384]. In addition, dysbiotic microbes have been reported to translocate directly from the gut to the kidney, causing renal tissue damage [385]. Experimental studies further demonstrate that correcting gut dysbiosis and restoring intestinal barrier function can effectively mitigate kidney injury in animal models of CKD [386]. Renal function is also intimately linked to nitrogen metabolism, including amino acid turnover and the production of urea and ammonia [387]. Together, these findings illustrate that the gut–kidney axis is governed by a complex, bidirectional signaling network involving the microbiota, microbial metabolites, and immune–inflammatory pathways. Perturbations in either organ can trigger a self-reinforcing cycle of dysfunction, highlighting the gut–kidney axis as both a pathogenic driver and a therapeutic target in renal ageing and disease.

Gut–kidney axis–associated diseases

CKD is a progressive disorder characterized by the gradual loss of renal function or structural injury, leading to impaired blood filtration. CKD affects an estimated 9.1% of adults worldwide, posing a major global public health challenge. As a primary cause of morbidity and mortality, CKD is responsible for approximately 1.2 million deaths annually [388] and represents a key risk factor for other chronic conditions, particularly cardiovascular disease (CVD) [389, 390]. The pathophysiology of CKD is multifactorial—encompassing hypertension, immune dysregulation, and metabolic disturbances—all of which have been closely linked to alterations in the gut microbiome, supporting the concept of a microecological dysbiosis model in CKD. A Recent metagenomic analysis of 1,550 elderly individuals (65–93 years) with comprehensive renal function profiling revealed a positive association between kidney health and the ammonia-metabolism pathway of Candidatus Copperflagellate, including the microbial gene asnA, which encodes ammonia-assimilating enzymes. In murine models, oral ammonium supplementation elevated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, thereby accelerating CKD progression. Strikingly, colonization with Staphylococcus aureus or Escherichia coli strains overexpressing asnA—but not asnA knockout strains—attenuated ammonia-induced CKD progression in mice, underscoring gut microbial ammonia metabolism as a critical driver of CKD pathogenesis and highlighting the therapeutic potential of microbiome-based interventions [391]. Cardiovascular complications remain the leading cause of death among CKD patients, accounting for ~ 50% of mortality. Recent work identifies the uremic toxin ISas a key metabolite promoting heart failure in CKD. In these patients, intestinal E. coli enhances IS production via tryptophan metabolism; IS in turn activates the AHR–CYP1B1 axis, disrupting mitochondrial function in cardiomyocytes and triggering apoptosis, thereby facilitating heart failure. Prospective cohort and randomized intervention studies further confirm that E. coli abundance serves as a predictive biomarker for CKD-associated heart failure, while the probiotic DM02, which suppresses E. coli, emerges as a potential therapeutic strategy [392]. These findings underscore the pivotal role of the gut microbiome in both renal and cardiovascular complications of CKD. Notably, the health impact of microbial alterations depends on the balance between beneficial and harmful metabolites, such as protective SCFAs and nephrotoxic uremic toxins. For example, GF mice exhibit markedly reduced or undetectable levels of uremic toxins—including indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate, and phenyl sulfate—alongside lower concentrations of SCFAs compared with specific pathogen–free (SPF) controls [393]. Conversely, hypertensive GF mice display exacerbated renal injury relative to SPF counterparts, a phenotype attributed to the absence of bacterial SCFAs production [394]. Indeed, several clinical studies have suggested that probiotics may hold therapeutic potential for patients with CKD. For example, in a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial involving patients with stage 3b–4 CKD (n = 47), daily administration of a probiotic-containing sachet (two sachets per day) led to a significant reduction in serum levels of the uremic toxin IS compared with placebo [395]. Moreover, FMT has emerged as a promising alternative strategy for restoring gut microbial homeostasis and potentially slowing CKD progression. A single-center, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial (NCT04361097) evaluated disease progression in CKD patients treated with FMT. Patients with diabetes and/or hypertension diagnosed with CKD stages 2, 3 or 4 were randomized to receive either oral FMT capsules or placebo for six months, followed by laboratory assessments and fecal metagenomic analyses. A total of 28 patients were enrolled (15 in the FMT group and 13 in the placebo group). Regardless of CKD stage, responses to FMT were consistent across patients. Disease progression occurred in 53.8% of individuals in the placebo group, compared with only 13.3% in the FMT group. Furthermore, the FMT group maintained stable renal-function parameters, including serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, whereas these measures declined in the placebo group [396]. These findings indicate that FMT may slow CKD progression and appears to be a safe, orally administered therapeutic option with potential clinical benefit.

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease worldwide, affecting approximately 40% of individuals with diabetes and representing a major driver of CKD progression [397]. DKD substantially increases the risk of cardiovascular events, healthcare costs, and mortality [398]. Pathologically, DKD is characterized by glomerular basement membrane thickening, mesangial cell hypertrophy, podocyte loss, glomerulosclerosis, and tubulointerstitial fibrosis, changes that ultimately lead to progressive proteinuria (albuminuria) and a decline in eGFR [399]. Recent studies reveal profound alterations in the gut microbiota of DKD patients. Both microbial richness and diversity are markedly reduced compared with healthy controls, with fecal samples showing increased abundance of Megasphaera, Acidaminococcus, and Lactobacillus, accompanied by decreased levels of Tyzzerella_3, Roseburia, and Lachnoclostridium [400]. Large-scale analyses further identify enrichment of Klebsiella, Citrobacter, and Escherichia, together with depletion of Roseburia, as key contributors to DKD-associated dysbiosis [401]. Such microbial imbalance compromises intestinal barrier integrity and increases gut permeability (“leaky gut”) [402]. Disruption of the barrier allows LPS, a surface antigen of Gram-negative bacteria, to translocate into the portal circulation, where it activates Toll-like receptor 2/4 (TLR2/TLR4) signaling and the NF-κB pathway, driving endotoxemia and inflammation. Activation of the TLR–NF-κB cascade is therefore considered a central mechanism by which gut microbiota mediate inflammatory injury in diabetic nephropathy [403]. The gut microbiome also functions as a rich endogenous metabolic reservoir that produces bioactive metabolites implicated in DKD pathogenesis. IPA, a unique tryptophan-derived metabolite generated by gut bacteria and absorbed by intestinal epithelial cells, circulates systemically and exerts renoprotective effects [404]. IPA stabilizes SIRT1 by suppressing its phosphorylation-dependent ubiquitination and degradation. Elevated SIRT1 levels promote the deacetylation and nuclear translocation of PGC-1α, thereby upregulating SOD2 and mtTFA expression. These downstream events enhance mitochondrial biogenesis, reduce oxidative stress, decrease proteinuria, and ultimately slow DKD progression [405]. Moreover, the uremic milieu of DKD, together with disease-related dietary modifications and pharmacological treatments, profoundly influences intestinal barrier function, microbial composition, and metabolite production [402, 406]. Gut-derived metabolites—including SCFAs, BAs, TMAO, UTs, and H₂S—are closely associated with DKD onset and progression [407]. These metabolites regulate epithelial barrier function by modulating receptor expression or activating transcription factors, thereby participating in key pathogenic pathways. Collectively, these findings reveal a strong association between gut microbial alterations and DKD phenotypes, including changes in renal function, glucose and lipid metabolism, renal histopathology, inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress markers. Such links highlight the potential of the gut microbiota as both a diagnostic biomarker and a therapeutic target in the management of diabetic kidney disease (Fig. 3).

Gut-centered mediation of long-range inter-organ signaling. Gut- or microbiota-centered inter-organ communication typically involves mechanisms whereby aging-associated gut dysbiosis or alteed microbial metabolites disrupt signaling, contributing to organ functional decline and the progression of age-related diseases. These mechanisms encompass multiple axes, including the gut–liver, gut–lung, gut–kidney, and gut–brain axes. Modulation of the gut microbiota in the elderly

The rising incidence of age-related diseases underscores the critical role of maintaining ecological balance within the gut microbiota for healthy aging and longevity. As the body’s largest immune and microbial ecosystem—and often referred to as the “second brain”—the gut exerts systemic influence through both metabolic and signaling pathways. Disruption of gut microbial homeostasis accelerates organ and organismal aging and predisposes individuals to age-associated disorders. This suggests that gut-centered regulation of organ aging may serve as a key driver of pathological aging processes. The gut plays a pivotal role in nutrient metabolism and in the production of bioactive metabolites that communicate with distant organs. Dietary strategies aimed at reshaping the gut microbiome, including the use of probiotics and prebiotics, have emerged as promising approaches to delay organ-specific and systemic aging by restoring microbial equilibrium. Of particular interest is the concept of gut-centered interorgan communication—encompassing the gut–brain, gut–lung, gut–liver, gut–heart, and gut–kidney axes—which represents a potential paradigm shift with significant clinical implications. Specifically, interventions targeting the gut microbiota to modulate its composition and diversity may influence microbial metabolite production and stabilize interorgan signaling networks. Such strategies could ultimately prevent or mitigate a broad spectrum of age-related conditions, including skeletal, cardiovascular, metabolic, and chronic inflammatory diseases, highlighting the gut microbiome as a central therapeutic nexus for promoting healthy aging.

Dietary interventions

Aging is a progressive decline in physiological functions that not only reflects the passage of time but also serves as a key risk factor for numerous chronic diseases. Importantly, the aging process can be modulated and delayed through targeted interventions. Among the various strategies explored to date, dietary restriction (DR) remains the most robust and reproducible method for extending both healthspan and lifespan. DR has demonstrated consistent benefits across nearly all animal models studied, and even short-term dietary restriction has been shown to confer measurable health advantages in humans [408]. Diet represents the most direct and modifiable factor for shaping the gut microbiota. By maintaining the ecological balance of the gut—the body’s “second brain”—appropriate dietary interventions can enhance intestinal health and slow systemic aging. Mechanistic studies of dietary restriction generally focus on two complementary dimensions. The first centers on nutritional parameters themselves, including the timing of nutrient intake and the functional roles of specific macronutrients. This perspective has given rise to popular approaches such as intermittent fasting, while nutrient composition analyses highlight protein—and more specifically, amino acids—as critical modulators of aging trajectories. The second dimension explores downstream molecular pathways that sense and respond to nutrient availability. Current evidence implicates the insulin/IGF-1 signaling cascade at the systemic level and the mechanistic target of mTOR pathway at the cellular level as key mediators of DR’s effects, linking nutrient sensing to metabolic adaptation and longevity promotion [409]. Together, these findings position dietary restriction as a powerful, evidence-based approach for modulating gut microbial ecology and extending healthy human lifespan.

From a nutritional perspective, both short‐term and long‐term dietary patterns are among the most powerful drivers of gut microbial diversity. Extensive evidence shows that diet plays a pivotal role in shaping the composition, function, and ecological stability of the gut microbiome, exerting profound effects on microbial community structure and metabolic capacity. In many industrialized societies, prevailing dietary habits—characterized by high intakes of animal protein, saturated fat, salt, refined sugar, and ultra-processed foods, together with low consumption of fruits, vegetables, and fiber—are strongly associated with chronic inflammation and a spectrum of non-communicable diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and various cancers [410, 411]. Notably, dietary components do not affect all microbial taxa equally. Instead, specific microbial members, including opportunistic pathogens, are selectively shaped in ways that can promote chronic inflammation. For example, diets rich in animal protein increase the abundance of Bacteroides, Alistipes, and Bilophila while depleting beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus, Roseburia, and Eubacterium rectale, thereby reducing overall microbial diversity [412, 413]. High-fiber diets, by contrast, enhance the production of SCFAs such as butyrate through microbial fermentation. SCFAs help maintain epithelial barrier integrity, suppress inflammation, and strengthen immune function. Dietary tryptophan (Trp), for instance, is metabolized by gut bacteria (Peptostreptococcus russellii, Lactobacillus spp., Clostridium sporogenes) into AHR ligands, which are also sensed by the pregnane X receptor (PXR) [414]. In mice, supplementation with 0.4% Trp increases the abundance of Verrucomicrobia while decreasing Firmicutes and Actinobacteria [415]. Similarly, diets enriched with 1.0% leucine reduce microbial richness and diversity, accompanied by an increase in Firmicutes and a decline in Bacteroidetes [416], highlighting the capacity of amino acid intake to modulate microbial composition. The optimal quantity and type of dietary carbohydrates required for gut health remain debated [417]. Cereal grains contain diverse polysaccharides, some of which are classified as microbiota-accessible carbohydrates (MACs)—fermentable substrates that selectively support beneficial taxa while suppressing harmful species and modulating luminal pH [418, 419]. Dominant gut phyla such as Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes efficiently utilize MACs as energy sources, producing metabolites that promote the growth of beneficial bacteria and inhibit pathogens [420]. Dietary fats also profoundly reshape the gut microbiome, with both quantity and type of fat influencing microbial composition and host lipid metabolism [421]. High-fat diets (HFDs) are consistently linked to impaired barrier integrity and enhanced inflammation, partly by reducing beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Bacteroides–Prevotella, Clostridiales, and Akkermansia muciniphila [422, 423], while increasing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio [424, 425]. These findings underscore the dual role of diet as both a modulator of microbial ecology and a determinant of host physiology. As understanding of diet–microbiome–host interactions deepens, targeted nutritional strategies are emerging as effective tools for health optimization and disease prevention. For example, a “microbiome reconstruction diet” modeled after non-industrialized eating patterns improved microbial function, enhanced the production of beneficial metabolites, promoted weight loss, and reduced multiple disease markers within just three weeks: low-density lipoprotein cholesterol decreased by 17%, blood glucose by 6%, and C-reactive protein (a marker of inflammation and cardiovascular risk) by 14% [426]. Similarly, high-fat diets have been shown to increase circulating palmitic acid by promoting Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron expansion, thereby exacerbating thrombosis, whereas hesperidin supplementation protects against palmitic acid–induced thrombogenesis [427]. In contrast to Western dietary patterns, fiber-rich, low-fat, and low-sugar diets—such as the Mediterranean diet—are consistently associated with improved health outcomes. This dietary model, which emphasizes daily consumption of fruits and vegetables while limiting red meat and processed foods, has been linked to reduced risk of multiple chronic and immune-mediated diseases [428, 429]. Dietary fiber, in particular, is critical for metabolic health: low intake predicts long-term weight gain and increases the risk of type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer [430]. Fermented foods such as natural yogurt, kimchi, and natto provide live Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, directly replenishing beneficial taxa and suppressing pathogenic overgrowth. By contrast, diets high in fat and sugar disrupt the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes balance, promote pro-inflammatory microbes, and elevate oxidative stress, thereby increasing the risk of metabolic disorders. Importantly, not all gut microbes can metabolize dietary fiber; indigestible complex carbohydrates selectively fuel taxa equipped with glycoside hydrolases, such as Bacteroides and certain Firmicutes [431]. Collectively, these studies highlight the central role of dietary nutrients—including amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids, micronutrients, and vitamins—in regulating the gut microbiota and host physiology. Although the Western diet remains dominant in industrialized nations, diverse dietary patterns worldwide warrant further investigation to elucidate their effects on the gut ecosystem, particularly with regard to pathogenic taxa and chronic inflammation. Ultimately, strategies aimed at modulating diet–microbiome interactions offer a promising framework for the prevention and treatment of aging-related diseases and the promotion of healthy longevity.

Probiotics and prebiotics

The recognition that gut dysbiosis contributes to a wide spectrum of diseases has spurred intense interest in therapeutic strategies aimed at modulating the gut microbiota and the immune system. Among the most promising interventions are dietary approaches such as probiotics, synbiotics, and prebiotics delivered as purified compounds or herbal formulations. The World Health Organization defines probiotics as “live microorganisms which, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host.” Probiotics have gained increasing popularity as dietary supplements and functional foods, and accumulating evidence indicates that they may also serve as modulators of cellular aging [432]. The most commonly used probiotic strains belong to the genera Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, both of which exhibit well-documented anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that can influence age-associated pathologies. Recent studies highlight the potential of probiotics to directly modulate aging-related signaling pathways. For example, Lactobacillus fermentum was shown to attenuate SASP activation and alter mTOR signaling in preadipocytes from aging mice [433]. In another study, a genetically engineered probiotic strain, ECNpE(C/A)2, effectively scavenged ROS, reshaped the gut microbiome, and markedly alleviated dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced acute inflammatory bowel disease, even when treatment was delayed [434]. Similarly, oncolytic virus therapy with VSVΔ51 was found to alter gut microbial balance in a murine model of hepatocellular carcinoma, and dysbiosis limited the antitumor efficacy of the virus. Replenishment with Lactobacillus significantly enhanced the oncolytic effect, enabling complete tumor regression in spontaneous liver cancer models [435]. These findings suggest that “targeted probiotic supplementation” may represent a feasible strategy to potentiate cancer immunotherapies by restoring beneficial microbial populations. The therapeutic potential of probiotics also extends to neurodevelopmental disorders. Given the emerging role of the gut microbiome in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), researchers identified Lactobacillus murinus from the gut of Chd8 conditional knockout (Chd8ΔIEC) mice as a candidate for intervention. One month of probiotic administration significantly improved deficits in social novelty recognition, increased the proportion of Drd2-positive neurons, and restored synaptic plasticity pathways, including Gria2 and Snap25. Single-cell transcriptomic analyses further revealed that probiotics may remodel neuronal–glial interaction networks by modulating gut inflammation, microbial metabolite secretion, and vagal nerve signaling [436]. These observations point toward the potential of personalized probiotic interventions to ameliorate neurological disorders through gut–brain communication. Probiotic-derived metabolites play a central role in mediating host–microbe interactions shaped by dietary factors. Several resident gut bacteria—including Lactobacillus, Ruminococcus, Bacteroides, Clostridium, Bifidobacterium, and Enterococcus—are capable of producing diverse tryptophan catabolites such as tryptamine, 3-methylindole, IAA, and indole itself [437–439]. These metabolites act as signaling molecules in inter-organ communication, influencing immune regulation, epithelial integrity, and neuronal activity. In a 3-week intervention trial, a multi-strain probiotic formulation containing nine bacterial strains—Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis SGB06, Bifidobacterium bifidum SGB02, Lactobacillus acidophilus SGL11, Lactobacillus delbrueckii DSM 20081, Lactococcus lactis SGLc01, Lactobacillus plantarum SGL07, Lactococcus lactis SGL01, Streptococcus thermophilus and Streptococcus thermophilus—each administered at a dose of 1.5 × 101⁰ CFU, was shown to modulate body composition, gut microbial profiles and psychopathological parameters in 60 obese or pre-obese women [440]. However, a systematic review encompassing 20 meta-analyses and a total of 16,676 adults reported only moderate effects of probiotics on overweight and obesity. The authors noted that, given the potential adverse effects in some populations, a careful risk–benefit assessment should precede the use of these products [441]. These findings indicate that, despite encouraging results from both preclinical and clinical studies, several challenges and concerns remain regarding probiotic supplementation. Importantly, the mechanisms through which probiotics modulate the gut microbiota are complex and far from fully understood. Considerations include: (1) the risk of fungemia or bacteremia, (2) the possibility of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, (3) contamination risks, (4) the need for individualized assessment prior to prescribing probiotics, and (5) the widespread classification of probiotic products as dietary supplements rather than regulated therapeutics. Consequently, rigorous clinical trials are essential for defining the most effective strain combinations, optimal dosages and treatment durations, as well as for evaluating the long-term efficacy and safety of probiotic supplementation.

Prebiotics act as dietary substrates that modulate the gut microbiota, serving as functional complements or alternatives to probiotics. The International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics (ISAPP) defines prebiotics as non-living food components that are selectively utilized by host-associated microbial populations, thereby conferring health benefits. These compounds include soluble and fermentable fibers, human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs), and non-digestible oligosaccharides (NDOs). Although the definition of prebiotics remains debated, they are most commonly described as dietary carbohydrates that are selectively fermented by the gut microbiota to reshape microbial composition, enhance the growth and activity of beneficial bacteria, and promote host health [442]. Clinical studies support the functional impact of prebiotics on mood and cognitive function. In one study, healthy participants receiving B-GOS or FOS demonstrated improved cortisol awakening responses, an established biomarker for stress and depression [443]. Another clinical trial using inulin-type fructans reported enhancements in mood, recognition memory, and recall relative to placebo [444]. Prebiotics also synergize with probiotics to form synbiotic formulations with pronounced health effects. Notably, a synbiotic composed of Ayurvedic polyphenol-rich herbs (Triphala) combined with Lactobacillus plantarum, Bifidobacterium longum infantis, and Lactobacillus fermentum extended lifespan in Drosophila, while reducing age-associated physiological stress, oxidative stress, and low-grade inflammation [445]. Indeed, the potential of prebiotics to modulate the gut microbiota and improve health outcomes extends beyond animal models and has also been demonstrated in recent clinical studies. In an open-label, non-randomized study in patients with PD, Hall et al. showed that supplementation with inulin promoted the growth of Blautia, Anaerostipes and Bifidobacterium, increased the production of SCFAs, enhanced intestinal barrier function, reduced inflammation, and lowered levels of the neurodegeneration-associated biomarker neurofilament light chain (NfL) (NCT04512599) [446]. Moreover, the effectiveness of prebiotics in metabolic disorders such as obesity and diabetes has also been validated. Inulin enriched with fructooligosaccharides selectively modulated the gut microbiota and significantly reduced body mass index z-scores and body-fat percentage in overweight or obese children (NCT02125955) [447]. These findings indicate that prebiotics hold therapeutic potential across a range of diseases. However, individualized responses must be considered to ensure both efficacy and reproducibility, and addressing safety concerns remains essential when implementing these strategies.

FMT

In recent years, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the gut microbiota is not only essential for the normal function of the digestive system but also plays a pivotal role in maintaining overall health, functioning almost as an indispensable organ of the human body. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiome has been frequently observed in a wide spectrum of chronic diseases, including obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disorders, Alzheimer’s disease, chronic kidney disease, liver diseases, and cancer. As previously noted, the microbiome communicates extensively with multiple organ systems, forming key inter-organ axes such as the gut–brain axis (e.g., autism and depression), the gut–heart axis (e.g., myocarditis and hypertension), the gut–lung axis (e.g., pneumonia), and the gut–liver axis (e.g., non-alcoholic steatohepatitis). Consequently, precise and efficient identification of microbial constituents and the discovery of beneficial gut microorganisms—including both bacteria and the virome—have emerged as major research priorities. Clinical and experimental studies of FMT have provided compelling evidence that transferring gut microbiota from healthy donors to patients can alleviate symptoms of multiple diseases. This observation suggests that the gut microbiota of healthy donors contains key functional bacteria required to sustain human health. Notably, the beneficial functional groups associated with disease remission are predominantly composed of SCFAs–producing bacteria, which degrade dietary fiber to generate SCFAs. These metabolites play critical roles in nutrient supply, immune regulation, and mental health. Moreover, these beneficial bacteria rarely harbor antibiotic resistance genes or virulence factors, rendering them safe for therapeutic application [448]. FMT was first reported in 1958 as a treatment for pseudomembranous enterocolitis administered via enema [449]. In 1983, Schwan and colleagues described the first successful homologous FMT for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection [450], marking a pivotal milestone in the transition of FMT from a folk remedy to an evidence-based clinical therapy. Daily supplementation with low-fermentable fiber has been shown to improve post-FMT insulin sensitivity by selectively modulating specific bacterial populations and enteroendocrine signaling. Furthermore, a single oral dose of FMT can be safely and effectively engrafted in patients with severe obesity and metabolic syndrome [451]. Beyond metabolic and aging-related disorders, modulation of the gut microbiota has profound implications for cancer therapy. Immune checkpoint inhibition targeting programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1) represents an effective strategy for cancer treatment, and numerous studies of anti–PD-1–resistant tumors have demonstrated that gut microbiome modulation exerts a substantial influence on immune system development and function. Importantly, the combination of FMT with PD-1 blockade holds promising potential for the treatment of malignant melanoma [452].

Notably, while inter-individual diversity of gut microbial species is relatively limited, strain-level diversity is highly variable [453]. The gut represents a unique semi-closed ecosystem characterized by constant temperature, diverse nutrient sources, and unidirectional flow. These ecological features constrain the diversity of certain microbial strains. Despite substantial advances in human gut microbiome research, our understanding of the basic strain-level composition remains incomplete, including the number of distinct strains within a given species that can stably colonize the gut—referred to as strain richness (SR). Recent studies have shown that the ecological characteristics of the gut set an upper limit on SR for each species and shape strain gain or replacement during microbiota transplantation [454]. This finding suggests that SR should be carefully considered when designing FMT-based therapies, as it may represent a key determinant of clinical efficacy. Indeed, this constraint may partly explain why FMT is currently approved only for the treatment of recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection (rCDI). Randomized, double-blind clinical trials of FMT have been conducted or are ongoing across dozens of diseases, including chronic infections, metabolic disorders, malignancies, and neurological conditions. However, for most indications other than rCDI, FMT has shown inconsistent and unpredictable efficacy, with variable correlations between therapeutic outcomes and donor strain engraftment [455]. Moreover, interactions between donor and recipient microbial subspecies play critical regulatory roles in the successful transfer of functional microbial communities and in determining clinical outcomes—a dynamic ecological process that has been observed across multiple disease-specific FMT metagenomic datasets [456]. Given the bacterial dominance of the gut microbiome, most FMT research has focused on bacterial transplantation, making bacterial transfer a promising strategy for improving FMT outcomes [457]. Several next-generation microbiota transplantation techniques have therefore been developed, including washed microbiota transplantation (WMT), bacterial spore preparations, and fecal virome transplantation. WMT aims to enrich bacterial components from washed fecal microbiota preparations by removing bacterial fragments, metabolites, soluble molecules, proteins, and viruses, while ensuring the elimination of fungi and parasitic eggs [458]. Bacterial spore preparations represent another approach, combining chemical and physical methods to selectively enrich bacterial spores [459].

Notably, analyses of post-FMT strain dynamics refute the “super-donor” hypothesis and underscore that strain-level diversity, together with the complementarity between donor and recipient strains, is critical for FMT efficacy[460]. This concept likely represents one of the key rationales for advocating personalized FMT strategies to enhance therapeutic success [461]. In addition, neglecting the safety considerations of FMT may lead to severe consequences, including the transmission of antibiotic-resistant microorganisms, pathogenic viruses, and pro-inflammatory metabolites [458]. A meta-analysis of FMT failure cases further indicates that advanced age, prior medical history, and hospitalization status are major predictors of unsuccessful outcomes. Therefore, rigorous assessment of patient suitability for FMT is essential to mitigate safety risks associated with treatment failure [462]. Overall, current FMT practices must meet several key criteria: the use of uncultured microbiota derived from healthy human donors, relatively defined microbial composition, enhanced safety demonstrated through clinical trials, and gastrointestinal delivery as the primary administration route. These standards are designed to preserve both known and unknown beneficial components of fecal material while reducing heterogeneity through more precise formulations and improved methodologies. Looking forward, next-generation FMT is moving toward transplantation of defined microbiota components—targeting specific bacterial and viral consortia—to achieve greater therapeutic precision.

Rational use of antibiotics

Antibiotics act as key modulators of the symbiotic relationship between the host and the gut microbiota and remain the most commonly used clinical strategy for altering gut microbial composition. Although antibiotics are designed to target human and animal pathogens of interest, their molecular targets—such as cell walls, ribosomes, and RNA polymerases—are highly conserved across the bacterial kingdom. Consequently, antibiotic exposure in the gut indiscriminately affects both pathogenic and commensal bacteria, thereby disrupting ecological niches responsible for numerous metabolic processes. Short-term antibiotic treatment can drive the gut microbiota toward a long-lasting alternative dysbiotic state, permitting disease progression and exacerbation. Studies in healthy adults have shown a sharp decline in α-diversity within the first few days of treatment, with incomplete recovery of the microbiome for up to 6 months post-treatment, typically measured by shifts in β-diversity between baseline and follow-up. These community-level alterations are accompanied by marked reductions in key members of the phyla Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria, with the most significant decreases observed in Faecalibacterium, Eubacterium, Roseburia, Bifidobacterium, Dorea, Anaerostipes, and Ruminococcus [463]. Importantly, antibiotics not only select for resistant pathogens but also seed the gut with a vast reservoir of resistance genes, shaping distinct resistome profiles [464]. Metabolite–microbe–antibiotic dynamics within the gut microbiome represent one of the key mechanisms driving microbial perturbation. Host-derived metabolites and bacterial signaling molecules can modulate intrinsic antibiotic susceptibility. For example, in Bacteroidetes, the presence of polysaccharides such as branched starch increases Bacteroides tolerance to amoxicillin, whereas glucose consumption sensitizes them. Similarly, human milk oligosaccharides alter membrane permeability, rendering Streptococcus agalactiae susceptible to trimethoprim [465]. These findings underscore the gut microbiome as a complex ecological network in which antibiotic effects can vary widely, disrupting both species- and community-level susceptibility through intricate mechanisms during antibiotic exposure. Emerging evidence links childhood antibiotic exposure to an increased risk of diseases such as asthma, juvenile arthritis, type 1 diabetes, Crohn’s disease, and various psychiatric disorders, with gut microbial dysbiosis proposed as a key underlying mechanism. A systematic review of antibiotics and pediatric gut microbiome disruption revealed consistent evidence of reduced richness, diversity, and taxonomic alterations following antibiotic use in children [466]. Despite heterogeneity in participant age, environment, duration of antibiotic exposure, drug class, route of administration, outcome measures, and timing of analysis, these factors collectively contribute to disturbances in microbial diversity and may exacerbate the risk of other gut-centered diseases. Notably, certain non-antibiotic medications can also perturb the gut microbiota, altering colonization resistance and increasing susceptibility to enteric pathogens [467]. For instance, the cardiac glycoside digoxin disrupts gut microbial communities and reduces their ability to resist infection by Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium [468].

Given the variable activity spectrum of antibiotics against different members of the commensal gut microbiota, the use of narrower-spectrum therapies can reduce microbiome disruption and accelerate gut recovery. For example, in mice, treatment with the narrow-spectrum agent fidaxomicin caused markedly smaller shifts in taxonomic composition compared with the broad-spectrum antibiotic vancomycin, promoted faster restoration of the pre-treatment community structure, and more effectively preserved colonization resistance against Clostridioides difficile [469]. Antibiotic specificity can also be modulated through combination regimens involving other antibiotics or non-antibiotic drugs. Such combinations may exert neutral, synergistic, or antagonistic effects on the activity of a given antibiotic, and these modulatory interactions can vary across microbial taxa. In addition, strategies that lower intestinal concentrations of systemically administered antibiotics can minimize off-target effects and limit collateral damage to the gut microbiome. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of rational antibiotic use to safeguard the integrity of the gut microbial ecosystem. Future research aimed at dissecting antibiotic–microbiota interactions will not only deepen our understanding of these complex dynamics but may also guide the development of diversified antibiotic strategies and novel microbiota-targeted interventions for disease prevention and control.

Precision microbiome-based therapies

In the practical modulation of the ageing gut microbiota, the simultaneous control of dietary, genetic and environmental factors remains highly challenging. Moreover, the widespread use of antibiotics can disrupt the ecological diversity of the gut microbiome. Although probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, postbiotics and AMPs have been explored as alternative approaches, their safety and efficacy remain insufficiently established [470, 471]. In addition to FMT, an expanding suite of precision microbiome-based therapeutic strategies is emerging. These include synthetic microbial communities (SynComs), genetically engineered microbes (GEMs), postbiotics and targeted bacteriophage therapy, all of which have demonstrated promising outcomes. SynComs are composed of defined microbial consortia capable of exerting beneficial effects on the host. Unlike FMT, SynComs are well-characterized yet inherently incomplete communities. The ability to add, remove or genetically modify strains enables precise manipulation of gut microbial functions [472]. Despite the advantages of improved controllability and reproducibility, the therapeutic performance of SynComs depends heavily on their rational design and assembly, processes that remain technically demanding. Beyond FMT and SynComs, GEM-based strategies employ engineering principles to enhance endogenous microbial functions or introduce novel capabilities to meet specific therapeutic needs. GEMs can influence host physiology through multiple mechanisms, including the incorporation of biosensors into engineered strains to detect pathogen-derived signalling molecules and thereby enable targeted disease control [473]; rewiring metabolic pathways to produce beneficial metabolites or degrade toxic compounds [474]; and improving traits such as adhesion or stress tolerance to enhance probiotic survival in the gut environment [475] Notably, engineering Lactococcus lactis to secrete β-lactamase within the intestine has been shown to degrade residual β-lactam antibiotics without altering serum antibiotic levels, thereby preserving therapeutic efficacy while mitigating microbiota dysbiosis [476]. Such findings highlight in situ microbiome editing as a compelling direction for future investigation. A variety of techniques—including transformation, conjugation, phage-based delivery systems and integrative and conjugative elements (ICEs)—have been developed for the construction of GEMs [477].

Among these, bacteriophages possess unique advantages due to their high specificity and favourable safety profile, positioning them as potential alternatives to antibiotics [478]. Phages can reshape the gut microbiota by selectively lysing target bacteria [479];, modulating host immunity, or serving as vectors for genetic material to facilitate in situ editing of specific microbial populations [480, 481]. Indeed, phage-based strategies have been widely applied in microbiota modulation and disease treatment owing to their precision in manipulating the GI microbial ecosystem. For example, λ phage particles engineered with chimeric tail fibres have been used to selectively infect target bacteria and deliver DNA encoding base-editing tools, enabling in situ gene editing of the GI microbiota in live mice [482]. Nonetheless, phage-related safety concerns remain non-negligible, particularly given the greater diversity and mutability of viruses relative to bacteria. High phage loads have been reported to induce pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IFN-γ, IL-12 and TNF, thereby triggering inflammatory responses [483]. Rigorous dose assessment is therefore essential. Strategies aimed at modulating the ageing gut microbiome—including prebiotics, probiotics, dietary interventions and FMT—ultimately rely on precise characterization of microbial communities. Only through multi-omics technologies can the complex relationships between the microbiome and host physiology be progressively decoded. Encouragingly, advances in high-throughput microbiome profiling have generated large-scale datasets, offering unprecedented opportunities to decipher the microbiome’s role in human health. However, the compositional, high-dimensional and sparse nature of metagenomic data presents substantial challenges for biomarker discovery, differential analyses and community visualization. These challenges are further exacerbated by the typically small sample sizes of microbiome studies, limiting the applicability of traditional statistical approaches. Consequently, artificial-intelligence-based frameworks are increasingly adopted to overcome these constraints, offering enhanced scalability for complex microbiome analyses. Among the diverse applications of AI, disease classification and diagnostic prediction represent the most clinically advanced. For example, Tataru et al. developed a predictive model trained on microbial attribute data to improve the classification of inflammatory bowel disease [484], whereas Pope et al. constructed a deep language model trained on large-scale unlabelled microbiome datasets for IBD prediction [485]. AI has also demonstrated significant promise in guiding microbiome-targeted interventions such as FMT and phage therapy. In phage engineering, AI can optimize receptor-binding affinities and bactericidal activities, while simulation-based models provide predictive insights into therapeutic outcomes, thereby enhancing clinical translation [486]. Beyond diagnosis and intervention, AI also plays a critical role in prognostic evaluation of microbiome-based therapies. The Cox-net model, integrating regularization with survival analysis, has been widely applied in therapeutic outcome prediction [487]. By combining patient-specific microbial features with clinical parameters, AI systems can estimate post-treatment survival risk and recurrence probability, facilitating personalized therapeutic adjustment [488]. Collectively, the convergence of AI with single-cell sequencing, multi-omics integration and knowledge-driven inference frameworks is poised to propel microbiome-based precision medicine into a new era.

Exercise

Exercise, as a fundamental biological activity, is widely recognized as a highly effective, low-cost intervention for promoting health and mitigating aging-related decline. Regular physical activity confers benefits across a range of chronic conditions, including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and metabolic disorders [489, 490]. Conversely, a sedentary lifestyle and severe physical inactivity are recognized risk factors for the development of type 2 diabetes. Exercise not only promotes skeletal muscle remodeling and functional coordination through contraction and extension but also exerts systemic effects on non-contractile tissues [491]. Beyond skeletal muscle, exercise can activate autophagy pathways in non-contractile tissues, including the liver, pancreas, and adipose tissue. For example, muscle-derived fibronectin 1 (FN1) mediates exercise-induced autophagy in stem cells and enhances insulin sensitivity through hepatocyte α5β1 integrin and downstream IKKα/β–JNK1–BECN1 signaling, thereby improving systemic metabolic homeostasis and alleviating diabetic pathology [492]. In rodent models, retrospective analyses of parental nutrition and exercise patterns suggest that maternal and paternal physical activity can mitigate metabolic dysfunction in adult offspring, including impaired glucose homeostasis and maladaptive responses of metabolic tissues. These effects are partially mediated by modifications in placental and sperm biology, indicating that regular exercise in reproductive-age parents may disrupt the transgenerational transmission of metabolic risk [493]. Recent studies have provided a detailed molecular and cellular atlas of acute versus chronic exercise responses in humans, revealing that the kidney functions as a key responder to exercise-induced benefits. Renal-derived metabolites, particularly betaine, act as critical signaling molecules that attenuate aging by inhibiting the innate immune hub kinase TBK1, thereby suppressing systemic inflammation and slowing multi-organ aging [494]. This highlights an emerging paradigm in which “endogenous metabolite-mediated exercise benefits” could inform the development of novel systemic anti-aging strategies, including potential “exercise-mimetic” therapeutics. Exercise also modulates the gut microbiome to influence immune and metabolic outcomes. Physical activity increases gut-derived formate levels, activating Nrf2 signaling and enhancing CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor immunity [495]. suggesting a role for exercise in cancer prevention and therapy. Similarly, aerobic exercise combined with dietary interventions in patients with NAFLD reshapes gut microbial composition and function, improving gut ecosystem stability and mitigating hepatic fat accumulation [496]. Collectively, these findings indicate that exercise-induced modulation of the gut microbiota and their metabolites, and the downstream signaling they mediate, represents a promising avenue for the prevention and treatment of aging-associated diseases (Fig. 4).

Challenges in gut microbiome research

Confounding factors and sample size

The gut microbiome is a dynamic and living ecosystem that fluctuates in response to human behaviour and physiological rhythms. Microbial composition can shift according to dietary intake [497], patterns of intestinal motility [498], and even circadian oscillations [499]. These inherent dynamics underscore the necessity of accounting for the complexity of host–microbe interactions, as well as the numerous potential confounders and covariates that arise during such interactions. Common confounding factors include age, sex, diet, medication use, psychological stress, body mass index, and environmental variables that influence the intestinal milieu [500]. Accurate identification and quantification of these confounders—including dietary habits, histories of antibiotic exposure, and inflammatory status—is essential for delineating the boundaries of a healthy gut microbial ecosystem and for enabling the discovery of robust disease-associated microbial signatures in clinical microbiome studies [498]. Evidence indicates that heterogeneity in human lifestyle and physiological traits can differentially shape the gut microbiota. By inferring the largest and most pervasive sources of variation across human gut microbial profiles, researchers have identified lifestyle and physiological factors that, if not adequately balanced between case and control groups, may confound microbiome analyses and lead to spurious associations between microbes and disease [501]. Thus, appropriate sampling strategies constitute a critical first step toward achieving accurate biological and clinical interpretations. This requirement highlights the importance of adopting more representative sampling frameworks in studies investigating microbiome heterogeneity and its relationships with ageing-related disorders. Whether through increased use of in situ sampling or through rigorous standardization, documentation and computational adjustment of covariates known to influence faecal microbiome variability, refined sampling approaches will ultimately enhance the precision and translational relevance of microbiome–ageing research.

Notably, substantial microbiome differences exist across geographic regions, cultural backgrounds and social groups; however, due to disparities in economic development and research capacity, the gut microbiome profiles of many populations remain insufficiently characterized. In addition, technical factors—including sample collection procedures, storage conditions and DNA extraction methods—may introduce methodological noise and confound analytical outcomes. Thus, substantially larger datasets are required to robustly assess the relationships between gut microbiota, ageing and ageing-associated diseases. For much of the past decade, limited availability of faecal samples has posed a major bottleneck, as only a small number of cohorts collected such data. Encouragingly, an increasing number of large-scale population studies now compare thousands to tens of thousands of individual samples, integrating human genotypes with gut metagenomic and metabolomic profiles through multi-omics approaches [502]. Despite these advances, large-scale, data-driven meta-analyses remain scarce in gut microbiome diversity research; traditional studies often rely on limited sample sizes, and phenotypic characterisation is not always comprehensive. A recent study aggregated 168,464 16S rRNA sequencing samples from 68 countries to construct a global human gut microbiome database (microbiomap.org) and systematically evaluated the effects of geographic and technical factors on microbiome composition [503]. These findings underscore the need to address sampling representativeness in human microbiome research. Increasing sample sizes and conducting meta-analyses with global coverage will be essential to achieve greater statistical power, enable more comprehensive characterization of the human gut microbiome and facilitate the discovery of previously unknown microbial taxa.

Challenges in bioinformatics and human sample diversity

A central technical and methodological challenge in studying gut microbiome alterations in ageing and ageing-associated diseases lies in the detection and accurate classification of microbial strains present in intestinal samples. Despite well-recognized technical biases and the limited taxonomic resolution inherent to subgenus-level classification, many studies still rely on characterizing microbial communities through fragments of the 16S rRNA gene [504]. Marker-gene metagenomics typically involves sequencing specific regions of taxonomically informative genes—such as the 16S rRNA gene for prokaryotes, the 18S rRNA gene for eukaryotes or the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) for fungi—thus enabling the identification of particular taxa within a sample. Although this approach has been foundational in microbial ecosystem profiling, it suffers from variability in diversity estimation across hypervariable regions and constrained taxonomic resolution [505]. Even with continuing improvements in metagenomic tools, sequencing non-bacterial components of the microbiome—such as viruses, fungi, archaea and eukaryotic cells—remains limited by methodological and reference database gaps, despite the critical role these organisms may play in disease processes. In contrast, whole-genome shotgun (WGS) metagenomics sequences and analyses the complete genomic content of all organisms within a sample, thereby enabling comprehensive profiling of ecological diversity across archaea, bacteria, viruses and eukaryotes. The high resolution and complexity of WGS data permit strain-level discrimination and direct functional potential analysis [506]. To address these analytical challenges, machine-learning (ML)–based classification approaches—including support vector machines (SVMs) and random forest models—have emerged as effective alternatives to alignment-dependent sequence classification methods [507]. Thus, large-scale gut metagenomic studies in older adults with ageing-related diseases should be strongly encouraged to elucidate microbe–host interactions and ultimately support the development of personalized therapeutic strategies.

A further challenge in gut microbiome research is the limited diversity of available human samples. Publicly accessible microbiome datasets currently lack representation, as most derive from individuals of European ancestry and are predominantly collected in Europe and North America [508], studies including African American participants remain exceedingly rare [509]. This imbalance restricts the potential health benefits and clinical applicability of microbiome research, confining insights largely to populations residing in affluent regions where research infrastructure is well developed—and even within these regions, typically to specific ethnic groups. Associations between microbial signatures and disease identified in these cohorts may fail to replicate in underrepresented populations or ethnicities [508]. Given that individuals in low- and middle-income countries often experience reduced quality of life following the onset of ageing-associated conditions, yet remain significantly underrepresented in global microbiome research, addressing this disparity is essential. Broadening the inclusion of underrepresented populations will be critical for filling key knowledge gaps and for elucidating globally relevant patterns linking the gut microbiome to ageing and ageing-related diseases.

Biological causality

Aging and aging-associated diseases have increasingly been linked to alterations in the gut microbiota, or “dysbiosis”. However, whether these microbial shifts represent a cause, a consequence or merely a bystander of disease remains largely unresolved. Human microbiota–associated (HMA) rodent models have become a cornerstone of microbiome science, providing critical tools for probing causal relationships between microbial perturbations and host pathophysiology. Although an expanding body of evidence indicates that the faecal microbiota of individuals with various diseases differs from that of healthy controls—encompassing changes in taxonomic composition, diversity indices and functional readouts [510] —a universal definition of a “normal” or “healthy” microbiome has yet to be established[511],. Consequently, most studies employ the term “dysbiosis” simply to denote microbiome configurations associated with a particular condition relative to controls. Whether such “unfavourable” microbial states are causal, consequential or co-driven by a third factor remains unproven in the majority of cases [512]. The most compelling experimental evidence for causality in human disease derives from demonstrating the therapeutic value of FMT [513]. Notably, when FMT is used to evaluate the microbial contribution to chronic disease risk, long-term prospective studies are essential to establish causal links between microbiome alterations and pathological outcomes. Furthermore, given the limitations of extrapolating animal findings to humans, new methods for establishing causality directly in human populations have emerged. Novel statistical frameworks—such as Mendelian randomization and mediation analysis [514, 515], —have been applied to infer causal relationships between gut microbial metabolites and host biomarkers, supported by mathematical and machine-learning approaches that can now be integrated with microbiome datasets. From a bioinformatics perspective, future microbiome research must also address specific biological and computational challenges. For example, inferring biological causality remains a persistent obstacle in computational microbiome studies, which aim to extract clinically meaningful interpretations from high-dimensional datasets dominated by correlation rather than mechanism [516]. Overall, given that the microbiome is a shared component across diverse diseases and pathological processes—irrespective of their complexity—there is an urgent need for more critical, rigorous and methodologically sound frameworks in microbiome research to advance causal understanding.

Conclusions and perspectives

Physiological decline poses a major threat to the physical and mental well-being of the elderly, making the extension of both lifespan and healthspan a critical focus of current research. Preventing age-associated diseases, improving functional capacity, and maintaining overall health to prolong healthy longevity have become pressing societal needs. Aging is a complex biological process characterized by multidimensional changes across molecular, cellular, tissue, organ, and organismal levels. During this process, energy regulatory mechanisms essential for maintaining physiological homeostasis are frequently disrupted, typically accompanied by metabolic imbalance and chronic inflammation, which together markedly increase susceptibility to disease. At the organismal level, cellular senescence-driven aging is often associated with functional decline in the cardiovascular, nervous, and urinary systems, thereby elevating the risk of chronic disorders. To a considerable extent, the maintenance of organ function depends on metabolic homeostasis to meet energy demands, which ultimately rely on nutrient digestion and absorption in the gut. Notably, deterioration of gut function is itself a key manifestation of systemic organ aging [517],, highlighting the gut microbiota as a promising avenue for healthy aging research. Accumulating evidence indicates that aging is accompanied by profound alterations in both the composition and function of the gut microbiota, shifting from eubiotic to dysbiotic states and from non-pathogenic to pathogenic configurations. Such transitions often occur during unhealthy aging and are implicated in the onset of multiple age-related diseases. Among these changes, gut microbiota–driven dysregulation of the intestinal mucosal immune system can trigger systemic chronic inflammation (gut microbiota–associated systemic chronic inflammation, GM-SCI), which is closely linked to aging-associated SASP factors, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, C-reactive protein, and IFN-γ. Integrating gut microbiome research with other aging theories—such as genomic instability, telomere attrition, and mitochondrial dysfunction—offers a powerful framework for understanding the biology of aging. This holistic perspective may ultimately reveal synergistic intervention targets capable of simultaneously modulating multiple mechanisms of aging, thereby advancing strategies to promote healthy longevity.

With advancing age, the structure and function of the gut microbiota undergo profound changes that can contribute to the onset or progression of various diseases. In recent years, substantial progress has been made in elucidating the roles and mechanisms of the gut microbiota in aging-related intestinal and extraintestinal disorders, and novel microbiota-targeted strategies have been developed to promote human longevity. Encouragingly, large-scale exploration of gut microbial dynamics using metagenomics and culturomics, the establishment of long-term longitudinal cohorts of healthy elderly individuals (particularly the very old), and the integration of multi-omics technologies—including genomics, epigenomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and immunomics—are beginning to reveal critical links between aging and the gut microbiome. A growing body of evidence indicates that gut microbes and their metabolites play key roles in maintaining host intestinal homeostasis and influencing the development of numerous diseases, including neurodegenerative, cardiovascular, metabolic, and gastrointestinal disorders. Fundamentally, aging of the gut itself, or alterations in gut microbial diversity (such as reductions in beneficial taxa), may disrupt multiple inter-organ communication pathways—including the gut–muscle, gut–brain, gut–heart, gut–liver, gut–lung, and gut–kidney axes—and thereby drive functional decline or pathological changes in these organs through diverse signaling mechanisms [518]. More importantly, the gut microbiome exerts compelling effects on host health by participating in a range of physiological processes, such as the digestion of dietary nutrients, defense against pathogens, production of bioactive metabolites, and molecular signaling to the brain and distant organs [519]. These metabolites—including lipopolysaccharide, peptidoglycan, trimethylamine, BA, and short-chain fatty acids—interact with specific receptors to activate diverse signaling pathways, ultimately influencing the aging and degeneration of multiple organs. These findings suggest that preventing or treating aging-related diseases by modulating the gut microbiota or its metabolites is a feasible strategy. Future research should aim to clarify the functional relationships among the gut microbiome, the aging process, and common degenerative diseases in the elderly; to improve intestinal and gut–extraintestinal aging through targeted microbial modulation; to identify aging-associated functional bacteria or metabolites; and to develop novel microbiota-based anti-aging interventions.

Notably, the composition of the gut microbiota is highly complex and shaped by multiple host and environmental factors, including genetics, sex, aging, lifestyle, and external exposures. These multifaceted determinants play critical roles in modulating the taxonomic structure, functional capacity, and diversity of the gut microbial ecosystem, exerting profound effects on its stability and metabolic output [520]. Among these, lifestyle habits such as diet, physical inactivity, and alcohol consumption can disrupt microbial homeostasis and trigger inflammation, thereby increasing the risk of aging-associated diseases, including cardiovascular disorders, kidney disease, and cancer. To prevent the progression of diseases driven by microbiota dysbiosis, a range of therapeutic strategies has been developed to modulate the gut microbiome and host immune system. These interventions include dietary approaches—such as the administration of probiotics, synbiotics, and prebiotics in isolated or herbal formulations [521]. In addition, antibiotic use, while capable of reshaping the microbial community, can compromise host health. Perturbations such as antibiotic therapy destabilize the intestinal ecosystem, leading to marked reductions in functional richness and microbial diversity and adversely affecting metabolic health. Thus, it is essential to maximize the therapeutic benefits of these agents while minimizing collateral damage to commensal microbes and limiting the broader spread of antimicrobial-resistant pathogens [522]. Looking ahead, strategies aimed at preserving or restoring a healthy gut microbiome following perturbation—such as FMT, supplementation with probiotics or non-digestible carbohydrates, and broader dietary interventions—hold promise for preventing or reversing dysbiosis-associated disease states.
