Acute sciatic nerve palsy secondary to postoperative seroma following soft tissue sarcoma resection: A case report
Authors: Ahmad Hammad, Rakan Nassereddine, Said Saghieh
Published: 2025

Introduction

Soft tissue sarcomas (STS) are rare and include a wide array of subtypes that arise from connective tissues, namely liposarcoma, rhadomyosarcoma, synovial sarcoma and angiosarcoma, among others [1,2]. STS account for merely 1 % of all adult solid malignancies [1,3].

The standard of treatment consists of a wide local surgical resection with the addition of radiation and/or chemotherapy as adjunct to surgery [1,3]. Surgical resection with negative margins is necessary to avoid local recurrence which occurs in 10–15 % [1]. However, surgery is not without immediate postoperative complications that include neuro-vascular injury, functional deficit, formation of hematoma/seroma or infection at surgical site [1,4]. The overall wound complications rate after lower extremity STS resection is 30.2 % and ranges from 17.6 % to 48 % [5].

Seroma is a fluid collection at a surgical site and most studies in the literature report on postoperative seroma and their associated complications following breast surgery, abdominoplasty, flap harvesting and lymph node dissection [4]. Seromas occur between 9 and 36 % of patients following STS resection and to are of lower incidence after vascular by-pass [3,6]. This study aims to report on acute sciatic nerve palsy secondary to postoperative collection following soft tissue sarcoma resection causing compartment syndrome. Reports on acute sciatic nerve compression by a seroma following STS resection are scarce; hence the theme of this case report to provide an overview of the resultant postoperative recovery, associated complications and treatment options. Both oral and written informed consents were obtained regarding the case study and future publications and the work has been reported in line with SCARE criteria [7].

Case presentation

This is the case of a 70-year-old male patient known to have multiple comorbidities including hypertension, coronary artery disease, hypertension, obstructive sleep apnea and coronary artery bypass graft maintained on Aspirin. He has a history of low grade liposarcoma of the right thigh that was initially diagnosed in April 2024 and resected at another institution.

The patient presented to our hospital for findings of recurrent large distal thigh mass encasing the femoral vein and artery seen on follow-up MRI of the thigh 6 months following primary resection. Biopsy of the mass was performed showed low grade liposarcoma without evidence of distant metastasis on PET scan. During the time, the patient had not underwent any chemo- or radiation therapy. To note, he was asymptomatic but reported feeling a lump in posterolateral aspect of right thigh without neurological deficit.

In the context of recurrent resectable tumor of the thigh engulfing the vascular bundle without distant metastasis or recent exposure to chemoradiotherapy, discussion with the patient and family yielded a decision to proceed along with vascular surgery team with radical re-resection of recurrent liposarcoma and end-to-end anastomosis of femoral vein and artery. Intraoperatively, following successful radical resection of tumor, repair of femoral artery and femoral vein end-to-end anastomosis was performed. The patient left the operating room with 2 drains at the harvest and resection sites. He was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for few days of proper monitoring having tolerated the procedure well and requiring 3 pRBC transfusions. At 2 days postoperatively, the patient was mobilized out of bed with restrictions on knee flexion and started on enoxaparin 40 mg to mitigate risk of thrombosis vs bleed.

The next day, the patient still in the ICU reported chest discomfort with an episode of tachycardia and desaturation reaching 88 %. The workup included chest CT angiography which was suggestive of pulmonary embolism (PE), normal echocardiography without right ventricular strain, and bilateral deep vein thrombosis (DVT) of the gastrocnemius veins as evident on lower extremity duplex. At the time, the patient required high-flow nasal cannula and started on therapeutic enoxaparin dose. The next day, the patient had worsening chest discomfort with several episodes of dyspnea reaching 80 %; repeat chest CT angiography showed new PE but stable DVT and the patient was then started on heparin drip. The drains were kept during the entire ICU stay and were yielding between 280 cc and 500 cc daily.

At day 9 postoperatively, the patient was stable and was transferred to regular floor. The drains output decreased to 40 cc per day and were removed at day 11. The next day, the patient was found to have significant swelling of right leg reaching the upper thigh and it was more tense as compared to the contralateral leg; however, the patient was still neurovascular intact with audible pulses on doppler. Duplex showed no new DVT but the formation of a new collection at site of tumor excision, and a compressive dressing was then applied. The edema, however, continued to increase and right lower extremity became more tense, with increasingly severe pain. Hence, needle aspiration was performed and yielded 80 cc. Few hours later, the patient complained of complete loss of sensation and motor power below the right knee. Urgent CT angiography of the right lower extremity showed 20 × 12 × 8 cm large intramuscular collections in the posterior and medial compartment of the right thigh reaching just above the knee with a 1 cm pseudoaneurysm in the right adductor magnus muscle compressing the femoral vein and sciatic nerve (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

The findings were discussed with the patient and the decision to proceed with emergent incision and drainage was agreed on. Along with vascular team, incision and drainage of intramuscular blood clots with evacuation of seroma and control of blood and sciatic nerve decompression was performed. The patient received multiple transfusions and 2 drains were inserted. The next day, an inferior vena cava filter was inserted as the patient could not be maintained on therapeutic anticoagulation given the high risk of bleed and high drain output. During the next days, the patient had decreased thigh edema and was ambulating to chair as he had significant improvement of motor power and sensation of the right leg but weaker ankle dorsiflexion (3/5). Few days later, he was started on therapeutic enoxaparin and was stable and discharged home with 1 drain that was yielding 80-130 cc per day. Several days later, the patient presented to clinic for follow-up and the drain output decreased to less than 50 cc per day and neurovascularly the patient had re-gained almost full motor power and sensation and was compliant with physical therapy.

Discussion

Seroma formation following STS resection results in a complex postoperative course with a prolonged hospital stay, functional limitations, wound complications and delayed adjuvant treatment [5]. Patients with STS of the lower extremity suffer from the worst health-related quality of life as demonstrated by the nationwide Observational Study (PROSa) [8]. This study reports a case of acute sciatic nerve palsy as a rare complication and a symptom of compartment syndrome secondary to seroma/hematoma following STS resection and provides an overview of the resultant postoperative recovery, associated complications and treatment options.

Seroma is a collection of plasma ultra filtrates that can be mixed with blood, and forms at surgical site postoperatively [5]. It is a non-infected fluid filled pocket that develops in the residual dead-space and may be identified clinically or incidentally on follow-up imaging [1]. It is not rare to occur following STS resection but is of lower incidence following vascular and revascularization procedures of lower extremity with rate between 0.7 and 0.8 % [6]. Several hypotheses could explain its formation including postoperative drop in albumin, lymphatic leakage or crushing and shearing strains leading to fluid collection [3,5].

Seroma formation is associated with smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, high body mass index, preoperative radiation, large-yolume tumor (>10 cm) in close proximity to skin (<3 mm from skin surface) and lower rather than the upper extremity involvement [3,[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Persistent large volume drainage within the first 3 days is a risk factor for seroma formation [10]. Complete and faster resolution of postoperative seromas occurred at higher rates among patients who did not undergo radiation postoperatively (49 % vs 11 %), had seroma size <85 cm3 (61 % vs 17 %) and upper extremity involvement. Whereas preoperative radiation is associated with seroma formation, postoperative radiation can be associated with progression of an existing seroma [3]. Multiple studies found higher grade edema and seroma to occur in patients who had radiation and surgery (76 %) as compared to surgery only (7.7 %) [12,13]. The use of sclerosants, including tetracycline or doxycycline, at initial surgery was found to increase the risk of seroma [4].

Most seromas after STS resection shrink over time and their clinical course is not well established. While many seromas remain asymptomatic and self-resolve, some cause discomfort, limit functionality and predispose to infections [3]. Andryk et al. found that 62.6 % of seromas were uncomplicated with 52.8 % resolving spontaneously and 13 % were symptomatic and 24.4 % caused infections [1]. Therefore, if there are indicators of pro-longed or complex drainage course, further diagnostic studies are warranted for early diagnosis and possible intervention.

There is no consensus on the appropriate treatment and several options exist to treat postsurgical seromas/hematomas ranging from non-operative drain application and needle aspiration to surgical evacuation and debridement. Effective strategies to prevent seroma formation include postoperative surgical site immobilization, limb compression, maintaining drain at a high pressure gradient and keeping it until the output volume is minimal, using sharp or ultrasonic dissection rather than cautery and using quilting or progressive tension sutures [1,10,14]. The use of talc in subcutaneous space and lymphatic reconstruction with interpositional flap were found to reduce seroma formation [15,16].

Dead space obliteration is important to decrease seroma formation (26 % vs 74 %) and need for revisions hence costs and clinic visits [17]. Papanikolaou et al. found that 41 % of postoperative seromas require needle aspiration with 61 % eventually requiring surgical treatment [10]. Nickerson et al. suggest that volume aspiration of >50 ml prompts surgery [18]. Andryk et al. found that seromas that were aspirated or drained did not develop infection despite that 50 % recurred [1]. Surgical wound repair includes debridement, negative pressure wound therapy, deep packing, secondary wound closure and plastic reconstruction [5]. Ishida et al. found that surgical neurolysis resulted in 80 % muscle power recovery as compared to 12.5 % in conservative group, and surgical intervention on same day of palsy onset restores full sensation and motor power [19].

Sciatic nerve palsy is a debilitating condition especially during the immediate post-operative course of rehabilitation where the patient requires the optimum functional capacity of the extremity for proper physical therapy. Immediate palsy of sciatic nerve is a recognized complication of hip arthroplasty due to direct injury, thermal injury or prolonged traction [20,21]. However, delayed-onset palsy due to seroma/hematoma collection is rare and can be attributed to recent increase in use of potent anticoagulants for thromboprophylaxis [21]. Historically, the incidence of sciatic nerve palsy increased from 0.2 % to 1.69 % following the implementation of low molecular weight heparin [22].

The mass effect in a non-compliant compartment results in venous congestion, arterial hypoperfusion of vasa nervosum hence progression from neurapraxia to axonotmesis especially if decompression is delayed [19]. Ideally, recognition of palsy within 24 h and early intervention with same-day surgical decompression and evacuation results in superior motor recovery and should be prioritized over serial aspirations due to high risk of recurrence and sensitive nature of nerve ischemia [18]. In our report, the patient had progressing symptomatic pulmonary embolism that restricted his out-of-bed mobility and required therapeutic anticoagulation. Hence in patients with high risk of DVT/PE and bleeding, a prophylactic vena cava filter should be considered. Analogous reports after hip arthroplasty describing delayed onset sciatic palsy from compressive hematomas in anticoagulated patients with better recovery when surgery is performed early [20,21]. Our case mirrors these observations and reemphasizes that patients requiring therapeutic anticoagulants require multidisciplinary coordination that balances hemorrhagic risk against time-sensitive need for neural decompression [22]. Focused, time-critical approach aligns our experience with the arthroplasty literature and underscores that surgical timing is the principle modifiable determinant of neurologic outcome in compression-related sciatic palsy.

In cases of postoperative seroma diagnosis, it is imperative to counsel patients that surgery might be required eventually despite trials of bedside drainage and needle aspiration due to high risk of recurrence and need for surgical revision. Clinicians should also be aware that simple drainage alone is insufficient in situations of complete sciatic nerve palsy due to risk of recurrence, and therefore surgical excision including capsule removal with nerve decompression seems to be the optimal rational approach. Recent focus on new modalities to decrease incidence of seroma include Bioglass/Ceria nanoparticles and the use of the anti-inflammatory agent triamcinolone [23,24]. Nonetheless, further research is needed to fully understand the mechanisms of action, their safety and efficacy.

Conclusion

Different preventive intra- and postoperative strategies need to be implemented to minimize wound healing disorders and associated seroma and hematoma complications following STS resection. Sciatic nerve palsy is a rare complication secondary to compression but is reversible with adequate monitoring and prompt timely intervention. Adequate and timely wound healing is necessary for early and rapid transition to adjuvant therapy. Further caution is warranted in patients using potent aggressive anticoagulants due to se-rious potential complications postoperatively.
