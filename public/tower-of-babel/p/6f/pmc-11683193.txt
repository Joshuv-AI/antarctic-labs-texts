12. Vascular pain: Ischemic pain in the extremities and Raynaud's syndrome
Authors: Antal van der Gaag, Steven P Cohen, Milan P Stojanovic, Frank J P M Huygen, Jan Willem Kallewaard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeripheral artery diseases (PAD) and Raynaud's syndrome are associated with substantial morbidity. PAD, through the restriction of blood flow to the extremities, may lead to critical limb ischemia with symptoms of pain at rest which may eventually progress to severe limb ischemia with gangrene. This serious and painful clinical condition requires extensive medical care, is limb‐threatening and, in case of delayed or unsuccessful treatment, is associated with a high mortality rate. In Raynaud's syndrome, the blood supply to certain parts of the body, usually the fingers and toes and less frequently the nose or ears, is restricted because of vasculopathy of the smaller vessels at acral sites. Under certain circumstances, with cold as the most well‐known provoking factor, blood flow restriction occurs, leading to demarcated color changes and symptoms such as pain, paresthesia, and numbness. In severe cases of Raynaud syndrome tissue ischemia may lead to necrosis and the need for amputation of the affected area.MethodsIn this narrative review, the literature on the diagnosis and interventional pain treatment of PAD and Raynaud's syndrome was updated and summarized.ObjectivesThis review focused on interventional pain treatment. In PAD, the effects of the intervention on limb salvage, ulcer healing, and ischemic pain were summarized. Additionally, results with respect to skin microcirculation and quality of life were reported if available. In Raynaud's syndrome, we focused on the effect of the intervention on peripheral blood flow metrics and pain intensity during attacks.ResultsIn PAD, prevention and treatment of risk factors are important. Initially, conservative treatment and pharmacological therapy are preferred first‐line therapies. However, when disease progression occurs, interventional management may be considered. The literature search yielded conflicting evidence for sympathectomy as a treatment for PAD. Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) as a treatment modality for advanced PAD had high‐quality evidence for limb salvage in subgroups of patients but conflicting evidence for other outcome measures such as pain, wound healing, and quality of life. The literature search for interventional pain management in Raynaud's syndrome was limited to only one randomized controlled trial (RCT) studying the effect of thoracic sympathectomy. This study had several limitations and hence the level of evidence for this interventional treatment is very low. No RCTs studying SCS in patients with Raynaud's syndrome were found.ConclusionsIn both PAD and Raynaud's syndrome, additional RCTs are needed to substantiate interventional (pain) management and bolster the evidence base for sympathectomy and SCS as treatment options.Keywords: evidence‐based medicine, ischemic pain, peripheral arterial disease, Raynaud's syndrome

INTRODUCTION

This narrative review on ischemic pain is an update of the 2011 article published in the series “Evidence‐based Interventional Pain Medicine According to Clinical Diagnoses”. 1 Atherosclerotic artery pathology is related to numerous disease entities. Atherosclerotic diseases such as coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral artery disease (PAD) are potential causes of acute ischemia of vital organs and extremities, leading to significant morbidity and mortality. Chronic perfusion disorders in PAD may present in a variety of ways. Mild symptoms including exercise pain and intermittent claudication may progress to more pronounced PAD, leading to critical limb ischemia with trophic disorders such as necrosis and gangrene. The manifestations and stages of lower extremity arterial disease can be described using the Fontaine classification, which is based on clinical symptoms, or the Rutherford classification in which clinical presentation is accompanied by objective criteria. 2

Peripheral vasculopathy with vasospasm of smaller vessels at acral sites typically affects fingers and toes, but can also involve the ears, nose, and nipples. This condition is known as Raynaud's syndrome. Primary Raynaud's syndrome is diagnosed if the symptoms are idiopathic and is also referred to as Raynaud's disease, whereas secondary Raynaud's syndrome, also referred to as Raynaud's phenomenon, is associated with underlying conditions such as autoimmune disorders and occasionally malignancies. In the early stages, microvascular injury leads to a triad of symptoms including pallor, cyanosis, and erythema. During successive stages, pain, discomfort, and functional impairment ensue.

Epidemiology

Critical PAD is a serious and frequently reported problem worldwide. 3 Patients experience ischemic pain and wounds with poor healing ability due to lack of perfusion. Secondary morbidity with partial amputation of an extremity or even death is common. 4 Risk factors for PAD include smoking, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and hypertension. Additional independent factors such as advanced age and chronic renal failure increase the risk of progression to critical limb ischemia (CLI). 5 The prevalence of PAD is approximately 5.6%, with gender differences regarding epidemiology, pathophysiology, and presentation, highlighting the need for gender‐specific treatment. 3 , 6 When the atherosclerotic process in the peripheral arteries progresses, a state of critical limb ischemia can emerge. This arterial insufficiency results in ischemic pain at rest, non‐healing wounds, and gangrene despite medical care. Eventually, this may lead to additional morbidity with functional loss, auto‐amputation, or the need for surgical amputation of part of a limb.

In general, primary Raynaud's syndrome has a reported prevalence of about 3%–5%. 7 Raynaud's syndrome is more commonly reported in colder regions. Cold is the characteristic trigger of symptoms with sequential changes in color patterns observed in affected regions. Physical and psychological stress are other common triggers. In primary Raynaud's syndrome, symptoms are relatively mild and benign in nature. In secondary Raynaud's syndrome, symptoms are more pronounced with acral digital ulcers, infections, and osteomyelitis sometimes necessitating amputation. Secondary Raynaud's syndrome accounts for 10%–20% of all cases. 7 Although symptoms at initial presentation are often diagnosed as primary Raynaud's syndrome, in subsequent years signs and symptoms of a connective tissue disorder emerge such as systemic sclerosis or mixed connective tissue disease. 8 , 9

METHODOLOGY

This narrative review is based on the article “Ischemic Pain in the Extremities and Raynaud's Phenomenon” published in 2011. 1 In 2016, an independent research company, Kleijnen Systematic Reviews Ltd., performed an update of the evidence on which the original article was based. As a result, in 2019 an update on the evolution of the quality of evidence and the strength of recommendations on interventional pain medicine in vascular pain was published. 10 For the current article, an additional literature search for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was conducted for the period 2015–2022. For ischemic pain, the search terms used were “extremities” AND “ischemic,” AND “pain,” associated with various interventional pain techniques such as “spinal cord stimulation” and “sympathectomy.” For Raynaud's syndrome, the search strategy included “Raynaud's syndrome” in combination with “spinal cord stimulation” or “sympathectomy.” In addition, the authors searched all retrieved bibliographic reference lists for relevant missing articles.

PERIPHERAL ARTERIAL DISEASE

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

Patients with PAD can present with classic symptoms of mild to severe lower extremity claudication. However, a subset of PAD patients presents with less specific symptoms (or occasionally no symptoms) such as nonspecific pain in the back and/or buttocks, or vague discomfort in the lower extremities. 11 When examining a patient with suspected PAD, disease‐specific symptoms should be distinguished from non‐vascular presentations such as neurogenic claudication and syndromes with pseudo‐radicular pain.

The ankle‐brachial index (ABI) is an instrument used to confirm the diagnosis of PAD. Alternatively, the toe‐brachial index using photoplethysmography and laser Doppler can be used as a diagnostic tool. In a symptomatic population, additional diagnostic modalities such as transcutaneous oximetry, laser Doppler flowmetry with skin perfusion pressure, angiographic perfusion imaging, computed tomography angiography, and magnetic resonance angiography can provide additional support in the diagnosis of PAD. 12 , 13

Chronic ischemia may also result in ischemic neuropathy as evidenced in both preclinical and clinical studies using electrodiagnostic and electroneurographic testing as the reference standard, with the degree of neural pathology correlating with the extent of ischemia. 14 , 15 The onset of neuropathic symptoms in patients with ischemic neuropathy is generally insidious, with significant overlap in the presentation with neuropathic pain from other etiologies. The prevalence of neuropathies in patients with peripheral vascular disease (PVD) ranges widely between less than 15% to over 60% in large arteriole vasculopathies. 16 The identification of a neuropathic pain phenotype is important as these individuals may benefit from adjuvants such as gabapentinoids and antidepressants, and topical lidocaine when allodynia is present. In some studies, patients with neuropathic pain are also more likely to respond to spinal cord stimulation (SCS). 17

TREATMENT OPTIONS FOR PERIPHERAL ARTERIAL DISEASE AND ISCHEMIC PAIN

Conservative treatment

PAD is a manifestation of systemic atherosclerosis and therefore, PAD patients benefit from measures to reduce cardiovascular and cerebrovascular risk factors. It is of the utmost importance to motivate and provide resources for patients to quit smoking. Smoking strongly contributes to the development of PAD and plays a clear and negative role in the progression to CLI. 18

In patients with PAD, exercise is an essential part of primary care. Exercise programs, whether supervised or home‐based, contribute to improved walking distance in patients with PAD. 19 Cognitive behavioral therapy, education, and exercise therapy in a multidisciplinary setting also contribute to positive outcomes. 20 , 21

Pharmacological therapy

To reduce the cardiovascular risks in this patient category, pharmacological therapy should be initiated as indicated. The goal of pharmacotherapy is to correct dyslipidemia, treat hypertension, manage diabetic mellitus, and minimize the clotting risk through the initiation of antiplatelet therapy. 5

Interventional management for ischemic vascular disease

Patients with symptoms of PAD are initially treated conservatively. However, when symptoms persist in patients with severe claudication, pain at rest, and ulcers with or without gangrene, a vascular intervention may be considered. Vascular bypass surgery, angioplasty, endarterectomy, thrombolysis, and SCS for chronic lower limb ischemia are reviewed and published in a Cochrane review by Antoniou et al. in 2017. 22 In this article, the authors focused on early perioperative outcomes including non‐thrombotic complications and procedural mortality, as well as longer‐term outcomes such as clinical improvement according to the Rutherford classification, amputation, patency of the graft or vessel, and mortality.

With the introduction of endovascular treatment, a new therapeutic entity has become available for CLI. Therefore, patients previously believed to have a non‐reconstructable vascular disease can now be offered an alternative. The scientific community is continuously striving to improve endovascular techniques, with the comparative outcomes of open surgical therapy versus endovascular therapy evolving. The verdict of this discussion will no doubt influence considerations regarding interventional pain treatments. Currently, in patients with non‐reconstructable PAD, sympathectomy, and SCS are non‐surgical interventional treatment options.

Sympathectomy

In addition to the previous literature search from Devulder et al. as published in the series “Evidence‐based Interventional Pain Medicine According to Clinical Diagnoses,” a new search was performed for sympathectomy. 1 The literature search by Devulder et al. yielded three RCTs. Our search did not identify any new RCTs. One Cochrane review was published in 2016. 23

In 1975, Fyfe et al. reported on 25 patients with either a history of intermittent claudication who were randomly assigned to receive a lumbar sympathectomy using phenol or a subcutaneous injection of a local anesthetic in the lumbar region. 24 Although 85% of patients in the lumbar sympathectomy group experienced an increase in limb temperature on the first day after treatment, this improvement declined over the subsequent 12 weeks. No temperature increase was observed in the control group. Despite this observation, no differences were found between the groups regarding improvement in intermittent claudication and differences in leg‐arm blood pressure ratio at 3‐month follow‐up.

In 1985, Cross and Cotton studied the effect of chemical lumbar sympathectomy on ischemic rest pain. Thirty‐seven patients (41 limbs) were randomized to receive either paravertebral blockade with phenol or blockade with bupivacaine. 25 Significant relief of rest pain was observed in the phenol group compared with the bupivacaine paravertebral block (83.5% vs. 23.5% improvement rate) at 1 week, which persisted for 6 months. The authors postulated phenol block leads to interference of pain transmission by chemical neurolysis of afferent pain fibers located in the sympathetic chain. However, ABI and peak blood flow in the foot were not significantly different between the phenol treatment group and the control group. Statistical comparison of groups after 6 months was impossible because of the need for additional interim treatments such as phenol injection, vascular surgery, and/or amputation. In conclusion, the authors concluded that phenol sympathectomy is an effective procedure to relieve rest pain.

The third RCT by Barnes et al. studied the effect of a simultaneous added sympathectomy during an iliac artery reconstruction in 51 patients. 26 Although sympathectomy as an add‐on treatment improved foot vascular resistance as measured by plethysmography, it provided no additional benefit on limb circulation. The fact that the lumbar sympathectomy was performed in a population with reconstructable options in limb ischemia limits the generalizability.

The Cochrane review by Karanth et al. asserted that because previous studies did not use objective selection criteria for CLI, additional RCTs are needed to determine the effect of lumbar sympathectomy in CLI with non‐reconstructable PAD. 23

Spinal cord stimulation

Our literature search for SCS in CLI yielded six RCTs. In addition, several meta‐analyses and systematic reviews have been published in recent years including two Cochrane reviews focusing on patients with CLI. 22 , 27

In 1994, a multicenter Belgian randomized study described SCS as an innovative treatment for ischemic pain in PVD. 28 This study was designed to evaluate the effect of SCS on ischemic pain, the recuperative effect of SCS on ischemic ulcers, and the effect of SCS on limb salvage in patients with PVD due to arteriosclerosis or arteritis. A total of 38 patients were randomized to treatment with SCS or conservative treatment in this trial. This study showed that patients in the SCS group (N = 20) experienced less pain and better ulcer healing in comparison with the conservative treated group (N = 18). Despite this positive finding, no significant differences between groups were found when comparing limb salvage. Subgroup analysis showed that patients with arteriosclerotic disease treated with SCS experienced better clinical improvement. Patients with previous surgical revascularization and those without hypertension also responded better to SCS. The success rate of SCS was higher in patients with uncomplicated rest pain and less pronounced ulcerations.

In accordance with the Belgian trial, another randomized study by Jivegard et al. also focused on limb salvage, comparing SCS with conservative analgesic treatment in patients with inoperable severe lower limb ischemia. 29 The hypothesis of this study was that SCS would reduce the amputation rate at 18 months compared to the control group. Twenty‐six patients were assigned to the control group and 25 patients to the SCS group. At the 18‐month follow‐up, there was no significant difference in the percentage of limbs saved between the SCS group and the control group. Secondary outcomes in this trial showed that the SCS group experienced less pain as measured on a VAS.

In 1996 Claeys and Horsch published an article highlighting TcPO2 as a predictor of clinical outcome in vascular patients treated with SCS. 30 In a population of 86 patients with non‐reconstructable Fontaine IV symptoms, patients were randomized to receive either treatment with prostaglandins or prostaglandins in combination with SCS. In the SCS‐prostaglandin group, significantly more patients improved clinically toward Fontaine stage III and II. Despite this difference, in agreement with the previous studies, no effect on limb salvage was observed. The overall TcPO2 improvement in the SCS‐prostaglandin group was better than in the group treated with prostaglandin alone. ABI did not differ between the two groups studied, but a significant improvement in ABI was found in a subgroup of SCS patients with complete ulcer recovery.

From 1999 until 2000, a Dutch SCS research group published three RCTs. On behalf of this research group, Ubbink et al. published the first results from a multicenter trial. 31 This RCT focused on the effect of SCS on skin microcirculation, as obtained by non‐invasive skin investigations, compared to standard treatment. Using clear inclusion criteria that included Doppler ankle systolic pressure ≤50 mm Hg or ankle/brachial pressure index ≤35%, 111 patients with non‐reconstructable CLI were randomized between standard treatment and SCS. Ubbink et al. did not find any difference between the groups in terms of limb salvage. In patients with a good microcirculatory status before treatment, the amputation rate was low and similar between the two groups. In the subgroups of patients with poor microcirculation, the amputation rate was high (75% after 18 months) in both treatment groups. In patients with intermediate microcirculatory perfusion, SCS patients showed a tendency toward better limb salvage, but statistical significance was not achieved.

From the same trial population, Klomp et al., as members of this Dutch research group, described the outcomes of 120 patients with non‐reconstructable CLI who were included in this RCT and assigned to medical treatment (medication and/or lumbar sympathectomy) or SCS. 32 Limb salvage at 2 years follow‐up was the primary endpoint. Amputation‐free survival was similar in both groups.

A third article originating from this research group compared patients with CLI who were randomized between standard treatment and medical treatment with additional SCS therapy. 33 This article focused on pain and quality of life with a follow‐up period of at least 18 months. Improvement in quality of life was observed in both groups with no significant intergroup differences. In the SCS group, mobility subscores and energy subscores were better at 6‐month follow‐up. The pain was measured using a VAS, McGill pain questionnaire and the Nottingham health profile. Both treatment strategies resulted in clinically meaningful pain reduction. However, this reduction was equivalent in both groups, with no statistically significant difference between the two treatment strategies observed. However, as measured using a medication quantification scale, patients who received SCS treatment used significantly less analgesics.

Members of this Dutch research group contributed to the two meta‐analyses that were published in the following years. In 2004, Ubbink et al. published a meta‐analysis that included RCTs of SCS in patients with surgically non‐reconstructable CLI. 34 The outcome of several variables was assessed. According to their analysis, SCS potentially contributed to limb salvage, resulted in significant pain relief in patients with CLI, and improved extremity arterial disease manifestations. The second meta‐analysis was published in 2009 by Klomp et al. 35 Based on five RCTs, this meta‐analysis focused on prognostic variables for the risk of amputation and predictors for a favorable SCS outcome in patients with CLI. The risk factor analysis showed that ischemic skin lesions were related to an unfavorable limb survival outcome. SCS did not reduce the overall risk of amputation compared with standard treatment. Subgroup analysis also failed to reveal a subgroup of patients who would benefit from SCS. The authors also noted that the individual RCTs did not have sufficient power to detect a positive effect of SCS with the individual study sample size calculations being underpowered. Consequently, the authors conclude that new RCTs are needed to establish the effect of SCS in (subgroups) of patients with CLI.

The Cochrane database of systematic reviews included a 2013 analysis by Ubbink and Vermeulen regarding CLI and SCS. 27 The authors concluded that RCTs evaluating SCS using limb salvage as a primary outcome showed no significant difference, despite a trend toward better limb salvage in the SCS groups. Subgroup analyses revealed some advantages in particular populations, for example, for selected patients with higher baseline TcPO2 and for normotensive patients. When data were pooled, SCS yielded a significant effect on limb salvage after 12 months (NNT 9). This review also evaluated secondary outcomes such as pain relief, wound healing, ABI, quality of life, costs, and complications associated with SCS. The authors found that although SCS resulted in pain reduction as measured by the VAS and pain questionnaires, in some studies pain reduction was similar to that observed in the control groups. Wound healing has been studied in several studies. When data were pooled, improvement in clinical stages according to Fontaine classification was observed. However, when pooling data related to the healing of ischemic ulcers, no difference was found between conservative treatment and SCS therapy. Subgroup analysis comparing wound healing in patients with or without hypertension yielded conflicting effects. The ABI as a measure of improvement also produced conflicting results. Quality of life, measured with different questionnaires, improved with SCS and conservatively treated patients, with no differences between groups. After pooling data from the two RCTs that measured TcPO2, no significant intergroup differences were found. Based on a single RCT, SCS therapy was associated with higher costs than conservative treatment. The authors concluded that SCS in carefully selected subgroups has the potential to contribute to limb salvage, pain reduction, and quality of life improvement.

In a more recent review, Asamikidou et al. re‐examined the contribution of SCS in PVD. The authors evaluated the six RCTs and compared the two existing meta‐analyses in detail. 36 Based on this comparison, the authors concluded that SCS in PVD should be considered as a treatment option. However, they also clarified that no additional RCTs were published in recent years and highlighted the need for new information to support efficacy and safety.

Complications of SCS in CLI

When considering SCS in CLI, the possibility of side effects and complications must be taken into account. These include complications related to the implantation procedure, the hardware implanted, and/or biological complications. 27 , 33 , 37 , 38

After pooling data, the Cochrane review by Ubbink and Vermeulen found that the risk of lead implantation failure was 8%. Hardware‐related problems such as lead migration with undesirable changes in stimulation, lead fracture with malfunction, or unexpected battery failure were reported in 12% of implants. Superficial or deep wound infections with subsequent infection of the SCS system are also reported frequently in SCS interventions. In pooled data from the CLI RCTs, the incidence was 3%. 27

RAYNAUD'S SYNDROME

Clinical presentation and diagnosis

Raynaud's syndrome is a vasculopathy of multifactorial origin involving the distal extremities. The proposed mechanisms and the extent to which these mechanisms contribute to the natural course of the disease have not yet been fully clarified. The pathogenesis of Raynaud's syndrome consists of structural vascular alterations, intravascular endothelial abnormalities, and neural imbalances. The relative contributions of these factors probably differ in primary Raynaud's syndrome compared to secondary Raynaud's syndrome and consequently, different phenotypes are observed in clinical presentation. In accordance with precision medicine, a better understanding of the mechanisms and their relative contributions to specific patients will help direct appropriate medical treatment. Because cold is known to trigger symptoms, patients are advised to avoid exposure to cold, maintain a high body core temperature, and wear gloves. Other important lifestyle interventions include avoiding emotional stress and quitting smoking. 39 In primary Raynaud's syndrome these non‐drug measures will improve the symptoms in a considerable number of patients. Some of the patients will require additional pharmacological treatment. While the attacks are interfering with quality of life and cause discomfort there is no permanent tissue damage. In secondary Raynaud's syndrome, the clinical presentation is determined by the context of the systemic disease. In advanced disease stages, persistent tissue hypoperfusion will be reflected by digital ulcerations and, in exceptional cases, even with digital ischemia. These conditions are extremely painful and require adjustment of the pharmacological therapy with the addition of pain‐relief medicines, including the temporary use of opioids. In the rare case of digital ischemia consultation with a vascular surgeon, hand surgeon, or pain specialist is advised to consider debridement, (digital) sympathectomy, or amputation.

The differential diagnostic considerations in secondary Raynaud's syndrome are numerous. In addition to auto‐immune‐based Raynaud's syndrome, there are several nonautoimmune manifestations of Raynaud's syndrome. Conditions involved in nonautoimmune Raynaud's syndrome include hematologic syndromes, obstructive vascular disease, thoracic outlet syndromes of neurologic or vascular origin, environmental factors, infections, and the use of certain medications. 7 , 40 , 41 , 42

TREATMENT OPTIONS FOR RAYNAUD'S SYNDROME

Conservative treatment

Pharmacological therapy

As a contributing mechanism to Raynaud's syndrome, addressing the imbalance between vasoconstriction and vasodilatation represents a therapeutic target. The overactive vasoconstrictive component can be modulated by calcium channel blockers. 43 Nifedipine is perhaps the most studied drug with its use resulting in a reduction of vasospastic frequency and intensity. Another treatment option is the topical application of nitroglycerin. Alternatively, PDE‐5 inhibitors such as sildenafil, l‐arginine (in secondary Raynaud's syndrome), alpha‐adrenoreceptor antagonists, and/or angiotensin receptor antagonists can be used. 41 In severe cases of Raynaud's syndrome, intravenous prostanoids are often utilized. 44

For patients who are refractory to oral, topical, and/or intravenous therapies, botulinum toxin injections at the bifurcations of superficial digital arteries or along the palmar vascular bundles to block the sympathetic adrenergic output have been shown to restore distal blood flow and provide prolonged benefit. 41 Although studies have demonstrated the superiority of lumbar sympathetic botulinum toxin injections compared to local anesthetic for pain relief and prolonged temperature increase in complex regional pain syndrome which is often accompanied by decreased perfusion and trophic changes, randomized trials have not been conducted for Raynaud's syndrome or PVD. 45 , 46

Interventional management

Interventional management may be considered in Raynaud's patients with an advanced disease presentation and in those who fail to respond to conservative and pharmacological treatment. The interventional pain techniques described in the treatment of Raynaud's syndromes are sympathectomy and SCS.

An upper thoracic sympathectomy can be performed either percutaneously or thoracoscopically. 47 , 48 Gabrhelik et al. described 50 patients who received either percutaneous radiofrequency (RF) thoracic sympathectomy at the T2 and T3 segments or percutaneous RF at T2 with the addition of phenol. During 90 days of follow‐up, peripheral blood flow, pain scores, and quality of life were compared to baseline values. Significant improvements in the peripheral temperatures under standard conditions and after cold exposure were observed in both groups, with no significant differences between treatment approaches. Susceptibility to cold‐related vasospasm remained unchanged in both groups. Pain scores were significantly lower after treatment in both groups, again without intergroup differences during 90‐day follow‐up. Similar results were found regarding quality of life. Besides this study, no other randomized trials describe the outcomes of an upper thoracic sympathectomy. Cohort studies have reported high recurrence rates. 49 , 50 An ongoing evaluation by Kuijpers et al. (2022) focusing on the long‐term effects of surgical sympathectomy, with 1‐year results already available, is of interest. The authors reported significant improvements in the treated versus untreated arm for pain, physical function, and duration of attacks, with some attenuation of effects. 48 Additional trials studying the effects of sympathectomy in Raynaud's syndrome are needed to determine the added value of this treatment.

The use of SCS for the treatment of Raynaud's syndrome has only been described in case reports and small cohort studies. 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 The proposed mechanism of effect is reduced sympathetic vasoconstriction with beneficial effects anecdotally reported for Raynaud's episodes, ischemic ulcerations, and pain control. Well‐designed randomized trials are needed to assess the potential role of SCS in the treatment of patients with Raynaud's syndrome.

Techniques

Lumbar sympathectomy

The goal of a lumbar sympathectomy is to disrupt the sympathetic nerve fibers to block the transmission of pain signals and decrease vasomotor tone in the extremity. As a result of this sympathicolysis, blood flow to the extremity may improve. A lumbar sympathectomy can be performed in several ways. In interventional pain treatment, percutaneous lumbar sympathectomy is usually performed under fluoroscopy by means of chemical neurolysis and/or RF ablation of the lumbar sympathetic chain. The procedure for percutaneous lumbar sympathectomy may also be guided by CT imaging, MRI, or ultrasound. Less commonly used techniques for a lumbar sympathectomy include both a direct open surgical approach and a laparoscopic approach.

Both RCTs mentioned in this review used a percutaneous procedure with chemical neurolysis to treat patients. Fyfe et al. 24 injected 5 mL of a phenol solution into the sympathetic chain, whereas Cross et al. 25 treated patients with either 7.5 mL phenol or 7.5 mL of bupivacaine solution. Studies have employed many different techniques to perform sympathectomy, with Cross et al. using two different procedures in the treatment group in an effort to maximize the success rate.

The sympathetic chain and its ganglia are located bilaterally at the anterolateral side of the vertebrae. The lumbar parts of this chain, particularly the L2–4 segments, are involved in the sympathetic output to the lower extremities, though occasionally lower extremity innervation may pass through the L1 and L5 ganglia. A sympathectomy at these levels, preferably multi‐segmental, will result in reduced sympathetic output and, as a result, reduced vasomotor tone of the lower extremities.

From a practical point of view, a fluoroscopic approach is preferred for a lumbar sympathetic block. The patient is placed in the prone position on a treatment table which is suitable for fluoroscopy. A C‐arm fluoroscope is used to identify the L2–4 levels (Figure 3).

Lumbar sympathectomy. Oblique projection. Site of entry. Additional caudal tilt may be needed to avoid the transverse process.

Rotation of the C‐arm to an oblique position is needed to identify the skin entry point. Lateral rotation of the C‐arm is continued until the distal end of the processus transversus is aligned with the (antero)lateral margin of the vertebral body. Burying the processus transversus within the vertebral body ensures that a needle trajectory just outside the bony margin will avoid hitting the structure. The entry site is chosen in a way that the physician is able to guide the needle toward the concavity of the vertebral body at its anterolateral aspect. The physician should be aware that too lateral of an approach could lead to trauma to vital organs such as the kidneys or ureters. In general, the needle skin entry point should be located no more than 7–8 cm from the midline. In a trajectory co‐axial to the C‐arm long axis, the needle is advanced until it touches or nears the vertebrae on the anterolateral aspect, as the sympathetic chain typically lies close to the lateral bony margin (Figure 4). In cases in which a direct approach to the anterolateral margin of the vertebral body is obstructed by the processus transversus, the entry point is chosen such that the needle will reach the target while passing the processus transversus caudally at the inferior margin. This is especially important at L2 where the ganglia are more likely to be situated toward the lower part of the vertebral body.

Lumbar sympathectomy. Needle directed to the concavity of the vertebral body using tunnel view positioning.

During the procedure, additional anteroposterior (AP) and lateral fluoroscopic views are obtained for visualization and monitoring of needle position. When a satisfactory position of the needle tip is obtained, contrast dye is injected under real‐time imaging to rule out vascular uptake and confirm the correct position. A small amount of contrast dye should be used to avoid obscuring the visual field should the need for repositioning the needle occur. In the AP view and lateral views, the contrast dye should be observed in the peri‐vertebral space with no evidence of diffusion into the psoas muscle or dorsolateral spread to the lumbar plexus which can undermine specificity by inadvertently anesthetizing the genitofemoral or a segmental nerve (Figure 5). The provider should also be aware of the possibility of intravascular or neuraxial injection.

Lumbar sympathectomy. Lateral view. Contrast dye in perivertebral space.

With the needle in the correct position, temporary sympathicolysis for diagnostic purposes is obtained by injection of 2–5 mL of a local anesthetic. For a definitive sympathetic block, chemical neurolysis with phenol or alcohol may be considered. Another option for a definitive sympathectomy is a RF denervation. However, the relatively small lesions produced by even large RF probes and the wide variability in the location of ganglia make this technique more prone to a high technical failure rate. This approach also requires the availability of suitable RF needles and an RF lesion generator to achieve a thermal RF lesion of the sympathetic chain.

Spinal cord stimulation for non‐reconstructable PAD

Prior to spinal cord stimulator implantation, intravenous antimicrobial prophylaxis is administered according to local practice guidelines for the prevention of surgical site and spinal cord stimulator system infection. Practical Evidence‐based recommendations for the optimal outcome can be found in the Neurostimulation Appropriateness Consensus Committee (NACC) guidelines by Deer et al. 55 The implantation of a spinal cord stimulator is performed by an experienced pain specialist or neurosurgeon under aseptic conditions in an operation theater. The patient is positioned in a prone position in a manner in which a relative lumbar kyphosis is created to facilitate access to find the epidural space. Standard monitoring equipment is connected to the patient. Field blocks and wound infiltration with local anesthetics are used to provide analgesia. If needed, procedural sedation and analgesia can be administered for anxiolysis and patient comfort.

A Tuohy needle is used to enter the epidural space at a lumbar level. The loss of resistance technique is used to confirm entry into the epidural space with the correct position confirmed using contrast injection and inserting a guidewire placed through the needle. The position of the guidewire in the epidural space is verified fluoroscopically in multiple views. Next, an electrode array is inserted using a through‐needle technique. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the cephalad tip of the electrode is positioned at the lower thoracic spinal cord segments. Test stimulation is performed using an external neurostimulator to determine if the electrode is in the optimal position by evoking paresthesias in the area(s) of pain. Subtle position modifications of the lead are performed to obtain the ideal coverage. When paresthesia coverage is deemed satisfactory, the lead is fixed at the lumbodorsal fascia and tunneled subcutaneously to an external pulse generator in the case of a trial period or to the implantable pulse generator pocket for connection to a permanent internal pulse generator. After implantation is completed, an X‐ray is taken to confirm the position of the lead and rule out accidental displacement. The stimulation parameters can be further adjusted as needed to optimize coverage and pain reduction. Patients are instructed before discharge on what to do in case of complications and how to manage their programing device.

SUMMARY

This narrative review updates the article “Ischemic Pain in the extremities and Raynaud's Phenomenon” 1 published in 2011, and complements the update published in Pain Practice in 2019 by Huygen et al. 10

A new search for evidence for lumbar sympathectomy did not identify any new RCTs. Therefore, the evidence remains unchanged. There is conflicting evidence regarding the efficacy of sympathectomy for the treatment of ischemic pain in the extremities. However, sympathectomy may be considered if symptoms are refractory to conservative treatment and a revascularization procedure is not possible due to technical limitations or comorbidities. Because the evidence is unclear, it is currently recommended that the procedure be performed only in clinical trials or in an attempt to salvage limbs in refractory patients.

Our literature search for SCS in CLI yielded six RCTs. These six trials were also included in the 2011 review by Devulder et al. 1 In addition, several meta‐analyses and systematic reviews have been published in recent years. Two Cochrane reviews focused on patients with CLI and SCS as a treatment option. There is high‐quality evidence that SCS results in higher limb salvage rates in selected subgroups of patients with PAD. 27 There is conflicting evidence for SCS to reduce pain, and improve wound healing and quality of life in subgroups of patients with PAD. The potential benefits of SCS must be weighed against the risks and increased costs. Serious adverse events are rare, with most being technically related. Large well‐designed RCTs focusing on new targets and stimulation technologies are needed to provide additional information to support the efficacy and safety of SCS in PAD.

The literature search for interventional pain treatments in the management of Raynaud's syndrome identified only one RCT which studied thoracic sympathectomy as a treatment. This study contained several limitations including the absence of a control group undergoing a sham procedure, the small sample size, and the limited follow‐up period of 3 months. Based on this trial there is very low‐quality evidence that T2–T3 thoracic RF sympathectomy, and T2 RF sympathectomy with supplemental local application of phenol, may improve peripheral blood flow, reduce pain, and improve quality of life in patients with Raynaud's syndrome. Based on this trial, it is not possible to conclusively state that a sympathectomy adds value to the treatment of acral ischemia in patients with Raynaud's syndrome. Further RCTs involving upper thoracic sympathectomy are recommended and needed.

The use of SCS for the treatment of Raynaud's syndrome has only been described in case reports and small case series. This results in very low‐quality evidence supporting the effectiveness of SCS in Raynaud's syndrome. SCS should be considered for the treatment of patients with Raynaud's syndrome only in a study setting or in severe, refractory cases in an attempt to preserve limbs.
