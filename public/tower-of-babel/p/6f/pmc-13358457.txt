Case Report: Congenital caudal tibial curvatures in two alpaca crias
Authors: Alexandra von Altrock, Teresa Maria Punsmann, Cornelia Schwennen, Matthias Gerhard Wagener, Doris Hoeltig, Nils Jannis Eckmann, Andreas Beineke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital deformities of the tibia are rare conditions in animals as well as in humans. The cause is often unknown. Although congenital defects in South American Camelids are more frequent than in other species, to our knowledge, there are no previous reports of congenital tibia deformities in alpacas (Vicugna pacos). Both unrelated presented crias showed a caudal curvature of the tibial diaphysis of either one or both legs soon after birth. Radiographs revealed a focal circumferential endosteal and intramedullary, increased mineralization in the curvature area of the diaphysis of one or both legs. One cria suffered a spontaneous fracture in this area. A histological examination, which was carried out only on the second cria, identified a circumscribed osteosclerosis in the affected region. Descriptions of tibial deformities in various animal species and in humans show similarities but do not fully correspond to the changes we observed in the crias. Whether the described congenital deformities are of hereditary origin can only be clarified through further future investigations.

Introduction

In Europe, the keeping of South American Camelids (SACs) has become increasingly popular in the last few decades (1–5), although precise figures on populations and distribution are lacking (6). According to an estimate from 2019, there were approximately 15,000 llamas and alpacas kept in Germany (7). Congenital defects can be observed in alpacas and llamas more frequently than in other animal species (8). Causes for the congenital malformations can be hereditary defects, but also environmental insults (9). Limb deformities in particular are also attributed to malpositioning in utero (10, 11) or can be the result of a complicated assisted birth (12). Mostly, however, the cause is unknown and a hereditary condition is usually suspected (13). The reason for this assumption is the limited genetic diversity due to a relatively limited number of SACs in Europe. Furthermore, based on the results of a survey, Jost et al. suggest that due to breeders’ narrow focus on certain characteristics such as wool quality and coat color they concentrate on certain promising males and their offspring for breeding (13). Artificial insemination is not yet in routine use, meaning that importing frozen semen is not an option for expanding the gene pool (14). As a consequence, inbreeding occurs, and, as a result, hereditary diseases are becoming more prevalent (13). Jost et al. (13) reported congenital musculoskeletal disorders as the most prevalent ones within their studied SACs population including spiral toe growth, hyperextension of the fetlock joint, angular limb deformities and axial rotation of the limbs. In another study, bone deformities accounted for 17% of the malformations observed in SACs (15). As the etiology of most of the congenital disorders in SACs is unknown, evaluation of the prevalence of those abnormalities is recommended (13).

In the following, we describe two cases of congenital tibia deformation in alpaca crias with a locally limited osteosclerotic change in the bone structure. To the authors’ knowledge, such alterations have not been previously described in alpacas.

Case presentations

Two unrelated newborn Huacaya alpaca (Vicugna pacos) crias were admitted to the Clinic of Swine and Small Ruminants, Forensic Veterinary Medicine and Ambulatory Service at the University of Veterinary Medicine Hannover, Germany.

Case 1

An 18-day-old brown, female alpaca cria was presented to the clinic in July 2019, as the owner had observed a lowered back and bent hip immediately after birth, once the cria stood up for the first time. The impaired posture did not hinder the cria from suckling at the dam’s udder. The cria’s birth had been uneventful. It had routinely received 1 mL of a vitamin E/selenium supplement and 1 mL of a vitamin D-containing supplement by injection 1 day after birth. The concentration of the supplements was unknown.

On admission, the cria was bright, alert, and in good body condition and weighed 9.4 kg. The vital parameters including rectal temperature (38.7 °C), pulse (92 beats/min), and respiratory rate (32 breaths/min) were within normal limits. Mucous membranes were pale pink and capillary refill time was below 2 s. When standing, the cria showed a distinct lowering of the hips. Its gait was severely unsteady in both hind limbs. Front and rear limbs showed a distinct valgus position. In addition, an umbilical hernia with a hernia ring of approximately 1.5 cm in diameter and mild brachygnathia superior were also observed.

Blood samples were taken after the general examination from the cria. As the dam was waiting in the trailer whilst the cria was being examined, blood samples were also taken from her for comparison. Blood samples were obtained from the jugular vein (Serum Monovette 9 mL Z; Sarstedt AG & Co. KG, Nümbrecht, Germany). No clinical examination of the dam was carried out. Calcium and phosphorus concentrations were analysed in serum in the in-house laboratory on the same day. Serum samples were sent to an external laboratory (Antech™ Lab Germany GmbH, Viernheim, Germany) for analysis of vitamin D (25-OH) concentration. The values of the dam were within the reference interval, while a mildly reduced phosphorus content was detected in the crias’ blood (Table 1). Based on the reference values given by the external laboratory, also a low vitamin D content was noted. In addition, an antibody test for Schmallenberg infection was performed (ID Screen® Schmallenberg virus Indirect, Innovative Diagnostics, Grabels, France). The result was negative.

A radiograph was taken of the pelvis, and no abnormalities were identified. Whilst the cria was being restrained for further radiographs of the hind legs, it spontaneously lay down in sternal recumbancy. A cracking sound and a short shriek from the cria could be heard. Subsequently, the right hind leg exhibited abnormal mobility of the tibia and the ends of the fractured bone were visible beneath an opening in the skin. The radiograph showed a complete transverse fracture of the right tibial shaft (Figure 1A). The tibia appeared to be bent caudally and the bone ends were irregular and showed a significantly denser bone structure (Figure 1B).

Radiographs of the fractured right tibia (case 1). (A) anterior–posterior view, (B) lateral view: The fracture ends are slightly displaced, their surfaces are irregular and the bone structure appears to be compacted in this area.

After consultation with the owner, the cria was euthanized. Subsequently, the left tibia was also radiographed. The anterior–posterior view showed a valgus deviation of the tibial diaphysis (Figure 2A). In the mid-diaphysis, the lateral view showed a caudal curvature of the tibia with a focal circumferential endosteal and intramedullary mineralized zone (Figure 2B). An increase in cortical density and thickness was noticed on the concave side of the curvature. Due to the curvature, the tibia shaft halves formed an angle of about 110°. The periosteum appeared unchanged. The owner declined a necropsy, so there were no histological reports on the altered bone tissue.

At the center, the cortex seems to be thickened and compacted on the inside of the bend, while the bone tissue appears loosened on the outside.

Case 2

Early September 2025, a-two-day-old white, female alpaca cria weighing 7.0 kg was admitted to the clinic because of lameness of the right hind leg. The owner could feel a marked increase in thickness below the stifle. The cria had been born at a normal gestational period, the birth had been unassisted and unobserved. It had regularly suckled the dam on its own. On presentation, the cria was bright, alert, and responsive, and in good body condition. The clinically assessed parameters were within the reference limits: Heart rate was 56 beats/ min, the mucous membranes were pink, respiratory rate was 32 breaths/min, and rectal temperature was 38.5 °C. A slight downward slope of the pelvis to the right was observed in the standing cria. A prominence of the right tibia, which felt firm, could be seen below the stifle. Manipulation of the leg elicited no signs of discomfort. When comparing the hind legs in an extended position, the right leg appeared to be shortened. When moving, the cria showed a mild mixed lameness in the right hind leg.

As described in case 1, blood samples were taken from the cria and the dam, standing on the trailer whilst the cria was being examined and tests for vitamin D, phosphorus and calcium concentrations were performed. In this case, too, the dam was not examined clinically. The cria’s values were within the reference interval. Based on the reference interval provided by the external laboratory, the dam was found to have a vitamin D deficiency (Table 2). An antibody test for Schmallenberg virus infection was not performed.

Discussion

To the authors’ knowledge, congenital angular deformities of the tibia in alpacas have not been described in the literature to date. Baird et al. described congenital maldevelopment of the tibia in two calves of different breeds (11). In both cases, the deformations were stable and occurred at the mid-diaphysis region of the right tibia. The changes led to a valgus position with an axial deviation of 75 and 45°, respectively. Radiologically, a thick lateral cortex and a radiating trabecular bone pattern were observed in both calves. The authors assumed that the deformities had been caused by bending stress in utero and remodelling of the bone during early gestation (11). A radiograph in a common textbook about camelids shows a fracture in the middle of the tibial diaphysis in a lamoid foetus (16). The author assumed that the cause was a tendon contracture of the gastrocnemius muscle. No deformity was mentioned by the author. In our cases, it could not be determined whether a previous fracture during the fetal period was the cause of the tibial changes in the alpaca crias.

Diaphyseal angular deformities were described in three young horses (12). Two foals, aged eight and 6 weeks, showed varus deformity on the right metacarpus. Radiologically, in the proximal part of the diaphysis near the nutrient foramen the cortical density and thickness were increased on the concave side of the curvature. In the third three-day-old foal, the right metatarsus showed a valgus deviation in the proximal third of the shaft, with radiological findings corresponding to the other two cases (12).

Unfortunately, histological examinations of the altered bone tissue were not performed on either the calves or the foals. Therefore, only the radiological findings can be compared with the tibial changes in the alpaca crias described above. In the foals, the increased bone density was limited to the concave side of the deformation in each case, while in the crias there was a ring-like densification of the bone shaft. According to White (12), a possible cause of the bone deformation in the foals was malnutrition of the cartilaginous bone precursor during development due to vascular aberrations.

Deformities of the tibia also occur in congenital pseudoarthrosis in humans, a rare condition (17) of unknown etiology (18). Here, anterior bowing localized in the middle or distal third of the tibia with spontaneous fractures or after a minor trauma can be observed. The change usually occurs on one side; descriptions of it occurring on both sides are rare (17). A surrounding thickened periosteum and a narrow cuff of fibrous tissue are described at the location. Histologically, the tissue changes were mostly nonspecific, while interosseous neurofibromatosis and fibrous dysplasia were also mentioned (17). Although various classifications of congenital pseudoarthrosis based on morphology exist, Patwa and Patel describe two main types (18): the atrophic type, which is more common, characterized by sclerosis or complete obliteration of the marrow, and the cystic type, which resembles that of fibrous dysplasia, and can also be observed in other bones (18). However, osteosclerotic changes with compact bone tissue, as observed in case 2, were not described in this retrospective study.

Further uncommon bone changes in children and young adults, which mostly involve the tibia are caused by adamantinoma, a rare, slow growing bone cancer, with a typical histological picture consisting of epithelial cell clusters surrounded by spindle-shaped osteofibrous components (19). However, these alterations do not match our findings. Another rare condition in humans that often affects the tibia is osteofibrous dysplasia, a benign, self-limited fibro-osseous lesion (20). The monostotic form of the fibrous dysplasia may also occur in the craniofacial bones, ribs, femurs, and humeri, while the less common polyostotic form may also affect the skull, pelvis, radius and ulna, lumbar spine, clavicle, and cervical spine. The alterations are usually unilateral, rarely bilateral (21), and are characterized by the replacement of normal bone tissue with excessively proliferating cellular fibrous tissue. Osteofibrous dysplasia is caused by a sporadic mutation of the GNAS gene in early embryonic development, which is located on chromosome 20 (22). Radiographically, the lesion is sharply defined and has a radiolucent, “ground-glass” appearance without a visible trabecular pattern. The cortex may be thinned, while the periosteal surface is nonreactive (21, 22). The histological and radiological findings of fibrous dysplasia do not correspond with the observations of tibial changes in the cases presented here.

Another uncommon condition of unknown etiology affecting the diaphysis mainly of the tibia in adult female humans is intramedullary osteosclerosis, which was first described in 1988 (23). It is characterized by cortical thickening and narrowing of the medullary cavity accompanied by local pain (24). Despite similarities in localization and histological appearance, to the authors’ knowledge no deformation of the affected bone is described in this disease.

In case 2, a vitamin D deficiency was diagnosed in the dam according to the reference interval provided by the external laboratory. The vitamin D content in the crias’ blood in case 1 was in the lower reference range, even though the owner had supplemented vitamin D in an unknown dosage 17 days before the blood sample was taken. Age-related criteria were not taken into account in the reference ranges specified by the laboratory. According to Van Saun and Herdt (25), the expected values for crias under 6 months of age are 26–344 nmol/L, and for adult SACs 30–414 nmol/L. When these figures were used for assessment, none of the animals were found to be deficient in vitamin D. Supplementation is only recommended when serum vitamin D concentrations are 30 nmoL/L (12 ng/mL) or lower (25).

Additionally, the phosphorus content in the cria in case 1 was below the reference interval. A lack of vitamin D results, among others, in an increase in parathyroid hormone impairing renal phosphate conservation (26), and in reduced intestinal absorption of phosphorus leading to an increased loss from salivary recycling, which in turn leads to hypophosphatemia (27). Therefore, despite the lack of evidence from the blood test, a previous shortage of vitamin D supply can be assumed.

Rickets is not an uncommon diagnosis in crias, especially during the winter and early spring. Due to their origin in the high altitudes of the Andes with high levels of UVB radiation, llamas and alpacas rely on cutaneous production of vitamin D. When relocated to temperate regions like Central Europe, the animals lack the intense solar radiation (28).

Rickets is characterized by deficient mineralization of newly formed bone osteoid, disruption of endochondral bone formation within the growth plate, and thinning of the cortices. Typical clinical signs of rickets in crias include decreased growth rate during the first 6 months of life, stiff gait, swollen and painful joints, moderate to severe angular deformities and higher risk of long-bone fractures associated with decreased bone density (16, 27, 29, 30). Stieger-Vanegas et al. (31) even described vertebral fractures in two alpaca crias with rickets syndrome.

Typical signs described above were only partially present in both cases due to the angular limb deformity. According to the owner, the dam in case 2 also showed no typical clinical signs of vitamin D deficiency. However, it seems unlikely that vitamin D deficiency causes the congenital changes described here in the tibia of the crias.

In humans, congenital malformations have been associated with vitamin D deficiency during pregnancy. In the context, Mokhtar et al. (32) described congenital heart defects. Adrien et al. (33) associated increased odds of anencephaly, hypospadias, septal defects, diaphragmatic hernia, and gastroschisis. Additionally, Ueda et al. (34) presented a context to long-term changes in immune cell proportions. Nonetheless, typical clinical symptoms of rickets at birth showing abnormal bone calcification only occur when the mother suffered from clinical osteomalacia (35).

Thyroid hormone also has a significant influence on skeletal development due to its impact on calcium metabolism. In particular, hyperthyroidism has been the focus of many studies in humans (36). Maternal hyperthyroidism is associated with congenital hip abnormalities (37) and the absence of knee epiphyses (38) due to the role of the thyroid hormone in regulating the process of endochondral ossification at the growth plate (39). Up to now, there is little information about the effects of thyroid disorders on calcium metabolism in animals (36). The thyroid hormone was not determined in the crias presented; reference values are missing. There is no information available on whether hypothyroidism in dams can lead to the described tibial malformation in crias.

Overall, the cause of the presented cases of tibial deformities in alpaca crias remains unclear.

Conclusion

Numerous congenital malformations observed in SACs have already been reported in the literature. To the authors’ knowledge, the congenital caudal tibial curvatures in two crias described here has not yet been reported. Although various forms of tibial deformities have been mentioned in the literature in both different animal species and humans, the characteristics of these abnormalities do not appear to correspond to our cases. The cause of the abnormality remains unclear; a hereditary condition cannot be ruled out. In general, the observation of congenital malformations of unknown etiology presents challenges for the attending veterinarians. After all, they must answer the owner’s questions concerning the cause of the abnormality and explain its significance for breeding and for the rest of the herd (9), as this affects the treatment of the individual animal as well as other future prophylactic measures in the breeding stock.
