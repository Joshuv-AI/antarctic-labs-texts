Do self-reported and objective hearing measures similarly relate to general and domain-specific cognition? Observational cohort study of older adults in England
Authors: Brendan Q O’Shea, Rebecca A Milan, Alden L Gross, Danielle S Powell, Lindsay C Kobayashi, Andrew Steptoe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWe compared associations between self-reported and HearCheck screening device measures of hearing difficulty with subsequent general and domain-specific cognitive function in a population-based sample of older English adults.DesignObservational cohort study.SettingPopulation-based sample of older adults in wave 7 of the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) in 2014/15 and its Healthy Cognitive Ageing Project (HCAP) in 2018.ParticipantsN=1119 adults aged ≥62 years.Primary outcome measuresFactor scores for general cognitive function and domains of memory, language, orientation and executive function were derived from the HCAP neuropsychological test battery (mean of 0 and SD of 1 for each). Hearing difficulty was assessed using a self-reported 5-point Likert-type scale and the HearCheck screening device, which administered a series of six tones in each ear.ResultsAccording to the HearCheck device, 48% of participants had a mild or moderate-to-severe hearing difficulty, while 25% self-reported fair or poor hearing. In multivariable-adjusted, population-weighted linear regression models, hearing difficulty identified via HearCheck was associated with worse general cognitive function (β=−0.34 SD units; 95% CI −0.60 to –0.07 for moderate-to-severe hearing difficulty vs good hearing) as well as worse function in domains of memory, language and executive function, each with a dose-response relationship. Self-reported hearing difficulty was not associated with general or domain-specific cognitive function.ConclusionsPeripheral hearing ability, as captured by the HearCheck screening device, may have stronger relevance for later-life cognitive outcomes than the broader construct of perceived hearing difficulty in one’s daily environment that is captured by a self-reported measure.

Introduction

The United Nations projects the worldwide population of adults aged ≥65 years will rise from 727 million in the year 2020 to 1.5 billion by 2050.1 Alongside this rapid population ageing, cases of dementia are expected to increase over threefold from 50 million in 2020 to 152 million by 2050,2 and the number of people with hearing impairment is expected to rise from 698 million in 2019 to 2.45 billion by 2050.3 Recent research has prioritised 14 modifiable risk factors that are estimated to account for 45% of all dementia cases: less education, hearing loss, high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, excessive alcohol, social isolation, air pollution and visual loss.2 Among these modifiable risk factors, hearing loss is estimated to have the highest population attributable fraction, accounting for an estimated 7% of global dementia cases.2

A body of research supports that hearing difficulty, as assessed using pure tone audiometry or handheld screening devices to capture peripheral hearing function as well as self-reported measures, is related to a range of later-life cognitive outcomes including poor cognitive performance and risk of dementia.248 Indeed, a recent randomised controlled trial provided evidence suggesting a hearing intervention was effective in reducing cognitive decline among older adults at elevated risk of cognitive decline.9 Whether hearing difficulty is causal in cognitive decline and dementia, or a risk marker, understanding the nature of how different measures of hearing and how they relate to cognition and related attributes can advance intervention efforts.10 11 Hearing difficulty could lead to an impoverished cognitive environment due to a lack of auditory stimulation from speech and language input to the brain, which may change its structure and function and increase the risk of dementia.10 Data from the UK Biobank support this hypothesis, reporting that individuals with hearing difficulty exhibit greater rates of atrophy in auditory and limbic regions of the brain compared with individuals with normal hearing, after adjustment for age, education and other covariates.12 Additionally, individuals with hearing difficulty may require additional cognitive resources to interpret auditory input.10 13 Indeed, prior research using brain mapping has found that individuals with hearing difficulty show reorganised functional gradients compared with those without, and these reorganised gradients are associated with cognitive decline.14 Other mechanistic paths linking hearing loss with risk of dementia include social engagement, depression or a common cause hypothesis.11 15 16

The measurement of hearing difficulty in research studies may impact its observed associations with later-life cognitive outcomes. Large, population-based studies of ageing commonly include brief and inexpensive measures of hearing difficulty, such as self-reports or handheld screening tools. Self-reports are the most used measure in these types of studies, yet their concordance with clinical assessments is variable.1723 In addition to true hearing difficulty, self-reported measures of hearing may also reflect factors influencing a person’s perception of their hearing ability.24 Indeed, nearly one-third of English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA) participants who had hearing difficulty, as identified using a handheld hearing screening device (the HearCheck device), did not self-report their hearing difficulty.17 In that study, predictors of unreported hearing difficulty were older age, female sex, low educational attainment, a routine or manual occupation and lifestyle factors including tobacco consumption, heavy alcohol intake and lack of moderate physical activity.17 Several of these factors are risk factors for dementia,2 and underestimation of self-reported hearing ability measures according to these factors may result in conservatively biased associations with later-life cognitive outcomes.

At the same time, self-reports may more accurately reflect hearing difficulty as experienced in daily life than the measures of peripheral hearing captured by research screening devices or clinical assessments using pure tones.24 25 Hearing function involves the peripheral detection of sound and central processing of stimuli, which cognitively involves the transfer, recognition and interpretation of sound.26 27 Despite the potential for misclassification against objective measures, self-reports of hearing difficulty may better capture the function of this system as experienced in daily life than clinical or screening-based measures of peripheral hearing since they consider the perceived hearing difficulty experience of the individual, which may differ across socioeconomic, lifestyle and environmental factors.24 Regardless of these perceptions, objective measures may better capture hearing difficulty that is subtle in nature, especially at an early stage of onset of hearing difficulty, or not otherwise identified by the person experiencing it. Objective hearing measures may thus provide research studies with more accurate estimates of the relationship between hearing difficulty and subsequent cognitive outcomes. However, the comparative associations of self-reported and objective measures of hearing difficulty with cognitive function in later life remain unknown.

In this study, we aimed to compare the longitudinal associations between each of self-reported and HearCheck measures of hearing ability and subsequent general and domain-specific cognitive function in a population-based sample of older English adults. We conceptualised self-reported hearing difficulty as representing hearing difficulty experienced in daily life, encompassing both the peripheral and central auditory processing systems, and likely capturing a degree of misclassification of true hearing difficulty due to its self-reported nature. We conceptualised HearCheck, a screening measure for objective hearing impairment, to represent peripheral hearing ability. Results from this study may give insight into the implications of hearing measurement methods for the observed association of hearing difficulty and cognitive function in later life.

Materials and methods

English longitudinal study of ageing

Data were from ELSA, a nationally representative longitudinal cohort study of middle-aged and older adults living in private residences in England.28 Hearing was assessed for all consenting participants during the ELSA wave 7 core interview in 2014/15.29 The general and domain-specific cognitive outcomes were measured in the ELSA Healthy Cognitive Ageing Project (HCAP) in 2018, a substudy of ELSA that aims to investigate risk factors for dementia.30 ELSA participants were eligible for ELSA-HCAP if they were aged ≥65 years in January 2018 and completed an ELSA interview at wave 7 or wave 8. As part of the ELSA-HCAP, participants completed a series of cognitive assessments during in-person interviews with trained interviewers.30 Reporting of the present study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.31

In the present study, we used data on hearing difficulty and sociodemographic covariates in the ELSA wave 7 core interview and data on health-related covariates from Harmonised ELSA data constructed by the Gateway to Global Ageing Data.32 Cognitive testing for the HCAP was conducted while people used hearing aids if they were required. Hearing aids were removed for the HearCheck, but not otherwise. Participants who were eligible for the present study were those who completed the wave 7 core and the ELSA-HCAP interviews (n=1243), had complete hearing data (n=1165/1243; 94%) and complete covariate data (n=1119/1165; 96%), for a total analytical sample of 1119. The prevalence and patterns of missing observations by variable are shown in online supplemental appendix tables 1 and 2, respectively.

Exposure: hearing difficulty

Self-reported hearing difficulty was measured in the wave 7 core interview via self-report on a 5-point Likert-type scale that asked participants, “Is your hearing (using a hearing aid if you use one) excellent, very good, good, fair or poor?”29 We classified self-reported hearing difficulty as a continuous variable ranging from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) and as a binary variable dichotomised at poor/fair versus good/very good/excellent. The Siemens HearCheck screening device measures peripheral hearing acuity through a set of tones. On the removal of hearing aids, if wearing any, the participants were administered a series of three 1 kHz tones (55, 35 and 20 dBHL) followed by three 3 kHz tones (75, 55 and 35 dBHL), for a total of six tones. As has been described previously in ELSA, hearing difficulty was classified in each ear as ‘good hearing’ if the participant heard all six tones, ‘mild hearing difficulty’ if the participant heard three to five tones and ‘moderate-to-severe hearing difficulty’ if the participant heard zero to two tones.29 We classified hearing difficulty according to the HearCheck screening device as a categorical variable reflecting the categories described above, using hearing function in the better ear.

Outcome: general and domain-specific cognitive performance

Cognitive performance was assessed in the in-person ELSA-HCAP interview with a series of cognitive tests by trained interviewers, performed 3–4 years following the hearing assessments in the ELSA wave 7 core interview.30 We derived factor scores reflecting general cognitive performance, as well as performance in specific domains of orientation, language, executive function and memory. Informed by the neuropsychological theory underlying the development of the HCAP and its selection of test items, we used factor analysis to derive the general and domain-specific factor scores using an item banking approach.33 Online supplemental appendix table 3 lists the individual cognitive test items included in each cognitive domain in the ELSA-HCAP. Further detail on the interview administration of these tests as well as details on our statistical methodology to derive factor scores are described elsewhere.30 33 All cognitive factor scores were each standardised to have a mean of 0 and SD of 1.

Covariates

Model covariates were assessed in the ELSA wave 7 core interview and chosen to be: age (continuous; collapsed at ≥90 to avoid release of personal information due to small numbers), sex (male; female), ethnicity (white; non-white), highest educational attainment (no qualification; national vocational qualification level 1/certificate of secondary education other grade-equivalent; national vocational qualification level 2/general certificate of education ordinary-level equivalent; national vocational qualification level 3/general certificate of education advanced-level equivalent; higher education below degree; national vocational qualification level 4/national vocational qualification level 5/degree or equivalent; foreign/other, do not know, refused), marital status (married or in a same-sex civil partnership; single, never married; divorced or separated; widowed), quintiles of net non-pension household wealth (continuous), total number of doctor diagnosed chronic health conditions (continuous; calculated from self-reports of high blood pressure, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart problems, emotional, nervous or psychiatric problems, arthritis, asthma, high cholesterol, cataracts, Parkinson’s disease, angina, abnormal heart rhythm and osteoporosis), presence of a long-standing, limiting illness (yes; no) and smoking status (never smoked; former smoker; current smoker).

Additional covariates that were used to describe the sample and in sensitivity analyses were also assessed at ELSA wave 7: self-reported hearing aid use (do not know or not applicable; yes, most of the time; yes, some of the time; no), and number of depressive symptoms according to the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (continuous, range 0–8).

Statistical analysis

We described the sample characteristics according to the mean and SD for continuous variables and frequencies for categorical variables. We calculated a Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient and a Bland-Altman plot to evaluate the agreement between self-reported and HearCheck measures of hearing difficulty.34 The Bland-Altman plot was adjusted for the linear relationship between the paired difference and paired average of these measures. We used multivariable adjusted linear regression models to estimate the relationships between each of self-reported and HearCheck measures of hearing and general and domain-specific cognitive scores as separate outcomes, adjusted for the covariates listed above. We applied ELSA-HCAP sampling weights to all models, which were calculated as the inverse of the probability of selection into the ELSA-HCAP multiplied by the cross-sectional weight from the last core ELSA wave in which the participant took part.30 This sampling weight accounted for selection probabilities including non-response into both the ELSA-HCAP and the core ELSA, ensuring that the sample was demographically representative of adults aged ≥65 years living in private households in England. Details on the construction of the ELSA-HCAP weights are available elsewhere.30 All analyses were conducted using Stata/SE, V.19.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted a sensitivity analysis where we re-ran the main analysis, but with additional adjustment for number of depressive symptoms according to the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale at ELSA wave 7, as the role of depression in the relationship between hearing difficulty and cognitive function is unclear. Depression may precede or follow the onset of hearing difficulty yet was measured simultaneously with hearing difficulty in this analysis.35 To evaluate the robustness of our results to missing data, we re-estimated our main analysis with missing observations for HearCheck hearing acuity filled in using multiple imputation by chained equations for ordinal data, with the covariates described above in the imputation model. A total of 20 imputed datasets were generated with a total of 71/78 missing HearCheck observations imputed due to some missingness in covariates.

Patient and public involvement

Participants from this observational cohort study were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of the present study.

Results

The mean age at baseline in 2014/15 was 73.1 (SD 7.2) years, 55% of the sample was female (619/1119) and 97% identified as white (1083/1119; table 1). While only 25% of the sample reported poor or fair hearing at baseline (283/1119), 41% (456/1119) had a mild hearing difficulty and 7% (82/1119) had a moderate-to-severe hearing difficulty in their better ear, according to the HearCheck screening device (table 1). Of these 82 individuals, only 13 reported that they did not wear hearing aids. We observed a strong, positive correlation between self-reported (continuous) and HearCheck measures of hearing difficulty (Spearman’s rho=0.42, p<0.0001). The linear relationship between the paired difference and paired average of the self-reported and HearCheck measures of hearing β=0.70 (95% CI 0.63 to 0.77) indicated that disagreement between the measures was not constant along their measurement ranges. The Bland-Altman plot adjusted for this linear relationship showed good agreement between the measures, with just 39/1119 (3.5%) falling outside the limits of agreement (figure 1).

Discussion

In this longitudinal, population-representative study of older English adults, hearing difficulty as identified by the HearCheck screening device was associated with lower subsequent general cognitive performance, as well as performance in specific domains of memory, language and executive function, all with dose-response relationships. This association was the strongest for memory function. In contrast, self-reported hearing difficulty was not associated with subsequent general or domain-specific cognitive scores. These results indicate that peripheral hearing ability, as captured by the HearCheck device, is more strongly associated with later-life cognitive outcomes than the broader construct of perceived hearing difficulty in one’s daily environment that is captured by a self-reported measure.

Objective hearing impairment and self-reported hearing difficulty may be related but distinct attributes. The choice of which to measure or focus on may depend on one’s research question or purpose.15 For example, each measure may have different utility across levels of prevention. Objective measures of hearing impairment may have more utility for large-scale prevention interventions, such as a hearing aid intervention, because they may be more sensitive among those with milder hearing loss who may not yet perceive difficulty in hearing. In contrast, self-reported hearing difficulty may be appropriate for indicated treatment efforts, given that people have recognised a problem with their hearing in daily life; this measure could differ across socioeconomic, lifestyle and environmental factors, and those differences should be expected.

Our results align with those of previous observational studies finding audiometric measures of hearing difficulty to be associated with cognitive outcomes.7 12 14 36 37 One mechanism that could explain this association is that the memory assessments in the ELSA-HCAP largely relied on recalling auditory stimuli provided by the study interviewer, and individuals with uncorrected hearing impairment may have performed disproportionately worse on these assessments. Future studies should evaluate the potential for differential item functioning of HCAP cognitive assessments that rely on auditory stimuli according to level of hearing difficulty.38 39

Our null findings for self-reported hearing conflict with previous studies that have found worse self-reported hearing to be associated with worse cognitive outcomes among older adults.44044 Notably, a prior study using longitudinal data from the ELSA and the US Health and Retirement Study found individuals with consistent self-reported suboptimal hearing throughout the study as well as individuals with self-reported hearing difficulty that deteriorated over time had significantly worse episodic memory scores than those reporting very good hearing throughout the follow-up period.43 Another nationally representative sample of older Korean adults also observed self-reported hearing difficulty to be associated with cognitive decline over time, although they used different measurement tools for both self-reported hearing and cognitive outcomes than those used in the ELSA-HCAP, which may have contributed to the inconsistent results.44 Self-reported hearing is thought to provide a more realistic depiction of hearing in one’s daily life by considering the processing of both direct and background noise, the impact of hearing aids on function and the experience of both peripheral and central auditory processing.17 However, our results indicate measured peripheral hearing function without the assistance of hearing aids may be more relevant for subsequent cognitive functioning than the reported experience of daily hearing function. A strength of our study relative to these prior studies is our more comprehensive measurement of the cognitive function outcomes and use of factor scores to represent these outcomes in ELSA’s HCAP. This approach gave us enhanced precision with which to identify general and domain-specific cognitive function and may have reduced measurement error in our outcome variables relative to other studies. An alternative explanation is that ELSA participants with worse cognitive function may have differentially under-reported poor hearing, which could occur due to the stigma commonly felt by those with hearing difficulty45 and would bias our results towards the null. However, there is no reason to believe this potential pattern of reporting bias would differ in ELSA relative to other studies. Simulation studies that can model bias may be helpful and a novel future direction in this area to help disentangle reporting bias from the aspects of hearing function represented by self-reported hearing.

This study has limitations. The self-reported hearing assessment used a single question that may not sufficiently measure true hearing difficulty.17 Although the Siemens HearCheck screening device may more accurately assess peripheral hearing function than a self-report, it is not a gold standard diagnostic assessment. Both self-reported and HearCheck measures of hearing difficulty are likely to have imperfect validity against a gold standard pure tone audiometry assessment. The HearCheck screening device assesses hearing acuity at 1 kHz tones of 55 dBHL, 35 dBHL, 20 dBHL, followed by 3 kHz tones of 75 dBHL, 55 dBHL, 35 dBHL. While hearing difficulty at all these levels would indicate hearing loss sufficient to negatively impact overall communication that would likely benefit from a hearing aid or hearing management strategies, it may not identify all clinically meaningful cases of hearing loss. Future studies should assess higher frequencies during hearing assessment, which are important for speech perception and often lost during aging-related hearing loss. Future studies should also further explore the convergence of self-reported and HearCheck measures and their construct validity, as well as their comparative relationships with other health outcomes.

Furthermore, the cognitive tests in the HCAP protocol were administered verbally in the face-to-face study interview. Although participants wore hearing aids during the HCAP interview if they usually wore them, those whose hearing loss precluded cognitive testing may have been excluded. It is also possible that cognitive test scores were impacted by unsupported hearing loss, although only 13 participants with moderate-to-severe hearing difficulty according to the HearCheck device reported not wearing hearing aids. There is an opportunity for cognitive ageing studies in the future to develop and implement cognitive tests that do not rely on auditory stimuli or use auditory amplification to improve accessibility among older adults with hearing loss.46 47 There are some precedents in the literature on these approaches, including approaches used to assess cognitive function and screen for dementia among deaf older adults.4850 Improving the accessibility of cognitive testing for older adults with hearing loss in research studies may also improve data quality and reduce missing data.

If ELSA participants who had poor hearing function in wave 7 were less likely to participate in the first wave of the ELSA-HCAP substudy or drop out of ELSA if sampled, or die prior to having the opportunity to be sampled for ELSA-HCAP, and if these individuals also had worse cognitive function than those who remained in the study and participated in ELSA-HCAP, then our results could be biased towards the null. However, the use of ELSA-HCAP sampling weights helps to correct for this non-response. Although hearing difficulty has previously been associated with missing cognitive data,39 51 we had complete data on cognitive factor scores in our sample and do not expect that differential missingness of cognitive data would impact our results.

This study has several strengths. We used a large, longitudinal study with sampling weights to ensure our sample was representative of the general older population of England. Our use of the HCAP cognitive measures allowed us to newly examine the relationships between hearing difficulty and performance in specific domains of cognition according to a robust battery of validated cognitive measures standardised to have a mean of 0 and SD of 1. There were low rates of missing data on cognitive outcomes and covariates. We also controlled our statistical models for numerous potential influences on hearing difficulty and cognitive function to reduce the likelihood of confounding bias.

This population-representative study of older adults in England demonstrates the differential relationships of self-reported hearing difficulty and the HearCheck screening device in relation to general and domain-specific cognitive performance. Although self-reported hearing difficulty was not associated with cognitive performance, we did observe that the objective HearCheck measure was prospectively associated with general cognition and performance in domains of memory, language and executive function, all in a dose-response fashion. After sensitivity analysis, these relationships between the HearCheck measure of hearing difficulty and general cognitive performance, memory, language and executive function remained significant. Results are supportive of a relationship between later-life peripheral hearing and subsequent cognitive health and should be confirmed in other populations and settings using high-quality measures of hearing difficulty.
