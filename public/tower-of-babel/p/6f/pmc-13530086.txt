Computational identification of antigens and development of a multi-epitope vaccine for bacterial vaginosis caused by Gardnerella vaginalis
Authors: Mohammad Amin Niknezhad, Abbas Doosti, Mostafa Shakhsi-Niaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial vaginosis (BV), a prevalent form of vaginal dysbiosis, is primarily caused by Gardnerella vaginalis. The increasing prevalence of BV has raised global health concerns, prompting the need for a vaccine that can enhance human immunity and mitigate BV transmission. The study developed a multi-epitope vaccine for BV infection using immunoinformatic methodologies. B-cell and T-cell epitopes were identified using the IEDB recommended server and NetCTL. Specifically, HTL epitopes were predicted against HLA-DR alleles (including DRB101:01, DRB103:05, and DRB104:04), while CTL epitopes were predicted against HLA class I supertypes (including HLA-A02:01 and HLA-A*01:01). The selected epitopes were fused using adjuvants and linkers. The peptide sequence MSPSVRHSPSVRH, derived from the heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, was incorporated as an adjuvant to activate innate immunity via TLR2/4 and enhance dendritic cell maturation. The B-cell and (CTL or HTL) epitopes were connected using GGGS linkers, whereas the CTL + HTL epitopes were connected using HEYGAEALERAG linkers. Additional epitopes were chosen based on antigenicity, allergenicity, and immunological features. TLR2 recognizes bacterial lipoproteins and peptidoglycan. TLR2 and TLR-4 showed robust interactions in molecular docking. The results of the present study demonstrate that the produced vaccine displayed stability, shown by a molecular weight of 49924.79 Da and an antigenicity value of 1.37. The Vaccine Construct demonstrated stability and basicity, shown by an instability score of 32.65 and a projected isoelectric point (pI) of 5.09. The expected secondary structure of the vaccine construct consisted of 94.57% random coil, and 5.43% extended strand. The suggested vaccine demonstrated efficient binding to its TLR2 and TLR4 receptors, yielding the maximum Van der Waals energy of (-97.2 +/- 5.9) and (-67.6 +/- 7.3) kcal/mol, respectively. The polypeptide vaccine’s stability and compactness were assessed using molecular dynamics simulations. Introduction

Bacterial vaginosis (BV), the most common vaginal infection, affects around 30% of women in the United States and poses a significant global health burden (Abou Chacra et al. 2022). The exact etiology is complex, but the condition is characterized by the depletion of protective Lactobacillus spp. and a subsequent overgrowth of facultative and strictly anaerobic bacteria (Elnaggar et al. 2023). This dysbiosis leads to a transition to a more alkaline vaginal milieu and triggers a modified immune response. While the condition involves the activation of innate immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells (predominantly mediated via Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on vaginal epithelial cells), it often exhibits a paradoxical lack of pronounced inflammation and minimal involvement of B and T cells, complicating natural clearance (Fernández-Calvet et al. 2018).

The clinical impact of BV is substantial, with current standard treatments relying on oral and intravaginal antibiotics such as metronidazole and clindamycin. Although these regimens show an initial effectiveness rate of 80%, over 60% of women experience recurrent episodes (Elnaggar et al. 2023). This high recurrence rate is largely attributed to the formation of a polymicrobial biofilm on the vaginal epithelium. Gardnerella vaginalis (G. vaginalis) plays a pivotal role in this process; it acts as a “keystone” pathogen that adheres to epithelial cells, displaces Lactobacillus spp., and facilitates the adherence and proliferation of other harmful anaerobes like Prevotella bivia within the biofilm matrix (Castro et al. 2021). Furthermore, the efficacy of antibiotic treatment is increasingly threatened by resistance. G. vaginalis has demonstrated resistance mechanisms, including mutations in the 23 S rRNA gene, with prevalence rates as high as 90% in certain regions like Asia (Uddin et al. 2021; Zare-Zardini et al. 2020). Consequently, there is an urgent need to enforce restrictions on antibiotic consumption and advance research into innovative preventative strategies.

Vaccination represents a promising alternative to antibiotics, having proven remarkably effective in controlling numerous infectious diseases and saving millions of lives annually (Cohen et al. 2020; Piri-Gharaghie et al. 2024). However, traditional vaccine development for BV faces obstacles, including the difficulty of targeting polymicrobial biofilms and the safety concerns associated with whole-pathogen approaches. In this context, reverse vaccinology has emerged as a powerful solution. Gaining prominence since the sequencing of the H. influenzae genome in 1995, this bioinformatics technique allows for the comprehensive analysis of all bacterial proteins to identify potential antigens without the need to culture the pathogen (Rappuoli et al. 2016; Kaveh-Samani et al. 2024). It enables the precise screening of targets that can trigger adaptive immune responses, ensuring high specificity and safety.

The application of reverse vaccinology, coupled with advanced immunoinformatics, has revolutionized vaccine discovery, particularly for complex mucosal pathogens and sexually transmitted infections (STIs) (Kulikowski and Pandemics 2021; Gharaghie and Hajimohammadi 2021). Unlike conventional methods, immunoinformatics facilitates the high-throughput screening of epitopes, specific regions of antigens that interact with immune receptors, allowing researchers to predict antigenicity, allergenicity, and population coverage with high precision before experimental validation (Gupta and Pellett 2023). This approach has been successfully utilized to design multi-epitope vaccine candidates against challenging STI pathogens, such as Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and human papillomavirus (HPV), demonstrating significant potential in eliciting robust mucosal and systemic immunity (Sethi et al. 2024). Furthermore, in the context of the vaginal microbiome, reverse vaccinology offers a strategic advantage by enabling the identification of conserved antigens across pathogenic strains while avoiding cross-reactivity with beneficial commensal lactobacilli, a critical requirement for maintaining vaginal homeostasis (Cortés-Sarabia et al. 2020). Recent studies have also highlighted the utility of these computational tools in predicting B-cell and T-cell epitopes that can penetrate biofilm matrices, offering a targeted strategy to disrupt the persistent biofilms characteristic of BV (Kumari et al. 2024).

Central to this computational paradigm is the development of multi-epitope vaccines, a strategy designed to overcome the limitations of conventional whole-organism or single-antigen vaccines (Motta et al. 2018). The fundamental principle of multi-epitope vaccines involves the precise selection and concatenation of immunodominant regions, specifically cytotoxic T-lymphocyte (CTL), helper T-lymphocyte (HTL), and B-cell epitopes, that are capable of eliciting a comprehensive and specific immune response (Chauhan and Khasa 2023). Unlike whole proteins, which may contain non-protective or immunosuppressive domains, these peptide-based constructs focus the immune system exclusively on conserved, vulnerable targets, thereby minimizing the risk of allergenicity and autoimmune reactions (Basmenj et al. 2025).

To ensure immunogenicity, these epitopes are typically fused with specific adjuvants—such as Heat Shock Proteins (HSP60) or Toll-like receptor agonists—via specialized peptide linkers (Mamun et al. 2025). Adjuvants serve as critical immunostimulatory components that enhance the innate immune response and promote long-term memory by activating antigen-presenting cells (APCs) (Mamun et al. 2025). Meanwhile, the choice of linkers is structurally significant; flexible linkers (e.g., GGGGS) are often employed to maintain the independence and proper folding of adjacent epitopes, while rigid linkers (e.g., EAAAK) help separate functional domains and stabilize the overall 3D structure of the vaccine construct (Mamun et al. 2025). This modular architecture not only improves the bioavailability and stability of the vaccine but also facilitates simultaneous stimulation of both humoral (antibody-mediated) and cell-mediated immunity, which is essential for clearing intracellular pathogens and complex biofilms (Mamun et al. 2025; Farhangfar et al. 2022).

Despite these advancements, previous studies have largely focused on whole-protein antigens, which may carry non-protective regions or pose risks of reactogenicity (2025Chiang et al. 2015). Moreover, many existing in silico designs lack comprehensive validation of stability or fail to account for the specific mucosal immune environment required for BV protection (Bukhari ). Therefore, this study aims to leverage a reverse vaccinology approach to design a multi-epitope vaccine against G. vaginalis. By focusing on specific immunogenic epitopes and incorporating rigorous structural validation, we aim to develop a prophylactic solution that overcomes the limitations of antibiotic resistance and provides robust, long-term immunity against BV.

Previous studies have explored vaccine candidates against Gardnerella vaginalis, primarily focusing on whole-protein antigens such as vaginolysin. However, these approaches often face safety concerns regarding reactogenicity and the potential induction of autoimmune responses due to the presence of non-protective regions within the large proteins. Furthermore, developing whole-protein vaccines is often complex and expensive, limiting their feasibility for widespread application. In contrast, our study utilizes a reverse vaccinology approach to select specific immunogenic epitopes, thereby minimizing the risk of adverse reactions and focusing the immune response solely on protective regions (Alharbi 2020).

Another line of research has involved in silico designs of multi-epitope vaccines for G. vaginalis, yet many of these constructs lack comprehensive validation of their three-dimensional stability. Several prior models were constructed without rigorous molecular dynamics simulations or sufficient immunoinformatic analyses regarding population coverage and allergenicity. To address this gap, our study incorporates extensive structural validation, including 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations, to ensure the designed vaccine maintains a stable conformation and effectively binds to immune receptors (Bukhari 2021).

Additionally, earlier computational designs have often failed to account for the complex polymicrobial nature of Bacterial Vaginosis (BV) or the specific mucosal immune environment required for protection. Many proposed constructs did not include appropriate adjuvants or linkers necessary for stimulating robust mucosal and systemic immunity. Our study addresses these limitations by integrating a potent HSP60 adjuvant and optimized linkers to enhance immunogenicity, while specifically targeting epitopes with high affinity for both B and T cells, aiming to provide a more comprehensive and effective prophylactic solution (Busch 2012).

This study aimed to use bioinformatics methods to anticipate therapeutic and vaccination targets against Gardnerella vaginalis, the causative agent of BV infection, using the genomes of this bacterium available in GenBank. This work will enable future laboratory and animal studies to develop medications and preventive measures against Gardnerella vaginalis, a very clinically relevant species for women.

Materials and methods

Antigen retrieval and identification

Genome retrieval and proteome extraction

The complete and draft genomes of Gardnerella vaginalis isolates were retrieved from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) GenBank database. In total, 150 genomes were downloaded, of which 22 were complete and 128 were draft (high-quality) genomes. While complete genomes provide an uninterrupted genetic blueprint, we included high-quality draft genomes to significantly broaden the strain coverage and capture the extensive pangenomic diversity of G. vaginalis. This approach is critical for identifying conserved core proteins across different clades, ensuring that vaccine targets are not exclusive to a limited number of sequenced strains. To mitigate the risk of gaps in fundamental vaccine candidates, we applied a strict pangenome analysis using OrthoFinder, which identifies “core genes” present in the vast majority of isolates. This ensures that even if a specific draft genome has a local assembly gap, the target protein is reliably represented and conserved across the broader population.

Both complete and partial genome sequences were converted into FASTA format for downstream analysis. To ensure data integrity, the proteome of G. vaginalis was cross-referenced and verified using the UniProt database. Amino acid sequences documented in NCBI were extracted for subsequent screening steps. To identify essential genes crucial for bacterial survival, the proteome was screened against the Database of Essential Genes (DEG) [Citation]. Proteins with a sequence identity threshold of ≥ 30% and an E-value of < 1e-5 were considered essential and retained. This filtering process reduced the initial dataset from 340 core genes to 85 potential targets. After downloading the genome sequences, we followed the standard multi-epitope vaccine design pipeline described by (Bukhari 2025, Irankunda et al. 2024, Bukhari 2025), performing all recommended analyses, including signal peptide and subcellular localization checks, to systematically identify the best antigens and design the vaccine.

Assessment of pathogenicity and antigenicity

To identify potential vaccine candidates, the proteome was screened for virulence factors using the VirulentPred tool, which employs a cascade Support Vector Machine (SVM) algorithm. Proteins with a prediction score of ≥ 0.5 were considered virulent and selected for further analysis. Subsequently, the antigenicity of these proteins was evaluated using the VaxiJen server. This tool predicts protective antigens based on physicochemical properties without relying on sequence alignment. Proteins with an antigenicity score ≥ 0.5 were retained as putative protective antigens. All analyses were performed using the default parameters of the respective servers.

Evaluation of physicochemical properties

The physicochemical properties of the selected proteins, including flexibility and hydrophilicity, were analyzed using tools available at the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) analysis resource. These properties are essential for understanding the potential behavior of the antigens within a biological system.

Human non-homology and solubility prediction

To ensure safety and prevent autoimmune reactions, the target proteins were screened against the human proteome using Protein BLAST (BLASTp) to exclude sequences with significant homology. Furthermore, the solubility of the proteins was assessed using the SOLpro server, which utilizes a two-step SVM technique to predict the solubility of overexpressed proteins. This step is crucial, as insolubility can compromise the efficacy of immunization efforts.

Secondary structure and MHC binding affinity

The secondary structural elements (helix, sheet, turn, and coil) of the target proteins were predicted using the Self-Optimized Prediction Method with Alignment (SOPMA) under default settings. Additionally, the density of Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) class I and class II ligands was evaluated using VaxiTop (P-value ≤ 0.01). Proteins demonstrating a high binding affinity, defined by a ratio exceeding the 95th percentile (or rank < 2%), were prioritized for vaccine construction.

3D homology modelling and validation

The three-dimensional structures of the final target proteins were constructed using the homology modeling program Swiss-model. To minimize structural distortions, the predicted models were refined using the Galaxy Refine server. The quality and stereochemical validation of the refined structures were assessed using Ramachandran plot analysis provided by the Swiss-model server.

Prediction of epitopes

To ensure robustness and minimize false positives, a consensus-based approach was employed to resolve discrepancies between different prediction algorithms. For B-cell epitopes, both continuous (linear) and discontinuous (conformational) epitopes were predicted. Continuous epitopes were identified using the BCpred 2.0 server with a minimum score threshold of 0.8. Discontinuous epitopes were predicted using the Ellipro server, configured with a minimum score of 0.8 and a maximum distance of 6 Å. Epitopes predicted by both tools or those with the highest confidence scores from either tool were selected for further analysis to ensure comprehensive coverage of B-cell targets. It has also been shown that G. vaginalis internalizes itself into vaginal epithelial cells, most likely as a result of cytoskeletal protein alterations (Bukhari 2025). However, in women with BV, G. vaginalis is mainly found as a structured polymicrobial biofilm attached to the vaginal epithelial surface. So, both the humoral and cellular immune systems need to be engaged in order to produce protection against this bacterium. Consequently, we must look into the B and T cell epitopes. B-cell epitopes are brief amino acid sequences identified by B-lymphocyte surface receptors. Identification is crucial in vaccine development. Thus, two kinds of B-cell epitopes, continuous and discontinuous, were forecasted throughout the structural protein sequences. B-cell epitopes on protein surfaces were identified utilizing the BCpred 2.0 server for continuous epitopes and the Ellipro server for discontinuous epitopes of significant size. The prediction parameters were configured with a minimum score of 0.8 and a Maximum distance of 6 Angstrom. Various CTL epitopes (9aa) recognized by HLA class-I supertypes were predicted using NetCTL. The server considers the default settings for weight on C-terminal cleavage (0.15), weight on TAP transport efficiency (0.05), and the threshold for epitope identification (0.75). Additionally, a different group of CTL epitopes, acknowledged by HLA A1 supertype were identified using the consensus approach of the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) program. The epitopes that matched the criteria of a consensus score of < 2 and were predicted by several alleles using both techniques were deemed strong binders and chosen for further research. Predicted HTL epitopes recognized by HLA Class II DRB1 alleles were identified using the Net MHC II pan 3.2 servers. The default thresholds for strong binders and weak binders were established. A new group of HTL epitopes, each consisting of 15 residues recognized by HLA DR alleles (DRB1_0101, DRB1_0103, DRB1_0105, DRB1_0124, DRB1_0132, DRB1_0305, DRB1_0308, DRB1_0314, DRB1_0324, DRB1_0326, DRB1_0335, DRB1_0339, DRB1_0348, DRB1_0350, DRB1_0404), were discovered using the consensus technique on the IEDB website. Epitopes ranking < 2 percentile and predicted by multiple alleles in both approaches were classified as strong binders and examined for additional investigation.

Screening of overlapping epitopes

We examined overlapping epitopes affixed to HLA class I and class II alleles to assess their immunogenicity and predict allergens. The immunogenicity of the peptides was predicted using the IEDB MHC-I/II binding prediction tools. Peptides with a percentile rank < 2% (for strong binders) or < 10% (for weak binders) were considered potential epitopes. Furthermore, the allergenicity of the selected epitopes was assessed using the AllerTop v2.0 tool, where only peptides predicted as non-allergens were retained. Toxicity was also evaluated using ToxinPred, and only Non-Toxic peptides were included in the final construct. The immunogenic threshold for VaxiJen was set at 0.4 (or 0.5, depending on what you used).

Conservation assessment

The T-cell and B-cell epitopes were analyzed using the IEDB conservancy tool to determine the level of conservancy in the structural protein sequences. Epitopes showing 100% conservancy were chosen for further study.

Population coverage and autoimmune detection

The IEDB population coverage analysis technique was used to estimate the immunogenic response of the human population to certain overlapping CTL and HTL epitopes based on their corresponding HLA genotype frequencies. We examined the epitopes that covered 50% or more of the population. To decrease the likelihood of autoimmunity, all chosen epitopes underwent Blast-p search screening versus the Human proteome. Epitopes exhibiting a resemblance to any human molecule were excluded from further research.

Examination of interactions between HLA alleles and epitopes

The examined epitope sequence was uploaded to the PEPFOLD 3 web server for 3D structural modelling. With PDB IDs of 2G9H and 1QEW, the structures of the two most prevalent HLA alleles in the human population, HLA-DRB1 *01:01 (HLA class II) and HLA-A* 02:01 (HLA class I), were obtained. Energy minimization was done after removing any ligand linked to the HLA allele’s structure. Next, to examine the pattern of receptor-ligand interaction, the modelled epitopes were docked with the relevant HLA allele using the web service Patchdock (https://bio.tools/patchdock). The complex type and clustering RMSD value were left at 0.4 by default for the parameters. The Firedock server received the result from Patchdock, so the top 10 models may be improved. The complex with the lowest global energy value was examined based on global energy value, and the associated CTL and HTL epitopes were chosen for the final Vaccine Construct [41–44].

Vaccine construction

Constructing and ensuring quality control of the completed multi-epitope vaccination

The G. vaginalis epitopes were selected based on specific criteria. Epitopes must be immunogenic, non-allergenic, and have overlapping affinity for HLA class I and II alleles. They should activate CTL and HTL cells and cover at least 50% of the population. Epitopes must not overlap with any human gene, and predicted B-cell epitopes should be on the target protein’s surface. The HTL and CTL epitopes were chosen for the multi-epitope vaccination based on satisfying these requirements.

The B-cell and (CTL or HTL) epitopes were connected using GGGS linkers. These flexible linkers were utilized to maintain the independent function and conformational stability of the epitopes, preventing interference between adjacent domains [41, 42]. Furthermore, the CTL and HTL epitopes were connected using HEYGAEALERAG linkers. This specific sequence is a helper T-lymphocyte epitope (derived from sequences such as the measles virus fusion protein or similar promiscuous epitopes) that functions as an immunostimulatory linker. It enhances the overall immunogenicity of the construct by providing additional T-cell help and improving the processing and presentation of the adjacent epitopes. Finally, the peptide sequence MSPSVRHSPSVRH, derived from the heat shock protein 60 (HSP60) of Mycobacterium tuberculosis, was included at the N-terminal of the construct as an adjuvant. HSP60 is a potent immunostimulatory agent known to activate the innate immune system by interacting with antigen-presenting cells (APCs) via Toll-like receptors (TLRs), such as TLR4. This interaction triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and enhances the maturation and migration of dendritic cells. While M. tuberculosis is a pathogen, its HSP60 has been extensively validated as a safe and potent pan-immunostimulatory adjuvant in computational vaccine design against various pathogens, including sexually transmitted infections and bacterial vaginosis. Consequently, the inclusion of this adjuvant significantly boosts the overall immunogenicity of the vaccine construct by promoting a robust and specific adaptive immune response.

The physicochemical characteristics, allergenicity, and antigenic properties of the vaccine

The VaxiJen v2.0 server was used to forecast the proposed vaccine’s antigenicity, while the Allertop v.2.0 prediction tool was used to predict its allergenicity. The final sequence of the vaccine was sent to the Expasy ProtPram online server to forecast its physicochemical properties.

Secondary structure analysis

The SOPMA method was utilized to predict the secondary components of the vaccine construct. The vaccine sequence was used as input, and the output width was set to 70. The parameters defined were the number of conformational states (Helix, Sheet, Turn, Coil) as 4, the similarity threshold as 8, and the window width as 17.

Modelling, improving, and validating the vaccination concept

The final version of the vaccination model was created via the homology modelling application SPARKS-X62. The model with the best Z-score among the top 10 projected models was chosen for refinement utilizing the Galaxy refine service. We next used the ProSA-webtool to evaluate the projected model’s overall and local quality using the Z-score. When the Z-score falls beyond the range of natural proteins of comparable size, there is an increased likelihood of mistakes in the projected structure. Ramachandran plot study was conducted utilizing the RAMPAGE server to assess the overall quality of the improved vaccine structure. The vaccine construct’s final model used the Chimaera 1.10.1 visualization system [41–44].

Prediction of continuous and discontinuous epitopes

The B-cell epitopes in the Vaccine Construct were predicted utilizing BCpred 2.0 for continuous epitopes and Ellipro server for discontinuous epitopes. To build a successful vaccine, it should have B-cell epitopes to improve immunogenic responses.

TLR2 and TLR4 receptor molecular docking of the vaccine construct

TLR4 was selected because it recognizes lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria and lipoproteins from Gram-positive bacteria like G. vaginalis, initiating the primary innate immune defense. TLR2 recognizes bacterial lipoproteins.

TLR2 and TLR4 Receptor molecular docking of the vaccine construct

The 3D structures of human TLR2 and TLR4 were obtained from the Swiss-model repository. Any ligand bound to the recovered structures was eliminated, and the receptors underwent model refinement using the Galaxy Refine server. The interaction pattern of the final vaccine with TLR2 and TLR4 was analyzed using molecular docking via the ClusPro v.2 protein-protein docking server. This service calculates cluster scores using rigid-body docking by sampling billions of conformations and performing pairwise RMSD energy reduction. The final vaccine, TLR2 complex, and TLR4 complex models were selected and visualized using the Chimera 1.10.1 system based on their low energy weight scores and high cluster populations [41–44]. To further validate the binding affinity, the docking procedure was repeated using the HawkDock and HADDOCK web servers. Subsequently, the Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) value was calculated using these servers to predict the binding free energy. The complex with the lowest MM-GBSA score was deemed superior.

Molecular dynamics simulation of the vaccine-TLR2 complex using GROMACS

To investigate the dynamic behavior, structural stability, and conformational changes of the vaccine construct in complex with the TLR2 receptor, a 200-nanosecond (200 ns) Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation was performed using the GROMACS 2024 software package. The initial complex structure, selected based on the optimal docking results, was utilized as the starting coordinate for the simulation. The system was solvated in a cubic water box using the TIP3P water model, and appropriate ions (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) were added to neutralize the system charge and simulate a physiological salt concentration. Energy minimization was carried out using the steepest descent algorithm to remove steric clashes and relax the system. Subsequently, the system was equilibrated under NVT (constant Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature) and NPT (constant Number of particles, Pressure, and Temperature) ensembles for 100 ps and 200 ps, respectively, to stabilize the temperature at 300 K and pressure at 1 bar. Finally, a production MD simulation was executed for a duration of 200 ns. The trajectory files were analyzed to evaluate parameters such as Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD), Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF), Radius of Gyration (Rg), and hydrogen bonding patterns to assess the stability and binding affinity of the vaccine-TLR2 complex over the simulation time.

Immune simulation study

C-ImmSim is a web-based simulation software that uses machine learning methodologies to forecast the potency of the immune response. This study included the delivery of three doses, each containing 1000 vaccines free of LPS. All remaining variables were preserved in their default configurations.

In‑silico cloning and vaccine optimization

The efficacy of the multi-epitope vaccine construct in cloning and transcription is crucial for vaccine creation. The completed multi-epitope Vaccine Construct underwent codon optimization in the Lactococcus lactis strain using the Java Codon Adaptation Tool (JCat). Furthermore, criteria such as avoiding rho-independent transcription terminators, bacterial ribosome binding sites, and cleavage sites of restriction enzymes were chosen for the final submission. The JCat tool outputted the optimized sequence’s CAI value and GC content. The CAI value should ideally be over 0.8 or close to 1, and the GC content should be within the 30–70% range to assure the vaccine’s capacity for translation, stability, and transcription efficiency. An in-silico cloning of a multi-epitope vaccination construct was conducted utilizing the SnapGene 4.2 program [41–44].

Results

Identification of Gardnerella vaginalis conserved proteins and subtractive genomics

The process in Fig. 1 summarizes the essential phases for target identification, the approaches used, and the total number of proteins reported in each step. Using the OrthoFinder program on the dataset of 150 genomes, we identified 340 core genes, 289 shared genes, and 50 singletons. The subtractive genomic study of critical genes decreased the number of possible targets from 340 to 85. We used the SurfG+ program for predicting protein localization. Of the 85 targets, 55 proteins were projected as cytoplasmic, 22 were periplasmic, and only 5 were outer membrane or extracellular proteins. These 5 proteins were selected for analysis as potential vaccine targets.

Workflow projected with the methodologies used and the total number of proteins identified in each step

The predicted vaccine candidates identified in this study align significantly with the known Outer Membrane Proteome (OMPome) of G. vaginalis. Specifically, the fimbria/pilus outer membrane usher protein and Fimbrial subunit type 1 are well-documented structural components essential for the assembly of pili and biofilm formation, a hallmark of Gardnerella pathogenicity. Furthermore, the outer membrane adhesin-like protein corresponds to known adhesins within the OMPome that mediate critical host-pathogen interactions. By successfully identifying these established surface-exposed proteins, our subtractive genomics approach demonstrates high specificity in filtering the proteome to isolate bona fide outer membrane targets, thereby validating the robustness of our selected antigens for vaccine development [52–54].

The final multi-epitope vaccine was designed

To ensure the structural integrity and immunogenicity of the multi-epitope construct, specific peptide linkers were selected based on their physicochemical properties and functional roles. GGGGS (Glycine-Glycine-Glycine-Glycine-Serine), a flexible linker, was employed to connect the B-cell epitopes to the CTL/HTL cluster. Glycine-rich linkers provide the necessary flexibility, allowing adjacent epitopes to fold independently and maintain their native conformations, which is crucial for B-cell epitopes that require specific 3D structures for antibody recognition. We preferred GGGGS over rigid linkers like EAAAK to ensure that surface-exposed B-cell epitopes remain accessible to B-cell receptors (BCRs) without steric hindrance. To facilitate the intracellular processing of T-cell epitopes, the HEYGAEALERAG linker was used to connect the CTL and HTL epitopes. This sequence contains a recognized Cathepsin B cleavage site, ensuring that once the vaccine construct is internalized by Antigen-Presenting Cells (APCs) via endocytosis, lysosomal enzymes cleave the linker to release individual CTL and HTL epitopes for efficient loading onto MHC Class I and Class II molecules, respectively. This approach was selected over other cleavable linkers like GPGPG due to its proven efficacy in similar in silico vaccine designs for bacterial pathogens. Finally, the short, flexible GSST linker was used to connect the N-terminal adjuvant (HSP60) to the B-cell epitope cluster. Its short length ensures that the adjuvant remains accessible for interaction with Toll-like Receptors (TLRs) on the APC surface, minimizing steric interference with the adjuvant’s immunostimulatory activity.

In conclusion, 13 B-cell epitopes, precisely 13 CTL + HTL, were chosen to develop the multi-epitope vaccine, as indicated in Table 5. The adjuvant was linked to the B-cell epitope using the GSST linker. Subsequently, the CTL + HTL epitopes were coupled using the HEYGAEALERAG linker, while the B-cell and CTL + HTL epitopes were connected using the GGGS linker (Table 4). The assessment of the vaccine construct’s antigenicity, allergenicity, physicochemical qualities, and secondary structure

The physicochemical properties of the designed multi-epitope vaccine construct were evaluated using the ProtParam tool to assess its suitability for expression and stability. The construct exhibited a molecular weight of 49,924.79 Da and a theoretical isoelectric point (pI) of 5.09, indicating an acidic nature. This acidic pI suggests that at physiological pH (7.4), the protein will carry a net negative charge, which can facilitate affinity purification using anion-exchange chromatography and may influence its solubility and interaction with cell membranes.

The instability index was calculated to be 32.65. Since values below 40 indicate that a protein is stable in vivo, this result suggests that the vaccine construct is highly stable and resistant to rapid degradation within the host. Furthermore, the aliphatic index of 42.01 and the Grand Average of Hydropathicity (GRAVY) score of −0.807 confirm that the protein is hydrophilic and likely soluble in aqueous environments, which is crucial for its proper folding and antigenic presentation.

Regarding expression and half-life, the construct was predicted to have a half-life of 30 h in mammalian reticulocytes, 20 h in yeast, and > 10 h in Escherichia coli. These extended half-lives indicate that the protein is stable enough to persist in the host system, allowing sufficient time for immune recognition and activation. This stability is particularly important for ensuring a robust and sustained immune response during vaccination.

Finally, the secondary structure analysis revealed that the construct is predominantly composed of random coils (94.57%) and extended strands (5.43%), with no alpha-helices or beta-turns. This high proportion of random coils is typical for short peptide-based chimeric vaccines, reflecting the flexible linker regions that separate the epitopes, ensuring their independent folding and accessibility to the immune system.

MD simulation

To evaluate the structural stability and binding affinity of the vaccine-TLR2 complex, we performed a 200 ns all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulation using the GROMACS 2021.4 package with the CHARMM36 force field and TIP3P water model, maintaining the system at 300 K and 1 bar pressure after energy minimization and NVT/NPT equilibration. The Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis revealed that the complex reached equilibrium after approximately 50 ns and maintained a stable average RMSD of 3.42 ± 0.45 Å, indicating minimal deviation from the initial docked structure. The Radius of Gyration (Rg) remained constant, confirming the compact and stable nature of the complex throughout the simulation. Furthermore, Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) analysis demonstrated low flexibility in the backbone residues, particularly at the binding interface, while hydrogen bond analysis identified a persistent network of 5–12 intermolecular hydrogen bonds, underscoring the robustness of the interaction. Finally, the binding free energy was calculated using the MM-PBSA method, yielding a favorable average value of −42.5 kcal/mol, which confirms the strong thermodynamic stability and spontaneous nature of the vaccine-TLR2 complex, thereby supporting its potential for effective immune activation via the TLR2 signaling pathway Fig. 6.

Discussion

Historically, the development of vaccinations has been regarded as the most effective approach for addressing diseases caused by infectious organisms (Land et al. 2015; Rahbar Takrami et al. 2019). Creating a potent antigen and appropriate vaccination formulation for Bacterial vaginosis has spanned many decades. This undertaking facilitated the identification of several possible antigens and the establishment of a collective comprehension of vaccination’s importance in combatting diseases (Piri-Gharaghie et al. 2022; Khoshandam et al. 2025). Various techniques have been examined, including DNA vaccines, attenuated parasitic organisms, recombinant compounds, synthesized peptides, and chimeric peptides (Tabibpour et al. 2023). Chimeric or multivalent vaccines are attractive due to their ability to integrate immunogenic components into a single structure. The first investigation into this approach to Gardnerella vaginalis was documented over two decades ago (Yan et al. 1997). Technological developments such as genomics and bioinformatics have made significant progress in developing chimeric antigens. The advancement of machine learning has created new possibilities, including the ability to screen epitopes across a diverse range of human leukocyte antigens (HLAs), analyze the structure of vaccination candidates, forecast antigenicity, and evaluate connections with human receptors (Luo et al. 2015; Akrami et al. 2011).

In this study, we propose the development of a novel multi-epitope vaccination candidate, taking into account an alternative protein source, using a similar methodology. The interaction between the exterior proteins of pathogens and the defenses of the host has the potential to elicit an immune reaction (Weissenbacher et al. 2014; Bakhshinejad et al. 2014). Most exposed proteins are located inside the outer membrane layer of multicellular organisms, such as G. vaginalis. We began our approach by evaluating all probable transmembrane patterns, particularly five antigens, provided by the database. This was necessary since no dedicated database explicitly provides sequences of outer membrane proteins. Additionally, we considered the possibility that G. vaginalis antigens may possess untapped potential. Subsequently, the utilization of online websites facilitated the capability to filter plasma membrane proteins (Sousa et al. 2023; Esmatabadi et al. 2017).

The expected physicochemical properties of the multi-epitope candidate provide strong evidence for using heterologous transcription and antigen isolation as methodologies. The potential application of this microorganism as a substrate for heterologous transcription has been proposed based on the projected half-life in Escherichia coli (10 h) and the molecular stability. We have cloned the vaccine potential codons in pNZ8123, a popular expression vector, using an in-silico method based on Lactococcus lactis. Furthermore, it is seen that the protein will acquire a negative overall charge when exposed to neutral pH conditions, as indicated by the anticipated isoelectric point. This characteristic enhances the process of affinity purification by employing columns containing nickel (Ni2+) immobilized nickel (Irankunda et al. 2024, Khan et al. 2021, Khan et al. 2022).

Moreover, substantial alterations in the protein composition are not expected to occur at these pH conditions. Additionally, the prediction of solubility plays a crucial role in facilitating a successful manufacturing procedure. After many decades of searching for an appropriate antigen to act as a vaccine against Gardnerella vaginalis, only a few molecules have successfully progressed to clinical trials. This has led to an ongoing search for alternative strategies (Pleckaityte et al. 2011; Tajer-Mohammad-Ghazvini et al. 2016).

While some studies (Bukhari 2025, Khan et al. 2022, Khan et al. 2022) utilized known antigens as a starting point for vaccine design, we employed a genome-wide reverse vaccinology approach to identify the most promising antigens. The primary advantage of our strategy is the ability to perform an unbiased, comprehensive screening of the entire G. vaginalis proteome, which enables the discovery of novel, highly immunogenic epitopes that may have been overlooked in traditional literature. Unlike antigen-based approaches that are restricted to previously characterized proteins and may not cover the diverse pathogenic mechanisms of all strains, our method identifies conserved proteins across multiple isolates, ensuring the vaccine targets essential and invariant regions of the pathogen. Although this genomic approach is computationally more intensive than focusing on pre-selected antigens, it is justified by the superior potential to uncover targets with optimal antigenicity, population coverage, and safety profiles compared to conventional candidates.

Limitations and future perspectives

While this study provides a robust in silico framework for designing a multi-epitope vaccine against Gardnerella vaginalis, several limitations must be acknowledged to guide future translational research. First, although antigenicity and allergenicity were assessed using VaxiJen and AllerTop, comprehensive toxicity prediction using tools such as ToxinPred was not extensively integrated into the final selection pipeline. Future studies should rigorously evaluate the toxicity of the construct to ensure its safety for clinical application. Second, the conservancy analysis of the selected epitopes across diverse G. vaginalis strains was limited in scope. Given the genetic diversity of G. vaginalis, it is crucial to perform a broader multiple sequence alignment (MSA) to ensure that the chosen epitopes are conserved across global isolates, thereby ensuring broad-spectrum efficacy. Third, while HLA binding predictions were performed, a detailed population coverage analysis using tools like the Population Coverage tool in IEDB was not included. This analysis is vital to estimate the percentage of the global population that would be protected by the vaccine, accounting for the polymorphism of MHC molecules. Finally, as this is a computational study, experimental validation through in vitro and in vivo models is essential to confirm the immunogenicity, safety, and protective efficacy of the designed vaccine. These limitations highlight the need for comprehensive multi-omics and experimental approaches in subsequent stages of vaccine development.

It is important to acknowledge that this study is exclusively in silico and lacks experimental in vitro or in vivo validation. While computational predictions provide a robust framework for identifying promising vaccine candidates, they cannot fully replicate the complex biological environment of a host organism. Therefore, the findings presented here should be interpreted as preliminary data guiding future experimental research.

Conclusion

In this study, we employed a comprehensive reverse vaccinology approach to design a novel multi-epitope vaccine candidate against Gardnerella vaginalis, the primary causative agent of bacterial vaginosis. By systematically screening the G. vaginalis proteome, we identified five promising outer membrane proteins characterized by conserved superfamily motifs and extracellular domains, which are critical for cross-protection and immune recognition. From these candidates, we constructed a chimeric vaccine comprising selected B-cell, CTL, and HTL epitopes, fused with an HSP60-derived adjuvant to enhance immunogenicity.

Extensive in silico analyses confirmed that the designed construct possesses favorable physicochemical properties, including high stability, solubility, and non-allergenicity. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulations demonstrated the structural stability and strong binding affinity of the vaccine complex with Toll-like Receptor 2 (TLR2), supporting its potential to activate innate immune signaling pathways. The integration of immunoinformatic predictions with structural validation provides a robust framework for vaccine development, reducing the time and cost associated with traditional experimental methods.

While this study establishes a strong computational foundation, experimental validation remains the next critical step. Future work will focus on the in vitro and in vivo evaluation of this vaccine candidate to assess its immunogenicity, safety, and protective efficacy against G. vaginalis infection. We anticipate that this multi-epitope construct will serve as a promising lead candidate for the development of an effective prophylactic vaccine against bacterial vaginosis, addressing the urgent need for alternatives to antibiotic-based therapies.
