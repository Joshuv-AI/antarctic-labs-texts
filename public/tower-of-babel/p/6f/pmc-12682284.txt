A mathematical model for the co-activation of inflammatory genes mediated by NF-κB and IRF-3 following viral infections
Authors: Syona Tiwari, Rajiv Kumar Mishra, Soumen Basak, Rakesh Pandey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe innate immune responses in host cells are activated through molecular signaling pathways, such as NF-κB and IRF-3 in response to viral infections. These transcription factors regulate the expression of antiviral genes that encode type I interferons (IFNα/β). However, the precise mechanism that governs their complex context-dependent temporal activation remains elusive. Here, we have developed a probability-based mathematical model to study the co-regulation of the IFNβ gene by NF-κB and IRF-3. Our results suggest that the rapid and elevated as well as a delayed but persistent expression of IFNβ would be observed due to a temporal bias in the binding of NF-κB and IRF-3, highlighting the need for co-regulation of IFNβ by two different signaling pathways. Also, the model predicts that the binding affinity of NF-κB to the promoter of IFNβ would be equal to or greater than that of IRF-3 for a quick, strong and persistent antiviral response.Subject areas: molecular mechanism of gene regulation, mathematical biosciences

Introduction

The innate immune response is the first line of defense of host cells against invading pathogens. It is initiated after identifying viral or bacterial nucleic acids through a pathogen-specific host receptor. This initial immune response activates a cascade of cell signaling pathways that target the containment and elimination of the pathogen. An essential aspect of this response involves the activation of pro-inflammatory and antiviral transcription factors, such as NF-κB, AP-1, NF-IL6, NFAT, IRFs, and STATs, which regulate the expression of interferons (IFNs).1,2

IFNs are cytokines that suppress viral replication in host cells by transcriptional induction of various IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs).3 Typically, three types of IFNs (type I, II, and III) are believed to play a role in activating the antiviral state of infected cells.4 Of these, type I interferons (IFNα/β) and their receptors are expressed by most cells that initiate the immune response in the early stage and immunosuppression during chronic infection.5 Type II IFN expression is mediated by a single gene (IFN γ) in T cells or natural killer cells (NK) that occurs in all cells expressing its receptors. However, type III IFNs (IFN λ1, IFN λ2, and IFN λ3) are produced by specific cell types and therefore cause an acute response to viral infection in cells (such as the formation of epithelial barriers).6 Respiratory viral infections, such as SARS-CoV-2, pose a high risk to these barrier cells in the respiratory tract, thus activating the targeted action of type III IFN.4

The induction and expression of IFN and its associated genes result from several molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways. Of which, the inflammatory signaling pathway mediated by the transcription factor NF-κB and the receptor-mediated retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) signaling pathway that involves the IRF-3 transcription factor are significant in the antiviral mechanism. NF-κB is observed in its nascent form, that is, the inhibitor of kappa B (IκB) is attached to it in the absence of any external stimulus, such as nucleic material from the invading pathogen. However, an external stimulus to the host cell by viruses (such as the viral analog poly I:C) or lipopolysaccharide (bacterial analog) leads to the activation of the NF-κB pathway.

After the entry of the viral genome into the cytoplasm, they are sensed by pattern recognition receptors (such as Toll-like receptor (TLR), RIG-like receptor (RLR), etc.). This leads to phosphorylation of IκBα bound to NF-κB, which is then degraded in the cytosol. IκBα is therefore disassociated from the NF-κB subunit, thus activating it.7 The active NF-κB transcription factor is then translocated into the nucleus. It binds to the promoter site of inflammatory genes such as type I IFNs (IFNα/β) and regulates their expression.3,8

The other signaling pathway activated upon recognition of non-self RNA (such as viral RNA) by the RIG-I receptor encodes the transcription of interferon regulatory factors (IRFs). Ingression of double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) in the cytosol leads to the specific activation of one of the IRFs, i.e., the IRF-3 signaling pathway.9,10 The entry of an RNA virus into the cytoplasm signals the adapter protein mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) to form a complex with RIG and propagate the signal to downstream molecules. Poly I:C is a synthetic analog of dsRNA used to mimic viral infections that produce dsRNA in their replication cycle. Since Poly I:C is mainly associated with RNA viruses, it has been suggested that it may also be relevant to study the immune response to SARS-CoV-2 infection.11 For this purpose, a viral analog Poly I:C infection initiates signaling through pattern recognition receptors (PRR) such as RIG-I.12 The signal propagated by the Poly I:C-RIG-I-MAVS axis recruits the TNF-receptor-associated factor (TRAF) protein toward itself.13 This TRAF protein relays the activation of the IRF-3 and NF-κB signaling pathways through TANK-binding kinase (TBK).14 TBK is phosphorylated into its active form that subsequently phosphorylates specific serine residues on IRF-3.15 After the phosphorylation of IRF-3, it dimerizes and localizes inside the nucleus. Here, it functions as a crucial transcription factor for binding to the promoters of type I IFNs (IFNα/β), several cytokines, and several antiviral ISGs.16

Simultaneous activation of IRF-3 and NF-κB signaling pathways transcribes IFNs (IFNβ) that encode the antiviral state in the host cell. The activation of such a state is believed to lead to rapid degradation of viral nuclei, inflammation in the host, and activation of IFN-induced genes and gene products.17,18,19,20,21,22,23,24,25

Expression of transcription factors NF-κB and IRF-3 to pathogenic stimulus and their biochemical mechanisms have been modeled previously.26,27,28,29,30,31,32 A mathematical model using the stochastic framework has previously been studied to model the regulation of IFNβ by these transcription factors.27 In this model, three inputs to the system (Poly I:C, IFNβ, and LPS) trigger the immune signaling pathways and their downstream effects on gene expression. Here, three transcription factors, STAT 1/2, NF-κB, and IRF-3, are modeled as regulatory systems activated after stimulation with Poly I:C or LPS.

However, a model for the coregulation of type I IFNs, specifically by NF-κB and IRF-3, is still elusive. Also, a model to calculate the promoter binding activity of IFNs coregulated by NF-κB and IRF-3 is less explored.

In the present study, we have modeled the coregulation of IFNβ in response to the Poly I:C input (viral analog) mediated by NF-κB and IRF-3 in the host’s cell through an ODE-based deterministic model (Figure 1). To model both signaling pathways, we have incorporated the established mechanisms that are not cell specific for the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway.26 The IRF-3 signaling pathway was modeled based on known biochemical interactions.27 We calculated the expression of IFNβ after binding both the transcription factors to the promoter site of the IFNβ gene using a Hill-like equation. We varied the parameters of the IRF-3 module to two and three orders of magnitude and studied its effect on the expression of IRF-3 and IFNβ inside the nucleus of the host’s cell.

Our model results demonstrate that for early and strong as well as delayed but persistent antiviral and inflammatory responses, binding of both NF-κB and IRF-3 are required. In addition, the early immune response to viral infection would be attributed to the activity of transcription factor NF-κB, whereas the transcription factor IRF-3 would be responsible for sustained albeit delayed IFNβ expression. This result is consistent with an observation for Newcastle disease virus (NDV) and vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV).33 We propose that it occurs due to a temporal bias in the binding of NF-κB and IRF-3. However, the binding of both transcription factors is required. This hypothesis aligns with the finding of Cheng et al.,34 and proposes the function of both transcription factors as a “Sequential OR gate”. Furthermore, our model predicts that the binding affinity of IRF3 would be weaker than that of NF-κB for an antiviral response observed experimentally.

Response of the mathematical model of IRF-3 arm to a viral analog input

We assume that both NF-κB and IRF-3 are stimulated through an input signal (Poly I:C) with a half-life of 20 min or 6 min (Figure 3A). Our results for the NF-κB arm suggest an immediate and sharp increase in the level of nuclear NF-κB in response to Poly I:C input signals (Figure 3B). Rapidly, the level of nuclear NF-κB reaches a maximum at ≈ 50 min, and after that, it starts to decrease. The activation of nuclear NF-κB lasts for about 100 min, coincident with the disappearance of the Poly I:C signal, ensuring no antiviral and inflammatory response once the stimulatory signal vanishes.

In contrast, model results for the IRF-3 arm suggest that initially the level of active IRF-3 increases monotonically and reaches a plateau post-viral infections (Figure 3C). However, an increase in the level of active IRF-3 does not occur immediately after the infection; rather, there would be a delay. Also, the maximum level of active IRF-3 would be less than that of NF-κB and remains persistent even after the viral input diminishes completely. Therefore, a sustained antiviral and inflammatory response would be observed for a longer period. We noticed that the increase in the level of active IRF-3 in the nucleus starts exactly when the Poly I:C signal and the nuclear concentration of NF-κB vanish.

In addition, our results suggest that an increase in the level of active NF-κB and IRF-3 would be proportional to the strength of the input signal Poly I:C (Figure 3).

Since the value of the model parameters (described in Tables S3 and S4) are unknown, we first assume a default value for them and later vary by 2–3 orders of magnitude to systematically explore the effect of changes in the values of the model parameters on the level of active IRF-3. A decrease in the rate k1, k5, and k9 leads to a significant decrease in the nuclear concentration of active IRF-3 (Figure S1). A significant increase and decrease in the active nuclear phosphorylated IRF-3 levels are observed for variations in the association rate of Poly I:C and RIG-MAVS complex (k3). A significant reduction in the IRF-3 levels is observed on increase in the value of following model parameters: rate of disassociation of MAVS-RIG-Poly I:C- TRAF-3 complex (k7), rates of disassociation of MAVS-RIG-Poly I:C-TRAF-3-TBK 1 (phosphorylated) complex (k10) and (k13), and rate of export of phosphorylated IRF-3 dimer from nucleus to the cytoplasm (k18). Expression patterns of IFNβ co-regulated by NF-κB and IRF-3

To investigate the coregulation of IFNβ by IRF-3 and NF-κB, we have modeled their binding to the respective promoter regions using a framework proposed in Equation 3. Also, two Poly I:C input signals, shaped by the half-lives and initial concentration of Poly I:C in human serum from an earlier study35 were considered (Figure 3, Figure 4A). In addition, the active concentration of NF-κB and IRF-3 (Figures 3B and 3C, respectively) inside the nucleus were considered as inputs in Equation 3.

We have modeled the individual promoter binding activity of NF-κB and IRF-3 using Equation 2 and the expression pattern of IFNβ, i.e., the level of transcribed mRNA was calculated. Our results suggest an immediate and strong IFNβ response when only NF-κB is bound and IRF-3 is not bound (Figure 5A), and a delayed sigmoid-shaped IFNβ response when only IRF-3 is bound and NF-κB is not bound (Figure 5B). A comparatively more realistic situation occurs when both NF-κB and IRF-3 bind to their respective binding sites in the promoter region upstream of IFNβ, which we have modeled through Equation 3. Equation 3 is a probabilistic modeling framework for the binding of NF-κB and IRF-3 concurrently. The model results suggest that an early peak followed by a decline in the level of IFNβ along with a sustained level of that would be observed for a longer time (Figure 5C). The peak would decline after a certain time (≈100 min) with a subsequent increase in the level of IFNβ albeit slightly lower than the height of the initial peak but would remain sustained for a longer duration.

This expression pattern of IFNβ is consistent with observations for NDV and VSV.33 Authors have demonstrated in their experiments on mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) that the replication of NDV and VSV were significantly higher in RelA−/− compared to wild-type (WT) cells (Figures S2 and S3). The expression of IFNβ in WT was higher than that of RelA−/− MEFs after ≈11 h but substantially reduced in RelA−/− MEFs at early time points (3–6 h) (Figures S4 and S5). This observation suggests a critical role of Rel A (a subunit of NF-κB) for initiating IFNβ response. In addition, IRF-3 activation (determined by dimer formation) was not detected up to ≈ 9 h after NDV infection by western blot in WT and IRF-3−/− (Figure S6). At approx. 12 h and onwards, the active IRF-3 level reached a peak and the expression of IFNβ was increased in WT but not in IRF-3−/− (Figure S6). Therefore, their results suggest that NF-κB is required for early antiviral response (i.e., increased level of IFNβ) when IRF-3 activation is low, and IRF-3 alone is sufficient for elicitation of antiviral response during the late phase, consistent with our model results.

We also calculated the IFNβ response for two inputs of Poly I:C with different half-lives and for three binding scenarios (Figures 3 and 5). These results suggest that the IFNβ response would not be exclusive to a particular viral analog. Also, a stronger viral analog (Poly I:C Input I) would lead to elevated levels of NF-κB, IRF-3, and IFNβ inside the nucleus. A suppressed level of these species would be observed for Poly I:C input II (Figures 3 and 5).

We modeled the binding activity of both the transcription factors NF-κB and IRF-3 at the promoter sites of IFNβ using a probabilistic framework (Equation 3) and estimated the level of expression of IFNβ regulated by both transcription factors. In the absence of experimentally known values of the model parameters, we systematically varied model parameters by two and three orders of magnitude to investigate the systems-level dynamic behavior (Figure 6). Poly I:C input I, with a half-life of 20 min and an initial concentration of 500 nM, was used as input for the present analysis of the model. Our results suggest that an increase of 2 or 3 orders of magnitude in the rate of phosphorylation of IRF-3 in the cytoplasm (k14) would lead to a slight increase in the expression of IFNβ with respect to the level of that for the default value of k14 denoted by a black colored curve (Figure 6A). Post 100 min, the level of IFNβ expression falls below the level of that for the default value of k14 for a 2 or 3 orders of magnitude decrease in k14. A rapid decline after ≈100 min coincides with the rapid fall in Poly I:C concentration.

Binding affinities of NF-κB would be equal to greater than that of IRF-3

Binding affinity is one of the measures to quantify the strength of interaction between transcription factors and their specific DNA binding sites at the promoter region upstream of a gene. Previous studies have proposed a mathematical framework to establish a relationship between binding affinity and transcriptional output of the target gene. The seminal study by Kim and O’Shea (2008)36 demonstrates that strong binding affinity (or lower dissociation constant kd) leads to increased transcription of the target gene. However, a weak binding affinity (or a higher dissociation constant kd) leads to reduced transcription of the target gene.

The general binding affinity of the transcription factor NF-κB to its canonical sites κB has been reported in terms of the equilibrium dissociation constant (kNFkB) and kNFkB varies between 3 and 10 nM under physiological conditions (37° C, 150 mM NaCl, and pH 7.5) replicating intracellular environments.37 This range is not specific to the κB site upstream of any particular type of IFNs. However, the binding affinity of the transcription factor IRF-3 to the upstream of IFNs is not known.

In the present study, we investigate the effect of relative changes in the binding affinities of NF-κB and IRF-3 on the expression pattern of the inflammatory gene IFNβ. We calculated the levels of transcribed mRNA for the following three distinct possibilities.

Case I: kNFkB>kIRF-3. We assumed the value of binding affinity of NF-κB (dissociation constant) as reported in the literature,37 i.e., kNFkB = 10 nM. Higher binding affinities (lower dissociation constants) for IRF-3 compared to those of the NF-κB were considered by decreasing kIRF-3 by a factor of 10, 100, and 1000, respectively.

Case II: kNFkB<kIRF-3. Again, the value of the binding affinity of NF-κB was assumed as reported i.e., kNFκB = 10 nM and lower binding affinities (higher dissociation constants) for IRF-3 were considered by increasing kIRF-3 by a factor of 10, 100, and 1000, respectively.

Case III: kNFkB = kIRF-3. Values of the binding affinities (dissociation constants) of NF-κB and IRF-3 were assumed equal and varied within the observed range of the binding affinity (dissociation constant) of NF-κB, i.e., between 3 and 10 nM.

For Case I (kNFkB>kIRF-3), model results suggest that for a 10, 100, and 1000-fold increase in the binding affinity of IRF-3, the IFNβ expression level would increase rapidly (Figure 7A). For all values of kIRF-3, we observed a maximum increase at ≈ 50 min. For kIRF-3 = 1 nM, it drops and settles at ≈ 90 nM. We noticed that the early response remains the same for all the values of kIRF-3. The expression level of IFNβ changes for changes in the values of kIRF-3 only after 100 min, when the role of IRF-3 sets in. These results suggest that the higher binding affinity of IRF-3 would result in a very strong and persistent antiviral response immediately after the infection and remains so even after the viral stimulus has vanished. For Case II (kNFkB<kIRF-3), our results suggest that for a 10, 100, and 1000-fold decrease in the binding affinity of IRF-3, the IFNβ expression level would increase rapidly initially followed by a rapid decline after 100 min (Figure 7B). Similar to Case I, the early response (until 100 min) remains the same for all the values of kIRF-3. A 10-fold decrease in the binding affinity of IRF-3 would lead to a decrease in the transcribed IFNβ mRNA level and settle at ≈ 40 nM after a long time post-infection. A 100 and 1000-fold decrease in the value of kIRF-3 would result in a further decrease in the IFNβ mRNA levels and settle at a relatively low level ≈10 nM after a long time post-infection. Since the binding affinity of IRF-3 is weaker than that of NF-κB, the level of transcribed IFNβ mRNA does not remain high after 100 min, suggesting a decline in anti-viral response. In Case III, when kNFkB = kIRF-3, initially a rapid increase in the expression level of IFNβ would be observed, similar to Case I and Case II. Around 100 min, a slight dip in the level of IFNβ would be observed. The overall levels of transcribed IFNβ mRNA would decrease with increasing values of kNFkB and kIRF-3. In this case, when both the binding affinities of NF-κB and IRF-3 are equal, both NF-κB and IRF-3 transcription factors would be responsible for early antiviral response (IFNβ≈ 90 nM) and sustained (between 70 and 90 nM) for a longer period. Therefore, the antiviral response in Case III would be stronger than Case II but weaker than Case I after the viral stimulus is vanished (after 100 nM) (Figure 7). These results collectively suggest that the binding affinity of IRF-3 would be either equal to or smaller than that of NFkB (i.e., kNFkB≤kIRF-3) for a strong and sustained antiviral response post-viral infections (Figure 7).

Discussion

Inflammation and tissue damage are common phenomena observed across diseases such as viral infection38 and autoimmune disorders,39 as well as a part of a protective mechanism of the immune system.40 It is widely accepted that inflammation induced by infections and tissue damage is an essential mechanism of innate immune response. Proper inflammatory responses provide broad-spectrum protection against infections and orchestrate long-term adaptive immunity toward specific pathogens. The activation of pattern recognition receptors and the production of effector molecules are essential components of the innate immune system following viral infections.41 Nuclear factor κB is one of the transcription factors that regulate the expression of inflammatory genes in multiple diseases.14 It has been established that the canonical NF-κB pathway has a role in eliciting the IFN response, which is one of the first lines of defense in the host’s immune system.27,42,43 However, the type I IFN response is regulated by more than one signaling pathway, for example, by the transcription factor IRF-3 along with NF-κB post-viral infection. Viral input to the host’s cells is sensed by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as TLRs (Toll-like receptors) and RLR (RIG-like receptors) that activate the NF-κB and IRF-3 signaling pathways.44 In infected cells, viral double-stranded RNA mimetics such as Poly I:C primarily activate TLR3 and cytosolic RLR pathways.45,46 They converge on the TRIF-TRAF3-TBK1/IKKϵ axis47,48,49 and induce IRF-3 activation that coordinates with NF-κB at the IFNβ promoter.33 Whereas, bacterial lipopolysaccharide involves the TLR4 stimulation mediated by TRIF to activate IRF-3. Here, NF-κB activation is mediated by MyD88–NF-κB pathway and the dominant output is pro-inflammatory cytokine profile (e.g., TNF, IL-1β, IL-6) with a relatively reduced IFN-β component.50 A study utilizing CHIP-seq and genome-wide association analysis (GWAS) has established that both the transcription factors NF-κB and IRF-3 cooperate to recruit factors that initiate the transcriptional machinery of IFN genes.51 It has been computationally predicted that the promoter of IFNβ contains NF-κB binding sites and two IFN-stimulated responsive elements (ISREs) recognized by phosphorylated IRF3/7.42 Thus suggesting the role of both transcription factors in co-regulating the expression of IFNβ. Previous mathematical modeling studies have shown that NF-κB activity is shaped by stimulus-dependent IKK activation and regulation by inhibitory proteins, such as IκBα, IκBβ, IκBϵ, and A20.28,30 Another study shows that IRF3 and ISGF3 often control many of the same antiviral genes, and which genes they affect depends on timing and magnitude of IFNβ signaling.52 In viral infections, IFNβ production generally requires co-activation of NF-κB and IRF3 transcription factors that is maintained for a sufficient duration.27 Another follow-up model links NF-κB–IRF3 co-regulation of IFNβ with STAT1/2 signaling, showing how Poly I:C-activated PKR and RNase L shift IFNβ-primed cells to an NF-κB/IRF3-driven state.53 Coordinated activation of these transcription factors ensures effective induction of antiviral genes, and their relative timing and magnitude play a critical role in shaping the overall immune response.

Previously, the induction of IFNβ by NF-κB and IRF-3 signaling pathways has been modeled along with their negative regulators, although using a stochastic approach.27 There is no mathematical model available to compute the IFNβ expression pattern in response to the viral analog, Poly I:C, which is attempted in the present study.

Here, we have developed a mathematical model for the co-regulation of the IFNβ gene by NF-κB and IRF-3 signaling pathways. Our model is not cell specific and the signaling cascades we have assumed for NF-κB and IRF-3 is believed to be conserved across all cell types. A slight variation in that is possible for specific cell types.41,42,43,44,45,46 The canonical signaling arm of NF-κB and IRF-3 was modeled using a mathematical model based on a deterministic ODE framework published elsewhere.26 It is believed that the canonical NF-κB pathway plays a role in the elicitation of IFNβ response,54,55,56 therefore, we gave null input to the NIK enzyme that activates the non-canonical NF-κB pathway. We modeled the IRF-3 signaling pathway by systematically identifying all the biochemical species and reactions involved, based on the existing literature.27 A systematic parameter variation analysis by varying model parameters of the model of the IRF-3 pathway was performed to explore the robustness of the model outputs and to identify critical parameters.

The DNA-NF-κB and DNA-IRF-3 binding are modeled here using a Hill-like equation to elicit the role of active NF-κB and IRF-3 in the regulation of IFNβ expression. The level of the transcribed mRNA of IFNβ was calculated using the concentration of respective transcription factors within the nucleus. In addition, we explored the expression pattern of IFNβ for three scenarios: when (1) only NF-κB, (2) only IRF-3, and (3) both NF-κB and IRF-3 bind to the respective binding sites upstream of IFNβ.

Our results suggest that a peak is formed in the response curve of the NF-κB lasting until 100 min (≈1 to 2 h) whereas significant active IRF-3 level appears only after ≈70 min. Therefore, the expression pattern of transcribed IFNβ mRNA level would reach a maximum immediately, presumably due to the binding of NF-κB, and post 100 min due to the binding of IRF-3 to the binding sites in the upstream region of IFNβ. Similar patterns of IRF-3 and NF-κB activation are observed in studies for NDV and VSV viral infections performed in MEFs.33 Both NDV and VSV are live viruses that are frequently used in experimental conditions, and our model predictions are consistent with them. Alternative viral inputs may affect early response strength and persistence of delayed response without changing core outcomes of the model.

In addition, these results suggest that the expression of IFNβ is coregulated by the activation of NF-κB and IRF-3 at different time points in response to the viral analog Poly I:C. Therefore, we propose a temporal separation in the activity of NF-κB and IRF-3 transcription factors. This is consistent with an earlier hypothesis of sequential transcriptional control proposed by Cheng et al. (2017).34 Through a transcriptomic and logic-gate-based modeling approach, Cheng et al. have demonstrated that the expression of IFNβ is regulated by temporal-separation in the activity of transcription factors, rather than their simultaneous co-binding. They have identified gene clusters regulated by either NF-κB or ISGF3 (an IRF-3 family member) or both, and proposed “sequential OR” and “sequential AND” logic gates based on the difference in transcriptional timing. We argue that a rapid and strong response due to NF-κB, followed by a sustained response due to IRF-3, resembles a “sequential OR” gate mentioned by Cheng et al.

Further, the effect of the relative change in the binding affinities of the transcription factors NF-κB and IRF-3 on the transcribed level of IFNβ is studied. Our results suggest that the binding affinity of IRF-3 would be equal to or weaker than that of NF-κB (i.e., for dissociation constant, kNFkB≤kIRF-3) for a strong and sustained antiviral response.

IFNβ, despite its complex promoter architecture, requires cooperative binding of NF-κB and IRF-3 at the enhanceosome,57,58 that is a central feature of our modeling framework. Alternate targets such as TNF are regulated primarily by NF-κB,14,59,60 while others, such as IFIT1 are induced downstream of type I interferon signaling without direct IRF-3 binding or NF-κB co-binding.61,62,63

In vivo, viruses antagonize these transcription factors to evade antiviral response. Borna disease virus (BDV),64 coronaviruses,65 herpesvirus,66 and hepatitis C virus (HCV)67 inhibit IRF-3, whereas reoviruses,68 human cytomegalovirus (HCMV),69 and influenza A virus (IAV)70 inhibit NF-κB. Highly pathogenic viruses, such as Ebola virus, certain coronaviruses, and paramyxoviruses target both IRF-3 and NF-κB.65,71 However, not all viruses show antagonism to these pathways and are believed to activate NF-κB and IRF-3 signaling cascades during infection such as NDV, VSV, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV).72,73 The present study models the response to the synthetic double-stranded RNA analog Poly I:C that activates both NF-κB and IRF-3 and does not show antagonism.27 Hence, in our simulations activation of NF-κB and IRF-3 is considered without viral antagonism since we are using Poly I:C and not live viruses.

In the present study we have modeled the core NF-κB-IRF-3-signaling axis to investigate the emergent co-regulation of IFNβ during early response to acute Poly I:C stimulation within a single cell. We intentionally omitted complex signaling interactions and their modules, including autocrine IFN signaling,43 to capture key antiviral signaling dynamics while maintaining model tractability and interpretability. These pathways involve crosstalk with other signaling cascades i.e., JAK/STAT27 that are believed to play a significant role in sustained signaling, immune regulation, and maintaining antiviral state during chronic stimulation or in vivo infection.74

Limitations of the study

One of the limitations of our model is that we have not considered the positive and negative feedback regulators of the biochemical species involved in the IRF-3 signaling pathway,75 or novel regulators of Type I IFN induction that are activated along with IRF-3.36 In addition, the present model does not consider the multiscale and multi-tissue dynamics, which are considered in some of the stochastic mathematical models.43,44,76 Cell-type-specific variations between epithelial and immune cells could influence antiviral signaling dynamics during viral infection such as differences in expression of receptors, kinetics of signaling cascades, and magnitude of IFNβ response. Although we have not explicitly modeled these differences in the current framework, our model can be adapted to account for such parameters as quantitative cell-specific data for viral infections. A key limitation of our model is the lack of direct experimental validation. Although the current model has not been validated through new experimental data, its findings are consistent with the biological patterns observed elsewhere33 thus providing a qualitative agreement. The model’s key predictions such as temporal dynamics of IFNβ expression and IRF-3 binding affinities across a broad spectrum of viral infections could provide critical insights that can be validated through large-scale, diverse experimental datasets.

In future studies, the present model can be extended by considering the negative regulators of the IRF-3 signaling pathway. In addition, the absence of autocrine IFN signaling, and multiscale interactions involving crosstalk with JAK/STAT pathway are important limitation of the present study. Future models could be refined by incorporating such interactions supported by experimental data to study chronic infections under physiological conditions. The construction of any mathematical model is constrained by the lack of experimental data, and the parameter estimation remains a fundamental challenge. The future models could address these challenges to build a more realistic model. Recent modeling studies of SARS-CoV-2 immune dynamics in primates76 have used ensemble modeling approaches that could be incorporated in the future iterations of our model along with Bayesian inference to account for parameter uncertainty and novel insights.

Method details

Model development

We simulate the host’s innate immune response to viral infection by modeling the biochemical reactions involved in activating NF-κB and IRF-3 signaling cascades using an ODE-based model (Figure 1). Here, we have assumed a signaling cascade triggered by a viral infection but not tailored to a specific type of infection. The paracrine activity post infection is not considered in the present study. Poly I:C, a viral analogue, acts as an input to the model, resulting in the activation of NF-κB and IRF-3 signaling pathways upstream of IFNβ. Here, the dynamic expression profile of IFNβ is computed through the numerical solution of the ODEs for the nuclear concentration of the transcription factors, followed by the calculation of promoter binding activity of respective transcription factors to the host’s DNA (upstream of IFNβ).

Modeling of the NF-κB pathway

NF-κB pathway has been comprehensively explored for its role in the inflammatory response across different cells.26,77,78,79,80,81 The canonical and non-canonical pathways of NF-κB’s activation have been mathematically modeled earlier.26 In our model, the canonical pathway is given a Poly I:C input upstream of the level of NEMO activation, and its time-dependent value is determined by its half-life and initial concentration. The non-canonical or alternative signaling of the NF-κB pathway is attenuated by giving a null value to the NIK input. The nuclear concentration of active NF-κB, in response to a viral stimulus, is subsequently plotted using MATLAB Software.82

Modeling of IFN regulatory factor - 3 pathway

Previous studies have introduced several mathematical models that utilize stochastic27 and deterministic36,52,53,75,83,84,85 mathematical modeling framework to model the IRF-3 signaling pathway as a part of their methodology. We have modeled the IRF-3 signaling pathway after surveying the literature for all the biochemical species involved in the sensing of viral nuclear material, transduction of its signal, and activation of IRF-3 transcription factor27 (Tables S1 and S2). To model the activation of IFNβ downstream of the IRF-3 pathway upon binding of IRF-3 transcription factor to the promoter region, we provide Poly I:C input to the RIG-mediated activation of IRF-3 arm as illustrated in Figure 1. MATLAB software is used to compute the numerical solution of the ODE systems involving all the biochemical species modeled of the IRF-3 pathway (Figure 2). The same input signal of Poly I:C (viral analogue) is given to the IRF3-arm that was given to the NF-κB arm, and the levels of a phosphorylated nuclear dimer of IRF-3 are calculated. Values of 24 kinetic parameters (rates reported in min-1) are assumed in the model, and the physiological range of them was obtained from literature.27,53,86 Values of these parameters are guessed by varying their values to the two and three orders of their magnitude (Figure 4; see also Figure S1).

Viral analogue input signal: Poly I: C

Poly I:C, the viral analogue of double-stranded RNA, is a synthetic mimic of viruses administered in vitro through transfection in the host’s system. Previous studies have determined its half-life in the human serum through radiochemical analyses.35 In our model, we provided two Poly I:C signals using an initial concentration of 500nM and 50nM, with a half-life of 20 min and 6 min, respectively (Figure 3).
