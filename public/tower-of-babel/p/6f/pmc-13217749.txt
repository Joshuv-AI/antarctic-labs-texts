Do executive functions moderate or mediate? exploring their role in the prospective association between psychopathic traits and conduct problems in children
Authors: Silvija Ručević, Dino Krupić, Sandra Brezetić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExecutive functions, including working memory, inhibition, sustained attention, cognitive flexibility, and planning are critical for regulating behavior and have been linked to externalizing outcomes. Similarly, psychopathic traits in childhood, particularly Callous-Unemotional, Grandiose-Deceitful, and Impulsivity-Need for Stimulation, are robust predictors of later conduct problems. Although studies have examined these relationships, findings are inconsistent regarding whether executive functions mediate or moderate the prospective association between early psychopathic traits and later conduct problems, and whether specific executive function components play distinct roles across psychopathy dimensions. Moreover, most prior work has relied on either teacher ratings or performance-based executive function measures alone, despite evidence that these approaches capture complementary aspects of executive functions.MethodsThis longitudinal study followed a community sample of 215 children (Mage = 8.2 years) over five years. At baseline, parents rated psychopathic traits, teachers assessed executive function difficulties (working memory, inhibition/regulation), and children completed performance-based executive function tasks (working memory, sustained attention, cognitive flexibility, and planning). At follow-up, new teachers rated conduct problems, ensuring independent assessments. Mediation and moderation analyses were conducted using PROCESS (Hayes, 2022), controlling for IQ, hyperactivity, and gender. Critically, both informant-based and behavioral measures of executive functions were used to capture distinct behavioral and neurocognitive processes.ResultsTeacher-rated inhibition and regulation deficits partially mediated and moderated the link between Impulsivity-Need for Stimulation traits and later conduct problems, suggesting impulsivity heightens behavioral risk when self-regulatory capacities are weak. Performance-based executive functions showed a different pattern for Callous-Unemotional traits: cognitive inflexibility mediated their association with conduct problems, while sustained attention deficits amplified this relationship. Stronger working memory buffered the Callous-Unemotional-conduct problem link, reducing risk. Grandiose-Deceitful traits were unrelated to executive functions or conduct problems. Notably, teacher-reported executive functions captured observable emotional/behavioral regulation deficits linked to impulsivity, whereas performance-based tasks revealed cognitive control vulnerabilities relevant to Callous-Unemotional traits.ConclusionsDistinct executive function mechanisms underlie different psychopathic traits, emphasizing the importance of multi-method executive functions assessment. Findings suggest that interventions targeting both behavioral regulation (inhibition) and cognitive control (attention, flexibility, working memory) may reduce conduct problems in at-risk youth.

Introduction

Executive functions (EF), including working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility, sustained attention, and planning, are critical for self-regulation and goal-directed behavior [11, 28, 32]. EF are often distinguished into cold components, which primarily involve cognitive control in emotionally neutral contexts (e.g., working memory, inhibition), and hot components, which operate in affectively or motivationally salient situations (e.g., behavioral and emotion regulation; [34]. This distinction is necessarily approximate, as these processes are closely intertwined and rely on overlapping neural systems [64]. Deficits in EF have been consistently linked to negative developmental outcomes, particularly within the externalizing spectrum. A recent meta-analysis found prospective associations between EF difficulties and increased risk for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), oppositional defiant disorder, conduct problems, substance use, and broader patterns of externalizing behavior [63].

Alongside EF, childhood psychopathic traits have been recognized as robust predictors of behavioral problems. Of these, callous–unemotional (CU) traits, characterized by low empathy, guilt, and emotional responsiveness, have received the most empirical attention and are prospectively associated with persistent oppositional behavior and conduct issues (e.g., [10, 18]), even after controlling for baseline problem behavior (e.g., [9]). However, psychopathy is a multidimensional construct. Beyond CU traits, the behavioral dimension (e.g., impulsivity, sensation seeking) is closely linked to aggression, defiance, and conduct problems (e.g., [15, 36]), while the interpersonal dimension (e.g., grandiosity, deceitfulness) is associated with manipulation, relational aggression, and bullying (e.g., [19, 33]).

Although EF difficulties and psychopathic traits independently predict externalizing outcomes, less is known about how these factors operate together across development. This question is particularly salient because executive function (EF) impairments are not a general feature of psychopathy, but tend to cluster around its disinhibitory and antisocial components [35]. Consistent with this view, a recent meta-analysis reported only small overall EF deficits, concentrated in lifestyle/antisocial traits rather than interpersonal or affective dimensions [7]. Whether this pattern is already evident in childhood, when executive control systems are still developing and psychopathic traits are more malleable, remains unclear. Contemporary vulnerability models further emphasize that understanding psychopathology requires identifying not only whether a risk factor predicts an outcome, but also the processes through which risk unfolds (mediation) and the conditions under which it is amplified or attenuated (moderation [21]).

Applied to psychopathic traits and conduct problems, executive functions can therefore operate in two distinct ways. They may form part of the developmental pathway linking early dispositional risk with later behavior problems, such that psychopathic traits are associated with poorer executive functioning, which in turn increases the likelihood of conduct problems (mediation). Alternatively, executive functions may condition this association, such that the behavioral impact of psychopathic traits depends on a child’s level of self-regulatory capacity (moderation). In this way, EF may index how psychopathic traits translate into later behavior problems, while also shaping when and for whom these traits are most likely to manifest in maladaptive outcomes.

However, the existing literature remains difficult to integrate. Results vary not only across studies, but also as a function of which EF component is examined, how EF is measured, and the population under study. Work in high-risk or clinical samples of older children and adolescents has typically relied on performance-based tasks tapping cold EF domains such as inhibition, working memory, and attentional control (e.g., [3, 42]), often showing that cognitive control moderates how psychopathic traits are reflected in behavior. By contrast, studies conducted in community samples of younger children have more often used informant-rated measures of everyday executive functioning (e.g., the Behavioral Rating Inventory of Executive Function [20]), capturing domains such as behavioral regulation, attentional control, and planning (e.g., [31, 47, 60]), and have yielded more mixed or null interactive effects. These approaches are not interchangeable: performance-based tasks index efficiency under structured conditions, whereas rating scales reflect self-regulation in daily contexts [58]. Together with the predominance of high-risk samples [3, 42], frequent reliance on single informants (e.g., [2]), and inconsistent control of confounds such as hyperactivity, intelligence, and socioeconomic status (e.g., [3, 60]), these differences make it difficult to determine whether discrepancies across studies reflect genuine developmental variation or artifacts of design. In addition, most work has focused on callous–unemotional traits (e.g., [42, 47]), leaving the behavioral and interpersonal dimensions of psychopathy relatively understudied, despite evidence that they may show distinct associations with conduct problems (e.g., [6, 10, 15, 49, 50]). As a result, it remains unclear whether specific EF components, assessed in different ways, differentially mediate or moderate the links between distinct psychopathy dimensions and later conduct problems.

Given these gaps, the present study examines whether executive functions, assessed through both teacher report and performance-based cognitive tasks, mediate and/or moderate the association between early psychopathic traits and conduct problems five years later in a community sample. Specifically, we test two complementary models. In the mediation model, poorer executive functioning is expected to partially account for the association between early psychopathic traits and later conduct problems, consistent with the view that cognitive control difficulties form part of the developmental pathway linking dispositional risk to behavioral outcomes. In the moderation model, executive functioning is expected to condition this association, such that psychopathic traits are more strongly related to conduct problems among children with poorer EF. These hypotheses are examined separately for callous–unemotional, impulsivity–need for stimulation, and grandiose–deceitful traits, and across both performance-based and informant-rated EF measures.

The present study focuses primarily on cold EF, as these components are most reliably captured by performance-based tasks and teacher ratings and have been most consistently linked to externalizing behavior in community samples.

Methods

The present study uses data from a longitudinal ECLAT study (Problem behaviors in elementary school-aged children: The role of Executive funCtioning, individuaL, familiAl, and geneTic factors). Children and their parents were chosen through a multistage random sampling procedure (see [49] for details). The current study specifically utilizes data collected in wave 3 (T3) and wave 8 (T8) of the ECLAT study.

Participants

At Wave 3, 215 children attending 34 primary schools were assessed (53% girls; M = 8.21 years, SD = 0.62), and at Wave 8 the mean age was 13.67 years (SD = 0.59). Retention across waves was 100%, with the same parents (155 mothers, 60 fathers) participating at both time points. Most parents had completed secondary (mothers: 38.9%, fathers: 43.3%) or university education (mothers: 37.6%, fathers: 31.3%); fewer than 4% had not finished high school. The majority (78%) were employed, and 5% of fathers were retired war veterans. At Wave 3, 15% of parents were formally divorced or separated, although many children continued to reside in two-parent households. Three children had a psychiatric diagnosis of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD); no other diagnoses were reported.

Measures

All predictors and mediators were assessed at Wave 3, whereas the outcome was assessed five years later at Wave 8. Specifically, children completed performance-based executive function tasks at T3, lower-grade teachers rated executive functioning and hyperactivity at T3, and parents reported on psychopathic traits at T3. Conduct problems were assessed at T8 by higher-grade teachers. The two groups of teachers had no contact regarding the assessments, ensuring temporal separation and independence of informants across waves.

Control variables

Sociodemographic characteristics were assessed with items developed ad hoc for the project ECLAT. To this end, parents provided information on variables such as the child’s gender, age, and the family’s socioeconomic level (1 = significantly below average; 5 = significantly above average).

Intelligence was assessed using the Block Design subtest of the Croatian version of the WISC-IV (WISC-IV-HR; [62]), which has a strong correlation with full-scale IQ (r = 0.93 [24],). Total scores, with higher scores indicating higher IQ, were included in the analysis. No child scored below the 75th percentile.

Hyperactivity was assessed using the Croatian version of the Hyperactivity/Inattention subscale of the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ; [22, 59]), a five-item measure rated on a 3-point scale (0 = not true, 1 = somewhat true, 2 = certainly true). Independent Variable

Psychopathic Traits Psychopathic traits were assessed using the Croatian version of the 28-item Child Problematic Traits Inventory (CPTI; [8, 49]) designed to measure three interrelated factors labeled as Grandiose-Deceitful (GD), Callous-Unemotional (CU), and Impulsivity-Need for Stimulation (INS). Parents rated children on a four-point Likert scale ranging from (1) ‘‘Does not apply at all’’ to (4) ‘‘Applies very well’’, with higher scores indicating more problematic traits. The fit indices of the three-factor model suggested a satisfactory fit for the data for both boys, TLI = 0.95, CFI = 0.96, RMSEA = 0.060 (0.00-0.11), and girls, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.95, RMSEA = 0.061 (0.00-0.12). The CPTI showed high internal consistency (see Table 1).

Mediating/moderating variables

Executive functions were examined using both informant-reported and performance-based measures.

Teacher-reported executive functions were assessed using the Croatian version of the CHEXI [57, 59], which measures working memory, planning, inhibition, and regulation across 24 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = definitely not true to 5 = definitely true). Higher scores indicate greater EF difficulties. As in prior studies (e.g., [59], the four domains loaded onto two factors: Working Memory Deficits (working memory and planning,α = 0.93 and Inhibition Deficits (inhibition and regulation; α = 0.93. Model fit was good: χ2(1) = 1.802, p > 0.05, CFI = 0.99, TLI = 0.99, RMSEA = 0.057 (0.00-0.10). These factors were used in subsequent analyses. Correlation between the two domains was high, r = 0.59, p < 0.001.

Performance-based executive function tasks assessed five subdomains, namely working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility and sustained attention, and planning. The last four were computerized and administered using the Psychology Experiment Building Language (PEBL; [41]).

Verbal working memory was examined using the Digit Span subtest from the Croatian version of the WISC-IV-HR [62]. The total number of correct trials on both the forward and the backward condition was used as a measure of verbal working memory.

In line with previous studies (e.g., [5]), inhibition was assessed using a go/no-go task (GNG; [40]). Participants were required to respond to frequent “go” stimuli and to withhold responses to infrequent “no-go” stimuli across two phases. In Phase 1, children responded to blue shapes and withheld responses to red shapes. In Phase 2, they responded to squares and withheld responses to triangles, irrespective of color. The task comprised 60 trials with a 77% go-rate. Inhibitory control was indexed by the number of commission errors (responses to no-go stimuli,possible range = 0–14), with higher scores indicating poorer inhibitory control.

Cognitive flexibility and sustained attention were measured using the Berg’s Card Sorting Test–Short Form (BCST-64; [4]). Children sorted cards by color, shape, or number, with the correct sorting rule changing after ten consecutive correct responses across a total of 64 trials. Two error indices were derived. Perseverative errors (range: 0–64) reflect failures to shift to a new rule after feedback indicates that the sorting principle has changed and therefore index cognitive inflexibility. Non-perseverative errors (range: 0–64) represent incorrect responses that do not involve perseveration and are commonly interpreted as reflecting broader executive difficulties, particularly in sustained attention (i.e., the ability to maintain task focus over time,e.g., [25, 48]). In the present sample, perseverative and non-perseverative errors were not significantly correlated (r = − 0.05, p > 0.05), supporting their treatment as distinct EF components.

Planning ability was assessed using the Tower of London (TOL) [52]. Children had to reproduce a target arrangement of three disks by moving them from an initial to a final position using the fewest moves possible, following rules that allow only the top disk to be moved. The task includes 12 increasingly complex subtasks, requiring 2 to 5 moves. Planning was measured by the number of correctly solved subtasks (range: 0–12), with higher scores indicating better planning ability.

Consistent with our aim to examine specific relationships between distinct executive function (EF) components and psychopathic trait dimensions, EF was modeled at the component level rather than as a single latent construct. This approach was further supported by the low intercorrelations among EF tasks in the present sample, indicating that each task captured relatively independent cognitive processes. (e.g., r = 0.08 for inhibition-planning, r = 0.29 for cognitive flexibility-planning).

Outcome variable

Conduct problems were assessed using the Croatian version of the SDQ [22, 59], a five-item measure rated on a 3-point scale (0 = not true, 1 = somewhat true, 2 = certainly true). Higher scores indicate greater levels of behavioral problems. The scale demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency (see Table 1).

Procedure

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board. All parents and teachers received written information about the study procedures. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians prior to participation, and children provided verbal assent. Teachers who completed the questionnaires also provided informed consent.

Wave 3 represents a continuation of an ongoing cohort; participants were not newly recruited or advertised at this stage. All families had been enrolled in earlier waves of the ECLAT study and were contacted directly by the research team to schedule the laboratory assessment. No additional inclusion or exclusion criteria were applied at this wave; all cohort members were invited to participate. Parent–child dyads were seen individually in the department laboratory for the administration of performance-based tasks. All laboratory assessments were conducted within a restricted time window between July and September, approximately one year after the preceding wave.

Teachers, who were familiar with the children for at least one academic year, completed questionnaires sent to schools, with a 100% response rate. At Wave 8, teacher ratings of conduct problems were collected within a comparable, restricted time window, ensuring that assessments were not dispersed across an entire academic year. Families received modest compensation (toys, school supplies, or clothing; approximately €20 in value).

The 100% retention rate across the five-year period reflects both structural features of the Croatian school system, where children typically remain in the same primary school from Grades 1 to 8, and active retention strategies implemented by the research team (e.g., regular contact with families, holiday and birthday greetings, workshops for children, parents, and teachers, and institutional collaboration with schools). No families formally withdrew from the study, and complete teacher ratings were obtained for all participants at follow-up.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted in SPSS using Hayes’ PROCESS macro (version 4.2; [26]). Separate models were estimated for each psychopathic trait dimension, Callous–Unemotional (CU), Grandiose–Deceitful (GD), and Impulsivity–Need for Stimulation (INS), and for each executive function (EF) component. Two complementary analytic approaches were used.

First, simple mediation models (PROCESS Model 4) tested whether EF components accounted for the prospective association between psychopathic traits at T3 and conduct problems at T8. Indirect effects were evaluated using bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5,000 resamples. An indirect effect was considered significant when the 95% confidence interval did not include zero.

Second, simple moderation models (PROCESS Model 1) tested whether EF components altered the strength of the association between psychopathic traits and later conduct problems. Significant interactions were probed using simple slopes at low (− 1 SD), mean, and high (+ 1 SD) levels of the moderator. Interaction effects were interpreted as evidence that EF conditions when and for whom psychopathic traits translate into later conduct problems.

Across all models, child gender, IQ, and hyperactivity were included as covariates, based on their associations with key study variables and previous studies (e.g., [56, 63]). EF components were analyzed at the domain level rather than as a single latent construct, consistent with our theoretical aim to examine specificity and supported by the low intercorrelations among EF tasks in the present sample. Separate models were estimated for teacher-reported EF and for each performance-based EF task.

This analytic strategy allows EF to be examined both as a potential developmental pathway (mediation) and as a vulnerability or protective factor that shapes risk expression (moderation), aligning with the theoretical framework outlined in the Introduction.

Results

Descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations are presented in Table 1. Higher parent-reported psychopathic traits showed moderate bivariate correlations with teacher-reported conduct problems five years later. Four of the five performance-based EF subdomains (verbal working memory, inhibition, cognitive flexibility, sustained attention) were correlated with conduct problems. Male gender showed small positive correlations with hyperactivity and psychopathic trait dimensions.

Separate simple mediation and moderation were tested for each psychopathic trait dimension, namely callous-unemotional (CU), grandiose-deceitful (GD), and impulsivity-need for stimulation (INS) using the Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS (version 4.2; [26]). In all analyses, gender, IQ and levels of hyperactivity were included as covariates based on the correlation analysis (see Table 1).

We first examined whether deficits in working memory and planning, as well as inhibition and regulation, assessed through teacher reports, mediated or moderated the prospective association between parent-reported psychopathic traits and teacher-reported conduct problems five years later.

Simple mediation analyses indicated that among the three CPTI dimensions, only the Impulsivity-Need for Stimulation (INS) dimension exhibited significant indirect effects through executive function. Specifically, INS predicted greater inhibition and regulation deficits, R2 = 0.21, F(4, 157) = 10.47, p < 0.001, which, in turn, predicted higher levels of conduct problems five years later, R2 = 0.32, F(5, 156) = 14.67, p < 0.001. The indirect effect was significant, ab = 0.030, Sobel’s z = 2.98, p = 0.003, while the direct effect of INS remained significant, c′ = 0.062, p = 0.002, indicating partial mediation. No significant indirect effects emerged for working memory or planning deficits, and neither Callous-Unemotional (CU) nor Grandiose-Deceitful (GD) traits showed significant mediation through either teacher-reported executive functioning scale.

Simple moderation analyses (i.e., Model 1, Process 4.2) further revealed a significant interaction between INS and deficits in inhibition and regulation, R2 = 0.35, F(6, 155) = 13.76, p < 0.001, b = 0.005, p = 0.011. Discussion

The present study aimed to clarify the role of executive functioning (EF) in the link between early psychopathic traits and later conduct problems. Guided by two complementary hypotheses, we tested whether EF operates as part of the developmental pathway linking dispositional risk to later behavior (mediation), and/or as a vulnerability or protective factor that conditions this association (moderation). These hypotheses were examined separately for callous-unemotional (CU), impulsivity-need for stimulation (INS), and grandiose-deceitful (GD) traits, and across both performance-based and informant-rated EF measures.

Overall, the findings support both models, but in trait- and EF-specific ways. Executive functions emerged neither as a unitary deficit nor as a universal correlate of psychopathic traits. Instead, distinct EF components shaped how different psychopathic dimensions translated into later conduct problems. This pattern is consistent with prior work suggesting that psychopathy is not characterized by a generalized executive dysfunction [7],Maes & Brazil, 2013), and that EF deficits are selectively linked to specific trait expressions (e.g., [2, 7].

For parent-reported Impulsivity–Need for Stimulation (INS) traits, teacher-reported deficits in inhibition and regulation both mediated and moderated the association with later conduct problems. This indicates that inhibitory control is not merely correlated with impulsivity, but constitutes a central mechanism through which this dispositional risk becomes behaviorally expressed. Conceptually, this pattern should not be surprising. Decades of work in the ADHD literature have identified poor inhibition as a core mechanism underlying impulsive and disruptive behavior (e.g., [13]). From this perspective, INS traits may reflect a dispositional expression of the same self-regulatory vulnerability, and EF specifies the mechanism through which this risk unfolds developmentally. The present results extend this framework by showing that inhibition and regulation both transmit and amplify the behavioral impact of impulsive traits over time.

In contrast, performance-based EF tasks revealed a distinct pattern centered on CU traits. Sustained attention moderated the CU–conduct problem association, such that CU traits predicted later conduct problems primarily among children with poorer attentional control. This aligns with findings by Dotterer et al. [12], who showed that attentional processes differentiate how CU traits relate to antisocial behavior. At higher levels of CU, intact attention may facilitate more deliberate and goal-directed antisocial behavior, whereas attentional deficits may intensify dysregulated and reactive conduct [44]. In this sense, sustained attention appears to affect how CU traits are expressed behaviorally.

Working memory also moderated the CU-conduct problem link in a buffering pattern: CU traits were most strongly associated with conduct problems at lower working memory capacity, and the association weakened as working memory increased. This supports the view that EF can function as a protective factor that constrains the translation of dispositional risk into behavior [12], and aligns with research in children with ADHD showing that working memory supports impulse control [45], delay tolerance [43], and sustained attention and task engagement [38, 46]. Recent work further indicates that working memory and sustained attention contribute uniquely and interactively to goal-directed behavior [53], and are associated with multiple aspects of social functioning, including peer acceptance, physical and relational aggression, and conflict resolution skills [39]. In youth high in CU traits, stronger working memory may therefore support “cold” self-regulation, maintaining goals, anticipating consequences, and inhibiting immediate responses, even in the absence of emotional empathy. Conversely, weaker working memory may reduce the capacity to pause and update behavior, increasing vulnerability to persistent conduct problems. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that working memory supports the strategic maintenance of deception [1, 14], a pattern also linked to CU traits [23].

Cognitive inflexibility further emerged as a mediating mechanism for CU traits. Perseverative errors, an indicator of poor cognitive flexibility, partially accounted for the CU–conduct problem association, replicating findings in at-risk samples [2]. This pathway suggests that difficulty adapting behavior in response to feedback may contribute to the persistence of maladaptive conduct in youth high in CU traits [17, 18, 51, 55].

Taken together, these results refine developmental models of conduct problems by demonstrating that psychopathic traits do not confer risk through a single executive pathway. Instead, distinct EF mechanisms translate different dispositional vulnerabilities into maladaptive behavior. INS traits operate primarily through failures of inhibition and regulation, consistent with disinhibitory models of externalizing behavior [13, 44]. CU traits unfold through cognitive inflexibility and attentional control and are buffered by stronger working memory, suggesting a pathway in which “cold” executive control constrains whether affective deficits become behaviorally expressed. Developmental accounts must therefore move beyond unitary risk models and consider how specific trait profiles interact with specific executive capacities over time.

An important methodological implication concerns the divergence between teacher-rated and performance-based EF measures. Although both approaches nominally assess overlapping constructs (e.g., inhibition, working memory), they capture different levels of functioning. Informant ratings reflect self-regulation as it manifests in everyday contexts, integrating motivational, emotional, and situational demands [58]. Performance-based tasks, in contrast, index efficiency of cognitive control under structured and decontextualized conditions [37, 58]. Modest convergence between these approaches is therefore expected. In the present study, teacher-rated inhibition emerged as central for INS traits, whereas task-based attention and working memory were implicated for CU traits. This pattern suggests that impulsive traits are expressed primarily through observable failures of behavioral regulation, while CU-related risk is shaped by underlying neurocognitive control processes that may be less visible in daily interactions. The findings thus underscore that “EF” is not a unitary construct and that method matters for theory.

Several limitations warrant consideration. Psychopathic traits and EF were assessed concurrently at baseline. Although conduct problems were measured longitudinally, the temporal ordering between traits and EF within the mediation models cannot be established. The observed indirect effects should therefore not be interpreted as causal pathways in a strict sense. Psychopathic traits and EF difficulties may reflect reciprocal or shared developmental processes, including common neurobiological substrates (e.g., [7]). Future studies with repeated EF and trait assessments are needed to disentangle directionality. The community nature of the sample may also limit generalizability to clinical populations where traits are more extreme (e.g., [47]). Performance-based tasks primarily assessed lower-order EF and may not capture higher-order strategic control (e.g., [27, 29, 30]). Teacher reports may reflect classroom behavior rather than underlying cognition [16, 61]. Finally, null findings for GD traits may reflect unmeasured social-cognitive mechanisms (e.g., [54]).

The present study focuses primarily on cold EF, as these components are most reliably captured by performance-based tasks and teacher ratings and have been most consistently linked to externalizing behavior in community samples. Nevertheless, psychopathic traits, particularly those involving affective deficits, may also be associated with impairments in hot EF, and clarifying the relative contribution of these domains remains an important direction for future research.

These findings have direct implications for practice. They suggest that children high in impulsive traits may benefit most from interventions targeting inhibitory control and behavioral regulation, consistent with approaches used in ADHD. In contrast, youth high in CU traits may require support aimed at strengthening attentional control, cognitive flexibility, and working memory, thereby enhancing “cold” self-regulation even when affective motivation is limited. For clinicians and educators, this implies that psychopathic traits should not be treated as a homogeneous risk category. Instead, assessment and intervention should be tailored to the specific trait profile and the executive processes that shape how that profile is expressed behaviorally.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that executive functions shape the developmental expression of psychopathic traits in specific and dissociable ways. Impulsive traits unfold through failures of inhibition and regulation, whereas CU traits operate through attentional control, cognitive flexibility, and working memory. Executive functions thus serve both as pathways and as boundary conditions for risk. A key take-home message is that psychopathic traits do not translate into conduct problems by default. Whether and how they do so depends on the child’s executive capacities. Developmental models and interventions must therefore move beyond unitary risk frameworks and attend to the specific executive mechanisms through which dispositional vulnerabilities become behaviorally manifest.
