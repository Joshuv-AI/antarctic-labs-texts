Unraveling the quality-of-life network: how activities of daily living are embedded in the multidimensional quality of life after stroke
Authors: Weifei Yu, Yiqing Zhang, Lu Shi, Lulu Tong, Jingjing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuality of Life (QoL) after stroke is a multidimensional construct, yet its internal interactive architecture and how objective functional capacity, specifically Activities of Daily Living (ADL), embeds within this subjective network remain poorly understood.ObjectiveThis study aimed to delineate the interdependent network structure of post-stroke quality of life domains and to investigate the specific role of ADL within this system.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, a sample of 451 stroke survivors was recruited. Psychometric network analysis was conducted on the 12 domains of the Stroke-Specific Quality of Life (SS-QOL) scale to estimate the core QoL network. Subsequently, ADL was integrated as an external node to examine its precise connections. Centrality indices and bridge centrality were calculated to identify hubs and bridges. Edge-weight accuracy and centrality stability were assessed via bootstrap analysis.ResultsThe core 12-domain QoL network was stable and interpretable. Self-Care and Thinking emerged as the most central hubs (strength centrality: 1.24 and 1.16). Social Roles acted as a key bridge (highest betweenness centrality: 26). Crucially, ADL showed its strong direct association with Self-Care (edge weight = 0.169) and Mobility (edge weight = 0.207). This pattern shows that ADL is directly associated with these core functional domains, without being directly linked to more subjective domains like Mood or Social Roles.ConclusionsThis study provides a cross-sectional map of interdependencies within the post-stroke QoL system. Within this observed network, Self-Care emerged as a central and interconnected domain, thereby suggesting it as a candidate for future intervention research. The peripheral yet specialized connectivity of ADL suggests its effect on broader QoL is likely system-mediated. These hypotheses regarding directional influences and intervention efficacy require explicit testing in longitudinal or experimental studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12883-026-05191-y.

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide. Nearly 12 million new cases occur annually, with an estimated 94 million survivors living with post-stroke consequences [1]. China accounts for a disproportionate share of this burden: a nationwide surveillance programme reported 3.4 million incident stroke cases in adults ≥ 40 years in 2020 [2]. Post-stroke disability is highly prevalent, affecting approximately 70–80% of survivors in China with varying degrees of motor and other functional impairment [3]. The resulting deficits directly compromise neurological function and independent living, while also exerting a profound and persistent negative influence on health-related quality of life (QoL) across physical, psychological, and social domains [4, 5]. Addressing these consequences is a central concern in occupational therapy, which focuses on enabling participation in meaningful activities, including self-care, productivity, and leisure, as both a means and an outcome of recovery [6, 7].

Given this impact, improving QoL for stroke survivors stands as a central goal in rehabilitation science.Numerous studies have identified factors associated with post-stroke quality of life. Research on QoL determinants has predominantly employed traditional statistical methods like regression and factor analysis to isolate independent predictors or test predefined constructs [8, 9]. By treating variables as largely independent, however, these approaches struggle to model the complex, dynamic networks of interaction likely existing among the multiple physical, cognitive, emotional, and social dimensions underlying QoL [10, 11]. Univariate or bivariate approaches, while informative for isolated effects, may under-specify the complex web of domain-specific interactions underlying post-stroke QoL [12].

Activities of Daily Living (ADL) is consistently identified as one of the strongest objective correlates of overall quality of life (QoL) [13], justifying its primary status as a rehabilitation target. This established link, however, does not fully explain how ADL functions within the experiential landscape of survivors. Conventional analyses struggle to determine if ADL improvements directly and uniformly enhance all facets of QoL, or if instead their impact is channeled more selectively through specific, mediating subjective domains [14]. In occupational therapy and occupational science, the relationship between ADL and quality of life has long been a central focus, with theoretical models emphasizing that engagement in meaningful activities—including self-care—contributes to health and well-being [7, 15]. Empirical studies in stroke rehabilitation have consistently demonstrated that ADL performance is associated with various domains of quality of life, although the specific network architecture of these relationships remains underexplored.

Thus, a systemic perspective on the internal architecture of post-stroke quality of life remains underdeveloped. Specifically, a critical gap in the literature lies in understanding exactly how an objective functional measure such as Activities of Daily Living integrates into and subsequently influences the network of subjective experiences.

Psychometric network theory offers a powerful alternative. Grounded in a complex systems perspective, it posits that constructs like quality of life emerge not from a single latent trait, but from the direct, dynamic interactions between their observable dimensions [16]. This network represents a system of conditional dependencies that may hint at putative causal pathways. Network analysis operationalizes this perspective, allowing researchers to visualize and quantify the unique associations among all dimensions, which in turn helps identify the system’s core driving hubs and critical connecting bridges [17].

This study therefore employs psychometric network analysis to elucidate the interdependent structure of post-stroke quality of life and to clarify the embedded role of Activities of Daily Living within it. Our analysis pursues two specific objectives: first, to estimate the network structure linking the 12 core QoL domains in stroke survivors and to identify the most central hubs and bridges; second, to integrate ADL as an external node, quantifying its connection patterns to derive testable mechanistic pathways. Providing such a “network map” could inform the development of more targeted rehabilitation strategies. From an occupational therapy perspective, understanding how ADL embeds within the subjective QoL network could further inform client-centered interventions that address not only functional performance but also the patient’s perceived competence and satisfaction in daily activities [18]. Ultimately, this approach may help shift the focus.

Methods

Participants and procedure

Data were collected from the rehabilitation departments of three hospitals in Zhejiang Province, China. These comprised two tertiary public institutions which together serve a broad patient population spanning urban and rural areas, alongside a third private rehabilitation hospital. Using a convenience sampling approach, we recruited stroke survivors and their primary family caregivers across all three sites between October 20, 2024, and December 1, 2025. The final analytic sample consisted of 451 hospitalized stroke survivors.

Stroke survivors were eligible if they: (1) were diagnosed according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Major Cerebrovascular Diseases (2019); (2) were aged 18 years or older; and (3) were clinically stable—specifically, having no transfer to intensive care, no need for emergent interventions such as reintubation or vasopressor use, and no observed neurological deterioration in the 72 h prior to enrollment. Additionally, all participants were required to provide voluntary, informed consent. Exclusion criteria comprised a diagnosis of severe systemic disease (e.g., metastatic cancer), altered consciousness, or withdrawal of consent during the study period.

Sample size

A sample of 451 stroke survivors was used for the network analysis. Although estimation guidelines indicate that simpler networks may be reliably estimated with around 250 observations, more complex networks typically require a larger sample of approximately 500 to ensure stability and accuracy [19].With our sample of 451 falling between these two benchmarks, it is adequate for estimating the post-stroke QoL network. The reliability of the resulting estimates is further supported by subsequent stability analyses, which yielded a CS-coefficient above 0.50.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki. All methods were carried out following relevant guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained orally from all participants. Ethical approval to conduct this study was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Ningbo Medical Center LiHuili Hospital(No: KY2023SL257). The participants could participate in the study voluntarily and could withdraw if they wanted it at any time.

Measures and data collection

Data were gathered through structured questionnaires, with supplementary information extracted from medical records. To ensure consistency, all data collectors underwent standardized training covering the study’s purpose, ethical protocols, questionnaire content, communication techniques, and procedures for addressing participant queries. The assessment focused on several primary domains: the demographic profiles and clinical characteristics of stroke survivors; their activities of daily living (ADL) and quality of life.

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Demographic data were collected for both patients and caregivers. For patients, this included age, gender, marital status, educational attainment, occupation, monthly household income, and primary payment method for medical costs. Clinically, stroke severity was assessed using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) [20].

Activities of daily living

Activities of daily living (ADL) were assessed using the Barthel Index, a widely used 10-item instrument that measures functional independence in basic self-care activities such as feeding, bathing, dressing, and toileting [21]. The Barthel Index is a well-established measure with strong psychometric properties in stroke populations. Higher scores indicate greater functional independence. This instrument captures objective functional capacity, as assessed by healthcare professionals (e.g., physicians or nurses) in this study.

Patient quality of life

Health-related quality of life was measured with the Stroke-Specific Quality of Life Scale (SS-QOL) [22]. This scale consists of 50 items, including 49 items grouped into 12 domains: Energy, Family Roles, Language, Mobility, Mood, Personality, Self-care, Social Roles, Thinking, Upper Extremity Function, Vision, and Work/Productivity, plus one additional item assessing overall self-rated health. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (e.g., 1 = “Cannot do it at all” to 5 = “No difficulty at all”), where higher scores denote better QoL (certain items are reverse-scored). The scale demonstrated excellent overall reliability in our sample (Cronbach’s α = 0.98); the internal consistency of its individual domains, as reported in the literature, ranges from. 77 to. 95 [23]. In contrast, the SS-QOL Self-care domain captures perceived functional difficulty as a component of subjective quality of life, reported directly by the patient. The overlapping items between the Barthel Index and the SS-QOL Self-care domain are feeding/eating, bathing, dressing, grooming, and toileting.

Outcome variable definition

The primary outcomes for the core network analysis were the 12 domain scores of the Stroke-Specific Quality of Life (SS-QOL) scale: Energy, Family Roles, Language, Mobility, Mood, Personality, Self-care, Social Roles, Thinking, Upper Extremity Function, Vision, and Work/Productivity.These domains, representing the multidimensional construct of post-stroke QoL, constituted the nodes for estimating the psychometric network. The central aim was to model the interdependent structure among these specific dimensions.

The total score of the SS-QOL scale served as a secondary outcome for overall sample description and for validating the network structure against a traditional global measure. Activities of Daily Living (ADL)was included as a critical external variable. Its integration as an additional node in an extended network model allowed for the precise examination of how this established objective correlate embeds within the subjective QoL system.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0) for descriptive purposes. Demographic and clinical characteristics were collected for descriptive purposes and univariate subgroup comparisons. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or, for non-normally distributed data, as median with the 25th and 75th percentiles. Categorical variables are summarized with frequencies and percentages. They were not included as nodes or covariates in the primary psychometric network analysis.

Data were collected manually with on-site completeness checks, resulting in minimalmissing data (< 5% across all variables). Mean imputation was used to handle the few missing responses. The final dataset used for network analysis contained no missing values.

The core network analysis was implemented in R (version 4.5.1). To model the interrelationships among the 12 Stroke-Specific Quality of Life (SS-QOL) domains and their associations with Activities of Daily Living (ADL), we adopted a psychometric network approach. The procedure unfolded in two steps: initially, we estimated a network comprising the 12 QoL domains; ADL was then integrated as an external node, forming a final 13-node combined network for analysis.

Spearman’s rank correlation was used as the correlation estimator prior to network estimation, as it is appropriate for ordinal-level data (the SS-QOL uses a 5-point Likert scale). Network estimation then used the EBICglasso method (gamma = 0.5). This technique applies Graphical LASSO regularization and selects the optimal model via the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion, producing a sparse partial correlation network. In the network plots, nodes represent QoL domains or ADL, and edges represent the regularized partial correlations between them. Edge thickness and color saturation correspond to the strength of these associations.

All analyses were conducted using the bootnet (version 1.6), qgraph (version 1.9.8), and dplyr (version 1.1.4) packages in R (version 4.5.1). Centrality indices including strength, closeness, and betweenness were computed for each node to characterize its structural role. Strength identifies highly connected, influential hubs. Closeness reflects a node’s potential to influence the network (i.e., average proximity to all other nodes). Betweenness reflects its role as a bridge (i.e., the extent to which a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes). Limitations of these metrics include potential instability in smaller samples; however, bootstrap analysis confirmed acceptable stability in our sample (CS-coefficient > 0.5 for both metrics). Edge-weight and centrality stability were evaluated via case-dropping bootstrap procedures (1000 samples) using the bootnet package. The correlation stability coefficient (CS-coefficient) quantified the robustness of centrality indices. Consistent with methodological guidelines, a CS-coefficient > 0.25 was deemed acceptable, while a value > 0.50 indicated good stability for interpretation.

The final analytic sample thus comprised 451 participants.

The median QoL scores demonstrated a strong, graded decline across the three ADL function groups: 4.47 (IQR: 3.95–4.70) in the high-functioning group (BI 90–100), 3.76 (IQR: 3.33–4.10) in the moderately dependent group (BI 60–89), and 2.67 (IQR: 1.88–3.24) in the severely dependent group (BI < 60) (χ² = 197.65, p < .001). A similar inverse gradient was evident for neurological impairment severity as measured by the NIHSS (p < .001). No significant differences in QoL scores were found by gender or the presence of major vascular comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease; all p > .05).

Discussion

The core finding emerging from our network analysis is that Activities of Daily Living (ADL) embeds within the post-stroke quality-of-life network in a specific, indirect pattern. ADL connects directly and most strongly to Self-Care and Mobility, while forming no significant direct links to pivotal subjective domains like Mood or Social Roles. This structural configuration suggests that Self-Care and Mobility may be important intermediaries; associations between basic ADL and enhanced psychosocial well-being may be indirectly linked through these functional hubs.

Factors associated with quality of life in stroke survivors

Consistent with prior research, univariate analyses in our cohort confirmed significant associations between QoL and several demographic and clinical factors, including age, functional capacity, stroke severity, and socioeconomic status.

The cross-sectional analysis treated age as a categorical variable, revealing significant disparities in quality of life (QoL) across the defined age groups.The oldest participants (> 75 years) reported the lowest median QoL scores. While this finding does not test for a linear decline, it is consistent with established literature indicating that older stroke survivors tend to report lower QoL, a pattern potentially attributable to a higher cumulative burden of comorbidities, diminished physiological resilience, and increased psychosocial challenges [25, 26].

Significant QoL variations were also observed across categories of marital status, education, employment, and income. Married and employed participants, for instance, reported higher median scores than their widowed and unemployed counterparts. These findings are consistent with evidence linking stronger social and economic resources to better post-stroke health outcomes [24, 27].

In contrast, stroke severity (NIHSS score) and ADL function (ADL score) exhibited a clear, interpretable gradient. Here, the association reflects a distinct directional relationship: greater neurological impairment and greater dependency were each associated with progressively lower median QoL scores. This underscores, in line with extensive evidence, the central importance of functional independence for well-being after stroke [28, 29].

A network analysis of the post-stroke quality of life system

The analysis delineates a stable, interdependent architecture of post-stroke quality of life. In the estimated 12-domain network, Self-Care (S7) and Thinking (S9) emerged as nodes with the highest strength centrality. In the observed network, these domains show the highest connectivity, suggesting that they may be the most influential if they were to change. This hypothesis is consistent with intervention research: for example, a randomized feasibility trial by Song et al. (2021) demonstrated that a Tai Chi-based program yielded significant improvements specifically in the Self-Care and Thinking domains of stroke-specific quality of life [30]. Such evidence suggests that Self-Care and Thinking are not merely central statistical hubs but also viable and high-impact targets for therapeutic interventions.

Social Roles (S8) occupied a distinct bridging position, connecting functional recovery with psychological and social reintegration. This network-derived insight is corroborated by recent research. Qualitative analyses from stroke survivors, for instance, identify social participation and environmental access as fundamental facilitators for returning to everyday life, highlighting the practical importance of social roles [31]. Quantitatively, social support has been shown to enhance patient activation and self-management, serving as a key serial mediator in recovery pathways [32]. Together, these findings are consistent with the view that social support plays a central role, potentially helping to link functional gains with improved life quality.

The network’s strongest connections align with clinical understanding. The robust link between Upper Extremity Function (S10) and Self-Care (S7) (edge weight = 0.505) underscores the foundational role of arm and hand function in enabling basic independence, a relationship well-supported in rehabilitation literature [33]. Similarly, the strong tie between Personality (S6) and Mood (S5) reflects the close, bidirectional relationship between emotional state and perceived personality changes after stroke, a pattern consistent with longitudinal data showing these domains tend to follow parallel, deteriorating trajectories [34].

Although Language and Vision received the highest self-report scores, their peripheral network positions imply that improving these relatively preserved domains may offer limited leverage for system-wide QoL enhancement. Conceptually, this aligns with empirical evidence indicating that non-motor deficits (e.g., hemianopia, aphasia) have a substantially smaller impact on generic QoL measures compared to motor deficits like limb paresis, which account for a greater share of variance [35]. Therefore, while addressing language and vision remains important for patient-centered care, they may not represent the most efficient therapeutic targets for broadly improving the QoL system.

Activities of daily living as a functional anchor in the quality of life network

Integrating the objective ADL measure into the subjective QoL network clarifies how basic functional capacity influences the broader recovery experience. ADL did not emerge as a broadly connected hub but rather functioned as a specialized “functional anchor.”

Its strongest direct connections were with Self-Care (S7) and Mobility (S4). This pattern aligns with the fundamental conceptual structure of ADL itself, classically comprising self-care activities and functional mobility [36]. Standardized clinical assessments like the Barthel Index operationally reflect this structure, itemizing these two core domains. Our network analysis thus demonstrates an association between basic ADL and a patient’s perceived capability in self-care and mobility within the QoL system.

Conversely, the absence of significant direct links from ADL to central psychological and social domains (Mood, Social Roles) suggests that its association with overall well-being may be largely indirect [37]. Empirical mediation models support this interpretation. For example, baseline ADL disability in stroke patients has been shown to exert a significant indirect effect on overall quality of life through depressive symptoms [38]. This corroborates our network inference: ADL likely influences mood and social participation indirectly via its strong connections to Self-Care and Mobility [39]. A regained ability in self-care (facilitated by ADL improvement) may bolster self-esteem and alleviate depressive symptoms [40], while improved mobility can enable community engagement, thereby enhancing social role fulfillment [41].

A key quantitative insight arises from the centrality metrics in the integrated network. “Self-Care” (S7) held the highest strength centrality (1.283), whereas the “ADL” node ranked ninth (0.769). This contrast indicates that while objective ADL forms the functional foundation, it is the patient’s subjective experience of self-care that occupies the most central and influential position within the lived experience of quality of life. Nevertheless, alternative explanations for the absence of direct ADL links with Mood or Social Roles include measurement differences (objective ADL vs. self-reported Mood) and stroke-related reporting variability (e.g., anosognosia, cognitive impairment). These factors should be considered when interpreting our findings.

This finding resonates with a growing consensus in rehabilitation. Research notes that healthcare professionals may underestimate the symptom severity reported by stroke patients [42], whose self-reported experiences are often more intense [43]. Critically, the patient’s perceived self-care ability has been identified as a central factor in managing chronic conditions [44]. Our network analysis, by demonstrating that the subjective experience of self-care occupies a more central position than objective ADL function within the QoL system, provides robust empirical support for this perspective. It thereby challenges a rehabilitation paradigm that overly relies on objective functional indicators as a “gold standard,” and instead underscores the pivotal, systemic role of subjective appraisal in recovery.

Our findings build upon and extend the occupational therapy and occupational science literature. Foundational models such as the Person-Environment-Occupation (PEO) framework and the Model of Human Occupation (MOHO) have long theorized that engagement in meaningful activities (including self-care) promotes health and well-being through the interplay of volition, habituation, and environmental context [7, 45]. While these frameworks posit a positive relationship between occupational performance and quality of life, the specific network architecture of these relationships, particularly the distinction between objective functional capacity and perceived difficulty, has received limited empirical attention in the occupational therapy literature [46]. The present study addresses this gap by providing a quantitative, data-driven map that could inform client-centered intervention targeting.

Conclusion and clinical implications

This network analysis delineates an interdependent architecture of post-stroke quality of life, characterized by central hubs, a functional anchor, and critical bridges. The findings translate into a roadmap for a more precise, systems-informed rehabilitation strategy.

Clinically, this suggests rehabilitation should firmly prioritize improving basic Activities of Daily Living as the foundational objective. Crucially, therapy must then consciously work to translate those functional gains into the patient’s enhanced subjective perception of “self-care capability” and “mobility mastery,” thereby activating the core hubs of Self-Care and Mobility within their lived experience. Interventions should concurrently and proactively aim to rebuild Social Roles, leveraging this key bridge to transmute functional progress into broader psychosocial well-being. Given the indirect linkage between ADL and Mood via Self-Care (edge = 0.169), adjuvant mood monitoring during ADL-based rehabilitation appears clinically reasonable based on these associative findings.

Strategically targeting high-leverage network nodes may amplify clinical impact, though this hypothesis requires testing in future intervention studies. Priority targets include Self-Care, Mobility, and Social Roles. This approach may foster more efficient and holistic recovery, ultimately improving life quality after stroke.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although the network reveals stable associative structures, the directionality of influence among domains remains unclear. Second, all participants were recruited from hospitals within a single region. While the sample size supports stable estimation, the findings may not generalize to stroke populations with differing cultural, socioeconomic, or healthcare contexts. Third, the Barthel Index (ADL) and the Self-care domain of the SS-QOL both assess basic self-care activities, although they capture different aspects (objective capacity vs. perceived difficulty). Their conceptual proximity may contribute to the observed association between these two nodes. Fourth, alternative explanations for the observed ADL-Self-care edge cannot be ruled out. For example, shared method variance (both measures rely on functional assessments) or unmeasured confounders (e.g., overall illness severity) may partly account for this association. Fifth, stroke-specific factors such as lesion location (hemispheric differences), anosognosia (altered awareness of deficits), time since stroke, and the absence of proxy reports may affect self-report validity and network generalizability. These factors warrant consideration when interpreting our findings.

Future studies should use instruments with clearer domain differentiation, include objective clinical measures, and employ longitudinal designs to test the directional hypotheses generated by this cross-sectional network analysis.
