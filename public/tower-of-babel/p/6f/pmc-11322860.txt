Comparison of EV characterization by commercial high‐sensitivity flow cytometers and a custom single‐molecule flow cytometer
Authors: James Kim, Shihan Xu, Seung‐Ryoung Jung, Alya Nguyen, Yuanhua Cheng, Mengxia Zhao, Bryant S Fujimoto, Wyatt Nelson, Perry Schiro, Jeffrey L Franklin, James N Higginbotham, Robert J Coffey, Min Shi, Lucia N Vojtech, Florian Hladik, Muneesh Tewari, John Tigges, Ionita Ghiran, Tijana Jovanovic‐Talisman, Louise C Laurent, Saumya Das, Olesia Gololobova, Kenneth W Witwer, Tuoye Xu, Al Charest, Kendall Van Keuren Jensen, Robert L Raffai, Jennifer C Jones, Joshua A Welsh, John P Nolan, Daniel T Chiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh‐sensitivity flow cytometers have been developed for multi‐parameter characterization of single extracellular vesicles (EVs), but performance varies among instruments and calibration methods. Here we compare the characterization of identical (split) EV samples derived from human colorectal cancer (DiFi) cells by three high‐sensitivity flow cytometers, two commercial instruments, CytoFLEX/CellStream, and a custom single‐molecule flow cytometer (SMFC). DiFi EVs were stained with the membrane dye di‐8‐ANEPPS and with PE‐conjugated anti‐EGFR or anti‐tetraspanin (CD9/CD63/CD81) antibodies for estimation of EV size and surface protein copy numbers. The limits of detection (LODs) for immunofluorescence and vesicle size based on calibration using cross‐calibrated, hard‐dyed beads were ∼10 PE/∼80 nm EV diameter for CytoFLEX and ∼10 PEs/∼67 nm for CellStream. For the SMFC, the LOD for immunofluorescence was 1 PE and ≤ 35 nm for size. The population of EVs detected by each system (di‐8‐ANEPPS+/PE+ particles) differed widely depending on the LOD of the system; for example, CellStream/CytoFLEX detected only 5.7% and 1.5% of the tetraspanin‐labelled EVs detected by SMFC, respectively, and median EV diameter and antibody copy numbers were much larger for CellStream/CytoFLEX than for SMFC as measured and validated using super‐resolution/single‐molecule TIRF microscopy. To obtain a dataset representing a common EV population analysed by all three platforms, we filtered out SMFC and CellStream measurements for EVs below the CytoFLEX LODs as determined by bead calibration (10 PE/80 nm). The inter‐platform agreement using this filtered dataset was significantly better than for the unfiltered dataset, but even better concordance between results was obtained by applying higher cutoffs (21 PE/120 nm) determined by threshold analysis using the SMFC data. The results demonstrate the impact of specifying LODs to define the EV population analysed on inter‐instrument reproducibility in EV flow cytometry studies, and the utility of threshold analysis of SMFC data for providing semi‐quantitative LOD values for other flow cytometers.Keywords: CellStream, CytoFLEX, equivalent reference fluorophore calibration beads, extracellular vesicles, limit of detection, single‐molecule flow cytometry

INTRODUCTION

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are cell‐secreted lipid bilayer‐enclosed nanoparticles that contain proteins and nucleic acids from their cell of origin and can elicit functional changes in nearby or distant cells (Théry et al., 2002; Van Den Boorn et al., 2013). EVs play a key role in intercellular communication in normal and disease physiology (Yates et al., 2021), are present in a wide variety of accessible biofluids, and contain molecular cargo that are potentially useful as biomarkers for early disease detection via liquid biopsy (Wang et al., 2017). However, progress in EV biomarker discovery has been inhibited by the inability to detect the full distribution of EVs in biological samples, by the use of poorly standardized assays that lack proper calibration, and by lack of reporting of limits of detection (LODs). Flow cytometry is a promising approach to EV analysis because it can provide multi‐parameter data at single EV resolution with high specificity and throughput, but conventional flow cytometers lack the sensitivity to detect many EVs, which can be as small as 30 nm in diameter (Vlassov et al., 2012). Efforts to improve the sensitivity of flow cytometers—by using more sensitive detectors, adding reduced wide‐angle forward scatter/medium‐angle light scatter collection, using higher‐power lasers, and reducing sample and sheath flow rates—have produced higher‐sensitivity flow cytometers with light scatter LOD for EVs down to ∼100 nm, although there is considerable debate on the models and assumptions used to generate such estimates (Brittain et al., 2019; Van Der Pol et al., 2021).

Several groups, including ours, have demonstrated fluorescence‐based EV detection (Andronico et al., 2021; Arraud et al., 2016; Stoner et al., 2016; Van Der Vlist et al., 2012), which has improved sensitivity. In addition, efforts to improve the standardization of EV assays (e.g., by using appropriate fluorescence and light scatter calibration standards, such as NIST traceable beads) and the standardization of reporting of EV flow cytometry results (Welsh, Van Der Pol, Arkesteijn, et al., 2020) led by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV), the International Society for Thrombosis and Hemostasis (ISTH), and the International Society for the Advancement of Cytometry (ISAC), have yielded concordant EV data across flow cytometry platforms independent of instrument settings and flow rate (Welsh, Jones & Tang, 2020; Welsh, Van Der Pol, Arkesteijn, et al., 2020; Welsh et al., 2024). However, many EVs are below the LODs of even the highest sensitivity commercial flow cytometers. Furthermore, lack of calibration and failure to report LODs in EV flow cytometry studies remain common.

In previous work, we have developed a single‐molecule flow cytometer (SMFC) with 100% single‐fluorophore detection efficiency for a variety of bright dyes including phycoerythrin (PE) and Alexa Fluor 647, together with methods for multi‐parameter EV characterization in terms of EV size, concentration and surface protein copy numbers (Andronico et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021; Jung et al., 2018; Mutch et al., 2007, 2011; Schiro et al., 2007). We first developed a statistical method for analysing photon spikes generated by each fluorophore (Schiro et al., 2007), and methods for quantifying the number of fluorophores on nanoparticles by deconvolving the fluorescence intensity distributions of single nanoparticles and free fluorophores (Jung et al., 2018; Mutch et al., 2007, 2011). We then developed methods for vesicle sizing based on the fluorescence intensity of a membrane dye (Andronico et al., 2021). We applied this set of methods to the multi‐parameter characterization of EVs, and validated the methods by comparing results with super‐resolution microscopy and cryo‐electron microscopy (Jiang et al., 2021).

This SMFC differs significantly from commercial high‐sensitivity flow cytometers not only in its optical system and sensitivity, but also in the methods used for calibration and measurement of EV concentration, size and cargo. For example, for multi‐channel / multi‐parameter EV analyses, commercial flow cytometers often employ bead‐based methods for calibration. These calibrators can take several forms: (1) The gold standard is calibration of detected fluorescence from a particular fluorophore using a reference surface‐stained or hard‐dyed bead with quantitated numbers of that fluorophore (MESF (molecules of equivalent soluble fluorophore) calibration) (Schwartz et al., 1998). (2) Ergonomic applications often employ a pre‐calibrated bead whose fluorescence is from a broad‐spectrum excitation and emission dye that has been calibrated to an equivalent intensity unit of the fluorophore being detected (ERF calibration) (Wang et al., 2016). ERF (equivalent reference fluorophore) calibration has also been cross‐calibrated to units of surface area from extrapolation of membrane dye intensity for a lipid vesicle reference material of known size (Tekkatte et al., 2023). (3) Antibody capture beads with a quantitated number of binding sites per bead can be used to capture the detection antibody being utilized in an assay to account for the fluorophore‐to‐protein (F/P) ratio (Schwartz et al., 1994). Calibration also enables estimation of an instrument's LODs in terms of number of antibody‐conjugated fluorophore molecules and vesicle size. EVs dimmer or smaller than these LODs may not be detected or are excluded from analysis (e.g., when defining EVs as membrane dye+/PE‐anti‐tetraspanin+ particles). To accurately compare EV measurements across platforms or labs, it is necessary to specify the relevant LODs and the resulting EV population analysed.

In contrast, the SMFC does not use calibration beads, but instead determines the copy number of an antibody conjugated to PE or another bright fluorophore used for labelling EV cargo by internal calibration in which the fluorescence intensity distribution of residual unbound dye‐tagged antibodies in the EV sample is used to deconvolve the fluorescence intensity from individual EVs to determine the absolute number of antibodies on each EV (Jiang et al., 2021; Jung et al., 2018; Mutch et al., 2007, 2011). Similarly, the SMFC can determine the F/P ratio (number of dyes per antibody) via comparison of fluorescence intensity distributions of dye‐Ab conjugates and unbound dyes. This method has been validated by comparing estimated EV surface protein copy numbers with results obtained by super‐resolution imaging (Jiang et al., 2021). The SMFC uses external calibration for measurement of EV size, but employs the EV sample itself rather than beads for this calibration, which involves comparing the fluorescence intensity distributions of EVs from both SMFC and super‐resolution imaging (e.g., dSTORM) (Andronico et al., 2021, Jung et al., 2023), and is performed once for each sample type or membrane/dye combination. The SMFC achieves 100% single‐fluorophore detection efficiency for a variety of bright dyes, including Alexa Fluor 647 and PE, with the entire distribution of photon burst intensities from unbound dyes or attached to antibodies in solution well above the detection threshold of SNR (signal‐to‐noise ratio) = 3 (determined using a buffer‐only sample).

Here we investigated the effects of instrument sensitivity and calibration methods on the characterization of EVs by using two commercial high‐sensitivity flow cytometers—CytoFLEX by Beckman Coulter and CellStream by Cytek Biosciences—and the SMFC system to characterize identical (split) EV samples derived from human colorectal cancer (DiFi) cells in terms of EV concentration, size distribution and copy number distributions of the surface protein markers epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and the tetraspanins CD9, CD63 and CD81. The results highlight the importance of calibration and measurement of LODs to define the EV population being analysed for comparisons of flow cytometry results across platforms or labs, and demonstrate the potential utility of the SMFC to provide semi‐quantitative LOD values for vesicle immunofluorescence and size for flow cytometers used in EV studies. The SMFC can also be used to cross‐calibrate nanobeads in terms of number of fluorophores per bead.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of EVs from DiFi cell conditioned medium

Human colorectal cancer (DiFi) cells (RRID: CVCL_6895 (SciCrunch | Research Resource, Resolver), a colorectal cancer cell line characterized by the Robert Coffey laboratory at Vanderbilt University) were cultured in a hollow fibre bioreactor with DMEM containing the defined additive CDM‐HD (FiberCell Systems, New Market, MD) at 37°C and 5% CO2 as described previously (Laplante et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2022, 2023). EVs were obtained from cell conditioned medium by centrifuging a cell suspension for 10 min at 250 × g, then centrifuging the supernatant for another 10 min at 2500 × g with 0.22‐mm polyether‐sulfone filtration (Nalgene, Rochester, NY) to remove cellular debris and larger vesicles. The filtrate was concentrated with a 100k Centricon Plus‐70 concentrator (Millipore) followed by ultracentrifugation at 165,000 × g for 6 h to pellet EVs (Zhang et al., 2022). Purified EVs were stored at 4°C until further use. One EV sample prepared as described above was used for all subsequent labelling and analysis.

Labelling EVs with PE‐conjugated antibodies and the membrane dye di‐8‐ANEPPS

Purified EVs were resuspended in phosphate buffered saline (PBS), separated into five aliquots of 1 µL at 1 × 1012 EV/mL, and labelled with either a phycoerythrin (PE; Invitrogen, P801)‐conjugated mouse anti‐human IgG1 monoclonal antibody against EGFR (clone AY13; BioLegend, 352904), or with a mixture of PE‐conjugated mouse anti‐human IgG1 monoclonal antibodies against the tetraspanins CD9 (clone HI9a; AAT Bioquest, 10090180), CD63 (clone H5C6; BioLegend, 353015) and CD81 (clone 5A6; BioLegend, 349505), at a 1:1 volume ratio of EV suspension to antibody solution, for 1 h at room temperature in the dark, using minimum saturating concentrations of each antibody (10 µg/mL for anti‐CD9, ‐CD63 and ‐EGFR antibodies and 12 µg/mL for anti‐CD81) determined by titration (Figure S1). The EV‐antibody mixture was then diluted to 200 µL with PBS and incubated with the membrane dye di‐8‐ANEPPS (Thermo Fisher, D3167) at 0.1 µM for 2 h. Labelled EVs were purified from unbound antibodies and dyes with a 35‐nm qEV size exclusion column (IZON Science, New Zealand), and were split into two samples for comparison of results between platforms. The EV labelling steps were performed twice: once for comparison of results between the SMFC and CytoFLEX, and a second time for comparison of results between the SMFC and CellStream.

Calibration, LOD determination and EV characterization for CytoFLEX and CellStream

Overview

To calibrate the CytoFLEX and CellStream instruments for EV characterization and to characterize instrument performance for all three systems, we used the Cellarcus Vesicle Flow Cytometry (vFC) assay (Vesicle Flow Cytometry with the, CytoFLEX), which includes calibrators and standards for instrument performance characterization and calibration. Two channels were used in each platform for excitation and collection of fluorescence signals from EVs; the three platforms used the same excitation wavelengths for di‐8‐ANEPPS (488 nm) and PE (561 nm), but different emission filters. The emission filters used for di‐8‐ANEPPS were 690/50 nm (central wavelength/bandwidth) for CytoFLEX, 702/87 nm for CellStream, and 600/50 nm for SMFC; the filters used for PE were 585/42 nm for CytoFLEX, 583/24 nm for CellStream, and 609/42 nm for SMFC.

Gating and calibration using calibration beads

For calibration of measurements of PE copy numbers and EV size and determination of LODs for immunofluorescence and size for the CytoFLEX and CellStream, we used vCal nanoRainbow beads (Cellarcus Biosciences, CA, CBS6‐1000). These beads are hard‐dyed and consist of four populations of 500‐nm beads that have been cross‐calibrated against external MESF, antibody binding capacity (ABC), and vesicle size standards in stoichiometric units of ‘molecules of equivalent soluble fluorophore’ (MESF) for PE and ‘molecules of equivalent surface area’ (MESA) for vFRed, which has similar properties as di‐8‐ANEPPS. Three of the bead populations are bright and are used for calibration, while a dim bead population is used for determination of resolution by comparison with a blank. Gating was used to exclude fluidic anomalies and background events while selecting events with fluorescence and light scattering properties characteristic of the calibration beads (Figures S2–S3). The vCal nanoRainbow beads were introduced into the CytoFLEX or CellStream instrument with the primary threshold set as a violet side scatter (VSSC) of 100,000 for CytoFLEX, or a forward scatter (FSC) of 2000 and side scatter (SSC) of 5000 for CellStream. Median fluorescence intensities (MFI) of the beads in the two channels were measured (Figure 1a, d) and the MFI values of the three bright beads were used to generate calibration curves—MFI (PE) versus MESF and MFI (di‐8‐ANEPPS) versus MESA (Figure 1b, c, e, f)—using FCS Express software (De Novo Software, CA). Spectral calibration to measure and account for spectral spillover between detector channels was performed using FCS Express with vFRed‐stained Lipo100 (Cellarcus, CBS1) and anti‐TS PE antibody‐stained nanoCal beads (Cellarcus, CBS7M) for the CellStream or QuantiBrite PE beads (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, Cat# 340495) for the CytoFlex as single stained controls and scatter noise‐triggered buffer as a background signal.

LODs

The CytoFLEX and CellStream LODs for PE dyes were determined at 95% confidence by using the PE channel MFI value at two robust standard deviations (rSD) above the MFI of the background noise (buffer only) using the calibration curves shown in Figures 1b and e. The CytoFLEX and CellStream vesicle size LODs were determined at 95% confidence by using the vFRed channel MFI value at two rSD above the MFI of the background noise (buffer only) using the calibration curves shown in Figures 1c and f. The CytoFLEX and CellStream LODs of immunofluorescence and size were determined on 5 different days (Table S1).

EV characterization

EVs labelled with PE‐conjugated antibodies and di‐8‐ANEPPS were loaded into the CellStream or CytoFLEX instrument, and fluorescence data were acquired using CytExpert (CytoFLEX) or CellStream acquisition software. Gating was used to exclude fluidic anomalies and background events while selecting events with fluorescence and light scattering properties characteristic of EVs (Figures S4–S5). For CytoFLEX, the vFRed‐H channel (threshold = 1900) was used to trigger detection; this intensity value corresponded to EVs with an ∼80‐nm diameter based on the bead‐assigned values. For CellStream, no primary trigger was used and vesicle gating was set post‐experiment following the Cellarcus vFC assay protocol. The generated data were analysed with FCS Express to report EV concentration and size and antibody copy number distributions. The CytoFLEX and CellStream configurations used are shown in Table S2.

Calibration, determination of LODs and EV characterization for the SMFC system

The SMFC system

The SMFC system has been described previously (Jiang et al., 2021). Briefly, the system consists of a line‐confocal optical system that uses light sheet illumination, a planar microfluidic sample chip with a high numerical aperture objective, and single photon detectors (avalanche photodiodes) to achieve single‐fluorophore sensitivity. Before sample analysis, the sample‐loading channel in the microfluidic chip was vacuumed and primed with flow buffer (PBS + 0.1% bovine serum albumin). A 10 µL sample was then introduced into the inlet reservoir and flow was initiated. DiFi EVs labelled with PE‐antibodies and with di‐8‐ANEPPS were then loaded, and fluorescence signals were measured in two channels: (1) ex:488 nm, em:600/50 nm for collection of di‐8‐ANEPPS emission; and (2) the PE channel (ex:561 nm, em:609/42 nm) for collection of emission from PE‐antibodies. Photon traces were analysed with an in‐house MATLAB program; a signal was assigned as an EV when a PE fluorescence peak co‐occurred within the expected time‐window with a di‐8‐ANEPPS peak, based on the flow rate. The flow rate of samples in the channel was calculated based on the delay time between detection in two laser lines and the geometric dimensions of the channel.

Limit of detection

The LOD of the PE channel was determined by calculating SNR = 3 as three times the average background signal from buffer (blue dashed line in the left plot in Figure 2a) and plotting the distribution of photon burst intensities from single PE molecules. The entire distribution fell above SNR = 3 (Figure 2b), indicating 100% single‐molecule detection efficiency.

Determination of antibody‐labelled surface protein copy numbers

The SMFC methods and algorithm for determining the copy number distributions of antibody‐labelled EV surface proteins have been described previously. Briefly, the method employs an internal calibration method that uses the fluorescence intensity distribution of unbound dye‐antibody conjugates to deconvolve the fluorescence intensity distributions of single EVs to determine the number of antibody‐dye conjugates bound per EV. PE‐antibody copy numbers provide good estimates of tetraspanin (TS) or EGFR copy numbers due to the use of saturating concentrations of antibodies (Figure S1) and assuming one antigen bound per antibody. This method for determining PE‐antibody copy numbers takes into consideration the environmental effect on the fluorescence intensity distribution during the fitting process, providing a precise determination of copy number distribution. The accuracy of copy numbers obtained using these methods has been confirmed previously using a model system (number of bound biotin molecules to a single streptavidin protein) (Jiang et al., 2021; Mutch et al., 2011) and with super‐resolution microscopy of EVs (Jiang et al., 2021). We acknowledge the limitation that the number of bound antibodies might not match the true copy number of the biomarkers/proteins present on EVs. Such mismatch can be caused by poor antibody affinity for the protein (we selected the highest affinity antibodies available for each target), by lack of binding by a particular antibody due to glycosylation of a protein, or by target protein dimers or clusters that prevent the binding of multiple antibodies due to crowding. Nevertheless, the use of identical antibody reagents and an identical sample allowed rigorous comparison of results across platforms.

EV sizing

The fluorescence intensity of channel 1 (di‐8‐ANEPPS emission) was converted to vesicle size based on a calibration of EV size measurements using direct stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (dSTORM) imaging (Figure 2d). These EV sizing methods have been described previously and were validated by using cryo‐EM (Andronico et al., 2021; Jung et al., 2023). EV sizing is performed once for each sample type or each membrane/dye combination. EVs are labelled with a membrane dye that is highly fluorescent only after intercalation, such as di‐8‐ANEPPS.

EV concentration

The SMFC can detect every fluorophore and fluorophore‐labelled particle that flows through the channel. We extract the linear velocity of each particle which together with the channel cross‐section allows determination of volumetric flow rate. Absolute particle concentration is calculated as the number of particles counted divided by the flow volume analysed.

Threshold analysis

The SMFC data were used to estimate the LOD values for CellStream and CytoFLEX by filtering the SMFC data using a range of LOD cutoffs to determine the cutoffs that yielded the best agreement between SMFC and CellStream or CytoFLEX TS and EGFR antibody copy numbers (also referred to as ‘PE copy numbers’). The threshold analysis methods were as follows: (1) PE intensity and di‐8‐ANEPPS intensity data from SMFC were converted to PE copy numbers and EV sizes using the methods described above. (2) The SMFC EV data were sorted based on size, and all EVs below a given LOD cutoff were excluded. (3) The remaining SMFC EV data were sorted based on PE copy number, and all EVs below a given LOD were excluded.

Controls for assay sensitivity and specificity

We used vFC assay controls based on ISEV guidelines (Vesicle Flow Cytometry with the, CytoFLEX) to establish assay sensitivity and EV specificity for all three platforms (Figures S7–S9). Buffer‐only controls were used to establish the background signal levels in the absence of EVs that define sensitivity. A detergent control (0.1% Triton X‐100 in PBS) was used to verify the vesicular nature of detected particles. An isotype control (DiFi EVs subjected to the same labelling reaction but with a PE‐conjugated mouse IgG1 Kappa antibody isotype lacking specificity for EGFR or TS) was used to measure the signal from non‐specific flow cytometry interactions. A dilution control was used to establish that the assay is measuring single particles and to establish assay linearity (Figure S9).

Orthogonal measurement of EV antibody copy numbers by single‐molecule TIRF microscopy

Orthogonal measurement of EV‐bound antibody copy numbers was performed by single‐molecule total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy. Briefly, DiFi EV samples were labelled with a mixture of PE‐conjugated antibodies against CD9, CD63 and CD81 or against EGFR, and with the membrane dye MemBright700 (Idylle, Inc., cat. # MCO‐MEM‐700‐1902) instead of di‐8‐ANEPPS to avoid photon crosstalk into the PE channel. After IZON filtering, EVs were attached to a plasma‐cleaned glass surface for imaging. For PE, a 488‐nm laser was used for excitation and a 560/50 nm bandpass filter was used for emission. For MemBright700, a 640‐nm laser was used for excitation and a 680/40 nm bandpass filter was used for emission. To calculate antibody copy numbers, the intensities of PE antibody signals from individual EVs were normalized using the median value of unbound PE‐antibodies.

Sizing EVs by dSTORM imaging

To measure EV size distributions, we used dSTORM imaging with MemBright700 because this lipid dye has higher photon number and optimal blinking events than di‐8‐ANEPPS. To gate out non‐EV particles produced by MEM700 aggregates, we used TS‐positive signals. Images were acquired in oxygen scavenger buffered solution (1% glucose, pH 8 Tris‐HCl, pyranose oxidase [7.5 U/mL = ∼0.8 mg/mL], and catalase [0.2 mg/mL = 400−1000 U/mL]) containing 2‐mercaptoethylamine (MEA, 100 mM) for reducing the dyes. Before acquiring blinking events from MEM700, we acquired TS fluorescence imaging to map EVs at a lower laser power, and used this mapping image as a reference to achieve colocalization with MEM700 signals to gate out non‐EV particles generated by MEM700 aggregates (Figure S10). dSTORM images of MEM700 were acquired using STORM microscopy in epi‐mode (NA 1.45, 100× magnification lens, pixel size = 76 nm, sCMOS camera). Images were analysed with ThunderSTORM in ImageJ. For sizing, a normal Gaussian function was used to fit summed intensity profiles of single molecules. Vesicle size was defined as the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the Gaussian function, and size data were corrected by localization error induced by intrinsic properties such as emitted photon number and shape of point spread function of single fluorophores as described previously.

RESULTS

The EV population analysed by each platform

DiFi EVs were labelled with the membrane dye di‐8‐ANEPPS and with either PE‐anti‐EGFR antibody or a mixture of PE‐anti‐CD9, PE‐anti‐CD63 and PE‐anti‐CD81 antibodies to label these three TS, and the washed samples were analysed using the three platforms. This labelling scheme is not sufficient to identify all EVs in the sample. For example, it would not be possible to distinguish between large ANEPPS aggregates and EVs that lack EGFR, CD9, CD63 and CD81. Nevertheless, it allows comparisons of detected EV populations between platforms using the same set of antibodies and membrane dye. The concentrations, size distributions, and total TS and EGFR antibody copy number distributions of the EVs detected and analysed by each system are shown in Figure 3 for the TS‐labelled sample and in Figure 4 for the EGFR‐labelled sample. The concentrations of TS‐labelled EVs (di‐8‐ANEPPS+/PE+ particles) detected by the three platforms were 5.5 ± 2.5 × 108 EVs/mL (mean ± SD; n = 7 experimental replicates on the same day) for SMFC, 2.6 ± 1.2 × 107 EVs/mL for CellStream (5.7% of the SMFC value), and 7.5 ± 3.1 × 106 EVs/mL (1.5% of the SMFC value) for CytoFLEX. The concentrations of EGFR‐labelled EVs detected were 1.3 ± 0.7 × 108 EVs/mL for SMFC, 4.3 ± 1.6 × 106 EVs/mL for CellStream (4.6% of the SMFC value) and 4.0 ± 1.3 × 106 EVs/mL (3.6% of the SMFC value) for CytoFLEX. Over 94% of EVs detected by SMFC were not detected by the CellStream or CytoFLEX, consistent with the different LOD values of the platforms. The lower concentrations of EGFR‐labelled EVs than TS‐labelled EVs detected by each system are consistent with the 2‐ to 3‐fold lower median copy number of EGFR antibodies than total TS antibodies determined by single‐molecule TIRF microscopy (5.7 TS, 2.4 EGFR) and by SMFC (5.4 TS, 1.9 EGFR): (1) any TS+/EGFR− EVs would count towards the concentration of TS‐labelled EVs but not EGFR‐labelled EVs; and (2) the lower copy numbers of EGFR than TS resulted in more EVs below the LOD of CellStream and CytoFLEX (10 PEs based on bead calibration). Median EV diameters for TS‐ and EGFR‐labelled EVs were 57 and 58 nm for SMFC, 83 and 87 nm for CellStream, and 105 and 109 nm for CytoFLEX. The ratio of TS to EGFR antibody copy numbers in the smaller‐diameter EV population analysed by SMFC was 2.8 (similar to the 2.4 value obtained by single‐molecule TIRF microscopy), while this ratio in the larger‐diameter populations of EVs analysed by CellStream and CytoFLEX was 1.1 for both systems. The differences in EV concentration, size distribution and TS and EGFR antibody copy number distribution between platforms shown in Figures 3 and 4 are unsurprising due to the different sensitivities of the systems, and demonstrate the importance of using accurate LOD values to define the population of EV analysed for comparison of results. DISCUSSION

EVs are heterogeneous nanoparticles whose size distributions and target copy number distributions often overlap with the LODs of commercial high‐sensitivity flow cytometers. The importance of instrument calibration and reporting of LOD in quantitative flow cytometry studies of EVs has been made clear by the joint efforts of the EV and flow cytometry research communities (represented largely by members of ISEV and ISAC, respectively) to promote the use of standardized assays and standardized reporting of EV flow cytometry results (Welsh, Van Der Pol, Arkesteijn, et al., 2020). Prior studies have demonstrated that calibration and defining the EV population above specified LOD in flow cytometry is critical for accurate cross‐platform comparisons. For example, Welsh et al. showed that light scatter, fluorescence, and concentration calibration can produce concordant data between flow cytometry platforms independent of instrument collection angle, gain/voltage settings, and flow rate (Cook et al., 2023; Pleet et al., 2023; Welsh, Van Der Pol, Arkesteijn, et al., 2020). However, few if any previous studies have attempted to extend this approach into the single molecule domain, or compared the effect of bead‐based versus single‐molecule calibration and measurement methods on EV results. Here we compared EV analysis results across platforms that use different methods for calibration and measurement of EV concentration, size and antibody‐bound surface target copy number. The results illustrate the importance of calibration and the use of LOD to define a common EV population analysed by all platforms for reproducibility, but also the challenge of obtaining accurate LOD using calibration beads and protocols designed to calibrate signals from brighter samples (individual cells containing hundreds to thousands of fluorophores compared to EVs containing one to a few tens of fluorophores), and the potential utility of single‐molecule flow cytometry for improving the accuracy of calibration in flow cytometry studies of very dim EV particles.

The estimated LOD for EV immunofluorescence and size for the CytoFLEX and CellStream flow cytometers were ∼10 PEs and ∼80 nm for CytoFLEX and ∼10 PEs and ∼67 nm for CellStream based on calibration using ERF beads (nanoRainbow beads, Cellarcus Inc). It is instructive to consider the basis of these values and their uncertainties. Hard‐dyed beads, in which fluorophores with broad excitation and emission spectra are embedded in the hydrophobic polymer matrix of the particle, are convenient for their uniform and stable fluorescence, and are attractive for longitudinal and inter‐lab studies, but have the drawback that they are not spectrally matched to any fluorochrome used in an assay, and thus calibrations based on them will vary among instruments with different excitation (laser wavelength, power density) and emission (filter band and width, detector spectral response) properties (Hoffman et al., 2012; Parks et al., 2018). Hard‐dyed bead‐assigned intensity values are derived by cross‐calibration against specific fluorophore standards (e.g., PE MESF and vFRed vesicle size) on a reference instrument, but such assignments are considered nominal and instrument‐specific and, for optimal accuracy, such a cross‐calibration should be performed on the specific instrument in question.

Beyond the spectral matching issues of hard‐dyed beads, the underlying MESF and vesicle size standards have their own uncertainties. MESF assignments are based on careful cuvette‐based measurements of fluorophore absorbance and emission, and of the fluorescence (after accounting for background of unstained beads) of a known concentration of beads, and themselves have an uncertainty of ∼20%. Hard‐dyed beads calibrated in ERF units have similar uncertainties, in addition to the spectral matching issues discussed above (Schwartz et al., 2004). The vesicle size calibration is based on a synthetic lipid vesicle with a lipid composition approximating that of a mammalian cell, but its size distribution is based on orthogonal measurements (nanoparticle tracking analysis or resistive pulse sensing), which themselves have significant uncertainties. Moreover, the vesicle size calibration is really estimating membrane lipid surface area and, while fluorescence‐based size measurements are generally concordant with orthogonal methods, it is possible that EVs with very high protein to lipid ratios, for example, might show different staining, in which case a calibration might be generated for that specific vesicle type. Thus, absent of a perfectly ‘average’ EV standard (which likely does not exist), EV size measurements by any method are probably accompanied by associated uncertainties. The SMFC uses super‐resolution imaging of the same vesicle type in its calibration of EV size versus membrane dye fluorescence to eliminate some of these uncertainties (Andronico et al., 2021; Jung et al., 2023).

With the uncertainties associated with these LODs in mind, we observed that the CellStream detected almost four times as many EVs labelled with di‐8‐ANEPPS and PE‐anti‐TS (CD9, CD63 and CD81) antibodies as CytoFLEX; the median copy number of total antibody‐bound TS for CellStream was less than half that measured by CytoFLEX; and the median size for CellStream was 83 nm while that for CytoFLEX was 105. When the CellStream data were filtered to remove data for EVs smaller than 80 nm (the nominal size LOD of CytoFLEX), the resulting median TS and EGFR antibody copy numbers were still much smaller than those for CytoFLEX (19.8 vs. 39.4 for TS, 17.2 vs. 36.5 for EGFR). Together, these results suggested a larger difference in sensitivity between CellStream and CytoFLEX than indicated by the LOD values from bead calibration, and illustrate the uncertainties that can be encountered when extrapolating nominal intensity assignments to the instrument background.

Unlike the ‘top‐down’ approach of calibrating flow cytometry systems for measurement of cells, which are much brighter than EVs, using beads with different median intensities/numbers of fluorophores, the SMFC uses a ‘bottom‐up’ approach of starting with a detection system that can detect every bright fluorophore or fluorophore‐labelled molecule in a sample, and developing methods for EV characterization using this system (Andronico et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021; Jung et al., 2018, 2023; Mutch et al., 2007, 2011; Schiro et al., 2007). The immunofluorescence LOD for the SMFC was 1 PE: the distribution of photon burst intensities from single PE dyes was well above SNR = 3, indicating 100% single‐fluorophore detection efficiency; the size LOD of the SMFC was ≤35 nm (the smallest EV observed by dSTORM). Due to the much lower LODs of the SMFC, this system was able to detect EVs below the LOD of the commercial systems, which in this case constituted the vast majority of the sample: of the 5.5 × 108/mL TS‐labelled EVs and 1.3 × 108/mL EGFR‐labelled EVs detected by the SMFC, only 5.7% and 4.6%, respectively, were detected by CellStream, and 1.5% and 3.6%, respectively, by CytoFLEX. In addition to its greater sensitivity, the SMFC uses a different approach to measuring copy numbers of EV surface epitopes. Rather than using ERF/MESF calibration beads, the SMFC applies internal calibration in which the fluorescence intensity distribution of residual unbound dye‐tagged antibodies in the EV sample is used to deconvolute the fluorescence intensity from individual EVs to determine the number of antibodies bound per EV. This method has been validated previously (Jiang et al., 2021; Jung et al., 2018; Mutch et al., 2007, 2011) and was validated again here by comparing EV surface protein copy numbers with results obtained by single‐molecule TIRF microscopy: median copy numbers of total TS and EGFR antibodies were 5.7 and 2.4 as measured by single‐molecule TIRF microscopy, and 5.4 and 1.9 as measured by SMFC. The much larger TS and EGFR antibody copy numbers as measured by CellStream (17.9 and 16.1) and CytoFLEX (39.4 and 36.5) reflect the larger EVs detected and analysed by these systems (median EV diameter 57 nm for SMFC, 83 nm for CellStream, and 105 nm for CytoFLEX for TS‐labelled EVs). The SMFC yielded a similar ratio of median TS to EGFR antibody copy numbers (TS:EGFR = 2.8) as obtained by single‐molecule TIRF microscopy (2.4), while this ratio was 1.1 for both CellStream and CytoFLEX.

Since the SMFC can accurately measure the number of PE fluorophores per EV, and more generally, the number of any bright fluorophore per particle, it is potentially useful for improving calibration in EV flow cytometry studies. Here we applied the SMFC data to obtain a semi‐quantitative estimate of the LOD of EV immunofluorescence and size of the CellStream and CytoFLEX systems by threshold analysis, that is, by varying the PE number and size cutoffs applied to filter the SMFC data to identify the cutoffs that yield the best agreement in median TS and EGFR antibody copy numbers. Since we performed two separate, sequential head‐to‐head comparisons—SMFC versus CytoFLEX and SMFC versus CellStream—we performed two threshold analyses using the two different datasets. We found that applying cutoffs of 12 PEs and 80 nm to the CellStream data resulted in the best agreement between SMFC and CellStream median TS and EGFR copy numbers, and cutoffs of 21 PEs and 120 nm resulted in the best agreement in between SMFC and CytoFLEX. The much higher cutoffs obtained for CytoFLEX than CellStream are consistent with the lower sensitivity of CytoFLEX versus CellStream indicated by the large differences in EV concentration, median EV size, and TS and EGFR antibody copy numbers obtained by the two systems as described above. In addition, applying the 21 PE and 120 nm cutoff to obtain a common EV population detected and analysed by all three platforms yielded good agreement in TS and EGFR antibody copy numbers between CellStream and CytoFLEX, even though the CellStream data were not used to obtain the CytoFLEX cutoffs. Together, these results suggest that the nominal LOD values determined by hard‐dyed bead calibration were lower than the actual values, and indicate the potential utility of the SMFC to assign more accurate instrument‐specific intensities for calibration and LOD determination.

Calibration of flow cytometry systems by using beads improves the precision of measurements and is important for reproducibility, but obtaining accurate/absolute LOD values for PE copy number and EV size is challenging due to the multi‐step calibration process, and the use of different fluorophores which have different spectral properties and sensitivities to environmental conditions such as pH. In addition, the use of LOD thresholding to define a common EV population for comparison of flow cytometry results across platforms and labs will become much more complex when multiplexing is increased. For example, in this study, only two fluorophores were used (EVs were defined as di‐8‐ANEPPS+/PE+ particles), and EV populations analysed were defined in terms a single immunofluorescence LOD (PE copy number) together with the size LOD, for example, ‘EVs ≥ 10 PEs and 80 nm.’ However, in 10‐plex experiments, EV populations will be defined in terms of the LOD of 9 dyes (LOD1, LOD2, LOD3, …, LOD9) plus the size LOD. Due to the SMFC's ultra‐high sensitivity (100% single‐molecule detection efficiency for bright dyes) and internal calibration method for accurate copy number determination, and size calibration using the EV sample itself, this system can be used to obtain semi‐quantitative estimates of LODs of other flow cytometers as shown here. In future work, we will explore the application of SMFC to assign ERF/MESF values to calibration beads to enable more accurate calibration and estimation of LOD values in EV studies using quantitative flow cytometry.
