The spatial spillover effects of establishing free trade pilot zones on FDI: Empirical evidence from China
Authors: Tingting Zhang, Wenxuan Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The establishment of free trade pilot zones (FTZs) in China has constituted a pivotal endeavor to enhance global collaboration, foster economic restructuring and transformation, and attract foreign direct investment (FDI). These objectives are pursued through a series of strategies, including implementing a negative list system, reducing the threshold for FDI, and enhancing the FDI service framework. Based on panel data from 47 cities between 2005 and 2018, this research employs a spatial difference-in-differences model to empirically examine the spatial spillover effects of establishing FTZs concerning FDI. The findings reveal the substantial positive impact of FTZs on fostering FDI within respective domains. Conversely, an adverse spillover effect is observed on FDI in neighboring vicinities. Coastal FTZs have a negative spillover effect on FDI in surrounding areas, whereas inland FTZs have minimal impact on FDI in surrounding areas. The first and third batches of FTZs have had negative spillover effects on FDI in surrounding areas, while the second batch of FTZs has had no significant spatial spillover effect on FDI. Based on the empirical conclusions, this study proposes policy recommendations to broaden the scope of FTZs, refine the spatial spillover mechanism, and foster the diversified development of FTZs. These measures are envisaged to optimize the efficacy of the FTZ strategy to advance FDI objectives.

Introduction

Amid uncertain international circumstances and a sluggish global economic landscape, economies across the globe are actively seeking novel modalities and approaches to invigorate economic recuperation. The significance of capital as a pivotal determinant impinging on economic expansion cannot be overstated. As a crucial source of capital, foreign direct investment (FDI) has an indispensable role in alleviating domestic funding constraints (Tang et al., 2008) [1], fostering technological advancement (Liu & Wang, 2003) [2], and stimulating economic growth (Iamsiraroj, 2016) [3]. FDI is an essential impetus for propelling the development of nations or regions. In recent years, China's use of FDI has only fluctuated slightly. However, the rate of growth has been relatively slow, and negative growth rates have even been experienced in some years, such as − 3.74% in 2012 and − 0.21% in 2016.1 To attract FDI, China's free trade pilot zone (FTZ) strategy enhances the facilitation and liberalization of FDI through reforming the management approach, refining the infrastructure, and elevating collaboration between domestic and foreign investment (Li, 2022; Cui et al., 2023) [4,5]. Leveraging the innovative policies within the FTZ framework, China has employed various strategies to entice FDI.

China has systematically established 21 FTZs in separate phases,2 facilitating consistent growth and geographical expansion. Each pilot FTZ has improved the liberalization and facilitation of FDI through innovative policies and institutions, significantly impacting FDI inflow within the zones and generating spillover effects in the surrounding areas. Constructing a spatial difference-in-differences (DID) model to analyze the effects of pilot FTZs on FDI inflow within the zones and spillover effects on FDI in the surrounding areas can enrich the research content regarding pilot FTZs, provide new perspectives and methods for scholars to evaluate the effectiveness of pilot FTZs, and summarize relevant outcomes that can be replicated and promoted nationwide. Furthermore, this work also has important theoretical and practical implications for deepening reform, expanding China's opening up, and attracting higher-level FDI.

This study uses panel data from 47 cities in China. Applying a spatial DID model, we examine the spatial spillover effects of establishing FTZs on FDI. The contributions of this study include three key aspects. First, in data analysis, multiple FTZs are incorporated into the research sample. Municipal-level data are employed instead of provincial-level data to analyze the impact of FTZ establishment on FDI. This approach aligns better with the practical scenario of FTZs being confined to municipalities or smaller provincial regions. Second, in terms of content exploration, the study scrutinizes the impact of establishing FTZs on FDI within zones, examining the spillover effects on FDI in neighboring regions. Building upon comprehensive sample analysis, we then separately examine the distinct effects of different types and phases of FTZ establishment on FDI, expanding the existing research landscape. Third, regarding methodological advancement, we integrate spatial elements into the traditional DID model, constructing a spatial DID model to facilitate simultaneous analysis of the impact of FTZs on FDI within the designated zones and its spillover effects on FDI in adjacent regions. This comprehensively enriched approach expands the dimensions of inquiry within this domain of research.

The relevant findings are threefold. First, the establishment of FTZs stimulates inward FDI within the designated areas while generating adverse spatial spillover effects on FDI in neighboring regions. Second, the impact of diverse types of FTZ establishment on FDI exhibits disparities. Regarding direct effects, coastal and inland FTZs attract FDI inflow. Concerning spillover effects, coastal FTZs have adverse spillover effects on FDI in adjacent regions, whereas inland FTZs do not significantly affect FDI in surrounding areas. Third, variations are evident in the impact of different batches of FTZs on FDI, particularly in terms of spillover effects. Across all three batches, FTZs notably bolster inward FDI. However, regarding spillover effects, the first and third batches exhibit detrimental impacts on FDI in neighboring regions, and while the second batch positively affects neighboring FDI, it lacks statistical significance.

The article has some limitations. First, certain city's data are incomplete due to data availability challenges, and we exclude cities with incomplete data in the empirical research segment, leaving only a subset of cities for the study and potentially omitting some significant urban centers. Consequently, the precision of our empirical results may be compromised. Second, FTZs only encompass specific sections within cities. Despite the continuous expansion of the experimental scope of these zones, they do not yet encompass entire urban expanses. As a result, this analysis based on city-level panel data may inherently incur limitations and inaccurately depict the true impact of FTZ establishment.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In section 2, we review existing literature on the effects of FTZs, providing a critical assessment of the available sources. Section 3 preliminarily examines the spillover effects of FTZs on FDI in neighboring regions. We establish the study's model in section 4, outlining data sources and descriptive statistics. Section 5 applies the model for empirical analysis, accompanied by robustness tests. Finally, section 6 details proposed policy recommendations based on the empirical findings.

Literature review

FTZs are pivotal experimental arenas for China's most recent profound reform and extended liberalization phase. Since the establishment of the Shanghai FTZ in 2013, scholars have assessed its effects from various perspectives. Regarding economic expansion, a consensus among scholars, including Wang et al. (2014), Tan et al. (2015), Wang and Liu (2017), and Yin and Gao (2017) has indicated that FTZs can indeed foster economic growth [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Nevertheless, the extent of FTZs' influence on economic growth has demonstrated variations across distinct regions, as evidenced by studies from Ye (2020) and Bai et al. (2020) [10,11]. Regarding financial market advancement, a prevailing consensus among scholars has emphasized the influence of FTZs on financial market progress, propelling interest rate liberalization (Luo & Zhou, 2015) [12], financial openness and innovation, and holistic improvement within the financial sector.

Conversely, a minority of scholars has advocated an alternative perspective, arguing that the financial risks of FTZs’ financial openness are unique and represent a new type of risk that is superimposed on traditional risks and is primarily manifested as capital flow risks under dual interest rate systems and exchange rates inside and outside of zones (Luo & Zhao, 2014) [13]. Research has also assessed the policy implications of FTZs from differing perspectives. For instance, Chen and Luo (2014) contended that the restructuring of the FDI access barrier mechanism within the Shanghai FTZ will yield affirmative policy outcomes, including transfer payments and expanded domestic demand [14]. Liu and Lv (2018) and Wu and Xie (2019) determined that FTZ policies only exhibit significant impact on indicators such as fixed asset investment [15,16], suggesting that the policy dividends that could result from FTZs remain partially untapped, warranting additional refinement and strategic calibration of assorted FTZ policies.

A consensus prevails among scholars regarding FDI attraction, suggesting that FTZs’ capacity to facilitate FDI inflow. Employing the synthetic control method, Xiang and He (2016) empirically examined the policy effects of the Shanghai FTZ on capital flow, revealing a substantial positive correlation between the FTZ and the promotion of FDI inflow within the Shanghai region [17]. According to Li (2017) and Mao (2018), FTZs have established an FDI management system centered on a negative list, establishing a system that is consistent with international investment norms [18,19]. Employing the synthetic control method, Huang (2018) examined the evolving repercussions of the Shanghai FTZ pilot on attracting FDI to Shanghai, revealing that the Shanghai FTZ significantly influences FDI attraction. Furthermore, amplification of this policy effect correlates with the sustained operational stability of the FTZ [20]. Qu et al. (2018) analyzed panel data encompassing the FTZs in Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangdong, and Fujian from 2012 to 2018, finding that the four FTZs promote FDI through a consumption-pulling effect [21]. From a quasi-natural experiment perspective, Han and Bo (2019) used provincial panel data covering 2004 to 2016 to examine the impact of FTZs in Shanghai, Tianjin, Guangdong, and Fujian on cross-border capital flow, determining that FTZs can effectively promote FDI inflow [22]. Li and Zhao (2019) demonstrated that FTZs can promote sustained FDI growth by improving the investment environment [23]. In contrast, some have presented alternative opinions. For instance, Deng et al. (2020) scrutinized panel data from 2000 to 2016 across 29 provinces, revealing a distinctive pattern in which solely the Shanghai FTZ has exerted a pronounced positive influence on FDI promotion, which is juxtaposed against the marked adverse effect on FDI induced by the FTZs in Guangdong, Fujian, and Tianjin [24].

This review of the extant scholarly works reveals that numerous studies have meticulously assessed the ramifications arising from FTZ implementation from diverse perspectives. Nevertheless, consensus regarding the impact of FTZs on FDI has not yet been reached. A majority of previous research has purported that the policy benefits of FTZs can effectively promote FDI; however, some scholars, propelled by empirical investigation, have contended that the full extent of the policy-induced dividend effect of FTZs is not yet fully unleashed. The existing literature presents areas for refinement and enhancement. Most importantly, concerning data sourcing, a substantial majority of scholars have employed provincial-level data in their analyses, which is inconsistent with the fact that FTZs are set up in only small portions of a province or municipality. Second, in terms of research methods and content, most research has used quasi-natural experimental methods such as synthetic control or DID models to investigate the impact of FTZ establishment on FDI in FTZs, and limited studies have incorporated spatial factors to examine the spillover effects of FTZ implementation on FDI in surrounding areas. This study uses urban panel data from 2005 to 2018 to construct a spatial DID model, which enables us to analyze the impact of FTZs on local FDI and investigate its spillover effects on neighboring regions. This approach bridges the gap in the existing literature and enriches the research.

Analysis of the spatial spillover effects of FTZs on FDI

Positive spatial spillover effects

Competition effect. FTZ implementation has effectively attracted FDI inflow by continuously reducing the negative list of FDI, lowering the threshold for FDI, and improving the investment environment, which also facilitates FDI agglomeration, with foreign enterprises tending to invest in areas with high targeted concentrations of FDI (Xiao & Lin, 2008) [25]. Foreign enterprises in the same industry tend to cluster in certain areas (Wu et al., 2011) [26]. However, the capacities for FDI within FTZs are limited. If a large amount of FDI from the same industry flows into the FTZ, it will intensify competition within the zone and decrease profit margins. Consequently, numerous foreign enterprises will pursue investment prospects near the FTZ, which allows them to access industrial clusters closely while circumventing undue competitive pressure.

The learning effect. Positioned as a vanguard in China's latest phase of reform and opening up, FTZs can attract FDI inflow while concurrently amassing and concentrating institutional innovation expertise. Furthermore, this knowledge can be propagated nationally through replication and promotion. In particular, surrounding areas with similar resource endowment and economic development status could actively learn and benefit from the advanced management experience and institutional innovation policies of the FTZ, integrating them into existing economic foundations, location conditions, and industrial trajectories to enhance competitiveness in attracting FDI and improve capabilities to attract FDI.

Information cost effect. In the due diligence process, foreign-invested enterprises examine comprehensive information encompassing economic progress, market dynamics, preferential policies, and other pertinent factors linked to potential investment gains within their intended investment locale to bolster profitability. In addition, foreign investors will gather information about the surrounding areas to understand the sales market for their products and transportation conditions. Serving as a vanguard for institutional innovation trials, FTZs naturally garner significant attention within the ambit of FDI. Nevertheless, intensified competition and elevated entry expenses may prompt some foreign-funded enterprises to establish branches in the periphery of FTZs to circumvent the competitive expenses linked with direct FTZ investments, concurrently capitalizing on the FTZ's “policy highland” influence. Furthermore, having already procured pertinent investment information, FDI enterprises exclude the necessity for redundant acquisition, which curtails information expenditure and can amplify investment returns.

Negative spatial spillover effects

The follower effect. Many large foreign-invested enterprises are attracted by the preferential policies of the FTZ and choose to invest in the area. In some industries, foreign-invested enterprises are interdependent, and one enterprise's practices may directly impact other enterprises in the same industry. Based on considerations such as market share, other companies may follow suit and choose to invest in the FTZ. As a result, foreign-invested enterprises that had previously invested or planned to invest in other regions can change investment destinations to the FTZ, establishing a follower effect in FDI. This increases the FDI in the zone; however, it may negatively impact FDI in surrounding areas.

Talent migration effect. Individuals who have received higher education possess specialized knowledge and skills that can generate more value creation. The purpose of FDI is to maximize profits; thus, the talent capital of the investment destination is necessarily considered in the selection of investment location. If the local talent capital is abundant, foreign-invested enterprises can quickly update and upgrade products and services and reduce training costs to maximize market share and expand enterprise profits. Notably, talent capital has mobility, and the policies that promote human resources reform in the FTZ will also attract talent to the zone from the surrounding areas, which could have a negative spillover effect on FDI in surrounding areas through the talent migration effect.

Technology attraction effect. Scientific and technological progress can elevate total factor productivity, lower expenditure, and increase profit. Driven by profit maximization, foreign enterprises tend to invest in countries or regions with advanced technological foundations. This strategic preference is fueled by enterprises' intention to assimilate and harness indigenous advanced technologies, which can increase profitability. FTZs emphasize technological development by implementing measures such as developing open innovation platforms, strengthening cooperation with universities, and protecting innovative achievements, which improve zones’ technological levels. Technology development and transfer can attract a large amount of FDI inflow into the FTZs, but it also attracts FDI that was initially invested in surrounding areas of the zone, resulting in adverse spillover effects on neighboring FDI.

Based on the above analysis, FTZ implementation may have positive spillover effects on FDI in neighboring areas through competition, learning, and information cost effects. In contrast, it may also have adverse spillover effects on FDI in neighboring areas through trend-following, talent transfer, and technology attraction effects. Hence, the focal exploration in this study investigates the spatial ramifications of FDI spillovers within FTZs.

Variable selection and descriptive statistics

The dependent variable is the level of FDI, which is represented by the natural logarithm of the actual use of FDI (lnFDI), referencing Jing and Zhang (2014) and Dong and Yan (2015) [32,33].

For the core explanatory variable, referencing Zhang et al. (2019) and Quan et al. (2020), we measure FTZ implementation using a dummy variable (DID), where DID = 1 if an FTZ is implemented in the sample city establishes in the current or future year and 0 otherwise [34,35].

Referring to Li and Li (2013), Zhang et al. (2013), and Xian and Xu (2013), FDI is related to macroeconomic development, market size, government size, labor force, and openness [[36], [37], [38]]. Thus, we control for the following factors as control variables.1.Gross domestic product (GDP) is the premier gauge of macroeconomic development, and is expected to exert substantial influence on FDI. As profit motives drive FDI, heightened macroeconomic development within a region can instill greater investor confidence, facilitating investments and profit generation within the region (Daniels & Quigley, 1980) [39]. Referencing Cheng and Wang (2013), Dong and Zhu (2016), Han and Hu (2016), Hao et al. (2014), and Liu et al. (2020), the variable for macroeconomic development is represented by the natural logarithm of regional GDP [[40], [41], [42], [43], [44]]. Log transformation is conducted to minimize heteroskedasticity as much as possible.2.Market size: Larger market size and rapid growth potential are more attractive to FDI (Zhou & Ouyang, 2013) [45]. A larger regional market size indicates stronger demand for products in the region and stronger investment demand from foreign investors (Bailey & Li, 2015; Aziz & Mishra, 2016) [46,47]. Considering the substantive role of market size in influencing FDI dynamics, the market size variable is incorporated as a controlling factor. Referencing Yu and Zhang (2014), this variable is represented by the natural logarithm of total retail sales of social consumer goods [48].3.Government size: The government has a tangible influence on economic development, and larger government size equates to heightened economic intervention capabilities. Local governing bodies frequently implement fiscal subsidies or tax incentives to shape FDI preferences (Zhu & Gu, 2018) [49]. Therefore, government size is used as a control variable, and it is represented referencing Afonso and Furceri (2010) and Li et al. (2010), using the ratio of total government expenditure to regional GDP [50,51].4.Labor force: If the investment location has abundant labor resources, foreign enterprises can obtain a dominant position in the labor market, pay low labor costs, and even engage in “reasonable implicit exploitation” of labor (Liu et al., 2014) [52]. Based on data availability and referencing Dong and Liang (2013) and Li et al. (2014), the number of urban employees at the end of each year in each city is used for labor force level [53,54].5.Labor cost: The primary goal of FDI is profit optimization, necessitating comprehensive cost reduction in which labor expenses are an essential determinant of location choice. Referencing Wang (2014), we use the logarithm of the average salary of urban employees as a proxy for labor cost [55].6.Openness: In the process of investment decision-making, foreign-funded enterprises must understand local market dynamics, regulatory frameworks, and cultural underpinnings, which are critical factors that affect investment returns. The process of collecting such information entails considerable transaction and information costs. A heightened degree of economic openness translates to facilitating the accessibility of information that influences investment decisions, attenuating external unpredictability in addition to associated transactional and informational burdens. We argue that the degree of openness potentially influences FDI dynamics. Referencing Zhang et al. (2013), Chao and Shen (2014), and Xing and Zhang (2018), we use trade dependence (the ratio of cities' total imports and exports to GDP) to represent the degree of openness [[56], [57], [58]]. Empirical sample

To date, China has established 21 FTZs in six batches. Since the FTZs piloted in 2019 and 2020 are still in the early stages and subsequently effects are yet to be fully reflected, they were not included in the treatment group, and the prefecture- and municipal-level cities where the first four batches of FTZs are located are used as the treatment group,3 while cities that have not established FTZs but are at comparable development stages to those with FTZs are employed as the control group.4 Thus, the sample period is limited to 2005–2018 to exclude the influence of FTZs established in 2019 and 2020.

Data sources

According to the indicators in Table 1, we compiled panel data encompassing 47 cities at prefecture and municipality levels from 2005 to 2018. The data on actual FDI use, GDP, per capita GDP, average employee wages, and total import and export volume were obtained from the Statistical Communique on National Economic and Social Development and each city's statistical yearbook. The data for employed personnel, local general budgetary expenditure, and social retail sales are obtained from the China City Statistical Yearbook. Referencing Zhou et al. We next present an analysis of the control variables. The direct impact of the macroeconomic development level is highly positive at the 1% significance level, signifying heightened economic development in the local region fosters FDI inflow to a region. A higher level of macroeconomic development instills greater confidence among foreign investors, encouraging investment and profit-making endeavors. Consequently, elevated local economic development establishes more favorable conditions for facilitating FDI inflow into a region. Conversely, the indirect impact is significantly negative at the 1% significance level, suggesting that economic development adversely influences FDI in neighboring areas. A heightened local economic development level will lead to increased competition for FDI in neighboring areas. The total effect is significantly positive at the 1% level, indicating that enhanced economic development is essential for fostering FDI inflow. The direct effect of market size is significantly positive at the 5% level, demonstrating that larger local market size and demand facilitate FDI inflow into a region. Conversely, the indirect impact is significantly negative at the 5% significance level, implying that a larger local market size will draw FDI away from neighboring areas, engendering an adverse spillover effect on FDI in those areas. The total effect is significantly positive at the 5% level, indicating that the positive direct effect of market size expansion on FDI inflow into a region can offset the negative spillover effect on FDI in neighboring areas, resulting in a significantly positive total effect.

The direct impact of government size is notably positive at the 1% significance level, suggesting that enlarging the government scale could attract FDI inflow into a region. The direct impact of government size is notably positive at the 1% significance level, suggesting that enlarging the government scale could attract FDI inflow into a region. Furthermore, enlarging the government's scope signifies its ability to offer additional tax incentives and other favorable policies to foreign investors to effectively foster FDI promotion. Conversely, the indirect impact is notably adverse at the 5% significance level, implying that the enlargement of the local government's scale yields an unfavorable spillover effect on FDI in neighboring areas. This may stem from the allure of the investment environment in the region, leading to the diversion of FDI from surrounding areas toward FTZs. The total effect is significantly positive at the 1% level, indicating that the expansion of government size has an overall positive spillover effect on FDI in the region and surrounding areas.

The direct effect of labor costs is significantly positive at the 1% level, indicating that an increase in local labor costs promotes FDI inflow. Conversely, the indirect impact is significantly negative at the 1% significance level, suggesting that heightened labor costs lead to reduced FDI inflow in neighboring areas. The total effect is significantly positive at the 1% level, indicating that labor costs positively impact FDI inflow. This finding contradicts the conventional theory of FDI cost determination; however, it could mirror China's unique national circumstances. Despite the initial inflow of FDI to China being driven by the availability of low-cost labor, the consistent movement of labor—especially low-cost labor—within China has led to labor costs not retaining primacy as the foremost determinant in the selection of location for FDI in China (Yang et al., 2010) [61]. Furthermore, China's demographic dividend has progressively waned in recent years, eroding the competitive edge of low-cost labor. China's appeal to FDI now appears to be rooted not in low-cost labor but rather preferential policies and technological advancement. Typically, regions boasting elevated levels of technological advancement exhibit relatively higher labor costs.

Robustness tests

In this section, we perform robustness tests from four perspectives. Initially, we conduct parallel trend tests. Second, we implement counterfactual assessments by shifting the initiation dates of FTZs to 3 years prior. Third, we estimate models employing both a DID spatial Durbin model (DID-SDM) and a DID general spatial autoregressive (SAC) model (DID-SAC). Finally, we conduct regressions by substituting variables.

Parallel trend test

Equations (10), (11) include a spatial lag term of the dependent variable (ρWY), which makes it difficult to directly interpret the effect of changes in DID on the dependent variable (β). This necessitates an effect decomposition, which is similar to the process in the DID-SAR.

Table 8 presents the estimation results of the spatial DID-SDM model in columns (1)–(3) and the DID-SAC in columns (4)–(6). Adequate infrastructure is an essential factor of a country's investment attraction capacity (Sun, 2003; Yin, 2006) [74,75]. From a direct effect perspective, a higher level of infrastructure in a region will promote FDI inflow. Regions with higher infrastructure levels can provide relatively complete supporting measures for foreign-funded enterprises, reducing foreign enterprises' fixed asset investments in the early stages and lowering FDI fixed costs. Additionally, higher infrastructure levels can improve enterprises' future competitiveness, increasing FDI investment return rates. From an indirect effect perspective, a higher infrastructure level in a region will attract FDI from surrounding areas, resulting in adverse spillover effects on FDI in surrounding areas. The results of the interaction term estimation show that a higher infrastructure level will weaken the impact of FTZ implementation on FDI to a relatively small extent. This may be because regional infrastructure is an essential factor that is considered by foreign enterprises when choosing a location, which reduces the degree of the impact of FTZ implementation on FDI in the region and surrounding areas.

The findings regarding financial institution liquidity in columns (4)–(6) of Table 10 indicate no significant direct impact on regional FDI; however, the results of the interaction term estimation indicate that it does significantly affect the degree of impact that FTZ implementation has on FDI in the region and surrounding areas. In particular, lower financial institution liquidity within a region negatively affects the impact of FTZ implementation on FDI. This implies that heightened financial institution liquidity within a region increases the promotional impact of FTZ implementation on FDI within a region, which subsequently exacerbates the adverse spillover effects on FDI in neighboring regions. Higher financial institution liquidity within a region indicates reduced risk exposure and fosters a more stable financial milieu that is conducive to attracting FDI. This amplifies FTZs’ appeal to FDI within the zone and its adjacent regions.

Conclusions and policy recommendations

Based on panel data from 47 Chinese cities from 2005 to 2018, this study uses a DID-SAR model to examine the spatial spillover effects of FTZ implementation on FDI. The results demonstrate that FTZs can effectively promote FDI inflow to the designated areas. Although there is a negative spillover effect on FDI in surrounding areas, the overall effect of FTZ implementation remains positive. Both coastal and inland FTZs can promote FDI inflow to the respective zones, but differences in the spatial spillover effects on FDI between the two are evident. The former has a negative spillover effect on FDI in neighboring areas, whereas the latter exhibits no significant spillover effect on FDI in the surrounding areas. All batches of FTZs are shown to effectively promote FDI inflow, and the first and third batches of FTZs have a negative spillover effect on FDI in neighboring regions. In contrast, the second batch of FTZs does not significantly impact FDI in the surrounding areas. Four robustness tests are conducted to verify the reliability of the results, all of which confirmed our benchmark conclusions. The mechanism analysis suggests that the level of regional infrastructure and the liquidity of financial institutions can alter the impact of FTZ implementation on FDI inflow to the designated and surrounding areas.

Based on the research findings, the following policy recommendations are proposed. First and foremost, the purview of FTZs must broadened, as FTZ implementation can effectively attract FDI. It is essential to explore the expansion of the regional scope of existing FTZs, leveraging the role of zones as testing grounds for innovative policies. Furthermore, based on the experiences and lessons learned from existing FTZs, it is advisable to establish new FTZs to further expand coverage.

Second, improve the spatial spillover mechanism of FTZs. FTZs are shown to have adverse spillover effects on FDI in neighboring areas; therefore, it is crucial to improve the spatial radiation mechanism of FTZs to neighboring areas to avoid adverse spillover effects and fully leverage the radiation and driving effect of FTZs on FDI in neighboring areas. Cities with established FTZs should synthesize experiences and promote the development of neighboring areas, use respective strengths, fully leverage existing FDI, and continuously attract new FDI. Furthermore, neighboring areas should actively learn from FTZs’ experiences that can be replicated and promoted and benefit from the policy advantages of FTZs to actively attract FDI that is suitable to existing regional development.

Third, promote differentiated development among FTZs. Given the varying levels of economic development and resource endowments across different FTZs, attractiveness to FDI also differs. Therefore, when formulating institutions and policies, it is essential to strategically consider the regional context, leverage local strengths, address weaknesses, and advance regionally diversified development among the FTZs. When developing policies, the government should fully consider factors such as regions’ resource endowments, infrastructure, and local conditions and formulate policy measures that are suitable for attracting FDI. In addition, each FTZ should accurately position itself based on its actual circumstances and advance differentiated development according to local conditions to attract high level and high quality FDI to avoid risks.
