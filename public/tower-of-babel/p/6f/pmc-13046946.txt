A comprehensive review of carbon dots for antimicrobial therapy: from design mechanisms to theranostic applications
Authors: Qian He, Liyun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs an emerging carbon-based nanomaterial, carbon dots (CDs) exhibit broad prospects in antimicrobial therapy owing to their unique physicochemical properties, excellent biocompatibility, and tunable multifunctional characteristics. This review systematically summarizes recent advances in the research on CDs in the antimicrobial field, including innovative construction strategies, mechanisms of action, theranostic applications, and safety evaluation. Regarding construction strategies, it highlights the design principles and antimicrobial properties of bio-derived CDs, drug molecule-directed CDs, charge-engineered CDs, metal-synergistic catalytic CDs, and amino acid-functionalized CDs. For mechanisms of action, it elaborates on the multi-target antimicrobial modes of CDs, such as physical disruption, oxidative stress induction, and metabolic interference. In terms of theranostic applications, it summarizes the innovative uses of CDs in photodynamic therapy, photothermal-photodynamic synergistic therapy, and tissue repair. Finally, it delves into safety concerns including the impacts of CDs on intestinal health, risks of metal doping, and environmental degradation behaviors. This review aims to provide theoretical guidance for the development of highly effective and safe antimicrobial CDs, while prospecting the challenges and opportunities in their clinical translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04481-1.Keywords: Carbon dots, Construction strategies, Antimicrobial mechanism, Theranostics, Safety evaluation

Introduction

Microbial infections have emerged as a critical threat to global public health security [1–3]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2019, deaths caused by bacterial infections account for 13.6% of global total deaths, and this is projected to become the second leading cause of death worldwide, surpassed only by ischemic heart disease. Antibiotics, the primary tool in traditional antimicrobial therapy, have led to severe drug resistance due to overuse [4–6]. In 2021, global deaths related to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) reached 4.71 million (with a 95% uncertainty interval of 4.23 million-5.19 million), of which 1.14 million (1.00 million-1.28 million) were directly caused by AMR [7]. More alarmingly, approximately 80% of clinical infections are associated with biofilm formation [8, 9]. This physical barrier, composed of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), significantly impairs the efficacy of conventional antibiotics [10]. Against this backdrop, the development of novel antimicrobial strategies is urgently needed.

Carbon dots (CDs), as emerging zero-dimensional carbon-based nanomaterials, exhibit immense potential in the antimicrobial field due to their unique performance advantages [11–13]. Compared to traditional antibiotics, CDs possess multiple antimicrobial mechanisms. Positively charged CDs can disrupt bacterial membrane integrity through electrostatic interactions. Their surface-abundant functional groups (-NH2, -OH, -COOH) endow them with excellent biocompatibility and water dispersibility, while their tunable optical properties enable theranostic integration [14]. Notably, the multi-target mode of action of CDs makes them less likely to induce bacterial resistance, offering a new approach to address the bottlenecks in current antimicrobial therapy [15].

In recent years, researchers have successfully developed various CDs systems with high antimicrobial activity through innovative material design strategies. Ranging from bio-derived precursors to drug molecule reconstruction, and from charge engineering modification to metal-synergistic catalysis, these innovative designs not only significantly enhance the antimicrobial efficacy of CDs but also endow them with additional functions such as tissue repair and microbial ecological regulation, laying the foundation for constructing a new generation of multifunctional antimicrobial platforms [16]. Unlike traditional single-function CDs that primarily target bacterial killing, the proposed “functional synergy design” paradigm aims to co-engineer multiple capabilities, such as targeting, antimicrobial action, diagnostic signaling, and tissue repair, into a single CDs entity. This integrated approach moves beyond simple additive effects, enabling synergistic interactions among different functional components to achieve enhanced therapeutic outcomes, reduce the risk of resistance, and address complex clinical needs such as biofilm eradication and wound healing.

A thorough understanding of the antimicrobial mechanisms of CDs is crucial for optimizing their performance. Studies have shown that CDs exert antimicrobial effects through multiple synergistic pathways. Their surface-abundant functional groups can directly disrupt bacterial membrane integrity. Photoexcitation generates reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce oxidative stress. Specific chemical structures can interfere with the activity of key enzymes in bacterial metabolism. Particularly noteworthy is the unique ability of CDs to intervene in biofilms, granting them significant advantages in treating recalcitrant infections. Through mechanisms such as penetrating and disrupting biofilm matrices, interfering with quorum-sensing signals, and degrading extracellular polymers, CDs can effectively overcome the challenge of conventional antibiotics’ inability to penetrate biofilms [17]. Additionally, the integration of CDs with phototherapeutic technologies (e.g., photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT)) has further expanded their application scenarios, providing new ideas for precise antimicrobial treatment.

As research on CDs advances, their safety has attracted increasing attention. Unlike traditional reviews that focus solely on acute toxicity, this review systematically explores the long-term biological effects and environmental behavior of CDs for the first time. Studies have revealed that the interaction between CDs and biological systems exhibits significant size-dependent and surface chemistry-dependent characteristics. Potential risks of CDs can be significantly reduced through rational structural design, such as introducing degradable groups and optimizing surface charge distribution [18, 19]. Simultaneously, research on the environmental fate of CDs provides a critical basis for evaluating their ecological safety. Advances in these safety studies have cleared obstacles for the clinical translation of CDs.

This review, based on the latest progress in antimicrobial CDs research, integrates multidisciplinary findings to comprehensively elaborate on the complete knowledge system from material design to mechanisms of action and safety evaluation. Compared with previous studies, the innovation of this review lies in three aspects. First, it proposes the concept of “functional synergy design”, emphasizing the integration of antimicrobial, theranostic, and tissue repair functions through molecular engineering. Second, it establishes a “mechanism-efficacy-safety” correlation model to provide theoretical guidance for the rational design of CDs. Finally, it systematically summarizes the challenges and solutions in the clinical translation of CDs (Scheme 1). These innovations not only deepen the understanding of CDs antimicrobial mechanisms but also point the way for developing next-generation intelligent antimicrobial materials. The publication of this review will provide valuable references for researchers in the field of nano-antimicrobial materials and accelerate the translation of antimicrobial CDs from basic research to clinical applications.

Construction strategies of antimicrobial CDs

The design of antimicrobial CDs has evolved from traditional single-functionality toward an era of precise molecular engineering. Its core lies in endowing CDs with multiple functions of targeted recognition, efficient bactericidal activity, and ecological regulation through the synergistic control of precursor selection and synthesis methodology.

Bio-derived precursors, such as fungal extracts and medicinal plants, are processed via hydrothermal carbonization (typically 180–220 °C), which retains natural antimicrobial components and inherent heteroatoms while converting them into stable, self-doped nanoparticles. Synthetic molecule-directed strategies, including antibiotic reconstruction, often employ microwave-assisted pyrolysis (e.g., 150–200 °C, minutes) to achieve rapid carbonization while preserving the pharmacophores of the parent drug, thereby inheriting targeting ability through molecular memory effects. Charge engineering enhances membrane interactions by regulating surface potential, which is achieved by using cationic precursors (e.g., polyethyleneimine) or post-modification under controlled hydrothermal conditions (160–200 °C, pH-dependent) to graft stable positive charges. Metal doping introduces enzyme-like catalytic activity through the co-carbonization of metal salts (e.g., Fe3+, Cu2+) with organic precursors in a one-pot hydrothermal process (180–250 °C, 6–24 h, precise pH control), forming defined metal-N/O coordination sites. Amino acid functionalization directly interferes with microbial homeostasis by utilizing amino acids as precursors or modifiers, commonly through mild hydrothermal synthesis (150–190 °C, 5–10 h) to incorporate specific functional groups (-NH2, -COOH, imidazole) on the CDs surface.

Thus, by strategically pairing precursor chemistry with tailored synthesis protocols, where parameters such as temperature, time, and pH are fine-tuned, researchers can programmably construct CDs with precise structural features for targeted antimicrobial action. This section will systematically analyze these five categories of design paradigms, elucidating the synthesis-structure-activity relationships that underpin their function.

Antimicrobial functionalization design of bio-sourced CDs

CDs derived from fungal extracts exhibit unique therapeutic potential. Blue-fluorescent Du-CDs with a quantum yield of 6.2% were synthesized via a solvent method using the acetic acid extract of Diaporthe sp. YSP3 culture as the precursor. Their antimicrobial activity and wound-healing ability are significantly superior to those of natural fungal extracts (Fig. 1a). This strategy avoids the common drawbacks of natural antimicrobial substances, such as high toxicity and poor stability, providing a new paradigm for the development of novel antimicrobial nanomaterials [16]. Similarly, using medicinal and edible homologous plants as precursors can endow CDs with enhanced biocompatibility and functional activity. Nitrogen-sulfur co-doped CDs derived from garlic (GCDs) are positively charged on the surface, with a MIC value against multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria an order of magnitude lower than that of traditional antibiotics, and can effectively inhibit biofilm formation [20]. Corn silk CDs (CS-CDs) retain the antimicrobial properties of traditional Chinese medicine while significantly improving water solubility and the release efficiency of active ingredients. They exert potent bactericidal effects through multiple mechanisms such as membrane disruption and oxidative damage (Fig. 1b) [21].

Theranostic CDs guided by drug molecular structures

Antibiotic-derived CDs achieve breakthroughs in drug resistance and theranostic synergy. CS-CDs synthesized via microwave irradiation using cefminox sodium (CS) as the sole precursor retain the structural characteristics of the parent drug and exhibit excellent efficacy in a mouse model of acute E. coli bloodstream infection (BSI). Their fluorescence intensity is negatively correlated with bacterial concentration, establishing a synchronous “detection-treatment” system (Fig. 1c) [22]. Kanamycin-derived CDs (KCDs) innovatively integrate antibiotic activity with photodynamic functions. Molecular docking confirms that their surface-modified components specifically bind to bacterial FabH enzyme. The CG-KCDs hydrogel formed by self-assembly with chitosan gel (CG) generates high concentrations of ROS under laser excitation. Transcriptome sequencing verifies that it can downregulate biofilm-forming genes (e.g., algD, pelA) while upregulating decomposition genes (e.g., disA, bdlA). This system significantly promotes tissue remodeling in infected wound models [23].

Precise antimicrobial activity of charge-engineered CDs

Surface design with positive charges is a core strategy to enhance the membrane targeting of CDs. Chitosan-grafted Fe-doped CDs (CS@Fe/CDs) eradicate biofilms through a synergistic mechanism. On one hand, Fe3+ activates the Fenton reaction to generate ·OH, which degrades extracellular DNA (eDNA). On the other hand, the positively charged groups of chitosan disrupt bacterial membrane integrity (Fig. 1d) [17]. Positively charged CDs derived from p-phenylenediamine/polyethyleneimine (PPD/PEI), utilizing their surface charge for membrane interaction, possess both antimicrobial and antioxidant functions. In a Staphylococcus aureus infection model, they accelerate wound healing by reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [24]. In the treatment of fungal keratitis, eye drops containing positively charged CDs (SP-CDs) can transiently open the tight junctions of corneal epithelium, enhance penetration to microorganisms in the deep stromal layer, and achieve efficient bactericidal activity through ROS-mediated membrane disruption (Fig. 2a) [25].

ROS Regulation by metal-synergistic catalytic CDs

Metal doping endows CDs with nanozyme-like catalytic activity to enhance antimicrobial efficacy. Fe/N co-doped CDs (Fe/N-CDs) exhibit super peroxidase (POD)-like activity under physiological H2O2 levels, with an inhibition rate of 80.2% against drug-resistant bacterial biofilms and negligible cytotoxicity (Fig. 2b) [26]. Single-atom copper CDs achieve multi-enzyme cascade catalysis. Cu-SLCDs derived from sodium lignosulfonate form a CuO4 coordination structure, and their oxidase (OXD), POD, catalase (CAT), and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities are all superior to those of CuN-CDs with CuN4 coordination. The p-π conjugated lignin network increases the electron density of the Fermi level. After catalyzing the production of ROS to eliminate pathogens, it can actively downregulate the ROS level in wounds to promote healing [27].

Ecological regulation by amino acid-functionalized CDs

CDs constructed from amino acid precursors can reshape the steady state of microbial communities. D-xylose-modified polylysine CDs (D-xyl@εPLCDs) block the adhesion and invasion of uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) to bladder epithelial cells through YadC-dependent targeting, and activate intracellular ROS and innate immune responses (Fig. 2c) [28]. Histidine-derived CDs (His-CDs) have SOD/CAT dual enzyme activities. Fecal microbiota transplantation experiments confirmed that they can regulate the gut microbiota in IBD models: increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as lactobacilli and restoring redox balance (Fig. 2d) [29]. Similarly, glucose/histidine CDs alleviate stress-induced depression by regulating the gut-brain axis through reducing oxidative stress levels [30]. Arginine CDs (Arg-CDs) are intelligently released from Schiff base-bonded hydrogels in an acidic microenvironment, and synergistically promote bone regeneration through ROS-mediated bactericidal activity and IL-10-mediated M2 polarization of macrophages [31].

Therapeutic applications

Material innovation and application expansion of CDs for PDT

A core challenge in the current field of photodynamic antimicrobial therapy is that traditional photosensitizers severely restrict application efficacy due to low [1]O2 quantum yield and poor water solubility. PDT operates via two distinct ROS generation pathways. Type I PDT involves electron or proton transfer between the photosensitizer and biological substrates, producing oxygen radicals such as superoxide and hydroxyl radicals (·OH) without directly consuming molecular oxygen. This mechanism retains efficacy under hypoxic conditions, offering potential advantage in low-oxygen environments. In contrast, Type II PDT relies on energy transfer to molecular oxygen to generate singlet oxygen, whose production is oxygen-concentration dependent. While singlet oxygen has limited diffusion range, Type I-derived radicals can propagate further via chain reactions, potentially broadening the cytotoxic effect. In response, long-lived room-temperature phosphorescent CDs (P-CDs) have broken through this bottleneck with high phosphorescence yield. A one-step hydrothermal synthesis strategy using diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) as the precursor endows the material with excellent water solubility and light-driven oxidase-like activity. Under 365 nm ultraviolet or visible light irradiation, P-CDs exhibit significant inhibitory effects on Gram-negative Escherichia coli and Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus. The further developed P-CDs/PVA films have verified their light-assisted antimicrobial potential in the preservation of strawberries and bananas, providing a new solution for food packaging [32]. In the direction of perishable fruit preservation, CDs@ZIF-8/PCL/EC nanofibrous films enhance PDT through a dual synergistic mechanism. ZIF-8 inhibits the self-aggregation of CDs and enhances light absorption, while the heterojunction reduces the band gap to promote e−-h+ separation, doubling the production of ·O2− and [1]O2, thus achieving efficient killing of E. coli (7.63 lg reduction) and S. aureus (7.27 lg reduction). The Zn2+ migration of this material is lower than the safety standards in Europe and America, successfully extending the shelf life of strawberries from 6 days to 12 days [33].

In addition to optimizing physical properties, intelligent response design targeting the bacterial infection microenvironment has become a new trend in the medical application of PDT. Based on this, hemin-modified CDs (H-CDs) have pioneered a new paradigm of chemiluminescence (CL) imaging-guided diagnosis and treatment: utilizing the hemin peroxidase-like activity and the substrate L012 to construct an H2O2-responsive CL system, which triggers specific luminescence in the infection microenvironment for real-time imaging, while simultaneously enhancing PDT efficiency through self-oxygen supply characteristics [34] (Fig. 3a). Antimicrobial photocatalytic therapy (APCT) achieves mechanism innovation through a binary system of CDs and covalent organic frameworks (COFs) - CDs act as electron extractors and storage media, promoting π-electron delocalization in the D-A type COF skeleton through the = C = O-H-N= hydrogen bond channel, significantly improving the separation efficiency of photogenerated carriers and ROS production [35].

Construction and disease application of photothermal-photodynamic synergistic therapy systems

Synergistic therapy overcomes the limitations of single therapy by integrating multiple mechanisms. In diabetic wound treatment, ascorbic acid-derived CDs (AA-CDs) integrate dual functions of photothermal antimicrobial activity and heat-enhanced antioxidant capacity: a high photothermal conversion efficiency of 41.18% directly kills drug-resistant bacteria, and the thermal effect synchronously enhances ROS scavenging ability (a “1 + 1>2” effect). When loaded in a thermoresponsive hydrogel, it significantly accelerates wound healing by regulating inflammatory factors [36] (Fig. 3b).

Synergistic therapy exhibits unique advantages in precise pathogen recognition and neurological disease fields. Guanosine-based CDs (G-CDs) achieve specific imaging of dead microorganisms through their negative charge characteristics, while broadly killing pathogens through a triple synergistic mechanism (intrinsic antimicrobial activity, photodynamic sterilization, and photothermal inactivation) [37] (Fig. 3c). For neurodegenerative diseases accompanied by infection, chlorin e6/yellow light CDs assemblies (yCDs-Ce6) break through the blood-brain barrier. Through PTT/PDT synergy, they clear β-amyloid deposits in the brains of APP/PS1 mice within 7 days, synchronously inhibit microbial growth, and show no tissue toxicity [38] (Fig. 3d).

Research on synergistic applications of multifunctional CDs in antibacterial activity and tissue repair

Through rational design and functional modification, CDs can achieve a synergistic effect between efficient antibacterial activity and tissue repair functions. In terms of antibacterial performance, copper-doped CDs derived from L-arginine (Cu-CDs) significantly enhance ROS levels by improving electron transfer efficiency and catalytic activity, thereby exhibiting excellent antibacterial and antibiofilm capabilities (Fig. 4a) [39]. The self-healing hydrogel CD-Plys, constructed by anionic CDs CD31 and ɛ-polylysine (Plys), not only has broad-spectrum antibacterial activity but also can significantly promote wound healing [40] (Fig. 4b). In addition, the I-CDs@LA-ASI system, formed by the combination of itaconic acid-derived CDs (I-CDs) and lipoic acid-asiaticoside hydrogel (LA-ASI), shows significant inhibitory effects on Pseudomonas aeruginosa and its drug-resistant strains [41].

In terms of tissue repair, quercetin CDs (QCDs) exert antibacterial effects by destroying bacterial cell walls, reducing drug resistance, and interfering with energy metabolism. Meanwhile, they can activate the Rap1 signaling pathway to promote osteogenesis, angiogenesis, and cell proliferation (Fig. 4c) [42]. The LR-S-CD/CpG@LNP system, constructed by mulberry leaf-derived CDs and immune adjuvant CpG, performs excellently in colorectal cancer treatment through a triple regulatory mechanism involving phototherapy, immunity, and microbial metabolism (Fig. 4d) [43].

These research results indicate that functionalized CDs act synergistically through multiple mechanisms, which can not only effectively kill pathogenic bacteria but also promote tissue repair and regeneration. Their unique performance advantages provide important ideas for the development of new antibacterial repair materials.

Research on synergistic antibacterial and therapeutic applications of theranostic CDs

Owing to their unique optical properties and biological activities, CDs exhibit revolutionary potential in the field of theranostics [44–46]. Through ingenious structural design and functional regulation, researchers have developed various CDs systems integrating precise diagnostic and efficient therapeutic functions.

In terms of fluorescence-guided antibacterial therapy, N and F co-doped CDs (N, F-CDs) not only possess multicolor fluorescence labeling capabilities (fluorescence quantum yield of 55.2% and Stokes shift of 112 nm) but also exert antibacterial effects by inhibiting bacterial division and energy metabolism [47]. Near-infrared emitting Cy7-CDs (λmax = 710 nm) achieve dual functions of bacterial species discrimination and photodynamic sterilization, with their surface-abundant functional groups specifically recognizing bacteria and generating ROS [48].

In the aspect of multifunctional theranostic platforms, PCBA-CDs containing boronic acid groups realize intelligent switching between fluorescent diagnosis and photothermal therapy through concentration regulation, achieving a 99% inhibition rate against Gram-positive bacteria under 808 nm irradiation (Fig. 5a) [49]. Bromine-doped CDs (BrCDs) synchronously generate type I/II ROS based on enhanced intersystem crossing effects, enabling imaging-guided efficient PDT [50] (Fig. 5b).

In innovative therapeutic strategies, manganese-doped CDs patches can not only monitor biofilm infections in real-time through pH-responsive fluorescence changes but also eliminate infections via photothermal/photodynamic/chemodynamic trimodal synergistic therapy [51]. Near-infrared carbonized polymer dots (NIR-CPDs) achieve NIR-II imaging-guided precise photodynamic antibacterial therapy by virtue of their stable π-conjugated system [52]. Notably, chiral D-cysteine-derived CDs (D-CDs) realize selective antibacterial activity and wound healing promotion through a triple mechanism (membrane disruption, photodynamic and photothermal effects) [53] (Fig. 5c).

These innovative studies demonstrate the unique advantages of CDs in theranostics: glutathione and biotin-derived CDs nanozymes not only visualize inflammation through red fluorescence but also regulate gut microbiota to treat colitis (Fig. 5d) [54]. This integration of precise diagnosis and efficient therapy makes CDs an ideal nanoplatform for combating infectious diseases and promoting tissue repair, providing new possibilities for clinical translation.

While preclinical synergy is promising, advancing photothermal-photodynamic theranostic platforms into clinical use requires overcoming interconnected translational challenges. Their efficacy depends critically on precise control of external parameters, such as irradiation wavelength, power, and duration, and reliable delivery of a consistent local concentration of CDs to the target site, both of which are difficult to maintain in dynamic, heterogeneous biological environments. Moreover, performance can be compromised by limited optical stability, non-specific protein adsorption, and enzymatic degradation in vivo, which may reduce the sensitivity and reliability of diagnostic functions such as concentration-dependent imaging or pH-responsive monitoring. Most studies remain proof-of-concept in small animals, with insufficient data on long-term biocompatibility, biodistribution, therapeutic windows, and clearance pathways. Translation will also depend on establishing scalable and reproducible manufacturing processes, standardised metrics for in vivo behaviour, and clinically relevant validation to demonstrate clear advantages over existing modalities in terms of safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness.

Research progress on the antibacterial mechanisms of CDs

Building upon the established multi-target antibacterial modes of CDs, recent studies have provided deeper molecular-level insights into their mechanisms. For example, carbon nanotubes (CNTs) cause membrane rupture in Escherichia coli, and graphene quantum dots (GQDs) cut phospholipid bilayers [55]. Meanwhile, CDs can induce oxidative stress to generate ROS. For instance, graphene oxide (GO) increases ROS level in Pseudomonas aeruginosa by 3.8 times. Under near-infrared light irradiation, CNT aggregates can locally heat up to 55 °C through photothermal effect to inactivate bacteria. Nitrogen-doped GQDs, as photosensitizers, generate singlet oxygen (1O2) through photodynamic effect.

At the molecular level, the antibacterial mechanism of carbon quantum dots (CQDs) is more complex. Quaternized carbon quantum dots (qCQDs) show different targets for Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Quantitative proteomics revealed that qCQDs mainly interfere with ribosomal proteins in Gram-positive bacteria, while downregulating various metabolism-related proteins in Gram-negative bacteria, especially those involved in the citric acid cycle pathway (Fig. 6a) [56]. Traditional CQDs mainly exert antibacterial effects by generating ROS (such as superoxide and ·OH) and destroying cell membranes, but these mechanisms still need to be further clarified at the molecular pathway level.

Significant progress has been made in studying the antibacterial mechanisms of metal-doped CDs. Taking iron-doped CDs (Fe-CDs) as an example, they penetrate into Escherichia coli, induce increased ROS, collapse of the glutathione (GSH)-dependent antioxidant system, and lipid peroxidation. Transcriptome analysis identified 425 differentially expressed genes, which are significantly enriched in membrane transport channels, cell permeability, motility, oxidative stress, and energy metabolism (Fig. 6b) [57]. In addition, metal-doped CDs can catalyze H2O2 to generate ·OH. For example, the synergistic bactericidal rate of Fe-CDs and H2O2 reaches 94.82% [56].

Other important mechanisms include: CNMs can destroy membrane integrity by extracting membrane phospholipids (e.g., GO nanosheets adsorb phospholipid molecules), inhibit energy metabolism (e.g., DGGs target MurD enzyme to block cell wall synthesis), bind to DNA to cause damage (e.g., S-CDs induce unwinding of DNA double strands), and isolate nutrients by wrapping bacteria with large surface areas (e.g., graphene nanosheets entangle microorganisms) [55]. This multi-target action mode enables carbon nanomaterials to effectively break through the three-layer membrane barrier of Gram-negative bacteria, providing important insights for the development of new antibacterial agents.

Research progress on the safety evaluation of CDs

As emphasized earlier, a holistic safety assessment of CDs requires evaluation across different biological kingdoms. This section reviews their biosafety from three interconnected perspectives: microbial ecosystems, animal models, and plants.

Impact on microbial ecosystems

While CDs are designed to target pathogens, their effects on broader microbial ecosystems, including commensal and beneficial microbiota, are crucial for assessing ecological and health risks. Studies indicate that the impact of CDs on microorganisms is highly dependent on their surface chemistry, charge, and dosage. For instance, ε-polylysine-derived CDs (PL-CDs) were shown to significantly inhibit the probiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus by inducing oxidative stress and damaging cell membrane integrity, highlighting potential risks to beneficial gut bacteria [18]. Conversely, some functionalized CDs exhibit selective toxicity. Histidine-derived CDs (His-CDs) can modulate gut microbiota in inflammatory bowel disease models, increasing the abundance of beneficial lactobacilli while suppressing pathogens, demonstrating a potential to reshape microbial communities towards a healthier state [29]. Furthermore, the interaction of CDs with environmental microbial communities is an emerging area. Their antibacterial mechanisms, such as ROS generation and membrane disruption, may also affect soil and aquatic microorganisms, potentially influencing nutrient cycling and ecosystem functions. The charge-engineered and metal-doped CDs, in particular, require careful evaluation for their long-term effects on microbial diversity and activity in non-target environments.

Biosafety in animal models

Animal studies provide critical insights into the systemic toxicity, biodistribution, and long-term health effects of CDs, which are foundational for clinical translation. Research has revealed that the biological interactions of CDs are significantly influenced by their size, surface charge, and coating materials. A key concern is gastrointestinal exposure. Intragastric administration of PL-CDs in mice induced intestinal inflammatory infiltration, impaired barrier function, and altered the gut microbiota composition (increased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio) [18]. Similarly, foodborne CDs have been shown to disrupt the gut-liver axis, promoting lipopolysaccharide translocation and exacerbating high-fat diet-induced insulin resistance and glucose homeostasis imbalance (Fig. 6c) [58]. These findings underscore the importance of evaluating CDs’ impact on intestinal health and systemic metabolism following oral exposure.

Beyond the gut, the fate of intravenously or locally administered CDs is equally important. Studies on various CDs formulations report generally good biocompatibility at therapeutic doses, with accumulation primarily in the reticuloendothelial system (liver and spleen) and eventual clearance via renal and hepatobiliary pathways. However, metal-doped CDs raise additional concerns due to potential metal ion leakage and associated toxicity, such as oxidative stress induction in non-target tissues. Strategies to mitigate these risks include using degradable matrices, optimizing surface coatings, and developing non-metal doping alternatives like phosphorus-doped CDs (P-CDs), which show excellent catalytic activity and improved biocompatibility [59]. Systematic long-term toxicity studies, including immunogenicity, reproductive toxicity, and carcinogenic potential, remain essential for advancing CDs towards clinical applications.

Interactions with plants and environmental fate

The environmental safety of CDs is paramount, especially considering their potential use in agriculture as nano-fertilizers, nanopesticides, or plant immune primers. The interaction between CDs and plants involves complex processes of uptake, translocation, and physiological response. Research indicates that CDs can be taken up by plant roots and transported to aerial parts, with their size and surface properties determining the efficiency and pathway of this transport. Once internalized, CDs can exert dual effects. At optimal concentrations, they may act as “plant strengtheners”. For example, foliar spraying of specific CDs (240 mg/L) enhanced disease resistance in tobacco, reducing gray mold incidence by 44% without compromising photosynthesis or yield [19]. This protective effect is attributed to the priming of plant immune responses and possibly the light-activated antimicrobial properties of CDs themselves.

However, at higher concentrations or with certain formulations, phytotoxic effects can occur, including inhibition of seed germination, root elongation, and chlorophyll synthesis. The degradability of CDs under environmental conditions is a critical factor for their sustainability. Encouragingly, some photosensitive CDs demonstrate gradual degradation under natural sunlight (within 90 days), as evidenced by the weakening of their characteristic FTIR and UV-vis peaks [19]. This photodegradation, potentially mediated by self-generated ROS, suggests a pathway for environmental removal. The long-term impact of CDs and their degradation products on soil health, plant growth, and food chain transfer requires continuous investigation through life-cycle assessment studies.

Towards a holistic safety framework

The biosafety of CDs cannot be described by a single parameter but must be evaluated within an integrated framework that considers the specific CDs design, intended application, and exposure scenario. Future safety assessments should: (1) Employ multi-kingdom models (microbial, animal, plant) to identify cross-species effects; (2) Investigate the long-term environmental behavior, including degradation, transformation, and bioaccumulation; (3) Standardize testing protocols to allow for comparative risk analysis across different CDs formulations. By addressing these aspects, the scientific community can guide the rational design of safer CDs and ensure their sustainable and responsible deployment in antimicrobial therapy and beyond.

A strategic roadmap for designing antimicrobial CDs: From molecular engineering to application

Conclusion and perspectives

Based on a comprehensive analysis of the latest advances in antimicrobial CDs, this review establishes an integrated knowledge framework spanning from molecular design to clinical translation. The proposed “functional synergy design” paradigm demonstrates that CDs can be engineered through bio-derived precursors, drug-molecule direction, charge modulation, metal doping, and amino acid functionalization to achieve concurrent antimicrobial, theranostic, and tissue repair capabilities. Mechanistic studies reveal that CDs exert multi-target antibacterial effects via physical disruption of membranes, ROS-mediated oxidative stress, metabolic interference, and unique biofilm penetration-attributes that significantly overcome antibiotic resistance barriers.

CDs demonstrate considerable versatility across key theranostic applications, including PDT, integrated photothermal-photodynamic systems, and real-time diagnostic imaging. This integrated capability allows for the precise elimination of pathogens while simultaneously monitoring treatment efficacy. A critical development in the field is the established “mechanism-efficacy-safety” correlation model, which helps to address previously underexplored risks. Specifically, the biological interactions of CDs depend on their size and surface chemistry, and these interactions may disrupt intestinal microbiota homeostasis and impair barrier function. For metal-doped variants, toxicity mitigation strategies are necessary. One prominent strategy is the use of non-metal dopants such as phosphorus. Environmental studies further confirm the photodegradability of CDs under natural conditions and reveal their dual role in agriculture. They can induce crop immune responses at low concentrations and also exert antifungal activity without causing phytotoxicity.

Despite these advances, several key challenges remain. In terms of material design, the precise regulation of the surface chemical properties of CDs to achieve selective antibacterial activity requires further exploration. Existing studies have shown that charge engineering and metal doping can significantly enhance antibacterial activity, but they may lead to reduced biocompatibility. Therefore, developing non-metal doping strategies (such as phosphorus doping) or preparing CDs from bio-based precursors has become an important research direction.

In terms of mechanisms of action, although CDs have been found to exert antibacterial effects through multiple targets, the molecular-level signaling pathways, particularly the interaction mechanisms with bacteria-specific proteins, still need further clarification. The application of transcriptomics and proteomics technologies provides powerful tools for unraveling these mechanisms.

In clinical applications, the theranostic integration capability of CDs offers unique advantages, but their stability, targeting ability, and long-term safety in complex physiological environments require systematic evaluation.

Future research on antibacterial CDs should focus on the following directions. One direction is developing smart responsive CDs to achieve precise antibacterial effects through pH-, enzyme-, or ROS-responsive release. Another is exploring the synergistic effects between CDs and existing antibiotics to overcome bacterial resistance. Additionally, optimizing surface modification strategies of CDs to improve their targeting ability against specific pathogens is important. Establishing standardized safety evaluation systems, particularly regarding the impact of long-term exposure on intestinal microecology and metabolic health, is also a key focus. Furthermore, promoting clinical translation research of CDs to address practical issues such as large-scale production and quality control should be emphasized. Moreover, the development of a clear regulatory framework tailored to nanotechnology-based antimicrobials is urgently needed to guide preclinical evaluation and clinical approval. Concurrently, establishing standardized protocols for scalable synthesis and rigorous characterization is essential to ensure batch-to-batch reproducibility, a key factor often overlooked in current research but critical for transitioning from lab-scale innovation to industrially viable and clinically reliable products.

With the advancement of interdisciplinary research, antibacterial CDs are expected to become novel nanomedicines for combating drug-resistant bacterial infections and promoting tissue repair, providing innovative solutions for clinical treatment. In addition, the antibacterial applications of CDs in agriculture, food packaging, and other fields deserve further exploration, but their environmental behavior and ecological risks require comprehensive assessment.
