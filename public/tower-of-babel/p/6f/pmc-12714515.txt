Purulent Pericarditis Leading to Cardiac Tamponade and Constrictive Physiology
Authors: Danielle N. Davis, Johanna Caicedo, Maxwell Uetz, Ayelet Shapira-Daniels, Grace Hsieh, Ashvin N. Pande
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 50-year-old woman with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset chest pain and epigastric pain. On arrival, she appeared uncomfortable. She was normothermic, tachycardic to 114 beats/min, and tachypneic to 40 to 50 breaths/min. Initial laboratory testing showed an elevated white blood cell count of 23,000 cells/μL (reference range: 4,000-10,000 cells/μL), lactate of 2.1 mmol/L (reference range: 0.9-1.7 mmol/L), high-sensitivity cardiac troponin of 61 ng/L (reference range: <14 ng/L), and creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL (reference range: 0.5-1.1 mg/dL), elevated from a normal baseline. Initial point-of care ultrasound showed normal cardiac systolic function, no pericardial effusion, no intra-abdominal fluid, and a normal-caliber aorta. Computed tomography angiography showed no evidence of dissection, pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, or pericardial effusion. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus tachycardia with diffuse nonspecific ST-segment and T-wave changes (Figure 1). Initial Electrocardiogram

The patient's initial electrocardiogram showed tachycardia with a rate of approximately 100 beats/min and diffuse nonspecific ST-segment and T-wave changes.

The patient remained clinically stable until approximately 12 hours after her initial presentation, when she developed progressively worsening dyspnea, hypoxia, and hypotension. Repeat laboratory values 14 hours after the initial tests were significant for an increased lactate of 4.9 mmol/L, high-sensitivity cardiac troponin of 218 ng/L, and creatinine of 2.2 mg/dL. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed no changes from prior. Approximately, 16 hours after presentation, the patient was intubated owing to hypoxic respiratory failure and was initiated on vasopressors for shock of unclear etiology.

Past Medical History

The patient was a healthy adult with a history of gastroesophageal reflux disorder and hysterectomy owing to uterine fibroids approximately 10 years before the current presentation.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial considerations for the patient's shock were obstructive, septic, and cardiogenic etiologies. The initial differential diagnoses of her chest pain were acute coronary syndrome, myopericarditis, infection, autoimmune disease, and malignancy.

Investigations

After intubation and initiation of vasopressors, an echocardiogram revealed a moderately sized pericardial effusion, measuring 1.8 cm anteriorly and 1.0 cm laterally. There was incomplete expansion of the right ventricle during diastole (Video 1) without exaggerated respiratory variation across the mitral (22%) or tricuspid valves (24%), and a dilated inferior vena cava without respiratory variation (Video 2). These findings confirmed impending cardiac tamponade and obstructive shock. An emergency bedside pericardiocentesis was performed, yielding approximately 470 mL of brown fluid with dense fibrinous material (Figure 2). After this procedure, the patient demonstrated near-immediate hemodynamic improvement. Several hours after pericardial drain placement, she was able to be weaned off vasopressors. Management

On hospital day 2, pneumococcal pneumonia was diagnosed based on a positive respiratory polymerase chain reaction and a chest x-ray with a new consolidation in the left lower lobe. Pericardial fluid cultures also returned positive for Streptococcus pneumoniae, confirming the diagnosis of purulent pericarditis, most likely from an intrathoracic focus. To evaluate for immunodeficiency, testing for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) was performed and was found to be negative. Immunoglobulin levels were also within normal limits.

The pericardial drain was removed on hospital day 3, after which the patient developed persistent chest pain, dyspnea on exertion, and jugular venous distention. A repeat echocardiogram revealed a minimal effusion, an elevated septal e′ velocity, near-restrictive mitral inflow velocity pattern, respirophasic shift of the ventricular septum, and a plethoric inferior vena cava (Videos 3 and 4), suggestive of early constrictive physiology (CP).

A cardiac catheterization was performed, which displayed exaggeration of X and Y descents on right atrial tracings and equalization of diastolic pressures, suggestive of CP (Figure 3). Discordance of the right ventricular (RV) and left ventricular (LV) pressures—also typically seen in CP—was suggested but could not be confirmed at the time. Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient was treated with intravenous penicillin, which was transitioned to oral linezolid, for a total antibiotic duration of 4 weeks. Additionally, she was treated with high-dose aspirin and long-term colchicine for management of her pericarditis. Unfortunately, she developed persistent chest pain concerning for complicated CP with either incomplete remission or recurrence. Repeat CMR obtained 6 weeks later confirmed residual pericardial inflammation. Corticosteroids were cautiously considered as the next line therapy. However, 3 months after her index admission and after a prolonged course of colchicine and aspirin, her symptoms resolved.

Discussion

We present a case of purulent bacterial pericarditis due to S. pneumoniae in a middle-aged woman with no significant past medical history and no signs or symptoms of pneumonia on initial presentation, who developed a rapidly accumulating pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, obstructive shock, and later CP. Historically, S. pneumoniae was a common cause of purulent pericarditis, often occurring as a complication of pneumococcal pneumonia. However, with the widespread use of vaccinations and antibiotics, purulent pericarditis has become exceedingly rare and is estimated to account for <1% of pericarditis cases.1

Seeding of the pericardium may occur through hematogenous spread or contiguous spread from an intrathoracic, myocardial, or subdiaphragmatic focus. S. pneumoniae is the most common organism to spread directly from an intrathoracic focus, which we believe is what occurred in this case. However, the vast majority of similar cases (>90%) have signs of pneumonia prior to the onset of purulent pneumococcal pericarditis.2 Here, the initial computed tomography study showed no evidence of pneumonia, and the patient denied any preceding cough, congestion, or fever. The absence of signs or symptoms of pneumonia on initial presentation suggests a rapidly progressing infection that led to the swift accumulation of pericardial fluid and cardiac tamponade.

CP can occur after any pericardial disease, and its full incidence is unknown.3 In this condition, the rigid pericardium restricts the expansion of the heart, particularly in diastole, causing equalization of diastolic pressures.4 In our patient, the classic signs of CP, including dyspnea on exertion and jugular venous distention, were seen just days after the development of pericardial disease. Cardiac catheterization is the gold standard diagnostic tool, and it is the guideline-recommended standard of care in any patient with suspected CP.5 Enhanced ventricular interdependence by analysis of the LV and RV pressure contours during the respiratory cycle is a highly sensitive and specific finding for CP. However, this subtle finding requires simultaneous high-fidelity micromanometer tracings in the RV and LV and is subject to numerous errors.6 A systolic area index of ≥1.1 has been shown to be highly sensitive and specific in CP.7 In our case it was retrospectively calculated to be 1.2, but discordance of the RV and LV pressures could not be confirmed at the time, so CMR was pursued.

CMR offers a noninvasive diagnostic adjunct in patients suspected of CP. Key features on CMR that are associated with CP include pericardial thickness exceeding 4 mm and pericardial contrast enhancement (with early enhancement signifying pericardial inflammation and late enhancement suggesting scar).8 A thickened pericardium (>4 mm) with late gadolinium enhancement is highly indicative of CP when there is no active inflammation.7 CMR may also elucidate ventricular coupling during real-time cine imaging with free breathing, which was seen in this case and is a specific finding for CP.3 As displayed here, CMR is a helpful diagnostic adjunct in patients with inconclusive invasive testing. Furthermore, CMR provides complementary information about pericardial thickening, effusion, and inflammation, which can help guide treatment decisions on medical therapy versus surgical intervention.

Conclusions

This case illustrates an uncommon and life-threatening presentation of purulent pericarditis due to S. pneumoniae in a previously healthy adult without initial evidence of pneumonia or an identifiable infectious source. The patient's rapid progression to cardiac tamponade and obstructive shock highlights the importance of early recognition, timely pericardial drainage, and targeted antimicrobial therapy. Additionally, the development of CP as a sequela emphasizes the need for appropriate diagnostic modalities, vigilant follow-up, and consideration of long-term consequences.Visual SummarySummary of Case PresentationImage created using BioRender (2025).
