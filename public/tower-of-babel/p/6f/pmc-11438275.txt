Commercial milk formula marketing entry points: setting the course of infant and young child feeding trajectories
Authors: Mireya Vilar-Compte, Sonia Hernández-Cordero, Rafael Pérez-Escamilla, Cecília Tomori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a limited understanding of the dynamic influences that shape infant and young child feeding (IYCF) decisions over time. We conducted an innovative qualitative study to reconstruct IYCF trajectories across early life course phases, in the context of the socioecological model (SEM) and the commercial determinants of IYCF.MethodsWomen of different socioeconomic status were interviewed in two large metropolitan areas in Mexico. Our specific goal was to allow us to better understand if and how the commercial milk formula (CMF) marketing influenced breastfeeding decisions in a complex dynamic way involving the individual, relational, community and societal levels.ResultsHospitals, health professionals, and interactions with social media were key category entry points throughout the prenatal, perinatal, early infancy period and beyond. The CMF industry interfered by engaging a wide array of actors across the different layers of the SEM, most prominently the health care system and the workplace. Through its marketing strategies the CMF operates subconsciously and its messages are most effective when health institutions, health care providers, workplace spaces and social norms are weak in their support for breastfeeding.ConclusionsThe cases in our study highlight how, together with a weak breastfeeding counseling system, and health professionals who lack training in breastfeeding and normal infant behavior, lead to the opportunity for CMF marketing to shape infant feeding, and ultimately to the decision to feed formulas that some mothers were not planning to use and cannot afford.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19997-3.Keywords: Breastfeeding, Commercial milk formula marketing, Commercial determinants of health, Socioecological model, Infant and young child feeding trajectories

Introduction

Previous studies have identified different barriers and facilitators for adequate infant and young child feeding (IYCF) across the socioecological model (SEM) in separate settings or at different timepoints [1]. However, there is a dearth of empirical research on understanding the dynamic influences that shape IYCF decisions over time. The present innovative qualitative study was designed to reconstruct IYCF trajectories across early life course phases, to uncover how different elements of the SEM interact in complex and dynamic ways with commercial determinants to influence IYCF decisions and practices.

IYCF decisions are influenced by various stakeholders and structural factors that jointly shape the perceived needs, beliefs, and attitudes of parents, caregivers, and health care providers starting from pre-conception and gestation. Understanding how early infant feeding decisions are made is crucial as these decisions strongly influence future feeding decisions in the infant and young child life. Previous work has shown that the commercial milk formula (CMF) industry asserts influence over infant feeding by engaging a diverse set of audiences from caregivers to policy makers and the general public, and across each level of the SEM [2]. However, the dynamic ways in which these influences across the layers of the SEM come together in shaping individual mothers’ decision making has not yet been fully explored. Additionally, both anthropological literature and life course theory emphasizes the importance of paying close attention to the lived experience of infant feeding over time [3, 4]. The current literature does not adequately highlight the temporal dimension that ultimately shape each infant’s unique feeding trajectory.

This study addresses these gaps and is based on an innovative early life course model that we developed focusing on the intersection of the SEM and life course perspectives (see Fig. 1). While there are different versions of the SEM, we selected one with four levels – individual, relationships, community, and societal – while there are different SEM node versions, the version that we chose has four core determinants of health that we considered essential to meet our main goal [5]. The latter was to understand and illustrate how the interaction of a group of determinants affects in a very dynamic way the IYCF decisions at different points of the life course within and across different layers of the SEM. We hypothesized that this model would allow us to better understand if there was CMF industry interference and how it worked across time in influencing IYCF decisions, including breastfeeding, at the individual, relational, community and societal levels.

The IYCF trajectories – SEM/life course model

Mapping the influence of the CMF industry to IYCF decisions

Recently, the CMF marketing playbook has been clearly described as a “multifaceted, sophisticated, well resourced, and powerful system of influence that generates demand and sales of its products at the expense of the health and rights of families, women, and children” [6]. The playbook includes a wide range of strategies, engaging caregivers, health professionals, policy makers, and the general public, which has proven to be effective as the CMF sales have grown over the past four decades from about US1.5 billion in 1978, to about US$55.6 billion in 2019 [2].

Marketing strategies create “category entry points” or “mental cue[s] that customers use to access thoughts and memories when in a buying situation” [2]. These category entry points play a critical role in IYCF decisions, but the public is rarely aware of the influence of marketing because most of its influence operates at the subconscious level. Our study focuses on mapping these points of influence and how they shape IYCF trajectories.

Brand lines are a key element of the marketing strategies in creating these entry points. Most CMF producers have brand lines, which not only includes CMF for infants, toddlers, and growing-up milks (GUMs) for older children, but also CMF for mothers, aiming at establishing brand loyalty even before the birth of a child. Specialized formulas constitute another element of the marketing strategy. These formulas are misleadingly marketed as remedies for supposedly “abnormal” baby behaviors like reflux, fussiness, and intermittent sleep during the night—behaviors that are natural and a part of normal early development that unfortunately have been pathologized for marketing purposes. Furthermore, these formula products often times also claim that they can prevent or mitigate food allergies and food without solid scientific backing, hence exploiting increasing parental confusion and concerns in these areas [1].

The CMF industry has deployed a wide range of practices to capture science by funding research and biasing its outcomes, targeting health professionals and policy makers to encourage CMF sales and optimize the business environment. The provision of support materials, funds for conferences and research, sponsor attendance at scientific meetings and training and publishing sponsored articles in scientific journals are some of the common actions to build loyalty of scientists and health professionals toward CMF-producing companies. Hence, the industry uses pseudo-science to build brand credibility and influence among health and other professionals (i.e., use of medically unsubstantiated claims for CMF products). Furthermore, through these strategies the CMF companies can influence guidelines and protocols for infant feeding and young child development [2]. Health professionals are viewed by parents and the public at large as influential and authoritative infant feeding sources, making them a particularly important target for the CMF industry, which strongly prioritizes them as category entry points [7–9]. Furthermore, the use of digital platforms and use of individual data for personalized targeting of marketing to caregivers has made the reach and tailoring CMF marketing more powerful than ever before, particularly by micro target marketing, a strategy that involves rapidly analyzing and leveraging consumer data to deliver timely and highly personalized marketing messages tailored to a person’s demographics, buying habits, and online behavior. Micro-target marketing is often used in digital advertising, where technology allows for the collection and analysis of vast amounts of user data to identify and reach very specific segments of the market with tailored messages [6, 10]. For example, the use of digital platforms and individual data for personalized targeting of marketing to caregivers could involve a CMF company using online data combined with AI to identify and target new or expecting parents. For instance, the company might use data from sources such baby store registries or parenting forums to personalized ads through social media platforms, search engines, or email, emphasizing the convenience, nutritional goodness, equivalence or superiority of their formula over breastfeeding. Furthermore, the ads are specifically designed to resonate with the concerns or lifestyle of the expecting parents, making the marketing more impactful.

The aim of this study was to describe key areas of influence over IYCF decisions among mothers of two metropolitan areas in Mexico and assess if feeding trajectories, from pregnancy to toddlerhood (up to 18 months of age) were influenced by CMF marketing in an environment of limited breastfeeding support.

In Mexico, breastfeeding practices have improved to some extent in the last decade during which exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) in children < 6 months increased from 14 to 33.6% [11, 12], although this practice remains far from the global World Health Assembly goals of 70% EBF by 2030. Mexico needs to overcome strong challenges to substantially improve breastfeeding outcomes through the implementation at scale of evidence-based breastfeeding promotion, protection, and support interventions [1]. For this to happen the government needs to invest much more to co-design, implement, monitor, and evaluate breastfeeding policies and programs [13]. Special attention needs to be paid to regulate the often times misleading marketing of CMF and other related products, including in digital media, in accordance with the International Code of Marketing of Breast Milk Substitutes and subsequent resolutions [14]. Mexico urgently needs a breastfeeding strategy coordinated through an empowered and well-funded entity to coordinate and help track goals to inform decision makers from the national to the municipal level [15, 16].

Methods

Study design

We undertook a qualitative analysis of secondary data from the Mexican component of the WHO/UNICEF multi-country study, which aimed to map out the marketing strategies employed by the CMF industry and explore how these strategies affected consumer perceptions and exposure to CMF, as well as mothers’ attitudes and perceptions regarding breastfeeding [17].

Data collection

Data were collected through convenience sampling in the two largest metropolitan areas of Mexico, Mexico City and Guadalajara, between 2020 and 2021. The current analysis focused on 10 in-depth interviews of mothers who were at least 18 years old and had an infant or young child 0 to 18 months old. The selection of mothers for the in-depth interviews provided heterogeneity in terms of socioeconomic level, infants’ age, mothers’ marital status, employment, and education level, as well as feeding practices. All interviews were carried out in Spanish and took place in the homes of the mothers or at any other convenient place for them. On average, interviews lasted 90 min and the semi-structured interview guide can be found in the Supplementary Material S1. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects.

Qualitative analysis

The in-depth interviews underwent an initial round of thematic analysis to identify key areas of influence on infant feeding decisions. Codes were developed by MVC and SHC and discussed extensively with the rest the other authors and by triangulating interview text with existing literature on IYCF decisions. These codes were developed into visual icons that were plotted across the life course and by the level of SEM where they were operating. Next, a second round of analysis was undertaken to identify CMF industry marketing entry points, understood as specific channels or opportunities that the CMF industry uses to introduce or promote their products to potential consumers and that influenced infant feeding decisions across each participant’s infant feeding trajectory. This allowed us to map and integrate the evidence of CMF industry marketing across the layers of the SEM. This part of the analysis was based on the prior characterization of the CMF industry playbook [1, 6] and led by MVC and CT. The CMF industry marketing playbook involves the deployment of a system that is sophisticated, agile, sustained, and exceptionally well resourced that influences families, health professionals, as well as scientific and policy processes to turn the birth of an infant and the care of parents into a business opportunity, undermining breastfeeding [2]. Category entry points from pregnancy through toddlerhood were discussed by the entire author group. Case studies with illustrative quotes for these entry points were selected based on different infant feeding trajectories.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of all the women who participated in the in-depth interviews. On average mothers were 25 years old and the infants and young children were 5.8 months (range 1 month to 14 months), the majority were married or living in civil union (i.e., only one woman described herself as single). One-third had delivered their last baby through a C-section, which is lower than the national prevalence of birth by C-Sect. (45%) [18]. Two-thirds were primiparas. Nearly all of the women (90%) had breastfed at some point, but only two (20%) declared to never had fed their child with CMF. This reflects the sampling approach that focused on representation of different infant feeding practices.

In the initial round of analysis, we found that participants described multiple determinants that affected their breastfeeding decisions that operated across the SEM. Some specific challenges of the maternal-child dyad, such as being born premature, had an influence on infant feeding decisions at the individual level. Family, friends, and social media mostly asserted their influence at the relational level; breastfeeding counseling, and health care providers mainly operated at the relational level, while hospitals asserted their influence at the community level; employment and workplace dynamics as described by the mothers shaped decisions at the community level. Furthermore, the women’s narratives indirectly suggested the influence of the societal level through limited maternity leave mandates. Lastly social level factors, such as stigmatization of breastfeeding in public, asserted its influence at the societal level (see Table 2).

Thematic analysis triangulating interview text with existing literature on infant feeding decisions

The robust thematic coding methodology followed allowed to identify the multiple factors operating at different levels of the SEM in shaping infant feeding decisions. Specifically, eight determinants were identified: family, friends, breastfeeding counseling, health care providers, hospitals and clinics, social media, the workplace, and breastfeeding related legislation. Figure 2 presents such determinants as visual icons and depicts them across the IYCF trajectories-SEM/life course model. It’s important to note that while these determinants are mapped to specific levels, different layers of the SEM interact with some of these determinants. For example, breastfeeding counseling interacts at the individual, relationship, and community levels. Equally important is that CMF was mentioned in relation to each of these determinants.

Determinants of IYCF trajectories – SEM/life course model

In the second round of analysis, we examined the category entry points for CMF industry marketing in relation to the determinants discussed by each participant over the course of their infant feeding trajectories. The influence of CMF marketing was evident and ubiquitous across these trajectories. To illustrate CMF industry influence over time, we present in detail 4 cases from the 10 women interviewed who followed distinct IYCF trajectories. Specifically, we present the infant feeding trajectories of one mother who followed optimal breastfeeding practices, another mother who formula fed since very early on after birth, another who mixed fed, and a mother with a young toddler, who also formula fed and continued to experiment with a variety of products into toddlerhood. These four mothers were between 21 and 32 years old, and infant and young children age ranged from 1 month to 14 months. Additional characteristics are summarized in Table 3.

Prenatal entry points

Participant interviews revealed multiple prenatal marketing entry points. One of them was prenatal education that downplays the importance of breastfeeding or presents CMF as a scientific or even superior option. Mothers received education from health care providers or hospitals, but they also received it from social media. For instance, the formula-feeding mother in Case 3 explained that during pregnancy she participated on an online club sponsored by the CMF industry in which “A doctor gave a conference online, she was talking about nutrition, about the benefits milk has, what the formula is, DHA, and all the ingredients it has and what the benefits are for the baby. In case the baby is lactose intolerant they have lactose-free formula”.

Entry points at the hospital and during the early infancy period

There were multiple manifestations of CMF entry points shortly after birth. For instance, in the case of the mixed feeding mother in Case 1, her infant was formula fed at the hospital based on the perceived need by her providers that it was needed to prevent neonatal hyperglycemia based on the incorrect assumption CMF was needed because women produce inadequate milk volumes during the first days after birth. “In the hospital they fed her with formula … they gave me the bottles. Babies can have hypoglycemia if they are not fed with milk during the first days. To avoid that, they (providers of babies born in a private clinic) fed them with milk (referring to CMF).”

During early infancy, health care providers, hospital staff and hospital policies played a particularly important role in shaping infant feeding decisions. For instance, the mixed feeding mother in Case 1 was advised to use formula by her pediatrician even though breastfeeding was going well: “The doctor told me to use formula and I did so, but I noticed it wasn’t good for her. If my body produces something for her, I don’t need to look for more options. If my milk wasn’t good for her, it is awesome that I can have formula as an option, I’m not against formula.” Despite not fully complying with her doctor’s recommendation as she continued breastfeeding, she was conflicted about formula recommendations, which eventually led to mix-feeding.

Poor breastfeeding counseling at the hospital also presented opportunities for very early exposure CMF marketing. For the exclusively formula feeding mother in Case 3, hospital staff undermined her belief in her ability to breastfeed and staff also mistreated her: “First they brought him to my breast. I had enough milk but they told me my baby wasn’t having enough; he was crying all the time and they yelled at me! I was trying but nothing happened! He was crying all the time. They told me to keep trying until they discharged me”. The lack of skilled support was also manifest in the experience of the mother in Case 4 who also ended up formula feeding because she was told that she did not produce milk: “Because I don’t have milk … (in the hospital) they made me do some exercises to stimulate and they didn’t work. And they told me how to do it, but nothing came out. Back at the hospital they gave me formula.” It is very uncommon for mothers not to produce milk at all, and most of these medical conditions should be identified prenatally [19]. It is much more likely that hospital staff was unable to resolve breastfeeding challenges, and instead they quickly moved to feeding with CMF. Others also had poor experiences with breastfeeding counseling. For instance, the mixed feeding mother in Case 1 experienced pain with her previous birth. She explained that “Some women bleed, and they don’t want to keep trying because it hurts. They prefer formula instead of pain.” Lack of skilled support can lead to pain, which provides opportunity for CMF to be presented as the solution.

Some mothers received minimal or no counseling during this early infancy period, such as the mother in Case 4, who ultimately ended up formula feeding, and being exposed to brands and specialized solutions to problems she perceived with her baby: “He’s had reflux and constipation ever since he was born (until about 6 months) … I tried lactose-free NAN, but it made no difference … From NAN to Novamil, from Novamil … back to NAN. And then to Enfagrow, but I couldn’t give him that one because it was too expensive”.

Some mothers were able to identify that hospital policies made a big difference ahead of the birth. For instance, in Case 2, the mother with the most successful exclusive breastfeeding experience stated “During the second trimester I realized that hospital policies can encourage or discourage that process … the policies say that they can feed the baby with formula if the mom doesn’t have milk and that makes breastfeeding harder. Breastfeeding was not the problem; the problem was choosing where I wanted to have my baby.” This level of awareness of differences in hospital policies and practices was rare. Even in this case, the mother was still aware that many people had bad experiences and was concerned: “My husband and I took courses and that helped me to be more comfortable with it … I heard a lot of painful experiences, fever, and blood… I was convinced that I wanted to breastfeed, but I was afraid!”

Entry points in late infancy & toddlerhood

For most of the participants, there was a sense that CMF would always be necessary at some point. For example, exclusively formula feeding mother in Case 3 stated, “The baby needs to continue drinking milk and someday the baby will drink formula anyway”. Even the mother in Case 2, who was most successful in her breastfeeding was unsure how long breastfeeding would continue to “work.” When asked for how long to breastfeed, she stated, “For six months and I heard from people that a year is enough. I’ve also heard that after a year it doesn’t work anymore, I don’t know if that’s true or not”. Statements such as the prior ones, suggest that the CMF industry works from pregnancy to toddlerhood in reinforcing the message that there are formulas for all the infancy and early childhood stages and for special needs that infants and toddlers may have. For example, the exclusively formula feeding mother in Case 3 had been exposed to such information through an online CMF sponsored club “They (health professionals hired by the CMF industry) talk about different topics – a baby’s development, which formula is better, what to do in different cases. It is like having a pediatrician… I feel safe!“.

The other formula feeding mother in Case 4, who represented the trajectory of a young woman who experimented with numerous CMF in the post-natal period, some of which were too expensive for her to buy, also opted to formula feed her toddler with GUMs. Despite the cost of GUM, she explained “I plan to give him formula until he’s 3 years old because I tried with cow’s milk, and it didn’t work out … I gave him Alpura (a local brand of cow’s milk). But he didn’t like it, and he’d poop but he’d go 4 times a day”.

Work and time management as an industry entry point

CMF was often perceived to save time, and as an easier infant feeding mode. As one of the mothers expressed “Breastfeeding takes a lot of time and if we are supported it makes it easier. I have friends that are alone, there’s no one to help them, their husbands don’t have enough time”. These issues are particularly salient for working mothers as exemplified by the mother in Case 4 who planned to do mixed feeding from the start: “I wanted to feed him both, because I was planning to start working, so I wanted to breastfeed him and give him formula”. Such perceptions ultimately influence beliefs and practices in the post-natal period. For example, the same mother stated “(Formula) is good because when you’re not with the baby, you can prepare it and leave it for them, it’s the only good part about it. And as I said, it helps them with their development”.

However, the comfort and versality attached to CMF were not restricted to employed mothers. As exemplified by the exclusively formula feeding mother in Case 3, “When I was breastfeeding him I had no time, I only had one hour to do everything. I had to change his diaper, I had to bathe him, and I was busy all the time. With the formula I just give him the bottle and he falls asleep. It is like having no baby!”.

Discussion

Our study documents pervasive CMF industry interference with breastfeeding across all early life course stages (Fig. 3), from pregnancy to toddlerhood. In addition, and as highlighted in Fig. 3, CMF interferes in all the levels and determinants and the persistent feedback loops that the CMF industry generates are very efficient in influencing caregivers’ IYCF choices. Though the industry directly interferes in all layers of the SEM, indirectly it also exploits weaknesses in systems of support and the regulatory environment for breastfeeding, such as lack of compliance with the international CMF marketing code [20], inadequate enforcement of CMF marketing restrictions, and limited or inexistent protections of breastfeeding at the workplace, among others. Overall, these findings are consistent with previous studies that documented the pervasive influence of CMF marketing [2] although none of those studies integrated the findings in the dynamic way that we did. Our findings are highly innovative not only because we clearly identify diverse marketing entry points for CMFs but also because we are able to describe how key marketing pathways work in relation to other determinants of breastfeeding in the SEM over the IYCF life course. In this context, the determinants of breastfeeding refer to factors that influence whether, for how long, and to what degree exclusive breastfeeding takes place among infants and young children. These determinants are spread throughout the SEM levels, including support and influence of family, friends, and healthcare providers, the availability and accessibility of breastfeeding counseling, and the breastfeeding cultural norms, media influence, and policies. On the other hand, the key CMF marketing entry points identified included mothers and other caregivers, healthcare providers, scientists, professional organizations, and policymakers. These entry points can occur at various life course stages, from pre-natal to post-natal care, and interact dynamically with the determinants of breastfeeding undermining the maternal infant feeding choice to breastfeed. Thes entry points are very strategic from a CMF marketing perspective as they are situated at the different levels of the socioecological model levels and collectively have a strong dynamic influence in very sensitive moments for the proper establishment of lactation during the life course. In Fig. 3 the CMF marketing entry points are portrayed by the overarching “cloud” labeled “CMF industry interferences”, as well as by the feedback loops across different levels and time points.

Influence of CMF marketing on IYCF trajectories – SEM/life course model

The pervasive influence of CMF marketing across infant and young child feeding trajectories

Marketing interference started during the prenatal period, via social media and health professionals, and played a very important role in the playbook. For example, in the case of the exclusively formula feeding mother in Case 3, exposure to marketing was very strong during pregnancy by participating in online clubs that provided ostensibly scientific information about formula. This exposure then created opportunities for these marketing messages to be remembered and acted upon during the postnatal period. The marketing messages were so effective [10] that they counteracted hospital messages about the importance of breastfeeding, leading her to believe that formula feeding was superior to breastfeeding and that hospital messages about the importance of breastfeeding were the result of “brainwashing”. These findings align with prior work on the growing influence of digital marketing in the CMF playbook [2] and showcase their influence over time: prenatal messages are particularly powerful because early “priming” creates category entry points for later on.

Other women were also exposed to highly effective marketing messages that helped shape their infant feeding trajectories. For instance, while the mixed feeding mother in Case 1 clearly expressed that her family supported breastfeeding, the constant but subliminal marketing of CMFs modified her feeding decisions. Industry influence started when she was pregnant and was reinforced during the perinatal period when her infant was formula fed at the hospital based on the incorrect assumption by her providers that it was needed to prevent neonatal hyperglycemia. This message was later reinforced by her physician who recommended formula when she was successfully breastfeeding. This is consistent with prior work on the role of health care providers in transmitting marketing messages [2, 21].

Carefully orchestrated CMF marketing interference persisted well beyond infancy. GUMs have become an important sales target of the CMF industry [22, 23]. These are expensive and unnecessary products that profit from the public’s lack of understanding and misperceptions about the properties of breastmilk after the first 12 months of life [2, 10]. The marketing of GUMs has led women to perceive that such products are superior feeding choices that are worth purchasing and using [22]. This was clearly reflected in the formula feeding mother in Case 4, who planned to give her child formula milk until age 3.

CMF marketing interacts with the SEM

CMF interference seldom acted alone in influencing IYCF decisions. Rather, it interacted with other determinants across the socioecological model. CMF marketing most prominently interacted with the health system, with hospital policies during the pre- and perinatal period (e.g., providing formula to newborns as a “just in case” preventive measure, separating newborns from mothers after birth), and with health care providers both in the peri- and postnatal stages. Social media forums and groups that included health care providers amplified these impacts across the entirety of the IYCF trajectory. Health care providers have significant credibility on IYCF recommendations and, if they are an entry point of the CMF marketing, then they become an efficient vehicle in affecting feeding decision [7, 24]. While previous work has documented the importance of each of these marketing entry points [2], no other work to our knowledge has discussed how they interact with the SEM over the IYCF life course.

Hospital policies and CMF marketing

The unnecessary use of CMF at the hospital for the Case 1 mother highlights how marketing messages can take hold in the context of the lack of adequate breastfeeding training among health care providers and the failure of institutional policies at the hospital level to protect, promote and support breastfeeding. Routine use of CMF to prevent hypoglycemia is not needed, and such use both physiologically undermines breastfeeding, and reinforces marketing messages that CMF is necessary for infant health [1, 2, 25]. Based on their own descriptions, few mothers were aware that hospital policies could influence breastfeeding; instead, most mothers assumed that their breastfeeding outcomes were determined by their bodies’ innate ability or inability to breastfeed. Only the mother who optimally breastfed in Case 2 stated that these policies could shape someone’s ability to realize their breastfeeding goals.

Inadequate or insufficient breastfeeding counseling provides opportunities for the CMF industry interference

Breastfeeding counseling is known to be an effective and powerful mechanism to empower women who want to breastfeed; when problems emerge, or mothers question their ability to adequately feed their IYC through their breastmilk, adequate counseling is fundamental [1, 26]. This was exemplified in the perinatal period of the Case 2 mother (the mother who breastfed optimally), who received adequate counseling including techniques and general support. In contrast, inadequate counseling led to painful and overall poor breastfeeding experiences for other women, for which the CMF industry offered a perceived solution. This is exemplified by the mother in Case 3 who formula fed since very early on. During the perinatal stage nurses simultaneously undermined the mother’s ability to feed her baby, “demanded” the mother to breastfeed without offering appropriate support and mistreated the mother by yelling at her. Early during the post-natal period, lack of counseling led to breastfeeding problems due to inadequate positioning and the subsequent decision to formula feed the infant by the mother, which was later reinforced by the pediatrician. This particular mother had already been highly influenced by CMF marketing during the pre-natal period through social media – the category entry point had been created. When she encountered ineffective counseling and mistreatment, she acted upon the marketing messages she came across during pregnancy, since they offered a solution.

Ineffective counseling also led to formula use for the mother in Case 4, who was given formula at the hospital after staff instructed her on breastfeeding but “nothing came out.” It is highly unlikely that this mother produced no milk, but without counseling and frequent feeding she had no opportunity to learn how to breastfeed. This was another case where insufficient breastfeeding counseling and support again reinforced the inherent necessity of CMF for infant health. These findings are consistent with previous work that shows how lack of appropriate counseling can lead to ineffective breastfeeding practices, perceptions of insufficient milk, reduced exclusive breastfeeding and, ultimately, early breastfeeding cessation [1, 26, 27]. Our work adds another dimension for how inadequate counselling interacts with CMF marketing messages.

CMF marketing and the capture of health professionals

Health system weaknesses were often compounded during the postnatal period when pediatricians frequently recommended formula to mothers. For instance, the mixed feeding mother in Case 1 was told to use formula at a time when she was successfully breastfeeding her baby. The pediatrician for the formula feeding mothers in Case 3 and 4 similarly reinforced formula feeding, rather than attempting to resolve the breastfeeding problems they encountered. These pediatricians’ recommendation may have resulted from the combination of lack of adequate provider’s training and counseling skills as well as the influence of the CMF industry playbook [2]. The playbook includes enforcing the belief among healthcare providers that CMFs are needed to “supplement” breastmilk, that CMF is the automatic solution whenever breastfeeding challenges arise, the need for specialty CMFs to address various baby behaviors, and the need for GUMs into toddlerhood [2].

Health professionals also played a prominent role in the social media parenting clubs hosted by CMF companies. For instance, it was doctors who touted the health benefits of formula in the presentation viewed by the mother in Case 3, who ended up exclusively formula feeding. These presentations convinced her that formula feeding was superior to breastfeeding. Recent work by Mota-Castillo et al. [28] has highlighted that health professionals in Mexico frequently engage in educational activities sponsored by CMF companies, even appearing in videos sponsored by industry, but do not perceive these interactions as a conflict of interest or a violation of the International Code of Marketing of Breastmilk Substitutes. Our findings add to the extensive literature that documents the importance of health professionals to the CMF industry as highly valuable category entry points [2] and highlights their influence across IYCF trajectories.

The influence of CMF marketing on perceptions of normal developmental behaviors: health professionals, social media, and parents

A common piece of misinformation that influences parents’ feeding decisions is the need to use CMF to address common baby behaviors including infant crying or unsettled, intermittent sleep, or breastmilk regurgitation [1]. In most instances these are normal baby behaviors, but they have been driven by the CMF industry playbook to be perceived as “problems” that can “easily” be solved by switching to CMF [22]. The marketing about these issues begins prenatally, as reflected by the topics discussed in the social media clubs as demonstrated by the Case 3 mother. Once the baby is born, behaviors are labeled problematic and requiring formula and specialized products. This last point was poignantly demonstrated by the mother in Case 4, who was constantly switching from brand to brand upon her pediatrician’s recommendation. These findings are consistent with prior work on how marketing exploits typical, developmentally appropriate infant behavior [1, 2]. The use of the SME/life course framework underscores how the CMF marketing messages are interacting with the health system as well as individual characteristics of the mother and baby.

Work, care work and competing demands

Our findings also highlight that CMF marketing can take hold when women’s care work is not adequately supported [6]. This was especially the case for women working outside of the household. This is consistent with extensive prior work on how insufficient maternity protections and unsupportive work environments undermine breastfeeding [1, 6, 29] and provide a marketing opportunity for the CMF industry. However, the idea that formula is simpler and made infants easier to care for was also present among some staying at home. The mother in Case 3, for instance, echoed multiple misleading CMF marketing messages (formula better supports infant development than breastfeeding, formula is more convenient) and combined them with the perception of lack of support for breastfeeding at the workplace. In this case the mother perceived that CMF replaces a substantial part of infant care, “It’s like having no baby at all!”, reinforcing the CMF marketing message that CMF is part of a more convenient approach to infant care [30]. This example showcases the power of marketing messages in shaping ideas about norms of infant care and the idea that formula feeding is “liberating” for mothers [2].

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of the study is its ability to unpack the dynamics of CMF industry interference across IYCF trajectories, demonstrating how category entry points are deployed over time, how they engage with different elements of the SEM, and exploit systemic weaknesses to undermine breastfeeding and increase profits. We developed a novel framework which incorporates the systemic and dynamic relationship between the marketing entry points at different socioecological levels and through the life course of mothers and their infants and young children. To our knowledge this is the first study that has put together these different perspectives that shapes IYCF.

A limitation of the current study is the small sample of women who participated in the in-depth interviews, as well as the geographic bias, as it included only mothers of large metropolitan areas. Nevertheless, prior research has documented the effects of CMF marketing on IYCF in rural areas in Mexico [31] as well as in other urban settings [32]. In addition, when triangulating mothers’ narratives with the evidence from prior studies [1, 6], we were able to provide rich insight into the dynamics of category entry points over time. The proposed framework was developed based on the SEM and the CMF playbook, and therefore could be employed to enlighten the dynamics elsewhere and to explore how marketing entry points are tailored across geographic regions.

Another potential limitation is a recall bias of mother’s experiences and their influence in their infant feeding decisions. However, the pool of mother entails women at different stages of their infant’s life trajectory and their narratives lead to the CMF entry points described in the recently published Lancet Breastfeeding Series [6]. These narratives become even richer among multipara women, as they also bring their prior experiences.

Conclusion

Our study offers a unique view into the pervasive influence of CMF marketing interference across the IYCF trajectories of mothers in Mexico. The cases in our study highlight how, together with a weak breastfeeding counseling system, and health professionals who lack training in breastfeeding and normal infant behavior, lead to the opportunity for CMF marketing to shape infant feeding, and ultimately to the decision to feed formulas that some mothers cannot afford. Hospitals, health professionals, and interactions with social media created many opportunities for category entry points (or mental reminders of the product) throughout the prenatal, perinatal and early infancy period and persisted into and well beyond infancy, ensuring that CMF would be considered necessary throughout the entire period. CMF marketing messages operate primarily at the subconscious level, making it difficult to consciously recognize their influence. Additionally, they engage a wide array of actors across layers of the SEM, most prominently the health system and the workplace. CMF marketing messages are most effective when institutions and health care providers cannot provide appropriate counseling and support to mothers and their infants, the workplace offers little support, and care work is devalued. In these situations, the CMF industry finds fertile ground to interfere with feeding decisions and offers an array of products to shape perceptions about infant feeding and solve “problematic” infant behavior.
