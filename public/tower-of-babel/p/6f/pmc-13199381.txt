Widespread atypical response regulator SecR governs bacterial growth on methylamines through the serine cycle
Authors: Jia Xu, Tao Li, Ning-Yi Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial utilization of methylamines (MAs) exerts profound effects on biogeochemical carbon cycles, yet the regulatory mechanisms enabling bacterial adaptation to fluctuating MAs availability remain elusive. Here, we report the discovery of previously unknown atypical response regulators, designated SecRs. They exhibit homology to RcsB from the two-component regulatory system but lack the conserved phosphorylation motif and a cognate kinase. SecRs serve as the gatekeeper of the serine cycle, an essential one-carbon assimilation pathway required for bacterial growth on MAs. Remarkably, Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ550 encodes both chromosomal and plasmid-borne secR homologs that differentially regulate identical targets encoding the serine cycle enzymes. Inactivation of secRs in strain NyZ550 abolishes its growth on MAs. Biochemical analyses demonstrate that purified SecRs form stable dimers capable of direct binding to the promoters of their own genes and those of the serine cycle genes, consequently activating transcription. SecR homologs are distributed among diverse terrestrial and marine methylotrophs, and frequently adjacent to the serine cycle genes in four distinct genomic arrangements, demonstrating that SecR-mediated regulation is conserved despite divergent evolutionary rearrangements of the serine cycle genes. The findings highlight the crucial role of SecR regulation in enabling microbial adaptation to biogeochemically important one-carbon compounds across diverse environments.Subject terms: Bacterial genes, Bacterial transcription

Introduction

Methylamines (MAs), including monomethylamine (MMA), dimethylamine (DMA), and trimethylamine (TMA) along with its oxidized derivative trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), are important compounds in global biogeochemical cycles of carbon and nitrogen. Derived from microbial degradation of nitrogen-rich organic substrates (e.g., proteins, quaternary ammonium compounds) and plant secondary metabolites1–4, their global flux, primarily from marine systems, reaches 0.6 Tg N yr⁻¹1, while animal husbandry contributes an additional 0.15 Tg N yr⁻¹2. Marine-atmosphere exchange releases MAs into air masses, where oxidation generates secondary organic aerosols5,6. These aerosols enhance cloud condensation nuclei activity by altering hygroscopicity and UV scattering, as observed during phytoplankton blooms in coastal upwelling zones such as California5, thereby influencing regional cloud microphysics and climate5,6.

Despite their widespread sources, MAs persist at strikingly low environmental concentrations (nM–μM) owing to rapid microbial assimilation7–10. MAs serve as critical nitrogen sources for diverse terrestrial and marine microorganisms, including methylotrophs and many heterotrophs11,12. Microbial MA metabolism proceeds through two main pathways: (1) anaerobic methylotrophic methanogenesis, and (2) oxidative demethylation via either TMAO-dependent (predominant) or dehydrogenase-mediated routes producing formaldehyde—a pivotal one-carbon (C1) intermediate driving downstream metabolic processes13,14. The resulting C1 units are assimilated through three principal pathways: the serine cycle (dominant in alphaproteobacteria), the ribulose monophosphate (RuMP) pathway, or the Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle14,15.

While MAs metabolic routes are well-characterized, the current understanding of their regulatory mechanisms remains fragmented. Regarding primary MAs oxidation, Lidbury et al.16 proposed a putative GntR-family transcriptional repressor potentially associated with TMA degradation in Ruegeria pomeroyi DSS-3, though the underlying regulatory mechanism remains unknown. For downstream C1 assimilation, regulation of the CBB cycle has been well-studied and relies on a LysR-type activator (CbbR), with its encoding gene typically positioned adjacent to cbb operons17,18. Notably, Kalyuzhnaya and Lidstrom19 characterized a CbbR-like regulator QscR that governs the serine cycle in Methylobacterium extorquens AM1, marking the first and only transcriptional regulator of serine cycle identified to date. They proved that formyl-tetrahydrofolate (intermediates of the tetrahydrofolate recycling) could increase DNA-binding activity of QscR19. Intriguingly, transposon mutagenesis suggested that scyR, which encodes a LuxR-type helix-turn-helix DNA-binding domain regulator, may regulate serine cycle expression in Paracoccus aminovorans JCM 768520; however, its mechanism remains enigmatic.

Two-component signal transduction (TCS) systems, comprising a histidine kinase (HK) sensor and a response regulator (RR), enable bacteria to adapt to environmental changes through a conserved His-Asp phosphorelay mechanism21,22. Atypical RRs (aRRs) are orphan regulators that lack cognate kinases and are independent of Asp phosphorylation. Instead, they employ divergent activation strategies and often restricted to specific lineages22–25. For instance, actinobacterial global regulator GlnR undergoes activity modulation via posttranslational Ser/Thr/Tyr phosphorylation or acetylation23, while JadR1 from Streptomyces venezuelae appears to be regulated through direct interaction with the specialized metabolite jadomycin B24. Besides, the Synechococcus PCC 7942 nitrogen regulator NblR may require protein-protein interaction with NarB for its functional activation25. The transcriptional regulation by aRRs is an emerging investigative focus of immense interest due to their diverse roles in regulating bacterial physiological processes including capsule formation, replication, motility, biosynthesis, etc.

This study, focusing on the regulation of C1 assimilation during MAs metabolism, identifies two homologous aRRs, designated SecRs, from the NarL/FixJ-family26 in Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ55027. This heterotrophic methylotroph, isolated from a wastewater treatment plant, utilizes MAs, metformin, and 1-methylbiguanide for growth, showing promise for industrial nitrogenous wastewater treatment27. Functional analyses revealed that both SecRs in strain NyZ550 played redundant yet hierarchical roles, activating the serine cycle genes expression and exhibiting positive autoregulation to form a coherent regulatory cascade. Biochemical characterization demonstrated that SecRs function as phosphorylation-independent homodimers, directly binding a conserved DNA motif without requiring an inducer molecule. A comprehensive bioinformatic search revealed that SecR homologs are widely conserved across diverse terrestrial and marine alphaproteobacteria, suggesting an evolutionarily conserved regulatory strategy for MAs metabolism with potential implications for C1 compounds cycling in natural ecosystems.

Results

Genes responsible for MAs assimilation in Aminobacter sp. NyZ550

The methylotrophic bacterium Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ550 utilizes the type 2 diabetes drug metformin, converting it to MAs as catabolic intermediates, which then serve as the sole carbon and nitrogen source for growth27. Bioinformatic analyses of the strain NyZ550 genome revealed the complete set of genes responsible for MAs assimilation (Fig. 1A, B), encompassing those encoding enzymes for the MAs-oxidizing catabolic pathway, tetrahydrofolate (THF) recycling, and the serine cycle. These genes are distributed in three gene clusters. The trimethylamine oxidation genes (tmm and tdm), dimethylamine monooxygenase genes (dmmABCD) and THF recycling genes (folD and fthS) are clustered in the chromosome. The monomethylamine catabolic genes and serine cycle genes form two separate clusters are located on the plasmid designated pMET (Fig. 1B).

Based on this genetic organization and functional predictions, key genes representing core steps of the MAs oxidation pathway (tmm for TMA oxidation, dmmABC for DMA oxidation, and gmaS for MMA oxidation) were selected for knockout mutagenesis to validate their predicted roles in MAs catabolism and assess the possibility of alternative pathways. To validate these predictions, key gene knockout mutants were generated for growth assays. The results confirmed the functionality of the putative MAs oxidation genes and ruled out the possible presence of alternative MAs catabolic pathways in strain NyZ550 (Fig. 1C). Notably, despite varying carbon yields from MMA (1× CH2 = THF), DMA (2×), and TMA (3×), wild-type strain NyZ550 achieved comparable final biomass levels when grown on 4 mM substrate (Fig. 1C). The comparable biomass levels were also retained in the tmm mutant when grown on DMA and MMA (Fig. 1C), suggesting that nitrogen release may represent the principal metabolic constraint.

To determine whether the aforementioned genes were actively transcribed during growth on MAs, a comparative transcriptome analysis was performed using strain NyZ550 (WT) cells grown in minimal salts medium supplemented with glucose (carbon source) and NH4NO3 (nitrogen source), or with TMA, DMA, or MMA (carbon and nitrogen source). As shown in Fig. 1D, expression of MAs-catabolizing genes, THF recycling genes and serine cycle genes was consistently induced in all three MAs-grown cultures compared to glucose-grown cells. Co-transcriptional analysis revealed that these genes are transcribed primarily as four operons (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1). The real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis of these MAs metabolic genes from the operons also echoes the transcriptomic data (Supplementary Fig. 2). These results indicate that expression of these genes is specifically induced during MA assimilation by strain NyZ550, consistent with the catabolic pathway elaborated in Fig. 1A.

Discovery of the methylamines catabolism regulator in strain NyZ550

To explore potential regulators for the transcriptional regulation of MAs catabolism in strain NyZ550, the hypothetical transcriptional regulator proteins adjacent to the genes for MAs assimilation were annotated in silico, resulting in five retrieves shown in Fig. 1B. Subsequently, knockout mutants of each regulator gene were constructed, and their growth assays were performed to test whether the genes were involved in the regulation of MAs catabolism. It turned out that strain NyZ550-ΔsecR1 exhibited a severe growth retardation (~50 h) in liquid minimal media with MAs as the sole source of carbon and nitrogen. In contrast, deletion of genes secR2, gntR, xre1 or xre2 had no effect on growth (Fig. 2A–F). The growth retardation of ΔsecR1 could be largely restored by complementation of secR1 (ΔsecR1::secR1) using its native promoter (Fig. 2D–F). These results indicate that SecR1, a NarL/FixJ-family response regulator, plays a crucial role in MAs metabolism in strain NyZ550.

SecR2, a homolog of SecR1, is also involved in the growth on MAs

Although the ΔsecR1 strain exhibited severely delayed growth on MAs, it ultimately reached stationary-phase biomass comparable to the wild-type strain after approximately 80 h (Fig. 2D–F). Moreover, this delayed-growth phenotype was stably maintained over multiple generations (Supplementary Fig. 3). Furthermore, expression of MAs assimilation genes remained induced during growth of ΔsecR1 on DMA (Fig. 1D). These observations prompted the hypothesis that a compensatory regulator might exist in strain NyZ550 for MAs assimilation. Therefore, a global search of secR1 homolog was performed across the whole genome of strain NyZ550. Surprisingly, the best hit (74% amino acid identity) corresponded to the previously identified gene secR2, which was annotated within operon II during our initial regulator screen for regulators (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, transcriptomic data also revealed MAs-induced upregulation of secR2 transcription in wild type. This expression pattern, similar to that of secR1 (Fig. 1d), implies possible regulatory activity of SecR2.

Notably, the ΔsecR2 strain exhibited a similar growth pattern on MAs to that of wild-type (Fig. 2A–C), likely due to the compensatory effect of SecR1. Therefore, a secR1 and secR2 double knockout strain (designated ΔsecR1-R2) was generated to investigate their synergistic regulatory effect. Resultantly, the growth capacity of the double knockout mutant on MAs was completely abolished, even with a culture time up to 140 h (Fig. 2G–I). However, growth was partially restored in the secR2 complemented strain ΔsecR1-R2::secR2 (with growth retardation), and fully restored in the secR1 complemented strain ΔsecR1-R2::secR1 (without growth retardation), using their own native promoters. This restoration pattern aligns with the growth phenotypes observed in the ΔsecR1 and ΔsecR2 single-deletion strains, respectively. Meanwhile, complementation of the secR1 and secR2 in double knockout strain fully restored the growth on MAs (Fig. 2G–I). Together, these results indicate that both SecR1 and SecR2 regulate the growth of strain NyZ550 on MAs. Nonetheless, the observation of secR1 and secR2 with different effects on growth phenotype suggests their non-redundant functions for MA catabolism in this strain.

Identification of the serine cycle regulators (SecRs) in strain NyZ550

Having demonstrated the regulatory role of SecRs in MAs assimilation, gene targets of SecRs were sought in strain NyZ550. No significant difference in differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was observed between DMA-treated single mutants of ΔsecR1 or ΔsecR2 cells and wild-type cells based on transcriptome analysis. In contrast, for the double mutant ΔsecR1-R2 cells, two plasmid-borne MAs-induced gene clusters no longer responded to DMA (Fig. 1D). Of them, one gene cluster (operon II) consists of the glyA and ppc genes, and the other cluster (operon IV) contains the sga, mcl, mtkB, mtkA, hpr and gck genes (Fig. 1D). Coincidentally, all of these genes are required for the serine cycle (Fig. 1A). The transcription of MAs-induced genes including those in operons I and III remained unaffected in ΔsecR1-R2, indicating SecRs-independent regulation and also suggesting alternative regulatory mechanisms for their transcriptional activation.

To verify these observations, glyA, ppc, sga and mtkA were chosen as representative genes for evaluation of the transcription levels of genes involved in serine cycle by RT-qPCR. Results confirmed the transcriptome data, showing DMA-induced transcriptional upregulation in wild-type, ΔsecR1 and ΔsecR2 strains but not in ΔsecR1-R2 (Fig. S4a). This indicates SecR1 and SecR2 jointly activate transcription of operons II and IV. Furthermore, promoter DNA regions of operons II and IV (designated Pppc and Psga, respectively) were fused with the lacZ gene for construction of promoter reporter systems in strain NyZ550. The results demonstrated DMA-inducible β-galactosidase activity in wild-type, ΔsecR1 and ΔsecR2 cells, but not in ΔsecR1-R2 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). These findings confirm positive coregulation of operons II and IV by SecR1 and SecR2.

To eliminate potential interference from endogenous regulatory systems in Aminobacter sp. NyZ550, promoter activity assays were replicated in E. coli cells upon heterologous expression of SecRs under the control of an IPTG-inducible promoter. The activities of PsecR1, Pppc and Psga increased with the expression of either SecR1 or SecR2 (Supplementary Fig. 4d, e). The ability of SecR1 and SecR2 to activate Pppc and Psga expression in E. coli is consistent with the results observed in strain NyZ550, suggesting that both SecR1 and SecR2 are functional when expressed in E. coli. For the PsecR1 promoter, both SecR1 and SecR2 activated its expression in E. coli. However, in strain NyZ550, promoter activity remained inducible regardless of the presence of secR1 or secR2 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). This regulatory divergence implies the involvement of auxiliary regulatory components controlling secR1 expression in strain NyZ550. Overall, these results indicate that both SecR1 and SecR2 positively regulate the transcription of serine cycle genes spread in two plasmid-borne operons, while maintaining their positive autoregulatory functions.

SecR1 and SecR2 are atypical response regulator proteins

Bioinformatic analyses of SecR1 and SecR2 revealed that both contained a hypothetical N-terminal receiver domain (REC) and a C-terminal DNA binding domain (DBD), same as the domain architecture of typical RRs of TCS (Fig. 3A). Both SecR1 and SecR2 were predicted to be the NarL/FixJ family RRs, and closest to the RcsB clade RRs (InterPro entry: IPR051015) (Fig. 3B). Sequence-based secondary structure analysis indicated that the REC domains of SecR1 and SecR2 had the canonical (βα)5 fold (β1-β5 and α1-α5), and the C-terminal DBD domains present the (α)4 fold (α7-α10) employing a predicted LuxR-like helix-turn-helix (HTH) fold (Supplementary Fig. 5). Typical RcsB-subfamily RRs, which activates via conserved Asp phosphorylation in its REC domain by cognate HKs, result in modulation of gene expression28. Additionally, RcsB-subfamily RRs generally maintain genomic co-occurrence with their partner HKs, reflecting their functional coupling in bacterial signal transduction. Strikingly, both SecR1 and SecR2 were found to be atypical RRs, lacking not only the cognate HKs (Fig. 1B) but also the essential phosphorylation Asp residue (Fig. 3C).

SecR1 and SecR2 comprise 233 and 215 amino acids, respectively. SecR1 was predicted to contain an additional N-terminal random coil sequence at 20 AA length (Fig. 3A). Heterologous expression of full-length SecR1 and SecR2 with C-terminal 6×His tags in E. coli cells yielded soluble products at expected molecular weights (~28 kDa and ~26 kDa, respectively; Fig. 3D). Size exclusion chromatography (SEC) determined apparent molecular weights of 58.9 kDa for SecR1 and 52.8 kDa for SecR2 (Fig. 3e), indicating their stable dimer formation in solution. β-galactosidase activity assays implied that heterologously expressed SecR1 and SecR2 in E. coli cells were active (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Together, these results indicate that SecR1 and SecR2 likely function as innate homodimers (Fig. 3E). Phosphorylation assays using purified recombinant SecR1 and SecR2 proteins suggested that no detectable phosphorylation could be observed under these experimental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6). These preliminary results suggest that their activation mechanism may differ from the canonical phosphorylation-dependent dimerization of RRs in TCS, though further validation would be needed to confirm this possibility.

Homodimeric SecR is competent to bind the promoters of serine cycle genes

Electrophoretic Mobility Shift Assay (EMSA) was employed to investigate the interaction between purified SecRs and potential promoter-containing DNA fragments. Five genomic regions were selected as detection targets, comprising 300–800 bp upstream sequences of four transcriptional units (operons I-IV) and a 423 bp putative promoter region upstream of the SecR1 coding gene (designated PfolD, Pppc, PmgsA, Psga, and PsecR1). As illustrated in Fig. 4A and S7, SecR1 and SecR2 formed protein–DNA complexes with Pppc, Psga, and PsecR1. In contrast, no protein–DNA complex formation was detected with PfolD and PmgsA, in agreement with the results from RT-qPCR and promoter activity assays. Generally, SecR2 showed a higher affinity to target promoters (e.g., Kd 0.60 μM for PsecR1) than that of SecR1 (e.g., Kd 0.07 μM for PsecR1), though SecR2’s inherent instability led to non-specific interactions and diffuse bands with increasing protein concentration.

The SecR1-P secR1 interaction was used as a representative example to precisely map the DNA-binding sites. Five overlapping short subfragments (P1–P5) covering full length of PsecR1 were generated for EMSA (Supplementary Fig. 8). With the exception of P1 and P4, all subfragments exhibited shifted bands in the presence of SecR1. These three subfragments share a conserved 26-bp overlapping sequence 5’-GGCGGCCTACCCGAACGGGTGGGTAA-3’, which was subsequently confirmed as the SecR1 binding site through DNase I footprinting assay (Fig. 4B). The transcription start site (TSS) of secR1 is determined at 91 bp upstream of the translation start codon by 5’-RACE (Supplementary Fig. 9), and the SecR1 binding site is positioned at −124 to −98 relative to TSS. Moreover, DNase I footprinting analyses revealed extended protected sections spanning 65 nt in Pppc and 63 nt in Psga (Supplementary Fig. 10), contrasting sharply with the precisely defined 26-bp binding site in PsecR1. Motif analysis using MEME29 identified a core consensus sequence (5’-CCYACCCG-3’) and its imperfect inverted repeat sequence (5’-CGGGTRGG-3’) (Fig. 4C).

To determine whether the palindromic sequence was essential for SecR1 binding, various mutations were executed on the core palindromic motif within PsecR1 for EMSA. Three conserved nucleotide positions (sites 2, 4, and 7) in 5’-CCYACCCG-3’ were individually substituted with non-cognate bases (A → G, C → A), followed by combinatorial mutations at all three positions (fragments BD1 to BD4). Parallel modifications were introduced at the inverted repeat positions within the palindromic structure (fragments BD5 to BD8), and simultaneous mutagenesis of all six positions (sites 2, 4, 7 and their palindromic counterparts) generates the hexa-mutation fragment BD9 (Fig. 4D). EMSA revealed that single-base substitutions caused slight reductions in their binding abilities, whereas simultaneous mutation of multiple conserved sites seriously interfered or completely abolished the SecR1-DNA complex formation (Fig. 4E). Notably, SecR2 exhibited identical binding patterns to SecR1 across all mutant constructs, demonstrating that SecR2 also recognized this very sequence motif.

To further investigate the binding relationship between SecR1 and SecR2, EMSA competition assays were performed (Supplementary Fig. 11). These assays demonstrated that SecR1 and SecR2 compete for binding to the same motif. This competition was evidenced by the progressive loss of the SecR2-specific complex band and concomitant gain of the SecR1-specific complex band as the concentration of the competing protein increased. These results establish that binding of SecR1 and SecR2 to this motif is competitive and mutually exclusive.

SecRs exhibit similar features to NarL family response regulator

Cumulatively, the aforementioned results suggest that SecRs share key characteristics with inherently competent aRRs. To elucidate the mechanisms for the intermolecular and protein–DNA interactions of SecRs homodimer, we attempted to determine the three-dimensional structure of SecR proteins through X-ray diffraction and single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). Unfortunately, initial crystallization attempts targeting the full-length protein, REC domain, and DBD domain were impeded by insufficient solubility (<2 mg/mL). Subsequent cryo-EM analyses of the SecR1 homodimer (~55 kDa) with or without DNA substrate also failed to resolve structural details, as particles were too small and their inherent flexibility prevented high-quality 3D reconstruction. Alternatively, a structural model of SecR1 homodimer in complex with 24-mer DNA containing the core palindromic motif of PsecR1 was generated by AlphaFold 330 with a pTM value of 0.55 (Fig. 5A, Supplementary Fig. 12a). To further validate the structural stability of the predicted SecR1-DNA model, 100-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted. Both the entire system and residues at the dimer interface exhibited minimal root mean square deviation (RMSD) fluctuations (<0.4 Å) throughout the simulation trajectory (Supplementary Fig. 12b, c), demonstrating remarkable conformational stability of the simulated complex.

The homodimeric SecR1 model resembles the active phosphorylated homodimer of RcsB (PDB no. 6ZJ2), particularly in their REC domain architecture (Fig. 5A, B). Structural comparisons demonstrate that the SecR1 REC domain (residues 21–145) adopts the characteristic (βα)5 fold observed in RRs, exhibiting strong conservation with RcsB (RMSD of 1.4 Å over 81 aligned residues). Moreover, both REC domains form symmetrical dimers via interactions at α1-α5 interface (Fig. 5A, B), a conserved dimerization mechanism among activated RRs in the NarL/FixJ subfamily31,32. This α1-α5 dimerization mode distinguishes them from OmpR/PhoB-family regulators, which assemble through α4-β5-α5 interface (Fig. 5B)26.

The SecR1 DBD-DNA complex exhibits structural homology to the NarL/FixJ family DBD-DNA co-crystal structures such as NarL (PDB no. 1JE8), RcsB (PDB no. 6ZJ2) and DosR (PDB no. 1ZLK) (Fig. 5C, Supplementary Fig. 13). All these systems maintain virtually identical HTH conformations in their DBDs, with dimerization mediated primarily by α10 helices and DNA recognition coordinated mainly through α9 helices (Fig. 5C). The conserved α9 recognition helix inserting into DNA’s major groove is a shared feature among NarL/FixJ subfamily RRs26,31. Alanine substitutions were also conducted to validate the function of putative SecR1-DNA binding residues revealed by the structural model (Supplementary Fig. 14). EMSAs revealed substantial reductions in DNA-binding capacity across all mutants of SecR1-DNA binding residues relative to wild type, with N183A, K198A and R213A variants exhibiting complete binding abolition (Fig. 5D).

SecR is widespread in bacteria associating with MAs catabolism

A search (E-value < 1e−6; maximum hits = 10,000) against the non-redundant 90% (version nr90_23_Jun) database of the Max Planck Institute Bioinformatics Toolkit33 retrieved 10,000 entries showing statistically significant similarity to SecR1. Among these, 7376 entries (73.76%) exhibited >50% coverage and >30% identity. Notably, 972 entries were identified as atypical RRs lacking the conserved phosphorylating Asp residue, indicating that SecR-like regulators are likely common in bacteria.

To better analyze co-occurrence and clustering of secR with MAs catabolic genes, a database of all available complete bacterial genomes (41,395) from the NCBI database was constructed and Cblaster34 was used to search over these genomes. Resultantly, 6930 (~17%) genomes encode at least one SecR-like protein (>50% coverage and >30% identity). A co-occurrence analysis of secR and MAs catabolic genes reveals that secR is present in 103 methylotrophic strains spanning 17 genera within the alphaproteobacteria class, with taxonomic distribution showing distinct habitat preferences: the Hyphomicrobiales strains predominantly comprise terrestrial soil and symbiotic bacteria, and the Rhodobacterales strains are primarily isolated from marine environments (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 15). Among the 103 bacterial genomes analyzed, MMA catabolic genes were identified in 102 genomes (99%), while TMA and DMA catabolic genes were detected in 74 (72%) and 73 (71%) genomes, respectively. Although most of the serine cycle genes are clustered with secR, serine cycle genes exhibit an obvious difference in their organization.

Based on gene cluster arrangements and phylogenomic analysis, serine cycle gene clusters can be divided into four types: Type I (common in Mesorhizobium), Type II (in Aminobacter), Type III (in Leisingera), and Type IV (in Roseovarius) (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. 15). For types I, III, and IV, secR is adjacent to the sga in forward or reverse directions. However, in type II strains such as NyZ550, secR is located at downstream of glyA, and commonly, there is another copy of secR adjacent to the MAs catabolic genes (Fig. 6B). Additionally, a fifth type (Type V) regulated independently of SecR by the distal LysR-type regulator QscR, has been reported in prior studies19,35. Its distinct genomic structure is included in Fig. 6B for comparative purposes.

Intriguingly, as much as 78% (79 strains) of surveyed SecR homologs exhibit substitutions at the phospho-accepting Asp residue (D → G/A/S/E/N) within their REC domains (Supplementary Fig. 16, Supplementary Table 2), aligning with the characteristic of aRRs. The remaining homologs retaining the conserved Asp residue could potentially function as aRRs, as exemplified by JadR1, which retains Asp but is activated by end-product binding24. Further in-depth bioinformatic analyses revealed ubiquitous conservation of putative SecR-binding motifs (5’-CCYACCCG-3’) upstream of sga operons in >95% of examined strains (Supplementary Fig. 15), suggesting an evolutionarily conserved regulatory mechanism. To further confirm the regulatory function of SecR from different bacteria, following representative SecR homologs from other three types were synthesized for heterologous expression and characterization: SecRNL23 from Hyphomicrobium nitrativorans NL23 (type I), SecRDSS3 from Ruegeria pomeroyi DSS-3 (type III), and SecRW115 from Roseovarius sp. W115 (type IV). All of them are successfully expressed in E. coli as soluble form, and identified to be dimer in solution (Supplementary Fig. 17). Indeed, EMSA analyses demonstrated specific binding of the three SecRs to their respective cognate Psga regions (Fig. 6C). Intriguingly, cross-activity assay revealed that SecRDSS3 from the model strain Ruegeria pomeroyi DSS-3 bound Psga from strain NyZ550, while SecR1 from strain NyZ550 also bound Psga from strain DSS-3 (Fig. 6D). Collectively, these results clearly indicate a conserved SecR regulation mechanism across phylogenetically divergent lineages and all four gene cluster types.

Further analysis of these 103 methylotroph genomes (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 15) also revealed the conserved presence of a gene encoding an XRE-family transcriptional regulator within their MAs catabolic clusters. Notably, genomic analysis of Aminobacter species (representing type II clusters) showed that these strains possess both chromosomal and plasmid-borne MAs catabolic clusters, each containing a homolog of this xre gene. Motivated by the potential functional redundancy of these two xre copies in Aminobacter sp. NyZ550, we constructed a double deletion mutant lacking both xre genes (Δxre1-xre2). However, under MAs utilization conditions, this double mutant exhibited growth phenotypes indistinguishable from the wild-type strain (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Discussion

Understanding MAs catabolic regulation is essential for deciphering microbial roles in global biogeochemical cycles. Through characterization of the SecR system in Aminobacter sp. NyZ550, we identified a phosphorylation-independent aRRs transcriptional activation mechanism governing MAs assimilation, a vital microbial metabolism in terrestrial and marine ecosystems. Subsequent phylogenomic analysis revealed this regulatory strategy is shared by phylogenetically diverse MAs-utilizing bacteria, establishing the SecR-subfamily as a conserved regulatory module. Particularly, the Aminobacter strains uniquely employ hierarchical redundancy of twin SecRs to dynamically align serine cycle gene expression with cellular carbon demands.

Regulatory model for MAs catabolism

With regard to the three SecRs-regulating promoters Psga, Pppc and PsecR1, knockout of secR1 and secR2 genes abolished MAs-responsive activation of Psga and Pppc, while preserving MAs-inducible expression at PsecR1 (Supplementary Fig. 4b). MAs oxidation genes (operons I and III) exhibit an MAs-inducible expression with a SecR independent manner (Fig. 1D). These results suggest that strain NyZ550 appears to employ additional MAs-sensing system to regulate the expression of secR1 and MAs oxidation genes (Fig. 7). Integration of our findings, a three-step hierarchical regulatory mechanism is proposed for MA catabolism in strain NyZ550 as follows: (1) MAs signals trigger primary activation of both MAs oxidation genes (operons I and III) and secR1 expression through an unidentified sensor system; (2) SecR1 subsequently activates transcription of Psga- and Pppc-driven serine cycle genes, and also autoregulates its own promoter PsecR1; (3) Activation of Pppc by SecR1 also increases the expression of SecR2 which could in turn activate Psga, Pppc and PsecR1 (Fig. 7). Although the plasmid-borne secR2 seems unnecessary in this regulatory framework, the similar function between SecR2 and SecR1 (Fig. 4A, Supplementary Figs. 4b, c and 7) imply an insurance and reinforcement for this positive feedback circuit through a homologous transcription regulator.

Working model of SecR-regulated MAs assimilation in strain NyZ550.

MAs (including TMA/DMA/MMA) are imported into the cell via MA transporters. Intracellular MAs trigger activation of the transcription of oxidative demethylation pathway genes and secR1 gene expression through an unidentified sensor system. SecR1 subsequently activates transcription of the serine cycle genes driven by Psga and Pppc promoters. Additionally, SecR1-mediated activation of Pppc enhances secR2 expression. SecR1 and SecR2 collectively form a regulatory pool (SecR pool) that co-activates Psga, Pppc and PsecR1. This cascade rapidly upregulates MA oxidation and serine cycle pathways, converting MAs into one-carbon units for biomass synthesis. Notably, SecR proteins are intrinsically unstable and undergo rapid degradation in the cytoplasm. Orange-red bold arrows: activation of transcription; Dashed lines: hypothetical components or reactions.

Our genomic analyses identified both the conserved SecR regulator and an associated XRE-family regulator within MAs catabolic clusters of diverse methylotrophs (Fig. 6A, Supplementary Fig. 15). While SecRs were confirmed as central to regulation of MAs catabolism, deletion of both chromosomal and plasmid-borne xre homologs in Aminobacter sp. NyZ550 surprisingly did not impact growth on MAs (Supplementary Fig. 18). This unexpected finding indicates these specific XRE regulators are not essential for MAs catabolism under the tested conditions, or their function is compensated by an unidentified mechanism. This result underscores the complexity of the regulatory network governing MAs metabolism and highlights that the core sensory component directly detecting MAs likely remains unidentified. Therefore, future work focused on identifying this upstream sensory element is essential to fully elucidate the MAs regulatory circuit.

Evolution of secR and serine cycle gene clusters

It has been reported that the serine cycle genes within the proteobacteria have undergone multiple independent evolutionary events, accompanied by specific differentiation in gene clustering patterns36. Combined with our analysis, there are at least five distinct gene-clustering patterns for serine cycle genes within the alphaproteobacteria class36 (Fig. 6B): (I) “secR-sga-mcl-mtkBA(-hpr)(-ppc)”, observed in and Mesorhizobium, Sinorhizobium, and Hyphomicrobium; (II) “sga-mcl-mtkBA-hpr…ppc-glyA-secR” in Aminobacter (also in certain Mesorhizobium), with an additional secR paralog adjacent to the MA oxidation genes; (III) “gck-mcl-secR-sga-mtkBA-hpr-ppc-glyA”, an expanded composite cluster evolved in Leisingera and Paracoccus lineages. (IV) “secR-sga-hpr(-mcl)-mtkBA”, a simplified cluster observed in Roseovarius; (V) “sga-hpr-mtdA-fch-mtkBA-ppc-mcl” conserved in obligate and facultative methylotrophs including Methylobacterium, Methylopila, and Methylosinus, and it’s positively regulated by a distally located LysR-type regulator QscR19,35.

This evolutionary diversity of the serine cycle gene clusters likely involves both vertical inheritance and horizontal gene transfer (HGT)36–38. Our comparative phylogenomic analysis revealed broad topological congruence between whole-genome and SecR-based phylogenies, albeit with localized phylogenetic incongruence at specific nodes (Supplementary Fig. 16). For example, SecR homologs from marine Rhodobacterales strains (Celeribacter indicus P73 and Roseivivax marinus TCYB24) exhibited striking sequence homology to those of soil-dwelling Aminobacter species (average amino acid identity ~60%), and their serine cycle gene clusters displayed more similar to Type II arrangements (Supplementary Fig. 15). Furthermore, six Paracoccus strains showed near-identical serine cycle cluster architectures to those in Leisingera species, which was previously attributed to HGT events37. Notably, the four defined serine cycle cluster types (I-IV) exhibited phylogenetic divergence within corresponding SecR clades, supporting co-dispersal of metabolic and regulatory components through either vertical co-inheritance or cross-lineage HGT events.

Taken together, although the serine cycle gene clusters have undergone complex evolutionary processes via vertical or lateral transfer as well as reorganization, a common SecR regulation mechanism is shared by a wide range of alphaproteobacteria. The marine Roseobacter clade (MRC), which can account for up to 20% of the total bacterial cells abundance in marine coastal waters, plays a dominant role in MAs catabolism39. The majority of marine-derived bacterial strains harboring SecR homologs belong to the MRC (Fig. 6A), suggesting that the regulation of the serine cycle by SecR is essential for MRC bacteria to grow on MAs as sole source of carbon, nitrogen and energy. Moreover, the co-occurrence of SecR homologs and MAs catabolic genes in nitrogen-fixing Mesorhizobium strains implies their potential to metabolize plant-derived MAs (such as those from choline or glycine betaine40) in the rhizosphere, thereby adapting to the nutritional niche of root nodules.

SecR: a subfamily of aRRs with unique mechanisms and functions

Herein, we describe SecRs as a class of aRRs that not only differ from canonical RRs such as RcsB (which require HK-mediated phosphorylation for activation), but also diverge from previously characterized aRRs by activating independently of protein–ligand interactions24, protein-protein interactions25, or post-translational modifications23. Although our current study lacks direct structural evidence, predictive modeling offers appealing clues for their unique adaptation. The putative phosphorylation pocket in SecR1 contains two strategically positioned arginine residues (R32 and R81) that would create a localized positive charge microenvironment, contrasting with the negatively charged D11 and neutral S58 at equivalent positions in RcsB (Supplementary Fig. 13b). This configuration appears to mimic the Mg²⁺-mediated electrostatic stabilization observed in classical RR activation, suggesting a charge compensation mechanism that preserves the structural integrity of the REC fold without requiring divalent cations22.

This intrinsic charge stabilization mechanism, combined with SecR’s constitutive dimeric architecture, enables its direct DNA-binding capability upon cellular expression. This unique property makes it a perfect signal amplifier in a defined regulatory cascade. Such phosphorylation-independent regulation may confer ecological advantages in environments with dramatic fluctuations in MAs concentration, particularly in marine surface layers and soil rhizospheres. Because these habitats frequently experience transient MAs pulses driven by algal blooms or root exudates, demanding rapid metabolic activation before resource depletion by competitors3,4.

Our bioinformatic analyses revealed that SecR-like regulators are phylogenetically widespread, with homologs detected in ~17% of surveyed bacterial genomes (6930 strains in 41,395 strains) collectively encoding >9000 distinct SecR homologs. Using ≤4 average-length genes as the genomic distance threshold for cognate pairing, we found ~40% of SecR homologs retain an associated HK gene. Conversely, the majority (60%) have lost their co-encoded HK partner, representing orphan RRs. We specifically identified 394 bacterial strains encoding aRRs characterized by mutation or deletion of the conserved phospho-accepting Asp residue (some retain genomic linkage to an HK gene). Co-occurrence of HK loss and Asp disruption in these aRRs supports an evolutionary trajectory where ancestral RRs progressively lose cognate HK association and phosphorylation dependency, transitioning into phosphorylation-independent aRRs. Notably, beta-/gamma-proteobacterial and actinobacterial aRRs homologs (Supplementary Fig. 19) show genomic linkages to diverse biological pathways like dimethylformamide degradation and drug resistance. While alpha-proteobacterial SecRs predominantly associate with serine cycle operons, this broader functional distribution of aRRs demonstrates their involvement in varied physiological processes beyond canonical phosphorylation-dependent regulation. It underscores both the functional plasticity of SecR-like regulators and the need to explore their additional regulatory roles in bacterial metabolic adaptations.

Methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ550 and its derivatives were cultivated at 30 °C with shaking at 180 rpm in either: (i) 1/5-strength tryptic soy broth (TSB), or (ii) minimal salts medium (MSM, pH 7.0)41 supplemented with additional carbon and nitrogen source when necessary. E. coli WM3064 were cultivated in Lysogeny broth (LB) supplemented with 0.3 mM 2,6-diaminopimelic acid at 37 °C and 180 rpm.

Construction of gene-knockout mutants and complementation

The plasmids and primers used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Data 1, respectively. To construct markerless gene-knockout mutants in strain NyZ550, upstream and downstream fragments of the target gene were amplified from strain NyZ550. And the resulting fragments were ligated to BamHI- and KpnI-digested pEX18Gm by using ClonExpress Ultra One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). The generated pEX18Gm derivatives were transferred into diaminopimelic acid auxotroph E. coli WM3064, which was then conjugated to strain NyZ550. Transconjugants were streaked onto TSB agar plate containing 15 μg/mL gentamicin (for the first recombination) or 15% sucrose (for the second recombination) at 30 °C. The in-frame deletions of the target genes were confirmed by PCR and then sequencing.

For stable chromosomal complementation, intergenic regions devoid of functional elements were selected as insertion sites to avoid disrupting endogenous genetic architecture. Specifically, SecR1 was integrated between divergently transcribed genes N7E70_026000 (forward orientation) and N7E70_026005 (reverse orientation); SecR2 was inserted between N7E70_000245 (forward) and N7E70_000250 (reverse). These intergenic loci were chosen based on the absence of coding sequences, promoter elements, and other regulatory features. The homologous sequences on the left and right sides of the insertion site, along with the genes and their respective promoter fragments were PCR amplified and ligated to BamHI- and KpnI-digested pEX18Gm. The resultant plasmids were transformed into the mutants as the aforementioned method.

Assessment of growth on different carbon sources

For growth experiments, Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ550 and its derivatives were cultivated in MSM, without additional carbon or nitrogen sources, supplemented with either 4 mM monomethylamine (30% in water, Aladdin, Shanghai, China), or 4 mM dimethylamine (40% in water, Adamas-beta, Shanghai, China), or 4 mM trimethylamine (30% in water, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) as the sole carbon and nitrogen source. The cells were incubated in the Bioscreen C (Oy Growth Curves AB Ltd; Helsinki, Finland) at 30 °C for 7 days with continuous, medium shaking, and the OD600nm was measured every 30 min.

For serial subcultivation growth of the ΔsecR1 mutant, strain was cultivated in MSM supplemented with 4 mM DMA as the sole carbon or nitrogen source. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C with OD600nm measurements taken at designated time points using a BioTek Epoch 2 microplate spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA). Each subculture was initiated by transferring 1% (v/v) inoculum from the preceding culture into fresh media.

Transcriptome analysis

RNA sequencing was performed on Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ550 and its derivatives. Derived mutants were cultivated under specific conditions detailed in Supplementary Table 5 (including strain identity, carbon/nitrogen source type and concentration, and nitrogen supplementation for glucose groups). Briefly, cells from two experimental groups—GLU group (cultivated in glucose) and MAs group (cultivated in methylamines)—were harvested at mid-log phase according to Supplementary Table 5 criteria and subjected to RNA sequencing at Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Gene expression levels were represented as Fragments per Kilobase of Mapped reads (FPKM). Fold changes in gene expression were calculated by comparing the FPKM values of the MAs and GLU groups. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by |log2 foldchange| > 1 (p value < 0.05).

Co-transcriptional analysis, 5’RACE and RT-qPCR

For co-transcriptional analysis and 5’ RACE, Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ550 cells were cultivated with 4 mM DMA as the sole carbon/nitrogen source and harvested at mid-log phase. For RT-qPCR experiments, wild-type NyZ550, ΔsecR1, and ΔsecR2 strains were first pre-cultured in GLU medium (4 mM glucose supplemented with NH4NO3) to mid-log phase. Cells were harvested, washed twice with Phosphate Buffer (PB, pH 7.4) to remove residual carbon, and re-inoculated at 1% (v/v) into two fresh cultures: GLU (identical to pre-culture) or DMA (4 mM DMA as sole carbon/nitrogen source). The ΔsecR1-R2 (unable to utilize methylamines as sole C/N source) underwent identical pre-culture and washing steps, followed by inoculation into GLU (4 mM glucose supplemented with NH4NO3) or DMA (4 mM DMA and 4 mM glucose supplemented with NH4NO3). All cultures were harvested at mid-log phase.

Total RNA was extracted from harvested cells using a TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) with critical modifications for bacterial samples: Cell lysis was initiated by enzymatic pretreatment with 4 mg/mL lysozyme (37 °C, 2 min), followed by chemical disruption in buffer RL containing 2% DTT (freshly added). The lysate was processed through a gDNA removal column to eliminate genomic DNA contaminants. RNA purification involved sequential washing with 70% ethanol and specific buffers (RWA/RWB) to remove proteins and organic residues, with a final ethanol-free drying step to prevent inhibition in downstream assays. Total RNA was eluted in 30 μL RNase-free ddH2O. All procedures strictly followed the manufacturer’s protocol with defined centrifugal forces (13,000 × g) and incubation times. RNA integrity and purity were verified prior to use. Removal of the genomic DNA and reverse transcription were performed using HiScript III RT Supermix for qPCR (+gDNA wiper) kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China).

Genomic DNA was extracted by using TIANamp Bacteria DNA kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Intergenic regions within gene clusters were amplified using cDNA, total RNA, and genomic DNA as sample templates, negative and positive controls, and used to analyze transcription units. The transcriptional start sites of PsecR1, Pppc and Psga were identified using SMARTer RACE 5’/3’ Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). TB Green Premix Ex Taq™ II (Takara, Dalian, China) was used for RT-qPCR, and the cycle thresholds were determined using a CFX Connect Real-Time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, USA). The 16S rRNA gene of strain NyZ550 was used as the internal control. All experiments were performed in strict accordance with the protocols of the respective manufacturers.

Gene cloning, protein production, protein purification and size exclusion chromatography

secR1 and secR2 genes were amplified from strain NyZ550 and ligated into NcoI/XhoI digested pET28a(+). The termination codon was deleted so that the His6-tag was attached to the protein C-terminus. The resulted plasmids pET28a-SecR1 and pET28a-SecR2 were transformed into BL21(DE3). Cells of the recombinant were cultured in LB at 37 °C to an OD600nm of 0.6 and then induced at 12 °C for 12 h via addition of 0.05 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Harvested cells were resuspended in elution buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.5), 300 mM NaCl, and 5% glycerol before being lysed by sonication. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 45 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was loaded onto a nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid column (GE Healthcare), and eluted in 250 mM imidazole. The proteins were then subjected to size exclusion chromatography using a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) with the elution buffer for column equilibration and elution. The purity and size of the protein was analyzed via 12.5% SDS-PAGE. The desired fractions were concentrated by ultrafiltration using 30-kDa Amicon Ultra tubes (Millipore, USA).

Phosphorylation assays

Purified SecR1 or SecR2 proteins were incubated in a reaction buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5), 150 mM NaCl, and 10 mM MgCl2, and supplemented with 50 mM acetyl phosphate (Sigma-Aldrich) for phosphorylation assays. The reaction mixtures were then incubated at 37 °C for varying durations (5, 10, 20, 40, or 60 min). Following each incubation period, samples were immediately analyzed by Mn2+-Phos-tag™ SDS-PAGE according to established protocols42.

Site-directed mutagenesis

Site-directed mutagenesis of SecR1 was conducted following a previously described method43. In brief, plasmid pET28a-SecR1 served as the template for PCR amplification using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd, China) and mutagenic primer pairs listed in Supplementary Data 1. The PCR products were subsequently digested with DpnI restriction enzyme (New England Biolabs, Inc.). The digested DNA was then transformed into chemically competent E. coli DH5α cells, and successful transformants were selected on LB agar plates containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin.

Agarose electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA)

Binding of SecR1 (or other proteins) to DNA fragment was performed in 20 μL reaction mixtures containing 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 9.5), 100 mM KCl, 12.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, and 5% (vol/vol) glycerol. Bovine serum albumin (BSA, 50 μg/mL) was used as a non-specific protein competitor to minimize non-specific binding. To optimize binding, the protein concentration was varied in the range of 0–7.2 μM, while the DNA concentration was kept constant at 50 nM. The reaction mixtures were incubated on ice for 45 min to allow for protein–DNA complex formation. After incubation, the samples were loaded onto a 6% (vol/vol) native polyacrylamide gel, and electrophoresis was performed at 90 V in Tris-glycine buffer (25 mM Tris, 192 mM glycine) to separate free and bound DNA. The gel was stained with nucleic acid dyes (ShareBio, Shanghai, China), and the DNA fragments were visualized under UV light. Bands corresponding to bound and unbound DNA were quantified using ImageJ software44.

To calculate the binding affinity of SecR1/R2 to DNA, EMSA results were analyzed using the Hill equation, which describes non-linear binding of proteins to DNA. The binding curve was generated by plotting the fraction of bound DNA (Y) against the concentration of the DNA ([L]) in the reaction. The Hill equation was fitted to the experimental data: Y=[L]nKdn+[L]n. The dissociation constant Kd and Hill coefficient n were determined by fitting the binding data to the Hill equation using non-linear regression in Origin (Learning Edition, USA).

DNase I footprinting

DNase I footprinting assay was performed as described previously45. The 6-carboxyfuorescein (6’FAM)-labeled probes were prepared by PCR and purified by using QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, Germany). The purified PCR product was incubated with the indicated amounts of proteins on ice for 45 min before digestion with 0.02 U DNase I (New England Biolabs, USA, dissolved in 10 μL freshly prepared 0.01 M CaCl2) for 1 min at 37 °C. Then 140 μL stop solution (200 mM NaCl, 30 mM EDTA, 1% SDS) was added and QIAquick PCR Purification Kit was used again to purify the digested products. DNase I digestion reactions were analyzed by capillary electrophoresis in an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, USA) with GeneMarker software v3.0 (SoftGenetics, USA).

Reporter plasmid construction and β-galactosidase assay

A 500-bp fragment for PsecR1, a 900-bp fragment for Pppc and a 656-bp fragment for Psga covering the TSSs of each tested operon were amplified and fused with lacZ gene fragment and SphI/BstBI digested pBBR1MCS-2-Gm vector. These constructed reporter plasmids were conjugated to wild-type NyZ550 and its mutant strains, ΔsecR1, ΔsecR2 and ΔsecR1-R2. These plasmids were also transformed to BL21(DE3) along with pET28a-SecR1 or pET28a-SecR2. β-galactosidase assays in Aminobacter sp. strain NyZ550 derivatives (wild-type, ΔsecR1, and ΔsecR2) were performed using cells cultured in MSM supplemented with 15 μg/mL gentamicin under two conditions: GLU (4 mM glucose, 1 mM NH4NO3) or DMA (4 mM DMA as sole carbon/nitrogen source). Due to its inability to utilize methylamines as sole carbon/nitrogen source, the ΔsecR1-R2 mutant was cultured under GLU conditions or in DMA supplemented with 4 mM glucose and 1 mM NH4NO3. All cultures were harvested at mid-log phase. For assays in BL21(DE3), cells grown in LB at 37 °C to OD600nm ~ 0.3 were induced by transferring 1 mL culture to 10 mL MSM containing 0.05 mM IPTG, followed by overnight incubation at 16 °C before collection.

For all assays, harvested cells were suspended in 1.1 mL Z Buffer (100 mM Na2HPO4·12H2O, 40 mM NaH2PO4·H2O, 10 mM KCl, 1 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 5.4 μL/mL β-mercaptoethanol). Of these, 100 μL was used to measure OD600nm, and the rest was mixed with 100 μL chloroform and 50 μL 0.1% SDS. After incubating at 30 °C for 30 min, 100 μL of the supernatant after centrifugation was mixed with 20 μL 4 mg/mL o-nitrophenyl-β-D-galactopyranoside, and incubated at 30 °C until the sample solution developed a yellow coloration if positive. The reaction was stopped by the addition of 50 μL of 1 M Na2CO3. Absorption at 420 nm (A420) and 550 nm (A550) was recorded using BioTek Epoch 2 (BioTek, USA). Miller units were calculated according to the following equation: β-galactosidase activity [miller units] = 1000*(A420-1.75*A550)/(OD600nm*reaction time in min* volume of reaction).

Bioinformatic analysis

Sequence similarity search

To identify homologous proteins to SecR1, a sequence similarity search was performed using the ProtBLAST/PSI-BLAST tool from the Max Planck Institute Bioinformatics Toolkit33. The search was conducted against the non-redundant 90% (version nr90_23_Jun) database, which is filtered for a maximum pairwise sequence identity of ~90% using MMseqs2. The query sequence for SecR1 was input as a single protein sequence, and a maximum of 10,000 target hits were retrieved. The search utilized the BLOSUM80 scoring matrix, chosen for its suitability in protein sequence alignment, and an E-value threshold of 1e−6 was applied to report statistically significant matches.

Cluster analysis

All complete bacterial genomes from the NCBI RefSeq database (accession date: July 11, 2024) were retrieved through the NCBI Datasets46 to establish a local genomic repository. From the 41,395 genome assemblies downloaded, co-localized SecR-containing gene clusters were systematically identified using Cblaster34 in sensitive search mode, with stringent thresholds: maximum E-value of 1e−2, minimum sequence identity of 30%, and query coverage ≥50%. And the maximum total hits to save from a local BLAST search was set at 10,000. Initial screening identified 9017 putative SecR homologs across 6930 bacterial strains. Then, co-localized SecR gene and serine cycle genes were further searched on the local database with same parameters. After manual redundancy removal, 103 gene clusters in total were finally obtained. Methylamines-oxidizing genes, the THF recycling genes and other conserved genes were searched and annotated manually.

Phylogenetic analysis

To investigate whether SecR1 and SecR2 differ from previously examined RRs, 22 reported typical and atypical RRs sequences were downloaded. The phylogenetic tree was generated in MEGA X47 using the maximum likelihood method. The sequences of RRs were aligned using ClustalW48 alignment tool, which is built-in MEGA X software. For the 103 curated strains, a comparative phylogenetic framework was implemented to resolve both genomic and protein-level evolutionary relationships. Taxonomic classification of these genomes was performed using GTDB-Tk v249 with the GTDB r207 reference database. Whole-genome sequences and corresponding SecR homologs were subjected to separate multiple sequence alignments using ClustalW. IQ-TREE 250 executed automated model selection via ModelFinder under the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), identifying LG + F + R5 (nucleotide substitution model) for whole-genome phylogeny and Q.insect+F + I + G4 (amino acid substitution model) for SecR homologs. Both topologies underwent dual topological validation through 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and Shimodaira-Hasegawa approximate likelihood-ratio test (SH-aLRT) analysis. Final visual optimizations were implemented in iTOL51.

The SecR1-binding consensus motif was identified using the Multiple Em for Motif Elicitation (MEME) software29. Potential DNA binding sites of SecR were systematically identified using the FIMO (Find Individual Motif Occurrences) algorithm52 from the MEME Suite, which scans Psga databases with the characterized SecR-binding motif under significance thresholds (p < 1 × 10−3).

Molecular dynamic simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using GROMACS 202053 with the CHARMM36 force field. The system was solvated in a 20 × 15 × 20 nm³ TIP3P water box and neutralized with counterions. Temperature (300 K) and pressure (1 bar) were maintained using V-rescale and Parrinello-Rahman methods, respectively. The system underwent 50,000-step steepest descent energy minimization (force threshold <1000 kJ/mol·nm), followed by 100 ps equilibration under NVT/NPT ensembles with positional restraints (1000 kJ·mol⁻¹·nm⁻²) applied to protein–DNA components. All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using LINCS. Long-range electrostatics were calculated via particle mesh Ewald method (0.12 nm grid spacing). Following 100 ns production simulation, root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis was performed using gmx rms, with structural visualization executed in Chimera X54.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were performed on data from RT-qPCR and promoter activity assays. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Significance between two groups was determined using unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; ns denotes not significant (p ≥ 0.05). Results for RT-qPCR, promoter activity assays, and other experiments (e.g., growth assays) are based on three biologically independent experiments (n = 3). All other experiments were repeated at least twice with consistent results observed.
