Challenges for Implementing Integrated Obesity Management in Municipal Health Plans of Mato Grosso do Sul (Brazil): A Qualitative Study
Authors: Renata Midoguti Joia, Bruna Paola Murino Rafacho, Cristina Alvarez, Elias Afif Elossais, Juliana Mara Elora Marques, Mariane Helen De Oliveira, Camila Medeiros Da Silva Mazzeti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDespite the growing burden of obesity‐related noncommunicable diseases, little is known about how obesity is incorporated into local health‐planning instruments. Therefore, this study examined the presence and structure of actions and goals related to obesity management in the municipal health plans (MHPs) of the state of Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory qualitative documentary analysis of 78 MHPs, guided by Bardin's content analysis framework and two regulatory instruments: State Technical Note No. 5/2017 and Ministry of Health Ordinance No. 424/2013 on obesity management. These documents defined four planning stages: situational analysis, problem prioritization, objectives/goals, and budget forecasting. Text segments that mentioned “obesity” or related terms were extracted and analyzed in the IRaMuTeQ software applying correspondence factor analysis, descending hierarchical classification, and similarity analysis.ResultsAmong the 78 MHPs (covering 98.7% of all municipalities in Mato Grosso do Sul), 52% mentioned obesity or related terms. However, obesity was rarely prioritized as a strategic health problem. In most plans, situational diagnoses were not translated into measurable goals or structured actions, and none included a dedicated budget allocation. Childhood obesity was seldom explicitly addressed, and primary care actions were described in generic preventive terms rather than as integrated care pathways. Textual analysis using IRaMuTeQ revealed that “obesity” occupied a peripheral position in planning discourse, reinforcing its limited institutional centrality. Only two municipalities’ plans demonstrated coherent alignment across all four planning stages, underscoring a systemic weakness in incorporating obesity into local health management.ConclusionsThese findings indicate not merely an omission, but a structural weakness in local health governance regarding obesity management. The absence of prioritization, measurable targets, and financial planning undermines the implementation of national obesity policies at the municipal level and contributes to fragmented care within the primary health‐care network. Strengthening technical planning capacity, integrating epidemiological evidence into strategic goal‐setting, and ensuring explicit budget allocation are essential to advancing comprehensive and equitable obesity management within Brazil's Unified Health System.

Introduction

Over recent decades, obesity has emerged as a central public health concern in Brazil and in the state of Mato Grosso do Sul [1]. As a multifactorial disease, obesity stands among the leading risk factors for noncommunicable diseases (NCDs)—including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease—and contributes markedly to morbidity, disability, and premature mortality in the country [2].

The complexity of obesity generates not only high direct medical costs but also substantial indirect and social costs, including reduced quality of life, loss of productivity, disability‐related retirement, and premature deaths [3, 4, 5]. In Brazil, rising obesity is strongly linked to escalating costs: each 1‐point increase in the population's mean body mass index (BMI) is associated with an additional BRL 237.51 million/year in hospital admissions, BRL 18.8 million/year in disability retirement benefits, and BRL 131 million/year in sickness benefits payments, while a 1‐percentage‐point increase in obesity prevalence adds BRL 48.8 million, BRL 3.97 million, and BRL 26.8 million per year, respectively [6].

Comprehensive obesity management requires multifaceted interventions—ranging from lifestyle modification and promotion of healthy eating to physical activity, psychological support, and, when necessary, pharmacological or surgical treatment [7, 8]. This complexity demands a multidisciplinary team approach focused not only on weight reduction but on improving overall quality of life, which remains an ongoing challenge for Brazil's Unified Health System [7, 8].

Within this context, it is essential that strategies for obesity prevention and management be systematically incorporated into health planning and management instruments [9]. In Brazil's Unified Health System, municipal health actions are guided by three main instruments: the municipal health plan (MHP), the Annual Health Program, and the Annual Management Report [9]. The MHP sets strategic guidelines and goals; the Annual Health Program operationalizes these actions, aligning them with the annual budget; and the Annual Management Report monitors progress and outcomes, as established by State Technical Note No. 5/2017 [10] and Brazil's Ministry of Health Ordinance No. 2,135/2013 [9].

The MHP therefore plays a central role in local health planning by identifying priorities, establishing objectives and targets, and guiding the allocation of financial resources to implement policies that strengthen obesity prevention and management [11, 12]. Understanding how obesity is addressed within these plans through qualitative analysis of their guidelines and goals reveals whether population needs, social vulnerabilities, and epidemiological realities are adequately considered. It also exposes whether obesity remains “invisible” to communities, public managers, and health professionals, thereby limiting the effectiveness of public health policies [13].

Given the epidemiological relevance of MHPs and their impact on health system organization, it is essential that they incorporate explicit, actionable goals and strategies for obesity prevention and management [11, 12]. Accordingly, this study aimed to analyze the presence and structuring of actions and goals related to obesity management within the MHPs of Mato Grosso do Sul State (Brazil), identifying advances, gaps, and challenges in local health management to strengthen public policy and prioritize obesity as a public health issue.

Methods

This study is an exploratory qualitative documentary analysis of MHPs from the state of Mato Grosso do Sul. It is reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) guidelines [14], and the procedures are summarized in Figure 1. We examined the content of these plans to understand how obesity is incorporated into local health planning. Because only publicly available documents were used, this study did not require ethics review under Brazilian National Health Council Resolution No. 510/2016 [15]. Nevertheless, all procedures complied with national standards for health research integrity and responsible conduct of research.

Flowchart of MHPs identification, selection, and analysis procedures.

We sought to obtain all 79 MHPs in effect from 2022 to 2025 in the state of Mato Grosso do Sul. Documents were retrieved through the Ministry of Health's Strategic Management Support Platform and, when necessary, requested by phone or email from Municipal Health Secretariats between January and June 2024. Plans were included if they corresponded to the MHPs for the 2022–2025 cycle. Plans were excluded only if they were not publicly accessible or were not provided after contact with the respective Municipal Health Secretariat. Of the 79 eligible MHPs, 78 were analyzed in full. Only one municipality was excluded from the study because it did not provide a valid copy of its plan.

Data processing followed three steps. First, each MHP was read to identify occurrences of “obesity” and related terms. The search strategy included variations such as “excess weight,” “overweight,” “above ideal weight,” “increased weight‐for‐age,” and “bariatric,” which were validated by the State Health Secretariat working group responsible for overweight and obesity management. Second, all text segments containing these terms were transcribed verbatim into an Excel spreadsheet. Third, the extracted segments were compiled into a single corpus file in.txt format.

Content analysis followed Bardin's framework [16] and was guided by two policy instruments: State Technical Note No. 5/2017 [10], which provides the structure for developing MHPs, and Brazilian Ministry of Health Ordinance No. 424/2013 [17], which redefines obesity management by integrating it into a comprehensive primary health care pathway. Based on these documents [10, 17], we developed four deductive analytical categories: (1) Situational Analysis; (2) Definition of Priority Problems; (3) Objectives, Guidelines, and Goals; and (4) Feasibility and Budget Forecasting. Segments that did not correspond to any category—typically generic excerpts that reproduced national policy language without local implementation—were classified as analytically irrelevant [10].

In a second step, we performed textual analysis with IRaMuTeQ (Interface de R pour les Analyses Multidimensionnelles de Textes et de Questionnaires) [18]. We prepared a single corpus and segmented it into Initial Context Units based on the occurrence of “obesity” or its variants—“excess weight,” “overweight,” “above ideal weight,” “increased weight‐for‐age,” and “bariatric.” In the plans, “excess weight” and “overweight” were generally used to indicate BMI values above recommended cut‐offs in adults and adolescents; “above ideal weight” referred to a nontechnical expression of the same condition; “increased weight‐for‐age” was used for anthropometric indicators in children and adolescents; and “bariatric” captured references to bariatric surgery or specialized obesity‐treatment services. Each municipality was treated as a specific case within the MHP variable, and nontextual elements were removed before analysis.

Following corpus organization, lexical preprocessing procedures were conducted to ensure consistency in the textual analysis. Spelling variants and synonymous expressions referring to the same concept were harmonized through dictionary cleaning to reduce redundancy within the corpus. In addition, automatic lemmatization was applied using the Portuguese dictionary integrated into IRaMuTeQ [18], which groups inflected forms of words (e.g., plural nouns and verb conjugations) under their root forms. This step reduces lexical dispersion and improves the reliability of word frequencies and co‐occurrences, thereby enhancing the stability of the lexical classes generated in subsequent analyses.

Three complementary techniques were applied: correspondence factor analysis to assess associations among terms; descending hierarchical classification to identify lexical classes from frequency and co‐occurrence; and similarity analysis to visualize semantic connections and predominant cores of meaning [18]. All analyses used the Portuguese dictionary integrated into IRaMuTeQ [18] and the software's default parameters for exploratory qualitative content analysis, preserving the semantic integrity of the original texts; results were subsequently translated into English for presentation in this article.

Results

Of the 79 municipalities in Mato Grosso do Sul, 78 MHPs were analyzed, representing 98.7% coverage of the state. The keyword “obesity” or its variations appeared in 41 plans, corresponding to 52.0% of the total. Across these 41 MHPs, 163 occurrences of the term were identified. Text segments containing the word “obesity” or its variations were submitted to deductive categorization, following Bardin's content analysis framework [16] and the Technical Note No. 5/2017 of the Mato Grosso do Sul State Health Secretariat [10]. In the situational analysis, the MHPs were expected to describe how health services were organized for the population (including nutritional surveillance), the local epidemiological profile, and key health indicators [10]. Municipalities that presented obesity data in this section typically drew on national health information systems, such as the Brazilian Primary Health Care Information System and the Brazilian Food and Nutrition Surveillance System, to characterize obesity across the life course. One MHP, for instance, reported:A significant reduction in underweight classification was observed, concomitant with a sharp increase in obesity and severe obesity. Among adolescents, the proportion of people classified with obesity increased from 11.76% (2017) to 20.72% (2020).

A significant reduction in underweight classification was observed, concomitant with a sharp increase in obesity and severe obesity. Among adolescents, the proportion of people classified with obesity increased from 11.76% (2017) to 20.72% (2020).

The analysis revealed that, although some MHPs mentioned obesity in the situational diagnosis, the condition was rarely prioritized as a public health issue. None of the MHPs applied the scoring system recommended in the Mato Grosso do Sul State Health Secretariat Technical Note No. 5/2017 [10], and a clear disconnect was often observed between the analytical categories “Situational Analysis” and “Definition of Priority Problems.” Additionally, problem prioritization tended to emphasize the progressive impact of overweight on the prevalence of NCDs. However, many diagnostic and technical analyses of nutritional status and food‐consumption markers indicated that local management teams had difficulty interpreting population needs and translating them into equity‐oriented decision‐making.

The third planning dimension, as defined in the Mato Grosso do Sul State Health Secretariat Technical Note No. 5/2017 [10], corresponds to “Objectives, Guidelines, and Goals.” It calls for defining management objectives aimed at addressing prioritized problems and establishing measurable targets for each intervention area. In practice, even when the term “obesity” appeared in this section, several shortcomings were evident, including the absence of baseline data, measurable indicators, expected targets, or monitoring mechanisms aligned with national health information systems. One MHP, for example, stated:Objective 1.1—Promote the maintenance, expansion, and problem‐solving capacity of Primary Health Care services, developing care for people across life stages. Target 1.1.14—Monitor therapeutic care for obesity in the Brazilian School Health Program by 30% through 2025.

Objective 1.1—Promote the maintenance, expansion, and problem‐solving capacity of Primary Health Care services, developing care for people across life stages.

Target 1.1.14—Monitor therapeutic care for obesity in the Brazilian School Health Program by 30% through 2025.

Another plan proposed the following:Indicator for monitoring and evaluation: reduction in statistical data on comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases (stroke, myocardial infarction, hypertension), and nutrition‐related and metabolic conditions (obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and related complications).

Indicator for monitoring and evaluation: reduction in statistical data on comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases (stroke, myocardial infarction, hypertension), and nutrition‐related and metabolic conditions (obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and related complications).

Additionally, some MHPs showed limited feasibility in their goals, given the chronic and multifactorial nature of obesity. For example, one plan set the target “to reduce obesity rates by 20%,” highlighting a clear disconnect between the proposed goal and local epidemiological reality and service capacity.

The fourth analytical category, “Feasibility and Budget Forecasting,” concerns the allocation of financial resources necessary to implement planned interventions. Among the MHPs analyzed, none presented a structured budget forecast or identified specific funding sources for actions related to obesity. Moreover, a substantial proportion of the plans—39.02% (n = 15)—contained only generic or irrelevant content, in which “obesity” appeared in introductory passages copied from federal policies or programs, with no connection to locally proposed actions or goals.

Overall, the analysis indicated that only two MHPs, among the 41 that mentioned obesity, demonstrated structured integration across the three fundamental planning pillars: situational analysis, problem prioritization, and the formulation of objectives, guidelines, and goals. These were considered the most complete and potentially effective plans for organizing obesity management at the municipal level. Unfortunately, none of the plans analyzed presented mechanisms for annual goal revision, which, according to the Mato Grosso do Sul State Health Secretariat Technical Note No. 5/2017 [10], should be recorded on the Ministry of Health's Strategic Management Support Platform to ensure ongoing updating through the Annual Health Program.

Across the IRaMuTeQ [18] outputs, 2171‐word forms (active and supplementary) were identified. In the correspondence factor analysis, the term “obesity” showed a negative association in most MHPs and was classified by the software as a “banal” word, indicating it did not contribute to distinguishing the lexical classes. These associations are presented in Table 2. Additional terms classified as “banal” included “action,” “care/attendance,” “risk factor,” “diabetes,” “deterioration of condition,” “promote,” and “comprehensive/integral.”

The descending hierarchical classification (Figure 2) partitioned the corpus into three classes: Class 1 (40.4%), centered on health care and the organization of services; Class 2 (22.4%), focused on health promotion, with emphasis on physical activity, adequate and healthy eating, and surveillance; and Class 3 (37.2%), comprising demographic data, risk factors, and NCDs, including epidemiologic terms such as hypertension, diabetes, cancer, age group, and mortality.

The similarity analysis (Figure 3) revealed a network predominantly oriented toward promotion and prevention, indicating that much of the content centers on public‐health interventions to foster healthy behaviors and prevent disease. At the central hub, the word “health” was strongly connected to “promotion,” “program,” “care,” “prevention,” “school,” and “integral,” reflecting preventive and intersectoral actions. A separate semantic fan formed around “disease” which was more linked to “chronic,” “population,” “diabetes,” “cancer,” and “hypertension,” echoing major NCDs. Conversely, links to “obesity” were weak, positioning it at the periphery of the network. Another cluster anchored on “obesity” connected to “overweight,” “smoking,” “food,” “nutritional,” “healthy,” and “diagnosis,” suggesting that obesity is mainly addressed as a nutrition‐related concern, often in childhood contexts. Furthermore, the terms “goal,” “indicator,” and “assessment” showed weak connections to the central “health” axis and remained distant from “obesity,” reinforcing that goal‐setting for obesity management is largely absent from most documents.

Discussion

This study is, to our knowledge, the first to systematically examine how obesity is incorporated into MHPs in a Brazilian state, using near‐census coverage of documents and a mixed qualitative–lexical approach. The analysis showed that, although more than half of the MHPs mentioned obesity, this inclusion rarely translated into concrete prioritization, coherent planning, or dedicated funding across the four analytical dimensions—situational analysis, problem prioritization, objectives and goals, and feasibility and budget forecasting.

In most plans, the term appeared in the situational analysis merely as a descriptive epidemiological datum, often extracted from national information systems such as the Brazilian Food and Nutrition Surveillance System or Brazilian Primary Health Care Information System, without being connected to decision‐making or strategic actions. This superficial approach indicates that obesity is still treated as a background condition rather than a central public‐health issue requiring structured interventions. The absence of comprehensive nutritional diagnoses by life stage also contravenes Brazil's Ministry of Health Ordinance No. 424/2013 [17], which establishes the national care pathway for overweight and obesity.

Institutional weaknesses emerged as a major barrier to effective obesity management. The recurrent discontinuity of interventions with each change in municipal administration prevents longitudinal monitoring and undermines continuity within Primary Health Care [19]. Limited investments in infrastructure, professional training, and adequate integration of information systems further constrains municipalities’ capacity to track individuals over time and coordinate across service levels [19]. Moreover, weak alignment between MHPs and other management instruments, including the Annual Health Program and municipal budget plans, further fragments obesity prevention and management and weakens coherence between diagnosis, planning, and financing [19].

During the problem prioritization stage, none of the MHPs applied the scoring system recommended in the Mato Grosso do Sul State Health Secretariat Technical Note No. 5/2017 [10] to determine which health problems should be addressed first. Instead, priorities were predominantly defined by mortality indicators, disregarding the growing impact of overweight as an underlying cause of premature death [2]. This finding underscores the persistent invisibility of obesity as a NCD within public management instruments [12]. Consequently, Primary Health Care teams shoulder a disproportionate burden because prevention and risk‐stratification activities, core components of the Care Pathway for overweight and obesity, are often overlooked in practice [20, 21]. This omission perpetuates a cascade of complications, including hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and mental‐health disorders, which continue to strain municipal health systems [5].

The section addressing objectives, guidelines, and goals revealed further inconsistencies. Even when obesity was referenced, targets were poorly defined, unrealistic, or disconnected from epidemiological data. Many plans lacked baseline values, indicators, or methodologies for tracking progress, resulting in vague commitments such as “reduce obesity by 20%.” These shortcomings reflect limited technical capacity to formulate evidence‐based, feasible goals. Furthermore, the weak alignment between stated goals and local epidemiologic profiles reveals a conceptual fragility that seriously compromises both implementation and evaluation. This fragmentation also undermines integration with other essential planning instruments, such as the Pluriannual Plan and Annual Health Program, preventing the establishment of sustainable and financed interventions [17, 20].

Budget forecasting, a critical component for operationalizing health policies, was entirely absent from all plans analyzed. Despite the high epidemiological burden of overweight and obesity in Brazil, where approximately 70% of adults are affected [22], the documents lacked specified financial allocations or funding mechanisms for related interventions. As a result, the MHPs function more as aspirational policy statements than operational planning tools, transforming them into bureaucratic documents devoid of executional power [12]. In addition, outdated guidance and weak budgetary alignment with federal and state policies, including Ordinance No. 424/2013 [17], further constrain the feasibility of implementing comprehensive obesity management strategies [19].

An additional consideration is the relationship between planning instruments and the implementation of health actions at the municipal level. The absence of structured strategies for obesity management in MHPs does not necessarily mean such actions are absent in practice. In some contexts, local management teams reproduce generic recommendations from federal policies or implement isolated initiatives without formally incorporating them into planning instruments. However, when obesity is not explicitly integrated into the MHP through defined goals, indicators, and budget allocation, these actions tend to remain fragmented, dependent on individual initiatives, and vulnerable to administrative turnover. Considering that the Brazilian Unified Health System is guided by the principle of comprehensive care and the MHP is intended to coordinate actions across different levels of service, the lack of explicit prioritization of obesity is particularly concerning. Given the magnitude of obesity as a public health problem in Brazil, its marginal presence in these planning instruments undermines the development of coherent prevention and control strategies and raises questions about how effectively national policy guidelines are translated into routine Primary Health Care practices.

The lexical analyses performed in IRaMuTeQ [18] further reinforced these findings by unveiling the lexical and thematic structure of the plans. Correspondence factor analysis indicated that “obesity” functioned as a “banal” term, meaning that it was frequent but weakly associated with other key concepts, suggesting a marginal position within the semantic field of health planning. The descending hierarchical classification divided the corpus into three classes centered on health service organization, health promotion, and NCDs epidemiology. Obesity was not a central theme in any class; instead, it appeared primarily in peripheral clusters related to nutrition or risk factors. Similarity analysis reinforced this marginal position. Terms such as “health,” “promotion,” and “program” formed the central core of the network, while “obesity” maintained weak semantic links and remained distant from planning‐related terms such as “goal” and “assessment.” This pattern reveals that obesity is rarely integrated into the broader discourse of planning, surveillance, and accountability. These findings align with evidence from the state of Espírito Santo (Brazil), where 80% of municipalities reported being unable to implement their MHPs, with high professional turnover cited as a key barrier by 50% of participants [11].

This study has several strengths. First, it achieved near‐census coverage of Mato Grosso do Sul State MHPs (98.7%), strengthening the representativeness of the findings. Second, the assessment was anchored in a transparent, standardized deductive framework derived from Bardin's content analysis [16] and the Mato Grosso do Sul State Health Secretariat Technical Note No. 5/2017 [10], with interpretive alignment to the national care pathway for overweight and obesity defined by ordinance No. 424/2013 [17]. Third, the textual mining with IRaMuTeQ [18], including correspondence factor analysis, descending hierarchical classification, and similarity analysis, added methodological triangulation that strengthened internal consistency and highlighted the semantic positioning of “obesity” within planning documents.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. As a documentary, cross‐sectional analysis of MHPs, the study captures what is written rather than what is implemented, and may therefore underdetect actions when obesity is addressed without explicit keywords or through heterogeneous terminology across municipalities. Additionally, we did not extend the review to the Annual Health Program or Annual Management Report, which may contain subsequent updates on targets or implementation details. The volume and dispersion of these documents precluded systematic inclusion at this stage, and may have led to an underestimation of recent managerial adjustments. Furthermore, the time frame (2022–2025) offers a snapshot that may not reflect earlier cycles or mid‐cycle revisions recorded in the Brazilian Ministry of Health's Strategic Management Support Platform [10]. Additionally, text classification results in IRaMuTeQ [18] is sensitive to corpus composition and dictionary rules, and qualitative interpretation, even when guided by predefined categories, retains an element of subjectivity. Moreover, the absence of explicit budget information in most plans prevented verification of implementation feasibility and the tracking of resource allocation for obesity management [17].

In summary, the analyzed MHPs reveal structural and managerial fragilities that limit the recognition of obesity as a public health priority at the municipal level. The absence of technical rigor, limited use of available data, lack of budgetary commitment, and weak alignment between strategic goals and local epidemiological needs expose systemic weaknesses in health‐planning governance. Strengthening the technical training of municipal teams, fostering intersectoral collaboration, especially between education and health through programs such as the Brazilian School Health Program, and ensuring continuous surveillance via national information systems are essential to advance comprehensive obesity management [8, 17, 23]. Ultimately, addressing obesity within MHPs requires a paradigm shift from fragmented, short‐term planning cycles toward integrated, evidence‐based, and equity‐oriented strategies aligned with the principles of the Brazilian Unified Health System.
