Validity testing of the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument: a comprehensive measure for dementia caregiving
Authors: Morgan J Minyo, David M Bass, Katherine S Judge, Joseph E Gaugler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesFew psychometrically tested, comprehensive measures exist for assessing dementia caregivers’ unmet needs. This has limited unmet need from being used as a key outcome in efficacy and/or effectiveness testing of programs supporting family/friend caregivers. This investigation tested the psychometric properties of the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument; a comprehensive measure designed for use in research with dementia caregivers.Research Design and MethodsThe Instrument was examined for 3 types of validity (structural, concurrent, discriminant) and internal consistency reliability using secondary baseline data from a large-scale translation study of 192 community-dwelling family/friend dementia caregivers living in the U.S.ResultsExploratory factor analysis confirmed structural validity of a 45-item Total Unmet Need Scale (loadings = 0.31–0.66) and 8 multi-item and 1 single-item subscales (loadings = 0.34–0.81). Background and objectives

Being a primary family or friend caregiver for people living with dementia increases the risk of a variety of negative outcomes, such as deterioration in mental and physical health; increased burden or strain; increased symptoms of depression; and heightened risk of morbidity (Bass et al., 2012; Ma et al., 2018; Minyo et al., 2025). A less commonly used but valuable caregiver outcome is perceived unmet need, which represents caregivers’ subjective perceptions of care-related issues with which they would like additional help or information (Pearlin, 1989). Only a handful of comprehensive unmet need measures have been developed, and few of these have undergone rigorous psychometric testing with samples of dementia caregivers (Bangerter et al., 2019; Novais et al., 2017). The purpose of this investigation was to establish the validity and reliability of the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument, one of the available measures for evaluating unmet need for family and friend caregivers and people living with dementia.

Unmet need as a key outcome

Three characteristics of the construct of unmet need make it a valuable outcome for understanding the challenges of caregiving and the benefits of non-pharmacological dementia caregiving programs. First, unmet need is a component of two widely used multivariate theoretical frameworks in the field of caregiving; the Stress Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1990) and the Behavioral Health Model (Andersen & Newman, 2005). The Stress Process Model examines the causes and consequences of caregiving stress, and how negative consequences can be decreased or offset by information and supportive resources used by caregivers. In this model, unmet need is conceptualized as an intermediate caregiver outcome, with the extent of unmet need contingent upon the use of resources, such as caregiving support programs and/or assistance from other family members and friends. The second framework, the Behavioral Health Model, conceptualizes unmet need as a “need factor,” which at higher levels is expected to increase the likelihood that caregivers will take action to seek help from services and/or support from other family members and friends.

Second, reducing unmet need is a primary goal of many non-pharmacological, evidence-based, dementia caregiving support programs that are being delivered by over 300 U.S. healthcare and community organizations (Cannon et al., 2025; Maslow et al., 2021). The alignment of this intended program goal and the construct of unmet need makes it an ideal outcome for use in clinical trials of caregiving support programs (Coster, 2013). For example, many evidence-based dementia caregiving programs have the goal of increasing caregivers’ abilities to manage cognitive and behavioral symptoms of dementia (Gitlin et al., 2020). A research outcome that measures whether there is a decrease in unmet need with managing dementia symptoms would be sensitive to detecting this intended benefit.

Third, a comprehensive measure of unmet need can represent the full range of medical and nonmedical issues that can occur over the extended duration of dementia (Gaugler et al., 2005). For people living with dementia, examples of possible unmet medical needs are getting a definitive diagnosis, recognizing symptoms, and managing co-existing health conditions (Black et al., 2013, 2019; Gaugler et al., 2005). Examples of nonmedical needs are getting assistance with personal care and instrumental daily activities, planning for legal and financial issues, and navigating services and community resources (Parker et al., 2020; Shiells et al., 2020; Zwingmann et al., 2019). Examples of nonmedical needs for caregivers are learning how to cope with stressful behavioral symptoms, overcoming difficulties finding and monitoring services, and managing other family and work responsibilities (Tatangelo et al., 2018).

Existing unmet need instruments

Despite its theoretical and pragmatic relevance, unmet need is not frequently used as a caregiver outcome measure. A recent analysis of 128 published articles on 45 evidence-based dementia caregiving programs in Best Programs for Caregiving found only five articles (3.9%) that used unmet need as an outcome for assessing program efficacy and/or effectiveness (Minyo et al., 2025). This is compared to more common outcomes such as depressive symptoms (81 articles, 63.3%), caregiver strain or burden (75 articles, 58.6%), and caregiver self-efficacy or confidence (60 articles, 46.9%).

One reason unmet need is rarely used as an outcome in studies of dementia caregivers is the lack of available, psychometrically tested instruments, with only three instruments identified in systematic reviews (Bangerter et al., 2019; Mansfield et al., 2017; Novais et al., 2017; Queluz et al., 2020): the Camberwell Assessment of Needs (Reynolds, 2000), the Caregiver Needs Assessment for Dementia (Wancata et al., 2005), and the Johns Hopkins Dementia Needs Assessment (Black et al., 2008). However, these three valuable tools were designed for selected purposes and have limitations for some types of research studies, including not representing caregiver self-reports about a full range of medical and nonmedical unmet needs, being designed for use by professional care providers in clinical settings, not being grounded in a theoretical or conceptual framework, requiring lengthy administration time, and not having been used as an outcome in completed research studies (Bangerter et al., 2019; Clemmensen et al., 2021; Mansfield et al., 2017; Novais et al., 2017).

The Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument addresses several limitations of other existing unmet need instruments. It was designed to gather caregiver self-reported, subjective perceptions of unmet needs for a comprehensive range of care-related issues, has a brief administrative time (approximately 10 minutes) so that it can be one component of a longer research interview, can be administered either by trained interviewers or be self-administered by caregivers, and has been successfully used as an outcome in completed research studies (Bass et al., 2013, 2019).

The Instrument was originally developed for the Chronic Care Networks for Alzheimer’s Disease (CCN/AD) Initiative, which was one of the first national demonstrations to improve dementia care and was led by the National Chronic Care Consortium and Alzheimer’s Association. CCN/AD developed and tested the feasibility of a comprehensive dementia care model implemented in 10 U.S. communities by large health systems that were members of the National Chronic Care Consortium in partnership with their local Alzheimer’s Association chapters (Maslow & Selstad, 2001; Maslow et al., 2002). The comprehensive dementia care model’s content was a compilation of standardized and/or widely used research and clinical instruments (e.g., the Alzheimer’s Association Early Warning Signs, Mini Mental State Examination, Functional Activities Questionnaire, Activities of Daily Living Questionnaire, Geriatric Depression Scale, and Benjamin Rose Caregiver Strain Instruments), along with practice guidelines and supplemental instruments developed by the CCN/AD Care Management Advisory Group. The model was successfully published and distributed (Alzheimer’s Association, 2003).

The Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument was created to serve as a primary outcome measure in an evaluation of the feasibility and impact of the CCN/AD comprehensive dementia care model for people living with dementia and their caregivers. The individual items included in the Instrument were developed to capture caregivers’ self-reported need for more help or information with topics covered in the published CCN/AD comprehensive dementia care model described above. In addition, two supplemental sources provided information about topics that should be represented by items in the Instrument: (a) the assessment protocol in the evidence-based program Benjamin Rose Institute (BRI) Care ConsultationTM that represents 35 areas of potential need for care recipients and caregivers (Bass et al., 2003; Clark et al., 2004) and (b) recommendations from the CCN/AD Education and Support Committee, an expert advisory panel of approximately 30 dementia-proficient physicians, nurses, social workers, and program administrators from numerous collaborating healthcare and community-based organizations. The Education and Support Committee reviewed three drafts of the Instrument and provided feedback on content, wording, and feasibility, drawing on their individual and collective experience working with families of people living with dementia.

The resulting Instrument included 45 questions representing a comprehensive range of domains of care such as finding and accessing different types of services, understanding memory problems and other health conditions, getting emotional support or respite, knowing how to manage activities of daily living of the person living with dementia, and finding alternative living arrangements. In the CCN/AD evaluation and the follow-up study “Partners in Dementia Care” (Bass et al., 2013, 2014, 2019), the total scale score on the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument was used as a primary outcome in efficacy and effectiveness investigations of BRI Care ConsultationTM. Results in both investigations showed caregivers who received the tested interventions had significantly reduced numbers of unmet need. However, these published studies did not conduct a comprehensive test of the Instrument’s validity and reliability.

The current study

Unmet need is a latent construct measured by observable proxy indicators. Comprehensive validity and reliability testing is essential for determining whether proxy indicators provide a good representation of the latent construct (DeVellis, 2016). Validity tests assess the accuracy of proxy indicators; reliability tests assess the consistency of indicators (Boateng et al., 2018; Cronbach, 1990). The current investigation tested the total scale score and the nine subscales of the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument for three types of validity (i.e., structural, concurrent, discriminant) and one type of reliability (i.e., internal consistency).

Research design and methods

Sample

The Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument’s psychometric properties were tested using de-identified, secondary data from a longitudinal translation study assessing the effectiveness of BRI Care ConsultationTM delivered by a large health system in partnership with two community-based organizations (Bass et al., 2019). The sample included 192 community-dwelling family and friend dementia caregivers residing in Northeast Ohio. All caregivers were assisting a relative or friend diagnosed with Alzheimer’s disease or other type(s) of dementia who received care from the Veterans Health Administration. Data came from baseline, structured, confidential caregiver telephone interviews administered by trained research staff prior to participants being offered BRI Care ConsultationTM. For more detailed information about this translation study, see Bass et al. (2019).

Caregivers averaged 67.4 years of age (SD = 12.7), 94.8% identified as female, 72.9% identified as White and 24.0% as African American, and 25.0% had a college degree. Most caregivers rated their health as Good (34.6%) or Very Good (28.0%). Approximately 82.0% shared a household with the person living with dementia, 62.0% of whom were spouses. Persons living with dementia needed assistance with an average of five of six instrumental activities of daily living (e.g., paying bills, shopping, and traveling to appointments) and three of six personal care tasks (e.g., bathing, dressing, and toileting).

Measures

In addition to unmet need, the Stress Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1990) guided the selection of four other measures used to test concurrent and discriminant validity. One of these was a measure of caregiver depressive symptoms that represented a general well-being outcome (α = 0.80) and was based on the 11-item Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D [Kohout et al., 1993]). Depressive symptoms were scored from hardly ever or never (0) to often (2), with higher scores indicating more feelings of depressive symptoms. Three of these measures represented secondary roles and intrapsychic strains and included lack of caregiving confidence [i.e., caregiver self-efficacy (α = 0.72)], relationship strain (α = 0.87), and emotional health strain [α = 0.87 (Bass et al., 1989, 1996; Pearlin et al., 1990)]. All items comprising these three strains were scored from strongly disagree (0) to strongly agree (3), with higher scores indicating more strain. Individual items for each measure are displayed in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analyses

Structural validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis to define the latent factors that explained meaningful patterns among unmet need items (DeVellis, 2016). The exploratory factor analysis was performed using principal axis factoring with varimax rotation. Following conventional recommendations, extracted factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 were retained in the analysis and considered to represent meaningful groupings of the 45 unmet need items. Existing literature also suggested that the minimum values of factor loadings for retaining items on their intended factors should be between 0.30 and 0.40 (Fabrigar et al., 1999; Worthington & Whittaker, 2006). Typically, the larger minimum loading of 0.40 is recommended for evaluating established measures that have previously been psychometrically tested, whereas the lower minimum loading of 0.30 is appropriate when testing newer or early-stage measures, with the conceptual importance of items also considered when determining item retention (Boateng et al., 2018; Worthington & Whittaker, 2006).

For this study, an initial preliminary exploratory factor analysis was estimated using the more conservative minimum cutoff value for factor loadings of 0.40. Results indicated several conceptually important items had factor loadings on their intended factors that were slightly below the 0.40 minimum. Therefore, a subsequent final exploratory factor analysis to test for structural validity used a value of 0.30 as the minimum acceptable loading. This approach was deemed appropriate because the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument is in the earlier stages of development (Boateng et al., 2018) and, to this point, had not previously undergone psychometric testing. The 0.30 value for factor loadings was also used to evaluate cross-loadings on factors other than the one that items were intended to primarily load.

Cronbach’s alpha (α) and McDonald’s omega (ɷ) were used to examine the internal consistency reliability of the Total Unmet Need Scale and unmet need subscales. Internal consistency is the degree to which items in a scale co-vary with one another, with alphas and omegas of 0.70 or higher indicating good internal consistency (Cronbach, 1990).

Concurrent validity, a type of criterion validity, tests whether a measure of interest is associated with another established measure of the same or similar construct assessed at the same point in time (DeVellis, 2016). Concurrent validity was tested using multiple regression analyses, with separate equations estimated using the Total Unmet Need Scale and each of the unmet need subscales as dependent variables and depressive symptoms or one of three care-related strains as the independent variable. To statistically control for other factors indicated by the Stress Process Model, each equation also included as covariates caregiver age, gender, race, education, and relationship to the person living with dementia, as well as the number of personal care dependencies with which the person living with dementia needed assistance. Good concurrent validity is indicated by statistically significant associations (i.e., regression coefficients with p ≤ .05 and 95% confidence intervals that do not include zero) between each measure of unmet need and caregiver depressive symptoms and each of the three types of care-related strain (Boateng et al., 2018; DeVellis, 2016).

Discriminant validity, a type of construct validity (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955; DeVellis, 2016), tests whether there is a weak and/or nonsignificant association between a measure of interest and other constructs to which it theoretically should not be related (Brown, 2010; DeVellis, 2016). A principal component analysis constrained to a two-component solution with orthogonal varimax rotation was used to test discriminant validity (Brown, 2010) of the Total Unmet Need Scale and unmet need subscales. Included in each principal component analysis was the total scale score, or one of the subscale scores, plus measures of depressive symptoms and three care-related strains. Acceptable discriminant validity is indicated if each measure of unmet need has a high loading (0.40 or greater) on a separate component from the component with high loadings for depressive symptoms and three types of care-related strain, and no cross-loadings greater than 0.30. By using the same measures to assess concurrent and discriminant validity, we can test whether unmet need is related to theoretically similar constructs but is not redundant with these similar constructs.

The final step was conducing descriptive analyses, offering basic summary information for the Total Unmet Need Scale and unmet need subscales that were tested for reliability and the three types of validity.

The rotated solution yielded ten factors that explained 63.1% of total variance among items. All factors had eigenvalues of 1.0 or higher. Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin of sampling adequacy was 0.90 and Barlett’s test of sphericity was significant, χ2(990) = 4005.65, p < .001, indicating the data were appropriate for factor analysis with sufficient correlations among the variables. The tenth factor had no items loading above the minimum cutoff (0.30) for meaningful interpretation and was not retained. The ninth factor included only one item, “Do you need more help or information with deciding if your (RELATIONSHIP) should stop driving?” which had a loading of 0.47 with no high cross-loadings. This factor was retained as a single item.

The final nine-factor solution explained 60.9% of total variance. All 45 items had loadings of 0.34 or higher on their intended primary factor. A notable finding was that 11 of the 45 items had cross-loadings ranging from 0.30 to 0.41, although all had their highest loadings on their intended primary factors. Because cross-loadings were modest and the higher loadings were on the intended primary factors, all these items were maintained in subsequent analyses.

Overall, the test for structural validity indicated the 45 original items were associated with a single component representing the Total Unmet Need Scale. Structural validity testing of unmet need subscales yielded a nine-factor solution and demonstrated adequate structural validity, although a small number of items had modest cross-loadings on secondary factors.

Internal consistency reliability

All Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega values (Table 2) were equal to or greater than the minimum cutoff of 0.70, indicating good internal consistency reliability for the Total Unmet Need Scale score (α = 0.95; ɷ = 0.95) and the eight subscales with multiple items (α = 0.70–0.88; ɷ = 0.70–0.88). The ninth subscale, Getting Partner to Stop Driving, could not be tested for internal consistency because it was a single-item (Allen et al., 2022).

Six of the nine subscales had small-to-medium-size significant associations with depressive symptoms, after controlling for covariates. These subscales included: Understanding Memory Problems (β = 0.26, p < .001, 95% CI [0.10, 0.35]), Dealing with Family Concerns (β = 0.25, p < .001, 95% CI [0.06, 0.23]), Finding & Accessing Services (β = 0.26, p < .001, 95% CI [0.07, 0.25]), Getting Emotional Support & Respite (β = 0.32, p < .001, 95% CI [0.07, 0.18]), Finding Alternative Living Arrangements (β = 0.17, p = .03, 95% CI [0.01, 0.09]), and Managing Daily Living Task (β = 0.16, p = .04, 95% CI [0.00, 0.10]).

Eight of the nine subscales (i.e., the exception was Getting Partner to Stop Driving) had significant associations with lack of caregiving confidence, ranging from 0.27 to 0.38, after controlling for covariates. See Table 3 for associations between each of the nine subscales and each of the three measures of care-related strains, including standardized regression coefficients, probability values, and confidence intervals. Four subscales (i.e., Dealing with Family Concerns, Finding & Accessing Services, Coordinating Advanced Care Planning, Getting Emotional Support & Respite) had small-to-medium-size significant associations with relationship strain, ranging from 0.15 to 0.24 after controlling for covariates. Seven of the nine subscales (i.e., the exceptions were Monitoring Medications and Getting Partner to Stop Driving) had small-to-medium-size significant associations with emotional health strain, ranging from 0.17 to 0.41. Overall, results indicated evidence of concurrent validity for the Total Unmet Need Scale and seven of the nine unmet need subscales.

Discriminant validity

Results in Table 4 from the principal component analysis with a two-factor rotated solution provided partial evidence of discriminant validity for the Total Unmet Need Scale and the nine unmet need subscales. Specifically, results for the Total Unmet Need Scale indicated its highest loading was on component 2 (loading = 0.92) and contrasted with loadings for depressive symptoms, relationship strain, and emotional health strain that had their highest loadings on component 1 (loadings = 0.71 to 0.88). However, lack of caregiving confidence, one of the three measures of care-related strain, cross-loaded on both components 1 and 2 (loadings = 0.43 and 0.68, respectively), but its highest loading of 0.68 was on component 2. This suggested lack of caregiving confidence was associated with both components, but its strongest association was with the component representing the Total Unmet Need Scale.

Descriptive findings

Having established an overall acceptable level of validity and reliability, Table 5 displays descriptive findings for the Total Unmet Need Scale and the nine unmet need subscales. Nearly all caregivers reported they wanted more help or information with at least one of the 45 unmet need items (96.9%) and three quarters (75.5%) reported 10 or more unmet needs. Findings for the nine unmet need subscales revealed considerable variation in the most to least commonly reported medical and nonmedical unmet needs. Discussion and implications

Unmet need is a valuable construct aligned with two widely used frameworks for understanding the experience of dementia caregiving, the Stress Process Model (Pearlin et al., 1990) and the Behavioral Health Model (Andersen & Newman, 2005). Additionally, it can serve as a key outcome for assessing the efficacy and/or effectiveness of dementia caregiving support programs, many of which have the goal of reducing the unmet needs of family and friend caregivers (Maslow et al., 2021). Despite its theoretical and program implementation relevance, unmet need has rarely been used as an outcome measure in research studies (Minyo et al., 2025). One reason is the limited availability of valid and reliable unmet need measurement instruments that are feasible to use and that directly represent caregivers’ subjective perceptions rather than professionals’ judgements (Bangerter et al., 2019; Mansfield et al., 2017; Novais et al., 2017; Tatangelo et al., 2018).

To fill this gap, this investigation conducted a detailed assessment of the psychometric properties of one available tool for measuring unmet need, the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument. This Instrument is person- and family-centered by focusing on caregiver expressed needs for help or information with a wide range of medical and nonmedical issues based on their personal and cultural values and preferences. The breadth of potential unmet needs represented by the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument was intended to be sensitive to the full array of issues associated with dementia, which contrasts with other assessments that primarily emphasize unmet medical needs of people living with dementia. It also was designed to complement clinical outcomes that are commonly used in research on non-pharmacological interventions, which are typically less directly applicable to the goals of caregiving support programs (Gaugler et al., 2019).

Results demonstrated that the Total Unmet Need Scale and nine unmet need subscales had acceptable structural validity, explaining 60.9% of total variance, and high internal consistency reliability. Moreover, the vast majority of the 45 items had high loadings solely on their intended factors, with only a few patterns of cross-loadings. However, these cross-loadings may help to fully understand the challenges of dementia caregiving. For example, cross-loadings between selected items in the subscales Understanding Memory Problems (e.g., “How to deal with other health conditions or problems he/she has?”) and Monitoring Medications (e.g., “The way your [RELATIONSHIP’S] medications are supposed to help?”) may reflect the challenges of caregiving for someone living with physical health conditions that co-exist with dementia, including difficulties caused by cognitive and behavioral symptoms with adhering to and monitoring medications. Another example of cross-loadings was among selected items in the subscales Finding & Accessing Services, Getting Emotional Support & Respite, and Coordinating Advanced Care Planning. Although the Finding & Accessing Services subscale primarily represented unmet needs related to using formal services, the other two subscales also included, but were not limited to, challenges using specific individual services; including respite, legal, and financial services. Both of these examples illustrate how some subscales are conceptually related. They also identify areas for future improvements to the Instrument that would enhance its structural validity.

In terms of concurrent validity, the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument demonstrated good concurrent validity for the Total Unmet Need Scale and seven of the nine unmet need subscales based on small-to-moderate-size, statistically significant associations with measures of depressive symptoms and care-related strains. One of the two subscales that did not have concurrent validity was the single-item Getting Partner to Stop Driving. This subscale had two major limitations that may account for the low level of concurrent validity. First, a single-item scale is more likely to underrepresent the underlying construct and be vulnerable to measurement error (Diamantopoulos et al., 2012), constraining its reliability and validity. Second, this study sample had a relatively small portion of caregivers (15.5%) who reported needing help or information with getting their care receivers to stop driving, which may attenuate its associations with depressive symptoms and care-related strains (DeVellis, 2016). The low percentage of caregivers reporting this unmet need may reflect the moderate to severe level of cognitive impairment of most people living with dementia in this study, with issues related to driving resolved during earlier stages of the disease.

Unmet needs with monitoring medications was the other subscale with low concurrent validity. In this circumstance, unmet medication needs may be less related to increased depressive symptoms and care-related strains and more related to increased feelings of anxiety or fear of making errors when ordering medications, tracking doses, or giving injections for complex regimens (Li & Look, 2022). Similar to issues of driving, the low percentage of caregivers reporting unmet medication needs in this sample may be reflective of issues already resolved prior to participation in the original study. There also are widely available tools and equipment (e.g., medication dispensers, reminder systems) to assist with medication management, providing practical solutions that may tempore distress and resulting negative consequences.

Results for discriminant validity were mixed. The Total Unmet Need Scale and nine unmet need subscales demonstrated good discriminant validity, indicated by consistently being differentiated from depressive symptoms and two care-related strains (i.e., relationship strain and emotional health strain). However, discriminant validity was low for all unmet need measures based on their consistent overlap with lack of caregiving confidence, the third care-related strain. In some cases, lack of caregiving confidence was more strongly related to unmet need measures than to depressive symptoms and the other two care-related strains.

One possible explanation is that unmet need and lack of caregiving confidence are both associated with a third construct, such as perceived quality of care or perceived capacity to provide care, whereas depressive symptoms and the other two care-related strains are negative consequences or outcomes of care. This interpretation implies that lack of caregiving confidence may have been misclassified as a care-related strain for purposes of testing discriminant validity and that alternative measures of negative caregiving consequences should be used in future testing of the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument.

Limitations

This study was limited by its relatively small and nonprobability sample that does not represent the population of all dementia caregivers (n = 192). Compared to dementia caregivers nationally (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025), the study sample over-represents caregivers who identify as female, are spouses of care recipients, and assist care recipients who receive services from the Veterans Health Administration. Additionally, all care recipients had a dementia diagnosis, and over-represent persons who identify as male, and have more functional dependencies and co-existing chronic health conditions.

Future testing of the Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument with larger, more representative samples of dementia caregivers is needed to provide more variability and power to replicate the current validity and reliability results. Ideal samples for psychometric testing should have at least 10 participants per item for more stable factor loadings and lower measurement errors (Everitt, 1975). To enhance generalizability, future testing should include samples of caregivers and people living with dementia who have more diverse social, demographic, and health-related characteristics, as well as providing care in more diverse caregiving contexts (e.g., inter-household living arrangement, long-term care settings, and a broader range of geographic regions).

A second limitation was the inability to assess all types of validity and reliability using the available secondary data. For example, future studies should validate the Instrument’s predictive validity (i.e., how well it predicts outcomes overtime) and test-retest reliability (i.e., how consistent its psychometric properties are overtime). Additionally, future validity testing should use more rigorous statistical procedures to further test structural and discriminant validity. For instance, confirmatory analysis should be used for structure validity, which can be modeled on the current study findings that were derived through an iterative exploratory process. Similarly, confirmatory factor analysis using interfactor correlations or multitrait-multimethod matrix approaches should be used to further evaluate aspects of discriminant validity.

Finally, some domains of unmet need were not fully represented by the Instrument, despite its covering of nine different domains. For example, only a single item represented the domain of driving-related challenges (“Do you need more help or information with deciding if your [RELATIONSHIP] should stop driving?”). Additional content that should be considered includes getting healthcare providers to discuss the need to stop driving, planning alternative transportation strategies, and building consensus among family members about whether the person should stop driving (Liddle et al., 2013). Another domain not fully represented was the perceived quality of dementia care. Additional content to consider represents whether care tasks are being performed in a timely and comprehensive fashion and whether signs or risk factors for abuse, neglect, or financial exploitation are suspected. A final domain that should be expanded represents unmet needs related to fulfilling competing responsibilities such as providing care for multiple family members, balancing caregiving and work, and making changes in employment to provide care.

Conclusion

The Benjamin Rose Unmet Need Instrument is one of the few comprehensive unmet need measures shown to have acceptable psychometric properties in rigorous testing with a dementia caregiving sample. Results showed that unmet need is distinct from other commonly measured outcomes and has several strengths, including providing a total scale score that represents both medical and nonmedical needs and nine subscales that can be used separately or in combination when only select components of need are the primary focus. While continued validation is needed, the current version should be considered a valuable, ready-to-use, person-and family-centered outcome measure for studies seeking to better understand the full range of caregiver-reported challenges and needed assistance, as well as an outcome measure in efficacy and/or effectiveness studies of non-pharmacological dementia caregiving programs.
