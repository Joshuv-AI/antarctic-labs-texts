Matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging as a new tool for molecular histopathology in epilepsy surgery
Authors: Cinzia Cagnoli, Dalia De Santis, Claudio Caccia, Italia Bongarzone, Giulia Capitoli, Laura Rossini, Michele Rizzi, Francesco Deleo, Laura Tassi, Marco de Curtis, Rita Garbelli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpilepsy surgery is a treatment option for patients with seizures that do not respond to pharmacotherapy. The histopathological characterization of the resected tissue has an important prognostic value to define postoperative seizure outcome in these patients. However, the diagnostic classification process based on microscopic assessment remains challenging, particularly in the case of focal cortical dysplasia (FCD). Imaging mass spectrometry is a spatial omics technique that could improve tissue phenotyping and patient stratification by investigating hundreds of biomolecules within a single tissue sample, without the need for target‐specific reagents.MethodsAn in situ proteomic technique called matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry imaging (MALDI‐MSI) is here investigated as a potential new tool to expand conventional diagnosis on standard paraffin brain tissue sections. Unsupervised and region of interest‐based MALDI‐MSI analyses of sections from 10 FCD type IIb (FCDIIb) cases were performed, and the results were validated by immunohistochemistry.ResultsMALDI‐MSI identified distinct histopathological features and the boundaries of the dysplastic lesion. The capability to visualize the spatial distribution of well‐known diagnostic markers enabling multiplex measurements on single tissue sections was demonstrated. Finally, a fingerprint list of potential discriminant peptides that distinguish FCD core from peri‐FCD tissue was generated.SignificanceThis is the first study that explores the potential application of MALDI‐MSI in epilepsy postsurgery fixed tissue, by utilizing the well‐characterized FCDIIb features as a model. Extending these preliminary analyses to a larger cohort of patients will generate spectral libraries of molecular signatures that discriminate tissue features and will contribute to patient phenotyping.

INTRODUCTION

Molecular phenotyping on human tissue specimens represents one of the new frontiers in advanced diagnostics. 1 Characterization of specific identified targets with histological and gene expression analyses is becoming a crucial diagnostic tool increasingly utilized by neuropathologists. In a subset of patients with focal drug‐resistance epilepsy (fDRE), surgery is a safe and effective treatment. 2 In both children and adults, the spectrum of structural brain lesions histologically detected in fDRE surgical specimens is broad and includes diverse types of alterations beyond the three most common categories that include focal cortical dysplasias (FCDs; types I, II, and III), hippocampal sclerosis, and low‐grade developmental tumors. 3 Postsurgical seizure freedom, which is one of the main expectations of patients during presurgical counseling, depends on both pre‐ and postsurgical factors such as the site of surgery, the presence of a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)‐detectable lesion, and the duration of epilepsy history, and it is largely influenced by the histological diagnosis. 4 However, multiple studies have clearly shown that for certain categories, particularly FCD type I (FCDI) and so‐called mild malformation of cortical development (MCD), the histopathological diagnosis based on microscopic hallmarks remains challenging, and their incidence and clinical presentations reveal discrepancies among epilepsy surgery centers. 5 , 6 This variability highlights the urgent need to integrate the standard neuropathological workup with new techniques that improve disease definition and diagnosis. The recent discovery of somatic genetic variants in postsurgical brain samples is a growing field of interest; to date, somatic mutations were identified in a subgroup of MCD such as FCDII and mild MCD, 7 and promising data are coming from DNA methylation profiling of epileptogenic lesional brain tissues. 8 , 9 An additional puzzle associated with fDRE diagnosis is represented by the cryptogenic cases with no detectable lesion (approximately 8% of fDREs) 3 that may result in less favorable postsurgical seizure outcome. 10 The identification of specific tissue biomarkers in these cases would open new prospects in the understanding of fDRE pathogenesis. In this context, advancements in omics studies might offer a valuable contribution in histopathology.

Mass spectrometry imaging (MSI) is a powerful technology capable of label‐free detection of various biomolecules in their native spatial context. In particular, matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization (MALDI)–MSI is one of the most versatile imaging techniques, providing a detailed map of biomolecules that may be altered in lesional tissue. 11 , 12 Largely used in cancer research and diagnosis, 13 , 14 this technique has never been applied to epilepsy surgery specimens. In this work, we present the first study describing MALDI‐MSI of tryptic peptides on fixed tissue sections from patients with histological diagnosis of FCDIIb, a highly epileptogenic brain lesion that is the major cause of DRE in the pediatric population. 3 We use the analysis of postsurgical FCDIIb tissue samples as a model to validate MALDI‐MSI against histopathology; FCDIIb presents histologically with disrupted cortical architecture, abnormal cells (dysmorphic neurons [DNs] and balloon cells [BCs]), and white matter (WM) abnormalities. 15 , 16 We examined the feasibility of MALDI‐MSI to recognize lesional and perilesional boundaries and to identify discriminant peak lists within the different regions that could be utilized as tissue‐specific molecular fingerprint patterns. This proof‐of‐concept study lays the basis for extending its applicability to more challenging epileptogenic cases encountered in epilepsy surgery settings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Specimen selection and sample preparation

We selected formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from 10 representative cases of FCDIIb from our epilepsy surgery tissue repository, characterized by the presence of a clear lesional and perilesional area within the same tissue section (as illustrated in Figure 1) and by the absence of relevant histological artifacts and/or hemorrhages. All cases were processed using standard fixation and inclusion protocols; serial sections were processed for histology (hematoxylin–eosin, cresyl violet, Luxol fast blue staining) and immunohistochemistry (antibodies against NeuN, glial fibrillary acidic protein, SMI311, vimentin, myelin basic protein [MBP]) as described elsewhere. 17 To systematically optimize standard operating protocols for MALDI‐MSI analysis, we utilized one “no FCD case” (Patient 11, temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis) due to the relative abundance of this tissue in our repository. The technical repeatability of the method was evaluated in one small FCD specimen (Patient 9; see Table 1), selected to limit MALDI‐MSI scan time. After a comprehensive electroclinical and MRI evaluation, 18 all patients underwent surgery for strictly therapeutic reasons at the Carlo Besta Neurological Institute Foundation and at the Claudio Munari Epilepsy Surgery Center of Niguarda Hospital (both in Milan, Italy). The ethics committees of both institutes approved the diagnostic and therapeutic procedures and the use of brain material for research.

Sample preparation for MALDI‐MSI

As schematically illustrated in Figure 2, FFPE sections were cut and mounted onto conductive glasses coated with indium tin oxide (ITO; Bruker Daltonics) and stored at room temperature until the analysis. Then, tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and incubated in 10 mmol·L−1 citric acid solution at 110°C for 20 min for antigen retrieval. Trypsin (Promega, 25 ng/μL in 20 mmol·L−1 ammonium bicarbonate solution) was deposited onto tissue sections using a robotic spotter (HTX TM‐Sprayer, HTX Technologies), and two different conditions were tested (50°C for 2 h or 37°C for 18 h). After drying the sections, a matrix solution containing 10 mg/mL α‐cyano‐4‐hydroxycinnamic acid (Bruker) dissolved in 70:30 acetonitrile:water with 1% trifluoroacetic acid was deposited using HTX sprayer. A mixture of standard peptides within the mass to charge ratio (m/z) range of 757 and 3150 (Peptide Calibration Standard II, Bruker) was spotted onto the slide before the analysis and was used for external calibration. For technical repeatability, after optimization of the previously described parameters, three 7‐μm‐thick consecutive sections from Patient 9 were collected on ITO glasses and stored at room temperature until the day of the analysis; the three replicates were analyzed on different days.

MALDI‐MSI parameters and data processing

All the mass spectra were acquired in reflector positive mode across an m/z range of 600–2600 (with a range of 7469–49 061 spectra per tissue section, depending on the size of the section) with the UltrafleXtreme mass spectrometer using flexControl 3.4 and flexImaging 5.0 software (Bruker). Spectra were collected with a laser diameter of 50 μm and a raster sampling of 80 μm. After MALDI analysis, the matrix was removed with increasing concentrations of ethanol (70% and 100%) and the slides were stained with cresyl violet and converted to digital format (Nanozoomer XR, Hamamatsu). Adjacent immunostained sections (right arm in Figure 2) were also converted to digital format for direct overlap and precise spatial interpretation of the MALDI‐MSI results and for region of interest (ROI) annotation (left arm in Figure 2).

Following acquisition, flexImaging 5.0 data, containing spectra of each entire measurement region, were individually imported into SCiLS Lab 2023a Pro 11.00 software (Bruker), to perform baseline subtraction (TopHat algorithm) and normalization (Total Ion Current algorithm) preprocessing steps on the loaded spectra. A series of further steps were performed to generate an average spectrum representative of the whole measurement region by peak picking (Orthogonal Matching Pursuit algorithm) and peak alignment (to align the detected ions with peak maxima). Edge‐preserving spatial denoising was applied to reduce spectrum‐to‐spectrum variation. 19

To evaluate repeatability of the method, the absolute intensities of the common peaks in the m/z lists generated from MALDI‐MSI scan of Patient 9 entire sections were used to calculate the coefficient of variation percentage (CV%) across three technical replicates. Common peaks were calculated by Venn diagram. CV% was obtained for each m/z feature and then presented as mean ± SD.

Statistical analysis

Different statistical techniques were applied for the unsupervised mining of the datasets. Spatial segmentation was performed using bisecting K‐means clustering in combination with the correlation distance. Spectra were clustered according to similarity, progressively increasing the number of clusters, until a dendrogram was obtained. Each branch of the dendrogram, which represented a class of spectra, was color‐coded and then visualized on the tissue section as a segmentation map. Principal component analysis (PCA) and probabilistic latent semantic analysis (pLSA) were applied to reduce dimensionality and to uncover different classes in the datasets. PCA analysis was conducted on all individual spectra from the tissue regions, with weak denoising, and unit variance scaling. The optimal number of PCA components was selected based on the maximum variance explained, whereas the optimal number of pLSA components for analysis was estimated in advance. For this purpose, we ran tests with different numbers of components and evaluated the number of different visible histological distribution patterns. 20 Seven components were the best to satisfy the quality control criteria above and were therefore used for all pLSA analysis.

To search for peptide values that discriminate between the lesion (with the maximal cytoarchitectural alterations) and the perilesion (characterized by the absence of pathological cells), four ROIs were manually selected based on immunohistochemistry and were defined as the following: (1) core of the FCD lesion (L) rich in DNs (L DN+, usually in gray matter [GM]), (2) lesion core rich in BCs (L BC+, usually in WM), (3) FCD perilesion (PL) in normal GM (PL DN−), and (4) PL in normal WM (PL BC−; see Figure 1 for an example of ROI annotations). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used as a tool to assess the discrimination quality of all m/z values for populations defined through spectra extracted from distinct ROIs. Separate ROC analyses were then calculated for all picked peaks between (1) group 1: L DN+ versus group 2: PL DN−; and (2) group 1: L BC+ versus group 2: PL BC − . The area under the curve (AUC) determined the most discriminant signals, with an AUC of ≥.80. ROC analysis was performed on the first three chronologically consecutive cases, and the common peaks were used for further analysis in all remaining cases (except for Patients 1 and 2, for whom signals were acquired with a different experimental protocol). Normalized peak intensities in each ROI were compared, and a p‐value of ≤.05 was selected for a peak to be considered statistically significant using Wilcoxon rank sum test (with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparison); these values were used to generate a heat map. All statistical analyses were performed with SCiLs Lab (Bruker), Prism (GraphPad Software, www.graphpad.com), and open‐source R software 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Putative identification of discriminant m/z features

We tentatively identified peaks of interest using information available via MSiMass List (http://ms‐imaging.org/msi‐mass‐list/) or from the theoretical tryptic peptides obtained through the Protein Prospector website (https://prospector.ucsf.edu/prospector/cgi‐bin/msform.cgi?form=msdigest). 20 Protein identification was tentatively assigned to a peak if an error of less than ±150 ppm was calculated between the observed m/z mass and the theoretical value of the amino acid sequence 21 ; immunohistochemistry was used to validate the protein identification on adjacent sections.

RESULTS

The main clinical characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The series included tissue from 10 FCDIIb cases, mainly operated on frontal lobe, with a mean age at seizure onset of 8 ± 6 years and a mean duration of epilepsy of 18 ± 12 years. In all but one case (Patient 6), the presurgical imaging investigation was positive and suggestive for FCD. The surgical specimens complied with the histopathological International League Against Epilepsy classification criteria for type IIb FCD, 7 presenting in GM with severe cortical layering disruption and enlarged, disoriented DNs with cytoplasmic accumulation of neurofilaments frequently invading the GM/WM boundary. Immature BCs rich in vimentin intermediate filaments, typically featured only in FCDIIb, were frequently concentrated in the underlying WM, where a reduction in myelin fibers was also observed. Based on these common histological findings, we identified four different regions for ROI‐based analysis of each selected tissue section as follows: L DN+, L BC+, PL DN−, and PL BC−, as described in the Materials and Methods section and shown in Figure 1.

Representative FFPE tissue sections were submitted to the analytical workflow of the MALDI‐MSI experiment as described in Figure 2. To set up the general method on the specific tissue type, the following parameters were optimized. First, to evaluate the effect of different section thickness, the average spectra were measured in 5‐, 7‐, and 10‐μm sections from Patient 11; an overall enhanced peak intensity was observed in 7‐μm‐thick sections compared to the 5‐μm‐thick sections, whereas 10‐μm sections partially detached from the ITO slide, compromising the analysis (not shown). Second, two different conditions for trypsinization were assessed: the number of signals in the typical peptide mass range, especially for m/z between 1500 and 2000, was higher using 37°C and overnight incubation compared to the other condition (50°C for 2 h). No significant loss of peaks was observed in the low mass range (Figure S1).

The technical variability of this method was evaluated using sections from FCD Patient 9 by comparing the average spectra obtained in three measurements replicated on separate days. A reproducible number of peaks was detected in the different analytical sessions (302 ± 11 on average); there were 215 common peaks. Medium CV% calculated on common peaks was 14 ± 12 (Figure S2). After these preliminary validations, the above optimized conditions were then systematically applied in all FCD samples, except the first two cases recruited in chronological order (Patients 1 and 2; Table 1).

Our fist aim was to determine whether signatures derived from MALDI‐MSI data could distinguish different histopathological features in both the FCD core and the perilesional tissue. To achieve this, the acquired spectra of each entire tissue section were segmented through bisecting K‐means. The generated segments were then correlated to tissue morphology by comparison with cresyl violet staining of the same sections after MALDI‐MSI experiments and on adjacent immunoreacted sections. The clusters of spectra successfully identified different tissue features, with the spectra derived from GM and WM being clustered under the first separate branches (Figure 3A) and the region of the dysplastic lesion core segmented from the surrounding perilesional areas in the subsequent branches (Figure 3B). These findings were validated on adjacent sections by the specific staining for myelinated fibers, Luxol fast blue (Figure 3C), and by immunohistochemistry for the markers of BCs, vimentin (Figure 3D), NeuN for neuronal density (Figure 3E), and neurofilaments for DNs (not shown). Of note, the narrow strip of the deep cortical layer at the boundary between WM and GM was segmented in three of 10 cases as WM at the first dendrogram node (Figure 3A) and then as a transition zone between GM and WM at the second dendrogram node (Figure 3B). In a single case with a small tissue sample available, the spectra segmentation did not discriminate the region of the dysplastic lesion core from the perilesion zone. Ion images generated using peptides with different localizations, but specific for morphological regions, were further analyzed. The spatial distribution of tryptic peptides based on theoretical mass values tentatively related to specific proteins, such as MBP (m/z 726.39 in Figure 3F and Figure S4), vimentin (m/z 1428.71 in Figure 3G), and NeuN (m/z 2407.14 in Figure 3H), generated multiple maps on a single tissue section with good correspondence with the related immunohistochemistry.

Another exploratory examination of MALDI‐MSI datasets was performed through PCA, a statistical technique that reduces the dimensionality of complex data collections to a set of new variables, called principal components (PCs). For all samples analyzed, PCs 1–5 accounted for >75% of the total variance observed in the tissues. The three best PCs that contained the maximum amount of the information, such as GM/WM distinction (PC1 score maps in Figure 4A,E) and lesional/perilesional features (PC2 and PC4 in Figure 4A, PC3 and PC5 in Figure 4E), were translated into a graphical representation (score plot, Figure 4B). Then, reassigning each point of the score plot according to previously known morphologically different areas color‐coded as L DN+ (blue), L BC+ (yellow), PL DN− (green), and PL BC− (magenta; Figure 4C), PCA showed good segregation between spectra originating from different regions, confirming the ability of MALDI‐MSI to uncover distinct morphological characteristics of the analyzed tissues (Figure 4D,F). pLSA, used as a further multivariate analysis approach, again confirmed the capability of MALDI‐MSI to recognize regions with distinct physiological (WM/GM edges) and pathological (L vs. PL) tissue characteristics (Figure S3).

Finally, to search for m/z values that could discriminate among the previously considered histological regions, we applied a supervised approach using ROC analysis and generated a heat map for the top marker ions. This analysis was limited to the eight cases processed under the same experimental conditions (Patients 3–10; Table 1). Differential hierarchical cluster analysis clearly showed the presence of specific m/z species enriched in the different regions (Figure 5A). Tentative identification of some m/z peaks confirmed vimentin (m/z 1428.71) as a useful marker for L area rich in BC (L BC+; Figure 5A) and MBP (m/z 726.39) for PL (PL BC−; Figure 5A). Interestingly, several other potential markers were found enriched, such as m/z 1495.74 and 872.47618.31 in the L BC+ region and m/z 643.35, 699.43, 721.35, and 1352.62 in PL BC− region, that need further analysis to be identified. The comparison between L DN+ and PL DN− showed an enrichment in several m/z species in PL area compared to L (m/z 1701.91, 738.39, 1307.66, and 1484.79).

DISCUSSION

Neuropathology based on specific staining and multiple immunohistochemistry is the gold standard to diagnose brain tissue samples obtained after surgery, and for the specifics of the present study, after therapeutic epilepsy surgery. 3 , 4 However, diagnostic discordance and/or uncertainty may be present, particularly in FCD, as demonstrated in several interobserver agreement evaluations, 6 , 22 , 23 and it may influence outcome prognosis and decision‐making in the follow‐up of the patients.

On these grounds, we aimed at generating additional insights into the histopathologic diagnosis in epilepsy surgery by assessing the potential value of MSI of postsurgical tissue samples. This is the first study to adopt the MALDI‐MSI technique for structural brain lesions responsible for fDRE in FFPE samples; we chose to analyze FCD type IIb as a model, because it has the most robust and homogeneous clinical, electroencephalographic, neuroradiological, and histopathological features among the different FCDs. 24 Overall, our study revealed the capability of MALDI‐MSI data to identify the lesional area from the surrounding perilesion with different unsupervised approaches and to generate discriminant peak lists that define specific tissue fingerprints of these regions.

Mass spectrometry is a powerful bioanalytical tool for identifying and quantifying molecules in specimens with mass accuracy and sensitivity. In particular, the preservation of spatial information with MALDI‐MSI has great potential for improving histological diagnosis. MALDI‐MSI is commonly used to visualize proteins and peptides in pathological tissue sections directly in situ, and it can also target a number of other analytes including lipids and metabolites, depending on the sample preparation, as for lipid imaging in an FCDII mouse model. 25 This proteomic technique, originally developed for the analysis of fresh‐frozen tissue sections with obvious methodological limitations, is now applicable to FFPE sections using trypsin digestion protocols that allow access to a large collection of clinically annotated samples. 26 , 27

FFPE analysis requires a complex and quite long process of sample preparation, with deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and enzymatic digestion as fundamental steps; even though general MALDI‐MSI methods have been suggested, several parameters must be optimized specifically for particular specimens. The thickness of the sections and the trypsinization procedures affect the final MALDI‐MSI data, as our findings demonstrate. From this perspective, we conducted a first round of targeted experiments to develop the best technique for our brain sample preparation. The technical repeatability of the procedure was assessed on successive sections of a single FCD case after the experimental conditions were established, and the observed coefficient of variation was consistent with recent works that examined tryptic peptides on FFPE tissue. 28 , 29

When submitting each complete MALDI‐MSI dataset to different unsupervised statistical approaches, such as spatial segmentation, PCA, and pLSA, it was possible to successfully distinguish different tissue morphological regions based on their spectral profiles.

Spatial segmentation was employed for a first data mining by clustering the spectra into distinct groups as per their similarities. For the clustering process, the bisecting K‐means method was selected, which is optimized for hierarchical clustering of large MALDI‐MSI datasets to find hidden structures in the unlabeled data. 30 The dysplastic lesional FCDIIb core was characterized by heterogeneity and could be separated from more normal adjacent regions. Similarly, the GM/WM boundaries were often segmented as an intermediate zone probably due to the decreasing gradient of myelinated fibers between WM and GM. However, this methodology required an expert pathologist to select the dendrogram nodes that reflect histopathological features. We therefore tested for unsupervised analysis such as PCA and pLSA.

In MALDI‐MSI, the variables represented by the peptide peaks and their respective intensities for each single position acquired in the tissue result in high‐dimensional datasets. PCA allows description of the dataset with a lower number of variables, while still retaining as much of the original variation as possible. This analysis on type IIb FCD indicated that tryptic peptide data were able to reveal changes within different histopathological regions, providing a general proof of the ability of MALDI‐MSI to distinguish dysplastic from nondysplastic tissue. Similar results were obtained using pLSA. Future work will need to examine lesions with boundaries that are not clearly identified and defined with traditional histology techniques. Interestingly, rapid MALDI‐MSI methods (<5 min) compatible with the analysis of excised tissue samples are currently under investigation, suggesting its applicability for intraoperative diagnostics in the not‐too‐distant future. 31 , 32 Another interesting advantage of MALDI‐MSI was the capability to visualize the spatial distribution of several well‐known diagnostic markers, enabling multiplex measurements on a single tissue section. We generated ion images of several m/z values that were observed unambiguously distributed in distinct regions. For instance, the WM was nicely visualized by m/z 726.39 that, according to both theoretical tryptic peptides website and published works 28 and to adjacent immunohistochemically stained sections, was identified as MBP expression. Notably, the MALDI image highlighted not only the MBP distribution but also the ion intensity according to the respective x/y positions, showing high level of expression in normal WM, low level in the region of dysplasia, and the presence of infragranular myelinated fibers in cortical GM (as illustrated in Figure S4). Similarly, the ion images generated by m/z 1428.71 and 2407.14 colocalized well with vimentin and NeuN expression areas, respectively. Vimentin, a typical marker of BCs, was highly enriched in the lesion, whereas NeuN expression was reduced inside the lesion, due to reduced neuronal density frequently observed inside the dysplasia. 33 The possibility to detect multiple markers without being restricted to the use of specific antibodies might be helpful in the case of limited tissue availability. This approach allows the acquisition of a large number of spectra (>10 000) per sample, thus improving the analytical robustness of the results. There is an important intrinsic limitation in the present data due to the low spatial resolution we used for these analyses (80 μm); this parameter was chosen to acquire the entire section and to explore the lesional/perilesional boundary as the primary aim of this study. Future work will focus on the analysis of regions containing multiple single cells of interest, ideally detected by different molecular expression patterns. Moreover, the recent advances in high spatial resolution and high sensitivity in MSI techniques make the possibility to perform imaging experiments at a single cell level more approachable. 34

Finally, a supervised approach guided by histology enabled the selection of differentially expressed peptides, which distinguish lesional from perilesional regions, either in GM or WM, in differential cluster analysis. Some potential m/z species were expected, such as the enrichment in vimentin expression in lesional WM compared to perilesional WM and the decreased myelin‐related proteins like MBP in lesional WM. In this initial study, proteins that are currently used to label DNs, such as phosphorylated and nonphosphorylated neurofilaments, were not identified by ROC analysis. This could be due to several reasons; as they are expressed also in normal neurons, the reduced density of neurons in lesional GM coupled with the spatial resolution of our analysis might have had a “dilution” effect on the signal in a pixel average. On the contrary, the analysis suggests some discriminant peptides for GM lesional area, rich in DNs, and GM perilesion are currently unknown and could be identified by further in‐depth analysis with high‐sensitivity techniques such as liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry. The strength of this analysis is indeed to highlight what is currently not known.

CONCLUSIONS

Extending MALDI‐MSI analysis to a larger cohort could generate spectral libraries of molecular signatures (defined as a combination of m/z values and intensities) that discriminate tissue features within and outside the epileptogenic lesion in different histologies and could allow an automated unbiased classification based on the detection of a specific mix of both identifiable and presently unknown molecules. Moreover, this study highlights the potential of this powerful approach to obtain multimolecular information, with a great spatial resolution, offering novel avenues for advanced FCD phenotyping.

The possibility to integrate this proteomic signature with in vivo MRI FCD profilings 35 , 36 , 37 and image postprocessing facilitates the development of future personalized treatments within a precision medicine framework.
