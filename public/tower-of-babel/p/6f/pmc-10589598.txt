Fibrinogen; a predictor of injury severity and mortality among patients with traumatic brain injury in Sub-Saharan Africa: A cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Ssenyondwa John Baptist, Kiryabwire Joel, Martin Kaddumukasa, Devereaux Michael, Larrey Kasereka Kamabu, Moses Galukande, Mark Kaddumukasa, Martha Sajatovic, Makumbi Timothy Kabanda
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Studies show that fibrinogen concentrations <2 g/L in patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) is associated with increased mortality. However, little is known regarding fibrinogen levels and TBI severity as well as mortality in sub-Saharan Africa despite shouldering a high burden of TBI. We therefore set out to determine whether fibrinogen levels are associated with TBI severity and outcome. To determine the sensitivity and specificity of fibrinogen levels and the association with severity and mortality among TBI patients at Mulago Hospital. We prospectively enrolled 213 patients with TBI aged between 13 and 60 years of age and presenting within 24 hours of injury. Patients with preexisting coagulopathy, concurrent use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents, preexisting hepatic insufficiency, diabetes mellitus and who were pregnant were excluded. Fibrinogen levels were determined using the Clauss fibrinogen assay. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify the association between fibrinogen level and 7-day outcomes. Majority of the patients were male (88.7%) and nearly half were aged 30 or less (48.8%). Fibrinogen levels <2 g/L were observed in 35.1% of the study participants. The average time spent in the study was 3.7 ± 2.4 days. The sensitivity and specificity using fibrinogen <2 g/L was 56.5% and 72.9% respectively. Fibrinogen levels predict TBI severity with an AUC = 0.656 (95% CI 0.58–0.73: P = .000) Fibrinogen levels <2 g/L (hypofibrinogenemia) were independently associated with severe TBI. (Adjusted odds ratio 2.87 CI, 1.34–6.14: P = .007). Levels above 4.5 g/L were also independently associated with injury severity (adjusted odds ratio 2.89, CI 1.12–7.48: P < .05) Fibrinogen levels more than 4.5 g/L were independently associated with mortality (OR 4.5, CI; 1.47–13.61, P < .05). The fibrinogen level is a useful tool in predicting severity including mortality of TBI. Fibrinogen levels may be used as an additional tool to screen TBI patients for injury severity especially among patients with Glasgow coma scale scores of <14.

1. Introduction

Trauma accounts for 11% of the world disability adjusted life years with 90% of these occurring in low and middle income countries.[1] Traumatic brain injury (TBI) per se is a major cause of disability globally with an incidence rate of 200 per 100,000 people per year.[2] In Uganda, head injuries with TBI are the commonest type of injuries accounting for 44% of trauma admissions at hospitals in Kampala[3] and mortality rate of 220/100,000.[4] The morbidity and mortality due to TBI is higher in low-income and middle-income countries[5,6] despite advancements in the clinical evaluation of patients with TBI using standardized protocols such as the Advanced Trauma Life Support protocols.[7,8] Evidence from prior studies shows that deaths from trauma can be prevented if adequate and timely identification of the problem is done and the appropriate line of management is decided early.[9] In low- and middle-income settings where there is limited access to prompt investigation modalities for TBI victims, clinicians often find themselves relying on trauma algorithms, trauma assessment tools and clinical examination findings to diagnose and direct TBI management. Some of the trauma assessment tools employed in the evaluation of TBI patients include; abbreviated injury score, trauma injury severity score and the Glasgow coma scale (GCS) specifically for TBI.[10] These assessment tools have been found to have considerable limitations and that they may not correlate well with severity of injury.[11] Among these, the GCS remains the commonest tool used to assess TBI severity in Sub-Saharan Africa.[12,13] Despite its wide utility, the GCS does not provide specific parametric clinical information about the pathophysiologic abnormalities in TBI which are the targets of our interventions.[14]

Fibrinogen, which is a positive acute phase protein[15] as well as a hemostatic protein[16] has been retrospectively studied as a prognostic indicator among TBI patients as well as a predictor of in hospital mortality.[17–19] Following trauma, fibrinogen levels deteriorate more frequently and earlier than other routine coagulation parameters.[20,21] Additionally hypofibrinogenemia has been described as a common occurrence in TBI possibly due to trauma induced coagulopathy.[18,20,22] A recent study showed that fibrinogen concentrations <2 g/L were associated with poor outcomes including mortality in contrast to concentrations above 2.5 g/L that are associated with favorable outcomes.[17,18] Despite the above findings, little is known regarding its sensitivity and specificity in diagnosis of severe TBI in sub-Saharan Africa.

Therefore, we set out to determine the sensitivity and specificity of low fibrinogen levels in predicting severity of traumatic brain injuries, to describe the association of fibrinogen levels with TBI severity and 7-day outcomes among TBI patients at Mulago Hospital. We hypothesized that plasma fibrinogen levels are associated with severity and short-term clinical outcomes in TBI patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This was a cross-sectional study with a follow up component where we prospectively studied 213 randomly selected TBI patients admitted to the Casualty unit at Mulago National Referral Hospital between December 2021 and May 2022. Mulago National Referral Hospital is the biggest public hospital in Uganda at approximately 5 kilometers from the city center and it receives 75% of injured victims in Kampala.[23] The Casualty unit of the hospital is the entry point for all trauma cases presenting to the hospital.

2.2. Study population and sampling

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients aged 13 to 60 years with a clinical diagnosis of TBI documented using computed tomography (CT) or GCS score by clinician and admitted within 24 hours of TBI occurrence. The age range of 13 to 60 years was used in consideration of the altered metabolism of fibrinogen that occurs at the young and elderly extremes of age.[24–26] TBI in this study was defined as any alteration of brain function or presence of other evidence of brain pathology based on the GCS score or head CT scan findings in a patient with clinical history and physical evidence of trauma caused by an external force such as vehicle accidents, assault, falls and burns.[27] Patients on concurrent use of anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents, medical diagnosis of liver disease, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus, patients admitted after 24 hours of the injury occurrence and pregnant women were excluded.

To study the relationship of fibrinogen with outcomes, we calculated the sample size using formula for cohort studies based on comparison of 2 proportions representing the event rates in both the exposed and the non-exposed groups.[28] Using proportions from the study by Lv et al,[18] with 95% CI and power of 80%, we determined a sample size of 140 patients for the cohort. Adjusting upwards for losses to follow up, we estimated the sample size to be 213 patients. We therefore enrolled a total of 213 patients using systematic random sampling to answer our objectives. All patients were evaluated and treated according to the local protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from the patients or legally authorized representative to be included in the study. For the unconscious patients without a legally authorized representative a waiver of informed consent was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Makerere University and Mulago Hospital Ethics committee.

2.3. Study procedure and data collection

The emergency unit admits about 50 patients with varying degrees of TBI per day. Using systematic random sampling, we approached and enrolled every 4th patient admitted based on the sampling frame for that particular day until the desired sample size was attained.

Data obtained included demographic information such as age, sex, occupation, time of injury, level of education, mechanism of injury and type of head injury. Clinical data including, blood pressure, pulse oximetry, temperature, pupillary reaction, CT scan results, GCS score and fibrinogen levels taken at time of admission were also obtained. The severity of injury was determined by the GCS score obtained by the neurosurgical team. The GCS score of ≤ 8 was categorized as severe TBI and scores 9 to 15 as non-severe TBI. Fibrinogen levels were measured by the Clauss fibrinogen assay using the “Yumizen G FIB 5” reagent. The test was carried out on fresh decalcified venous blood obtained from the participants. On admission, 5 milliliters of venous blood were drawn from each of the participants into 3.2% sodium citrate vacutainers and transported to the laboratory within 60 minutes of collection for analysis. Samples were centrifuged to obtain plasma that was prepared for analysis as a 1:10 dilution with Yumizen G. Imidazol buffer. The prepared sample was then analyzed using an automated analyzer and results recorded in g/L. We obtained levels <1 g/L as well as those above 5 g/L that were retested at 1:5 dilution and 1:20 dilution respectively to obtain final results. The fibrinogen levels were categorized as: normal fibrinogen levels between 2 and 4.5 g/L.[29] A fibrinogen level of <2 g/L was considered as being low and a level of >4.5 g/L as high according to standard laboratory reference values. The patients were followed up daily for 7 days and outcomes documented. The clinical outcome studied was in hospital mortality within 7 days of admission. The 11 patients lost to follow up were not analyzed for outcomes. They were however included in the analysesis for the association between fibrinogen and injury severity on admission to the hospital.

2.4. Statistical analysis

All study data collected was entered in Epidata version 4.6 software, cleaned and exported to STATA version 14 for analysis. Continuous variables were summarized as means with standard deviation. Categorical variables are expressed as percentages. Bivariate analyses of categorical variables were performed using Pearson chi test and presented as P values. A receiver operating characteristic curve was used to describe the sensitivity and specificity of fibrinogen levels in patients with TBI. The sensitivity ± positive predictive value and specificity ± negative predictive value of fibrinogen levels was calculated using a 2 × 2 table. Patients with GCS score of 9 to 15 were categorized as non-severe head injury and those with GCS of 8 and below as severe head injury. Fibrinogen levels were categorized at 3 levels as; <2 g/L, 2 to 4.5 g/L and those above 4.5 g/L basing on the clinical ranges of normal levels. Simple logistic regression models were used to describe the relationship between individual predictors and the severity of injury. The predictors with an α ≤ 0.2, those with biologic plausibility and non collinear to each other were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. The predictors studied included the categorized fibrinogen levels, temperature, oximetry, systolic blood pressure (SBP), head injury, age and sex. All patients with missing fibrinogen results and those lost to follow up were excluded from the analyses for the outcome, that is, mortality. The models were tested for multiple collinearities, goodness of fit and all independent variables with correlation coefficient above ± 0.4 were excluded from the logistic regression model. The relationship is presented as odds ratio with 95% confidence intervals. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata statistical software, StataCorp. 2015. Stata Statistical Software: Release 14. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP. For all analysis, the level of statistical significance was considered when P < .05.

3. Results

A total of 213 TBI patients were included in the study analysis (Fig. 1). Road traffic crashes were responsible for TBI in 72.3% (n = 154) of the patients followed by assault (n = 45, 21.13%). 101 (47.42%) of the patients had severe TBI while 112 (52.58%) had non severe TBI. The majority of the participants were male (n = 189, 88.73%, M: F = 189:24) and aged 30 or less (n = 104, 48.83%). The average age of the study population was 32.42 ± 11.98 years with the minimum age of 13 and maximum age of 60. The majority of patients had closed head injuries (n = 115, 53.99%) (Table 1).

In the majority (107, 50.71%) of the patients, the fibrinogen levels were between 2 and 4.5 g/L. The maximum level observed was 7.81g/L. The minimum values were as low as <1 g/L. Levels <2 g/L were observed in 74 (35.07%) of the patients. Levels of fibrinogen >4.5 g/L were observed in 30 (14.22%) of the patients. During the study, 93 patients were discharged within the 7 days of the study, 73 died, 11 patients lost to follow up, 34 were still admitted by 7 days of the study and 2 patients had erroneous fibrinogen assay results. Forty-seven patients 47 (64.38%) died at Casualty within 24 hours of admission, 14 (19.18%) died in the Neurosurgery unit and 12 (16.44%) in the intensive care unit.

Low fibrinogen levels <2 g/L were associated with GCS below 8, with a P = .00; 95% CI 1.876 to 6.504 (Table 2). The sensitivity of using fibrinogen <2 g/L in identifying severe TBI was SN = 56.5%, PPV = 64.9%, (P < .001) The specificity of using fibrinogen <2 g/L to identifying severe TBI was SP = 72.9%, NPV = 61.7% (P < .001) with an AUC of 0.656, 95% CI (0.58–0.73) P < .001 (Fig. 2).

4. Discussion

This study set out to determine the specificity and sensitivity of fibrinogen levels <2 g/L in predicting severe TBI and the association between fibrinogen level with TBI severity and mortality at Mulago Hospital. There are no studies reviewing the sensitivity and specificity of fibrinogen levels from the sub-Saharan Africa region and it is the first study in Uganda to describe the association with severity. Despite the numerous evidence available regarding the predictability of fibrinogen in the prognosis of TBI outcomes from prior studies,[18,30] little is known concerning its diagnostic ability in the diagnosis of severe TBI.

Hypofibrinogenemia (fibrinogen levels <2 g/L) was common in TBI patients occurring in 35.07% of TBI patients on admission in our study. This is similar to 38.6% found in a previous study done by Lv, et al[18] Our study also found that 20.4% of patients with TBI had high levels of fibrinogen (>4.5 g/L). Our study found that low fibrinogen levels (<2 g/L) had a sensitivity of 56.5% and specificity of 72.9% in predicting severe TBI with an AUC = 0.656. The sensitivity found in our study was comparable to the sensitivity of 56.1% and specificity of 82.2% from a prior study that studied multiply injured TBI patients.[31]

Recent research showed that for patients admitted with severe TBI, fibrinogen levels <2 g/L on admission are strongly related to increased mortality.[18] In addition, studies have demonstrated that in severe trauma, fibrinogen is reduced to critical levels.[20,32] The debate about the critical value of fibrinogen in trauma is an ongoing matter of contention.[33] Floccard et al defined critical levels as being ≤1.0 g/L and abnormal levels being 1.0 to 1.8 g/L all of which have been reported in patients with severe trauma.[34]

This study shows that the likelihood of having a severe form of TBI increases with low levels of fibrinogen (<2 g/L). Possible explanations for the above relationship stem from the presence of intracranial bleeding which is a common occurrence in TBI as noted in the CRASH trial.[35] Trauma induced coagulopathy is a crucial element in severe TBI especially when compounded with intracranial bleeding. The consumption of clotting factors and platelets in response to intracranial bleeding further lowers fibrinogen levels.[36] TBI is also associated with systemic hyperfibrinolysis which occurs in as much as 20% of critical trauma patients hence lowering fibrinogen levels further.[37,38] In addition, prior studies have shown that fibrinogen levels drop drastically and most rapidly during hemorrhage.[32] Therefore, the association of low fibrinogen levels with severe TBI is possibly due to a combination of hemorrhage and trauma induced coagulopathy that occurs in severe TBI.[39]

We found no association between low fibrinogen levels <2 g/L with 7-day mortality among the patients with TBI. While one of the strengths of our study is that it was carried out prospectively in a high-volume trauma center, some limitations need to be acknowledged. This was a single center study with a rather small sample size and with limited duration allocated to conduct the study due to specified time frames for research activities. Secondly, fibrinogen is not a routine test in Mulago Hospital for TBI patients and replacement therapy is not currently part of the management protocols in patients with TBI; hence, despite the identification of abnormalities among the participants, correction therapy with concentrate was not possible for the participants. Patients with coagulopathies however received other supplements such as tranexamic acid and Fresh frozen plasma.

5. Conclusions

The study reveals that the sensitivity of fibrinogen levels <2 g/L in predicting severe TBI is 56.5% and the specificity is 72.9%. Fibrinogen levels <2 g/L do not predict 7-day mortality.

Fibrinogen may be further studied as a biomarker in evaluating severe TBI. Our study highlights the relevance of future larger research in resource limited areas with high volume of trauma. Additional prospective studies with larger sample size and longer study duration are needed to confirm the predictability of TBI severity and clinical outcomes using fibrinogen levels.
