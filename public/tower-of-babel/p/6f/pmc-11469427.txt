Histone-like transcription factor Hfl1p in Candida albicans harmonizes nuclear and mitochondrial genomic network in regulation of energy metabolism and filamentation development
Authors: Xiaodong She, Xiaowei Zhou, Meng Zhou, Lulu Zhang, Richard Calderone, Joseph A Bellanti, Weida Liu, Dongmei Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCandida albicans is an opportunistic fungal pathogen known for surviving in various nutrient-limited conditions within the host and causing infections. Our prior research revealed that Hfl1p, an archaeal histone-like or Hap5-like protein, is linked to mitochondrial ATP generation and yeast-hyphae morphogenesis. However, the specific roles of Hfl1p in these virulence behaviours, through its function in the CBF/NF-Y complex or as a DNA polymerase II subunit, remain unclear. This study explores Hfl1p’s diverse functions in energy metabolism and morphogenesis. By combining proteomic analysis and phenotypic evaluations of the hfl1Δ/hfl1Δ mutant with ChIP data, we found that Hfl1p significantly impacts mitochondrial DNA-encoded CI subunits, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and morphogenetic pathways. This influence occurs either independently or alongside other transcription factors recognizing a conserved DNA motif (TAXXTAATTA). These findings emphasize Hfl1p’s critical role in linking carbon metabolism and mitochondrial respiration to the yeast-to-filamentous form transition, enhancing our understanding of C. albicans’ metabolic adaptability during morphological transition, an important pathogenic trait of this fungus. This could help identify therapeutic targets by disrupting the relationship between energy metabolism and cell morphology in C. albicans.KEYWORDS: Candida albicans, mitochondrial transcriptional factor, filamentation repressor, glycolysis, mitochondrial function, ChIP-seq analysis

Background

Opportunistic human pathogen Candida albicans possesses a complex genomic regulatory network governing virulence-related processes. These mechanisms encompass stress adaptation, morphogenesis, drug resistance, and energy metabolism. While the model yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae has provided valuable insights into these mechanisms, the genome of C. albicans contains unique gene families, such as adhesins, morphogenesis regulators, and lipases/proteases, which are absent in S. cerevisiae [1]. Studies both from independent and our own laboratories have demonstrated that these pathogenic traits are intricately co-regulated with metabolic activity.

Regarding energy metabolism, notable disparities exist between pathogenic C. albicans and the non-pathogenic S. cerevisiae. Firstly, S. cerevisiae lacks an electron transport chain (ETC) complex I (CI). Conversely, C. albicans possesses a CI composed of nearly 40 subunit proteins, several of which are specific to CTG species [2]. Certainly, the absence of a CI in S. cerevisiae was expected to have different regulation on carbon utilization, morphogenesis, and signalling pathways from C. albicans [3,4]. In C. albicans, the deletion of specific CI subunit proteins reduces cell wall levels of mannoproteins and glucans, ergosterol synthesis, and signalling events, ultimately have been shown to result in an avirulent phenotype in animal models [5–7].

Secondly, glucose metabolism regulation differs between S. cerevisiae and C. albicans. For instance, while mitochondrial retrograde signalling (Rtg) triggers transcriptional reprogramming of carbon metabolism in S. cerevisiae [8], it integrates the nutrient TOR sensor into sphingolipid synthesis instead in C. albicans [9]. Additionally, Snf1, a protein kinase activated by glucose input in S. cerevisiae to promote fermentation metabolism, is constitutively expressed in C. albicans to maintain baseline levels of both respiration and fermentation with varying glucose availability [10].

Nevertheless, the integration of nutrient sensing in the cytosol with the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and respiration constitutes a pivotal aspect of energy metabolism in C. albicans. However, the precise regulatory mechanisms governing this intricate integration remain largely unknown. Since many mitochondrial proteins engaged in ETC and TCA cycle are encoded by the nuclear genome, it becomes reasonable to consider the possible regulator connecting mitochondrial fitness with cytosolic metabolic processes. Notably, our earlier studies highlighted three C. albicans transcription factors – Hfl1p, Rbf1p, and Dpb4p – as potential contributors to the coupling of mitochondrial energy metabolism [11].

Hfl1p and Rbf1p in our previous study, in particular, play critical roles in maintaining enzymatic activities of mitochondrial CI and CIV. We found that null mutants (rbf1Δ/rbf1Δ and hfl1Δ/hfl1Δ) fail to grow on medium with glycerol as the sole carbon source. Even in non-hyphae inducing media, they exhibit extensive and persistent hyphal growth. Intriguingly, neither gene mutant impact the copy number of mtDNA, which is different from the dpb4Δ/dpb4Δ mutant. The dpb4Δ/dpb4Δ mutant exhibits reduced mtDNA copies but fewer defects in morphogenesis and energy metabolism. While Rbf1 is well known as a morphogenesis repressor, the precise function of Hfl1p remains unclear.

The unaffected mtDNA copy number in hfl1Δ/hfl1Δ dismisses the notion that Hfl1p is essential for mtDNA synthesis pertaining to energy metabolism. The functional annotation of Hfl1ps apparently could be elicited by in comparison to orthologs in other organisms. The HFL1 gene (orf19.3063) on chromosome 1A of C. albicans exhibits dual characteristics. On one hand, Hfl1p is recognized as an HAP5-like protein within the CGD database. In comparison to the Dpb3p ortholog of S. cerevisiae or the FgscA4 ortholog of Aspergillus nidulans, the sequence similarities with both orthologs range between 32% and 33%. Both orthologs function as a subunit 3 of DNA polymerase ε, contributing to the elongation of the leading DNA strand during nuclear DNA replication.

On the other hand, Hfl1p (A0A8H6F1J6) is designated as an archaeal histone-like transcription factor (CBF/NF-Y) in UniProt. Here, CBF stands for the CCAAT motif-binding factor, operating as a multimeric complex with Hap2p/Hap3p/Hap5p in yeast or NF-YA/NF-YB/NF-YC in mammalian cells. Originally designated as a master regulator of mitochondrial respiration in S. cerevisiae, it identifies the CCAAT consensus sequence situated in the promoter regions of the CYC1 (cytochrome c in ETC) and numerous cytochrome genes [12]. While the Hap2/3/5 subcomplex of S. cerevisiae requires Hap4p association, Candida species have multiple paralogs (Hap43p, Hap41p, and Hap42p) that act as repressors or activators for Hap complex targets. Intriguingly, the HAP subunit null mutations (hap2p, hap3p, hap4p, and hap5p) of S. cerevisiae hinder growth on nonfermentable carbon sources, resembling the phenotype of the hfl1∆ null mutant [11]. In contrast, deletion of CaHAP2, CaHAP3, CaHAP5, or CaHAP43 does not affect growth on glycerol medium. Instead, the HAP complex in C. albicans is implicated in iron homoeostasis, although CYC1 transcription remains among the target genes of Hap complex [13]. Thus, energy metabolism regulation in C. albicans seems unrelated to the Hap complex.

In the context of fungi like C. albicans, morphogenesis involves the transition between different growth forms, i.e. the switch between a single-celled yeast form and a filamentous hyphal form. Since this ability to morphogenesis between different growth forms is often associated with virulence and pathogenicity, infection-causing fungi may utilize morphogenesis to adapt to different environments within the host, facilitating tissue invasion and evasion of host immune response. Morphogenesis is disrupted by the deletion of HFL1, preventing the customary yeast-to-hyphal transition and resulting in a hyperfilamentous phenotype in both glucose-rich and hyphal-inducing environments. This contrasts with the hyperfilamentous growth in mutants lacking each subunit of the HAP complex (hap2Δ, hap31Δ, hap32Δ, hap5Δ, hap41Δ, hap42Δ, hap43Δ) under glucose-depleted conditions, and a non-hyphal state in hyphal-inducing conditions [12,14]. Again, the role of Hfl1p dos not appear to align with the function of the HAP complex in regulating cortex of morphogenesis. Based on these findings, we propose that Hfl1p, the HAP5-like protein, operates distinctly from Hap5 in assembling the HAP complex. It functions independently to couple energy metabolism with morphological transitions in C. albicans.

In the present study, a chromatin immunoprecipitation-sequencing (ChIP-seq) approach was employed to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms governed by Hfl1p in both energy metabolism and morphogenesis. By investigating the genomic interactions between DNA and Hfl1p in C. albicans, our overall goal is to provide insights into the underlying causes of abnormal mitochondrial function and morphogenesis in null hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutants, as well as the mutant’s virulence in a systemic murine model. Additionally, we compare identified ChIP-seq targets with proteomic findings and previously published differential expression profiles of the mutant. Moreover, we validate the function of Hfl1p in mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) assembly assays using Blue Native Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (BN-PAGE) and examine mitochondrial colocalization under fluorescent microscopy. By unravelling these molecular interactions, we aim to bridge knowledge gaps on the mechanism coupling morphogenesis with energy metabolism, potentially identifying novel therapeutic targets.

Results

Validation of Hfl1p-tap by western blot

The TAP-tagged method has proven to be effective in studying protein-DNA complex interactions in C. albicans, owing to its highly immunogenic peptides and limited biological activity [15]. To mitigate allele interference, we employed a heterozygous HFL1/hfl1∆ strain to create the HFL1-TAP1-ARG4 (HTA) strain, as illustrated in Figure 1a. The HTA construct at the HFL1 locus was confirmed by a series of PCR assays (data not shown). Subsequently, three positive transformants—HTA2, HTA6, and HTA9—were identified using an anti-TAP antibody by western blotting, as depicted in Figure 1b.

To optimize the antibody concentration for subsequent ChIP experiments, we evaluated various commercial products and found that anti-TAP-Tag mAb (ABclonal) at a 1:5000 dilution produced a clean product within the 38–52 kDa range, consistent with the 47 kDa size of TAP-tagged Hfl1p protein. We excluded HTA2 from the following experiment due to its poor reproducible performance on western blot. However, when comparing HTA9 and HTA6, the morphological phenotype of HTA6 closely resembles that of the wild-type strain (WT), as seen in Figure 1c. This is evidenced by the reduced filamentous growth, a predominant growth form of the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant [11], observed in YPD broth and on agar plates.

Alignment of Hfl1 protein with orthologs and prediction for mitochondrial localization

In archaebacteria (prokaryotes), histone-like proteins can be classified into two main functional groups: one involved in DNA packing and the other acting as regulators for nearby operon expression, as described by Sakrikar et al. [16]. The GO annotation of CaHfl1p in the CGD (Candida genome database) indicates its similarity to Dpb3p of S. cerevisiae, which is noted in the UniProt database with identifier Q5AHY9. Additionally, the same Hfl1p protein is duplicated in the UniProt database, identified as a histone-like transcription factor (CBF/NF-Y) and an archaeal histone family protein with identifier A0A8H6F1J6. However, there is no experimental evidence available regarding the DNA-binding capabilities and the subcellular location of CaHfl1p in both databases. Upon comparing the sequence similarities among CaHap5p, CaHfl1p, and ScDpb3p using the EMBI-EBI Clustal Omega multiple sequence alignment tool (Figure 1d), it was observed that all three proteins possess the histone fold domain (domain #2) [17]. The identity rates between CaHfl1p and CaHap5p (31%) are similar to those between CaHfl1p and ScDpb3p (30%). Nevertheless, unlike CaHap5, both CaHfl1p and ScDpb3p and mammalian NF-YC lack the first domain, which plays a crucial role in recruiting Hap4 in the yeast.

The mitochondrial localization of Hfl1p is then predicted by various online programs. Although no mitochondrial targeting peptide can be detected by the TargetP-2.0 platform, the DeepMito platform [18] yielded prediction scores of 0.53 for CaHfl1p and 0.17 for ScDpb3p. The score for CaHfl1p is similar to that of known mitochondrial CI proteins such as Nuo1p (0.56), Nde1p (0.45), and Goa1p (0.9). These findings suggest a potential mitochondrial localization for CaHfl1p.

ChIP-evidence of Hfl1p-interacting genes and transcription factors

Quality and quantities of mapped clean reads in ChIP-seq analysis

The Fastq files provided high-quality clean reads, with IP_HTA_6 and IP_HTA_9 samples yielding a total of 48,721,480 bp and 49,661,804 bp, respectively (as summarized in Table S2). The ChIP-seq data have been submitted to the NCBI SRA repository and can be accessed with the BioProject ID: PRJNA1057507. Both ChIP samples demonstrated excellent read quality, with Q20 and Q30 reads exceeding 95.68% and 92.19%, respectively. The GC content in these ChIP samples was found to be 35.35%. Perfect mapping rates against the C. albicans genome were observed to be 79.28% for IP_HTA_6 and 89.24% for IP_HTA_9. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the reads revealed clear differentiation between the ChIP sample (IP_HTA6) and the un-IP treated sample (In_HTA6) in Figure S1a. However, the distinction between IP_HTA9 and In_HTA9 was less evident in the PCA plot (Figure S1a). For subsequent analyses, the HTA6 dataset was mainly utilized for GO analysis, KEGG and other analyses. It is noteworthy that a significant proportion (75%) of the total peak callings in HTA6 ranged from 240 to 450 bp in length, as depicted in Figure S1b.

Distribution of enriched peaks indicates an active involvement of Hfl1p in regulating transcription and gene expression

In HTA6 strain, a total of 166 peaks were identified, while only 32 peaks were detected in the HTA9 strain. Enriched peaks were found to be distributed across 8 chromosomes (chr1 to chr7, and chR) as well as the mitochondrial DNA in both HTA6 and HTA9 strains, as illustrated in Figure 2a. The binding regions of IP_HTA peaks were primarily located in promoters, accounting for 70.14% of the peaks, followed by intergenic areas, which represented 26.24% of the peaks (Figures 2b,c). Additionally, a small proportion of peaks (3.62%) was identified within exon areas, with a notable concentration observed in mtDNA.

Dual roles of Hfl1p: regulating energy metabolism and filamentation

The annotations of 155 genes, combined from HTA6 and HTA9 samples, are listed in Table S3, with the yellow-highlighted genes indicating those detected in both HTA6 and HTA9. By employing GO annotation (Figure S1c) and KEGG analysis (Figure 3a), these genes in biological processes (BP) and annotated pathways are functionally linked to filamentation, stress responses to drugs and high pH, as well as respiration, TCA, glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and glyoxylate pathways as anticipated.

Following manual validation of protein ortholog functions in other fungal species, we found that 43 genes (27.7%) still have unknown functions (Table S3), with most of them lacking orthologs in S. cerevisiae. Upon further categorization, 18 transcription factors and 17 transporters were identified as peaks in IP-HTA, as shown in Figure 3b. Transcription factors under Hfl1p regulation include 7 well-known filamentation activators (TEC1, EFG1, NRG2, TCC1, RHA1, pH regulator RIM101) and repressor CUP9 [19], white-opaque regulators WOR3 and WOR4, and energy regulator MSN4 and HAP41.

Consistent with filamentation repressors, a few FGR6-related genes and the adhesin ALS1 gene were repeatedly detected in both HTA6 and HTA9, primarily at intergenic areas. Additionally, other adhesin-like genes PGA59, mannoprotein gene MP65, and α-1,2-mannosyltransferase gene MNN24 were positively detected. The transporter targets were classified into two types: uptake-type transporters, involved in importing hexose, ammonium, and sulphite, and efflux-type transporters that are associated with drug resistance. The latter includes ABC-family pump CDR1, MDR-family pump FLU1, and plasma membrane P2-type H+-ATPase PMA1. These findings shed light on the diverse regulatory functions of Hfl1p, impacting various cellular processes and responses in C. albicans.

Hfl1p-interacting transcription units of mtDNA

C. albicans mtDNA encodes 14 protein-coding genes, 2 ribosomal RNA genes, and 24 tRNA genes. Unlike nuclear-encoded genes, the transcription of these genes (Figure 4a) is achieved through the generation of eight polycistronic transcription units (TU1-TU8) [20]. In the HTA6 sample, two distinct peaks were observed on the mtDNA genome, displaying robust signals with 1.8–2.0-fold higher intensity compared to the In-HTA6 sample. The initial peak spans a length of 5447 nt, encompassing positions 75 nt to 5521 nt (green bar, Figure 4b), with its apex positioned at 3667 nt, corresponding to the COX2 exon within TU1 [20]. Although this peak was originally situated in the promoter region of CaalfMt18 (tRNA-mtAla)/CaalfMp02 (NAD6)/CaalfMp03 (NAD1), the default program assigned tRNA-mtAla as a target gene. The second peak, detected by the default program, covers 21,214 nt (green bar, Figure 4c) within TU5, starting from 12,350 to 33,623 nt, with an absolute summit at 30,686 nt (black arrow). CaalfMp10 (NAD3) was identified as the regulated target in this peak.

In the HTA9 sample, four distinct peaks emerged on the mtDNA, aligning with CaalfMt18 (tRNA-mtAla) within TU1, CaalfMt30 (tRNA-mtArg1) within TU5, CaalfMt35 (tRNA-mtMet1) within TU6, and CaalfMt36 (tRNA-mtGln) within TU7 (as depicted in Figure S2). In comparison to the HTA6 dataset (illustrated in Figure 4b), discernible signals are apparent at positions 19,121 nt and 27,840 nt (indicated by the blue circles), while the signals at the other two peak regions are relatively weaker. Specifically, the 19,121 nt position corresponds to the 3’-end of CaalfMp08 (COX1), immediately followed by the promoter region governing 5 tRNA genes (tRNA-mtArg1, tRNA-mtVal, tRNA-mtTrp, tRNA-mtPhe, and tRNA-mtArg2), along with the bicistronic entity CaalfMp09 (NAD3)/CaalfMp10 (NAD2).

Collectively, the ChIP-seq data obtained from two distinct biological samples provide compelling evidence that mtDNA serves as a notable target of the transcription factor Hfl1p, notably impacting genes within TU1 and TU5. This discovery underscores the potential regulatory influence of Hfl1p in shaping mitochondrial gene expression, thereby implying its contribution to mitochondrial functions and metabolic processes.

Characteristics of motifs associated with Hfl1p

The potential Transcription Factor Binding Sites (TFBSs) of Hfl1p were screened using HOMER software, leading to the identification of 38 motifs in all potentially targeted genes via Jaspar motif scanning (Tables 1 & S4). In order to enhance our comprehension of Hfl1p’s DNA-binding motifs, we conducted an analysis of the distribution of these 38 motifs using the complete mtDNA sequence as a reference template. Notably, we observed two prominent motif-enriched regions, situated upstream of tRNA-mtAla/COX2 within TU1, and NAD3 within TU5. These regions present a marked contrast to the motif-sparse regions observed elsewhere in the mtDNA. The initial motif cluster corresponds to distal motif 32 (M32) at approximately −1.5 kb, along with a proximal grouping of M10-M25-M10 at around −160 bp preceding the COX2 start code (depicted in Figure 5). The second motif cluster is situated within the exon of COX1, which functions as the promoter for 5 tRNA genes, NAD2, and NAD3. Within this cluster, M32 is positioned at approximately −1.27 kb or −1.7 kb upstream of the NAD2 or NAD3 start code, respectively (Figure 5). Interestingly, several motifs, including M32, M5, M35, and M3, are shared between both promoters.

Similarly, M3 motifs are predominantly located on the antisense strand, and a smaller motif cluster featuring two adjacent M3 motifs and one M25 motif is situated within the Inverted Repeat (IR) region.

The six common motifs outlined in Table 1 (M5, M32, M35, M3, M25, and M15) that are present in the two distinct mtDNA regions were subsequently scrutinized within other genes encoded by nuclear DNA. Notably, M5 emerged as the most prevalent motif, appearing in 59 genes associated with carbon metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, hyphae development, and efflux transporters. In some gene promoters, such as CYC1, TCC1, and CDR1, the M5 sequence was even repeated three times. The second common motif, M35, has been reported in genes regulated by Mac1, a copper-fist transcription factor involved in copper transporters [21]. Among the 38 motifs, M32 obtained the highest prediction score (0.869) but was found to be less frequently distributed in Hfl1p-interacting genes. While M3, M25, and M15 were also detected in gene promoters related to glycolysis, respiration, CDR1, and morphological regulators, their occurrences were notably lower when compared to the prevalent distribution of M5 and M35 motifs.

Upon comparing these six motifs for any common patterns, a consensus sequence appeared to be shared between M5 and M32 (Table 1). Specifically, the TACAYAATTA sequence in M5 resembles the target sequence of a Drosophila eve/dmmpmm protein, which is crucial for the development of both odd- and even-numbered parasegments [22]. Motif 32 (TATTTACTTA) is reported to be a binding site of forkhead box FOXM1, a master regulator controlling cell cycle-dependent gene expression [23]. Based on their popularity in HTA6-IP target genes and high predicted scores, we hypothesize that TAXXTAATTA might be the best candidate consensus sequence for Hfl1p binding. On the other hand, six motifs out of the 38 motifs were exclusively shared by a group of FGR genes (Table S4), namely Smad4, Klf7, Arf27, Rbm5, Med-1, and abd-A/dmmpmm (fly). It is highly unlikely that these motifs are specific to Hfl1p binding.

The roles of Hfl1p in mitochondrial function are verified through both proteomic and phenotypic analyses

The proteomic data obtained from hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mitochondria revealed significant reductions in the abundance of 9 Complex I (CI) subunits including mtDNA-encoded Nad3, Nad4, Nad5, and Nad1, and CI regulator Goa1, compared to the wild-type (WT) strain (Figure 6a). The decreased levels ranging from 1.33-fold (Nuo2p) to 1.74-fold (Goa1p). Moreover, reductions were observed in mitochondrial tRNA synthases, rRNA proteins, and enzymes involved in tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle input, such as Kgd1p, Mdh1-3p, Sdh7p, Cat2p, and Rgt1p (Figure 6a). Additionally, several transporters for divalent cations, β-oxidation/peroxins/catalase (Cat1p), and synthesis of ergosterol, sphingolipids, pyrimidines, and cell wall polymers were down-regulated. In response to these down-regulated mitochondrial functions, compensatory mechanisms led to the upregulation of proteins involved in non-glucose utilization, CIV of ETC and Tims (translocates proteins produced from nuclear DNA into mitochondrial matrix for use in oxidative phosphorylation) in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant (Figure 6a).

In consistence with proteomic data, CI content in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant was decreased on blue native-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE) (Figure 6b). Under high glucose concentration (2%), the CI content in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant was only 43% of the WT level, and increased to 63% when the culture had been switched from 0.5% to 2% glucose. Remarkably, in YP medium with glycerol, the difference of CI contents became less than 10% between mutant and WT. Meanwhile, the densities of Complex III (CIII) and Complex V (CV) were less affected under these conditions, which are consistent with high yields of proteins for CIV assembly in proteomic data.

To further validate its involvement in mitochondrial function, we investigated the localization of Hfl1p within mitochondria using MitoTracker staining in conjunction with fluorescent microscopy. As shown in Figure 6c, the colocalization of fluorescent-tagged Hfl1p with MitoTracker in C. albicans cells was observed, in contrast to the negative overlay signals in the WT. The overlay of fluorescence signals confirms the physical involvement of Hfl1p within the mitochondria.

The roles of Hfl1p on morphology and drug efflux pump: proteomic and phenotypic evidence

In glucose-rich medium, the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant exhibited pseudohyphal growth, accompanied by the upregulation of filamentous regulators Tac1p, Tcc1p, and the CO2-dependent hyphal transcription factor Sfl2p. Conversely, the yeast-form regulator Cgr1p was found to be less produced in the mutant. Interestingly, the proteomic analysis showed no significant changes in the levels of Efg1p and Fgr1p, suggesting that Hfl1p primarily regulates hyphal formation by suppressing transcription factors involved in yeast-hyphal conversion. Nevertheless, the decrease in glucose oxidation in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant resulted in an avirulent phenotype in a disseminated murine model (Figure 7a), highlighting the role of Hfl1p in fungal pathogenicity.

The roles of Hfl1p on virulence and drug efflux pumps in C. albicans. (a) Reduced virulence of the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant in a disseminated murine model when compared to the normal virulence of WT and the dpb4∆/dpb4∆ mutant. Survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism software and compared using the log-rank (mantel-cox) test to determine statistical significance between the groups. A p-value of <0.05 was observed between the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant and WT. (b) The hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant displays a delayed glucose-induced rhodamine 6G efflux, indicative of a downregulation of multidrug resistance efflux transporters. This reduced transporter activity contributes to the heightened susceptibility to azoles, consistent with findings from previous studies.

Previous research indicated that the deletion of HFL1 in C. albicans resulted in increased susceptibility to azoles [14]. Despite the upregulation of Hsp12p and Tac1p (a Cdr1/Cdr2 activator), the levels of Cdr1 and Cdr2 proteins were not elevated and were even slightly decreased in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant. Furthermore, DHA-type pumps, including Ifu5p, Qdr1p, Pdr6p, and the spermine transporter Tpo3p [24], were significantly less produced in the mutant, which was also corroborated by the reduced R6G efflux. Figure 7b illustrates that while glucose addition induced quick R6G extracellular release peaks 1 and 2 in the wild-type (WT) strain at 10 and 25 min, respectively, the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant exhibited a delayed and combined peak pattern, lacking peak 1. This down-regulation of efflux pumps, particularly the DHA-type pumps, resulted in higher intracellular drug concentrations in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant, making fungal cells more susceptible to drug-induced killing, especially in the context of compromised stress responses involving Pkc1p, calcineurin pathway, Fkh2p, and Hsp70p in proteomic view.

Discussion

The combination of ChIP data, proteomic analyses, and phenotypic evaluations in this study provides robust evidence underscoring the pivotal regulatory roles played by Hfl1p in maintaining the functional integrity of mitochondrial DNA-encoded CI (Complex I) subunits and ensuring proper tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle functionality. Our findings suggest that Hfl1p in C. albicans functions as a versatile regulatory factor, operating either independently or in conjunction with other transcription factors to facilitate transcriptional activation, particularly concerning respiration/glycolysis and morphogenesis (Figure 8).

Roles of Hfl1p in coordinating carbon metabolism and morphogenesis regulation in C. albicans. 1). Nutrient-dependent regulation: under conditions of abundant nutrients, particularly high glucose concentrations, Hfl1p assumes a pivotal role in orchestrating cellular responses. 2) Hfl1p-mediated gene regulation: Hfl1p exerts its regulatory influence by interacting with genes encoded by both nuclear and mitochondrial DNA, recognizing a conserved DNA motif (TAXXTAATTA). 3) Hfl1p directly interacts with specific genes (green circles) associated with carbon metabolism, mitochondrial respiration, and a group of regulators governing filamentous growth. In certain cases, Hfl1p’s impact is mediated indirectly through a network of transcription factors (orange rectangle box), reflected by a collection of transcription factors for filamentous growth and morphogenesis: EFG1, TEC1, TCC1, RIM101, WOR3, WOR4, and CUP9. The function outcomes of Hfl1p include a enhanced glycolysis and respiration, leading to heightened energy production; an increased susceptibility to fluconazole (FCZ), a crucial antifungal drug and repressed filamentation growth, preserving yeast-like forms.

The analysis of the common motifs associated with Hfl1p reveals interesting patterns and potential consensus sequences (TAXXTAATTA) for Hfl1p binding. The prevalence of certain motifs in specific gene sets further suggests their functional relevance and association with Hfl1p-mediated gene regulation. However, further experimental validation is needed.

In C. albicans, Hfl1p is designated as a Hap5-like protein involved in energy metabolism; however, its regulatory mode appears to operate independently of the Hap complex (Hap2/3/5) that tightly governs mitochondrial respiration in S. cerevisiae [12]. Notably, Hap5 itself is elevated by 1.4-fold in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant. Structurally, Hfl1 lacks domain 1—a crucial element for the recruitment of Hap4 homologs in S. cerevisiae—yet it shares similarities with the mammalian Hap5 counterpart, NF-YC. In mammalian cells, the NF-Y system collaborates synergistically with other highly regulated activators to govern gene expression, which likely mirrors Hfl1p’s DNA binding mechanism in C. albicans for the regulation of energy-related gene expressions.

Absence of CI and Hfl1p in S. cerevisiae underscores the crucial role of this energy regulation pathway in the pathogenesis of C. albicans. The regulatory role of Hfl1p on the expression of mtDNA CI subunits was an unexpected discovery, yet it explains the predominantly affected complex CI assembly in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant. The mitochondrial genome of C. albicans encodes a total of 14 proteins, among which seven are CI subunits (Nads). Our investigation reveals the presence of two distinct mitochondrial peaks in the Hfl1p-ChIP analysis, aligning precisely with TU1 and TU5 [20]. These transcription units encompass tRNA(A)-NAD6-NAD1 and a bicistronic configuration of NAD2-NAD3, respectively. Concurrently, two motif clusters are situated precisely within the promoter regions of TU1 and TU5. Furthermore, the detected decreases in the protein levels of Nad1 and Nad3 in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant. These findings collectively emphasize the pivotal regulatory function of Hfl1p in governing the regulation of mtDNA-encoded NADs, with specific emphasis on NAD3.

On the other hand, the heightened presence of cytochrome c (Cyc1) alongside increased levels of proteins involved in CIV assembly, the mitochondrial membrane protein transfer system (Tims), Sod1, and enzymes for non-glucose utilization (Figure 6a) in the mutant suggests a compensatory response to the perturbed mitochondria resulting from the deletion of HFL1. This compensatory reaction is likely facilitated by a spectrum of stress regulators, exemplified by a significant upregulated Sho1p/Hog1p, and heat-shock proteins observed in hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant at the protein level. At the same time, the Pkc1, the Calcineurin, and Tor1 pathways are downregulated in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant, suggesting the positive regulation roles of Hfl1p in these signal pathways as well.

Morphologically, our early study revealed that none of the Hap complex mutants (hap2∆, hap3∆, and hap5∆) in C. albicans exhibited growth defects in glycerol medium, a phenotype distinctively observed in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant [11]. In line with this, Singh et al. emphasized the Hap complex’s pivotal role in maintaining iron homoeostasis during both in vitro and in vivo growth stages in C. albicans [25]. Notably, a selection of genes associated with iron uptake, along with Cyc1 (cytochrome c) and Hap41, were all positively identified within the Hfl1p-ChIP peaks. These disparities serve to further underscore the inherent differences between Hfl1p and the Hap complex.

The hyphal form is frequently encountered at sites of tissue invasion, and its failure can significantly diminish the virulence of C. albicans [26]. Regarding morphogenesis, our ChIP-seq data firmly establish Hfl1p as a master regulator of C. albicans morphogenesis. Hfl1p-TAP exhibits direct physical interactions with the promoters of key regulatory genes including Efg1, Cph1, Tec1, and Rim101. Notably, the cAMP-regulated Efg1 transcription factor is pivotal across various growth conditions [27], while the pheromone response MAP kinase (CEK1/2)-regulated Cph1 and Tec1 operate under more specific circumstances [28,29]. Additionally, Rim101, the third transcription factor, is activated by alkaline pH. Our findings also include Nrg2 and Tcc1, the DNA-binding partners of the filamentous repressor Tup1 [30,31], within the Hfl1p–TAP interactions.

A subset of Filamentation Growth Regulators (FGRs) [32], along with hyphae-associated genes like ALS1 and MP65, were also identified in the Hfl1p-ChIP analysis. These FGRs spans intergenic regions on chromosomes 1, 2, 4, 6, 7, and R. However, the annotation of FGR proteins remains limited. Earlier studies have linked the DNA binding pattern of universal morphogenesis regulators, such as the repressor Rfg1 and the inducers Efg1/Cph1, with the ALS1 promoter [33]. Notably, the adhesin ALS family and the mannan protein MP65 play pivotal roles in cell face-mediated hyphal morphogenesis. These proteins serve as major antigens of C. albicans and elicit a host cell-mediated immune response [34]. Furthermore, our data show that Hfl1p-TAP targets the opaque activator Wor3, Wor4 transcription factors, and the opaque-specific Ops4. Remarkably, Wor3 is significantly upregulated by 9-fold at the transcriptional level in the hfl1 mutant. The biphasic “white-opaque” switch, closely tied to the virulence of C. albicans [1], is intricately linked to Efg1 [35] and Tup1, acting as a prerequisite for mating conditions [36]. Collectively, the pseudohyphal growth observed in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant is likely an outcome of the intricate interplay between these positive and negative transcription factors, which are further coordinated with various pathways under distinct stress conditions.

In addition to carbon metabolism and filamentation, ChIP analysis detected the involvement of CDR1, IFU1, and PMA1, which contribute to drug efflux. Activation of multidrug efflux pumps such as Cdr1/Cdr2 (ATP- binding cassette pumps), Mdr1 (major facilitator superfamily), or DHA (drug + antiporter) pumps is known to confer antifungal drug resistance in C. albicans [37]. Other genes, including those involved in phospholipid synthesis and PIL1 [38], a component of the eisosome that organizes enzymes related to cell wall synthesis, also emerge from the ChIP-detected list. Finally, the ribosomal DNA repeat RDN5, translation elongation factor 1-alpha (TEF2) aligns well with the upregulated Tor1 pathway and proteins associated with ribosomal biogenesis in the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant.

While the correlation between the metabolic state of the cell and its morphological form has received relatively limited attention, traditional notions have suggested a link between the yeast form of C. albicans and mitochondrial respiration, while the hyphal form is associated with cellular fermentation in vitro [39,40]. In simpler terms, fungal cells exhibiting a robust mitochondrial respiratory pathway are more likely to adopt the yeast form, whereas the hyphal form is favoured when cellular respiration is compromised or in the presence of unfavourable carbon sources. This paradigm appears consistent with the behaviour of Hfl1p. However, our own research, as well as findings from other studies focusing on mitochondrial mutants, presents contrasting examples. For instance, the disruption of GOA1 and NDH51—proteins necessary for complex I assembly and core subunits of the electron transport chain’s complex I – leads to impaired germination [7,41]. Similar outcomes are observed in the pdx1Δ mutant (associated with mitochondrial dehydrogenase complex X) and in the wild-type strain when subjected to a complex I inhibitor challenge. These findings underscore the complex interplay between mitochondrial function and morphological transitions in C. albicans, challenging traditional paradigms and emphasizing the need for further investigation into the intricate regulatory mechanisms governing fungal morphogenesis and metabolism.

Conclusions

This study delves into the intricate regulatory landscape of Hfl1p in C. albicans, illuminating its multifaceted roles in coupling mitochondrial function, energy metabolism, and morphogenesis. Through a comprehensive analysis by ChIP data, proteomic insights, and phenotypic assessments, we unveil Hfl1p as a pivotal regulator of crucial cellular processes, evident in its direct control over mitochondrial DNA-encoded CI subunits and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. The dynamic interplay between Hfl1p and other transcription factors, along with unforeseen connections between Hfl1p and essential elements such as iron homoeostasis, filamentation, drug efflux, and ribosomal biogenesis provides a comprehensive portrayal of its regulatory scope. Moreover, the findings presented herein lay the foundation for future investigations aimed at deciphering the complex regulatory networks that govern C. albicans’ virulence and adaptation, contributing to advancements in antifungal strategies and therapeutic interventions.

Methods

Strains of C. albicans, growth media, and murine models of disseminated murine candidiasis

The mitochondrial phenotypes of null mutant (∆hfl1/∆hfl1) utilized in the present investigation have been characterized in a previous study [11]. Other strains used included SN250 used as wild-type (WT) and SN152 [42] that was used to construct heterozygous strain HFL11 (HFL1/∆hfl1) and HTA strain (HFL1-TAP/∆hfl1) in this study. All strains were grown at 30°C in YPD (yeast extract peptone dextrose) with 2% glucose, or YP medium supplemented with 2% glycerol (YPG) as carbon sources.

Animals and ethics statement

All the animal experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE Guidelines 2.0. All experimental protocols applied in this study were approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Committee of Institute of Dermatology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CAMS).

Experimental design

A mouse model of disseminated candidiasis was used to measure the virulence [7] following the guidelines of the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments. All the 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice were obtained from Yangzhou University (China). The overnight growth of each strain in YPD broth was collected and washed in 0.2% Tween 20 PBS. The filamentous growth of ∆hfl1/∆hfl1 was fragmented to 10 to 50-micron segments with a tissue grinder with a serrated tip plunger (Fisher Scientific) in PBS [43]. Each mouse was injected with 1 × 106 yeast cells or a fragmented mycelium preparation with 0.6 -absorbance at OD600 inoculum via the lateral tail vein. Groups of 10 Mice were randomly assigned to each group, given food and water ad libitum and were examined daily for those moribund. Survival rates were calculated and plotted into Kaplan–Meier survival charts using a GraphPad program.

Blue native polyacrylamide gel (BN-PAGE) electrophoresis

Mitochondrial preparations were made from harvested cells by methods described previously [2]. Concentrated mitochondrial protein preparations were next solubilized in 10% DDM (n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside) prior to loading onto a 4% to 16% gradient BN-PAGE Invitrogen gel (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). An aliquot of 20 μl of each protein sample (~100–150 μg of protein) was mixed with Novex™ Tris-Glycine Native Sample Buffer (2X) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and electrophoresis was performed in an X-Cell SureLock mini-cell system (Invitrogen) with 200 ml of cathode buffer in the upper (inner) buffer chamber and 150 ml of anode buffer in the lower (outer) buffer chamber. The cathode buffer contained 0.02% Serva Blue G-250, supplemented with 0.02% DDM, and the anode buffer consisted of 50 mm bis-Tris, pH 7.0. Electrophoresis was initially conducted at 65 V for 1 h, followed by an overnight increment to 120 V at 4°C. The intensity of blue bands was measured using ImageJ software [44,45] (a Java-based image processing program developed at the National Institutes of Health and the Laboratory for Optical and Computational Instrumentation (LOCI, University of Wisconsin).

Measurement of glucose-induced R6G efflux

As prescribed previously [46], yeast cells (1 × 108 cells/mL), cultivated in 100 mL of YPD broth and incubated at 37°C for 4 h, were harvested. They were then suspended in a 50 mL glucose-free PBS buffer at 37°C for 1 h while being agitated (at 200 rpm). After incubation of the cells with 10 µM of Rhodamine 6 G (Sigma Labs, Santa Fe, NM) for 25 min, a cell suspension of 10 mL was collected by centrifugation at 5000 × g for 5 min. The pellets were reconstituted in PBS supplemented with 1 mole of glucose and maintained at 37°C on a shaker. At 5-min intervals, samples of 500 µL each were extracted and subsequently centrifuged at 9000 g for 2 min. A 200 µL aliquot of the supernatant was collected, and the absorption was measured at 515 nm using a Triad spectrophotometer (Dynex Technologies, Chantilly, VA). The extracellular R6G concentration in each sample was then calculated by referencing a standard curve established with various R6G concentrations.

Construction of heterozygous HFL1/∆hfl1

A PCR-based fusion method targeting wild-type Candida albicans genomic DNA was performed to delete one allele of the HFL1 gene, following a previously described protocol with some modifications [42]. The fusion PCR strategy is shown in Figure 1a together with the primer sequences which can be accessed in Supplement Table S1. The 5’ fragment (305 bp) was amplified using primer 1 and primer 3, positioned 35 bp upstream of the ATG start codon. The 3’ fragment, spanning 288 bp and situated 55 bp downstream of the TAA stop codon, was amplified with primer 4 and primer 6. Simultaneously, the central marker fragment was amplified employing primer 2 and primer 5, utilizing pSN40 plasmid DNA containing CmLEU2 as a template. The ultimate fusion of the 5’-fragment, the LEU2 marker, and the 3’-fragment was achieved through amplification using primer 1 and primer 6, resulting in a ~ 2100 bp product suitable for transformation. Transformation was carried out by lithium method [47] in SN152 (Arg−, Leu−, His−). Leu+ transformants were selected on yeast nitrogen base-minimal medium lacking leucine but containing arginine and histidine. The integration of the transforming DNA was confirmed by PCR analysis of transformants with 2 pairs of primers Check 7/Check 8 and Check 9/Check10.

Construction of HFL1-TAP-ARG4 strain

Employing a modified version of the PCR-TAP-tagging method originally developed by Lavoie et al. [15], a HFL1-TAP1-ARG4 cassette was generated using the pAF-TAP-ARG4 vector (FJ160458.1). The PCR reactions were carried out with two primers: Hfl-TAP-F, which encompassed a 100 bp segment of the HFL1 open reading frame (ORF) immediately before the stop codon TAG, and a 20 bp 5’ flank region of TAP; and a second primer consisting of Hfl-TAP-R, corresponding to a 100 bp sequence downstream of the TAA stop codon and a 20 bp 3’ flank region of ARG4, as shown in Figure 1a.

Subsequently, the heterozygous strain HFL11–1 (Figure 1b) was employed as the host for transforming the 3480 bp HFL1-TAP1-ARG4 cassette. Before the transformation step, the PCR products from 8 separate amplifications were purified using PCR purification columns from Qiagen. Transformants exhibiting the Arg+His−Leu+ phenotype were selected for further confirmation through PCR analysis. The successful integration of the cassette into the HFL1 locus was verified using primers Hfl1-C-11 and Hfl1-C-12, as detailed in Supplement Table S1.

Western blotting confirmation

Verification of HFL1-TAP1 expression was carried out using a mouse anti-TAP-Tag monoclonal antibody (ABclonal, Woburn, MA). Fungal strains were cultivated overnight and subsequently harvested, followed by a single wash with PBS. Whole-protein extraction was next performed in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube, achieved by grinding cells using glass beads and a turbulence-mixing system adapted onto a vortex machine (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The grinding process was repeated for six cycles, involving 1-min intermittent periods of shaking followed by 2 min of cessation after which the cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min, all conducted at 4°C. Subsequently, the protein suspension was separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE gel and next transferred onto a PVDF membrane using an Invitrogen iBlot mini gel transfer device (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). The primary antibody was prepared in TBS with 5% skim milk powder at a dilution of 1:5000 and an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody IgG (H+L) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was employed at a dilution of 1:10000, in compliance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. The resulting blot was visualized Western Blotting Luminol Reagent-sc2048 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA).

Hfl1p-MitoTracker colocalization

A fresh culture of C. albicans was obtained by inoculating a single clone in a 10 mL centrifuge tube containing 2 mL of YPD liquid medium and shake vigorously overnight at 30°C. Collected cells by centrifugation were washed with PBS once, resuspended in 1 mL of PBS at 1 × 108 cells/mL, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Subsequent washing steps with PBS were performed three times for 5 min each. The cells were then permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 for 20 min followed by another three washes with PBS. Blocking with QuickBlock™ Immunostaining Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, P0260) was carried out at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, Mouse anti-TAP-Tag mAb (ABclonal, AE021) diluted at 1:1000 with QuickBlock™ Immunostaining Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime, P0262) was added and incubated overnight at 4°C, with gentle shaking at 50 rpm. Following washing steps, the secondary antibody (DyLight 488, Goat Anti-Mouse IgG, Abbkine, A23210) diluted at 1:2000 with SignalUp™ Immunostaining Secondary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime, P0278) was added and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. After washing, cells were incubated with Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, C1035) at a 1:1000 dilution at room temperature for 30 min, followed by washing with PBS three times. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with DAPI (Abbkine, BMD0063) at a 1:1000 dilution at room temperature for 20 min. After final washing steps, 100 μL of stained cells were placed in a confocal dish (NEST 801,001), covered with a coverslip (biosharp, BS-18-RC), and observed using a confocal laser microscope with excitation wavelengths of 405 nm, 488 nm, and 543 nm. Pictures were taken with a 60× objective lens.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation of DNA fragments bound to TAP-Tag mAb

Two strains of HTA (HFL1-TAP1-ARG4), i.e. HTA6 and HTA9 were processed by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments using the same mouse anti -TAP-Tag mAb employed in the Western blotting analysis. The ChIP methodology followed the protocol described by Lavoie, et al. [15]. Briefly, the procedure consisted of cell culture until reaching an optical density of 0.6 in 50 mL YPD medium. Subsequently, crosslinking of cells was accomplished using 1% formaldehyde for a duration of 10 minutes at room temperature, employing gentle agitation followed by quenching of the cells by the addition of 0.125 M glycine for 5 min. After two rounds of cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) washes containing protease inhibitors, the fungal cells were lysed utilizing glass beads in a low salt buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Following cell lysis, the lysates were subjected to sonication on ice-inducing fragmentation of the chromatin into fragments sized between 200 and 500 bp. After eliminating cellular debris, the chromatin fragments were captured via the addition of mouse anti-TAP-Tag monoclonal antibody, using protein A/G magnetic beads.

The subsequent steps involved sequential washes of the beads utilizing buffers of varying salt concentrations, specifically low salt buffer, high salt buffer, lithium chloride buffer (LiCl), and Tris-EDTA (TE) buffer. The isolated chromatins were subsequently eluted from the beads using a buffer containing sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS) and sodium chloride (NaCl), and then subjected to overnight heating at 65°C to reverse the crosslinking present, facilitating the release of DNA fragments which were then purified using a DNA purification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA).

ChIP-seq analysis of DNA fragments using illumina NovaSeq platform and GO annotation

The DNA libraries were next constructed from two groups of cell populations identified on the basis of prior CHIP treatment. These included: 1) cells that had undergone a ChIP procedure, i.e. the IP sample, IP, and 2) cells from the same testing strain that had not undergone ChIP treatment, i.e. the input sample, In. Each DNA library was then prepared by end-repairing, adding an A-tail and ligating adapters for PCR amplification. Subsequently, the DNA libraries underwent quality assessment through molecular and genomic metrics utilizing a bioanalyzer following which, the libraries were subjected to sequencing using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencer, following manufacturer’s instructions. The NovaSeq Control Software (NVCS) automatically transfers base calling to raw reads (Fastq). The quality of sequencing library was further assessed by examining fastq files with FastQC (version 0.11.5). Sequencing adaptors were removed using Trimmomatic (version 0.36) and bases with low quality scores (Q < 20) and short reads (length <20) were removed. Q = −10log10E, where E is an estimate of the probability of error. Q20 and Q30 represent error rates of 1 in 100 or 1 in 1000 bp sequencing reads, respectively.

The ChIP-Seq reads were then mapped to Candida albicans genome SC5314 ID 21 (assembly ASM18296v3) using BWA-MEM (version 0.7.15-r1140). The “view” function of samtools (version 1.6) was applied to eliminate duplicates and reads with low quality score below Q20. Subsequently, read-enriched regions, also known as peaks, were pinpointed using MACS2 (version 2.1 January 20160309), and bedgraph files were generated using the default settings through the utility “pileup.” To standardize the number of reads from IP and In samples in the bedgraphs, they were adjusted using a reference-derived normalization factor, resulting in reference-adjusted reads per million (RRPM). Significantly enriched genomic regions, determined by a q value of less than 0.05 for ChIP signal, were identified by subtracting the signal from the input sample out of the signal from the IP sample, accomplished through a peak calling algorithm. For potential regulatory region associations with nearby genes, peak annotation was performed using a tool such as ChIPseeker or GREAT. The resulting bigwig files were then uploaded to JBrowse (version 1.12.1) for the purpose of visualizing the data. To gain insights into the functions of the identified genes, a functional enrichment analysis was carried out to identify enriched Gene Ontology (GO) terms and biological pathways using the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) approach.

DNA motifs detection

DNA sequence motifs within the ChIP-seq peaks were pinpointed through de novo motif discovery tools, specifically HOMER (Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment). These motifs were discerned using diverse algorithms, and their statistical significance was gauged by comparing them to motifs in the Jaspar database. Two distinct methodologies were employed to unveil both novel motifs and those aligned with established ones. The outcomes from HOMER and the pre-existing motifs represent two contrasting strategies for the recognition and delineation of DNA or protein sequence motifs.

Mass spectrophotometric (MS) profiling of mitochondrial proteins

Mitochondria were isolated according to the protocol previously described [48]. Subsequently, mass spectrophotometric analysis was conducted in accordance with the same protocol. Prior to mass spectrophotometric analysis, the quality of mitochondrial preparation isolated from various strains was assessed using anti-HSP60 antibody (mitochondria), anti-cytochrome C (cytoplasm and mitochondria), and anti-α-tubulin (cytoplasm) in a primary western immunoblot experiment. The samples not containing α-tubulin were used for MS analysis. To evaluate the technical reproducibility, a total of 3 runs of the hfl1∆/hfl1∆ mutant were compared to those of WT to assess the correlation of peptide intensity among triplicate samples.
