Prevalence and predictors of burnout in critical care health workers in palestine
Authors: Qaaqour Ahmad, Haddara Wael, Hegazy Ahmed F, Jobran Afnan, Almassri Nasrallah, Gucciardo Dorotea, Thomson Benjamin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstablish the prevalence and risk factors of burnout in critical care health workers in Palestine. Two surveys and a thematic analysis. The final Survey combined results of thematic analysis with Maslach Burnout Inventory. Health workers from critical care units in Palestine. Two cross-sectional surveys. Survey 1 established perceived risk factors of burnout. A thematic analysis was completed on Survey 1, to identify unique risk factors to be added to the Maslach burnout survey. The combination of the standard Maslach burnout survey, and unique risk factors identified in survey 1, made up survey 2. There were 141 survey respondents. Burnout levels were high in each of the three domains of the Maslach Burnout survey (occupational exhaustion 59.0%, depersonalization and loss of empathy 55.0, low level of personal accomplishment 58.0%). Higher scores on occupational exhaustion domain were found in respondents with higher education level (H statistic 12.582, p = 0.014), in physicians rather than nurses or other job category (H statistic 7.689, p = 0.021), in those respondents who felt they were not respected at work (H statistic 21.894, p = 0.001), those who felt burned out due to the economic situation in Palestine (H-statistic 12.977, p = 0.043), those who felt salary was too low (H-statistic 19.020, p = 0.004), those who felt stressed because of the high patient acuity in the intensive care unit (H-statistic 21.564, p = 0.002) and those who felt unsupported by colleagues at their work (H-statistic 28.259, p = 0.0001). Higher scores of burnout on the depersonalization and loss of empathy domain were found with higher education (H statistic 12.270, p = 0.015), in physicians rather than nurses or other job category (H statistic 8.406, p = 0.015), respondents who did not feel respected at work (H-statistic 22.625, p = 0.001), and those who felt unsupported by work colleagues (H-statistic 23.389, p = 0.0001). Higher scores on burnout on the personal accomplishment domain were found with never-married marital status (H statistic 7.107, p = 0.008), and with respondents who felt unsupported by work colleagues (H-statistic 12.959, p = 0.044). Health workers in critical care units in Palestine have high rates of burnout. Several factors unique to Palestine predict burnout. Further research is required to establish efficacy of interventions to improve burnout in critical care health workers in Palestine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22093-0.Subject terms: Occupational health, Public health, Outcomes research

Introduction

Burnout is highly prevalent in health care workers in critical care settings, reported by 41% of physicians and 44% of nurses1. Contributing factors include staff shortages,2 low perceived level of administrative support,2-4 decreased sleep or increased work hours,5–8 lack of physical exercise,9 female gender,6,10,11 physician subspecialty,12 and documentation needs3,13,14. Several demographic factors have inconsistent relationship with burnout, including age,7,15–17 work experience,15–17 marital status8,17 and having children15,18,19. Burnout has detrimental impacts on staff turnover, work performance, absenteeism, and patient care,20–22 while significantly increasing health care associated costs23. It is thus essential that burnout be understood and reduced, to enhance worker and patient experience.

The standard instrument for measuring burnout, the Maslach Burnout Inventory Human Services Survey, considers three dimensions, which are emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalization, and reduction in personal and professional achievement24. The original Maslach survey was created for the service industry, with an additional version designed for education occupations24. It has also been used to evaluate burnout in humanitarian workers,25–27 pandemics,28–32 and humanitarian emergencies33. However, the use of the Maslach survey, and the assessment of burnout in conflict settings, remains poorly studied.

Gaza and the West Bank have been under occupation by Israel since 196734. The occupation has adverse impacts on Palestinians’ health and provision of health services,35 social and economic opportunities,36 and ability to achieve self-determination34. Furthermore, it leads to delays travelling through military checkpoints and limits access to medical education37. While burnout in critical care health setting is well studied outside Palestine,1,3 there remains a paucity of literature assessing prevalence and risk factors of burnout in the unique Palestinian context.

This study had two objectives. The first objective was to establish the prevalence of burnout in critical care health workers in Palestine. The second objective was to identify risk factors for burnout in critical care health workers in the Palestine; this includes not only traditional risk factors, but also those related to the unique social and political climate of the Palestine.

Materials and methods

Design

This was a cross-sectional descriptive study with 2 surveys. A two-survey approach was chosen because standardized burnout inventories often fail to capture unique, context-specific stressors. Therefore, a preliminary qualitative survey was conducted to identify themes relevant to the Palestinian context, which were then integrated into the final quantitative survey. The study population were health workers (nurses, physicians or allied health) in 27 critical care centers, including 7 in Gaza and 20 in West Bank. Survey 1 was completed in July to August 2022, and survey 2 between September 2022 and January 2023.

Survey 1

Survey 1 (Appendix 1) evaluated which factors were thought by respondents to be associated to burnout in Palestine. Inductive thematic analysis was used, as previously described38. Three researchers (BT, AQ, WH) each separately coded the responses for themes. The goal of this process was to assure saturation of themes. All three researchers identified unique codes associated either directly or indirectly with the unique political environment in Palestine. Discussion between the three researchers reached consensus on codes that assured comprehensive capture of all themes. Saturation was achieved from a thematic perspective.

Each theme from Survey 1 was converted into a single unique theme question, with the identical 7-point Likert scale as used in the Maslach burnout inventory. For example, the theme “lack of respect” was converted to “I feel I am not respected in my work,” with answer choices being every day, several times a week, once a week, several times a month, at least once a month, at least few times a year, or never.

Survey 2

Survey 2 combined the standard Maslach burnout survey39 and the themes identified from Survey 1. Questions created from the thematic analysis from Survey 1 were called “Unique Theme Questions.” Survey 2 had two purposes. The first purpose was to establish the prevalence of burnout. The second purpose was to identify the importance not only of traditional risk factors, but also of potential risk factors identified in Survey 1, in burnout in critical care health workers in Palestine.

Survey validity

Each survey was reviewed by 5 health workers in Canada, and 5 health workers in Palestine. The English survey was translated to Arabic by a bilingual speaker who worked in health care in West Bank. Surveys were available in English or Arabic.

Face validity for each survey was established by reviewing the survey with health workers working in the critical care medicine setting in Palestine and Canada. Content validity was established by review of surveys with not only the same health workers, but also two health workers with expertise in survey research methodology. Several of the health workers were intimately familiar with the health literature on burnout research.

Survey distribution

Surveys were automated within Qualtrics XM (Toronto, Canada). Sampling was by convenience sample through social media and leadership contacts. A survey link was shared in the official Facebook pages for the Palestinian Medical Association and the Palestine Medical Council, and the WhatsApp group for the Palestine Medical Association. Researchers also contacted the leadership of critical care medicine in Gaza and the West Bank, so that the survey availability could be shared broadly.

A lecture was presented by the research team (AQ) to the staff of the largest ICU in West Bank (Al-Makassed University Hospital, Jerusalem West Bank), with a web link shared simultaneously with the largest ICU in Gaza (Al-Shifa Hospital, Gaza City). The lecture purpose was to introduce the staff to the study, and to encourage study participation.

To prevent multiple survey entries per respondent, respondents were instructed (on the survey) to complete the study only once. Furthermore, only one response was permitted per Internet Protocol (“IP”) address through the Qualtrics XM software.

Statistics

Study data was downloaded from Qualtrics XM (Toronto, Canada) into Microsoft Excel (Version 16.16.27 for Mac).

Burnout prevalence was calculated for each component of the Maslach survey (Occupational exhaustion, Personal accomplishment, Depersonalization and loss of empathy). Only those surveys with complete answers for each of the components of the Maslach survey were considered, Scores (low, moderate, high) were calculated as previously described24. The relationship between each component of the Maslach survey and demographic factors was evaluated by the Krusker-Wallis H statistic, which is a non-parametric test that does can be used for Likert data, and does not require normality of data, nor the assumption of homogeneity of group variances40.

The number of health workers in Gaza and West Bank is unknown. However, the number of health workers in each of the largest ICUs in Gaza (Al-Shifa hospital)(n = 24) and West Bank (Al-Makassed hospital)(n = 42) is known. We identified survey respondents who worked at either Al-Shifa hospital or Al-Makassed hospital so we could establish a survey response rate in those units.

Ethics

Research Ethics Board approval for this study was granted by the Al-Quds University (Jerusalem) Deanship of Scientific Research Ethics Committee (Reference number 245/REC/2022). All methods were performed in accordance to the regulations of Al-Quds University Research Ethics Committee. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s).

Survey 2- respondent characteristics

There were 141 total survey respondents, with 99 complete surveys. The average age of respondents was 32.5 years (Table 2), with good representation of females (n = 42, 34.1%). Most (n = 78, 63.4%) respondents were married. No respondents were divorced, separated or widowed, so all respondents were either married or never married. Most respondents had completed either 4 year nursing degree (n = 35, 28.9%) or Bachelor’s degree in Medicine (n = 37, 30.6%). Most respondents were either physicians (n = 68, 56.2%) or nurses (n = 48, 39.7%). There was high levels of participation from both West Bank (n = 78, 64.5%) and Gaza (n = 33, 27.3%). More respondents worked in the governmental sector (n = 66, 54.5%). The respondent cohort was well represented across all work experience durations, with 13.3% (n = 16) having over 15 years experience, and the greatest proportion in the up to 5 years work experience (n = 76, 63.3%). The response rate for Al-Shifa hospital was 54.2% (13/24), and for Al-Makassed hospital was 66.7% (28/42).

Survey 2 – relation between demographic factors with burnout

Higher scores on burnout on Maslach personal accomplishment domain were found with never-married marital status (H statistic 7.107, p = 0.008) (Table 3; Fig. 6).

Discussion

Comparison with prior studies

The prevalence of burnout in this study is approximately 15% higher than those previously reported in a systematic review of studies that quantified burnout in health workers in intensive care settings1. This study identified several unique exposures for respondents in this study. Unique political factors related to the illegal occupation, siege, military checkpoints, and economic blockade have previously been reported to impair health34–36. While these factors were commonly reported as causes of burnout by survey respondents in this study, feeling burnout out due to the economic situation in Palestine was the only political factor that predicted Maslach burnout, in the occupational exhaustion domain.

Prevalence of burnout in a variety of health care providers in Palestine has been reported, including dentists (high on 48% emotional exhaustion domain),6 critical care nurses during the COVID-19 pandemic (high burnout on 70% personal accomplishment, 59.5% depersonalization, 65% emotional exhaustion domains),41 and anesthesiologists (high on 71.2% emotional exhaustion, 59% depersonalization, 68.4% personal achievement domains)18. In medical trainees, moderate to severe burnout on the Copenhagen burnout inventory was reported in over 75% of respondents8. Therefore, the rates of severe burnout in this study (59.0% occupational exhaustion, 55.0% depersonalization, 58.0% personal accomplishment domains) are similar to those studies already described in Palestine. Differences in burnout rates with studies in Palestine may be explained by health subspecialty, study timing during the COVID-19 pandemic, level of training of survey respondents, or other unmeasured factors. Confirmation of the reasons for these differences should be a focus for future research.

Interpretation of key findings

This study collected more demographic and political factors than any previously published burnout study in Palestine, and important predictors for burnout were identified. Unique factors that were reported by survey respondents to lead to increased burnout included military checkpoints, occupation, siege, and the economic situation. However, only the economic situation statistically predicted burnout, in the occupational domain. Similarly, experiencing stress passing through checkpoints did not meet statistical significance in predicting burnout on the occupational exhaustion domain.

Perceived contributors to burnout differed from statistically significant contributors. There are several potential reasons for this. Firstly, Palestinian health workers may have high levels of resilience,42 although this would need to be evaluated with future study. Secondly, burnout is typically considered in the context of chronic workplace stress, and it is in this context that the Maslach burnout inventory was developed. Several of the unique factors perceived to contribute to burnout are acute stressors, such as military checkpoints. The Maslach burnout inventory is not ideally suited to detect burnout from acute stressors, and the coping response (individual perception, risk perception, behavior change)43 differs between acute and chronic stressors, and subsequently development of burnout. This too would require further study. Thirdly, political factors (e.g. The Israeli Occupation and Siege on Gaza) have highly detrimental economic, social44 and humanitarian effects,45–47 which in turn could precipitate burnout by affecting salaries, availability of medical equipment, or the availability of education or training opportunities. It’s well established that the lack of medical equipment, and salary regularity are a consequence of the siege and occupation,44,48,49 as are likely other factors that are established to cause burnout.

Several factors predicted domains of the Maslach Burnout Inventory. Most of these factors are not readily modifiable, such as education level, job category and marital status. However, several of the unique risk factors identified in this study are modifiable, such as being respected at work, low salary, and feeling unsupported by colleagues. It’s important to contextualize these factors with established models of burnout. The Job Demand-Control-Support (J-DCS) model suggests that high job demands and low job control lead to higher levels of burnout50. Respondents to study survey 2 identified high work demands (patient acuity), and the lack of colleague support or respect at work may reflect an underlying lack of control in the work environment. This may explain higher rates of burnout in our study versus those outside Palestine. The Four-Dimensional Model of Burnout identifies four psychological domains of burnout, being cynicism, depersonalization, exhaustion and professional efficacy51. While this study did not directly address each of these domains, the amount and regularity of salary could impact cynicism, depersonalization and professional efficacy. Similarly, being less supported or respected at work would likely lead to greater levels of depersonalization and decreased professional efficacy. Golembiewski-Munzenrider-Carter studied and expanded the Maslach Burnout Inventory,30 and considered people to progress from depersonalization to diminished personal accomplishment to emotional exhaustion. People can be protected by coping strategies that “distance” themselves with “detached concern” or alternatively show counterproductive behavior such as withdrawal, disengagement, or rigidity. This study confirmed being older and having more work experience associated with lower burnout rates; it’s possible that these factors improve coping behavior. Similarly, being married associated with decreased burnout in this study, suggesting that being married may enhance ability to distance oneself from work with “detached concern.”

Implications

Targeting modifiable predictors would be a rational approach to lowering rates of burnout in intensive care units in Palestine. How to design an effective intervention to reduce burnout remains controversial; a systematic review failed to identify consistently effective strategies in work settings,52 but it appears interventions that target the broader organization are more effective than those targeted to the individual53. This study identified strong relationship between feeling not respected or supported by colleagues and depersonalization and loss of empathy; further study is required to determine if interventions that improve perceived support and respect, may improve this component of the Maslach score, thus reducing burnout.

Study strengths and limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the response rates could not be established accurately because the total number of health workers in intensive care units across Palestine could not be established. However, we integrated questions in the survey to determine response rate from each of the largest intensive care unit in Gaza and West Bank. The response rates in those units were significantly higher than several studies on health care worker burnout,12,54 and comparable to other studies33,55. Secondly, this study relied on self-reporting, which introduces the possibility of recall bias. However, this is an inherent limitation in any survey study, which is partially neutralizes by use of a validated instrument such as the Maslach burnout inventory survey. Thirdly, the selection process limited generalizability of prevalence rates, by relying on social media and leadership contacts. This non-random process may have led to selection bias as people affected by burnout may be more likely to participate. Finally, the survey may have been affected by social desirability bias. However, this was addressed by assuring confidentiality and anonymity, each of which reduces social desirability bias56. On the other hand, this study yielded a large sample size, in both Gaza and West Bank. The design enabled identification of unique risk factors for burnout that have not previously been reported. Similarly, this is the most in-depth study of burnout reported for Palestine health workers from intensive care unit settings. The findings can be utilized for evidence-based interventions to reduce burnout in critical care workers in Palestine.

Conclusions

Burnout is highly prevalent in Palestinian critical care workers. Burnout is associated with higher levels of education, younger age, decreased work experience, and never married status. Unique risk factors in Palestine that associate with burnout include feeling burned out due to the economic situation in Palestine, feeling that salary is too low, feeling not respected or supported by colleagues at work. Future studies should evaluate interventions aimed at modifying these factors.
