Harnessing the PpHog1 Regulatory Network to Engineer a UPR‐Adaptive Komagataella phaffii Chassis for High‐Copy Secretory Protein Production
Authors: Jiacheng Shi, Zhanqing Lv, Chaoyu Lu, Bohao Fang, Zerui Zou, Mian Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Komagataella phaffii is one of the most widely used eukaryotic protein expression systems. Increasing the copy number of exogenous genes is a common method to enhance expression efficiency. However, at extremely high copy numbers, the resulting endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) can hinder further improvement in the expression efficiency of secreted proteins. And elucidating the underlying molecular mechanisms serves as the foundation for engineering and constructing stress‐adapted strains. High‐copy secretory expression was first confirmed to adversely affect yeast growth fitness, a phenotype consistent with DTT‐induced unfolded protein response (UPR). Screening of a kinase knockout library established that UPR signalling in K. phaffii was modulated by the PpHog1‐mediated MAPK pathway. PpHog1, the K. phaffii ortholog of the well‐characterized Saccharomyces cerevisiae Hog1 (67% sequence identity), is traditionally known for its roles in osmotic stress and cell wall integrity. Notably, while ScHog1 has also been implicated in ERS regulation, the downstream pathway remains obscure. Further comparison of the PpHog1 interactome with versus without DTT treatment, combined with follow‐up genetic knockout/knockdown screens, leads to the identification of two critical UPR regulators: PpLRR‐0498 and PpPINT‐0120. Subsequently, an engineered UPR‐adaptive chassis (UPR‐Ad+) was constructed by over‐expressing these three key regulators. Using human serum albumin (HSA), human calcium‐regulated actin binding protein LCP1, and α‐amylase as reporters, UPR‐Ad+ enhanced the production of high‐copy secretory proteins by 30%–46%. Collectively, this work advances the understanding of UPR regulation in K. phaffii and provides a strategic basis for designing high‐yield strains.Keywords: endoplasmic reticulum stress, high‐copy secretory expression, Komagataella phaffii, unfolded protein response, UPR adaptive chassis

Introduction

Komagataella phaffii has emerged as a premier cell factory for recombinant protein expression, notably for the production of secretory proteins (Jallouli et al. 2017; Shen, Xue, et al. 2016). Along with promoter engineering and codon optimization, increasing the gene copy number is widely used to boost recombinant protein yields (Aqeel et al. 2024; Sygmund et al. 2012; Shen et al. 2012; Sallada et al. 2019). However, a threshold exists for gene copy number; although beneficial initially, further increases become helpless or even counterproductive to recombinant protein production (Aw and Polizzi 2013; Cámara et al. 2017).

Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress (ERS) is a main reason for the above‐mentioned problem (Zhu et al. 2009). Within cells, the ER serves as the central hub for protein folding, modification and quality control (Bhaduri et al. 2023; Ito and Nagata 2021). When unfolded or misfolded proteins accumulate excessively in the ER—exceeding the cell's capacity for folding or clearance—ERS is induced, disrupting ER homeostasis and subsequently triggering the unfolded protein response (UPR) (Hwang and Qi 2018; Li et al. 2017). Cellular stresses can also induce ERS. Perturbation in calcium homeostasis, glucose deprivation and cholesterol overloading can disrupt the protein folding process (Zhang and Kaufman 2004). Additionally, chemical agents such as dithiothreitol (DTT) and tunicamycin can directly interfere with the protein folding process, also eliciting ERS (Xie et al. 2024).

In mammalian cells, the UPR is usually initiated through three major transmembrane sensor pathways: Ire1α, PERK and ATF6 (Hughes and Mallucci 2019; Salvagno et al. 2022). As in yeast, the Ire1‐Hac1 pathway is the well‐known UPR signalling cascade. Activated Ire1 splices the HAC1 mRNA precursor, enabling it to be translated into a transcriptional activator that induces the expression of downstream ERS‐responsive genes (Miyazaki et al. 2013; Rubio et al. 2011). ERS‐responsive genes generally encompass four functional aspects: up‐regulate chaperones (like BiP, Kar2 and PDI) to assist in protein folding (Wiseman et al. 2010); accelerate lipid biosynthesis to expand ER membrane area and lumen space (Papagiannidis et al. 2021; Sriburi et al. 2004); initial Endoplasmic Reticulum‐Associated Degradation (ERAD) to eliminate misfolded proteins (Hwang and Qi 2018); global translation suppression to alleviate immediate stress and facilitate the repair process (Hughes and Mallucci 2019; Samali et al. 2010).

In K. phaffii, the full spectrum of molecular mechanisms triggered after UPR induction is still not well understood. Whether any pathway exists beyond the canonical Ire1‐Hac1 axis also remains unknown. Recent studies have revealed that K. phaffii exhibits constitutive basal UPR activity even without external stress, and Ire1 possesses Hac1‐independent functions, including involvement in cytosolic protein quality control and heat stress resistance (Fauzee et al. 2020, 2023). The cellular redox state, particularly the glutathione redox potential in the ER, has also been identified as a key factor influencing protein folding and secretion under varying oxygen availability (Kostopoulou et al. 2025).

Extensive efforts have been devoted to modulating UPR to enhance recombinant protein production in K. phaffii. Overexpression of Hac1 remains the most widely exploited strategy, with co‐expression of HAC1 significantly improving the secretion of diverse heterologous proteins, including glucose oxidase (Yu et al. 2020), stem bromelain (Varadharaj et al. 2025) and ovomucoid (Kim et al. 2025; Zhang, Gong, et al. 2024; Zhang, Lin, et al. 2024). Moreover, a module combination strategy of co‐expressing SEC53, CNE1 and GCN4 genes further elevated the yield and activity of glucose oxidase by 2.53 and 5.11 folds, respectively (Gu et al. 2015). Besides, signal peptide optimization to redirect proteins into co‐translational translocation and overexpression of signal recognition particle components have both been shown to enhance secretion capacity (Zhang, Gong, et al. 2024; Zhang, Lin, et al. 2024). Deciphering additional UPR mechanisms would offer new targets for engineering, paving the way for synergistic strategies that combine with chaperone modulation to enhance overall expression efficiency.

Here, we screened over 30 protein kinases in K. phaffii and identified the PpHog1 pathway involved in UPR signalling. A flow cytometry‐based screening was then performed to identify partners or downstream components of PpHog1. Finally, a UPR‐adaptive chassis was constructed and evaluated for its capacity to express secretory proteins under high gene copy numbers.

Materials and Methods

Strains and Culture Conditions

The Escherichia coli TOP10 strain was used for plasmid construction and propagation. E. coli cells were cultured in LB or LLB medium, containing 0.5% yeast extract, 1% tryptone and 1% NaCl (0.5% for LLB) at 37°C. 100 μg/mL of ampicillin was added to the medium when required.

The K. phaffii strain GS115 served as the wild‐type for constructing transgenic strains. For liquid culture, yeast cells were shake‐cultured at 30°C with 220 rpm. Culture media are YPD (2% tryptone, 1% yeast extract, 2% glucose, 2% agar for plates), YPM (2% tryptone, 1% yeast extract, 0.5% methanol, 2% agar for plates), BMGY (2% tryptone, 1% yeast extract, 1.34% YNB, 1% glycerol, 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer), BMMY (2% tryptone, 1% yeast extract, 1.34% YNB, 1% methanol, 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer).

For transcriptional regulation analysis, yeast cells were cultured in YPD medium to an OD600 of 6–8, after which the DTT‐treated group was supplemented with 40 mM DTT (from a 1 M stock solution), while the untreated group received no addition. Following a 6 h incubation, cells were harvested for ChIP or RNA analysis.

For recombinant protein expression, yeast cells were cultured in YPD to an OD600 of 6–8, harvested, washed twice with sorbitol, and transferred to BMMY. 1% methanol was supplemented every 24 h.

Plasmid Construction and Strain Generation

For ɑHSA and HSA strains harbouring different copies, and HSA sequences were PCR‐amplified from previous lab stock and cloned into the plasmid pPICNɑ or pPICN (N for zhongshengmycin resistance gene, ɑ for ɑ factor) under P AOX1 . The plasmid was linearized and then electroporated into the wild‐type GS115 strain or UPR‐Ad+ chassis. Single colonies were picked from YPD plates containing zhongshengmycin (2 mg/mL), and qPCR was performed to verify gene copy number. The same method was used to construct LCP1, ɑ‐Amylase and eGFP reporter strains.

For PpHog1 complementation, the P HOG1 ‐PpHOG1 sequence was PCR‐amplified from the K. phaffii genome, cloned into the vector pPIC3.5 k containing HIS4. A Flag tag was added at the C‐terminus of PpHog1. The plasmid was linearized at the HIS4 locus and electroporated into the ΔPphog1 strain. Single clones were picked from YND plates and verified by PCR to select for strains containing a single copy, resulting in the ΔPphog1‐PpHOG1‐Flag strain.

For overexpression of PpHOG1, PpLRR‐0498, PpPINT‐0120, 0333 and 1034, each ORF sequence was PCR‐amplified from the K. phaffii genome and then cloned into the plasmid pGAPZ under the GAP promoter (deleting the alpha factor sequence), maintaining the 6 × His tag at the C‐terminus. The resulting plasmid was then linearized within P GAP and electroporated into the GS115 strain. Single colonies were isolated from zeocin‐containing (0.1 mg/mL) YPD plates, followed by qPCR analysis to identify those with the highest integration copy number (Piva et al. 2020).

For mScarlet‐I reporter strains, different promoter sequences (P 0931 , P 0567 , P 0008 , P 0377 ) were PCR‐amplified from the K. phaffii genome, while mScarlet‐I was PCR‐amplified from lab stock. Each promoter and mScarlet‐I were overlapped and cloned into the plasmid pPIC3.5k containing HIS4. The plasmid was linearized and then electroporated into the GS115 strain. Single colonies were picked from YND plates, and PCR was performed to verify the presence of a single integration copy.

To construct the UPR‐Ad+ chassis, PpLRR‐0498 and PpHOG1 were cloned into the pGAPZ vector under P GAP and P 0052 (promoter of PAS_FragB_0052, strong constitutive promoter) promoters, respectively, to generate two expression cassettes (P GAP ‐PpLRR0498 and P 0052 ‐PpHOG1). PpPINT‐0120 was cloned into the pPIC3.5K vector under GAP promoter. The resulting two constructs were linearized at the P GAP or HIS4 loci, respectively. After electroporation, single colonies were isolated from zeocin‐containing (0.5 mg/mL) YND plates, followed by qPCR analysis to identify those with the highest integration copy number (Piva et al. 2020).

The CRISPR‐Cas9 method was used for gene knock out (Liu et al. 2019).

All primer sets used in this study were included in Table S7.

Gene Copy Number Determination

To determine target gene copy numbers, a recombinant plasmid containing single copies of both GAPDH and the target gene was constructed. The plasmid was quantified, serially diluted 10‐fold to generate standards ranging from 104 to 109 copies/μL, and amplified by qPCR using primers specific for GAPDH and the target gene, respectively. Standard curves of log10 (copy number) versus Ct value were generated for both genes.

Genomic DNA from individual transformants was then analysed using the same primer pairs, and the absolute copy numbers of both genes were interpolated from the corresponding standard curves. The target gene copy number was calculated as the ratio of target gene copies to GAPDH copies, with GAPDH serving as a stable single‐copy reference.

Spot Assay

Yeast cells in the logarithmic growth phase were harvested and serially diluted in YPD medium to OD600 = 1.0, 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001. For each dilution, 5 μL of the cell suspension was spotted onto YPD, YPM or YPD + DTT (or tunicamycin, with the specific concentrations indicated in the figures) plates.

Immunoprecipitation

Shaking cultured yeast cells were cross‐linked with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min and quenched with glycine. Cells were then harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS and resuspended in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP‐40, 0.25% Sodium deoxycholate, pH 7.4). Cells were disrupted using a vibrating cell disruptor (YeTuo) and centrifuged to collect the supernatant.

The supernatant was mixed with beads that had been pre‐blocked and pre‐incubated with 6 × His tag antibody (Abcam, Catalogue No. ab9108) overnight, followed by mixing and incubation overnight at 4°C. The beads were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with RIPA Buffer and twice with AMBIC Buffer. Proteins were then eluted by boiling with loading buffer.

The pulled‐down proteins were then examined by SDS‐PAGE/western blot or mass spectrometry (APPLIED PROTEIN TECHNOLOGY). For western blot, Flag tag mouse monoclonal antibody (Beyotime, Catalogue No. AF2852, 1:1000 dilution) was used as primary antibody and HRP‐conjugated goat anti‐mouse IgG(H + L) (Beyotime, Catalogue No. A0216, 1:1000 dilution) was used as secondary antibody.

RNA Extraction, qPCR and RNA‐Seq

Shaking cultured yeast cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS and resuspended in TRIeasy (YEASEN, Catalogue No. 19201ES60). Cells were then disrupted using a vibrating cell disruptor (YeTuo), and centrifuged to collect the supernatant. A same volume of Phe‐Chl (phenol: chloroform: isoamyl alcohol = 25:24:1) was added to the supernatant. After thorough mixing and centrifugation, the upper colourless solution was collected. Then, 1/10 volume of 3 M sodium acetate and 2.5 volumes of ethanol were added; after mixing, the mixture was incubated overnight at −80°C. RNA was obtained by centrifugation, washed twice with 80% ethanol and dissolved in DEPC‐treated water.

For RT‐qPCR, 1 μg of total RNA was used for reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA (YEASEN, Catalogue No. 11119ES60). The cDNA was used for quantitative PCR (BioRad), and GAPDH was selected as an internal control.

For RNA‐seq, 5 μg of total RNA was sent to Genewiz for sequencing analysis. Raw data are deposited in the Sequence Read Archive of NCBI database (PRJNA1417261).

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation and Sequencing (ChIP‐Seq)

Shaking cultured yeast cells were cross‐linked with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min, quenched with glycine and then harvested by centrifugation. Chromatin immune‐precipitation was performed as described (Zhang, Gong, et al. 2024; Zhang, Lin, et al. 2024). Anti‐6 × His tag antibody (Abcam, Catalogue No. ab9108) was used for pull down. After elution, samples were subjected to library construction (NEBNext ChIP‐Seq Library Prep Master Mix Set for Illumina) and high‐throughput sequencing (Genewiz). Raw data are deposited in the Sequence Read Archive of NCBI database (PRJNA1417261).

The analysis began with quality assessment of the raw sequencing data using FastQC, followed by adapter trimming and alignment to the genome with Bowtie2 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012). Significant binding sites (peaks) were identified using MACS2 (Li 2011) with a stringent threshold. The resulting alignment and peak files were then visualized in the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV).

The CRISPRi‐dCas9 Knockdown System

Both dCas9 and gRNA expression vectors were constructed according to previously described methods (Liao et al. 2021; Qiao et al. 2023). dCas9 was fused with the general transcription repressor Tup1RD1 and expressed under P CAT1 . gRNA sequences were designed by CHOPCHOP (https://chopchop.cbu.uib.no/). For each gRNA, two complementary ssDNAs were synthesized, each with 5′ overhangs compatible with the LguI digestion site. The gRNAs were then ligated into the pAG‐P tRNA ‐GFP vector to replace the GFP expression cassette, thereby generating the gRNA library expression vectors (Figure 4A).

(C) Volcano plot of RNA‐Seq data from wild‐type yeast cells, comparing DTT‐treated vs. untreated conditions. (D) mScarlet‐I fluorescence reporting UPR response of four candidate promoters (P 0008 , P 0377 , P 0931 , P 0567 ) (n = 3). (E) Flow cytometry analysis of yeast cells with and without the P 0567 ‐mScarlet‐I reporter under ±DTT conditions. *p < 0.05; ****p < 0.0001.

Flow Cytometry

Shaking cultured yeast cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS and diluted until OD600 = 1. A 500 μL aliquot of the cell suspension was subjected to sorting by flow cytometry (SONY SA3800), with an excitation wavelength of 561 nm and detection via the FL2 channel.

Fermentation in 1‐L Bioreactor

High‐cell‐density fermentation was performed in a 1‐L bioreactor (Shanghai Baoxing Bio‐Engineering Equipment) following the Invitrogen Pichia Fermentation Process Guidelines. A single colony was inoculated into 5 mL of YPD medium and incubated at 30°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 24 h. This primary culture was then transferred into 60 mL of BMGY medium and incubated under the same conditions for 16 h as the secondary seed culture.

Fermentation was carried out in BMGY medium with an initial working volume of 600 mL, containing 40 g/L glycerol, and inoculated at 10% (v/v). The agitation was set at 400 rpm (maximum 700 rpm), aeration at 2.7 vvm and temperature at 30°C. The pH was maintained at 5.5 by automatic addition of 50% (v/v) ammonium hydroxide. The glycerol batch phase proceeded until a sharp increase in dissolved oxygen (DO) indicated glycerol depletion. After a 1‐h carbon‐limited period, methanol fed‐batch was initiated with methanol containing 1.2% (v/v) PTM1 trace elements at an initial feed rate of 3.6 mL/L/h, and subsequently adjusted to maintain DO above 20%. Samples were taken at regular intervals to monitor cell density and enzyme activity.

Quantification of HSA and LCP1

For HSA and LCP1 quantification, the proteins were first purified by affinity chromatography. In the case of HSA, the culture supernatant was harvested by centrifugation and loaded onto a Blue Sepharose 6 Fast Flow column (Cytiva) pre‐equilibrated with 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). After extensive washing with equilibration buffer, bound HSA was eluted with phosphate buffer containing 1.5 M NaCl. As for LCP1, which carries a C‐terminal 6 × His tag, the supernatant was loaded onto a Ni–NTA agarose column (Thermo Fisher) pre‐equilibrated with binding buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 10 mM imidazole, pH 7.5). After washing with wash buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 25 mM imidazole, pH 7.5), bound LCP1 was eluted with elution buffer (50 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, pH 7.5).

The eluted fractions were pooled, and the protein concentration was determined using the BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime) according to the manufacturer's instructions, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard.

α‐Amylase Enzyme Activity Assay

Fermentation broth samples collected at different time points were centrifuged to separate the supernatant from the cell pellets. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22‐μm membrane for extracellular enzyme activity measurement. The cell pellets were washed, resuspended and disrupted by bead beating to release intracellular enzymes. α‐Amylase activity in both fractions was determined using the 3,5‐dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method (Zhang et al. 2025).

Fluorescence Measurement

Shaking cultured yeast cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed twice with PBS, then resuspended in PBS and diluted until OD600 = 1. Subsequently, 200 μL of the diluted cells were loaded into a 96‐well plate. Fluorescence measurements were performed by microplate reader (BioTek). Excitation/emission wavelengths were 488/525 nm for eGFP and 569/594 nm for mScarlet‐I.

Quantification and Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates, and data are presented as mean ± SD. For comparisons involving two independent variables (e.g., genotype, time or culture condition), two‐way ANOVA was performed followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For comparisons involving a single independent variable with three or more groups, one‐way ANOVA was used, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. For comparisons involving a single independent variable with only two groups, an unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test was used; equality of variances was assessed by F‐test, and Welch's correction was applied when necessary. Statistical significance is indicated as *(p < 0.05), **(p < 0.01), ***(p < 0.001), ****(p < 0.0001). All statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 10.

Results

High‐Copy Expression of Secretory Genes Significantly Compromises Cell Fitness

To evaluate how high‐copy secretory genes affect cell viability, we constructed a series of strains carrying different human serum albumin (HSA) copy numbers for intracellular or secretory expression. Spot assays revealed that all strains exhibited comparable fitness on YPD plates, where HSA was not expressed. In contrast, induction of HSA expression with methanol caused severe growth defects in the secretory expression strains (Figure 1A). Moreover, the severity of the growth defect increased in a clear dose‐dependent manner with higher HSA copy numbers. In stark contrast, the intracellular‐expression strains, even at the highest copy number, maintained robust growth under the same inducing conditions.

High‐copy expression of secretory genes significantly compromises cell fitness. (A) Spot assay showing cell fitness of K. phaffii strains carrying different HSA copy numbers for intracellular or secretory expression. Strains are named as GS‐copy number × (with α, secretory expression; without α, intracellular expression) HSA. (B) RT‐qPCR comparing expression levels of known UPR genes in wild‐type (GS115 ± DTT) and GS‐25 × αHSA strains (n = 3). *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Interestingly, RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of secretory HSA in the 10–17 copy‐number strains were substantially lower than those of the 4–7 copy‐number intracellular‐expression strains (Figure S1). This counterintuitive observation suggests that ER stress actively represses global transcription as a feedback mechanism to reduce the protein load entering the ER. This reinforces that the fitness cost is specifically associated with ER lumen congestion rather than generic transcriptional burden.

To further dissect the molecular basis of this secretory‐specific stress, qPCR was performed to examine the expression profile of UPR marker genes in the high‐copy secretory strain (GS‐25 × αHSA). As anticipated, some canonical UPR genes were upregulated or downregulated, a response analogous to that elicited by DTT treatment (Figure 1B). This confirms that high‐copy secretory gene expression strongly triggers UPR, and the resulting global transcriptional/translational attenuation ultimately compromises cellular fitness.

While chaperone co‐expression (e.g., SSA1) can partially remediate these fitness defects (Figure 1A, last row), more extensive targets beyond conventional chaperones are critically involved in this secretory burden and remain to be identified.

The PpHog1‐Mediated MAPK Pathway Modulates UPR Signalling in K. phaffii

Kinases commonly serve as key regulatory hubs in signal transduction networks. Therefore, we then screened the 31 strains in the previously built kinase knockout library (Shen, Kong, et al. 2016). As shown by Figure 2A, deletion of PAS_chr1‐3_0232, PAS_chr1‐4_0271 and PAS_chr1‐4_0498 showed the most severe DTT sensitivity. Interestingly, PAS_chr1‐3_0232 and PAS_chr1‐4_0271 were annotated as components of the MAP kinase cascade (Capaldi et al. 2008; Shen, Kong, et al. 2016), corresponding to a MAPK and a MAPKK, respectively, within the signalling hierarchy. And PAS_chr1‐4_0498 was annotated as one of three possible beta‐subunits of the Snf1 kinase complex (De Schutter et al. 2009).

The PpHog1‐mediated MAPK pathway modulates UPR signalling in K. phaffii. (A) Spot assay screening for DTT‐sensitive strains using the 31 strains in the kinase knockout library. (B) Sequence alignment of ScHog1 and PpHog1. (C) Spot assay showing cellular fitness of PpHog1 knockout and complementation strains under a higher DTT concentration. (D) Functional analysis of differentially expressed genes from 40 mM DTT‐treated ΔPphog1 versus wild‐type strains using annotation and GO classification.

PAS_chr1‐3_0232 shared 67% protein sequence homology with Saccharomyces cerevisiae Hog1 (Figure 2B), which was well‐known to function in osmotic stress and cell wall integrity pathways (O'Rourke et al. 2002; Zhao et al. 2021). Moreover, ScHog1 had also been reported to regulate ERS, although the downstream pathway remained obscure (Bicknell et al. 2010; Torres‐Quiroz et al. 2010). PAS_chr1‐3_0232 was then named PpHOG1. The reduced fitness of the ΔPphog1 strain was exacerbated by increasing DTT concentrations in a dose‐dependent manner, and this defect was fully rescued by genetic complementation with PpHog1 (Figure 2C). These results confirm that the observed DTT sensitivity is specifically attributable to the loss of PpHog1, rather than secondary mutations, and further support a role for this MAPK in mitigating ER stress under acute reducing conditions.

To gain mechanistic insights into the DTT sensitivity of the ΔPphog1 mutant, we performed comparative RNA‐seq analysis on wild‐type and ΔPphog1 strains following DTT treatment (Figure 2D, Table S1). Differential expression analysis (|log2FC| ≥ 1, adjusted p < 0.05) identified 17 upregulated and 290 downregulated genes in the mutant relative to wild‐type. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the most significantly repressed categories were predominantly associated with translation and ribosome biogenesis. This global translational shutdown is consistent with the reduced fitness phenotype observed in the ΔPphog1 strain under ER stress (Figure 2C). Beyond the core ribosomal components, we also observed a marked decrease in transcripts encoding diverse RNA‐binding proteins (RBPs) that function in mRNA stability, splicing, nuclear export and translational control. These results position PpHog1 as a critical regulator of the translational machinery under ER stress conditions.

PpHog1 Interacts With PpLRR‐0498 to Regulate UPR Signalling in K. phaffii

To identify key components cooperating with PpHog1 in UPR signal transduction, we compared its interactome under normal and DTT‐induced ERS conditions. This comparison was performed using Immunoprecipitation‐Mass Spectrometry (IP‐MS). GO analysis of the 197 identified differential interacting proteins (Table S2) is shown in Figure 3A. Guided by functional annotation and domain prediction, we first excluded proteins unrelated to UPR and then focused our investigation on the seven candidate genes exhibiting the most pronounced differential interaction, as summarized in Table S3. Of these, knockout strains were successfully generated for four candidates (PAS_chr2‐2_0498, PAS_chr4_0550, PAS_chr1‐3_0102 and PAS_chr2‐1_0502). Spot assays identified the Δ0498 strain as exhibiting the most severe DTT‐sensitive growth defect (Figure 3B).

(B) Spot assay showing cellular fitness of multiple knockout strains under DTT‐induced ERS. (C, D) Functional domain (C) and 3D structure (D) prediction of PpLRR‐0498 by NCBI and AlphaFold, respectively. (E) Spot assay showing cellular fitness of PpHog1 or PpLRR‐0498 overexpression strains under DTT‐induced ERS. (F) Immunoprecipitation assay examining the association of PpHog1 with PpLRR‐0498. (G) PpLRR‐0498 enrichment peaks (0 vs. 40 mM DTT) visualized by IGV.

Annotated as a hypothetical protein, structural prediction of 0498 indicates the presence of numerous leucine‐rich repeats (LRR), supporting its possible role in protein–protein interactions and transcriptional regulation (Figure 3C,D) (Draper et al. 1994). For clarity and readability, we hereafter refer to this locus as PpLRR‐0498. This designation is used for descriptive purposes only and does not imply a confirmed functional annotation.

Conversely, overexpression of either PpHog1 or PpLRR‐0498 robustly boosted the cells' tolerance to DTT (Figure 3E). To further validate the interaction between PpHog1 and PpLRR‐0498, a co‐immunoprecipitation (IP) assay was performed. As shown by Figure 3F, a specific interaction between PpHog1 and PpLRR‐0498 was detected only upon DTT treatment and was undetectable in the absence of the stressor.

To identify genes differentially regulated by PpLRR‐0498 in response to DTT, we compared its genome‐wide binding profiles under normal and DTT‐treated conditions by ChIP‐seq (Figure 3G). Two candidate downstream genes were then identified (PAS_chr3_0053 and PAS_chr4_0360), with stronger enrichment of PpLRR‐0498 observed at promoter regions in the absence of DTT. Consistently, the transcript levels of both 0053 and 0360 were significantly higher under non‐stressed conditions than upon DTT treatment (Figure S2), suggesting that PpLRR‐0498 likely functions as a transcriptional activator of these two genes.

Combining the CRISPRi‐dCas9‐Tup1RD1 Knockdown System With an UPR Reporter Enabled a Functional Screen for Novel Factors That Interact With PpHog1 During UPR Signalling

Given the large number of candidates identified in the IP‐MS analysis, the conventional approach of generating individual knockout strains was low‐throughput and inefficient. Furthermore, the inability to disrupt essential genes may hamper comprehensive screening, leading to the potential omission of important candidates. Therefore, we employed the CRISPRi‐dCas9‐Tup1RD1 knockdown system, enabling an efficient functional screen of the PpHog1 interaction candidates to uncover additional regulators of UPR (Replogle et al. 2022) (Figure 4A). The knockdown efficiency of the system was first assessed on several randomly selected endogenous genes. Efficiency ranged from 40% to 80% depending on the gRNA target site, with PpGUT1 (PAS_chr4_0783) shown as a representative example (Figure S3A). Following a re‐evaluation of the interaction list based on protein abundance and predicted functions, 51 high‐priority targets were selected. For each, 2–3 gRNAs were designed to target the −50 to −150 region upstream of the transcription start site (Table S4). The gRNA plasmid library was constructed using LguI digestion and T4 ligase ligation, as described in methods (Figure 4B).

A UPR reporter was then required to monitor the output of the CRISPRi‐dCas9‐Tup1RD1 knockdown system. mScarlet‐I driven by the well‐known UPR‐responsive promoter P kar2 (Guerfal et al. 2010; Resina et al. 2007) was examined first. However, the red fluorescence intensity after DTT treatment was merely 2‐fold of that before treatment (Figure S3B). Therefore, guided by comparative RNA‐Seq analysis of ±DTT conditions (Figure 4C), four additional promoters (promoters of PAS_chr4_0008, PAS_chr3_0931, PAS_chr3_0377 and PAS_chr4_0567, Table S5) were selected, and their strength was subsequently verified using the mScarlet‐I reporter (Figure 4D). P 0567 with the strongest DTT‐responsive induction (11.89‐fold) and the weakest background expression was selected as UPR reporter. Consequently, DTT treatment allowed for clear discrimination of cells by fluorescence intensity via flow cytometry (Figure 4E).

By integrating the UPR reporter P 0567 ‐mScarlet‐I into the knockdown system, we used flow cytometry to collect cells exhibiting aberrantly high or low‐fluorescence (Figure 5A). The genes responsible for these phenotypes were subsequently identified by sequencing their associated gRNAs. As a result, three hits were obtained from the elevated fluorescence group: PAS_chr3_1034, PAS_chr2‐2_0333 and PAS_chr2‐2_0120 (Table S6). Targeted knockdown (Figure 5B–D) and subsequent up‐regulation of UPR (Figure 5E) were validated in the respective strains.

(G) Immunoprecipitation assay examining the association of PpHog1 with 0120. (H) Functional domain prediction of 0120 by NCBI. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

When overexpressed, only 0120 significantly enhanced cell fitness under DTT stress (Figure 5F). Co‐IP was performed again to examine the interaction between PpHog1 and 0120, which was markedly enhanced upon DTT treatment (Figure 5G). These findings suggest that 0102 can also synergize with PpHog1 to regulate UPR. Protein domain prediction revealed that the hypothetical protein 0120 contains a PINT (PCI) domain, a motif found in subunits of the proteasome, the COP9 signalosome, and the translation initiation factor eIF3 (Figure 5H) (Kouba et al. 2012; Scheel and Hofmann 2005). For clarity and readability, we hereafter refer to this locus as PpPINT‐0120. This designation is used for descriptive purposes only and does not imply a confirmed functional annotation.

The UPR‐Ad+ Chassis Confers UPR Adaptation and Enhances High‐Copy Secretory Protein Production

So far, PpHog1, PpLRR‐0498 and PpPINT‐0120 are all involved in UPR mediation, and overexpression of any of these genes confers significant resistance to DTT. A combined overexpression was then performed to construct a UPR‐Ad+ strain. As expected, UPR‐Ad+ showed great DTT resistance in the spot assay (Figure 6A). Importantly, the protective effect of UPR‐Ad+ was not specific to DTT, as the strain similarly acquired resistance to tunicamycin, indicating a broadly enhanced capacity to cope with distinct ER stress triggers (Figure 6B). To test whether enhanced adaptive fitness could alleviate the burden of high‐copy gene expression, we employed the engineered UPR‐Ad+ chassis for the high‐copy secretory production of human serum albumin (HSA), human calcium‐regulated actin binding protein LCP1, and α‐amylase as a proof of concept.

The UPR‐Ad+ chassis confers UPR adaptation and enhances high‐copy secretory protein production. (A, B) Spot assay showing cellular fitness of the UPR‐Ad+ chassis under DTT (A) or tunicamycin (B)‐induced ERS. (C, D) Coomassie blue staining (C) and quantification by BCA assay after purification (D) of HSA expressed under 25‐copy secretory conditions in wild‐type and the UPR‐Ad+ chassis after 72 h of methanol‐induced fermentation (n = 3). (E, F) Coomassie blue staining (E) and quantification by BCA assay after purification (F) of LCP1 expressed under 7 or10‐copy secretory conditions in wild‐type and the UPR‐Ad+ chassis after 72 h of methanol‐induced fermentation (n = 3). (G, H) Extracellular enzyme activity (G) and specific activity (H) of α‐amylase expressed under 16‐copy secretory conditions in wild‐type and the UPR‐Ad+ chassis (n = 3). (I) Time profiles of cell growth (OD600) and extracellular amylase activity (U/mL) of the UPR‐Ad+ chassis and wild‐type strain during 1‐L fed‐batch fermentation. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01.

At a high copy number of 25 for the secretory HSA gene, the UPR‐Ad+ chassis yielded significantly 46% higher expression levels than the wild‐type GS115 strain after 72 h of methanol‐induced fermentation (Figure 6C,D), while a 37% improvement was observed for LCP1 at 10 copies (Figure 6E,F). As for α‐amylase, expression in UPR‐Ad+ chassis had a 30% enhancement in extracellular enzyme activity and a 19% improvement in specific enzyme activity at the end of fermentation (Figure 6G,H). Moreover, intracellular amylase activity was also moderately elevated in the UPR‐Ad+ chassis (Figure S4), reflecting an expanded pool of correctly folded, transport‐competent protein within the cells. This advantage was further validated in 1‐L bioreactor fermentations, where the UPR‐Ad+ chassis exhibited improved cell growth and achieved a 40% higher volumetric enzyme activity than the wild‐type chassis at 96 h (Figure 6I). Since ERS/UPR are primarily driven by the folding burden of secretory proteins in the ER, the UPR‐Ad+ chassis, as expected, provided no expression advantage for intracellular eGFP (Figure S5).

Discussion

In K. phaffii, ERS is a common problem caused by high‐copy secretory production of recombinant proteins. However, the molecular mechanisms of ERS‐induced UPR remain poorly characterized beyond the involvement of a few well‐known chaperones. Through a kinase knockout library screen, this study identified the critical regulatory role of the PpHog1‐centred MAPK signalling pathway in the UPR. Further investigation identified PpLRR‐0498 and PpPINT‐0120 as novel interactors of PpHog1, revealing their collaboration in modulating the UPR signalling pathway. By over‐expressing all three genes, the UPR‐Ad+ chassis presented great UPR adaptation and greatly improved high‐copy secretory protein production.

The identification of PpHog1‐MAPK as a UPR modulator reveals an interesting crosstalk between osmosensing and ER stress response pathways in K. phaffii. This is consistent with emerging evidence in other fungal species that the HOG pathway interfaces with UPR signalling. In Candida albicans , Hog1 is activated during tunicamycin‐induced ER stress and functions to attenuate Ire1‐dependent UPR activity, with hog1 deficient cells retaining spliced HAC1 mRNA for longer periods, reflecting a delay in UPR attenuation (Husain et al. 2022). In S. cerevisiae , Snf1 negatively regulates Hog1 signalling during ER stress, with snf1 mutants exhibiting ER stress resistance (Mizuno et al. 2015). However, here we identified PpHog1 (MAPK), PAS_chr1‐4_0271 (MAPKK) and PAS_chr1‐4_0498 (Snf1 subunit) positively supporting cell resistance towards ER stress in K. phaffii (Figure 2A). These findings suggest that the Hog1‐UPR crosstalk may be evolutionarily conserved but functionally modulated in a species‐ and stress‐specific manner. Unlike S. cerevisiae , which relies heavily on glycerol accumulation for osmoadaptation, K. phaffii exhibits a marginal glycerol induction response and instead emphasizes stress‐induced damage repair for survival under hyperosmotic stress (Lara‐Martínez et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2021). Therefore, the MAPK pathway may have been co‐opted in K. phaffii to coordinate protein‐folding quality control with the cell's relatively limited osmotic adjustment capacity.

The identification of PpHog1‐MAPK as a UPR modulator reveals an unexpected crosstalk between osmosensing and ER stress response pathways in K. phaffii. To rapidly prioritise UPR‐specific candidates from the PpHog1 interactome, we established a CRISPRi‐dCas9‐Tup1RD1 knockdown‐based screening pipeline. A gRNA library for the CRISPRi‐dCas9‐Tup1RD1 system was designed and expressed, generating a comprehensive knockdown library. Together with the UPR reporter P 0567 ‐mScarlet‐I, genes that either enhanced or suppressed UPR activity could be readily identified. Unfortunately, we failed to identify any genes whose knockdown reproducibly resulted in the low‐fluorescence (UPR suppression) phenotype.

The functions and working mechanisms of PpLRR‐0498 and PpPINT‐0120 merit further exploration. The presence of the LRR motif raises the possibility that PpLRR‐0498 may function as a scaffold or adaptor, facilitating the assembly of signalling complexes at the ER membrane or tethering PpHog1 to downstream effectors (Vaga et al. 2014). Similarly, the PINT domain typically functions as a scaffold mediating protein–protein interactions within large multiprotein complexes, rather than possessing catalytic activity on its own. Therefore, PpPINT‐0120 may also act as an adaptor or scaffold protein, potentially facilitating the assembly or stability of a multi‐subunit complex involved in ER stress signalling. Future studies aimed at dissecting the direct vs. indirect interactions between PpHog1 and these two proteins, as well as identifying their specific downstream targets, will be essential to fully understand their mechanistic roles.

The three UPR‐regulatory genes identified in this study were ultimately leveraged to engineer a UPR‐adapted chassis UPR‐Ad+. The UPR‐Ad+ chassis therefore outperforms its parental strain in expressing high‐copy secretory genes. Consistent with this design, a two‐gene intermediate strain (PpHog1 + PpLRR‐0498, lacking component PpPINT‐0120) exhibited an intermediate production level, confirming that the three regulators act cooperatively to achieve the full beneficial effect (Figure 6H). Based on established reports, the rational combination of the UPR‐Ad+ chassis with the co‐expression of known chaperones (e.g., SSA1) may synergistically enhance recombinant protein production.

The mechanism by which the UPR‐Ad+ chassis enhances production remains unclear. To distinguish between ‘stress alleviation’ and ‘enhanced stress tolerance’, the transcript levels of key UPR marker genes—the master transcriptional activator (HAC1) and two chaperone genes (BiP and SSA1)—were measured under DTT treatment in both UPR‐Ad+ chassis and wild‐type control. As shown in Figure S6, HAC1 expression was comparable between the two strains, indicating that the perceived stress intensity was similar. However, BiP and SSA1 transcript levels were significantly higher in the UPR‐Ad+ chassis. Therefore, the UPR‐Ad+ chassis does not simply “alleviate” ER stress. Instead, it has a heightened downstream transcriptional output or a more robust chaperone production capacity upon UPR activation. This supports a model of ‘enhanced stress tolerance’, in which the UPR‐Ad+ chassis enhances production primarily through augmenting the cell's intrinsic capacity to handle protein‐folding challenges, rather than by reducing the initial stress burden.

Finally, while our results establish the UPR‐Ad+ chassis as an effective platform for enhancing secretory protein production in K. phaffii, the long‐term genetic stability of strains with high copy numbers remains an open question that warrants further investigation. Future work will focus on longitudinal stability assays to determine whether the observed expression improvements are sustained over extended cultivation periods, which is a critical parameter for industrial‐scale applications. In addition, scaling up to larger bioreactors will be necessary to fully validate the robustness and industrial applicability of this engineered chassis.
