Clinical and molecular characterization of Krabbe disease in Iranian patients: case report and literature review
Authors: Parnia Asgari, Iman Elahi Vahed, Sahand Tehrani Fateh, Farzad Hashemi-Gorji, Mohammad-Reza Ghasemi, Ali Mardi, Seyed Hassan Tonekaboni, Mohammad Miryounesi, Shadab Salehpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKrabbe disease (KD, OMIM #245200) is a rare autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder characterized by severe demyelination affecting both the central and peripheral nervous systems. Here we report the clinical and molecular findings of two unrelated Iranian patients with KD, originating from consanguineous families. Genetic analysis was initially performed using whole-exome sequencing (WES), then validated by Sanger sequencing. WES identified a novel homozygous variant in the GALC, c.836T > G (p.L279X), in patient-1. In patient-2, WES detected a previously reported homozygous variant, c.578T > C (p.I193T), in the GALC. Sanger sequencing confirmed homozygosity of these variants in the affected patients and heterozygosity in their parents. The affected brother of patient-2 was also homozygous for the variant c.578T > C. The pathogenicity of the detected variants was supported by various lines of evidence, including in silico predictive tools, segregation analysis, and population genetic databases frequency. Our findings expand the mutational spectrum of GALC and highlight the clinical heterogeneity of KD. Morover, these finding contribute to improved genotype-phenotype correlations, which are essential for accurate diagnosis, prognostic valuation, and genetic counseling.Keywords: Krabbe disease, Novel variant, Whole exome sequencing (WES(, GALC gene

Background

Krabbe disease (KD, OMIM #245200) is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder that leads to demyelination of the central and peripheral nervous systems [1]. The incidence of the disease has been estimated at 1 in 300,000 live births and is caused by mutations in the GALC (galactosylceramidase) gene [2, 3]. KD was first described more than a century ago, and the subsequent identification of GALC deficiency as the underlying biochemical defect laid the groundwork for later molecular and therapeutic investigations [4].

The GALC gene maps to the 14q31 chromosomal region, spans approximately 60 kb, and consists of 17 exons. The encoded enzyme, galactosylceramidase, hydrolyzes galactose ester bonds of galactosylceramide (GalCer), and galactosylsphingosine, or psychosine, a principal myelin lipid found in the brain, cerebral cortex, and small intestine and exerts a potent proinflammatory effects [5, 6]. Deficiency of this enzyme results in a profound reduction of galactocerebrosidase activity, leading to the accumulation of cytotoxic psychosine, which has strong proinflammatory and neurotoxic effects, ultimately causing progressive demyelination and a characteristic clinical syndrome [5–7].

KD is classified into four clinical phenotypes according to age of onset and severity, following the classification by Escolar et al.; infantile (onset 0–1year), late infantile (onset 1–3 years), juvenile (onset 4–16 or 18 years), and adult (onset > 18 years) [2]. The infantile form, is the most common and severe, typically presenting within the first 12 months of life with extreme irritability, feeding difficulties, reflux, developmental delay, spasticity, hypotonia, optic atrophy, growth retardation, and seizures [2, 8]. The infantile KD course has been further divided into four distinct clinical stages based on the evolution of these symptoms [9]. Affected infants exhibit neurological regression, including loss of spontaneous movements, hyporeflexia, generalized cerebral atrophy, and seizures. The later-onset form has a more variable and often less severe clinical course with a relatively favorable prognosis [10]. Progressive neurodegeneration leads to decerebration, and most patients succumb to the disease by 2–4 years of age due to respiratory failure or apnea [2, 11–13]. Other clinical features include irritability, poor feeding, swallowing difficulty, appendicular spasticity, loss of hearing and vision, developmental delay, and seizure [10, 14]. Constipation, dysautonomia, and congenital generalized muscular hypertrophy have also been observed in certain patients [8, 15, 16]. This report presents Iranian KD cases with pathogenic GALC variants.

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with international ethical guidelines, including the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments, and ethical approval was obtaining from the Deputy of Research Affairs at Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. Two unrelated families were referred to the interdisciplinary genetic clinic of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (SBMU) for genetic counseling and investigation of the disorder’s etiology. Written informed consent of adult patient was obtained from parents/legal guardians, due to the limited capacity to independently provide fully informed written consent. For participants younger than 16 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from parents/legal guardians prior to enrolment.

Genomic DNA was extracted from proband peripheral blood samples using the standard salting-out method [17]. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA). Whole exome sequencing (WES) was performed on an Illumina HiSeq4000 sequencer with paired-end sequencing (101 bp read length, 100X coverage). SureSelectXT2 V6 kits (Agilent Technologies) were used for enrichment of exonic regions and adjacent exon–intron boundaries. Raw sequencing reads were quality-filtered and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner (BWA). Duplicate reads were marked using SAMtools, and base quality scores were recalibrated. Variants, including single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions/deletions (indels), were called using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) best practices pipeline. The detected variants were annotated with ANNOVAR software. Subsequent variants filtering, annotation, and prioritization were performed using an in-house analysis pipeline based on established clinical and molecular standards.

Sanger sequencing was performed to validate the GALC variants identified by WES in both probands. Segregation analysis involved sequencing available family members (parents and siblings) of both families in an ABI 3500XL Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA) using BigDye Terminator v3.1 chemistry (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). Target GALC regions were amplified with primer pairs listed in Table 1. Routine procedures were followed for the PCR amplification, PCR product purification, and Sanger sequencing. Structural modeling and visualization were performed using SWISS-Model [18], Phyre-2 [19], and PyMOL (1.3).

Literature review

We conducted a structured literature search to identify published cases of KD with genetically confirmed GALC variants. Searches were performed in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science,, using combinations of following keywords: Krabbe disease, globoid cell leukodystrophy, GALC, mutation, variant, genotype, phenotype, galactocerebrosidase, and psychosine. Inclusion criteria were studies reporting patients with confirmed pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the GALC gene and sufficient clinical information to classify phenotype (infantile, late infantile, juvenile, adult). Exclusion criteria were reviews, editorials, or conference abstracts without primary patient data and reports lacking genetic confirmation. Then, articles were screened by title and abstract and those meeting inclusion criteria were included in the final synthesis. Because the review was not registered in PROSPERO and did not strictly follow PRISMA guidelines, we have clarified that this represents a narrative review.

Results

Case presentation

Case 1: The patient was a one-year-old Iranian male, the second child born to consanguineous parents (Fig. 1A), delivered via cesarean section following an unremarkable gestational period (with normal Apgar scores, 2740 g weight). Initial postnatal development appeared normal until approximately two months of age, when the child exhibited a progressive loss of limb motor function, recurrent vomiting, marked irritability, and sleep disturbances. Neurological symptoms worsened, with progressive visual and hearing loss. At 12 months of age, the patient experienced recurrent seizures.

Due to limited clinical resources and the patient’s worsening condition, biochemical testing and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were not performed. WES was carried out at 12 months of age. Despite supportive care, the patient died due to respiratory and heart failure at 22 months of age. There was no family of similar condition reported in the family. WES analysis revealed a biallelic GALC (NM_000153.4) variant in the proband. Case 1 carried a novel homozygous nonsense variant, c.836T > G (p.L279X), in exon 8. This variant was absent from population frequency database (gnomAD, Iranome) and was also not reported in ClinVar, supporting its novelty. Segregation analysis among the family members revealed that the parents were heterozygous carriers of the variant, consistent with autosomal recessive inheritance. The c.836T > G variant was heterozygous in proband’s sibling in family-1. Based on age of onset before one-year, proband-1 was diagnosed with early-infantile Krabbe disease.

Case 2: A 22-year-old man, the first child of consanguineous parents (Fig. 1B), showed normal developmental milestones until 10 years of age, following progressive regression in muscle force and spasticity in lower limb, without involvement of hearing and vision. MRI at 11-years-old showed symmetrical areas of abnormal signal in the periventricular white matter of the both biparietal regions and the corpus callosum at the same axial level. Additional signal changes were seen in the occipital periventricular white matter. These results suggested widespread white matter issue that tends to affect the periventricular area. The symmetric pattern and involvement of the corpus callosum pointed to possible conditions like demyelinating disorders such as multiple sclerosis, microvascular ischemic changes, or leukodystrophies. The patient’s 15-year-old brother (Fig. 2b, case IV-10) developed similar symptoms at age of eight. He had progressive visual loss, weakness of left limb, and claudication in his left foot with spasticity, although he could still walk. All these symptoms improved after he received allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) at age 11 years. Biochemical test results were not available for both brothers to include in this study. In this family, patient 2 was considered the proband, as he presented at 10–11 years of age with overt clinical manifestations and enabled the genetic diagnosis at 22 years of age. His younger sibling had developed symptoms earlier, at 8 years of age, but was described in relation to patient 2 rather than as a separate proband. WES result showed that case 2 was homozygous for known missense variant, c.578T > C (p.I193T, rs1555383498), in exon 5. This variant has an already established existing association in ClinVar, and is extremely rare (not found in the gnomAD, Iranome), in favor of a rare pathogenic allele. Segregation analysis revealed the parents as heterozygous carriers of the variants according to autosomal recessive inheritance. The c.578T > C variant was also homozygous in second affected child of family 2. Based on age of onset, 4–18 years, patient 2 and his brother were classified with juvenile-onset Krabbe disease.

Candidate GALC variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing in both probands and were found to segregate within the families. The variants were predicted to be deleterious by a robust suite of computational algorithms (BayesDel_addAF, DANN, MutationTaster, MetaLR, REVEL, and Varity) and classified as pathogenic (PM2, PM3, PP3, PS1, PP1, and PP4) according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines.

Literature review and genotypic landscape

Literature was reviewed methodically and identified 220 patients from 204 unique families (drawn from 40 published studies and the present cohort) with genetically verified KD were identified (Tables 2 and 3). The patients had a total of 99 distinct pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the GALC gene. Mutational analysis revealed several recurring mutations: the most frequent one was the large deletion c.1161+6532_polyA+9kbdel in 19 patients from non-related families of predominantly Danish (n = 11), Italian (n = 7), and Icelandic (n = 1) origin. Two missense variants also exhibited notable recurrence: (1) c.749 C > T (p.T250M) occurred in six patients, predominantly of Greek descent. (2) c.1901T > C (p.L634P) occurred in six patients, with most reports in Chinese individuals (Table 3 ).

A series of 220 patients from 204 families revealed a balanced gender distribution among cases where data were available (69 male, 69 female); gender was unspecified in 82 individuals. Geographic origin of patients was highly heterogeneous. Many cases were reported among populations of Italian (n = 60), Japanese (n = 52), Chinese (n = 15), and Danish (n = 13) descent. Additional cases were reported among diverse ethnic group, including Greek, Mexican, Indian, Canadian, Tunisian, Turkish, Iranian (Persian), Polish, Pakistani, Caucasian, Portuguese, German, Brazilian, Macedonian, Saudi, Icelandic, and Moroccan. The present study contributes two additional cases with Persian probands from Iran. Consanguinity was reported in 28 patients (including the two probands from this report), absent in 49 patients, and unreported in the remaining 136 individuals. Genetically, 51 patients were homozygous for mutant GALC alleles, and 87 were compound heterozygotes. Zygosity was unknown in the rest of the cohort. Of the 99 unique GALC variants, the mutational spectrum consisted predominantly of missense variants(n = 37), followed by frameshift (n = 9), nonsense (n = 8), and large deletion (n = 1) variants.

To date, the literature has reported infantile (93 cases), late infantile (35 cases), juvenile (26 cases), and adult (37 cases) KD phenotypes according to this classification (Tables 2 and 3). The most frequent manifestation in KD patients is spasticity, present in 42% of all patients and observed across all phenotypes. The prominent clinical features include spasticity (42%), hypotonia (37%), and severe irritability (34%), along with feeding difficulties, gastroesophageal reflux, developmental delay, optic atrophy, failure to thrive, and seizures [8]. In late-infantile patients, spasticity was the most common symptom (54%), followed by developmental delay (40%), seizure (26%), and weakness (26%). The most common presentation in juvenile-onset was decreased visual acuity (50%), spasticity (46%), and abnormal gait (31%). In adult-onset phenotypes, spasticity and abnormal gait (30%) were the most frequent symptoms, with weakness also reported in 26% of patients. Other frequent signs and symptoms described in studies include constipation, dysarthria, urinary incontinence, pneumonia, respiratory distress, nystagmus, deafness, pes cavus, macro or microcephaly, and erectile dysfunction.

Discussion

Here, we present the clinical and molecular characteristics of two patients from two unrelated Iranian consanguineous families with genetically confirmed KD. Both probands presented with hypotonia as a common initial symptom, along with epilepsy, bradycardia, spasticity, vomiting, and decreased visual acuity, which collectively led to the diagnosis of KD.

The pathogenesis of KD is a multifactorial, involving multiple interrelated mechanisms such as psychosine-induced cytotoxicity, activation of globoid cells (multinucleated macrophages), and direct neurodegeneration. Among these, psychosine accumulation is considered the dominant pathogenic mechanism. Due to deficient GALC activity, psychosine concentrations rise to cytotoxic levels, inducing profound apoptosis of myelin-producing cells, including oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system and Schwann cells in the peripheral nervous system. This cellular degeneration precipitates extensive demyelination, which underlies the clinical manifestations of KD [20].

As highlighted by Wenger et al., psychosine measurement has been proposed as a useful second-tier test to improve diagnostic accuracy [21]. While the psychosine hypothesis is well supported, recent findings suggest that it may not account for all disease manifestations, indicating the involvement of additional pathogenic mechanisms [22, 23]. The precise molecular basis of psychosine toxicity remains incompletely understood. The most recent hypothesis proposes that psychosine acts as a detergent, disrupting lipid raft structure and plasma membrane integrity, ultimately inducing apoptotic cell death in oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells [24–28].

Consistent with the importance of early detection, Guenzel et al. reported that measuring psychosine levels in combination with GALC enzyme activity allows reliable differentiation between infantile and late-onset forms, and helps to avoid false positives in newborn screening programs. Complementarily to this, Escolar et al. found that umbilical cord blood transplantation, when done in pre-symptomatic infants with the infantile form, can greatly improve survival and maintain neurodevelopmental outcomes [29]. While these studies mainly focus on biochemical or therapeutic aspects instead of the specific distribution of clinical features, they emphasize the same main point: early and precise diagnosis is essential for improving patient outcomes. Our phenotypic data add to this by offering a clear description of symptom patterns, which can help with timely recognition and direct targeted diagnostic testing and intervention.

Classically, the definitive diagnosis of KD has relied upon neuropathological examinations demonstrating the presence of the distinctive globoid cells. These characteristic multinucleated phagocytic cells contain a distended cytoplasm, enlarged lysosomes, unique galactosylceramide (GalCer) inclusions, and remnants of partially digested myelin [28, 30–32]. The cellular origin of globoid cells has been debated, with evidence supporting derivation from both infiltrating peripheral macrophages and CNS-resident microglia. It is established that GALC-deficient macrophages undergo a transformation into globoid cells, acquiring a pro-inflammatory molecular phenotype that contributes to the cycles of demyelination, axonal degeneration, and neuroinflammation, which define the disease pathology [33, 34]. Recent evidence suggests that pathology is not limited to myelin-producing cells. GALC is also highly expressed in neurons, and its deficiency can lead to direct neuronal damage—a “neuron-autonomous” effect—that occurs independently of, or prior to, significant demyelination and contribute to the overall neurodegeneration [23, 35–39].

In this report, biallelic variants in the GALC gene were identified: a novel homozygous LoF nonsense variant, c.836T > G (p.L279X), in the proband-1, and a previously known homozygous missense variant, c.578T > C (p.I193T), in the proband-2 and his affected brother (Fig. 1, IV-10). The variant c.836T > G is stop-gain mutation resulting in premature termination of protein translation, leading to loss of the C-terminal domain and major portions of the protein, consistent with the clinical phenotype observed in proband 1 (Figs. 1a and 2b, and 2d).

Bioinformatic analysis of p.I193T indicated that substitution of the wild-type isoleucine (I) residue with threonine (T) reduces the side chain volume, as threonine is smaller than isoleucine (Fig. 2b and c). In addition, the wild-type residue exhibits greater hydrophobicity compared to the mutant, suggesting that the mutation might disrupt hydrophobic interactions within the protein. Such disruption could create a cavity or destabilize the protein’s structural integrity. Sequence analysis revealed that the wild-type residue is typically conserved at this site in homologous sequences, although variability has been reported, indicating that the mutation may be tolerated in some contexts. Nevertheless, the mutant residue lies near a highly conserved region within a functionally critical domain. This domain directly interacts with another domain of considerable functional importance, and degradation of these interactions may impair protein conformation or dynamism, thereby altering biological activity.

The phenotypic variation in the two probands clearly underscores the role of type of mutation type in affecting protein structure and functional capability, thereby influencing course of disease and age of symptoms onset. In family-1, the causal mutation was identified as a chain-terminating variant, which most likely results in truncated or absent protein products due to early termination of translation. Such mutations typically cause complete loss of protein function, leading to clinical symptoms manifesting at a younger age.

In contrast to proband 1, proband 2 and his affected brother carried a missense variant, p.I193T, which a single amino acid is replaced. This mutation has the potential to produce a wide range of protein structural and functional changes, from mild to severe. However, in most cases, a substantial degree of protein function is preserved, thereby postponing disease onset to a later age. Several studies have suggested that the presence of hypomorphic variants, either in homozygous or compound heterozygous state, may contribute to a milder clinical presentation of Krabbe disease [40, 41]. Consistent with previous reports, including Madsen et al. (2019), patients carrying at least one hypomorphic allele often show later onset and slower progression compared to those with two fully deleterious variants [40]. Our case supports this correlation, as the identified variant appears to act as a hypomorphic allele, potentially explaining the relatively attenuated phenotype observed. In family 2, the proband’s affected sibling (second child, aged 15) had undergone hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for similar clinical symptoms and had transient neurological features. This finding further supports the evidence that the c.578T > C genotype correlates with the disease phenotype in this family.

Wenger et al. reported over 260 GALC variants worldwide, and disease severity largely depends on the combination of alleles, with even minimal residual activity sufficient to delay onset and alter clinical features. Our patients (c.836T > G and c.578T > C) illustrate this variability and contribute to the growing recognition of novel mutations in non-European populations such as Iran. Clinical and genetic information detailed in this study emphatically confirms their pathogenicity, demonstrated by patient’s homozygous state with consistent KD phenotype, family history of a second child with similar symptoms and homozygous state of the variant, segregation of the disease within the family, absence of the candid variant in healthy siblings, and concordant predictions of harmful effect by multiple bioinformatic tools. In accordance with the ACMG guidelines, both variants were classified as pathogenic, confirming the molecular cause of the disease in our patients.

Conclusion

This report presents two Iranian patients with KD, one with an infantile and the other with a juvenile presentation, who harbored a novel LoF nonsense variant and a rare homozygous missense variant in GALC, respectively. These findings expand the recognized genetic and phenotypic spectrum of Krabbe disease, particularly within the underrepresented Iranian population. While our literature review highlights broad genotype-phenotype trends, it also highlights the extensive clinical heterogeneity of the disorder. Our combined results demonstrate a wide geographical distribution of cases, with the highest prevalence reported in European and Asian populations, and an equal gender distribution. Further investigations integrating molecular, biochemical (including psychosine), and neuroimaging data from diverse populations are required to improve prognostic predictions and guide targeted treatment strategies.
