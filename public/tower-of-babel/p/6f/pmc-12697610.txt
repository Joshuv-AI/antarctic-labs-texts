Cervical Internal Carotid Artery Plaque Composition and Chronic White Matter Disease in Patients with Noncardioembolic Stroke: A Multicenter Analysis

Thon, Timothy Carroll, Karan Patel, Mary Penckofer, Michael Dubinski, Lucas Garfinkel, Omnea Elgendy, Sofia Mazuera, Aditya Jhaveri, Sachin Kothari, Harsh Desai, Matthew M. ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDCerebral white matter disease (WMD) may result from the accumulation of silent embolic brain infarcts in the setting of subclinical, nonstenotic cervical carotid atherosclerosis. The contribution of cervical plaque to the burden of WMD is not well established.METHODSA multicenter, retrospective cohort of consecutive adult patients with stroke due to cervical carotid atherostenosis (>50% luminal stenosis), small vessel disease, or cryptogenic mechanism with unilateral hemispheric stroke was queried. Maximum cervical carotid plaque thickness was used to predict higher grade WMD (Fazekas grade 2–3 versus 0–1) in unadjusted logistic regression, stratified by quartile of interside plaque (mean total plaque in axial dimension of the left and right cervical carotid arteries), and adjusted for age, stroke mechanism, atherosclerotic risk factors, and clustering by site.RESULTSOf the 375 included patients, the median age was 66 years (interquartile range 58–74), 170 (45.3%) were female, and the median interside cervical internal carotid artery plaque thickness was 1.8 mm (interquartile range 0.2‐3.2). Compared with patients in the lowest quartile of interside plaque (<0.2 mm), those in higher quartiles had higher grade WMD (Q3 adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.44, 95% CI, 1.09–1.89; Q4 aOR 1.85, 95% CI, 1.37–2.50). The association with higher grade WMD persisted in a sensitivity analysis considering interside plaque thickness as a continuous variable (adjusted incidence rate ratio/1 mm plaque 1.09, 95% CI, 1.02–1.17). The effect was preserved across stroke mechanisms, sex, and infarct pattern (cortical versus subcortical); however, younger patients had a stronger association between plaque thickness and WMD, whereas the eldest patients had no association (P interaction <0.01).CONCLUSIONSIn this cohort of patients with noncardioembolic stroke, greater interside cervical carotid plaque thickness was strongly associated with greater WMD. This association supports a potential role of subclinical cervical carotid artery atherosclerosis as a contributor to WMD, which may represent the accumulation of silent brain infarcts.

Cerebral white matter disease (WMD) has been historically attributed to lipohyalinosis affecting small intracranial arteries, causing progressive hypoperfusion via ischemic demyelination. 1 As a consequence, progressive white matter dysfunction accounts for the second most common form of dementia (vascular dementia) 2 and is also a prominent contributor to Alzheimer disease‐related cognitive impairment. 3 There is modest, but limited, evidence suggesting the benefit of blood pressure control and statin use for the prevention of WMD and vascular dementia. 4 In addition to ischemic demyelination or hypoperfusion as contributors to progressive WMD, silent brain infarcts (SBIs) or covert emboli from other potential embolic sources may also contribute to progressive white matter injury. Among these sources, nonstenotic plaque <50% luminal stenosis of the cervical internal carotid artery (ICA) has been reported in higher frequency ipsilateral to acute cerebral infarcts, typically classified as cryptogenic 5 , 6 , 7 and perhaps strokes attributed to small vessel disease 8 , 9 (although this is controversial 5 , 10 , 11 ). Such nonstenotic plaque is not only associated with incident stroke 12 but contributes to SBIs 8 , 13 and progressive WMD. 14 Whether ICA plaque (or plaque subcomponents) contributes to WMD in the absence of luminal stenosis warrants an updated understanding.

In this study, we sought to investigate the independent relationship between total cervical ICA plaque and WMD in patients at high risk of cerebral ischemia (eg, those with acute infarction). By including patients with a wide range of ICA stenosis (including those with >50% stenosis), among whom acute stroke could be potentially related to cervical ICA atherosclerosis, we aimed to estimate the association between carotid plaque thickness, luminal stenosis, and WMD. We hypothesized that greater interside plaque diameter (mean total plaque in the axial dimension of the left and right cervical carotid arteries) would be associated with a higher grade of WMD. Further, we hypothesized this relationship would exist irrespective of the posited stroke mechanism (eg, large artery atherostenosis, small vessel disease, or cryptogenic embolism).

Patient Population

Consecutive adult patients (age ≥18) diagnosed with acute ischemic stroke and treated at 2 Comprehensive Stroke Centers were eligible for inclusion (site 1: July 2023–April 2024; site 2: July 2019–May 2022). Patients were excluded if they underwent endarterectomy or stenting of either cervical carotid artery prior to the index stroke and were included if they had a primary stroke mechanism that was attributed to cervical carotid atherostenosis, small vessel disease, or was of cryptogenic origin despite comprehensive neurodiagnostic testing (eg, embolic stroke of undetermined source 16 ). Patients with stroke due to cardioembolism, intracranial arterial stenosis >50% proximal to the infarct, or other defined mechanisms were excluded. A comprehensive neurodiagnostic workup included computed tomography angiogram of the neck and head, transthoracic echocardiography, electrocardiography, and a minimum of 24 hours of continuous cardiac telemetry. For this analysis, patients must also have undergone magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain within 72 hours of stroke, with Fazekas grade assessed by a board‐certified vascular neurologist. The most likely stroke etiology was defined according to modified TOAST (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) criteria 17 : (1) cervical artery atherostenosis if >50% luminal stenosis (or ulceration) of the carotid artery proximal to the territory of infarction; (2) small vessel disease if the infarction was a singular, subcortical diffusion‐weighted imaging lesion affecting the cerebral white matter and/or deep grey hemispheric structures and <2.0 mm in maximal diameter on diffusion‐weighted imaging; and (3) cryptogenic if the mechanism could not be identified after a comprehensive inpatient evaluation. 16

Exposures

Atherosclerotic plaques of the left and right cervical ICAs 2 cm above and below the carotid bifurcation were segmented from axial source computed tomography angiogram images of included patients. The maximum axial diameter of atherosclerotic plaque and the maximum calcified plaque thickness of each vessel were measured manually by board‐certified vascular neurologists as previously described. 5 , 6 , 7 Soft plaque thickness was calculated as the difference between total and calcified plaque thickness. The primary exposure was the mean total plaque thickness of the left and right sides (“interside plaque thickness”). This was stratified according to quartiles owing to the anticipated high frequency of patients with minimal or no cervical atherosclerotic plaque and the magnitude of association between plaque thickness and the primary outcome being greatest for extremes of difference in plaque thickness (eg, a nonlinear relationship). Ipsilateral and contralateral plaque thickness (and calcified and soft plaque component thicknesses) were also assessed as continuous measurements in sensitivity analyses.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome was the grade of white matter disease according to the Fazekas scale, 18 assessed by a vascular neurologist with >5 years of experience grading WMD. Because interside variations in WMD (eg, left side, right side) were not quantified in this study, a holistic approach to Fazekas grading was used, such that the score was selected that best represented the totality of bihemispheric disease—for example, an average of WMD burden between each side—as the score was originally described. Although there is interside variability in WMD burden (and potentially Fazekas grade) as it relates to distinct, regional anatomic factors (including carotid disease), this variability is small and unlikely to be captured by interhemispheric differences in the crude Fazekas grading. Although the Fazekas grade of the entire supratentorial white matter was selected as the outcome in this study and may lead to a small type II error, we dichotomized the Fazekas score as 0–1 versus 2–3 to maximize sensitivity of comparisons across Fazekas grade (where there are small differences between grades 0–1, and 2–3 are traditionally classified as severe WMD with confluent or nearly confluent microvascular changes 19 , 20 ). This approach was also determined a priori, given the interside variability in WMD burden, which may have yielded single‐point differences in Fazekas grade between sides.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize population and subgroup characteristics, with categorical data described using frequency counts and proportions. Nonnormally distributed continuous data are summarized using medians with interquartile range (IQR), with confirmation of normality of distribution using histograms and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between‐group comparisons were made using the chi‐square test (or Fisher's exact test, when contingency table cell counts were ≤5) or the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous data. Matched data (paired carotid comparisons) were analyzed using McNemar's test for binary comparisons and the signed rank test for nonnormally distributed continuous data.

The association between the primary exposure (quartile of interside cervical ICA plaque) with the primary outcome of Fazekas grade (0–1 versus 2–3) was assessed using unadjusted binary logistic regression. This model was subsequently adjusted for ipsilateral cervical ICA stenosis, age, sex, stroke etiology, diabetes, prior stroke, and tobacco use, and the model was clustered by site. Other vascular risk factors with variance inflation factors >10, such as coronary artery disease, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, were excluded due to multicollinearity; however, age and history of prior stroke were retained in the model despite high collinearity because of how strongly they are associated with carotid plaque and WMD. Effect estimates are summarized using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs.

Two subsequent sensitivity analyses were performed. In the first sensitivity analysis, an ordered logit model was fitted to estimate the association between interside plaque stratified by quartile, using the lowermost quartile as the referent, for the ordered outcome of the Fazekas scale. This model was adjusted for the aforementioned covariates, clustered by site, and repeated across subgroups according to stroke etiology (cervical carotid atherostenosis, small vessel disease, and cryptogenic). The proportional odds assumption was confirmed using the Brant test of parallel regression. In a second sensitivity analysis, a modified Poisson regression model was fitted for the exposure of mean interside plaque as a continuous variable as a predictor of higher Fazekas grade (2–3 versus 0–1), with adjustment for the aforementioned variables and clustering by site. Effect estimates are summarized using incidence rate ratios with 95% CI. Analyses were repeated across key subgroups, including age (stratified by tertile), sex, stroke etiology (cervical carotid atherostenosis, small vessel disease, cryptogenic), and presence versus absence of cortical diffusion‐weighted imaging lesion. Heterogeneity of association was assessed using multiplicative interaction terms.

For all analyses, missing data were not imputed. No a priori sample size calculation was done due to the exploratory nature of the study. All tests were performed at the 2‐sided level with an alpha (significance level) set at 0.05. No adjustments were made for multiple hypothesis testing, and results should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating. All analyses were performed using STATA v18.0 (College Station, TX).

RESULTS

Of the 2567 patients admitted for acute ischemic stroke with an atherosclerotic or cryptogenic stroke mechanism, 375 met the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). The median age of the cohort was 66 years (IQR 58–74), and 170 (45.3%) patients were female; the median overall total plaque thickness was 1.8 mm (IQR 0.2–3.2 mm). Patients with higher‐grade interside plaque thickness were generally older with a higher prevalence of atherosclerotic vascular risk factors, including tobacco use, dyslipidemia, coronary artery disease, and hypertension (Table 1). Proportionally more patients with higher interside thickness were classified as having a stroke attributable to >50% cervical carotid atherostenosis (median 80% luminal stenosis, IQR 5%–99%, for patients in Q4 of interside plaque thickness).

When considering all grades of the Fazekas scale, rather than dichotomizing by high versus low grade, greater interside plaque was observed in patients with increasingly severe WMD (Figure 2). In the first sensitivity analysis considering the Fazekas grade as an ordered outcome from 0–3, each higher quartile of interside cervical ICA plaque was strongly associated with a higher grade of WMD using the Fazekas scale (compared with Q1, ORQ2 2.33, 95% CI, 1.36–4.00; ORQ3 3.35, 95% CI, 1.94–5.80; ORQ4 3.33, 95% CI, 1.95–5.70). Although the strength of the association attenuated after multivariable adjustment and with clustering by site, the relationship remained statistically significant and exhibited a clearer exposure‐response (adjusted ORQ2 1.22, 95% CI, 1.00–1.49; ORQ3 1.93, 95% CI, 1.19–3.11; ORQ4 2.21, 95% CI, 1.72–2.84; Figure 3). The ordered association between higher interside plaque and higher Fazekas grade was driven by the subgroup of patients with cryptogenic stroke (n = 229 patients), where a strong exposure‐response was observed. The effect estimates for the subgroup of patients with cervical carotid atherostenosis were extreme (OR >1 000 000) and are not reported due to lack of precision and probable confounding related to the magnitude of association between inter‐side plaque and diagnosis of cervical carotid atherostenosis.

In the second sensitivity analysis, in which the association between interside plaque was assessed as a continuous measure, greater total interside plaque remained strongly and independently associated with high‐grade WMD (Table 2). The association between interside plaque and high‐grade WMD appeared driven by the amount of calcified plaque (adjusted incidence rate ratio 1.14/mm plaque, 95% CI, 1.04–1.25) as opposed to the noncalcified/soft plaque.

In the subgroup analyses, age significantly interacted with interside plaque for the primary outcome of high‐grade WMD such that the relationship between plaque and WMD was greatest for patients in the youngest age tertiles (P interaction<0.01; Table 3). There were no significant interactions with respect to sex, stroke mechanism, or infarct pattern being cortical versus subcortical. Notably, there were significant associations between interside plaque and WMD in patients with acute stroke attributed to small vessel disease (on the basis of radiographic measurements, adjusted OR 1.10/mm plaque, 95% CI, 1.04–1.17) and in patients with acute subcortical infarcts (adjusted OR 1.10/mm plaque, 95% CI, 1.03–1.76), the majority of whom were not diagnosed with acute stroke due to small vessel disease (n = 157 with nonlacunar etiologies, 65.4%).

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter analysis of manually segmented cervical ICA plaques, we found that greater plaque thickness was strongly and independently associated with higher‐grade, chronic WMD on MRI. This relationship was observed in patients with acute ischemic stroke in the territory of cervical carotid plaque after accounting for the degree of carotid luminal stenosis. Moreover, the association was observed in patients with acute lacunar infarcts and in patients with stroke due to a cryptogenic mechanism. The relationship was strongest when comparing the uppermost quartile of inter‐side plaque against the lowermost quartile of interside plaque, and an exposure‐response was observed in both the primary and sensitivity analyses evaluating interside plaque as a continuous measurement. Because we restricted inclusion of patients to those with noncardioembolic and possible (or probable) atherosclerotic stroke mechanisms, plaque in the cervical ICAs supplying the acutely infarcted cerebral hemispheres represents potentially clinically relevant plaque.

As in prior studies, we have found nonstenotic cervical ICA plaque is associated with a strong risk of cerebrovascular disease, which not only includes a higher prevalence of symptomatic infarcts in the territory of greater ipsilateral ICA plaque 6 but also WMD (which may represent SBI related to plaque emboli). Nonstenotic cervical ICA plaque is thought to account for nearly 1 in 5 cases of embolic stroke of undetermined source 21 and likely accounts for more of these events in younger patient populations. 7 Even for patients with stroke due to cardioembolism 22 and small vessel disease, 23 there is clinically relevant proximal ICA plaque. The accumulation of SBIs and WMD in patients at risk of stroke from nearly any mechanism is associated with increased risk of future stroke and dementia, with a 5‐fold greater risk of dementia when compared with nonstroke controls. 24 Certainly, much of these white matter changes can be attributed to ischemic demyelination 25 and lipohyalinosis 26 ; however, atherothrombosis has been observed to underlie up to one quarter of these lesions, 27 with parent artery atherosclerosis being more commonly observed in patients with advanced microvascular changes. 28 , 29

Among key subgroups, we observed a significant interaction with respect to patient age when stratified by tertiles, such that interside plaque independently predicted higher grade WMD for the younger and middle‐aged groups. We suspect that the lack of association between WMD and carotid plaque among older patients is attributed to other contributors of WMD as part of the aging process, including accumulation of silent (or symptomatic) brain infarcts related to atrial fibrillation, 30 Alzheimer disease‐related pathology, 18 and other processes. Although the strength of the association between plaque and WMD burden was greatest for young people for whom Alzheimer disease and other pathologies would be less common, a nonsignificant association between carotid plaque and WMD persisted in the oldest tertile. White matter lesions are present in 10%–20% of the population at 60 years old and exceed 90% prevalence in individuals >80 years. 31 For younger patients, cervical carotid atherosclerosis may be a driving contributor to the earlier onset of WMD. Among other key subgroups, there was no significant interaction between inter‐side plaque and sex, stroke etiology, and infarct pattern for the outcome of Fazekas grade 2–3 WMD.

Our study has a few notable limitations. First, the data are retrospective and analyses were observational, which precludes a definitive causal association between cervical ICA plaque and WMD. Furthermore, systemic atherosclerotic disease could cause both nonstenotic plaque and ipsilateral WMD independently, which would need to be tested prospectively or following an intervention. Second, regional and lateralizing plaque and WMD were not directly compared. The grade of bilateral WMD using the Fazekas scale was compared against the mean of left and right cervical ICA plaque diameters. Although there is usually a small amount of interhemispheric variability in WMD for a given patient, there is a low probability of an increase or decrease in Fazekas grade between sides due to the low spatial sensitivity of the tool. In contrast, there may be considerable variability in the amount of cervical ICA plaque owing to local factors such as carotid bifurcation angle, 32 as an example. That said, we observed only a small difference in total plaque thickness between sides (median difference 0.5 mm, or 26% of the plaque thickness of the cervical ICA proximal to the infarct territory). Although the lack of interrater reliability for plaque and WMD represents another limitation in this analysis, previous studies 21 (including those we have conducted 33 ) indicate substantial interrater reliability for axial carotid plaque measurements and for Fazekas grading. 34 In addition, the crudeness of WMD assessment using the semiquantitative Fazekas score precludes a more precise estimation of the relationship between WMD and carotid atherosclerosis. A more sophisticated, quantitative imaging of plaque volumes and WMD burden would be necessary to determine interhemispheric differences in WMD based on proximal plaque composition, and this analysis is planned. Ultimately, estimation of these relationships based on average interside measurements could have contributed to a type II error; however, we still observed significant, robust associations in the primary and in sensitivity analyses. Using these preliminary data, a more sophisticated segmentation analysis of volumetric plaque subcomponents and WMD (stratified by side) is planned and should better illustrate the relationship between proximal ICA atherosclerotic disease, white matter changes, and possibly the accumulation of SBIs as a mediator of this process.

Ultimately, we observed a strong, independent relationship between cervical ICA atherosclerotic plaque and WMD in a cross‐sectional cohort of patients with acute, noncardioembolic infarcts. The relationship exhibited an exposure‐response, with greater interside plaque being associated with higher grade WMD according to the Fazekas score, with the strongest association observed in patients with cryptogenic stroke and in young or middle‐aged patients with stroke. These relationships were illustrated using a simple, axial measurement of total ICA plaque; however, high‐resolution MRI and other noninvasive imaging modalities may facilitate identification of high‐risk plaques. Perhaps most concerning is the finding that the majority of patients included in this study had nonstenotic plaque (<50% luminal stenosis, 83% of the cohort), and the relationship with chronic WMD remains preserved in the absence of carotid steno‐occlusive disease. With this in mind, cervical ICA atherosclerosis not only serves as a possible risk factor for WMD but may represent a target for intervention to prevent WMD and the long‐term neurocognitive complications. These findings warrant validation using a more sophisticated segmentation analysis of plaque subcomponents of the cervical as well as intracranial arteries and WMD, which is currently planned.
