Predictive and prognostic value of genetic aberrations of EGFR-TKI treatment: a real-world analysis of EGFR-mutant non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yuxin Cheng, Ye Liu, Xin Lv, Fufeng Wang, Min Wei, Jiani Yin, Chunhong He, Shu Su, Fangjun Chen, Lifeng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEpidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are the standard first-line therapy for patients with EGFR-mutant (EGFRm) non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Although many patients benefit from targeted therapies, a significant proportion of patients experience suboptimal outcomes. The mechanisms underpinning this poor efficacy and the prognostic factors influencing outcomes remain insufficiently explored. This study aims to identify the genetic and clinical biomarkers that influence treatment efficacy in patients with advanced EGFRm NSCLC.MethodsOverall, 102 patients were enrolled. The clinical data of 77 patients with EGFRm NSCLC were retrospectively analyzed in a discovery cohort. Next-generation sequencing was performed using a lung cancer panel consisting of 139 genes. An external cohort of 690 patients with genetically profiled tissue samples from the cBioPortal database was used for external validation.ResultsIn the discovery cohort, the median progression-free survival (PFS) was 17.20 months and the median overall survival (OS) was 37.47 months. A history of smoking [hazard ratio (HR) 2.25, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.99–5.12, P=0.046] and male sex (HR 2.18, 95% CI: 1.03–4.60, P=0.04) were significantly associated with poor outcomes. Treatment with third-generation EGFR-TKIs significantly prolonged PFS compared with first- and second-generation TKIs (HR 3.01, 95% CI: 1.17–7.73, P=0.02). EGFR copy number variations predicted shorter PFS across the different TKI generations. Patients with TP53 mutations exhibited poor OS (HR 8.74, 95% CI: 1.07–71.21, P=0.02), whereas those with CTNNB1 mutations exhibited good OS (HR 0.13, 95% CI: 0.02–1.02, P=0.02). In addition, higher maximum somatic allele fraction was correlated with a poor prognosis. The findings were validated in the external cohort.ConclusionsThis study identified key biomarkers associated with the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs and survival in patients with EGFRm NSCLC, thereby providing new insights for personalized therapeutic strategies based on genetic risk stratification.Keywords: Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (EGFR-TKI), biomarker, CTNNB1, EGFR copy number variation (EGFR CNV)

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases (1). Among the types of NSCLC, advanced lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) accounts for a substantial proportion. However, around 80% of patients with NSCLC are diagnosed at the advanced stage of the disease (2). The epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) has been shown to have a higher frequency of somatic mutations in Chinese patients with NSCLC compared with data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (38.3% vs. 14.0%, respectively) (3). The advent of EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) has remarkably improved survival outcomes for patients with EGFR-mutant (EGFRm) NSCLC compared with conventional chemotherapy (4,5). Although a small subset of patients exhibit a minimal initial response, most eventually develop secondary resistance after initial robust responses. This underscores the need to unravel the genetic and clinical factors that influence the efficacy of TKIs for EGFRm NSCLC. Previous studies evaluating the mechanisms of acquired resistance have focused on secondary mutations and the activation of bypass and downstream signaling pathways (6,7). Nonetheless, our understanding of the mechanisms underpinning the poor treatment efficacy remains limited.

The integration of next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology into clinical practice has revealed concurrent mutations that exist alongside mutations in EGFR (8). Therefore, it is not only essential to investigate the mechanisms underpinning the limited response to treatment, but it is also important to determine the clinical implications of non-EGFR alterations within the context of EGFR-TKI therapy. Previous studies have shown that patients with EGFRm NSCLC with concurrent mutations, such as PIK3CA and TP53 mutation, were associated with poor progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) when treated with EGFR-TKIs (9,10). These findings indicate that concurrent genetic aberrations might serve as potential biomarkers for determining treatment outcomes in patients with EGFRm NSCLC undergoing targeted therapies, highlighting the complexity of the treatment resistance mechanisms and the necessity for personalized treatment approaches (11).

In this study, we used NGS to perform a real-world analysis of the genomic and clinical data of patients with advanced EGFRm NSCLC. We also aim to perform a similar analysis in an external cohort to validate our findings. By characterizing the molecular landscape, we aim to stratify patients based on genetic risk factors, thereby elucidating the impact of these genetic risk factors on survival outcomes related to EGFR-TKI therapy. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2024-2159/rc).

Methods

Study design and participant enrollment in the discovery cohort

The clinical and genetic data of 102 patients with NSCLC treated at Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital were retrospectively reviewed and analyzed. Patients were excluded if they had not received EGFR-TKI therapy, lacked sufficient clinical information, or had not undergone EGFR mutation profiling. The discovery cohort included 77 patients with advanced EGFRm NSCLC who possessed sufficient clinical and genetic data. PFS was defined as the interval from treatment initiation to disease progression or death, while OS spanned from treatment initiation to death by any cause. All tissue and plasma samples were subjected to NGS using a lung cancer panel consisting of 139 genes at a College of American Pathologists-accredited, Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments-certified, and ISO 15189-accredited laboratory (Nanjing Geneseeq Technology Inc., Nanjing, China). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital (No. 2021-560-01) and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

DNA extraction, quantification, and library preparation

Genomic DNA from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples and peripheral blood samples was extracted using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and QIAamp Circulating Nucleic Acid Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), respectively. DNA was qualified using the Nanodrop 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), and its concentration was quantified by Qubit 3.0 utilizing the dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Life Technology, Carlsbad, USA). Subsequent library preparation was performed using the KAPA HyperPrep Kit (KAPA Biosystems, Wilmington, USA). Briefly, fragmented genomic DNA were subjected to end-repairing, A-tailing, indexing with adapters, and magnetic bead DNA purification (Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA). Target enrichment was performed using customized xGen lockdown probes covering 139 lung cancer-related genes, followed by sequencing on the HiSeq 4000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Targeted sequencing and variant calling

Base calling was performed on bcl2fastq V.2.16.0.10 to generate sequence reads in FASTQ format. Quality control was performed using Trimmomatic software (12). High-quality reads were then mapped to the human genome (hg19, GRCh37) using Burrows-Wheeler Aligner V.0.7.12 with the maximal exact matches algorithm under default parameters to create SAM files. SAM files were converted into compressed BAM files using Picard V.1.119 and then sorted according to chromosome coordinates. The Genome Analysis Toolkit V.3.4.0 (https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/) was used to locally realign the BAM files at intervals with insertion/deletion (indel) mismatches and recalibrate the base quality scores of reads in the BAM files. Varscan V.2.4.0 was also used to call somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels. Somatic gene fusion variants were called by Delly with default parameters. Variant annotation was performed using ANNOVAR, ClinVar, HGMD, LOVD, 1000 Genomes Project (1000g), ExAC, and gnomAD. Variants with a frequency of ≥1% in any of these databases, as well as in our in-house database, were removed. Somatic copy number variations (CNVs) were identified using CNVkit with default parameters and were categorized as gains or losses when the fold change was ≥1.6 and ≤0.6, respectively. SNVs and indels with a variant allele frequency of >1% for tissues and >0.1% for circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), and at least three unique mutant reads, were retained to establish a further mutational landscape. Furthermore, matched white blood cell DNA was used as the negative control to remove germline variants and clonal hematopoiesis in tumor and plasma samples.

Validation cohort information

The genomic and clinical data of patients with advanced NSCLC used in the validation cohort are available from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/study/summary?id=nsclc_ctdx_msk_2022) and are publicly accessible (13). Following the removal of duplicate samples, 1,119 plasma samples and 690 tissue samples were included in the subsequent analyses. Within the tissue cohort, 168 individuals with common EGFR exon 19 deletions (19del) and L858R were identified as patients with EGFRm NSCLC.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative variables are expressed as medians and ranges. Co-occurrence and the exclusivity of gene alterations were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test. The Kaplan-Meier method was used for the survival analyses. The Kaplan-Meier curves were compared using the log-rank test, and hazard ratios (HRs) were calculated using Cox proportional-hazards models. Cox proportional-hazards models were also used for multivariate survival analysis to estimate HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The Mann-Kendall trend test was used to assess the impact of the maximum somatic allele fraction (MSAF) on OS. Two-tailed P≤0.05 was considered statistically significant, unless indicated otherwise. All statistical analyses were performed using R software V.4.2.3.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the discovery cohort

The discovery cohort comprised 77 patients with advanced EGFRm NSCLC who had undergone EGFR-TKI therapy. The study design is depicted in Figure S1A. The median PFS (mPFS) was 17.20 months (95% CI: 16.17–23.37) (Figure S1B), and the median OS (mOS) was 37.47 months (95% CI: 29.43–79.17) (Figure S1C).

In the discovery cohort, fifty patients (64.9%) were female, with a median age of 67 years (45–98 years) (Table 1). Eleven patients (14.3%) reported a history of smoking. LUAD was diagnosed in 72 patients (93.5%). Stage IV disease was observed in 74 patients (96.1%). Bone metastases occurred in 41 patients (53.2%) and brain metastases in 23 patients (29.9%). Seventy-two patients (93.5%) received first-line EGFR-TKI therapy. Monotherapy with targeted therapies was used in 62 patients (80.5%). Twenty-four patients (31.2%) had the EGFR 19del mutation and 21 (27.3%) had the EGFR 21858R mutation.

Associations between clinical characteristics and prognosis in the discovery cohort

We first evaluated the associations between clinical characteristics and prognosis within the discovery cohort (n=77). A history of smoking was associated with shorter PFS, with an mPFS of 15.20 months for smokers compared with 19.30 months for non-smokers (HR 2.25, 95% CI: 0.99–5.12, P=0.046) (Figure 1A,1B). Moreover, patients treated with third-generation EGFR-TKIs exhibited longer PFS than those treated with first- or second-generation TKIs (mPFS 26.37 vs. 16.17 months, HR 3.01, 95% CI: 1.17–7.73, P=0.02) (Figure 1A-1C). Additionally, male patients demonstrated shorter OS than female patients, with an mOS of 29.40 months compared to 45.63 months, respectively (HR 2.18, 95% CI: 1.03–4.60, P=0.04) (Figure 1D,1E).

Associations between the mutational landscape and prognosis in the discovery cohort

To evaluate the impact of genetic alterations on prognosis, we analyzed 48 tissue samples from patients with EGFRm NSCLC. The most frequently observed concurrent mutations were identified in TP53 (60.4%), RB1 (12.5%), and CTNNB1 (12.5%). CNVs were commonly detected in EGFR (18.8%) and MDM2 (10.4%) (Figure 2). Genes mutated in three or more patients were included in the association analysis between mutations and therapeutic outcomes. No significant difference in PFS was observed between patients with EGFR 19del and those with L858R mutations (Figure S2A). However, a trend toward longer OS was noted in patients with EGFR 19del (P=0.056) (Figure S2B). Patients harboring EGFR CNVs demonstrated shorter mPFS than those without (13.10 vs. 19.30 months, HR 3.06, 95% CI: 1.11–8.45, P=0.02) (Table 2, Figure 3A), consistent across various TKIs (Figure 3B). EGFR amplification was not significantly associated with clinical characteristics (Table S1), and the gene correlation results showed that EGFR amplification was not significantly associated with other gene mutations (Figure S3).

Distinct effects on prognosis were observed for TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations. Patients with TP53 mutations demonstrated shorter mOS (29.43 vs. not reached, HR 8.74, 95% CI: 1.07–71.21, P=0.02) (Table 2, Figure 3C). In particular, patients harboring alterations in exon 6 of TP53 exhibited unfavorable PFS (P<0.001) and OS (P=0.01) compared with those harboring other TP53 mutations (Table S2). By contrast, those with CTNNB1 mutations experienced longer OS than non-carriers (mOS: not reached vs. 29.43 months, HR 0.13, 95% CI: 0.02–1.02, P=0.02) (Table 2, Figure 3D). The investigation of co-occurrence and exclusivity of common gene mutations revealed no apparent mutual exclusivity between TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations (Figure S3).

Clinicopathological characteristics and prognosis in the external validation cohort

To confirm the prognostic significance of the genetic features identified in the discovery cohort, we performed a similar analysis on the data of 690 patients from the external validation cohort (13). Indeed, patients harboring TP53 mutations exhibited significantly shorter OS than those without (P=0.03) (Figure 4A). Conversely, those with CTNNB1 mutations showed a trend toward longer OS (P=0.12) (Figure 4B). Similar trends were also observed in the subset of 168 patients with EGFRm NSCLC, where TP53 mutations correlated with worse outcomes and CTNNB1 mutations correlated with more favorable outcomes (Figure 4C,4D). Further analysis showed that TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations were each associated with a complex interaction network involving distinct genes, such as ATM, MDM2, and EP300 (Figure 4E,4F). The prognostic analysis of TP53 and CTNNB1 networks yielded similar results to the respective individual genes, although these findings did not reach statistical significance (P=0.09 and P=0.08, respectively) (Figure 4F-4H). Furthermore, females exhibited a significantly better prognosis than males among the 690 patients (P=0.005) (Figure S4A), which was not replicated within the subset of 168 patients with EGFRm NSCLC (P=0.68) (Figure S4B).

Risk stratification based on TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations in the combined cohort

We conducted univariate and multivariate analyses of a combined cohort of 216 patients with EGFRm NSCLC, comprising 48 individuals from the discovery cohort and 168 from the validation cohort. The univariate analysis revealed a significant association between TP53 mutations and decreased survival (HR 1.74, 95% CI: 1.09–2.78, P=0.02). Conversely, CTNNB1 mutations were positively associated with survival (HR 0.32, 95% CI: 0.10–1.00, P=0.04). The multivariate analysis substantiated the negative impact of TP53 on survival (adjusted HR 1.68, 95% CI: 1.05–2.69, P=0.03) and suggested a survival benefit of CTNNB1 (adjusted HR 0.33, 95% CI: 0.11–1.07, P=0.06) (Table S3). These findings underscore the distinct prognostic implications of TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations.

We categorized patients into four groups based on the mutation status of TP53 and CTNNB1, and significant differences in OS were observed among these groups (P=0.03) (Figure S4C). Patients harboring CTNNB1 mutations exhibited significantly better survival outcomes than those without, irrespective of their TP53 mutation status. Building upon these findings, we stratified patients into three distinct risk groups for clarity and simplicity: low-risk (patients with CTNNB1 mutations regardless of TP53 mutation status, n=17), medium-risk (patients without CTNNB1 or TP53 mutations, n=68), and high-risk (patients with TP53 but lacking CTNNB1 mutations, n=131). Significant prognostic disparities were identified among the three risk groups (P=0.01) (Figure 5A). The low-risk group displayed the longest OS. The high-risk group had an mOS of 50.13 months, and the medium-risk group had an mOS of 33.97 months. We observed significant differences in both 5-year OS (P<0.001) and 3-year OS (P=0.002), while 2-year OS was not significantly different (P=0.054) (Figure 5B).

Associations between the MSAF and survival outcomes

The MSAF in ctDNA can serve as a surrogate for the anatomic and metabolic tumor burden. In the present study, we investigated the value of the MSAF as a non-invasive biomarker by exploring the association between the MSAF and survival outcomes. In the present study, tumor-specific mutations were identified in plasma samples from 51 patients (80.4%). Most of these mutations featured low allele fractions, with a median value of 1.5% and a range from zero to 88.4% (Figure S5A). As expected, we observed that a higher MSAF was significantly associated with unfavorable OS, but not with PFS (Figure S5B,S5C). Upon exclusion of EGFR driver mutations, the predictive relevance of the MSAF for OS became more robust using the Mann-Kendal trend test (P=0.04) (Table S4). However, no similar trend was observed for PFS. Stratification at a 1% MSAF threshold revealed that patients with a lower MSAF generally experienced more favorable outcomes than those with a higher MSAF, both prior to (P=0.002) (Figure S6A) and after (P=0.01) (Figure S6B) exclusion of EGFR driver mutations. There was no significant difference in the clinical characteristics between the different MSAFs (Table S5).

Discussion

EGFR-TKIs are the standard first-line treatment for advanced EGFRm NSCLC (14). Owing to the inevitable challenge of drug resistance with EGFR-TKIs, other treatment options are applied clinically, such as EGFR-TKIs combined anti-angiogenesis therapy (15,16), TKIs combined with chemotherapy (17), and TKIs combined with the bispecific antibody amivantamab (18). Such treatment models have been tested in phase III clinical studies (16,18,19). These combined therapies improve PFS when used as first-line therapies. However, combined therapies significantly increase the possibility of drug toxicity and increase the economic burden compared with the use of TKIs alone (20). Moreover, no statistically significant improvements in OS have yet been demonstrated with combined therapies. Therefore, identifying key biomarkers associated with the efficacy of EGFR-TKIs in patients with EGFRm NSCLC and selecting suitable patients to receive combined therapies is important to improve treatment efficacy.

This study comprehensively analyzed the genetic and clinical data of patients with advanced EGFRm NSCLC. By evaluating the prevalence and prognostic significance of genetic features, we identified the associations of EGFR amplifications, TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations, and MSAF with survival outcomes. Specifically, EGFR CNVs emerged as a negative predictor of the response to EGFR-TKIs in patients with advanced NSCLC, suggesting its potential as a predictive biomarker. Furthermore, TP53 was linked to a poorer prognosis, whereas CTNNB1 mutations were associated with more favorable outcomes.

The clinical application of molecular-targeted therapies, particularly EGFR-TKIs, has markedly improved the prognosis of patients with EGFRm NSCLC. However, poor treatment efficacy remains a critical issue. In the present study, smokers exhibited significantly poorer PFS when treated with TKIs, while no significant difference in OS was observed by smoking status or treatment. Although smoking is significantly associated with PFS, considering the sample size of this study, further validation with larger samples is needed in the future. Sex significantly influenced prognosis, with male patients typically experiencing worse outcomes, as previously reported (21). EGFR amplification has been identified previously as a prognostic factor for poor survival outcomes and a contributor to secondary resistance to EGFR-TKI therapy (22). The present study revealed that patients with EGFR amplification exhibited notably shorter PFS during TKI therapy, a trend that was consistent across various TKI therapies. This is the first demonstration that EGFR CNVs are associated with poor efficacy of TKI therapy, prompting future studies to optimize the treatment strategy.

In addition to EGFR alterations, TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations were identified as two robust biomarkers of the response to TKI therapy in the present study, underscoring their significance in the oncogenic landscape of NSCLC. TP53 mutations are widely studied as prognostic factors, but conclusions are controversial. A previous study showed that patients with TP53 co-mutations had shorter PFS and OS (23), while another study indicated that only mutations in exon 4, 7, or 8 of TP53 had a negative effect on PFS (24). In the present study, no significant difference in PFS was observed among patients with TP53 mutations, though OS was poorer. This finding was further validated in the external validation cohort. Notably, patients with mutations in exon 6 of TP53 exhibited distinct PFS and OS outcomes (25). Collectively, these findings illustrate the multifaceted role of TP53 alterations in influencing the clinical outcomes of patients with NSCLC, particularly with regard to the response to EGFR-TKI therapy.

CTNNB1 encodes β-catenin and is involved in the WNT/β-catenin pathway. It has been associated with favorable clinical outcomes and survival in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (26). High CTNNB1 expression correlates with improved prognosis in endometrial cancer, melanoma, and renal cancer, but it is associated with poorer outcomes in adrenal cortical carcinoma (27). In terms of lung cancer, previous studies have shown that patients with NSCLC who have co-mutations in NF1 and CTNNB1 have better OS (28). The prognostic significance of CTNNB1 mutations is context-dependent, varying with the type of mutation. Our findings showed that CTNNB1 mutations correlate with significantly better OS in patients with NSCLC, suggesting their potential as robust prognostic indicators.

Furthermore, our findings underscore the clinical significance of the MSAF in ctDNA as a non-invasive marker for the dynamic monitoring of patient outcomes. Specifically, higher MSAF correlated with poorer OS, as previously described (13), reinforcing the potential of the MSAF as a predictive biomarker for survival outcomes, particularly when excluding genetic variation drivers. Non-invasive methods can be used to dynamically monitor treatment efficacy and provide an avenue for timely intervention upon detection of resistance alongside established clinical biomarkers. Standardizing the MSAF threshold and integrating MSAF testing into clinical practice could facilitate the implementation of more responsive and adaptive treatment plans, promoting proactive oncology care.

Based on the above findings, we suggest that molecular markers may be useful to optimize treatment choices for patients with EGFRm NSCLC. For patients with advanced EGFRm NSCLC with CTNNB1 mutations, EGFR-TKI therapy may be sufficient as a first-line treatment. However, in patients with advanced EGFRm NSCLC harboring concomitant TP53 mutations or a high plasma MSAF, EGFR-TKIs combined with other therapies might be a better option. Meanwhile, the EGFR and c-MET bispecific antibody amivantamab combined with EGFR-TKI therapy could be the better choice for patients carrying EGFR CNVs.

Despite the relatively small sample size and non-uniform targeted therapeutic approaches, this real-world study offers a comprehensive analysis of treatment efficacy and prognostic indicators in the EGFRm NSCLC population from clinical and molecular perspectives. To our knowledge, this study is the first to explore biomarkers influencing the efficacy of EGFR-TKI therapy in the real-world setting with validation in an external cohort.

In summary, this study emphasizes the intricate interplay between genetic alterations and therapeutic efficacy in EGFRm NSCLC, offering critical insights into the prognostic and predictive value of TP53 and CTNNB1 mutations, EGFR CNVs, and MSAF. These findings advocate for a more personalized approach to treatment, integrating comprehensive genomic profiling and non-invasive monitoring to optimize clinical outcomes.

Conclusions

Identifying biomarkers that influence the efficacy of EGFR-TKI therapy in patients with EGFRm NSCLC is important for improving treatment efficacy and increasing survival time and may facilitate the design of more personalized therapeutic strategies based on genetic predictive and prognostic biomarkers.
