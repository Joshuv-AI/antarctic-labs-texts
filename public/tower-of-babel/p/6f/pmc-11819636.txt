Quantification of DNA, in dried blood, saliva tinged with blood in comparision with fresh blood for forensic identification- A pilot study
Authors: Kangan Manjunathan, Radhika Manoj Bavle, Mrinalini Menon, Reshma Venugopal, Soumya Makarla, Sankha Nair
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The important tenet of forensic science is personal identification based on DNA studies. The use of DNA has changed the specificity and sensitivity of victim identity tests associated with criminology, trauma, and natural disasters in Forensic Science. Blood is a proven source of DNA quantification for these analyses. The use of saliva, which can be acquired more easily in a simple and atraumatic method is gaining preference; as some of its advantages include – collection of larger sample numbers, easy replication of tests in duplicates and triplicates, and repetitions if advocated, can easily be performed. Though it yields a comparatively slightly lesser quantity of DNA, some of the samples – like dried blood and blood-tinged saliva, have the potential to be used in forensic sciences and provide a better result than simple saliva. Such samples are valuable sources of genetic material that can be utilized in crime and forensic investigations.The present study is one of few studies to be explored in this sphere as very few studies using blood-tinged saliva and dried blood have been conducted so far.Aims and Objectives:To determine if blood-tinged saliva or dried blood yields a good amount of DNA. To compare it with fresh blood samples for authentication.Materials and Methodology:The study groups: Group A – n = 20 normal blood samples, Group B – n = 20 of saliva-tinged blood samples and Group C – Dried Blood samples from the same subjects. DNA was isolated from these 60 samples using Xplorogen DNA isolation and extraction kit. The quality and quantity of DNA isolation were determined using a spectrophotometric technique using a Biospectrometer.Results:The extracted DNA was quantified and the yield was 83.3 ± 9.4, 55.1 ± 7.4, and 73.4 ± 8.7 μg/ml in fresh blood, dried blood, and blood-tinged saliva, respectively. Out of the 60 samples assessed, all 100% samples yielded a sufficiently good DNA content. Extraction from Blood tinged with saliva showed promising results when compared to dried blood and the levels obtained were closely comparable to whole blood (fresh).Conclusion:Blood is a standard sample for ample DNA yields in forensic studies. We conclude in this study, that blood-tinged saliva gives an equally good sample for utilization in DNA detection in those situations where such samples are traced (even at a later date) at the scene of crime, or mass disasters for forensic analysis.

INTRODUCTION

A forensic investigation, offers a variety of samples, for investigation and DNA studies. Blood, saliva, body tissues, hair, and other body fluids are all used for DNA retrieval.[1] Many times dried blood samples of blood, time, and blood-tinged saliva end up as the samples left behind on the crime scene, either on the subject or on materials like glass, tabletop, plates, edible things, or clothes. These samples can also be used as a source for DNA collection.

Blood samples are very good sources of DNA.[2] A lot of body fluids, especially saliva are being increasingly used. Saliva alone has been used for DNA retrieval with variable results in several quantitative studies.[345] A practical approach in forensic analysis by forensic odontologists or forensic experts in cases of crime or trauma leads to the availability of certain samples for DNA analysis that have not been utilized or exploited well. Blood-tinged saliva and dried samples of blood are discovered at several crime scenes for forensic analysis for utilization by a forensic expert. These can be commonly found on the victim’s body or material objects like glasses, spoons, edible things, and clothes.

Samples containing blood-tinged saliva and dried blood with passage of time or upon late discovery can also be utilized for DNA retrieval in crime scenes and are of relevance today; as they offer a novel approach or second chance for analysis.

Very few such studies using blood-tinged saliva and dried blood have been done; for observation of quantitative yields offered in DNA studies. The present study was devised to assess the use of blood-tinged saliva and dried blood as potent samples for DNA isolation and to compare them to fresh blood for authentication.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Pathology and Department of Biotechnology in collaboration.

Sample size and collection: A total of n = 60 samples were collected and analyzed under three groups.

Group A: Fresh blood; n = 20 samples

Group B: Blood-tinged saliva; n = 20 samples

Group C: Dried blood; n = 20 samples

Sample collection: The blood samples collected for routine hematological tests in the outpatient department were utilized for the study. The inclusion criteria included blood samples of a specific amount (2 ml) remaining post-analysis, drawn from males or females of the 2nd to 5th decade, with no systemic ailments, acute oral infections, or malignancies.

Blood samples were divided into three groups as mentioned above in an aseptic process.

Saliva samples from the same subjects were drawn in a sterile environment for analysis after informed consent and approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee. A sample of 1 ml was collected for preparing the samples in Group B.

Group C samples were prepared and stored for 24 hours in a sterile, uncontaminated state, before being utilized.

Samples were prepared by aliquoting specifically measured quantities in Eppendorf tubes:

Group A: comprised of n = 20 samples of 450 μl of freshly collected blood sample

Group B: comprised of n = 20 samples of 450 μl of blood-tinged saliva was used as a sample (simulating real-time) (equal quantities of blood + saliva, i.e. 225 μl of each)

Group C: comprised of n = 20 samples of 450 μl of blood, which was dried under natural conditions on a glass slide (at 25–28°C at a humidity of 70%) for 24 hours, before utilization as a sample.

The dried blood sample was collected from the glass slide using a wet cotton bud, which was wet with PBS buffer. The sample was then transferred to buffer solution to make up the volume to 450 μl for further analysis.

DNA isolation: Xplorogen DNA isolation and extraction kit [which had 5 buffers Xplorogen (XPL) 1 to 5] was used for the study.

METHODOLOGY

Sample: Quantity and quality analysis

Sample = 450 µl of blood (Sample A and C), 450 µl of blood-tinged saliva (Sample B)

1 ml of XPL1 was added to the appropriately labeled samples of each study group.

From this 900 μl was extracted and placed into a 2 ml sterile vial. To this, 700 μl of XPL1 was added to the vial containing the samples. Once it is added, the vials are vortexed gently. They were further incubated at 56°C for 30 minutes (and inverted after 15 mins), and centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 4 minutes.

900 μl of supernatant obtained was then transferred to sterile 2 ml vials. To that 1 ml of XPL2 was added and vortexed for 5 sec. 700 μl of lysate was transferred to the spin column and centrifuge at 20000 rpm for 2 minutes and the flow through was discarded. The above two steps were repeated to collect all the lysate.

Next, 600 μl of XPL3 was added to the spin column and centrifuged at 20000 rpm for 2 minutes and flow-through was discarded. To this, 600 μl of XPL4 was added and centrifuged at 20000 rpm for 2 minutes. Again the flow-through was discarded. The empty spin column was subjected to centrifugation for 5 minutes at 20000 rpm.

The spin column was then placed into a sterile 1.5 ml vial. 30 μl of XPL5 was added and centrifuged at 20000 rpm for 5 minutes. The spin column was then discarded. The elution tube was stored for further processing.

Quantification of DNA

The extracted DNA from the study samples obtained was then subjected to a BioSpectrophotometer to assess the Absorbance and optical density (OD) values.

DNA concentration

The isolated DNA was assessed by determining the optical density at 260 nm.

For this,

Blank = 990 μl of ethyl alcohol/TE buffer + 10 μl of XPL5

Sample = 10 μl of sample + 990 μl of ethyl alcohol/TE buffer

Tris EDTA = 1 M Tris base solution (100 gm) and 0.5 M EDTA (10 × TE buffer), pH 7.4–8 was used.

The absorbance and concentration values for each sample from the three groups were recorded.

DISCUSSION

The concept of validating different samples for utilization of DNA retrieval opens up a success rate of tests associated with DNA identification from crime scenes for forensic analysis.

The availability of different kinds of body tissues, and samples of body fluids demand good validation before they are used for DNA testing. Samples of dried blood and blood-tinged saliva can be discovered at a closer or later date, but it gives a second chance for DNA detection for the complete identification of individuals involved.

For a long time to come, blood samples were held as a standard for DNA analysis. DNA yield in blood samples ranged from 30 to 35 ± 12.45 μg/ml[67] in various studies.

The methods used for DNA isolation vary from Chromatography-based methods and solution-based extraction methods (organic solvents, salting out methods) to solid phase extraction methods (silica matrices, anion exchange resins, magnetic beads, chelex, and filter paper extraction); from manual to automated extraction methods.[89] Only in the last few decades, has work been done using different body fluids and tissues for forensic analysis.[1011] Saliva, as a sample is also being used for DNA retrieval[51213] though a few drawbacks have been factors like low yield, infection, and contamination[14]

This study aimed to find the utility value of different samples like blood-tinged saliva, and dried blood present at crime scenes for successful DNA isolation and analysis. The same was compared to the yield provided by potent samples like fresh blood.

A spectrophotometric method using UV spectroscopy (Eppendorf) was utilized for the quantification of DNA. The results showed a good recovery of DNA samples from all 60 samples used. A good amount of DNA could be isolated from all the samples analyzed. A 100% success rate was observed in terms of DNA isolation.

A variable range of DNA was obtained from Groups A, B, and C. The highest yield found in group A was 83.3 ± 9.4 μg/ml; since fresh blood has the highest quantity of DNA due to its cellular content. Group B (blood-tinged saliva) showed a slightly lower yield for DNA in a range of 4.5 to 167.4 μg/ml. Group C (dried blood) showed a yield of 1.62 to 142.2 μg/ml.

As the statistical analysis using One-way ANOVA and Scheffe’s test between the groups did not show any significant P value, we could infer that fairly adequate amounts of DNA can be extracted from all three study groups.

The DNA levels obtained in the present study can be comparable to Khare et al.,[5] who in their study concluded that the mean quantity of DNA obtained in saliva was 48.4 ± 8.2 μg/ml, and in blood was 142.5 ± 45.9 μg/ml, comparable to our findings.

In a similar study by Looi et al.,[15] the values obtained were slightly lower in the range of 27.4–46.8 μg/ml for blood samples and 4–32.5 μg/ml for saliva.

Stanzick et al.[6] in their study reported values of extracted DNA with a mean of 32.15 ± 12.45 μg/ml from anticoagulated blood samples and 26.92 ± 12.56 μg/ml from clotted blood, slightly lower than the levels that were obtained in group C.

Smalling et al. in their study examined the DNA levels in blood and saliva over calculated periods and found that quantifiable amounts of DNA can be recovered from dried blood, which is comparable to the present study.[1617]

The literature search did not provide any direct comparison with studies using blood-tinged saliva as a potential sample.

UV spectroscopy is a simple method, highly useful as the samples yield DNA in microgram quantities. Samples with an OD (ratio of 260/280 nm) of 1 are believed to contain approximately about 50 μg/ml dsDNA or 40 μg/ml ssDNA.[18]

More than 40% of the samples in our study groups, were within the OD range of 1.3 -1.8 which was close to the optimal range of 1.6–2 signifying the purity of the DNA, whereas some samples were below this range (Group B and C of saliva tinged blood and dried blood respectively). The reasons for this could be attributed to impurities in the sample like phenol contamination, the presence of salts, extracellular proteins, and other peptides and also handling errors during sample preparation of Group B and C.

Utilization of these samples (dried blood, blood-tinged saliva) corroborates more methods and approaches to repeat a test or allow delayed evaluation or re-evaluation of genetic material for final confirmation. Other techniques commonly used for DNA quantification include gel electrophoresis and evaluation of short tandem repeats (STR) segments, widely used, especially in forensic studies.[18]

Employing these techniques would have further strengthened the pivot of this study, but due to lack of funding these tests could not be further performed. Also, a larger sample size and using STRs to authenticate the presence of the genomic material will further validate the potential of such studies.

The study is one of the few, which uses samples like dried blood and one of the first to use blood-tinged saliva for DNA analysis. The result clearly shows that these samples can yield a significant amount of genomic DNA, therefore allowing the utilization of different kinds of samples for DNA extraction for forensic evaluation.

Numerous studies advocate the use of saliva as an alternate and effective source for DNA isolation when compared to blood. Our study is the first of its kind, which highlights the efficacy of using blood-tinged saliva as a reliable sample/source of DNA for forensic analysis.

The limitations of the study were the small sample size, though spectrophotometry is an economical, easy method of DNA quantification, a better method of DNA quantification such as gel electrophoresis, and STR segment detection would have enhanced the validity of the study.

CONCLUSION

DNA content in blood-tinged saliva and dried blood can be used for DNA studies as they give a good yield of DNA to be utilized for different kinds of forensic studies.

In the case of forensic analysis or criminal assaults, any of the media can be used in quantifying DNA.
