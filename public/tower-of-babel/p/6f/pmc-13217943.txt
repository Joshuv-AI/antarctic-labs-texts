Assessing the accuracy and educational value of ChatGPT-generated content for core topics in cardiology: a descriptive analysis at Selçuk University Cardiology Clinic
Authors: Hüseyin Tezcan, Abdullah Tunçez, Kadri Murat Gürses, Yasin Özen, Muhammed Ulvi Yalçın
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLarge language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have garnered growing attention for their potential to support medical education, including specialty areas like cardiology. However, the accuracy, guideline adherence, and pedagogical usefulness of these AI-generated materials remain incompletely characterized.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the factual accuracy, guideline alignment, and teaching utility of ChatGPT-generated responses on 15 core cardiology topics.MethodsFifteen high-yield cardiology subjects (e.g., acute coronary syndrome, heart failure, valvular disease, arrhythmias) were identified based on standard medical curricula. Each topic was queried with 10 standardized prompts (150 total). Two cardiologists independently rated each ChatGPT response across five domains (factual accuracy, completeness, clarity/readability, guideline alignment, and teaching utility) using a 5-point rubric. Representative examples of higher- and lower-performing responses were also reviewed qualitatively to illustrate recurrent strengths and common error patterns. Inter-rater reliability was assessed via Cohen’s kappa (κ), and descriptive statistics summarized performance by domain and by topic. All prompts were entered using the free web version of ChatGPT (OpenAI; GPT-4o) under standardized default settings during October–November 2024.ResultsClarity/readability (4.4 ± 0.5) was the highest-scoring domain overall, while factual accuracy (3.7 ± 0.6) showed moderate performance. Guideline alignment varied across the 15 topics, ranging from 4.2 in stable angina to 3.0 in complex pericardial diseases. Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) also had lower guideline concordance (3.1 ± 0.6). Illustrative examples indicated that lower scores were most often driven by omission of newer therapies, incomplete discussion of advanced procedural indications, and overly generic explanations in more complex subspecialty topics. Cohen’s kappa values (0.78–0.85) indicated substantial inter-rater agreement.DiscussionChatGPT provided coherent explanations suitable for introductory learning across most cardiology topics, but gaps were evident in rapidly evolving areas like advanced heart failure and congenital anomalies. These findings underscore ChatGPT’s value as a supplementary educational tool while highlighting the need for careful expert oversight in verifying content accuracy and currency. Strategies for safely integrating AI-generated materials into medical education are discussed.ConclusionsWhile ChatGPT can aid cardiology instruction through clear, concise overviews, reliance on it as a primary source risks propagation of incomplete or outdated information. Ongoing refinement of LLMs, coupled with consistent expert review, will be crucial for leveraging AI effectively in cardiology education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09141-7.Keywords: ChatGPT, Cardiology education, Large language models, AI in medicine, Medical education, Guideline adherence

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing multiple facets of healthcare, ranging from diagnostic image interpretation to virtual patient monitoring [1, 2]. In the field of medical education, AI-driven large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT have recently emerged as promising tools that generate human-like responses to diverse inquiries [3]. Educators have noted potential advantages of these models for creating quizzes, explanatory content, and interactive study materials [4].

Cardiology, which addresses some of the most prevalent and high-stakes conditions globally, demands both depth and accuracy in its educational resources [5]. Guidelines from professional bodies like the American Heart Association (AHA), American College of Cardiology (ACC), and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) are regularly updated, reflecting new evidence on diagnostics, pharmacotherapies, and interventions [6, 7]. Integrating these evolving guidelines into teaching materials can be time-consuming for faculty. From the quality of cardiovascular information on digital video-sharing platforms to AI-assisted diagnosis and medication-related clinical decision-making, the digital world and cardiovascular medicine are now increasingly intertwined [8–10] .Consequently, an automated tool like ChatGPT that offers quick, coherent overviews may prove invaluable if it can maintain accuracy and currency [11].

Despite these promising prospects, early evaluations indicate that while ChatGPT can score comparably to medical students on standardized exams, it sometimes provides incomplete or erroneous answers [12, 13]. The specialty-specific utility and pitfalls of such models remain poorly defined, especially in cardiology. To address this gap, we systematically assessed ChatGPT’s performance across 15 common cardiology topics, focusing on factual accuracy, adherence to clinical guidelines, clarity, and overall educational value.

By detailing ChatGPT’s strengths and limitations, our aim is to inform best practices for instructors who might consider incorporating AI-generated content into cardiology curricula. With the global burden of cardiovascular disease continuing to rise, the efficient dissemination of accurate educational materials is more essential than ever [14]. Understanding whether—and how—LLMs can contribute to this effort is both timely and significant for medical training.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a descriptive, cross-sectional analysis at the Department of Cardiology, Selçuk University. Since no human participants or identifiable patient data were involved, local institutional review board approval was deemed unnecessary. All responses were generated using the free web version of ChatGPT (OpenAI; GPT-4o) during October and November 2024. The same platform and standardized query workflow were used throughout data collection.

Topic selection

Fifteen high-yield cardiology topics (Table 1) were selected through a consensus process, leveraging standard medical education syllabi and board examination blueprints (12). The chosen topics span acute coronary syndromes (ACS), heart failure syndromes, valvular disease, arrhythmias, pericardial and congenital disorders, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, infective endocarditis, cardiomyopathies, cardiac rehabilitation, and preventive cardiology.

Prompt development

For each topic, we devised 10 standardized prompts (150 prompts total). These prompts covered pathophysiology, diagnostic criteria, management strategies, and quiz-style questions. Examples included:

“Explain the pathophysiology of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.”

“Explain the pathophysiology of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.”

“List key principles of primary prevention of coronary artery disease according to recent guidelines.”

“List key principles of primary prevention of coronary artery disease according to recent guidelines.”

“Create five multiple-choice questions on atrial fibrillation with correct answers and brief explanations.”

“Create five multiple-choice questions on atrial fibrillation with correct answers and brief explanations.”

The prompts aimed to simulate the typical types of inquiries medical students or residents might pose or encounter. The complete inventory of all 150 standardized prompts is provided in Supplementary Appendix S1.

ChatGPT response collection

Each prompt was entered once into the free web version of ChatGPT (OpenAI; GPT-4o) under standardized conditions. To minimize carry-over context, every prompt was submitted in a new chat session. No follow-up prompts were added, no responses were regenerated, and no browsing, file upload, or custom instructions were used. No manual modification of generation settings was applied; all outputs were obtained using the platform’s default settings. Responses, typically ranging from 200 to 400 words, were documented verbatim without editing. Duplicate or overlapping text generated by the model was retained to preserve authenticity. All responses were date-stamped and catalogued for subsequent evaluation.

After completion of independent scoring, the two reviewers jointly selected representative examples from both higher-performing and lower-performing topics to contextualize the quantitative findings. These examples were chosen to reflect recurrent patterns observed across the response set—such as omission of key guideline-directed therapies, incomplete procedural recommendations, or overly generalized discussion of complex conditions—rather than isolated outlier statements. To improve methodological transparency, the full prompt set is provided in Supplementary Appendix S1, and selected illustrative scored responses are presented in Supplementary Appendix S2.

Evaluation rubric

Two board-certified cardiologists, each with a minimum of five years of academic practice, independently reviewed all 150 responses. A five-domain rubric was applied to each response, rating on a 0–5 scale:

Factual Accuracy: Concordance with established cardiology guidelines and standard texts.

Completeness: Inclusion of essential epidemiological, pathophysiological, and therapeutic details.

Clarity/Readability: Language coherence, logical organization, and accessibility for the target learner audience.

Guideline Alignment: Consistency with recognized recommendations from ESC, AHA, and ACC for relevant conditions.

Inter-rater reliability was quantified using Cohen’s kappa (κ), with discrepancies resolved through discussion [15, 16]. These illustrative examples were not treated as a separate scoring domain. Rather, they were used to clarify the reasoning behind the assigned scores and to improve reader interpretation of the observed strengths and limitations. Representative scoring sheets for selected responses are provided in Supplementary Appendix S2 to demonstrate how the five-domain rubric was applied in practice.

Statistical analysis

Domain-specific scores were summarized as means ± standard deviations (SD). We also performed topic-specific analyses to compare performance variations among the 15 focal areas. No advanced inferential statistics were deemed necessary. Findings are presented in descriptive form and tabulated where appropriate.

Results

Overall domain performance

All 150 ChatGPT responses were successfully retrieved and scored (see Table 2). Clarity/readability emerged as the highest-scoring domain overall (4.4 ± 0.5), reflecting the model’s consistent ability to produce well-structured and understandable explanations. Factual accuracy (3.7 ± 0.6) and completeness (3.8 ± 0.7) were moderate, with occasional omissions of critical details. Guideline alignment scored 3.5 ± 0.6, demonstrating variability in how well ChatGPT adhered to up-to-date recommendations. Lastly, teaching utility averaged 4.1 ± 0.5, indicating that many responses were deemed useful for educational purposes.

Topic-specific findings

Table 2 displays the mean scores by topic, and Table 3 presents representative examples that contextualize these ratings. Stable Angina Pectoris and Acute Coronary Syndromes had the highest guideline alignment (4.2 ± 0.4) and relatively strong factual accuracy (3.9 ± 0.5). In these higher-performing topics, ChatGPT generally covered key features such as chest pain characteristics, the role of cardiac biomarkers, and recommended reperfusion strategies in a structured and readable manner. By contrast, Pericardial Diseases (particularly constrictive pericarditis) and Congenital Heart Disease in adults showed lower guideline alignment (3.0 ± 0.5 and 3.1 ± 0.5, respectively), often omitting nuanced diagnostic criteria, referral considerations, or newer interventional insights.

Heart Failure topics, including HFrEF and HFpEF, scored between 3.1 ± 0.6 and 3.3 ± 0.6 in guideline alignment, revealing gaps in contemporary guideline-directed therapy. Several responses provided clear general overviews but omitted newer cornerstone therapies such as SGLT2 inhibitors and other components of comprehensive HFrEF management. Arrhythmia prompts, such as atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachyarrhythmias, performed moderately, with a mean score of 3.6 ± 0.5 in guideline alignment. Notably, some responses described rate control, rhythm control, and anticoagulation appropriately at a general level but did not address selected catheter ablation indications or left atrial appendage closure strategies. Other topics like Hypertension, Hyperlipidemia, and Preventive Cardiology were generally well summarized, albeit with limited incorporation of recent trial-based nuance for newer agents.

Illustrative examples of response strengths and common errors

“To complement the quantitative ratings, we reviewed representative examples of response patterns from both higher- and lower-performing topics (Table 3). In higher-performing areas, ChatGPT typically generated concise, logically sequenced explanations that were appropriate for introductory learners and covered the major diagnostic and management principles expected at a general educational level.

In lower-performing areas, the dominant shortcomings were usually not incoherent language, but clinically meaningful incompleteness or insufficient guideline-level nuance. Recurring patterns included omission of newly guideline-endorsed pharmacotherapies in heart failure, limited discussion of procedural indications in arrhythmia management, and overly general explanations for specialized conditions such as constrictive pericarditis and adult congenital heart disease. These examples help explain why clarity/readability remained high even when factual accuracy and guideline alignment were only moderate.”

Inter-rater reliability

Cohen’s kappa values ranged from 0.78 to 0.85 across domains, indicating substantial agreement between the two reviewers. The strongest concordance was seen for clarity/readability, while guideline alignment exhibited slightly lower inter-rater reliability due to subjective interpretations of how thoroughly ChatGPT adhered to the latest evidence.

Discussion

This descriptive analysis demonstrates that ChatGPT can generate well-structured and coherent educational content across a broad spectrum of cardiology topics. In line with earlier studies highlighting the capacity of large language models (LLMs) to simplify complex medical knowledge our findings underscore ChatGPT’s potential to facilitate introductory learning through high clarity and ease of understanding [17–19]. This characteristic alone can be valuable for medical educators, who face the challenge of conveying intricate cardiovascular pathophysiology and evolving clinical guidelines [20, 21].

The representative examples added in the revised manuscript clarify an important interpretive point: ChatGPT’s limitations were more often subtle than overt. Many lower-scoring responses were fluent, well organized, and pedagogically accessible, yet still incomplete with respect to current guideline-directed therapy or specialty-level nuance. This distinction is especially important in medical education, because novice learners may interpret confident and readable prose as evidence of correctness [22–24].

Nevertheless, our data reveal that ChatGPT’s performance is not uniformly robust across all domains. While it excels at common and foundational topics—such as acute coronary syndrome and stable angina—its factual accuracy and guideline alignment weaken for specialized or fast-changing areas, including advanced heart failure management, specific pericardial pathologies, and congenital heart disease in adults. This variability likely stems from the model’s reliance on pre-existing training data, which may inadequately reflect recent treatment paradigms or less frequently encountered conditions [25–27]. The omission of newer heart failure therapies, for instance, mirrors the rapid evolution of guidelines that incorporate novel agents such as SGLT2 inhibitors [20, 28]. As a result, educators should treat ChatGPT’s outputs as starting points, rather than definitive sources of truth.

Our findings extend prior research on AI-based educational tools in medicine by zooming into the field of cardiology, which remains a critical specialty given the global burden of cardiovascular diseases [29]. Although some studies have shown that ChatGPT or similar LLMs can achieve near-passing scores on standardized exams, specialty-specific analyses are still limited [25, 30]. Indeed, even in broader medical contexts, AI-generated responses have been reported to contain both subtle inaccuracies and outright “hallucinated” facts [26, 31]. Such pitfalls have heightened implications when disseminating cardiology guidelines, which are frequently updated to incorporate emerging evidence from high-impact clinical trials [32, 33] .

Moreover, the present study helps clarify how ChatGPT might fit into existing educational frameworks. On one hand, the model’s consistently high scores for clarity and teaching utility point to its suitability for generating preliminary study guides, practice questions, or quick-reference summaries [17, 18]. By automating these more rudimentary tasks, faculty can invest greater effort in higher-order teaching activities—such as guiding students through clinical reasoning exercises, critical appraisals of new evidence, and patient-centered case discussions [34, 35]. However, reliance on unverified AI outputs risks spreading outdated or incomplete information; accordingly, faculty oversight remains essential to ensure both academic rigor and patient safety [17, 36] .

Our topic-specific analysis also underscores the heterogeneity of ChatGPT’s performance. Conditions with extensive research and long-established management protocols—such as acute coronary syndromes—demonstrate comparatively better guideline alignment. In contrast, rare or evolving cardiology domains, including certain congenital defects or complex valvular pathologies requiring advanced interventions, garner lower concordance scores. This discrepancy suggests that LLMs could benefit from domain-specific “fine-tuning,” or more frequent re-training on newly published guidelines and emerging studies [37, 38]. Notably, integrative approaches that update ChatGPT in real time, or that incorporate specialized data repositories, have been proposed as a means to enhance both accuracy and completeness [39–41] .

Another key contribution of this work lies in its methodological rigor. We used a standardized rubric with five domains—factual accuracy, completeness, clarity, guideline alignment, and teaching utility—along with two independent cardiologist reviewers. The substantial inter-rater reliability observed (κ = 0.78–0.85) bolsters the validity of our results and provides a structured evaluation framework for similar investigations. Educators and researchers interested in other clinical specialties could adopt analogous frameworks to systematically evaluate the suitability of AI-generated content for medical instruction.

Finally, our findings highlight the importance of thoughtfully integrating LLMs into the cardiology curriculum. In their current form, tools like ChatGPT can serve as helpful adjuncts, particularly for foundational overviews and test-question generation. Yet, several best practices emerge from our data. First, learners should be reminded that AI outputs are not infallible, and verification against authoritative sources—such as major cardiology guidelines or reputable textbooks—is essential [20, 33]. Second, consistent faculty or expert moderation can mitigate the risk of misinformation, ensuring that novices do not over-rely on AI for complex clinical decision-making or advanced pathophysiological insights [21, 36]. Third, additional research is warranted to explore whether domain-specific AI models—trained exclusively on a corpus of cardiology literature—could outperform general-purpose LLMs like ChatGPT in delivering accurate, guideline-concordant content [38, 42].

In conclusion, ChatGPT appears promising as a supplemental educational resource in cardiology because it provides readable summaries and practical teaching support. However, caution remains necessary in rapidly evolving or highly specialized areas, where expert review is essential. Future improvements in model updating and domain-specific fine-tuning may further enhance reliability.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. First, we only evaluated 15 cardiology topics with 10 standardized prompts each. Although these topics were chosen to represent core areas in cardiology education, they may not encompass the full breadth of the specialty. Second, although we now specify the platform, model, and query conditions used in this study, exact reproducibility remains limited because ChatGPT is a proprietary system that may change over time through platform and model updates.Third, no direct comparisons were made with alternative educational tools or other large language models, which limits the ability to contextualize ChatGPT’s relative performance. Finally, our rubric-based evaluation, though systematic, still carries inherent subjectivity. Despite achieving substantial inter-rater reliability, certain nuances in guideline interpretation or completeness might remain open to debate. These limitations emphasize that while ChatGPT shows promise as a supplemental teaching resource, it should be used with expert supervision and continually validated against updated, authoritative guidelines. Although representative examples were included to improve interpretability, these examples were necessarily selective and should not be interpreted as capturing the full variability across all 150 responses.
