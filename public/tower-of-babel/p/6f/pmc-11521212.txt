Solitary Hybrid Neurofibroma–Schwannoma of the Brachial Plexus in a 4-Year-Old Child
Authors: Varunkumar Maddileti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report the case of a 4-year-old child with a rare solitary neurofibroma–schwannoma hybrid tumor in the brachial plexus, not associated with known syndromes. Surgical management through a transclavicular approach enabled complete tumor excision with favorable postoperative outcomes. Histopathology and immunohistochemistry confirmed the tumor’s hybrid nature.

INTRODUCTION

Brachial plexus tumors in pediatric patients are rare, with limited literature available, mainly in the form of case reports and part of the adult series. Neurofibroma and schwannoma are commonly encountered, usually presenting as pure lesions. However, hybrid neurofibroma–schwannoma tumors (N/S HNST), comprising distinct areas of both neurofibroma and schwannoma, pose diagnostic challenges. These tumors, recognized as a distinct entity in the World Health Organization classification, may occur in isolation or be associated with syndromes such as neurofibromatosis (NF) and schwannomatosis.[1] To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case of solitary N/S HNST of brachial plexus region in a child.

CASE REPORT

A 4-year-old girl presented with a 6-month history of swelling in the left supraclavicular region of the neck. The swelling gradually progressed and began to appear in the left axillary region. The child occasionally experienced pain and paresthesia in the left hand on movement of the upper limb. Swelling was firm extending from the left supraclavicular area to the left axillary area. Bidigital palpation confirmed the continuity of the swelling. Left upper limb movements were normal, except at the hand, where the grip was weaker compared to the right hand. Clinically, there were no cutaneous markers suggestive of NF. Ultrasound showed a 10 cm × 5 cm homogenous, hypoechoic solid mass. Magnetic resonance imaging showed a 10.5 cm × 5 cm × 3 cm swelling extending from the left supraclavicular region through the cervicoaxillary canal into the left axilla [Figure 1a]. The swelling was in line with the brachial plexus, and the intensity was not different from the brachial plexus. Axillary vessels were not infiltrated. Fine-needle aspiration cytology showed benign cytology but inconclusive regarding the specific tumor.

(d) After dissection, the tumor being pushed down into the axilla (e) Tumor removed in toto. (f) Healed surgical scar

A transclavicular approach for the brachial plexus was utilized to achieve good exposure, and the tumor was excised in toto. The tumor was found to be posterior to the brachial plexus. Through a combined use of instruments and blunt finger dissection, the tumor was easily dissected off the plexus in the canal, and then pushed inferiorly and delivered out from the axilla [Figure 1b-e]. The tumor appeared to be arising from the sheath of one of the branches of the brachial plexus; the exact nerve of origin could not be defined because of distortion of anatomy. The mid-third segment of the clavicle which was removed temporarily was placed in its usual position without screw fixation, and the periosteum was closed. The tendon of the pectoralis minor was repaired, and the incision was closed in layers after the placement of a negative suction drain [Figure 1f].

The histopathologic examination showed a benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor (PNST) which exhibited a hybrid of morphologies, with features of a neurofibroma as well as a schwannoma. Regions of the neoplasm exhibited loose, myxoid regions with bland spindled cells which were positive for CD34 – a characteristic of neurofibroma [Figure 2a and b]. Other foci showed a proliferation of spindle cells in short fascicles and palisades with no significant pleomorphism, mitosis, or necrosis, which were negative for CD34, compatible with schwannoma [Figure 2d and e]. The lesional cells from both components showed diffuse, strong nuclear and cytoplasmic positivity for S100 [Figure 2c and f].

(a) Areas of neurofibroma showing lower cellularity with wavy nuclei in a collagenous stroma and cells CD34+ (b) S100+ (c). (d) Features of cellular schwannoma with spindle cells arranged in fascicles and palisades, spindle cells CD34 negative (e), (f) S100+

Postoperatively, a shoulder sling was used. Postoperatively, the child experienced left upper limb weakness with power grade 2 due to neuropraxia. Physiotherapy was initiated, and by 1-month postsurgery, the child had recovered and regained full power in the left upper limb with a normal hand grip. She has been regularly followed up for 2 years and is doing well without any local recurrence or neurological deficits. Genetic assessment was performed for any NF gene, and it came out negative, suggesting the sporadic nature of the tumor in this child.

DISCUSSION

Hybrid PNSTs were first described by Feany et al. in 1998; all of the cases were of adult age group and the majority were associated with NF type 1 (NF1).[2] N/S HNSTs are composed of schwannoma-like nodules within a neurofibroma-like tumor, and >60% of cases are associated with an underlying tumor predisposition syndrome. In particular, 26%, 17%, and 9% of N/S HNST patients are afflicted with NF type 2 (NF2), schwannomatosis, and NF1, respectively. Most of the hybrid PNSTs appear to be occurring in young adults.[3] The exact pathogenetic basis of dual differentiation in hybrid PNSTs is not known. It may result from a clonal genetic alteration or from a localized change in the microenvironment.[4] The index case is of the neurofibroma/schwannoma type and is not associated with any syndrome, as confirmed by the absence of relevant clinical features and the lack of gene association in genetic analysis. Solitary N/S HNSTs in children has been rarely reported in the literature. In a report of five cases of hybrid PNSTs by Ud Din et al., only one case, 5 years of age, was of N/S HNSTs on thigh.[4] Another solitary hybrid neurofibroma/schwannoma of parapharyngeal space was described by Chaturvedi et al. in an 8-year-old child.[1] The largest series of hybrid neurofibroma/schwannoma was described by Harder et al., which involves the retrospective analysis of PNSTs resulting in identification of 60 cases (only two pediatric cases).[5] Frequent association with NF1, NF2, and schwannomatosis was noted in the latter series where majority had multiple tumors with plexiform type of neurofibroma component.[5] Brachial plexus tumors present a surgical challenge because these lesions are structurally intrinsic to the nerves and the normal neurovascular anatomy may be greatly distorted and often results in neurological deficits. Transclavicular approach to the brachial plexus gives a good, wide exposure and better opportunity of safe dissection; the transected clavicle heals well either by plating or by just placing in the periosteum.[6] Neuropraxia can occur postbrachial plexus surgery, especially when tumor is disproportionately larger than the cervicoaxillary space and dissection results in pressure on the brachial plexus. Conservative management with active and passive physiotherapy forms the mainstay of treatment for neuropraxia.
