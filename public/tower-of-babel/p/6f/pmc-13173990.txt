The association between gestational diabetes mellitus and Dietary Diabetes Risk Reduction Score: a case-control study
Authors: Fatemeh Maleki Sedgi, Amir Hossein Hassani, Shahsanam Gheibi, Amin Mokari-Yamchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a prevalent pregnancy complication influenced by dietary patterns. The Dietary Diabetes Risk Reduction Score (DDRRS), a composite index evaluating diet quality in relation to diabetes risk, may be a useful tool in understanding GDM etiology, yet its association with GDM has been underexplored.ObjectiveTo investigate the relationship between adherence to the DDRRS and the risk of GDM in an Iranian population.MethodsA case-control study was conducted among 320 pregnant women in Urmia, Iran, including 150 women diagnosed with GDM and 170 age- and gestational age-matched healthy controls. Dietary intake was assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire, and DDRRS was calculated based on intake of eight food components. Logistic regression models were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for GDM risk across DDRRS tertiles, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsWomen with GDM had significantly lower DDRRS compared to controls (20.52 ± 4.11 vs. 21.53 ± 4.76, p = 0.04). In the fully adjusted model, participants in the highest tertile of DDRRS had a significantly lower risk of GDM compared to those in the lowest tertile (OR = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.24–0.85; p-trend = 0.01). Additionally, women with GDM reported higher intake of red and processed meats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and high-glycemic index foods, and lower intake of Poly unsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) / Saturated fatty acid (SFA) ratio, magnesium, zinc, and folate.ConclusionGreater adherence to the DDRRS was associated with a reduced risk of GDM. These findings suggest that promoting dietary patterns characterized by higher intake of fiber, nuts, and unsaturated fats and lower intake of red meats and sugary beverages may contribute to GDM prevention and maternal health improvement.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a form of glucose intolerance that is first diagnosed during pregnancy, usually developing in the second or third trimester and often continuing until childbirth [1]. Although the exact pathophysiology of GDM remains unclear, its management generally involves dietary regulation and, in some cases, insulin therapy. Based on available data, GDM affects approximately 7% of pregnancies in the United States, accounting for an estimated 200,000 cases annually [2]. Globally, the prevalence of GDM is around 14%, although rates vary widely by region, ranging from 5% to 25.5% [3]. In Iran, a 2023 meta-analysis reported a GDM prevalence of 7.6% [4].

Numerous risk factors have been consistently linked to the development of GDM, including pre-pregnancy overweight and obesity, excessive gestational weight gain, westernized dietary patterns, ethnicity, genetic predispositions, and advanced maternal age [5–10]. Despite the identification of these risk factors, there is currently no universally accepted approach for the prevention or definitive treatment of GDM. Lifestyle modifications, particularly dietary changes and increased physical activity, remain the primary recommendations. While emerging oral hypoglycemic agents, such as glyburide and metformin, show promise, concerns about their long-term safety for both mother and fetus remain unresolved [5, 11].

A thorough understanding and precise evaluation of modifiable risk factors particularly dietary factors are critical for developing effective strategies to prevent and manage GDM. Investigating the association between overall diet quality and chronic disease risk, rather than focusing on isolated nutrients or food items, may yield more practical and applicable results. One such approach is the use of composite dietary indices, such as the Dietary Diabetes Risk Reduction Score (DDRRS), which was introduced by Rhee et al. in 2015 to evaluate the relationship between diet quality and diabetes risk [12]. This score is based on the consumption of food components either positively or inversely associated with diabetes and assigns scores accordingly to reflect overall dietary patterns.

Recent evidence supports the relevance of diet quality in relation to GDM. For instance, Jahromi et al. demonstrated that higher DDRRS adherence was associated with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes among Iranian adults [13]. Additionally, a large U.S.-based cohort study revealed that higher DDRRS scores were linked to lower risks of all-cause and cause-specific mortality, including deaths from cardiovascular disease and cancer [14]. Despite numerous studies globally, data on the role of diet quality indices such as the DDRRS in predicting GDM risk remain scarce, especially in Middle Eastern and low- to middle-income countries (LMICs). For instance, high prevalence of diabetes and GDM has been reported in some Arab countries, largely driven by lifestyle changes and genetic predispositions [15]. However, targeted research on dietary quality and its association with GDM in these populations is limited.

Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the association between adherence to the DDRRS and the risk of developing gestational diabetes mellitus in an Iranian population. Understanding this relationship may provide valuable insights into the role of diet quality in the prevention of GDM and contribute to the development of more effective, evidence-based nutritional strategies to improve maternal health outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

A case-control study was conducted involving a total of 320 pregnant women, including 150 individuals diagnosed with GDM and 170 healthy pregnant women as the control group. Controls were selected to achieve a comparable distribution of gestational age with the case group. Gestational age was additionally adjusted for in the multivariable logistic regression models. Recruitment was carried out between February and October 2024 at 20 randomly selected primary healthcare centers in Urmia, Iran.

Participants were selected using a simple random sampling method. Eligible participants were aged 18–45 years with a gestational age of at least 24 weeks. Exclusion criteria included pre-existing chronic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, hematological disorders, malignancies, chronic infections, hypothyroidism, as well as psychiatric disorders (e.g., anorexia nervosa, binge eating disorder, depression, bipolar disorder), and any dietary restrictions or medications that could affect glycemic levels. Women with a personal or familial history of GDM, glucose intolerance, macrosomia, recurrent miscarriage, corticosteroid use, stillbirth, intrauterine fetal death, preeclampsia, eclampsia, or cesarean delivery were also not included in the study. Furthermore, participants who did not complete the FFQ (defined as missing more than 35 items) (n = 12), those with reported energy intakes outside the specified range of 800–4,200 kilocalories [16] (n = 3), and those with missing covariate data (n = 6) were excluded from the analysis. Therefore, the final analytic sample comprised 150 women with GDM and 170 healthy controls (n = 320).

Diagnosis of gestational diabetes mellitus

GDM diagnosis was established based on national guidelines provided by the Iranian Ministry of Health. Women meeting any of the following oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) thresholds were classified as having GDM: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 92 mg/dL, 1-hour post-OGTT glucose ≥ 180 mg/dL, or 2-hour post-OGTT glucose ≥ 153 mg/dL.

Data collection procedures

Participants were briefed on the study’s objectives, procedures, and voluntary nature, and provided written informed consent. Anthropometric data, including pre-pregnancy weight, height, and GDM screening results, were extracted from medical records. Eligible individuals were contacted by telephone and invited to attend their local health center, where trained interviewers conducted structured, in-person interviews in private settings to collect questionnaire data.

Dietary intake assessment

Dietary intake was evaluated using a semi-quantitative, previously validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) consisting of 168 items developed for the Iranian population [17]. Participants reported their usual dietary intake during pregnancy up to the time of GDM screening, including consumption frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, or annually) and portion sizes. The FFQ was completed prior to the disclosure of OGTT results and before any dietary counseling related to GDM was provided. The frequency and portion size data were used to calculate daily nutrient intakes. Nutrient analysis was performed using Nutritionist IV software (First Databank Inc., Hearst Corp., San Bruno, CA, USA), which had been customized for Iranian dietary patterns.

Calculation of the Dietary Diabetes Risk Reduction Score (DDRRS)

The DDRRS was calculated following the methodology proposed by Rhee et al. [12], incorporating eight dietary components. Protective components included higher intakes of cereal fiber, nuts (e.g., walnuts, almonds, pistachios, hazelnuts), coffee, and a favorable polyunsaturated-to-saturated fat ratio (P: S ratio). Risk components consisted of red or processed meats (e.g., hamburgers, sausages, salami), sugar-sweetened beverages (e.g., fruit juice, soda, malt drinks), trans fatty acids, and high glycemic index (GI) foods. GI values were calculated using the international tables of GI and GL [18]. For foods not listed, GI values were estimated based on nutritionally similar items or calculated using related formulas [19]. To determine each participant’s dietary GI, the carbohydrate content of each food was multiplied by its GI and consumption frequency, and the sum of these products was divided by the total daily carbohydrate intake [20].

Participants were ranked into quartiles based on their intake for each component. For beneficial items, higher quintiles received higher scores (4 to 1), while scoring was reversed for risk-associated components. The final DDRRS was obtained by summing the scores across all components, yielding a total score ranging from 8 (lowest adherence) to 32 (highest adherence). Although the DDRRS was originally developed in Western populations, we applied it using a previously validated FFQ for Iranian adults [17] that capture culturally relevant foods and dietary patterns. Similar DDRRS applications have been successfully used in Iranian populations [13, 21].

Socioeconomic and demographic data

Participants’ height and weight were measured directly by trained staff, and body mass index (BMI, kg/m²) was calculated using the standard formula. Information on participants’ occupation was collected through structured, in-person interviews conducted by trained interviewers. Information on occupation, ethnicity, household monthly income, and maternal education level was collected through structured, in-person interviews conducted by trained interviewers. These procedures were applied consistently to both women with gestational diabetes and healthy controls. Participants’ education levels were categorized into four groups: less than high school (including incomplete high school), high school diploma, diploma (post-high school certificate), and college degree or higher. Occupational status was classified as employed (Yes) or not employed (No), with the latter group including housewives and unemployed individuals. Household monthly income was reported in Iranian Rials and categorized into four brackets: less than 80 million IRR, 80–150 million IRR, 150–200 million IRR, and over 200 million IRR.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) and analyzed using independent t-tests. Categorical data were summarized as counts and percentages, and group differences were assessed using chi-square tests. To examine the relationship between dietary patterns and the risk of GDM, logistic regression models were applied. The results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) across quartiles of DDRRS. For association analyses, the DDRRS was categorized into tertiles to ensure adequate sample size within each category and improve statistical power.

Two regression models were constructed: Model 1 unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for age, gestational age, BMI, occupation, education, ethnicity, parity, total calorie intake and physical activity. Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature and biological plausibility, and included variables that may act as both confounders and potential mediators. Continuous variables were entered into the model as continuous terms. Categorical variables were entered according to the categories presented in Table 1. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 22 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 320 pregnant women participated in the study, including 150 women diagnosed with GDM and 170 healthy controls. As shown in Table 1, women with GDM were significantly older (32.24 ± 7.22 vs. 28.75 ± 7.49 years, p = 0.001) and had a higher mean BMI (29.0 ± 4.58 vs. 26.40 ± 4.70 kg/m², p = 0.001) compared to the control group. There was also a significant difference in educational attainment between the two groups (p = 0.007), with a larger percentage of control participants achieving higher education levels. Additionally, physical activity levels were significantly lower among women with GDM (33.43 ± 3.87 vs. 34.78 ± 4.09 Metabolic Equivalent Task hours per week (MET-h/week), p = 0.003).

As shown in Table 2, the mean DDRRS was significantly lower in women with GDM compared to controls (20.52 ± 4.11 vs. 21.53 ± 4.76, p = 0.04). Among the DDRRS components, GDM participants had significantly lower PUFA/SFA ratios (0.63 ± 0.19 vs. 0.69 ± 0.20, p = 0.005) and higher intake of red and processed meats (0.79 ± 0.49 vs. 0.68 ± 0.20 servings/day, p = 0.01) and sugar-sweetened beverages (3.97 ± 2.62 vs. 3.34 ± 2.16 servings/week, p = 0.02). GI was also significantly higher in the GDM group (60.90 ± 11.78 vs. 56.89 ± 12.24, p = 0.003). In terms of overall nutrient intake, women with GDM consumed significantly more total calories (2520.0 ± 900.72 vs. 2259.0 ± 938.02 kcal/day, p = 0.01) and had lower intakes of magnesium (294.17 ± 53.84 vs. 310.22 ± 58.05 mg/day, p = 0.01), zinc (11.71 ± 6.08 vs. 13.11 ± 6.39 mg/day, p = 0.04), and folate (467.24 ± 126.27 vs. 499.85 ± 143.75 mcg/day, p = 0.03).

Table 3 shows the association between DDRRS tertiles and the risk of GDM. In the unadjusted model (Model 1), participants in the highest tertile of DDRRS had significantly lower odds of developing GDM compared to those in the lowest tertile (OR = 0.55; 95% CI: 0.31–0.97; p-trend = 0.04). This inverse association remained statistically significant in the adjusted model (Model 2), which accounted for age, gestational age, BMI, total calorie intake, physical activity, ethnicity, parity, occupation, and education status (OR = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.24–0.85; p-trend = 0.01).

Discussion

This case-control study showed that women with GDM were older, had higher BMI, and reported lower physical activity. In terms of DDRRS components, women with GDM had lower intake of PUFAs/SFAs, magnesium, zinc, and folate, as well as higher intake of red and processed meat, sugar-sweetened beverages, foods with higher GI, and overall energy intake. Additionally, individuals in the highest tertiles of DDRRS had a lower risk of GDM, even after adjustment for confounding variables.

As shown in our study, women with GDM were older and had higher BMI and lower physical activity compared with the control group. In line with our results, Li et al. in a systematic and meta-analyses study showed that risk of GDM increases linearly with maternal age [22]. Also a study by Yong et al. showed that older maternal age and being overweight/ obese were significantly associated with risk of GDM in agreement with our results [23]. In line with our results Russo et al. in a systematic review and meta-analysis showed that physical activity in pregnancy provides a slight protective effect against the development of GDM [24]. A study by Doi et al. also demonstrated that initiating physical activity programs within healthcare facilities before the 20th week of pregnancy can substantially reduce the occurrence of GDM in women who are at high risk [22]. The precise mechanism linking maternal age to GDM remains unclear; however, factors such as increased insulin resistance, elevated circulating adipokines and inflammatory markers, along with oxidative stress, may partially contribute to this association [25]. Additionally, physical inactivity exacerbates insulin resistance, while regular physical activity improves glucose uptake and insulin responsiveness and enhancements in beta-cell and epithelial function improve insulin production, which may help reduce the risk of GDM [26, 27].

Our findings revealed that women with GDM had a lower intake ratio of PUFAs to SFAs. Consistent with these results, a cross-sectional study by Barbieri et al. reported an inverse relationship between higher intake of n-3 PUFAs and the risk of GDM [28]. Recent evidence further supports that a higher PUFA-to-SFA ratio is associated with reduced body fat, improved insulin sensitivity, better lipid metabolism, and favorable hepatic enzyme profiles [29, 30].

Additionally, our results indicated that individuals with GDM consumed higher amounts of red and processed meats. Supporting this finding, Norouziasl et al. showed a direct link between consuming red and processed meats and a higher risk of developing GDM [31]. Consistent with our findings, a prospective analysis from the Nurses’ Health Study II, involving 21,457 women with singleton pregnancies, found that participants in the highest quintile of total red meat intake had double the risk of developing GDM compared to those in the lowest quintile [32]. However, contrasting results were observed in a smaller hospital-based case–control study conducted in Iran, which found no significant association between the consumption of red and processed meats or other animal-based protein sources and the risk of GDM [33]. It is noteworthy that cooking methods such as roasting or grilling red meat at high temperatures over an open flame can lead to the formation of harmful compounds, including heterocyclic amines and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) [34]. Moreover, studies have identified correlations between urinary PAH biomarkers specifically 1- and 2-hydroxynaphthalene and inflammatory markers, as well as an increased prevalence of type 2 diabetes [35].

Women with GDM in our study reported significantly higher intake of sugar-sweetened beverages, foods with a high GI, and overall caloric intake compared to controls. These findings are consistent with previous studies For example, Ker et al. reported a significant positive correlation between consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages and an increased risk of GDM [36]. Moreover, a randomized controlled trial by Moses et al. demonstrated that adherence to a low-GI diet in women with GDM reduced the need for insulin therapy by 50%, highlighting the clinical relevance of glycemic load management [37]. Evidence suggests that low-GI diets help reduce postprandial hyperglycemia, enhance insulin sensitivity, and lower insulin requirements both during and outside of pregnancy [38]. Additionally, Chen et al. found that excessive caloric intake was associated with an accelerated decline in insulin sensitivity and β-cell function in women with prior GDM, putting them at increased risk for progression to type 2 diabetes [39]. The underlying mechanisms are multifactorial. High-GI foods and sugar-sweetened beverages lead to rapid spikes in blood glucose levels, causing exaggerated insulin responses. Chronic postprandial hyperglycemia contributes to insulin resistance and β-cell exhaustion, both of which are central to the pathophysiology of GDM. Moreover, excess caloric intake especially when derived from refined carbohydrates and added sugars promotes adiposity, systemic inflammation, and lipotoxicity, further impairing insulin signaling and pancreatic β-cell function.

In this study, women with GDM exhibited significantly lower levels of key micronutrients, including magnesium, folate, and zinc, compared to controls. Previous research supports these findings; for example, Looman et al. demonstrated that sufficient intake of micronutrients such as folate before and during pregnancy could reduce the risk of developing GDM [40]. Similarly, Li et al. reported that an adequate folate intake is associated with a decreased risk of GDM [41]. Qu et al. found that magnesium supplementation positively influences insulin sensitivity [42]. Furthermore, Fan et al. observed lower serum zinc levels in women with GDM, particularly among Asian populations and during the second trimester compared to women with normal glucose tolerance [43]. Mahmoud et al. also noted a significant reduction in zinc levels among diet-treated diabetic pregnant women compared to healthy pregnant women, suggesting zinc’s potential role in modulating immune function [44].

Mechanistically, in vivo studies have shown that magnesium can upregulate the mRNA expression of genes involved in key signaling pathways, including peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) and AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) [45, 46]. Additionally, magnesium supplementation may restore the gene expression of PPAR-γ, GLUT-1, and LDL receptor (LDLR) in women of reproductive age without diabetes [46]. With regard to zinc, circulating levels may contribute significantly to immune regulation [44] and zinc complexes have demonstrated insulin-mimetic properties, potentially aiding in glucose homeostasis [47]. Genetic predisposition also plays a role in GDM development. Genetic predisposition also plays a role in GDM development. In particular, polymorphisms in the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene, such as the C677T variant (rs1801133), may influence folate metabolism and thereby modulate the association between folate status and GDM risk [48, 49].

Regarding the DDRRS, our results indicate that individuals in the highest tertile of DDRRS had a significantly lower risk of developing GDM. Although direct evidence on the association between DDRRS and GDM is limited, findings from related metabolic conditions support its potential benefits. For instance, Karimi et al. found that higher adherence to DDRRS is linked to improved lipid and insulin profiles, as well as reduced liver enzymes, visceral adiposity index (VAI), and lipid accumulation product (LAP) [21]. Similarly, Mirmiran et al. reported that greater adherence to DDRRS was associated with a reduced risk of metabolic syndrome (MetS) in Iranian adults [50]. Additionally, the high fiber content and low GI of this dietary pattern may contribute to better metabolic outcomes, including enhanced glycemic control, reduced insulin resistance, improved lipid profiles, lower obesity rates, and better blood pressure regulation [51].

This study has some limitations, including its observational case-control design, which precludes causal inference, the potential for recall bias in dietary reporting, and limited generalizability to populations with different dietary habits, ethnicities, healthcare systems, or GDM diagnostic criteria. Additionally, the possibility of reverse causation cannot be excluded, as dietary data collection may have occurred after GDM diagnosis, potentially influencing participants’ eating behaviors. The restrictive exclusion criteria may have introduced selection bias, limiting representativeness. Residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured variables may also affect the findings. Furthermore, the use of a Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) to assess diet is subject to inherent limitations such as recall bias and measurement error.
