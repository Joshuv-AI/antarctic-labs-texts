Injectable, reversibly thermoresponsive captopril-laden hydrogel for the local treatment of sensory loss in diabetic neuropathy
Authors: Amit Chandra Das, James M Nichols, Caitlin V Crelli, Lu Liu, Riddhi Vichare, Hoang Vu Pham, Caitlyn M Gaffney, Fisher R Cherry, Peter M Grace, Andrew J Shepherd, Jelena M Janjic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractA major and irreversible complication of diabetes is diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), which can lead to significant disability and decreased quality of life. Prior work demonstrates the peptide hormone Angiotensin II (Ang II) is released locally in neuropathy and drives inflammation and impaired endoneurial blood flow. Therefore, we proposed that by utilizing a local thermoresponsive hydrogel injection, we could deliver inhibitors of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) to suppress Ang II production and reduce nerve dysfunction in DPN through local drug release. The ACE inhibitor captopril was encapsulated into a micelle, which was then embedded into a reversibly thermoresponsive pluronics-based hydrogel matrix. Drug-free and captopril-loaded hydrogels demonstrated excellent product stability and sterility. Rheology testing confirmed sol properties with low viscosity at ambient temperature and increased viscosity and gelation at 37 °C. Captopril-loaded hydrogels significantly inhibited Ang II production in comparison to drug-free hydrogels. DPN mice treated with captopril-loaded hydrogels displayed normalized mechanical sensitivity and reduced inflammation, without side-effects associated with systemic exposure. Our data demonstrate the feasibility of repurposing ACE inhibitors as locally delivered anti-inflammatories for the treatment of sensory deficits in DPN. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first example of a locally delivered ACE inhibitor for the treatment of DPN.Keywords: Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), Thermoresponsive hydrogels, Captopril, Angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE), Inflammation, Local delivery, In situ gelationSubject terms: Medical research, Molecular medicine, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The National Center for Chronic Disease Prevention and Health Promotion estimated that 30.3 million people in the U.S. live with diabetes (9.4% of the population) and as many as 84.1 million adults (33.9% of U.S. adults) are prediabetic1. One of the major complications of diabetes is diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), which can lead to significant disability and reduced quality of life and represents the most common peripheral neuropathy worldwide2,3. The impaired nerve regeneration and sensorimotor deficits associated with DPN represent a frequent source of disability which, if left untreated, often leads to ulceration and infection, which can necessitate amputation1,4–6. Current guidelines for management of DPN focus on pain, recommending treatments including tricyclic antidepressants, anticonvulsants (e.g. pregabalin), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (e.g. duloxetine) and, in severe cases, opioids. However, DPN is most commonly associated with loss of sensory acuity7, and with the exception of drugs to regain normal glycemic control, most other pharmacotherapies for DPN focus on pain relief8. Restoring sensory loss found in DPN could lead to significant functional improvements and increased quality of life.

Given the urgent need for more patient-friendly, safe, and effective therapies, repurposing clinically approved drugs for local delivery is an attractive new therapeutic approach for diabetic neuropathy. Here we present one such strategy, where we incorporated a known, safe and effective angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor, captopril (CAP), into a reversibly thermoresponsive, locally injectable, pluronics-based hydrogel for local delivery to affected peripheral nerve terminals.

The peptide hormone angiotensin II (Ang II) has recently received significant attention for its potential as a therapeutic target in DPN9. Circulating levels of Ang II are elevated in obesity, metabolic syndrome and type two diabetes (T2D)10. Furthermore, polymorphisms in genes that form the renin-angiotensin system can elevate T2D risk11. For example, insertion/deletion polymorphisms in ACE, the rate-limiting enzyme for conversion of Ang I into bioactive Ang II, are associated with a several-fold increase in risk of developing DPN12. Recent preclinical studies13 and a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 clinical trial14 have already shown the potential of targeting Ang II and downstream signaling for neuropathic pain. Inhibition of Ang II signaling is also suggested to be neuroprotective, as well as anti-inflammatory. A preclinical model of type 1 diabetes15 and patients with neuropathy16–18 both showed improved neuroprotection and autonomic/sensory nerve function if they received angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) or angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors.

The most well-established mechanistic basis for targeting Ang II in DPN is that excessive Ang II-induced vasoconstriction in DPN impairs endoneurial blood flow15, leading to ischemia, nerve degeneration and sensory loss – one of the predominant symptoms of DPN7. The second, less well-understood basis for targeting Ang II is its induction of inflammation and oxidative stress, two key drivers of DPN pathology19–23. Crucially, these effects are likely due to paracrine as well as endocrine actions of Ang II24,25. Produced at sites of vascular inflammation, Ang II triggers expression of adhesion molecules, cytokines and chemokines, acting as an immune cell attractant19,26–28. This is consistent with the chronic pro-inflammatory state and immune cell dysregulation seen in humans and mouse models of DPN that is thought to contribute to neuropathy23,29.

Collectively, this suggested that localized inhibition of Ang II production may prove beneficial for the treatment of DPN. To specifically target local production of Ang II, we developed a novel hydrogel-based local delivery approach for the ACE inhibitor captopril (CAP). Current clinical strategies use ACE inhibitors to manage hypertension and rely on systemic delivery. Earlier studies showed that systemically delivered ACE inhibitors impacted pain perception in patients30,31. Systemic delivery of CAP faces several challenges before it can show therapeutic efficacy, including degradation by gastric enzymes and acidic pH and rapid clearance by the first-pass metabolism when given orally, leading to very low bioavailability. Moreover, systemic delivery can lead to ACE inhibitor-related side-effects such as hypoglycemia, headache/dizziness, musculoskeletal pain and dry cough32,33. Therefore, we hypothesized that we would improve both the efficacy and safety of ACE inhibition in DPN by delivering CAP directly to the site of neuropathy.

There is limited prior literature on biomaterial-based delivery systems for ACE inhibitors. In one study, pH-responsive gelatin-polyvinylpyrrolidone based hydrogels were used34, while another study reported polymeric microspheres as sustained release vehicles for captopril delivery35. However, these studies did not report in vitro or in vivo pharmacological data. To the best of our knowledge this is the first report of captopril delivery in vivo using a locally applied hydrogel with demonstrated pharmacological impact on mechanical hyposensitivity in the Leprdb mouse model of T2D. CAP is a small molecular weight, highly soluble and permeable drug which belongs to Biopharmaceutical Classification System (BCS) Class I and, due to its water solubility, can be easily incorporated into a hydrogel matrix. The local delivery of CAP, encapsulated in hydrogels for diabetic neuropathy, avoids the limitations associated with systemic delivery and offers potentially extended exposure at the site of neuroinflammation in DPN.

For the purpose of local captopril delivery, we chose a hydrogel matrix built from two pluronics: Pluronic F127® as the primary and Pluronic F68® as an additive introduced to reduce viscosity at ambient temperature. In a previous study, we showed that mixed micelle-based hydrogels built from two Pluronics (F127 and F68) can be optimized into reversibly thermoresponsive hydrogels for drug delivery. Low ambient temperature viscosity is needed for easier injectability and local application36,37. Vichare et al. showed that Tacrolimus-loaded nanoemulsion can be dispersed into the pluronic gel matrix38. Pluronic F127® self-assembles in aqueous media into micelles alone or in combination with other pluronics39,40. The sol–gel transition is driven by non-ionic interactions between micelles formed by the Pluronic F127® polymer in aqueous media, which associate into a three-dimensional ordered network. The sol–gel transition can be fine-tuned by introducing other polymers and pluronic surfactants to respond to changes in pH41, or temperature42. Pluronic F127® based hydrogels are well-studied, biocompatible and generally recognized as safe (GRAS) materials widely used for delivery of biologics43,44, small molecule drugs and nanosystems45. Typically, these formulations behave as viscous liquids (sol) at temperatures below 20 °C but rapidly transform into a stable gel at physiological temperature, which can produce a local depot of pharmacologically active compounds after subcutaneous injection45. Although commonly used in drug delivery, there is limited literature on Pluronic F127® in diabetes or related pathologies. In one example, Li et al. show successfully encapsulated insulin into Pluronic F127® gels for sustained in vivo release to control hyperglycemia44.

Our data shows that Pluronic F127/F68 mixed hydrogels can release captopril locally and in 2–3 days leading to marked improvement in DPN-related sensory loss. We also show there is clear reduction in pro-inflammatory mediators in hind paw tissue upon injection of captopril-loaded hydrogels and that the gel itself does not cause local tissue irritation. Furthermore, we show there is no systemic effect of captopril due to local delivery, as demonstrated by no changes in blood pressure in treatment groups vs. controls. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time ACE inhibitors have been used for local therapy to manage DPN-associated sensory loss and local neuroinflammation.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

All materials were purchased from commercial vendors. Captopril was purchased from MilliporeSigma (Catalog number 211875), along with Kolliphor® EL (Cr-EL, Catalog Number C5135) and Pluronic® F-127 (Catalog Number P2443). Pluronic P-105 (Catalog Number P4283) was purchased from US Biological Life Sciences. Poloxamer 188 (F68, Catalog Number P1169) was obtained from Spectrum Chemical. Acetone (Acros Organics, Fisher Catalog number AC176800025); Sterile 1X Phosphate Buffered Saline (1X PBS) (Corning™, Fisher Catalog Number MT21031CV). CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Viability assay kit was purchased from Promega (Catalog Number G9242).

Preparation of drug-free and captopril-loaded micelles

Micelles were prepared by adapting a previously reported method46. Briefly, captopril in acetone (2 mL, 100 mg/mL), Kolliphor® EL (Cremophor-EL, Cr-EL) in acetone (4 mL, 120 mg/mL), and Pluronic P-105 (P105) in acetone (4 mL, 80 mg/mL) were mixed in a 250 mL round-bottomed flask. The flask was rotated at 35 °C at 150 rpm for 30 min. Acetone was slowly evaporated using high vacuum (556 mbar) to form a thin film. The final film was rehydrated in 16 mL distilled water and consisted of a captopril-loaded Kolliphor EL (CrEL)-Pluronic P105 mixed micelle with a final concentration of 12.5 mg/mL captopril. The final solution of micelle has a concentration of 50 mg/mL (3% w/v Cr-EL/2% w/v P105). Drug-free micelles were prepared by using only acetone (2 mL) without captopril in the above-mentioned process. The resulting micelles were filtered using 0.22 µm filters to yield sterile micelles for encapsulation into hydrogels.

Drug-free hydrogel (DF-HG) and captopril-loaded hydrogel (CAP-HG) formulation

Hydrogel formulations were prepared by adapting previously reported methods46. All solutions (excluding F127®) were sterile filtered and consumables were pre-sterilized using UV light. Pluronic F127® solution is prepared by dissolving the polymer in 1X PBS in cold by magnetic stirring and final solution sterilized in an autoclave. Poloxamer 188 solution (5% w/v in deionized water), captopril micelles (12.5 mg/mL), and F127® in PBS (30% w/v) were all placed on ice 20 min prior to processing. A disposable 100 mL cup was placed inside the cooling adapter. 30% w/v F127® in 1X PBS (15 mL) was added to the cup, followed by Poloxamer 188 solution (5 mL, 5% w/v in deionized water), and pre-formed captopril micelle (5 mL). The cooling adapter was mixed at 2000 rpm for 90 s using the THINKY Mixer ARE-301. Drug-free hydrogels were prepared by using drug-free micelles (5 mL) instead of captopril-loaded micelles. The captopril-loaded hydrogel had a theoretical drug concentration of 2.5 mg/mL. The thermoresponsive behavior of the gels was confirmed by using the simple inversion test after incubating hydrogels at 37 °C for 10 min in a glass scintillation vial.

Colloidal stability of micelles and hydrogels

Captopril micelles and captopril micelle hydrogels were tested for their colloidal stability using a previously described battery of tests such as filtration, centrifugation, serum exposure as well as shelf-life with storage at 4 °C47. Particle size was measured at 25 °C using Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) taken on Zeta Sizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, United Kingdom), using a He/Ne gas laser (λ = 644 nm) and detector at 173°. Hydrogels were diluted in deionized water to a dilution of 1:40 v/v. For serum stability, the hydrogel was diluted in water and in a solution of 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in DMEM and incubated for 3 days at 37 °C. Centrifugation test was carried out using a Refrigerated Microcentrifuge (Prism R Refrigerated Microcentrifuge, Midwest Scientific). Samples of hydrogel (300 µL, undiluted) were subjected to 1100 rpm and 3000 rpm for 5 min respectively with the centrifuge set at 4 °C. A small sample was collected post-centrifugation and diluted in water to measure the particle size on DLS. All the colloidal stability test protocols are adapted from Janjic et al.48.

Hydrogel sterility testing

Hydrogel sterility was assessed for both captopril-loaded and drug-free products before their use in vivo. Sterility testing was performed as per USP 34 microbiological testing/71 guidelines as per our previously published literature, [U.S. Pharmacopoeia [USP 34 Microbiological Tests / Sterility Tests]. Rockville, MD2011. p. 65–70]49. Briefly, 300 µL of hydrogels were diluted in 11.7 mL fluid thioglycollate medium (MilliporeSigma, Catalog STBMCTM12) or trypticase soy broth (MilliporeSigma, Catalog STBMTSB12). Dilutions were split into 2 portions: 6 mL of each dilution was used for initial pH and 6 mL was covered with a parafilm and incubated. pH was recorded using Orion Ross Sure-Flow Electrode (Thermo Fisher) using a pre-calibrated Orion Star A111 pH meter. Trypticase soy broth dilutions were incubated at 25 °C for 14 days and fluid thioglycollate medium dilutions were incubated at 37 °C for 14 days. Dilutions for each set were also incubated with negative controls: undiluted soy broth and undiluted thioglycollate medium. After 14 days of incubation, samples were inspected for microbial growth. pH values were measured and compared with controls to check for differences.

Captopril quantification in micelles and hydrogels

Captopril was quantified using HPLC (DIONEX Ultimate 3000, Thermo Scientific™). A reverse-phase HPLC column (C18 Hypersil Gold 150 × 4.6 mm—Thermo Scientific) was used and the method for detection was used as described50. Micelle and hydrogel samples were dissolved in methanol and vortexed for complete dissolution. Samples were further diluted 1:1 with water containing 0.1% of orthophosphoric acid. Samples were quantified using a 1:1 mobile phase containing acidified water and methanol with a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min. The total run time was 6 min with detection of captopril at 220 nm at a retention time of 3.3 min.

Drug release kinetics

Drug release behavior was studied for free captopril and CAP-HG using dialysis bags of 3.5 MWCO (molecular weight cut-off, CAT# 68035, Thermo Scientific, USA) as previously described51,52. Briefly, CAP was dissolved in 1X PBS (1 mg/mL, free CAP) and an equivalent drug containing CAP-HG was placed in another dialysis bag. Dialysis bags with CAP-HG were kept at 37 ± 0.5 ∘C for 20–30 min until hydrogel was fully gelled. The receptor compartment consisted of 15 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4). To maintain sink conditions, at regular intervals (0.5 h, 1 h, 3 h, and 6 h) the dialysis bags were switched into fresh medium. For quantitative analysis of CAP was performed as described in the “HPLC analysis” section (Sect. "Captopril quantification in micelles and hydrogels"). The cumulative percentage of CAP release from CAP-HG and free CAP was plotted against time to study the release behavior.

Rheological assessment of thermoresponsive and visco-elastic behavior

Rheology testing of hydrogels (drug-loaded and drug-free controls) was carried out using the Discovery HR20 Rheometer (TA Instruments, Waters™ USA). Viscoelastic and thermoresponsive behavior of the hydrogels was characterized by determining the storage and loss modulus by studying the amplitude sweep, frequency sweep, temperature ramp, oscillation temperature ramp, and oscillation time sweep study using previously reported methods46 with some modifications. All tests were run in triplicate. In all tests, the hydrogel samples were kept on ice prior to each experiment, and the Peltier plate was brought down to 22 °C before the initiation of each run. The runs used a 20 mm parallel plate, with stainless steel geometry. The loading gap was 45 mm with 0.35 mL of sample loaded. The trim gap of 1.05 mm was used to remove the excess sample and the geometry was used at a final gap of 1 mm. The solvent trap was used to nullify the loss of sample due to evaporation. Oscillation temperature ramp and time ramp study were done from 20 °C and 50 °C at a constant strain of 0.1% with an angular frequency of 10 rad/s. Amplitude sweep studies were carried out at 37 °C with a 15-min wait time, and the strain percentage was varied from 0.1 to 100%, using a constant angular frequency of 10 rad/s to determine the storage and loss modulus. Similarly, frequency sweep measurements were carried out by using a constant strain percent of 0.1% which was within the linear viscoelastic range and the angular frequency was varied from 0.1 to 100 rad/s to determine the storage and loss modulus at 37 °C. Temperature ramp studies were carried out between 5 °C and 50 °C to determine sol-to-gel transition temperature. A wait time of 1 min was given before the measurements and a ramp rate of 5 °C/min was used with a shear rate of 100/s. Alternate step strain tests were performed at varying strains of 0.1% and 100% repeatedly and with varying temperatures between 37 °C and 20 °C.

Cell viability

Cell viability upon hydrogel exposure (drug-loaded and drug-free controls) was assessed using the RAW 264.7 macrophage cell line (ATCC TIB 71) following previously reported protocols53. Briefly, macrophages were plated at 5,000 cells per well in 96 well plates, incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2, and cultured for 24 h. CAP-HG and drug-free hydrogels (DF-HG) were diluted starting from 80 µL up to 0.625 µL hydrogel / mL media. Concentration-matched free captopril dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was used as a free drug control. Viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Glo 2.0® assay kit. Post 24-h treatment the media was removed and replaced with 100 µL of fresh culture media. 40 µL of CellTiter-Glo reagent was added and the plate was shaken at 70 rpm for 20 min. Cell lysates were transferred from the culture plate to an opaque white 96-well plate and run for Luminescence (BioTek Synergy HTX Multi-Mode reader).

Inhibition of ACE activity by CAP-HG and controls

To test the ability of CAP-HG to inhibit angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE), the wells in a 96-well flat-bottomed plate were filled with 200 µL of high glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin / streptomycin and 20 mM HEPES. 20 min prior to addition of angiotensin I (Ang I; Ang II precursor), CAP-HG or DF-HG were added. Increasing concentrations of Ang I (0, 0.5, 5, 50, 500 ng/ml) were then introduced and Ang I was allowed to convert over 30 min to Ang II. In a follow-up experiment, conversion of Ang I to Ang II by RAW264.7 macrophages was inhibited in a similar manner. For this experiment, 10,000 cells in 100 µL media/well were cultured in high glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 20 mM HEPES overnight. The next day the media was aspirated and serum-free media containing free captopril, CAP-HG, DF-HG, or no treatment was added to the cells. 20 min later, 1 µg/mL of Ang I was added to the media and given 5 h to convert to Ang II. After both experiments the supernatants obtained were stored at − 80 °C prior to performing an Ang II enzyme immunoassay (EIA; RayBioTech) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. For these experiments, CAP-HG treatments were based on a captopril concentration of 460.21 μM (0.1 mg/mL) and DF-HG volumes were the same as CAP-HG volumes.

Animals

The use of animals in this study and all associated methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All experiments involving animals were approved by the MD Anderson Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and Animal Care and Use Review Office (ACURO)—USAMRDC. Data is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org). Male Leprdb and LeprWT (WT) (BKS.Cg-Dock7m + / + Leprdb/J) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME) at approximately 10 weeks of age and used in experiments between 18–20 weeks of age. Mice were housed two to five per cage in a temperature (22 °C ± 1 °C), and humidity (40–60%) controlled room. The room was kept on a 12-h light dark cycle, and mice had access to food and water ad libitum.

Inflammatory mediator quantification

To compare baseline differences in hindpaw inflammatory mediator content, hindpaws from WT and Leprdb mice were collected after transcardial perfusion with 20 mL of cold 1X PBS. For comparison of DF-HG and CAP-HG, hindpaws were collected 72 h after intraplantar injection of DF-HG or CAP-HG (10 µL) into both hindpaws. To generate hindpaw lysates for analysis, the hindpaw of each mouse was digested in fresh homogenization buffer (50 mL of deionized water containing 5.48 g sucrose/0.5 mL of 5 M sodium phosphate, 0.21 g sodium fluoride, 1X Halt protease inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% (v/v) Triton X-100) for 1.5 h at 4 °C while being gently rotated to aid digestion. Hindpaws were then placed into a 12-well plate and the plunger of a 1 mL syringe was used to gently disrupt the remaining tissue. The suspension was pipetted from the 12-well plate into individual 1.5 mL tubes and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C prior to storage at − 80 °C. To perform the proteome profile assays, a bicinchoninic acid assay was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions to determine the protein content of each sample. Proteome profiles were performed using a mouse cytokine XL array (Biotechne) incubated with 800 µg of total protein from each sample. Analysis of the cytokine content of lysates obtained from mouse hindpaws was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions and quantified using an Amersham ImageQuant 800 blot scanner and ImageQuant TL toolbox v8.2.0 software (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences).

Von frey test of mechanical sensitivity

10 μL of 1X PBS was injected into the left hindpaw and 10 μL of CAP-HG or DF-HG was injected into the right hindpaw of WT and Leprdb mice (n = 7–8). Von Frey filaments (0.04–2.00 g) were presented five times to both hindpaws of each mouse progressing from the smallest to the largest filament, as described previously22,23. The number of responses to each filament was then used to generate an area under the curve (AUC) value for each mouse. These measurements were performed before the hydrogels were injected (baseline) and 1, 3, and 7 days post-injection.

In vivo near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) imaging

Two-color fluorescent nanoemulsions, which we have used previously to track macrophages in vivo in the Leprdb model53, were injected (5 μL / g up to a maximum of 200 μL) i.v. 3 days prior to subcutaneous plantar injections of 10 µL 1X PBS (left hindpaw) and DF-HG or CAP-HG (right hindpaw) in WT and Leprdb mice (n = 5). DiR signal was detected with an IVIS Lumina system (Caliper Life Sciences; Waltham, MA) using the auto exposure setting immediately prior to hydrogel injection and 4 days after injection to detect potential variations in macrophage density caused by the hydrogel. To detect DiR from the nanoemulsion a 745 nm (bandpass: 20 nm) excitation and 800 nm (bandpass: 35 nm) emission filter were used. For analysis of DiR signal from each mouse, the average radiant efficiency [p/s/cm2/sr]/[µW/cm2] of the hydrogel-injected hindpaw was compared to the PBS-injected hindpaw for the same mouse to identify inflammatory changes that were caused by DF-HG or CAP-HG injection.

Blood pressure and thermal imaging

Blood pressure and thermal images were taken on day 2 post-injection to measure effects of CAP-HG and DF-HG on systemic and local vasodilation in WT and Leprdb mice (n = 5). Similar to the injections for von Frey and NIRF imaging, 10 μL of 1X PBS was injected into the left hindpaw and 10 μL of CAP-HG or DF-HG was injected into the right hindpaw. For measurement of systolic blood pressure, WT and Leprdb mice (n = 5) were placed in restraints on top of a warmer platform to habituate for 30 min the day before the experiment. On the day of the experiment, mice were restrained and placed on the warmer for 10 min to acclimate before recording blood pressure using the tail cuff-based Coda High Throughput System (Kent Scientific). A total of 15 readings were recorded using Coda software version 4.1 and an average was taken for systolic blood pressure. On the same day a FLIR C5 camera (Teledyne) was used to capture thermal images of the hindpaws of WT and Leprdb treated with CAP-HG and DF-HG. FLIR Thermal Studio software was used to measure the difference in temperature between the PBS-injected hindpaw (left) and the hydrogel-injected hindpaw (right).

Immunofluorescent detection of IENF density

10 μL plantar injections of CAP-HG or DF-HG were performed on WT and Leprdb mice (n = 5). Guided by the results of the von Frey analysis, mice were euthanized 3 days post injection using CO2 followed by perfusion with 20 mL of cold 1X PBS. Plantar skin biopsies were then placed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 °C, followed by 30% sucrose for 2 days at 4 °C. Samples were embedded in Tissue-Tek® OCT compound (Sakura) for cryosectioning. Sections from each sample were cut at 30 μm, placed on Superfrost Plus slides (Fisher Scientific) and stored at − 20 °C until staining. To prepare the tissue samples for staining, slides were removed from − 20 °C and warmed at 37 °C for 1 h. A hydrophobic barrier was then drawn on each slide using an ImmEdge™ Pen (Vector Laboratories). Slides were washed 10 times in PBS to remove residual OCT before adding blocking solution [1X PBS, 10% (v/v) goat serum (Millipore Sigma), 1% Bovine serum albumin (Fisher Scientific), 0.3% Triton X-100 (Fisher Scientific)]. Slides were incubated in blocking solution for 1 h at RT. The blocking solution was replaced with rabbit anti-PGP9.5 (1:250) (Abcam; Clone: EPR4118) antibody diluted in blocking solution. The primary antibody was incubated overnight for at 4 °C in a humidified slide staining chamber. The next day the slides were rinsed 10 × in PBS, and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500) (Invitrogen) and DAPI (500 ng/ml; Millipore Sigma) were added to each slide in PBS. The secondary antibodies and DAPI were incubated for 3 h at RT. The sections were rinsed 10 × in PBS and mounted with FluorSave mounting medium (Millipore Sigma). Images were obtained using 40x (Numerical aperture:1.30) objective on a Nikon A1R Confocal Microscope (Nikon Instruments Inc., Melville, NY), and image analysis was performed using Nikon NIS-Elements Advanced Research Software (Nikon Instruments Inc.). To quantify the number of intra-epidermal nerve fibers (IENFs) present within each skin sample, the number of IENFs were counted and the length of the skin section in each image was recorded, such that IENF density per 200 μm could be represented.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism Software (version 9). Unpaired, two-tailed, t-tests were performed for the release kinetics samples. Two-way ANOVA were performed on data obtained from serum Ang II immunoassays, von Frey, IVIS imaging, thermal imaging, blood pressure tests, and IENF calculations. One-way ANOVA was performed on the RAW 264.7 cell Ang II immunoassays. Post-hoc analysis was performed using Šidák’s multiple comparisons test for serum Ang II immunoassays, von Frey, thermal imaging, blood pressure tests, and IENF calculations. Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was used for RAW 264.7 cell-based Ang II immunoassays and NIRF live image analyses and signal quantification.

Results

To achieve local delivery of CAP, which is highly soluble and permeable BCS Class I drug, we used a two-stage drug loading strategy into a hydrogel that is administered subcutaneously using standard needles and syringes. Mixed micelle were first prepared with two non-ionic surfactants (Pluronic P105® and Cr-EL) and then dispersed into the matrix, an aqueous solution of two pluronics (F127® and F68®), by a gentle planetary mixing54. This pluronics combination produced a soft, injectable hydrogel with an optimal sol–gel transition temperature above 20 °C which can be mixed with nanosized delivery systems36. Therefore, the Pluronic P105/Cr-EL mixed micelle is as primary carrier for CAP which is further dispersed into the Pluronic F127/F68 mixed micelle assembled hydrogel. We hypothesized that a small micellar size (~ 15–25 nm) would possibly avoid uptake by local phagocytic cells (e.g. macrophages)55, providing preferable delivery of CAP to the DPN affected nerve terminals upon local hydrogel injection. We present here in detail micelle and hydrogel formulation and characterization in vitro including ACE inhibiting action in macrophages in vitro and in vivo impact on DPN pathology in Leprdb mice. Formulation of captopril loaded micelles and hydrogels

The presented hydrogels are complex colloidal structures with two types of micelle, Pluronic F127/F68 micelle forming the hydrogel matrix with drug loaded micelle dispersed into the matrix (Fig. 1A). CAP incorporation into the aqueous phase of the hydrogel would not lead to significant retention in situ upon injection, due to the drug’s high solubility and high permeability. Further, the goal was to deliver CAP extracellularly while avoiding premature local metabolism. Small size micelle offers drug stabilization and potential avoidance of the phagocytic uptake upon release from the locally applied hydrogels55. To incorporate CAP into the hydrogel we first packaged it into mixed micelle prepared from Pluronic P105 and Cr-EL non-ionic surfactants at a 2:3 w/w ratio, based on our earlier reported mixed surfactant micellar systems56. The resultant mixed micelles were small size (15–20 nm) with narrow distribution which remains unchanged upon two levels of CAP loading to achieve a final CAP concentration of 12.5 mg/mL (1X CAP-M) and 25 mg/mL (2X CAP-M), Fig. 1B. The drug and surfactants were mixed in an organic solvent, followed by thin film formation by solvent evaporation and micelle incorporation of the drug during the hydration step that promotes self-assembly of the mixed micelle. Once drug loaded micelles were formed they were incorporated into a Pluronic based hydrogel matrix by planetary mixing as reported earlier, providing a homogenous drug distribution46,54. The micelle formation was highly reproducible, showing consistent narrow distribution and small size across multiple batches (Supplemental Fig. S1A). We compared the shelf-life of CAP-loaded micelles (CAP-M) when freshly prepared (Day 0) and after 30 days storage at 4 °C. Micelles remained stable with no change in size and size distribution (Fig. 1C). The stability of CAP-M was further evaluated under stress conditions by adapting earlier reported methods49. Size distribution was not affected by filtration of the micelles (Supplemental Fig. S1B). When incubated in cell culture media for 72 h at 37 °C, the micellar size slightly increased, likely due to protein adsorption (Supplemental Fig. S1C). After confirming that the colloidal stability and cell viability remained above 80% upon exposure to high doses in cell culture (Supplemental Fig. S1D), we proceeded to optimize drug- CAP-M encapsulation into Pluronic F127/Pluronic F68 mixed hydrogels. The colloidal properties of the gels were also evaluated by Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) upon dissolution in water at ambient temperature. CAP-M and drug-free micelle (DF-M) were used to prepare drug-loaded (CAP-HG) and drug-free (DF-HG) hydrogels by planetary mixing54. The micellar size in hydrogels was determined by pre-dissolving gels in deionized water at typical concentration of 2–5% and then measuring by DLS at ambient temperature. Unsurprisingly, the mixed micelle laden pluronic hydrogel gave consistent but broader size distribution profiles over micelle measured alone once dissolved into water, with PDI = 0.36. This value was significantly larger than for free micelle, Fig. 1D, while the distribution remained unchanged with drug incorporation. Next, we compared three different batches of captopril micelle-loaded hydrogels and did not observe any significant batch-to-batch variation in size distribution profiles (Supplemental Fig. S2A). The colloidal stability of the hydrogels was further evaluated by testing the micellar size variation of freshly prepared samples with samples stored for 90 days at 4 °C (Fig. 1E). Gels retained the same micelle size distribution profile when made in water and buffered solution (Supplementary Fig. S2B) Additional stress tests, centrifugation and exposure to cell culture media, showed no significant changes in size distribution, demonstrating that micelles retain their colloidal properties and stability upon release from gels (Supplementary Fig. S2C,D).

Captopril hydrogels are thermoresponsive, viscoelastic, and injectable

The thermoresponsive behavior is shown in Figure S3A in the vial inversion test where CAP-HG behaves as a liquid at 20 °C and sol-to-gel transition occurs after incubation at 37 °C for 20 min forming a transparent gel. Viscoelastic behavior of the hydrogel was quantified by measuring the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) as a function of oscillation strain. In both Fig. 2A and C, the amplitude sweep studies of DF-HG and CAP-HG illustrate a higher G’ value than G” which implies that the gels behave more like an elastic solid than viscous liquid. However, the crossover point where G’ = G” after which the liquid state dominates. Time-dependent stability was also shown by comparing G’ and G” at day 1 and day 7 (Supplemental Fig. S3B and C) which were almost similar. The oscillation frequency sweep in Fig. S3D and E showed that both storage and loss modulus are almost independent of angular frequency. Comparable data between DF-HG and CAP-HG were consistent with the fact that CAP has no significant impact on the gel strength.

OA tests were performed using an angular frequency of 10 rad/s at a strain range of 0.1% to 100% at 37℃. (B,D) Temperature ramp studies of DF-HG and CAP-HG respectively were performed from 5℃ to 50℃ with a ramp rate of 5℃/min. The temperature ramp studies showed the thermoresponsiveness of DF-HG and CAP-HG under a constant shear (Fig. 2B and D). For animal studies, injection at ambient temperature is feasible due to reduced viscosity evident from rheological tests. Both DF-HG and CAP-HG react quickly at the transition temperature and attain a significantly higher viscosity at physiological temperature. This behavior ensures that the gel depot will be retained locally without flowing to other unwanted sites when administered into the body. Due to its reversibly thermoresponsive behavior37, pluronic F127 was used as the primary polymer which when mixed with pluronic F68 in an aqueous solution, creates a viscous liquid at temperatures less than 25 °C, and rapidly transitions into a gel at physiological temperature around 37 °C. In the oscillation temperature ramp study, the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) were plotted as a function of temperature, starting from 20 °C to 50 °C (Fig. 2E). Around 36 °C, the two moduli overlapped indicating the gelation temperature of CAP-HG. In Fig. 2F, gelation time was measured by plotting the moduli against step time. The crossover between storage and loss modulus starts around 190 s and the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) starts at around 250 s. The crossover point of the moduli is the starting point of gelation and LVR can be regarded as complete gelation. This confirms that the gelation time of CAP-HG is around 60 s. The alternate step strain test proves the reversibility in terms of viscoelastic behavior upon exposure and withdrawal of strain in a repeated fashion (Fig. 2G). At a lower strain of 0.1%, CAP-HG behaves as a hydrogel but at a higher strain of 100%, the loss modulus is higher than the storage modulus indicating sol-like behavior. However, withdrawal of strain will again result in hydrogel formation of initial strength. For injectable hydrogel, this reversibility is much needed to form a gel after injection. In Fig. 2H, the moduli are plotted against temperature to show the reversible thermoresponsiveness of CAP-HG. At 37 °C, the hydrogel is in a complete gel state which is evident from the storage modulus value of 104, similar to the storage modulus in the amplitude sweep test in Fig. 2C. However, at a lower temperature of 20 °C, the storage modulus is around 103 which is similar to the loss modulus value at 37 °C, and after increasing again to a higher temperature of 37 °C gives moduli of complete gel. This test, along with the vial inversion test shown in Fig. S3A, are complementary to each other to prove the reversible thermoresponsive behavior of CAP-HG.

Sterility and captopril release kinetics

Due to the multiple steps involved in creating the final product CAP-HG maintaining sterility approved to be a challenge. For hydrogels intended for in vivo use and cell culture experiments, it is imperative that the sterility of the hydrogels be maintained post-manufacturing during storage and use. CAP-HG and DF-HG were tested according to USP 34 microbiological test/ 71 Sterility testing guidelines. The guidelines recommend that no aerobic, anaerobic, and fungal growth be observed upon incubation for 14 days in microbiological media. Thioglycollate media is intended for the growth of anaerobic bacteria. However, it also allows aerobic growth. The pH of the media is maintained at near neutral and should not change upon incubation with the test product. Trypcase soy broth allows for the growth of aerobic bacteria as well as fungi and is as well maintained at pH 7.0. Thioglycolate media is incubated at 35 °C and trypcase soy is incubated at 25 °C with respective controls as shown in Fig. 3A–D. As shown, the pH of the media did not change for DF-HG or CAP-HG after 14 days of incubation, suggesting a lack of growth of microbes. Furthermore, a visual inspection for change in turbidity of the media was also completed and no significant difference was found. The gels pass the sterility test of 14 days and are cleared for use in culture experiments and animal studies. Once sterile produced, gels are packaged in sealed sterile autoclaved vials and refrigerated until used. For CAP quantification we developed an HPLC method; a standard curve run in triplicate is shown in Fig. 3E. The in vitro release behavior of CAP from CAP-HG and free CAP was studied using a dialysis membrane at a controlled temperature of 37 °C ± 0.5 °C51,52. The percentage cumulative release of CAP from CAP-HG was 30.5 ± 3.1% within the first half-hour at 37 °C. However, CAP solution demonstrated a relative burst release pattern, as 53.3 ± 1.9% of CAP was released in the same duration (Fig. 3F). The release from CAP-HG was more sustained as compared to free CAP, as 64.1 ± 5.3% of CAP was released from CAP-HG at the end of six hours, versus 92.2 ± 2.5% CAP release from the free CAP solution. Overall, at each time point, the release from CAP-HG was significantly lower than the free CAP, demonstrating a sustained release profile.

Cell viability and conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II inhibition

To ensure the pharmacological results were not confounded by any toxic effects of either gels or CAP we evaluated the impact of DF-HG and CAP-HG on viability of RAW264.7 macrophages after 24-h exposure. Both gels are non-toxic and cell viability was maintained above 80% for all concentrations tested. Concentrations were deliberately chosen to be higher than those needed in pharmacological tests and animal studies. There were no statistical differences between the viability of cells treated with DF-HG versus CAP-HG (Fig. 4A). We also compared the effect of free CAP to CAP-HG for 24 h at matched drug concentrations (Fig. 4B). Our results indicate that free CAP exposure leads to significant macrophage cytotoxicity when compared to the highest concentrations of CAP-HG, which supports our hypothesis that slower drug release and buffered vehicle for local delivery of CAP is superior to the use of free drug solution. Cell viability data was collected prior to testing the ability of CAP released from CAP-HG in cell culture to inhibit ACE-mediated conversion of Ang I into Ang II.

In addition to examining the cytotoxicity of these formulations, we also tested the effectiveness of captopril within the CAP-HG formulation on ACE inhibition. To do this we designed two angiotensin conversion experiments which used ACE endogenously present in fetal bovine serum or expressed by RAW264.7 cells to convert Ang I to Ang II and tested the inhibitory capacity of CAP-HG against various controls. To determine the effectiveness of ACE inhibition by CAP-HG, cell culture media containing 10% fetal bovine serum was dosed with CAP-HG or DF-HG prior to addition of Ang I. Ang I was converted to Ang II when media was pre-treated with DF-HG, but this process was significantly inhibited when media was pre-treated with CAP-HG (Fig. 4C). To recapitulate these results under live-cell conditions, cultures of RAW 264.7 macrophages in serum-free media were pre-treated with either free CAP, DF-HG or CAP-HG before adding Ang I. Under these conditions, cells exposed to free CAP or CAP-HG showed similar inhibition of Ang II formation when compared to cells exposed to DF-HG or no treatment (Fig. 4D). These data show that CAP-HG significantly inhibits the conversion of Ang I to Ang II by ACE as compared to DF-HG. Combined with the comparable suppression by free CAP and CAP-HG in serum, this indicates that CAP-HG has no significant loss of function compared to free CAP. This data suggested direct engagement of CAP with ACE upon release in biologically relevant media and in the presence of cells at the comparable timeline to our drug release studies (Fig. 3F). These angiotensin conversion experiments clearly demonstrate that CAP-HG is capable of inhibiting production of Ang II in vitro, and that this inhibition is comparable to that of free captopril. Taken together these results suggest that when captopril is embedded in our hydrogel it protects RAW 264.7 cells from the toxicity seen with higher doses of free CAP while maintaining the ability to inhibit ACE activity.

Leprdb mouse hindpaw tissues show signs of metabolic and inflammatory dysregulation

The systemic pro-inflammatory state associated with DPN is thought to contribute to the development of neuropathy57. In order to assess the inflammatory microenvironment of the Leprdb mouse hindpaw, we analyzed homogenates of hindpaw tissue from wild-type (WT) and Leprdb mice using proteome profile arrays (Fig. 5A). Comparing fold-change in expression of a broad panel of cytokines, chemokines and growth factors showed the expected substantial inflammatory dysregulation in Leprdb hindpaw samples compared to age-matched WT mice (n = 4; Fig. 5B). The data identify differential expression of factors in Leprdb mice with established associations with metabolic derangement, including leptin, receptor for advanced glycated end-products (RAGE), dipeptidylpeptidase-4 (DPPIV), delta-like non-canonical Notch ligand-1 (DLK-1), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor and adiponectin58–62. This indicates that there is substantial inflammatory dysregulation in diabetic mouse hindpaws, which we hypothesize could be normalized by local delivery of an ACE inhibitor in the form of CAP-HG.

CAP-HG biocompatibility in vivo

Before assessing the efficacy of CAP-HG, we first wanted to determine whether DF-HG was well-tolerated in vivo. Cytokine arrays of WT and Leprdb mouse hindpaw homogenates were generated 2 days after intraplantar DF-HG injection. Figure 6A shows the proteins with a > 1.5-fold change in expression in Leprdb mice injected with DF-HG versus WT mice injected with DF-HG. As expected, many of the factors identified as differentially expressed in un-injected Leprdb hindpaws remained dysregulated in Leprdb mice after DF-HG injection, including regenerating family member 3-gamma (Reg3g), resistin, DPPIV, RAGE, LDL receptor, DLK-1, CCL20, leptin and CRP. The extensive overlap with expression changes noted in naïve WT and Leprdb hindpaws (Fig. 6B) suggests that there are no major pro- or anti-inflammatory effects of DF-HG in Leprdb mice. Consistent with this finding, labeling of macrophage infiltration in mouse hindpaws using NIRF imaging of two-color nanoemulsions showed similar DiR fluorescence before and after intraplantar injection of DF-HG or CAP-HG (Fig. 6B). This was confirmed by observing no significant difference in CAP-HG or DF-HG-injected hindpaw fluorescence when compared to the contralateral hindpaw which received an equal volume of 1X PBS as an injection control (Fig. 6C). Overall, this suggests that the hydrogel platform is well-tolerated in vivo, and that intraplanar injection of hydrogel does not substantially affect the inflammatory microenvironment of the hindpaw.

CAP-HG restores sensitivity to the hindpaws of Leprdb mice with minimal systemic effects

To determine the effectiveness of CAP-HG in the treatment of DPN, WT and Leprdb mice were injected with either CAP-HG or DF-HG. Von Frey analysis of these mice showed that prior to injection Leprdb mice had a significantly reduced sensitivity to tactile stimulation compared to WT mice (Fig. 7A). No change in mechanical sensitivity was noted after DF-HG injection in WT or Leprdb mice. However, after injection with CAP-HG, Leprdb mice showed an increase toward normal levels of sensitivity in the injected foot at 1 day and 3 days post-injection with a return to mechanical hyposensitivity by seven days post injection (Fig. 7B). No significant changes in mechanical sensitivity occurred in hindpaws of WT mice that received CAP-HG (Fig. 7B). We next assessed whether CAP-HG was able to blunt pro-inflammatory mediator expression in Leprdb mouse hindpaws. 48 h after CAP-HG injection, the only protein that remained elevated in Leprdb mice was resistin. The proteins that were downregulated in Leprdb mice after CAP-HG injection were: matrix metallopeptidases -9 and -3 (MMP-9/MMP-3), epidermal growth factor (EGF), insulin-like growth factor-binding protein-5 (IGFBP-5), Chitinase 3-like 1, MMP-3 and Complement Factor D (Fig. 7C). This suggests that many of the differentially-expressed inflammatory mediators that were detected in naïve Leprdb mouse hindpaws (Fig. 5B) and were unaltered by DF-HG injection (Fig. 6A) are normalized by CAP-HG injection at a timepoint where restoration of normal sensory sensitivity was detectable. To determine the effects of CAP-HG on systemic blood pressure and local vasodilation, hindpaw temperature (Fig. 7D and E) and systolic blood pressure (Fig. 7F) were measured 2 days after DF-HG or CAP-HG injection. For these experiments sterile PBS was injected into the left hindpaw and DF-HG or CAP-HG were injected into the right hindpaw. Thermal imaging showed no significant differences in hindpaw temperature between injection groups. The average temperature difference between the HG-injected hindpaw and the PBS-injected hindpaw in WT mice was + 0.2 (± 0.6) °C for CAP-HG and − 0.5 (± 0.7) °C for DF-HG. The average temperature differentials for Leprdb CAP-HG and Leprdb DF-HG were 0.5 (± 0.8) °C and 0.2 (± 0.6) °C respectively (Fig. 7D and E). The average systolic blood pressure for WT mice that received CAP-HG or DF-HG was 102.9 (± 10.6) and 130.2 (± 28.8) mm Hg respectively, while the average for Leprdb CAP-HG and Leprdb DF-HG was 111.6 (± 12.5) and 128.9 (± 16.6 mm) Hg respectively (Fig. 7F). Taken together with the results from our Von Frey analysis, this suggests a return of functional mechanical sensitivity to the hindpaws of Leprdb mice after local treatment with CAP-HG, without overt changes in blood flow to the foot or significant alteration of systemic blood pressure. Finally, we determined whether the behavioral effects seen with CAP-HG injection were caused by modulation of previously reported deficits in IENF density in Leprdb mice at 18 weeks of age69. In our experiments, we found that 3 days after injection of either CAP-HG or DF-HG, there was no significant increase in IENF density in WT or Leprdb mice (Fig. 7G and H), suggesting that the improvement in mechanical sensitivity seen in Leprdb mice after injection of CAP-HG was not associated with alterations in cutaneous innervation.

Discussion

A major problem faced by diabetic patients today is that over half of them will eventually develop neuropathy as a sequela to their diabetes70. Currently there are no disease-modifying drugs for diabetic neuropathy, and even the efficacy of glycemic control for limiting progression of the disease is questionable in T2D71. One class of drugs that has recently shown some promise for use in DPN are ACE inhibitors, which are prescribed to some diabetic patients for control of hypertension15. In the present study, we developed and characterized a thermoresponsive injectable hydrogel for local delivery of ACE inhibitor captopril in the Leprdb mouse model to ameliorate the symptoms of DPN. We evaluated the novel delivery strategy both in vitro and in vivo. A major challenge of formulating in situ hydrogels for local injection is product sterility. Despite the ability of these gels to flow at ambient temperatures, they are too viscous to be filtered. Instead, we presented a method where pluronic hydrogels can be produced under controlled conditions to achieve sterility without need for post-production sterilization. The gels were found to be colloidally stable and rheological test confirmed their thermoresponsive behavior. The presented formulations are in sol state at ambient temperature for easy injection, and undergo sol to gel transition at body temperature, which is helpful to maintain the product at the site of application. In vitro cell culture experiments showed the hydrogels are non-toxic in relevant concentration range where they showed effective ACE inhibition, suggesting the drug is releasing and available to its target in biological media and presence of cells. These tests led to in vivo studies aimed to demonstrate ACE inhibition via locally delivered captopril can control neuroinflammation and produce improvement in mechanical sensitivity in DPN.

After confirming the safety and efficacy of our formulation in vitro we began our in vivo experiments by examining the effects of DF-HG and CAP-HG on proteome of the foot microenvironment after subcutaneous injection of our hydrogels. In both the un-injected animals and the DF-HG injected animals, several of the same proteins showed differential expression in Leprdb mice. Of note, in both sets of mice VEGF, DPPIV, resistin, leptin, and CRP were elevated, and they have all been previously reported to be upregulated in diabetic patients63–72. In contrast, the LDL receptor, which was upregulated in both un-injected and DF-HG injected mice, has been previously reported to be downregulated in patients with T2D, which might suggest a differential pathology in rodent models and patients with diabetes76. Additionally, previous reports showed that MMP9 concentrations are increased in the serum of diabetic patients with nephropathy and in foot ulcers of diabetic patients. In these reports, however, the increase in MMP9 appeared to correspond with pathological conditions, which might suggest that our mice have not reached the stage of clinical diabetes critical for the upregulation of MMP977,78. Of the proteins increased by > 1.5-fold in Leprdb, the only protein to remain upregulated after treatment with CAP-HG was resistin. Considering the role for resistin in insulin resistance, these results might suggest that although we see a large shift in the proteome of the hindpaw, CAP-HG likely does not resolve insulin resistance74. After injection of CAP-HG, a head-to-head comparison of WT vs Leprdb mice showed the total number of proteins with a greater than 1.5-fold change was reduced to only seven proteins. This indicates that CAP-HG normalized the microenvironment of the Leprdb hindpaw to more closely resemble that of WT mice. When these results are considered in tandem with the results from our IVIS imaging showing no significant increase in macrophage infiltration after CAP-HG injection, we conclude that this shift in the proteome of the hind foot occurs without invoking an overt inflammatory response from the innate immune system.

To better understand the therapeutic potential of our new CAP-HG formulation in a model of DPN, Leprdb and WT mice were given intraplantar injections of CAP-HG or DF-HG, and Von Frey testing was performed on the injected foot pad over the following seven days. In these experiments Leprdb mice exhibited significant hyposensitivity at baseline which was subsequently eliminated by treatment with CAP-HG, and unchanged by DF-HG, for 3 days after injection. By seven days post injection Leprdb mice had returned to baseline levels of hyposensitivity. This result in conjunction with the results obtained from the proteome profile would suggest that DPN is reversed in the Leprdb model through a normalization of the proteome of the hind paw; however, it did not provide a mechanistic reason for the reversal of the hyposensitivity. Since ACE inhibitors are potent vasodilators, and previous work by Coppey et al.15 suggested that restoration of blood flow to neural tissues by enalapril was responsible for reversal of neuropathy in a model of type I diabetes, we examined both local and systemic blood flow for alterations after injection of CAP-HG. Mice were injected with either DF-HG or CAP-HG and at two days post injection, a time point which was chosen based on our Von Frey results, we measured systemic blood pressure and regional temperature changes in the foot pads of Leprdb and WT mice. Interestingly, neither the systemic blood pressure nor the temperature of the CAP-HG injected foot with respect to mice injected with DF-HG, which would suggest that restoration of vascular integrity is not the mechanism by which the proteome and sensitivity of the hind paw are restored. One of the diagnostic tests used to confirm DPN in human patients is measurement of IENF density in skin samples of the distal limb. It has been reported that Leprdb mice share this pathology69,79, and although we did not observe a significant difference in the number of IENFs present with foot pad samples from our Leprdb mice as compared to WT mice, we did note a moderate decrease in IENF density which appeared unchanged by injection of CAP-HG and DF-HG. Thus, it would appear that restoration of neuronal outgrowth is not the mechanism by which CAP-HG reversed sensory loss in Leprdb. Nevertheless, our study indicates that local delivery of ACE inhibitors may represent a viable means of restoring normal sensory sensitivity in patients with DPN and sheds new light on the role of locally-released Ang II in neuroinflammation and neuropathy.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge this is the first report of local delivery of an ACE inhibitor achieved with a sterile, safe, thermoresponsive hydrogel in a DPN model. We show that ACE inhibitor captopril local delivery reversal of the neurosensory symptoms lasted several days without measurable adverse systemic effects. The presented work also suggests that ACE inhibition in DPN is a promising future therapeutic strategy leading to recovery from sensory loss, one of the major complications of DPN. The injectable hydrogel also offers the means of repeated treatment in DPN by safe and non-iritating local injection. The limitation of the study lies in the fact we only tested one dose. Future studies are underway investigating the effects of repeated dosing of CAP loaded hydrogels and their effects on DPN symptoms. DPN is a major complication of diabetes and local delivery strategies are promising approach to reduce systemic drug burden, decrease associated side effects and open the doors to more personalized and adjustable treatments.
