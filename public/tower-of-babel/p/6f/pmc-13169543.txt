Ethnographic mapping of edible insects in Tanzania: cultural knowledge, biodiversity, and food system implications
Authors: Mercy Mmari, Suleiman Rashid, RPC Temu, John Kinyuru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEdible insects have long contributed to food security, nutrition, and cultural identity in sub-Saharan Africa, yet systematic ethnographic documentation remains limited in many countries, including Tanzania. The lack of coordinated data constrains policy integration, conservation planning, and safe food system development.MethodsThis study employed a mixed-methods ethnographic design combining geo-referenced household surveys (n = 131), semi-structured interviews, key informant discussions, participant observations, and specimen collection across nine regions of Tanzania (2022–2025). Geographic Information Systems (GIS) were used to map existence of insects in their habitats as mentioned by the respondents for harvest and consumption hotspots, while qualitative data were thematically analyzed to document cultural practices, taboos, and perceptions related to edible insects.ResultsOver 40 edible insect taxa across six taxonomic orders were documented, consumed by 29 ethnic groups. Orthoptera and Lepidoptera were the most widely consumed orders. Consumption patterns were strongly shaped by cultural norms, seasonality, ecological habitats, and sensory preferences. Edible insects were integrated into child feeding practices, with82% of respondents reporting feeding insects to children under five years. Medicinal and therapeutic uses were widely reported, though some adverse health effects were linked to poor hygiene and processing practices rather than inherent toxicity.ConclusionEdible insects remain culturally embedded, nutritionally valued, and ecologically significant in Tanzania. However, erosion of indigenous knowledge, habitat pressures, and limited food safety awareness threaten their sustainability. This study provides the first national ethnographic baseline to inform nutrition policy, biodiversity conservation, and sustainable insect-based food system development in Tanzania.

Introduction

Although entomophagy has deep historical roots globally, including in Classical societies such as Ancient Rome and Greece, its traditional role has often been underreported. This framing overlooks the fact that insects have long been consumed across the world, as evidenced by biblical references (Leviticus 11:20–23; Matthew 3:4) and archaeological records documenting their prehistorical uses in European countries including Spain and France [1]. This historical neglect has contributed to framing edible insects as a novel food, shaping cultural resistance and complicating their inclusion in modern food systems [2]. In Tanzania, however, edible insects are recognized in the national food guideline as an important part of the human diet [3]. This study provides the first comprehensive ethnographic mapping of edible insects in Tanzania, linking cultural practices with their ecological distribution.

Global food systems are under increasing pressure from population expansion, climate change, and the rising demand for animal-source proteins [4]. By 2050 the world population is projected to reach approximately (9) nine billion, presenting significant challenges for sustainable food production and nutritional security [5]. Entomophagy, holds potential to stand in the gap as a natural ancient practice, predating modern food systems and remaining deeply rooted in the traditional food culture of approximately two billion people worldwide, particularly across Asia, Latin America, Africa, and Oceania [6]. Unlike novel, highly industrialized food alternatives such as cultured meats, edible insects are natural, traditional foods that have evolved alongside human societies and the planet, offering high nutritional value with relatively low environmental costs compared with conventional livestock [1, 7, 8].

Beyond sustainability, nutritional aspects are equally critical, edible insects are rich in protein (35–80% dry matter, depending on species and life stage), essential fatty acids, and micronutrients such as iron and zinc, positioning them as important supplementary foods for addressing malnutrition [9]. Indigenous beliefs, and taboos shape which species are consumed by who, when at which stage and how they are prepared. Among the Haya community, for example, serving the longhorn grasshopper Ruspolia differens (locally known as senene) is considered a sign of respect and cultural pride [10].

Despite their importance, the scientific basis for entomophagy in Tanzania remains limited. Specific ethnographic details linking diverse tribes and regions to particular edible insect species associated traditional technologies and consumption rituals (for example, whether young children are fed insects) remain limited outside of a few well-studied species like R. differens [10–13]. Without systematic documentation, valuable indigenous knowledge is at risk of being lost as younger generations migrate to urban areas, adopt more processed diets and as habitats face ecological pressures.

This knowledge gap limits opportunities for policy support, value-chain development, the establishment of safe processing protocols, and effective integration of edible insects into national and global food and nutrition strategies. This comprehensive ethnographic mapping study addresses these gaps through a systematic ethnographic and survey-based approach to documenting edible insects in Tanzania. We combined geo-referenced household surveys, semi-structured interviews, and observations to map the distribution, existence of insects in their habitats, seasonality of key edible insect species and document the associated cultural practices and beliefs associated with their consumption. The study provides baseline data essential for promoting the sustainable use of edible insects and their habitats, enhancing food security, and preserving cultural heritage.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a mixed-methods ethnographic design to document edible insect existence in their habitats, consumption, and associated cultural practices across selected regions in Tanzania. The use of mixed methods allowed for triangulation to ensure validity and reliability, through the integration of qualitative ethnography with descriptive quantitative. The research was conducted between July 2022 and January 2025, covering nine regions representing six national ecological and cultural zones of Tanzania.

Sampling and participants

Study participants were selected through purposive snowball sampling starting from indigenous knowledge experts such as clan chiefs and elders, due to the socially embedded and uneven distribution of this knowledge, which is primarily held by elders and experienced practitioners [14]. Initial contacts were facilitated through local administrative leaders and cultural officers, after which additional participants were identified through snowball referral.

Regional sample sizes varied according to the number of local experts available, which in some cases was limited. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached, defined as the point at which no new information on edible insect knowledge emerged resulting in a final sample of 131 respondents across nine regions. Triangulation with qualitative data from interviews and observations helped to validate the results. The sampling targeted communities naturally engaged in traditional insect harvesting, consumption, and individuals with experiential knowledge of edible insects and thus higher information richness of their use as a food source. The respondents were from three groups; (i) General adult community members with lived experience of insect consumption. (ii) Key informants including elders, clan chiefs, harvesters, traders, and (iii) processors recognized for their knowledge of cultural practices and technologies.

The ethnographic approach proved vital for comprehensive capturing of indigenous knowledge. Structured group discussions among elders facilitated recall of historical consumption patterns and seasonal availability often through association with other significant past events, such as weddings, where insects were traditionally associated with food ceremonies or gifts.

Data collection

Interviews and key informant discussions

Questionnaires guided face-to-face interviews with a total of 131 respondents, explored cultural perceptions, consumption practices, and processing methods. The questionnaires were administered in Swahili, with local translators assisting where other indigenous languages were required. Field notes and key informant discussions supplemented interviews, which lasted 80–120 min each on average.

Observations

We conducted participant and non-participant observations focusing on harvesting, processing, preservation, commercialization, and cultural rituals involving edible insects. Both informal and naturalistic observation techniques were employed, and data were documented through structured checklists, photographs, and video recordings.

Geospatial mapping

Locations of edible insect habitats, harvesting, and commercialization sites as reported by respondents were geo-referenced using data collection tablets. ArcGIS® version 10.7 (ESRI 2019) was used for initial visualization and spatial data management, while QGIS 3.34 (2024) was used to produce final distribution maps of edible insects species across regions.

Sample collection and identification

Raw edible insects samples were stored in 70% alcohol, while dried insects obtained from markets or households were stored in clean zipper bags. All samples were transported to Sokoine University of Agriculture for taxonomic identification using morphological characteristics.

Data analysis

Quantitative Survey data were analysed using R Version 4.5.1 (R Core Team 2025) for statistics analysis and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft 2016) for data management. Geospatial data were analyzed using QGIS 3.34 (2024) to generate distribution maps of edible insect species and consumption hotspots. Qualitative data from interviews and observations were synthesized thematically and triangulated according to accepted practice [14] to identify recurring cultural practices, beliefs, and consumption patterns.

Results and discussion

Socioeconomic, educational, age and gender profile of participants

Almost half of respondents (48.9%) reported monthly household incomes below TZS 50,000 (Fig. 1). Most participants had at least primary education (49.6%), and 59.2% were older than 45 years. Younger individuals were less frequently identified as primary knowledge holders during snowball referrals. Male respondents constituted 56.2% of the sample.

The predominance of older, rural, and low-income respondents reflects the social contexts in which entomophagy remains most prevalent in Tanzania. Similar patterns have been reported across sub-Saharan Africa, where indigenous food practices are often maintained among older generations and in rural livelihoods [15]. While men often dominate commercial harvesting and wholesaling activities within edible insect value chains (e.g., Ruspolia differens in Uganda), women and children traditionally play central roles in the collection, processing and preservation of edible insects across many Africa contexts [16, 17].

However, ethnographic studies focusing on Tanzanian delicacies such as senene (R. differens) indicate that women frequently serve as primary informants due to their close involvement in harvesting and processing activities [10].

Edible insects cultural and geographic diversity

The study achieved substantial cultural representation, documenting participants from 29 distinct tribes, with more information richness observed in regions including Rukwa (18.5%), Ruvuma (17.4%), Katavi (10.9%), and Iringa (12.3%) where a higher number of knowledge experts were identified (Fig. 2). These distributions reflect the wide geographic and cultural contexts in which entomophagy is practiced in Tanzania. Key represented tribes included the Haya (12.2%), Kerewe (8.4%), Matengo (7.6%), and Nyamwanga (7.6%).

The prominence of the Haya is notable, given their well-documented cultural association with the consumption of senene in the Lake Victoria basin [10]. The cultural awareness observed in these regions suggest that edible insects are not merely “famine foods” but are integral components of traditional food systems and regular diets [18]. The local communities with higher entomophagy practices had local systems for generational sharing of indigenous skills in identifying, trapping, and preparing edible insects [19]. This tribal diversity is essential for understanding the specific roles different insects play in various communities, where they may serve as delicacies, energy sources, or medicinal supplements [6].

Species abundance and commonly consumed edible insects

The study found more than 40 taxa consumed as part of local diets across nine regions in Tanzania (Table 1; Fig. 3). Six major taxonomic orders were represented and consumed at both larval and adult stages. The observed taxonomic diversity indicates widespread practice of entomophagy in Tanzania, aligning with other findings across Africa [8, 15, 20–22]. The orders Orthoptera (grasshoppers, crickets, locusts) and Lepidoptera (caterpillars) were the most frequently recorded, reflecting their general ecological abundance and well-documented cultural preference across the continent [21].

Orthoptera included the senene (Ruspolia differens), and other commonly consumed species including various locusts and grasshoppers commonly known as Nzige and Panzi (Locusta m. migratoria, Schistocerca gregaria) (Fig. 5). Lepidoptera locally known as viungu including the gregarious caterpillars of (Anaphe panda and Gonimbrasia spp.) locally known as Makarata in Mtwara and Magando in Katavi were among the most consumed (Fig. 4). These consumption patterns are consistent with trends reported in other entomophagous societies [2].

Hymenoptera (bees and ants) including the (Cerebara Vidua) locally known as mang’eng’i or manyama and the Honeybee (Apis mellifera larvae) were widely consumed often in raw form. Blattodea historically classified as termites, specifically winged adults widely known as Kumbikumbi in Swahili language, dominated Isoptera, these were widely consumed in all nine regions. Diptera lake flies (Chaoborus edulis) locally known as likungu, were found in massive numbers, appearing as dense clouds from Lakes Victoria and Nyasa. In these regions, after harvesting, the insects were pressed into likungu cakes for consumption (Fig. 5a). Communities residing in regions bordering DR Congo, Zambia and Uganda consumed more edible insects than other communities. These observations suggest that geographic proximity and shared ecological zones may be factors in the cultural adoption of specific insect species [23], though this would need to be confirmed through additional observation.

The dominance of Orthoptera and Lepidoptera reflects both ecological abundance and longstanding cultural preferences, which have been documented globally [2] and across multiple African entomophagy contexts [8].

The higher diversity and frequency of edible insect consumption observed in border regions may reflect shared ecological landscapes and cross-border cultural exchange rather than national boundaries per se. However, local ecological conditions may also influence observed patterns.

Land and Soil Habitat

The soil and anthill-dwelling species (Blattodea, historically Isoptera),, primarily consisting of termites and winged termites (Macrotermes spp., Acanthotermes spp., Pseudacanthotermes spp.) locally known as Kumbikumbi and Mchwa, constituted the most abundant group together with Manyama, and Nsike beetles making 26.05% of all reported existence (Fig. 5). These were reported consistently across rainy and post-rainy periods (April-July, November-February), with seasonal peaks extending into March-July. Manyama (Celebara Vidua) (3.68%) and Nsike beetles (1.12%) were recorded sporadically during the onset of rains, mainly in November and early rains months.

Other soil-dwelling Orthoptera, such as the burrowing crickets, were reported to occur across overlapping seasonal periods.

Based on reported habitat categories and seasonal distribution patterns (Fig. 6), insect availability appeared to vary across ecological niches and rainfall periods. Similar habitat-driven patterns have been documented in other African entomophagy systems, where forest cover, rainfall, and agricultural cycles determine insect emergence and harvesting periods [21]. Reported shifts in seasonal timing may reflect broader climate variability and land-use change, which could have implications for the long-term sustainability of wild-harvested edible insects.

Aquatic and managed habitats

Water-associated species, Likungu (Chaoborus edulis) (Fig. 5a), represented the smallest proportion of recorded edible insects and were less frequently reported (5.04%) compared with terrestrial species. These species were recorded primarily between January–June and July–October during warmer, wetter months, often coinciding with secondary rainfall periods. This temporal pattern is consistent with the dependence of their larvae on the benthic zone of lakes, such as Lake Victoria and Lake Nyassa [24].

Masega (Apis mellifera larvae) and their honeycomb accounted for 9.52% of recorded observations, with availability distributed across nearly all seasons (April–May, August–October, November–February, September–November) (Fig. 6).

Unlike most wild-harvested edible insects, the extended seasonal availability of honeycomb reflects human management of beehives, which allows harvesting to continue into the dry season alongside natural production cycles.

Some species were reportedly absent during the data collection period, as elders noted that these insects have not appeared during their usual seasonal periods. Perceived declines in availability and possible local extinctions were attributed to climate variability, increased change and pesticide use, particularly on cashew trees that serve as host plants for insects such as Makarata (Gonimbrasia zambesina). Such reductions in insect availability linked to environmental change and reliance on wild harvesting are recognized concerns within African entomophagy systems [8]. These locally observed shifts also resonate with broader global discussions on climate change, biodiversity loss, and the vulnerability of traditional food systems, where edible insects are increasingly framed within sustainability and food security agendas [2, 4].

The contrast between aquatic and managed insect habitats highlights the importance of human intervention in stabilizing edible insect availability. While aquatic insects such as lake flies remain tightly coupled to environmental conditions, managed systems such as beekeeping provide greater temporal reliability. This distinction has implications for strategies aimed at improving year-round access to edible insects.

Cultural roots, traditions, taboos

The consumption of edible insects was found to be deeply rooted in cultural norms, religious beliefs, and taboos, which governed who consumes which species, when, and how insects are harvested and prepared. Many of the taboos targeted children and pregnant women. Such restrictions may function both as protective measures for vulnerable groups and as mechanisms regulating access to valued food resources within households.

In Ruvuma regions, pregnant women were restricted from consuming more than a spoonful of Mbwambo ants (Oecophylla longinoda); based on the belief that excessive intake could result in giving birth to bald-headed infants. The observation that this insect is perceived as “sour and therefore highly desirable” highlights a tension between cultural prohibition and palatability. Moreover, in Kagera Region, pregnant women were discouraged from consuming senene (Ruspolia differens) due to belief that it would result being born with a cone- shaped head resembling the insect. Similarly, feeding insects to children was traditionally associated with delayed speech development. Allowing children to consume large quantities of viegele caterpillar was believed to impair hearing ability. When the elders were asked to explain this observation, they stated that children engrossed in eating these insects might fail to respond when called for other tasks. This explanation suggests the strong desirability and sensory appeal of edible insects rather than a perceived physiological effect.

Restriction on insect consumption among pregnant women are consistent with taboo patterns documented across Africa entomophagy contexts [25]. Elders in Songwe Region reported that harvesting Maswethi caterpillars before 21 days after initial appearance would result in severe rainfall accompanied by thunder. Furthermore, all caterpillars harvested were traditionally required to be presented to the chief prior to consumption, with the belief that failure to do so would negatively affect the following season’s harvest. Although no empirical explanation was offered to validate these beliefs, elders emphasized that such practices should not be questioned. Discussions with the key informants suggested that these taboos were not merely punitive threats but functioned as mechanisms to maintain social order, promote conservation and potentially enhance food safety, as reported in other African studies [26].

While instances of overexploitation of certain insect species were observed in semi-urbanized communities and have been reported elsewhere in Africa, cultural mechanisms linking sustainability to taboos and spiritual sanctions continue to operate effectively in some rural communities [27]. Another reported taboo concerned insect nomenclature: caterpillars were not to be referred to by any name other than viungu, particularly in proximity to their habitats, due to beliefs that improper naming could cause skin rashes. This practice emphasizes the cultural significance of proper categorization and naming in local food systems that is important for species recognition, consumption practices, and conservation.

Consumption drivers for consuming edible insects

Perceived Consumption motivations and benefits

The consumption of edible insects was primarily driven by sensory appeal and cultural familiarity. Across species, many respondents indicated that insects were consumed because they are “delicious,” while others reported eating them simply because they “just like them.” Cultural pride and tradition also emerged as important motivations, alongside the seasonal availability of particular species. Termites (Microtermes spp.), Nsike beetles, caterpillars, black ants (Cerebara vidua), and grasshoppers were frequently associated with taste-related motivations, while senene (Ruspolia differens) and locusts were more often linked to seasonal availability.

Taste-related motivations aligned with the perceived benefits- perceived outcomes after consumption (Fig. 7), where respondents frequently associated edible insects with being delicious or culturally valued foods. Nutritional value and personal preference (“I just like them”) were also reported as reasons for consumption. These patterns indicates that entomophagy is motivated by both cultural familiarity and sensory satisfaction, consistent with previous studies identifying taste and tradition as dominant drivers of edible insect consumption in Africa [25, 28, 29]. Perceived benefits of consumption (Fig. 7) further emphasized hedonic and cultural reasons. In other studies, a substantial proportion of respondents > 40%, associated edible insects with health benefits, reflecting awareness of their nutritional richness in proteins and micronutrients [30].

A small proportion of respondents self-reported to have experienced negative health effects including diarrhea, stomach pain and vomiting that demotivated them from frequent consumption of edible insect. These beliefs are reported as cultural narratives and do not imply causal biological effects. These effects were commonly attributed to poor hygiene practices during processing and storage, which may introduce microbial contamination rather than reflecting intrinsic insect toxicity. Similar concerns have been documented in studies reporting microbial contamination of edible insects sold in local markets [31]. While entomophagy is sustained by taste, cultural pride, and nutritional beliefs, ensuring hygienic preparation and improved awareness of safe handling practices is critical for maintaining consumer confidence and supporting the broader acceptance of edible insects beyond their local markets [7].

Edible insects in child feeding

Edible insects were widely integrated into child feeding practices, with 82% of respondents reporting that they feed one or more insect types to children under five years of age. This high level of inclusion indicates that entomophagy is a well-established and normalized component of traditional diets in many Tanzanian households. The insect species most frequently fed to children belonged to the Orthoptera and Isoptera orders. These included winged termites (19%), grasshoppers (12%), longhorn grasshoppers (9%), and termites (9%). In contrast, only 3% of Coleoptera species, including beetles and small beetles, were reported to be fed to children Table 2.

The preference for Orthoptera and termite species in child feeding may reflect a combination of seasonal availability, softer texture, palatability, and cultural familiarity. In contrast, the limited inclusion of beetles may relate to their harder exoskeletons, perceived difficulty in chewing, and cultural caution regarding their suitability for young children. The study did not assess nutritional adequacy, reported feeding practices were based on caregiver self-reports. Similar patterns of early exposure to edible insects have been reported in other contexts, where gradual introduction during childhood contributes to long-term dietary acceptance [15, 32].

The main motivations for feeding edible insects to children were that “children like them” (38.4%), followed by the cultural driver that the practice “is part of family tradition” (26.1%), and recognition of nutritional benefits (17.1%). Regional patterns indicated that “children like them” and “it is part of our tradition” were dominant motivations across most study areas, particularly in Iringa, Katavi, Mtwara, Rukwa, Ruvuma, and Songwe. In contrast, nutritional value was more frequently cited in Dodoma, Kagera, and Rukwa. The prominence of child preference and cultural tradition suggests that traditional food identity continues to shape dietary choices despite ongoing social and dietary transitions. Recognition of nutritional benefits further highlights the potential role of edible insects as complementary foods, consistent with findings from other studies positioning edible insects as nutrient-dense foods with potential contributions to sustainable diets and food security beyond traditionally entomophagous regions [6, 32, 33].

Preferences for winged termites, grasshoppers, and caterpillars appear to reflect a combination of seasonal availability and sensory appeal, as these species are abundant during the rainy periods and possess flavors and textures that are easily accepted by children. Introducing edible insect-based foods during early childhood my support long-term dietary acceptance, given that food preferences and perceptions of what constitutes appropriate food are formed early in life [34].

A minority of respondents (18%) reported that they do not feed insects to children. Reported reasons included practical challenges such as chewing or swallowing difficulties (2.8%), as well as psychological and cultural barriers, including fear or dislike (4.5%) and taboo-related restrictions (3.4%).

Despite high overall acceptance, these barriers highlight sensory and physical challenges associated with the insects’ appearance and chitinous exoskeleton, particularly for young children. Processing edible insects into age-appropriate forms suitable for complementary feeding would therefore be necessary prior to their formal nutrition interventions [33, 34].

Perceived medicinal, health and therapeutic benefits of edible insects

Medicinal and health-promoting properties of edible insects were reported across all study regions, highlighting the intertwined nature of food, health, and culture within Tanzanian entomophagy. In total, seven insect species were associated with 28 therapeutic applications spanning six health categories. Honeycombs (Apis mellifera larvae) exhibited the broadest therapeutic range, being linked to nine distinct health conditions, followed by Nsike beetles with six reported applications. Gastrointestinal ailments were the most frequently cited category, with six of the seven insect types reported as remedies.

The most frequently reported medicinal resources was bee larvae obtained in honeycomb, which were believed to treat coughs, flu, tuberculosis, ulcers, stomachache, and skin rashes, enhance men’s potency and eyesight (Table 3). Coleoptera, Formicidae and Chaoborus were associated with relief from constipation, menstrual stomach cramps, scalp ringworm in children and skin infections, while Lepidoptera larvae were commonly reported as remedies for stomachache. The larvae of Madimu (Thithones confinis) was known for reducing alcohol hangover effects. These reported uses align with studies reporting the historical ethnomedicinal use of certain ants to address constipation, caterpillars for digestive disorders and praying mantis and hawk moths for enhancing potency [35, 36]. Research indicates that edible insects are increasingly recognized as functional foods globally due to the presence of bioactive compounds, including antioxidants, peptides, and chitin, which exhibit antihypertensive, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial properties [37–39]. Interestingly, some respondents cautioned that, excessive consumption of crickets, termites, and grasshoppers could induce cough or flu-like symptoms. These perceptions may arise from throat irritation linked to fine chitin particles or respiratory discomfort from insect texture [6, 34].

Such cautious perceptions contrasts with the known ethnomedicinal practices documented elsewhere, where termites (Microcerotermes exiguus and Nasutitermes corniger) are traditionally used to treat asthma, bronchitis, flu, and coughs. Stink bugs are known for managing asthma-related symptoms in Zimbabwe and South Africa [27, 40, 41].Reported medicinal uses reflect cultural knowledge and perceptions and do not imply clinically validated therapeutic efficacy.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study provides the first nationally coordinated ethnographic baseline of edible insect use in Tanzania, demonstrating that insects remain deeply embedded in food systems, cultural identity, and indigenous health practices across diverse ecological and ethnic contexts. More than 40 edible insect taxa spanning six taxonomic orders were documented. Consumption patterns were shaped by seasonality, habitat availability, sensory preferences, and long-standing cultural norms while barriers were rooted from taboos, fear, and chewing difficulty in children. The widespread inclusion of edible insects in child feeding and traditional medicine highlights their continued nutritional and social relevance. Our findings about culturally embedded perceptions and ecological availability of edible insects resonate with global agendas that position insects as part of future sustainable diets.

While most adverse health effects reported by communities were associated with poor hygiene and inadequate processing rather than inherent toxicity, gaps in food safety awareness and post-harvest handling remain evident. Furthermore, the predominance of elders as custodians of indigenous knowledge emphasizes both the richness and fragility of this knowledge system, which is increasingly threatened by urbanization, habitat loss, and changing dietary preferences. Collectively, the findings emphasize that edible insects in Tanzania represent not only a culturally significant food resource but also an underutilized opportunity for nutrition improvement, biodiversity conservation, and resilient food system development.

Limitation of the study

The study-encountered challenges in species identification, a common limitation in ethno-entomological research where vernacular naming systems do not always correspond to formal taxonomic classification. In some cases, species could only be identified by using local names or through photographs and morphological descriptions provided by informants. Consequently, a single vernacular name encompass multiple scientific species, potentially leading to an underestimation of overall species diversity documented in this study.

We recognize that the respondents sample was reasonably small, this was limited by the number and availability of expert knowledge holders across the communities studied, which represent a small proportion of the overall community. The depth and breadth of the mixed methods and triangulation approach goes some way to addressing this but we recognize that further study is needed to ensure the results are representative and reproducible.
