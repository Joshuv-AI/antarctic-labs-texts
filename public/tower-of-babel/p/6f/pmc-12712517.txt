Combined Reconstruction of the Anterior Cruciate Ligament, Anterior Oblique Ligament, and Medial Collateral Ligament of the Knee: Surgical Technique Description
Authors: Vinicios Barreto Melo, Saulo Santos Blunk, Sérgio Marinho de Gusmão Canuto, Gustavo da Silva Caldeira, Vitor Barion de Castro de Pádua, Camilo Partezani Helito, Pedro Baches Jorge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Failures in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction have prompted adjunct techniques addressing rotational instabilities. This article describes a combined reconstruction of the ACL, anterior oblique ligament, and medial collateral ligament using autografts (rectus femoris and semitendinosus tendons). The technique’s main feature is the shared femoral tunnel for the anterior oblique ligament and medial collateral ligament, enabling a reproducible procedure without special implants. This approach aims to restore anteromedial rotatory instability control and reduce ACL graft failure rates.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are among the most common in noncontact sports, especially those involving sudden direction changes, deceleration, jumping, and landing.1 Despite efforts to identify risk factors, ACL injuries have doubled in the past decade.1 ACL reconstruction is one of the most frequently performed procedures in orthopaedics, yet it still presents considerable failure rates.

The medial collateral ligament (MCL) is frequently injured in torsional knee traumas. Grade II and III MCL injuries are often associated with ACL ruptures, although isolated cases may occur.2 The incidence of combined ACL and MCL injuries ranges from 16.5% to 67% in the literature.3 An MCL injury can result in valgus instability and anteromedial rotatory instability (AMRI), which, if not recognized and treated, has now been identified as a potential cause of ACL reconstruction failure.4

The anterior oblique ligament (AOL) was described by Jorge et al.4 as a structure originating anterior to the medial epicondyle and inserted along the upper edge of the tibial plateau. It is part of the medial cruciate complex, composed of the deep and superficial MCLs, the posterior oblique ligament, and the AOL, which collectively provide varus and rotational stability to the medial compartment.4 The AOL plays a key role in controlling external tibial rotation, minimizing residual AMRI in patients with this instability pattern.5

Combined ACL, MCL, and AOL injuries are more prevalent than previously recognized.6 To address the high failure rates and residual instability seen in isolated ACL reconstructions, this work presents a combined surgical technique for ACL, MCL, and AOL reconstruction to improve valgus and external rotational control.

Indications

This combined reconstruction technique is indicated for patients with ACL tears associated with grade II or III injuries of the MCL, particularly when clinical signs of AMRI are present. It is especially recommended in the following scenarios:•Residual valgus or rotational laxity following ACL reconstruction•Combined ACL and MCL injuries confirmed by clinical testing and imaging•Positive anteromedial drawer external rotation (ADER) test, suggesting AMRI•Patients at high risk of ACL graft failure due to unaddressed medial instability2, 3, 4,7, 8, 9

Patient Positioning and Examination

The patient is placed in the supine position under spinal anesthesia. A pneumatic tourniquet is applied at the proximal thigh. Examination under anesthesia confirms the ACL and MCL insufficiency, and the presence of AMRI is assessed using the ADER test. The test is performed with the knee flexed at 90° and the leg positioned in 15° of external rotation.6

Graft Harvest and Preparation

The rectus femoris (RF) tendon is harvested for ACL and AOL reconstruction. A 2- to 3-cm longitudinal incision is made approximately 2 cm proximal to the patella (Fig 1). After identifying the quadriceps tendon, 2 longitudinal incisions (∼10 mm wide) are made in the superficial lamina of the RF, which is dissected with Metzenbaum scissors and harvested using a closed stripper (Fig 2). After debridement, the graft length ranges from 28 to 35 cm (Fig 4, Table 1). The semitendinosus tendon is harvested through a standard anteromedial approach to the proximal tibia and prepared with absorbable sutures for MCL reconstruction10 (Fig 3).

Arthroscopic Evaluation

A standard diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to confirm the ACL rupture. The medial femorotibial compartment is evaluated with a probe to assess the medial joint line opening. The presence of the “floating meniscus” sign or the medial drive-through sign confirms injury to the MCL complex.11

ACL and AOL Reconstructions

Femoral landmarks are identified after intercondylar notch preparation. The anteromedial portal or an outside-in guide creates the femoral tunnel between the anteromedial and posterolateral bundles over the bifurcate ridge. The tibial tunnel is made at 55° between the anteromedial and posterolateral bundles, posterior to the anterior root of the lateral meniscus. On the external cortex, with the intra-articular tip stable in the desired position, the guide is positioned at a 45° medial angle relative to the tibial crest. In this position, from this point toward the posterior border of the medial epicondyle, the graft remnant will cross the topography of the AOL, thereby resulting in a nonanatomic reconstruction of the ligament. Fixation is achieved using bioabsorbable interference screws (Arthrex), with the knee in 20° flexion and neutral rotation (Fig 12). At this point, the ACL reconstruction is complete, and there is a graft remnant of at least 10 cm emerging from the tibial tunnel, ready to be directed to the femoral insertion of the AOL. MCL Reconstruction (Canuto’s Technique)

Two converging tibial tunnels are created 6 cm distal to the joint line using a 4.5-mm drill at an angle of 90° (Fig 8, Fig 13A). Isometry is assessed by connecting the tibial guidewires to a femoral guidewire placed 3 to 5 mm proximal and posterior to the medial epicondyle (Fig 9). The semitendinosus graft is passed through the tibial tunnels. All grafts are measured together to determine the diameter of the femoral tunnel (Fig 11). Once confirmed, the femoral tunnel is drilled. After passing through the tibia and verifying the diameter along with the AOL, both reconstructed MCL portions are passed beneath the subcutaneous tissue, in a juxta-articular path, proximally toward the femoral tunnel. The AOL, in an oblique direction and through a different path, is also passed toward the femoral point, carefully positioned beneath all fatty tissue so that it lies close to the joint capsule (Fig 10). Together with the AOL graft, it is tunneled subfascially beneath the sartorial fascia toward the medial femoral tunnel. Final fixation is performed using a bioabsorbable interference screw (Arthrex) with the knee in 20° flexion, neutral rotation, and a slight varus force applied10 (Fig 12, Table 2).

Discussion

The incidence of combined ACL and MCL injuries ranges from 16% to 67%. Although the MCL has an intrinsic healing capacity, some patients may present with persistent medial laxity or residual instability after nonoperative treatment.2, 3, 4,8 Studies have shown that partial MCL injuries can increase ACL graft loading by up to 127% during external rotation at 30° of knee flexion, compared to knees with intact MCLs.8 Persistent, unaddressed MCL laxity is a known risk factor for ACL graft failure.8,9

AMRI is controlled by the superficial MCL, deep MCL, and the AOL—both the MCL and AOL act as primary stabilizers against external tibial rotation and valgus loads. Current literature supports surgical treatment of the MCL in the context of ACL reconstruction to reduce graft failure rates and long-term medial instability.2,9

Although single-bundle MCL reconstruction has been considered the gold standard, it may be insufficient to control AMRI in some instances.8 Anatomically addressing both angular and rotational components of medial instability may improve biomechanical stability and long-term outcomes.

The technique described in this study integrates anatomic and biomechanical principles in a reproducible and accessible manner without requiring specialized implants. By combining ACL, MCL, and AOL reconstruction, the technique aims to provide enhanced valgus and rotational forces control across the medial compartment.

Combined reconstruction of the ACL, AOL, and MCL (Fig 13B) offers an anatomic and biomechanical approach to restore the anteromedial rotatory stability of the knee. It is especially valuable in patients with combined ligament injuries or failure of isolated ACL reconstructions due to unaddressed medial laxity. The described technique is reproducible and safe and may reduce ACL graft failure rates in the presence of AMRI.
