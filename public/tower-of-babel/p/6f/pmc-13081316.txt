The ‘transactional–relational–developmental’ model for early-career physicians’ postgraduate education in psychological contract theory
Authors: Yimeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting competency frameworks with high international recognition often lack a theoretical basis for explaining the implicit expectations between organisations and individuals as well as intrinsic motivation. This study aims to construct a competency model for early-career physicians based on psychological contract theory to provide insights for further postgraduate education.MethodsA mixed-methods approach was adopted. A literature analysis, expert consultation and presurvey were implemented to develop a 16-item questionnaire. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was used to explore the model structure; 305 valid questionnaires were analysed, and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was employed for cross-validation.ResultsExploratory factor analysis revealed three factors, with a cumulative variance explanation rate of 69.987%. Confirmatory factor analysis indicated good model fit (χ2/df = 1.967, CFI = 0.995, RMSEA = 0.056). Background

Modern medical education has reached a global consensus on competency-based training. This shift has prompted the creation of comprehensive competency frameworks that go beyond traditional medical knowledge and technical skills [1]. Among these, the Canadian CanMEDS framework holds the broadest international recognition [2]. It precisely defines seven essential physician roles, positioning the Medical Expert at the core, surrounded by six key intrinsic roles—Communicator, Collaborator, Leader, Health Advocate, Scholar and Professional [3]. On the basis of this structure, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) lists six core competencies: patient care, medical knowledge, practice-based learning and improvement, interpersonal and communication skills, professionalism, and systems-based practice [4]. Similarly, according to the UK Foundation Programme Curriculum, the Scottish Doctor triple-circle framework offers an intuitive model that delineates the hierarchical nature of a competent physician's attributes [5]: the inner circle represents professional skills, the middle circle encompasses general skills, and the foundational outer circle consists of the personal qualities that underpin all others. The integration of the three authoritative physician competency frameworks and related practices in postgraduate training reveals several key commonalities. For instance, all three frameworks emphasise the mastery of medical knowledge as a fundamental requirement, incorporate interpersonal skills as an essential competency and acknowledge that physicians are in a unique profession demanding continuous individual growth and self-actualisation [6]. These common elements reflect the expectations that society and organisations hold of physicians and can be effectively analysed through the transactional, relational and developmental dimensions of psychological contract theory [7].

This study focuses on early-career physicians who have completed medical education and obtained full clinical qualifications [8]. The cohort includes physicians who hold 'resident physician' or 'attending physician' titles during their initial postgraduate practice phase [9]. The competency development of early-career physicians typically involves three sequential phases: school education, internship training and postgraduate training. These phases are administered by medical colleges, internship hospitals and employing hospitals, respectively [10]. Employing hospitals serve as the primary career launchpad for early-career physicians and play a central role in their postgraduate training. Recent evidence has shown that many hospitals have designed new competency frameworks for early-career physicians using internationally recognised models [11]. Some researchers have conducted satisfaction studies on the forms and effectiveness of postgraduate education [12], while others have performed empirical analyses of environments and management models for competency development [13]. However, there is little research on the relationship between competency development and psychological well-being. A current gap is the incomplete integration of theories of human resource development with psychological knowledge. This omission results in an insufficient understanding of the internal drivers among physicians in their early career stages.

This study examines three core questions: Which latent dimensions best represent the proposed competency elements? How do these dimensions align with psychological contract theory? and Do the resulting scales show adequate reliability and validity in the sample population?

Methods

Literature analysis and expert consultation

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify publications on competency-based postgraduate education for early-career physicians. Three databases were selected for their complementary coverage: China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) for Chinese academic literature, Wanfang Data for Chinese conference proceedings, and ScienceDirect (Elsevier) for international peer-reviewed journals. This approach ensured comprehensive coverage of both Chinese and English publications. The search employed Boolean logic for precision. Key concepts were combined using the following phrases: ('early-career physician' OR 'junior doctor' OR 'resident') AND ('post-graduation medical education' OR 'graduate medical education') AND ('competency' OR 'skill' OR 'medical education model'). Three filters were applied: publication date (2013.1.1–2023.12.31), document type (journal articles, dissertations, and conference papers), and language (English and Chinese).

The initial search identified 240 records. In accordance with the PRISMA guidelines, 215 records were screened after duplicate removal. After the exclusion of 135 records during title/abstract screening, 80 articles were retained for full-text review. The full-text assessment excluded 45 articles for specific reasons: 22 were nonempirical studies, 13 lacked competency frameworks, and 10 had ineligible populations. The final synthesis included 35 studies.

This review did not conduct a formal quality assessment of the 35 included studies, which is consistent with its scoping purpose of theoretical framework development rather than intervention comparison. Methodological heterogeneity existed across studies, including varied research designs (questionnaires, expert consultations, theoretical discussions) and diverse participant characteristics. However, the systematic search strategy, transparent screening process, and cross-verification in content analysis ensured theoretical saturation. These methodological variations represent both a study limitation and reflect the field's practical reality. Future research should employ standardised assessment tools (e.g., AHRQ/CASP/MMAT) to provide more rigorous evidence.

The study used NVivo 12.0 for qualitative content analysis. The codebook was developed through a purely inductive approach. The codebook was generated entirely from the data, allowing categories to emerge organically from the textual material. Two trained coders performed independent coding following a two-week training period. This training covered NVivo 12.0 operations, coding protocols, and trial coding exercises and established unified interpretation standards. The coding followed three stages: initial independent open coding by both coders, discussion-based integration that merged codes into 18 elements, and reliability assessment, which indicated substantial agreement (Cohen's kappa = 0.82). Disagreements were resolved through consensus discussions, with unresolved cases elevated to a senior researcher for final determination. Thematic saturation was confirmed when five consecutive articles yielded no new codes and when the code relationships stabilised. The analysis used text paragraphs as units to preserve contextual meaning. For example, the excerpt ‘Young physicians should be able to translate research findings into clinical practice to improve diagnosis and treatment plans’ [14] was initially coded as ‘achievement transformation ability’ and ultimately categorised under ‘innovation and transformation capacity’. The final codebook is provided in Appendix 1.

The 18 initial competency elements were refined through structured expert consultation. A purposive sampling strategy ensured comprehensive domain coverage in the evaluation process. The expert panel (n = 15) reflected a combination of academic and practical expertise: university-based healthcare HR scholars, HR directors from public tier-3 hospitals, and health policy administrators from national and regional commissions. All the members held senior positions with more than 10 years of relevant experience. The consultation followed a modified Delphi method with two iterative cycles. In the first round, the experts independently rated each element for relevance and clarity on a 5-point scale while providing qualitative suggestions for modification, consolidation or elimination. During this round of expert consultation, the phrase 'educational ability' was revised to ‘teaching ability’, 'environmental adaptability' was amended to 'adaptability', and ‘capacity for continuing professional development’ was revised to ‘self-directed learning ability’. The revised list of 18 elements was then resubmitted for a second-round review, during which greater than 90% consensus was achieved on all remaining competencies.

The presurvey

A presurvey was conducted using a structured questionnaire distributed through a combination of institutional channels and professional networks over a two-month period. Participants were recruited through medical colleges, hospital departments, and professional associations. Eligibility required either being an early-career physician within 10 years of graduation, serving as a clinical department head, or holding a medical faculty position involved in curriculum development. The questionnaire collected demographic data and measured perceptions of the 18 elements on a 5-point Likert scale. It also gathered feedback on potential framework adjustments. A total of 121 questionnaires were distributed, and 115 valid responses were obtained (valid response rate = 95.04%). The instrument showed good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.892) and sampling adequacy (KMO = 0.931). Bartlett's test of sphericity confirmed the suitability of the factor analysis (χ2(33) = 426.44, p < 0.001). Following data cleaning, descriptive statistics and one-sample t tests were performed on the 18 elements.

The results indicated that 'communication ability' and 'title promotion ability' did not significantly differ from the theoretical median of three. These two elements were consequently excluded from the final framework. Ultimately, 16 elements were identified and finalised. These are shown in Table 1.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

On the basis of competency elements identified through the literature analysis, expert consultation and presurvey results, this study developed the 'Questionnaire on competency elements of the postgraduate education model for early-career physicians’, which included a demographic survey and an evaluation of the elements. The 16 competency elements identified through prior qualitative content analysis were translated into measurable item stems. Each element was operationalised as a behavioural or attitudinal statement requiring respondents to evaluate its perceived importance for professional practice. To maintain conciseness and enhance response compliance within the physician population, each competency element was measured using a single directly worded item. Elements were evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale, with responses ranging from 'strongly agree' (5 points) to 'strongly disagree' (1 point). The questionnaire underwent rigorous pretesting through a two-stage validation process. First, an expert panel (n = 5) comprising medical education specialists and senior clinicians evaluated content validity, which resulted in refined item phrasing to reduce ambiguity. Second, pilot testing with early-career physicians (n = 30) demonstrated excellent practicality, with high self-reported comprehension scores (4.5/5) and a manageable completion time (mean = 12 min), thereby confirming the readiness of this instrument for full implementation. The complete questionnaire containing all the item stems, response scales and demographic components appears in Appendix 2. The anonymised dataset supporting this study's findings is archived and is accessible through correspondence with the corresponding author.

In this study, 400 questionnaires were distributed through the personnel departments of major public hospitals across Jiangsu Province during the data collection window from March 2023 to November 2024. Participants could complete the questionnaire anonymously during paid working hours, with 326 questionnaires returned and 305 valid questionnaires retained after completeness verification (valid response rate = 76.25%). All the responses were collected without identifying information to ensure participant confidentiality. The inclusion criteria required participants to hold both medical practitioner qualifications and standardised training certification while maintaining resident or attending physician positions. The term ‘early-career physicians’ served as an operational definition for study purposes, with this clarification provided to participants through inclusion criteria on the front page of the questionnaire. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was first conducted on the 16 items of 'Questionnaire on competency elements of the postgraduate education model for early-career physicians'. The suitability test results indicated a KMO value of 0.898 and a Bartlett’s test of sphericity approximate chi-square value of 3219.043 (df = 120, p < 0.001), suggesting that the data were highly suitable for factor analysis.

Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was applied for factor extraction. The initial analysis revealed four factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, among which ‘public health capacity’ loaded as an independent factor. From a conceptual standpoint, according to China’s Physician Law, physicians are categorised into distinct types, including clinical physicians and public health physicians, each with legally defined remits. The law specifies that the core duty of clinical physicians is the ‘diagnosis and treatment’ of individual patients within medical institutions, whereas the primary duty of public health physicians is ‘disease prevention and control’ for populations. As stated in our background, this study specifically targeted early-career clinical physicians and their psychological contract with hospital organisations. Therefore, this factor was considered conceptually beyond the theoretical scope of the current research. Moreover, as a single-item factor, it has inherent limitations in measurement. Therefore, this item was excluded, and the remaining 15 items were reanalysed.

As shown in Fig. 1, the scree plot displayed a distinct break after the third factor, with the eigenvalues leveling off thereafter.

The reanalysis, which forced the extraction of three common factors, yielded a cumulative variance explanation rate of 69.987%. To examine the stability of the three-factor competency model for early-career physicians proposed by EFA, this study employed confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for cross-validation. The initial model included all 15 items, but its fit indices did not meet acceptable standards (χ2/df = 4.927, CFI = 0.892, RMSEA = 0.114). A series of revisions were subsequently implemented:

First, the item ‘basic/clinical laboratory research capacity’ was removed because of its low standardised loading (λ = 0.607 < 0.70) on its corresponding latent Factor 3. Its squared multiple correlation (SMC = 0.369) further indicated that most of its variance remained unexplained by the factor, justifying its deletion to enhance the measurement purity of H3.

Second, diagnostic checks revealed a high correlation (r = 0.622) between the latent variables Factor 1 (H1) and Factor 2 (H2). To address potential collinearity while preserving the theoretical three-dimensional structure, item parcelling was applied to the H1 and H2 indicators. This method was considered appropriate to enhance model stability given the focus of the study on modelling the relationships between latent factors rather than item-level psychometrics [15]. Specifically, the six items under H1 were merged into two parcels: ‘professional foundation’ (H1_A), comprising professional and technical skills, self-directed learning ability, and innovation capacity; and ‘foundational job competencies’ (H1_B), comprising teamwork skills, interpersonal skills, and the ability to maintain health status. The six items under H2 were merged into two parcels: ‘organisational interaction competence’ (H2_A), comprising organisational and coordination skills, career planning skills, and adaptability; and ‘team interaction competence’ (H2_B), comprising teaching ability, emotional intelligence, and foreign language ability. The H3 factor continued to be measured by its two original items: research translation ability and academic qualifications enhance competence.

Final validated measurement model

Specifically, the two item parcels for the transactional contract factor (H1: professional foundation and foundational job competencies) showed high loadings, thus confirming the centrality of core clinical and professional skills. Similarly, the two parcels for the relational contract factor (H2: organisational interaction competence and team interaction competence) were strongly represented, thereby highlighting the importance of career management and interpersonal coordination. With respect to the developmental contract factor (H3), both the remaining items (‘research translation ability’ and ‘academic qualifications to enhance competence’) loaded highly, thus providing a parsimonious yet valid measure of academic advancement competencies.

The standardised correlations among the three latent factors were positive and moderate in magnitude, supporting theoretical coherence. These findings suggest that although the three psychological contract dimensions are distinct constructs, they are interrelated aspects of early-career physicians’ overall competency and reflect the multifaceted nature of professional development in medical practice.

Following this CFA, one item (‘basic/clinical laboratory research capacity’) was removed because of its low standardised loading and poor contribution to its target construct. Thus, a final set of 14 items was retained to form the validated three-factor model.

Results

The progression from initial competency elements to the final measurement model followed a sequential, data-driven screening process: (1) The literature analysis and expert consultation yielded an initial pool of 18 elements; (2) the presurvey led to the removal of two elements (‘communication ability’ and ‘title promotion ability’), resulting in 16 items for the main survey; (3) exploratory factor analysis (EFA) identified ‘public health capacity’ as a single-item factor, which was excluded on the basis of both conceptual focus and statistical rationale, leaving 15 items; and (4) confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated a low standardised loading (λ < 0.70) for ‘basic/clinical laboratory research capacity’, justifying its removal to achieve a final model of 14 items distributed across three latent factors.

The combined results of EFA and CFA indicate that the postgraduate education model of early-career physicians, constructed on the basis of psychological contract theory, has three pivots. Compared with existing research, the unique value of this model lies in its pioneering operationalisation of Rousseau's three-dimensional psychological contract theory into measurable physician competency indicators. Through statistical validation, this model reveals the concrete manifestations of transactional, relational, and developmental psychological contracts within the early-career physician competency structure. These findings are illustrated in Fig. 3.

Three-dimensional competency element model of psychological contracts for early-career physicians

The transactional factor comprises six elements, including professional and technical skills, which physicians leverage for economic rewards and career security. This dimension aligns with national policies promoting early-career physicians' development and the ‘more pay for more work, better pay for better performance’ principle while also addressing their basic material needs. However, while established methods such as practical training and clinical mentoring effectively develop medical teaching and research capacity, innovation capacity cultivation remains exploratory. Our empirical data show a relatively low factor loading (0.752) for innovation capacity, confirming its assessment and incentivisation as persistent weaknesses in current management systems [16].

The relational factor, which includes elements such as career planning skills and emotional intelligence, reflects physician competencies that extend beyond economic exchange to establish lasting organisational membership [17]. These competencies foster emotional belonging and mutual loyalty by helping physicians integrate into their organisational culture and build quality workplace relationships. All six elements align with national social competency standards for early-career physicians, meeting expectations for professionals to assume diverse roles. The ‘collective–team–individual’ organisational framework requires early-career physicians to fulfil broad social functions in hospital activities and team-based collaboration. However, socioeconomic shifts have altered group dynamics. Younger physicians now view collectives differently, display stronger individualism, and often lack mature interpersonal skills. These traits risk causing alienation and impairing optimal performance.

Developmental factors, comprising research translation ability and academic qualification enhancement, reflect a mutual growth mechanism between organisations and physicians. Early-career physicians typically progress through five stages after they transition from medical school [18]. They grow from undetermined to resident and then to attending physician, often while balancing overlapping clinical, teaching, and research duties. This model outlines competencies that help physicians direct their professional development and sustain self-improvement. However, talent development is dynamic and often crosses staged boundaries. Overreliance on qualification-based standards, while providing structure, may also induce psychological bias and neglect individual differences, thereby diminishing personal fulfilment in professional growth (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This three-dimensional model applies psychological contract theory to construct a postgraduate education model to stimulate early-career physicians' endogenous factors. For hospital administrators, implementing this model requires focusing on physicians' career goals and comprehensively developing competencies across all three dimensions. This approach accelerates knowledge acquisition, improves clinical skills, and enhances output, ultimately strengthening physicians' sense of achievement and well-being [19].

To improve the transactional factor, our analysis revealed that the ‘innovation capacity’ item demonstrated a relatively lower standardised loading (0.752) than other core transactional competencies, such as ‘professional and technical skill’ (0.934) did. These findings suggest that innovative behaviours may not be as consistently recognised or effectively measured within the current performance system governing the economic exchange between hospitals and early-career physicians. To align the formal transactional contract with this identified gap, we propose that hospital administrators could develop a specialised evaluation metric [20] for ‘innovation contribution’ and integrate it into the performance assessment framework [21]. Linking such a metric to tangible rewards, such as specific bonuses or dedicated research resources, would explicitly incorporate innovation into the reciprocal economic exchange, thereby potentially strengthening this dimension of the psychological contract [22].

With respect to the relational factor, CFA validated 'career management and interpersonal coordination competencies' as a high-reliability construct (α = 0.890). This empirical consolidation indicates that these interpersonal and self-management skills are not isolated but rather form an interconnected cluster that underpins the physician–organisation relationship. The observed gradient in factor loadings (as to ‘emotional intelligence’ at 0.743 vs. ‘adaptability’ at 0.652) further suggests potential variability in the mastery of these linked skills. To address this integrated yet uneven development, training interventions could move beyond skill-building. We propose that a 'multidisciplinary team (MDT) practicum' could place early-career physicians in scenarios requiring simultaneous team coordination, conflict mediation and cross-departmental communication [23]. It would provide a holistic context to develop this entire competency cluster in tandem, thereby more effectively strengthening their relational psychological contract.

To improve developmental factors, hospital administrators should establish multichannel career pathways beyond professional title promotion. Developmental factors predominantly rely on ‘research translation ability’ and ‘academic qualifications enhance competence’. These two indicators are closely tied to conventional promotion criteria, yet measurement of diverse competencies, such as the mastery of cutting-edge technologies and service process improvement capabilities, remains lacking [24]. This gap reveals an imbalanced organisational focus on academic achievements and degree advancement in physician development, which overlooks and fails to incentivise other essential professional growth dimensions. We can establish parallel career pathways for physicians in clinical, research and comprehensive tracks. The traditional publication and grant metrics should be replaced with new evaluation indicators, including innovative techniques and complex surgical performance [25]. In this way, outstanding early-career physicians can earn recognition and rewards aligned with their strengths, thus harmonising individual and organisational growth [26].

Limitations and future directions

While this study provides an empirically validated model, several limitations should be acknowledged, which also illuminate productive paths for future research. First, the use of single-item measures for each competency limits the assessment of internal consistency for individual constructs and may affect the stability of factor loadings. Future studies should develop multi-item scales for each dimension to enable more rigorous psychometric evaluation. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences about how these competencies develop or influence outcomes over time. Longitudinal studies are needed to track the evolution of these competencies and their relationship with career progression. Second, the sample was drawn exclusively from large public hospitals in one province, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to early-career physicians in private hospitals, primary care settings, or other geographical regions. Future research should aim for multicentre and multicontext sampling to test the robustness of the model across different healthcare systems and cultures. Third, the application of item parcelling to handle high correlations between latent factors, while statistically justified to achieve model fit, can obscure potential issues at the individual item level. Future iterations could explore alternative model specifications or seek to refine the item pool further. Furthermore, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce common method bias. Employing multisource evaluations would strengthen the evidence. In conclusion, these limitations do not undermine the present findings but rather chart a clear course for advancing this line of inquiry. Addressing these points will lead to a more applicable competency model for the development of early-career physicians.
