Introduction

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare disease with an etiology that has not been fully elucidated, characterized by abnormal clonal proliferation of Langerhans-like dendritic cells, involving aggregates of specialized dendritic cells in various organs. Due to its dual characteristics of inflammation and malignancy, it is classified as an inflammatory myeloid neoplasm in many studies [1, 2]. Cutaneous single-system LCH is relatively uncommon, typically presenting as erythematous papules located in the inguinal or abdominal areas, chest, or back [3]. Cases of LCH initially manifesting with haemorrhagic vesicles eruptions are extremely rare.

Case report

A full-term male infant, delivered via cesarean section due to family request at the onset of labor, presented with haemorrhagic vesicles at birth. Physical examination upon admission revealed multiple skin lesions of varying sizes (2–7 mm in diameter) on the trunk, limbs, palms, and soles. Some lesions were vesicular containing dark red fluid, while others showed skin ulceration, particularly prominent on the soles where some vesicles were exudative or crusted (Fig. 1A, B). No enlarged superficial lymph nodes were palpated. The liver was palpable approximately 1 cm below the costal margin, with soft consistency; the spleen was not palpable.

A, B Physical examination revealed multiple scattered skin lesions of varying sizes (diameter 2–7 mm) on the trunk, limbs, palms, and soles. The lesions were partially vesicular and contained dark red fluid, with some areas showing skin breakdown. Laboratory investigations: Interleukin-6 (IL-6) was mildly elevated.Complete blood count, reticulocyte count, metabolic panel, and coagulation tests, were all within normal limits. The initial differential diagnosis included infections such as syphilis, toxoplasmosis, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and herpes simplex virus; however, specific antibody testing for these pathogens in both the mother and infant returned negative results. A skin biopsy was performed in the dermatology department using tissue from the infant’s left foot.The specimen showed a dense infiltration of lymphocytes and histiocyte-like cells in the superficial dermis (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemical staining was positive for S100, CD1a Fig. 2B, CD207, and CD68, and negative for CD3 and CD20, confirming the diagnosis of Langerhans cell histiocytosis.

Due to the presence of skin lesions and elevated IL-6, the infant was started on cefotaxime-sulbactam for infection prophylaxis. Following dermatology consultation, topical fusidic acid and mucopolysaccharide polysulfate cream (Hirudoid) were applied to the skin lesions. The lesions demonstrated spontaneous resolution, gradually crusting over, and the patient was discharged. Post-discharge, the patient was followed weekly via scheduled telephone calls. Follow-up at 3–4 weeks postpartum showed resolution of the skin lesions. At the 2-month postpartum follow-up, there were no signs of recurrence. Figure 3 illustrates the timeline of this case.

Discussion

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is caused by the clonal expansion of myeloid precursor cells, which differentiate into CD1a+/CD207 + cells within lesions, leading to potential organ damage and dysfunction. LCH can occur at any age but is more common in children, with an annual incidence of 2.6 to 8.9 cases per million children [4].

Definitive diagnosis of LCH requires integration of clinical presentation, histology, and immunohistochemistry. Histopathological examination typically reveals an inflammatory infiltrate containing varying proportions of LCH cells with characteristic ‘coffee-bean’ shaped grooved nuclei and eosinophilic cytoplasm. Positive immunohistochemical staining for CD1a and CD207 (langerin) is diagnostic [5].

In this report, we describe a case of neonatal LCH with an initial presentation of hemorrhagic vesicles, some of which were ruptured and crusted, which was diagnosed by skin histopathological biopsy, and the skin lesions demonstrated spontaneous regression. We reviewed the published literature in the PubMed database using the keywords “Histiocytosis, Langerhans-Cell” and “Infant, Newborn”. A review of 13 reports published between March 2021 and March 2026, encompassing 13 cases, was conducted, and these findings are summarized in Table 2. Among the 13 patients, 10 presented with diverse cutaneous manifestations (including 4 cases of blueberry muffin rash, 1 cases of skin ulcers, 1 cases of papulovesicular lesions, 1 cases of hemorrhagic papules, 2 cases of pustules, and 1 stillborn fetus with targetoid lesions on the body). Two cases presented primarily with eyelid swelling, and one case presented with cholestasis.

In conclusion, we report a case of neonatal Langerhans cell histiocytosis presenting initially with diverse cutaneous manifestations and demonstrating spontaneous regression. To better understand the varied skin presentations of LCH in the neonatal period, a review of the published literature was also conducted. The rarity of neonatal LCH and the diversity of its cutaneous manifestations make its diagnosis challenging. Furthermore, given the potential for spontaneous resolution of the skin lesions, LCH can be easily overlooked or misdiagnosed without comprehensive histological and immunohistochemical examination. This poses significant challenges for the pediatric medical team in establishing a timely diagnosis and initiating appropriate treatment for neonatal LCH.

Conclusion

Neonatal Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare neoplastic disorder, whose rarity poses significant challenges in diagnosis and treatment. This study analyzes a neonatal case of LCH presenting with hemorrhagic vesicles and partial erosion/crusting as the initial symptom, supplemented by a literature review, aiming to enhance clinicians’ awareness of this rare disease, minimize misdiagnosis in clinical practice, and underscore the importance of multidisciplinary management.
