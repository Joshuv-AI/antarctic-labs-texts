Integrating whole-genome bisulfite sequencing and TCGA data reveals methylation patterns associated with the MTHFR 677 C > T Variant
Authors: Guoping Zhang, Juanjuan Hou, Mingliang Guo, JiamingYan, Xingzhe Gao, Run Yang, Zhenyun Wang, ZhiChun Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the influence of the methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) 677C > T polymorphism on bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA) using whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data. The TT genotype was associated with reduced global DNA methylation, thereby altering the molecular landscape of BLCA. Differential methylation analysis identified three molecular subtypes of BLCA. Subtype 2, characterized by the lowest methylation levels, was associated with favorable prognosis, earlier disease stage, and a higher frequency of FGFR3 mutations, along with increased infiltration of plasma cells, memory B cells, and naive CD4+T cells. In contrast, Subtype 3 exhibited increased neutrophil infiltration and upregulation of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-L1 and CTLA-4. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that low methylation and high expression of TRIM27 in Subtype 2 were associated with improved prognosis, whereas high methylation and low expression of RASSF1 in Subtype 3 were associated with poorer outcomes. Metabolic pathways were enriched in Subtype 2, whereas cell cycle-related genes were overexpressed in Subtype 3. These findings indicate that the MTHFR 677C > T polymorphism modulates DNA methylation and significantly influences the molecular characteristics, immune microenvironment, and prognosis of BLCA. Methylation-based subtyping may provide biomarkers for precise diagnosis and therapeutic stratification in BLCA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54966-3.

Introduction

Bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA) is among the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, with 613,799 newly diagnosed cases and 220,347 associated deaths reported in 2022, placing a substantial burden on global public health systems1. Although surgical resection, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy have markedly improved patient survival rates in recent years, the recurrence and mortality rates of advanced and metastatic BLCA remain alarmingly high2. This clinical challenge underscores the urgent need for personalized diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, with epigenetic changes serving as key regulators of tumor biological behavior and offering new research directions3.

DNA methylation is a crucial epigenetic modification that influences tumor initiation and progression by regulating gene expression4. Aberrant changes in methylation levels, particularly in the promoter regions of CpG islands of the genome, are well established as hallmarks of cancer5. For instance, hypermethylation of tumor suppressor gene promoters and genome-wide hypomethylation have been demonstrated to correlate with the aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance of various malignancies6. In line with this, in BLCA, alterations in methylation patterns are closely associated with tumor grading, metastatic potential, and patient prognosis7. Studies have demonstrated that aberrantly methylated genomic regions in BLCA are associated with dysregulation of key pathways, including cell cycle control, DNA repair, and immune evasion8,9. These findings underscore the importance of systematically characterizing DNA methylation patterns in BLCA and their dysregulation, not only to elucidate tumor heterogeneity but also to inform the development of novel biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

The Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase (MTHFR) gene plays a central role in folate metabolism and DNA methylation10. MTHFR catalyzes the conversion of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, which serves as the primary methyl donor in DNA methylation reactions11. Common polymorphisms of the MTHFR gene, such as C677T (rs1801133), can significantly influence genome-wide methylation levels by altering enzyme activity and the availability of methyl donors12. Studies have demonstrated that MTHFR polymorphisms are associated with the susceptibility to and prognosis of various diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, neural tube defects, and certain cancers13. In BLCA, abnormal expression levels of MTHFR and the C677T polymorphism have been reported to potentially correlate with tumor risk and clinical outcomes14. For example, it has been shown that patients with the TT genotype exhibit more pronounced genome-wide hypomethylation in cancer tissues than those with the CT genotype, which is associated with differing survival prognoses15. In our previous work, significantly elevated methylation levels of TWIST1, ONECUT2, and VIM were identified in urinary exfoliated cells from patients with BLCA. In addition, the high-prevalence MTHFR 677 C > T variant was associated with increased methylation of the TWIST1 promoter. These findings suggest that the T allele may serve as a genetic risk factor for BLCA and is closely associated with aberrant methylation of key genes16. However, the mechanisms by which the MTHFR 677 C > T polymorphism influences the molecular characteristics and biological behavior of BLCA through alterations in DNA methylation regulation remain unclear.

To date, there has been a lack of systematic research regarding the relationship between MTHFR (677C > T) gene polymorphism and BLCA methylation patterns, particularly in the form of large cohort studies that integrate whole-genome methylation data with multi-omics analysis. This study intended to address this research gap by investigating the regulatory role of the MTHFR gene polymorphism (TT vs. CT) on BLCA methylation patterns and elucidating its impact on tumor biological behavior and clinical prognosis. To achieve this, we utilized whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data from 10 pairs of BLCA and adjacent normal tissues, complemented by 450 k methylation array data and RNA-seq data from TCGA-BLCA cohort, to systematically analyze the associations between the MTHFR (677C > T) gene polymorphism and methylation patterns, gene expression, and clinical characteristics. By identifying differentially methylated positions (DMPs) and performing unsupervised clustering, we delineated methylation subgroups of BLCA and further explored the relationships between these subgroups, the MTHFR (677C > T) genotype, key signaling pathways, and patient prognosis. This study provides additional evidence for the epigenetic dysregulation associated with BLCA and identifies potential biomarkers to support the development of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies within a precision medicine framework.

Methods

Sample collection and clinical information

For this study, paired samples of tumor and adjacent normal tissues were collected from 10 patients diagnosed with pathologically confirmed BLCA at Qinyang People’s Hospital. The clinical data included age, sex, tumor grade, and stage. The methylation status of TWIST1, ONECUT2, and VIM genes in urinary exfoliated cells was assessed using methylation-specific PCR (MSP). Twist1 methylation was analyzed by Anhui Dajian Medical Technology Co., Ltd., while ONECUT2 and VIM methylation were evaluated by Guangzhou Benchmark Medical Co., Ltd. The study received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Qingyang People’s Hospital (Approval No. 202202–01). All participants signed informed consent forms and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

MTHFR (677C > T) gene polymorphism detection

Genomic DNA was extracted from whole-blood samples using a Nucleic Acid Extraction Kit (Beijing Tiangen Biology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The MTHFR 677 C > T polymorphism was detected using a PCR-based fluorescent probe melting curve method with a Human MTHFR Gene 677 C > T Mutation Detection Kit (Shenzhen Yilifang Biology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Differences in probe-binding stability resulting from base variation (C/T) among genotypes produced distinct melting temperatures (Tm). Genotypes were determined by analysis of the dissociation curves and corresponding Tm distributions: CC genotype (wild type, 55–58 °C), TT genotype (homozygous mutant, 62–65 °C), and CT genotype (heterozygous, two peaks).

whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS)

WGBS was performed on 10 paired samples of BLCA tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues, including four pairs with the TT genotype, five pairs with the CT genotype, and one pair with the CC genotype. After DNA extraction, 200 ng of genomic DNA was mixed with 1 ng of unmethylated λDNA and subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold kit (Zymo Research, Genesee Scientific, San Diego, CA, USA). A DNA nanoball sequencing (DNBSEQ) WGBS library was prepared and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform (MGI Tech Co., Shenzhen, China) using a 150 bp paired-end protocol.

Quality control of the raw sequencing data (in fastq format) was conducted using FastQC (v0.11.9), and low-quality reads along with adapter sequences were removed using Trim Galore (v0.6.5). The cleaned data were aligned to the human reference genome (hg38) using Bismark (v0.22.3) with the default alignment parameters. Methylation levels (β values, ranging from 0 to 1) were extracted using Bismark’s methylation_extractor module, with a focus on CG sites. For CpG sites located at the same genomic position on opposite DNA strands, the mean methylation value was calculated, and each site was treated as a single entity. Only CpG sites with a minimum read coverage of ≥ 5 × across 50% samples were retained for downstream differential methylation analysis.

TCGA-BLCA data acquisition and preprocessing

Illumina HumanMethylation450 BeadChip (450 K) data (Level 3, β-value matrix) for 412 BLCA samples were obtained from the TCGA-BLCA cohort. Data acquisition was performed using the TCGAbiolinks R package (v2.18.0) (parameters: project = “TCGA-BLCA”, data.category = “DNA Methylation”, data.type = “Masked Intensities”, platform = “Illumina Human Methylation 450”), followed by downloading IDAT files using GDCdownload. The IDAT files were preprocessed with the minfi R package (v1.36.0), including removal of probes with more than 20% missing values and those located on the sex chromosomes.

RNA sequencing data (HTSeq-Counts) for TCGA-BLCA were also obtained using TCGAbiolinks (parameters: data.category = “Transcriptome Profiling”, data.type = “Gene Expression Quantification”, workflow.type = “STAR—Counts”) and normalized using the DESeq2 R package (v1.30.0) to generate a variance-stabilized transformation (VST) expression matrix. Clinical data, including age, sex, tumor grade, stage, and survival time, were retrieved and curated using TCGAbiolinks (parameters: project = “TCGA-BLCA”, data.category = “Clinical”, data.type = “Clinical Supplement”, data.format = “BCR Biotab”).

Acquisition and preprocessing of validation datasets

The methylation microarray data were obtained from the GEO database. Two methylation datasets are available in GEO, both utilizing the 850 k (Infinium MethylationEPIC) platform: GSE183920 (peripheral blood methylation data from 603 patients with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer) and GSE193208 (cfDNA methylation data from 72 bladder cancer patients prior to neoadjuvant chemotherapy). Raw data were preprocessed using the minfi R package with the same parameters to generate β-value matrices. To provide external validation of the immunotherapy response predictions, the IMvigor210 cohort (atezolizumab-treated metastatic urothelial carcinoma) were obtained from the package IMvigor210CoreBiologies (v1.0.1). According to the phenotype, 168 samples were included with tissue = “bladder”and available transcriptomic data and immunotherapy results.

Genome-wide differential methylation analysis

Whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) data from 10 paired BLCA tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples were analyzed to identify differential methylation using either a paired t test or a Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Samples with the TT genotype (n = 4 pairs) and CT genotype (n = 5 pairs) were analyzed separately. Differentially methylated positions (DMPs) were defined using the criteria Δβ > 0.2 and an adjusted p value < 0.05. Unless otherwise specified, adjusted p values denote Benjamini–Hochberg FDR computed at the CpG level across all tested sites within each comparison. The CBS algorithm (PSCBS package, v1.8.0) was employed to identify differentially methylated regions (DMRs) using the following criteria: regions must contain at least three CpG sites, be longer than 200 bp, and exhibit average Δβ > 0.2. Based on these criteria, we obtained genotype-specific DMPs and DMRs for both TT and CT genotypes. DMRs were further classified according to Δβ values as hypermethylated (mean Δβ > 0.2, indicating increased methylation in the TT genotype) or hypomethylated (mean Δβ < −0.2, indicating decreased methylation in the TT genotype). DMR coordinates were mapped to the hg38 reference genome to identify differentially methylated genes (DMGs). Particular emphasis was placed on DMRs located within 2 kb upstream of transcription start sites (TSSs), as methylation changes in these regions are most likely to influence gene expression.

Four scenarios were defined for DMR distribution within these promoter-proximal regions: (1) regions containing only hypermethylated DMRs, (2) regions containing only hypomethylated DMRs, (3) regions containing both hypermethylated and hypomethylated DMRs, and (4) regions without DMRs. Genes corresponding to scenarios (1) and (2) were defined as DMGs.

Molecular subgroup classification

Coordinate-based matching was performed to align DMPs identified from WGBS data with CpG sites in the TCGA-BLCA 450 K array dataset, thereby generating a set of overlapping sites. Overlapping sites were defined only when the CpG site identified by WGBS precisely matched the genomic coordinates of the target CpG interrogated by the Illumina HumanMethylation450 array probe; no positional deviation within the probe-binding region was permitted. Duplicate probes were removed, and only probes uniquely annotated across datasets were retained to minimize redundancy. Unsupervised clustering was performed using the non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) R package (v1.54.0). The maximum number of clusters was set to 10, and 50 iterations were conducted. The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined based on the cophenetic correlation coefficient and silhouette width. The same coordinate-mapping strategy was applied to two GEO datasets to identify corresponding probes. Subsequent NMF clustering analysis of these datasets similarly identified an optimal clustering solution of k = 3. Clustering was conducted on batch-corrected beta values (ComBat-adjusted) without explicit adjustment for tumor purity. Notably, as direct MTHFR 677 C > T genotyping data are not available in the TCGA-BLCA cohort, the subgroup assignment in this dataset represents an inference based on CpG site overlap between WGBS-derived DMPs and the 450 K array, rather than direct genotypic classification.

Survival analysis

The differences in overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) among various molecular subgroups were assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and the log-rank test (survival R package, v3.2–11). Clinical characteristics were analyzed and compared across subgroups using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. To assess whether the methylation-based subgroup classification provides independent prognostic information beyond standard clinicopathological variables, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed. Covariates included age (continuous), sex (male/female), tumor stage (TNM stages I/II, III/IV), tumor grade (low/high), and methylation-based subgroup (Subgroup 1, 2, 3). Hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported (survival R package, v3.2–11).

Association between methylation and gene expression

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was conducted for each molecular subgroup in TCGA-BLCA employing the DESeq2 R package (|log2FC|> 1, FDR < 0.05). Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the clusterProfiler R package (v3.18.1)17, concentrating on Gene Ontology (GO). The correlations between differentially methylated positions (DMPs) in promoter regions and the expression of the respective gene were calculated based on Spearman’s correlation coefficient and the corresponding p values, with correction for multiple testing applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Gene pairs exhibiting significant negative correlations-defined as increased methylation associated with decreased expression, or vice versa-were identified using an FDR threshold < 0.05.

qPCR and bisulfite sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from frozen tissue samples using an Animal Tissue/Cell Total RNA Extraction Kit (ServiceBio, Wuhan, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. For each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, Dalian, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on a LineGene real-time PCR system (Bioer, Hangzhou, China) using SweScript All-in-One RT SuperMix (ServiceBio, Wuhan, China). Primer sequences were as follows: TRIM27, forward 5′-AGCCCATGATGCTCGACTG-3′ and reverse 5′-GGGCACGACACGTTAGTCT-3′; RASSF1, forward 5′-AGGACGGTTCTTACACAGGCT-3′ and reverse 5′-TGGGCAGGTAAAAGGAAGTGC-3′. β-Actin was used as an internal control. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Genomic DNA was extracted from fresh tumor tissues using the TIANGEN DNA Extraction Kit (DP304, Beijing, China) and subjected to bisulfite conversion. Methylation-specific primers were designed using MethPrimer to amplify promoter regions of TRIM27 and RASSF1. PCR products were purified and sequenced by Sanger sequencing (Wuhan AuGCT Biotech Co., Ltd.). Methylation levels at individual CpG sites were quantified from chromatograms based on the relative peak heights of cytosine and thymine, reflecting the degree of methylation at each site.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2). Continuous variables were compared between groups using either the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or the t-test, while categorical variables were analyzed using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. To control the FDR, multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For the WGBS data, all samples were processed in a single sequencing run, minimizing technical batch effects. For the TCGA-BLCA 450 K array data, samples were processed through the standardized GDC pipeline. Each GEO validation dataset was processed and normalized independently prior to cross-cohort comparison to avoid introducing batch artifacts.

Genome-wide differential methylation analysis

Using whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS), we first assessed the methylation levels across samples of different genotypes. In tissues with the TT genotype (n = 4), the average methylation level across the genome (0.57 ± 0.08) was significantly lower than that in the CT genotype group (n = 5) (0.64 ± 0.07) (p = 0.031, Fig. 1A). This observation aligns with the mechanism of reduced availability of methyl donors due to decreased activity of the MTHFR enzyme. Genomic annotation analysis revealed that the methylation levels in the TSS_up5kb, transcript, and TTS_down5kb regions were significantly lower in the TT genotype than in the CT genotype (Fig. 1B-D). A total of 96,867 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) were identified in the TT genotype group and 187,796 in the CT genotype group (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Table S1). Genomic annotation indicated that 4.53% of DMRs in the TT group were located in promoter regions (defined as 2 kb upstream and downstream of the TSS), whereas 55.73% were intragenic (intronic and exonic) and 22.65% were intergenic (Fig. 2E). Corresponding proportions in the CT group were 3.06%, 48.45%, and 27.39%, respectively (Fig. 2E). The CT genotype group exhibited a greater number of differentially methylated genes (DMGs) (n = 1,395) than the TT genotype group (n = 787) (Fig. 2F; Supplementary Table S2). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis demonstrated that CT-associated DMGs were significantly enriched in biological processes including protein catabolism, lipid metabolism, and responses to growth factors (Fig. 2G; Supplementary Table S3). In contrast, TT-specific DMGs were predominantly enriched in processes related to embryonic morphogenesis, central nervous system neuron differentiation, and head development (Fig. 2H; Supplementary Table S4). Pathways overlapping between the two groups were primarily associated with skeletal system development. These findings suggest that MTHFR 677 C > T genotypes may differentially influence BLCA development and progression through distinct biological processes, including protein and lipid metabolism as well as developmental and differentiation pathways.

Relationship between molecular subgroup classification and clinical prognosis

Comparison of DMPs identified by WGBS with CpG sites from the TCGA-BLCA 450 K methylation array yielded 1,977 overlapping CpG sites, including 1,158 CT-associated DMPs and 819 TT-associated ones. NMF clustering was performed using the β values of these sites. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the cophenetic correlation coefficient (0.98) and silhouette width (0.85) (Fig. 3A,B). Accordingly, TCGA-BLCA samples (n = 408) were classified into three molecular subgroups based on WGBS-derived methylation characteristics (Fig. 3C). Subgroup 1 (n = 77) exhibited high methylation levels (median = 0.42), Subgroup 2 (n = 135) displayed the lowest methylation (median = 0.36), and Subgroup 3 (n = 200) showed high methylation (median = 0.45) (Fig. 3D, E). Although significant differences in methylation levels were observed among these three subgroups, all demonstrated reduced methylation levels compared with the normal samples (Fig. 3F). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that the overall survival (OS) of patients in Subgroup 2 was significantly better than that of those in the other two subgroups (log-rank p = 0.008, Fig. 3F), whereas no significant difference in progression-free survival (PFS) was observed (p = 0.71). We then performed multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses incorporating the methylation-based subgroup classification alongside standard clinicopathological variables. For overall survival (OS), Subgroup 2 (HR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.40–0.79, p = 0.048) and subgroup 1 (reference) showed significantly better outcomes compared to subgroup 3 (HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.12–1.52, p = 0.05), after adjusting for age, sex, stage, and grade (Supplementary Table S5). For progression-free survival (PFS), the methylation subgroup classification did not show prognostic significance (p > 0.05). These results indicated that the methylation-based molecular classification provides prognostic information independent of standard clinicopathological variables in terms of overall survival.

Independent validation

Clustering analysis of the GSE183920 dataset further supported the robustness of the three molecular subgroups (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). This dataset provided event data regarding patient mortality over a 10-year period. In subgroup 1 (n = 258), 163 patients died within the 10-year survival period, resulting in a mortality rate of 63.18%. In subgroup 2 (n = 153), 77 patients died, corresponding to a mortality rate of 50.32%. Finally, in subgroup 3 (n = 192), 133 patients died, leading to a mortality rate of 69.27%. Macrophage populations (M0, M1, and M2) were significantly reduced in Subgroup 2 compared with the levels in Subgroups 1 and 3 (Supplementary Fig. S2E-G). Additionally, the proportion of monocytes was significantly higher in Subgroup 2 (Supplementary Fig. S2I), whereas neutrophil infiltration was significantly lower (Supplementary Fig. S2J). No significant differences were observed among subgroups for eosinophils or activated and resting natural killer (NK) cells (Supplementary Fig. S2H, S2K, S2L). Activated and resting CD4 memory T cells were most abundant in Subgroup 3 (Supplementary Fig. S2M, S2N), whereas naïve CD4+ T cells were most abundant in Subgroup 2 (Supplementary Fig. S2O). Although CD8+ T cells were slightly more abundant in Subgroup 2 than in Subgroups 1 and 3, the difference was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. S2P).

Integrated transcriptomic data analysis

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was performed for each TCGA-BLCA subgroup, identifying a total of 2,333 significant DEGs (FDR < 0.05, |log₂ fold change|≥ 1; Supplementary Fig. S3A; Supplementary Table S6). Genes specifically upregulated in Subgroup 1 (n = 17) were primarily associated with positive regulation of calcium ion import and the phosphatidylcholine biosynthetic process (Supplementary Fig. S3B; Supplementary Table S7). In Subgroup 2, 1,291 upregulated genes were identified and were predominantly enriched in processes such as RNA splicing and ATP synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S3C; Supplementary Table S8). In contrast, 1,025 genes upregulated in Subgroup 3 were significantly associated with immune-related biological processes, including leukocyte-mediated immunity (Supplementary Fig. S3D; Supplementary Table S9).

Integration of methylation and transcriptomic data demonstrated a significant inverse correlation between promoter methylation and gene expression (median ρ = − 0.17, adjusted p < 0.05). This association was most pronounced in Subgroup 2 (median ρ for Subgroups 1, 2, and 3: − 0.12, − 0.16, and − 0.13, respectively).

We further discovered that TRIM27, a gene marker for the subgroup 2, and RASSF1, a gene marker for the subgroup 3, exhibited the most significant negative correlation between methylation and expression levels. Notably, 122 methylation probes were associated with TRIM27, of which 59.84% demonstrated a significant negative correlation (Fig. 6A). By sorting the samples from low to high based on expression levels, we observed that samples from the subgroup 2 were predominantly located within the high-expression range, with the promoter-related region probes of the subgroup 2 samples exhibiting lower methylation levels (Fig. 6B). A box plot (Fig. 6C) further confirmed that the expression level of the TRIM27 gene in the subgroup 2 was significantly higher than those in the other two subgroups. Survival analysis indicated that patients in the group with high TRIM27 expression (top 25% of samples) had a significantly better prognosis than those in the low expression group (lowest 25%) (Fig. 6D), with a log-rank P-value of 0.0025 and a hazard ratio (HR) of 0.49. Similarly, our analysis of the RASSF1 gene revealed that it exhibited the highest expression level in the subgroup 3 (Fig. 6E), while the methylation level of its promoter region was the lowest in this subgroup. Experimental validation of the TRIM27 and RASSF1 methylation–expression association

Gene expression quantification and bisulfite Sanger sequencing were performed for TRIM27 and RASSF1 in 10 fresh tumor tissues to evaluate the relationship between promoter methylation and gene expression. Similarly, methylation of the key CpG site in the RASSF1 promoter region was inversely correlated with gene expression (Fig. 7B; Supplementary Table S12). Discussion

MTHFR is a crucial enzyme involved in folate metabolism, responsible for converting 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate into 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, which is essential for the re-methylation of homocysteine to methionine. Genetic polymorphisms are the primary contributors to the reduced activity of this enzyme, and related studies have established a significant association between the MTHFR (677C > T) polymorphism and an elevated risk of bladder cancer18. This study integrates whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS), multi-omics data from the TCGA-BLCA cohort, and single-cell RNA sequencing and methylation datasets from the GEO database to systematically investigate the impact of the MTHFR (677C > T) gene polymorphism (TT vs. CT) on methylation patterns, molecular subgroups, and clinical prognosis in BLCA. The results indicate that TT and CT genotypes exhibit distinct methylation signatures that influence different biological pathways. Based on these features, BLCA samples were classified into three molecular subgroups with significantly different prognostic outcomes. Among these, Subgroup 2 was characterized by the lowest methylation levels, the most favorable prognosis, and distinct immune microenvironment features.

This study represents the first systematic analysis of differences in DNA methylation between BLCA patients with MTHFR (677C > T) TT and CT genotypes. Our findings indicate that samples associated with the MTHFR (677C > T) CT genotype exhibit higher genome-wide methylation levels, aligning with the expectation that reduced MTHFR (677C > T) enzyme activity results in altered levels of the methyl donor S-adenosylmethionine (SAM)19. In addition, high methylation frequencies of ONECUT2 and VIM (100%) were observed across the cohort, in agreement with previous reports16. However, the limited sample size-particularly the inclusion of only one case with the CC genotype-restricts statistical power and limits robust comparative analysis. Larger cohorts are required to validate these findings and to more precisely define their biological and clinical implications.

CT genotype-associated DMRs were primarily enriched in pathways related to protein catabolism, lipid metabolism, and growth factor responses, whereas TT genotype-associated ones were enriched in processes such as embryonic morphogenesis, neuronal differentiation, and head development. These differences likely reflect genotype-dependent variation in MTHFR enzymatic activity and methyl donor availability. The CT genotype retains enzymatic activity closer to that of the wild type, thereby maintaining more efficient conversion of 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate to 5-methyltetrahydrofolate and supporting localized hypermethylation at specific genomic loci15. Epigenetic silencing of genes involved in proteolysis, lipid metabolism, and growth factor signaling may contribute to BLCA progression by disrupting protein homeostasis, reprogramming metabolic pathways, and enhancing proliferative signaling. In contrast, the TT genotype is associated with a marked reduction in MTHFR activity, leading to depletion of 5-methyltetrahydrofolate and consequent global hypomethylation19. WGBS data suggest that this hypomethylation may relieve transcriptional repression of developmental genes, consistent with dedifferentiation, acquisition of stem-like properties, and increased phenotypic plasticity. Reactivation of embryonic signaling pathways, including Hedgehog, Wnt, and Notch, has been implicated in tumor initiation, progression, and therapeutic resistance in bladder cancer. Preferential hypomethylation of these pathways in TT genotype carriers suggests a mechanism by which impaired MTHFR function disrupts epigenetic regulation of developmental programs. These observations are consistent with prior studies linking the MTHFR 677 C > T variant to increased cancer risk20–22.

By integrating DMPs identified through WGBS with TCGA-BLCA 450 K methylation data, BLCA samples were stratified into three molecular subgroups. These subgroups exhibited significantly different survival prognoses and clinical characteristics. Notably, subgroup 2 demonstrated the lowest genome-wide methylation levels, the best overall survival rate, and the highest chemotherapy response rate. These findings were confirmed in two independent cohorts, underscoring the robustness and generalizability of this classification. Subgroup 2 was associated with a lower tumor grade and an early stage, and was enriched with FGFR3 and STAG2 mutations, aligning with the molecular characteristics of muscle-noninvasive bladder cancer23. In contrast, subgroups 1 and 3 exhibited higher methylation levels and poorer prognoses, with subgroup 3 being enriched in RB1 and PIK3CA mutations, typical of muscle-invasive bladder cancer23. This molecular classification, based on WGBS-derived methylation patterns, not only serves as a prognostic prediction tool but also reveals the underlying molecular mechanisms in the different subgroups. Our findings support the molecular subgroup classification system of bladder cancer proposed in previous studies24, with the unique aspect of our association of these subgroups with WGBS-derived methylation patterns identified here for the first time.

Transcriptomic analysis further revealed gene expression patterns specific to different subgroups. By integrating methylation and expression data, we identified a significant negative correlation between promoter methylation and gene expression, which was particularly pronounced in subgroup 2, demonstrating the best prognosis. TRIM27, serving as a marker gene for subgroup 2, exhibited a hypomethylated and highly expressed state significantly associated with favorable outcomes.

In contrast, RASSF1, as a marker gene for subgroup 3, displayed upregulated expression linked to poor prognosis, consistent with a report by Bao et al. describing that abnormal expression of RASSF1A in BLCA patients correlates with lower disease-free survival (DFS)25. Previous studies have also documented frequent epigenetic inactivation of RASSF1A in human bladder cancer26, with experimental evidence indicating that RASSF1A inactivation may result from the interplay of epigenetic and genetic mechanisms27. Experiments performed in 10 bladder cancer tissue samples confirmed an inverse relationship between promoter methylation and expression of TRIM27 and RASSF1, providing additional support for the methylation-mediated regulatory mechanisms identified through bioinformatic analyses. These findings underscore the critical role of epigenetic regulation in the progression of BLCA, influenced by the MTHFR (677C > T) genotype. However, these findings still require careful interpretation given the established tumor-suppressive role of RASSF1A in bladder cancer27,28. The RASSF1 gene produces two major isoforms via alternative promoter usage: RASSF1A, a well-characterized tumor suppressor frequently silenced by promoter hypermethylation, and RASSF1C, which has been reported to exhibit oncogenic properties including promotion of cell proliferation29,30. Since TCGA RNA-seq data and our qPCR validation measure total RASSF1 transcripts without distinguishing isoforms, the elevated RASSF1 expression in the poor-prognosis Subgroup 3 may reflect predominant RASSF1C expression-consistent with concurrent RASSF1A silencing-rather than contradicting its tumor-suppressive role. Future studies employing isoform-specific analyses are needed to resolve this complexity and clarify the distinct contributions of each isoform to BLCA progression.

Analysis of the immune microenvironment revealed that the prognostically favorable subgroup 2 is characterized by higher infiltration of plasma cells, memory B cells, and naive CD4 T cells, whereas the prognostically unfavorable subgroup 3 is marked by neutrophil infiltration and elevated expression of immune checkpoints. This disparity in the immune microenvironment may explain why different subgroups vary in their responses to immunotherapy and chemotherapy31,32 These findings indicate that the subgroups based on WGBS-derived methylation characteristics are also characterized by distinct features of the immune microenvironment, which may constitute an important mechanistic link between epigenetic regulation and clinical prognosis in BLCA. Subgroup 3 demonstrates the highest Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score, suggesting that patients in this category may experience a poorer response to immunotherapy, consistent with their elevated T-cell dysfunction scores.

Mutational spectrum analysis further revealed that different subgroups exhibited characteristic mutational patterns, with subgroup 2 being enriched for FGFR3 mutations, while subgroups 1 and 3 were predominantly characterized by RB1 mutations. These genomic features provide potential targets for targeted therapy33,34. The high mutation rate of FGFR3 in subgroup 2 may explain its potential sensitivity to FGFR inhibitors, while the elevated expression of immune checkpoints in subgroup 3 suggests possible benefits from combined immunotherapy. In summary, our study is the first to reveal that the MTHFR (677C > T) gene polymorphism may regulate the molecular characteristics, immune microenvironment, and clinical prognosis of bladder cancer by influencing DNA methylation patterns.

Despite these findings, several limitations of this work should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of the in-house WGBS cohort was limited (10 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues), with relatively few patients carrying the TT or CT genotype, which may reduce the statistical robustness. Larger, independent cohorts are required to validate the obtained results. Second, direct MTHFR 677 C > T genotype data were not available for the TCGA-BLCA cohort; therefore, genotype–methylation associations were inferred indirectly, which may have introduced bias. Future studies should incorporate direct genotyping in well-characterized BLCA cohorts to clarify causal relationships between genotype, methylation patterns, and clinical outcomes35. Third, although experimental validation of methylation-expression relationships for TRIM27 and RASSF1 was performed using qPCR and bisulfite Sanger sequencing in clinical tissues, these analyses establish correlative rather than causal relationships between promoter methylation and gene expression. Definitive evidence for a causal link would require functional assays, such as treatment of BLCA cell lines (e.g., T24, UM-UC-3, or HT-1376) with the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5-aza-2’-deoxycytidine (5-aza-dC), followed by quantitative assessment of TRIM27 and RASSF1 (isoform-specific) expression changes via RT-qPCR and methylation changes via bisulfite sequencing. Furthermore, CRISPR-dCas9-based targeted demethylation of the TRIM27 and RASSF1 promoters would provide even more specific evidence for the causal role of promoter methylation in regulating these genes. While these functional experiments are beyond the scope of the current investigation, they represent important next steps that are actively being planned. Additionally, validation of immune microenvironment features using orthogonal methods, such as multiplex immunohistochemistry or imaging mass cytometry, would further strengthen the computational deconvolution findings.

In summary, the results of this study highlight the potential clinical utility of MTHFR-associated methylation signatures as biomarkers for BLCA stratification. Patients in Subgroup 2 may exhibit improved responses to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and could benefit from more intense chemotherapy regimens, whereas patients in Subgroup 3 may derive greater benefit from combination immunotherapy. MTHFR 677 C > T genotyping may also complement existing risk assessment strategies. For individuals with the TT genotype, folate supplementation may be considered to modulate methylation status, whereas for those with the CT genotype, genotype-specific methylation patterns and their implications for treatment response should be taken into account. Although methylation of the MTHFR gene itself was not evaluated, genotype-associated genome-wide methylation patterns demonstrated potential utility for BLCA stratification. Future studies should compare these signatures with established biomarkers and assess their detectability in circulating cell-free DNA or urine-derived DNA, thereby facilitating the development of noninvasive monitoring strategies and personalized therapeutic approaches.
