A GABAergic neural circuit from the suprachiasmatic nucleus to DG drives cognitive impairment in migraine mice
Authors: Qingfang Xie, Yu Zhang, Ying Huang, Wen Yan, Yanjie Zhou, Zheman Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive impairment and circadian rhythm disturbances are prevalent comorbidities of migraine, yet their underlying neural circuit mechanisms remain poorly understood. The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), as the master circadian pacemaker, and the hippocampus, essential for memory, are key regions involved in these symptoms. The dentate gyrus (DG) in the hippocampus acts as the main entry point for information and is essential for pattern separation, a key memory process. We hypothesized that a direct neural pathway from the SCN to the DG might link circadian dysfunction to cognitive deficits in migraine.MethodsUsing a nitroglycerin (NTG)-induced chronic migraine mouse model, we combined behavioral assays, immunofluorescence, chemogenetics, monosynaptic retrograde tracing, and projection-specific optogenetics to investigate the role of the SCN and its potential connection to the DG in migraine-related cognitive impairment.ResultsNTG-treated mice exhibited not only pain hypersensitivity but also significant circadian rhythm disruption and deficits in recognition and spatial working memory. Our immunofluorescence analysis revealed a marked increase in c-Fos expression within the SCN of NTG-induced migraine mice. Notably, despite increased total c-Fos expression in the SCN, the density and proportion of c-Fos⁺/GAD67⁺ cells were reduced, whereas c-Fos⁺/CaMKII⁺ cells showed no significant change. These findings indicate an altered recruitment of neuronal subpopulations rather than uniform changes in SCN neuronal activity. This interpretation was further supported by chemogenetic manipulation of SCN neurons, in which inhibition exacerbated, whereas activation rescued, cognitive deficits without affecting pain sensitivity. Immunostaining revealed increased c-Fos recruitment of GABAergic interneurons in the DG. Using convergent anterograde and retrograde tracing approaches, we obtained anatomical evidence supporting the presence of a direct SCN→DG projection with preferential association to DG inhibitory interneuron populations. Projection-specific optogenetic activation of SCN terminals in the DG was sufficient to reverse cognitive impairments in migraine mice.ConclusionsOur research reveals a previously underappreciated GABAergic pathway from the SCN to the DG that is functionally implicated in cognitive deficits associated with migraine. The findings suggest that migraine is associated with altered recruitment of neuronal subpopulations and network-level coordination within the SCN and along the SCN-DG pathway. This is accompanied by increased recruitment of GABAergic interneurons in the DG and impaired DG information processing. Together, these results identify the SCN–DG circuit as a potential neuromodulatory target for future investigation of cognitive symptoms associated with migraine.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02341-y.Keywords: Migraine, Cognitive impairment, Suprachiasmatic Nucleus, Hippocampus, GABA, Neural circuit, Circadian rhythms

Introduction

Migraine is a prevalent and complex neurological disorder that extends beyond recurrent headaches to include a spectrum of debilitating comorbidities [1, 2]. Notably, cognitive dysfunction, affecting memory, attention, and executive function, is reported in approximately 40% of migraine patients, with severity often correlating with attack frequency [3–5]. Concurrently, circadian rhythm and sleep disturbances are observed in more individuals with migraine, influencing both disease chronicity and daily functioning [6–8]. Importantly, circadian rhythm disruption is established as an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment [9]. This is supported by evidence from both clinical populations and experimental models, which links it to structural and functional alterations in memory-critical brain regions, such as hippocampal atrophy and impaired neural synchronization [10, 11]. Therefore, we hypothesized that cognitive dysfunction in migraine may be linked, at least in part, to underlying circadian rhythm disturbances. This knowledge gap hinders a comprehensive pathophysiological understanding of migraine and the development of effective therapies targeting its non-headache manifestations.

The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus, the master circadian pacemaker [12], is a prime candidate for integrating these functions. Beyond generating daily rhythms, the SCN regulates broad neural networks via its efferent projections [13–15], influencing arousal, mood, and cognition [16–18]. The precise timekeeping and stable output of the SCN depend on highly rhythmic neuronal activity and a finely tuned local microcircuit where GABA serves as the principal neurotransmitter and GABAergic neurons constitute the majority of neuronal populations [19–21]. Growing evidence suggests that SCN dysfunction may contribute to the pathophysiology of various neuropsychiatric disorders including cognitive impairment [22–24]. However, whether and how the SCN participates in migraine-related cognitive impairment remains unexplored.

The hippocampus is a core brain region for episodic and spatial memory [25]. Its dentate gyrus (DG) subregion, acting as the primary gateway for information entering the hippocampus [26], plays crucial roles in pattern separation and memory encoding. Notably, hippocampal function itself is subject to circadian modulation [27–29]. While anatomical studies suggest that the SCN can influence hippocampal activity indirectly via polysynaptic pathways [14, 30], it remains unknown whether a direct projection exists between the SCN and the DG, and if so, whether this pathway contributes to the cognitive impairment of migraine.

Based on this background, our study aimed to systematically address the following questions: First, does the functional state of the SCN alter in chronic migraine? Second, could such alteration affect hippocampus-dependent cognition via a specific neural circuit? To this end, we established a nitroglycerin (NTG)-induced chronic migraine mouse model and employed a multimodal approach combining behavioral tests, immunofluorescence, chemogenetics, monosynaptic retrograde tracing, and projection-specific optogenetics. We identified a novel, direct GABAergic neural pathway from the SCN to the DG and demonstrated its functional involvement in migraine-related cognitive deficits. Our work not only provides a novel circuit-level perspective for understanding the neural mechanisms of migraine comorbidities but also lays a potential theoretical foundation for developing targeted therapeutic strategies against cognitive symptoms in migraine patients.

Methods

Animals and ethics

The study used 6–8-week-old adult male C57BL/6 mice, sourced from Hunan Slack Jingda Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. The animals were kept in a controlled environment with a 12-hour light/dark cycle (lights on at 06:00, defined as Zeitgeber time 0, ZT0), a temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and 50 ± 2% humidity, with unrestricted access to food and water. The Animal Care and Use Committee of Renmin Hospital, Wuhan University, approved all experimental protocols (IACUC Approval No 20241003 A), ensuring adherence to ethical guidelines aimed at minimizing animal suffering and using the fewest animals necessary. Following a one-week acclimatization period, mice were randomly assigned to the specified experimental groups. For specific monosynaptic retrograde tracing experiments, GAD-Cre mice on a C57BL/6 background were employed.

Chronic migraine mice model

To induce a chronic migraine state, mice were administered nitroglycerin (NTG) via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection [31]. A commercially available NTG stock solution (5.0 mg/mL, Ke Xing, Beijing) was diluted with 0.9% saline to achieve a 1 mg/mL working concentration just before use. The treatment protocol included five intraperitoneal (i.p.) doses. NTG (10 mg/kg) was administered every other day for nine days. Control animals underwent the same injection schedule with an equivalent volume of saline vehicle.

Stereotaxic viral injection

Stereotaxic surgeries were conducted on mice under anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal administration of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and secured in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Supplementary Table 1 provides a list of the viruses utilized in this study. Coordinates relative to bregma were determined using a standard mouse brain atlas. Viral suspensions were injected using a microsyringe (Nanofil, World Precision Instruments) at a controlled rate of 50 nL min⁻¹. Following infusion, the injection needle remained in place for 15–20 min to aid viral diffusion before being gradually withdrawn.

SCN manipulation

For chemogenetic manipulation of SCN neuronal activity, AAV2/9-hSyn-hM3Dq-mCherry or AAV2/9-hSyn-hM4Di-mCherry (200 nL per side) was injected into the SCN (AP: -0.46 mm, ML: ±0.10 mm, DV: -5.95 mm).

Anterograde tracing

To map SCN efferent projections, AAV2/9-hSyn-mCherry (200 nL) was injected into the SCN.

Monosynaptic retrograde tracing

To pinpoint direct presynaptic inputs to DG GABAergic neurons, a mixture (1:2 ratio, total 100 nL) of helper virus (rAAV-Ef1α-DIO-TVA-P2A-NLS-dTomato-WPRE-pA and rAAV-hsyn-DIO-RVG-WPRE-pA) was injected into the DG (AP: -2.82 mm, ML: ±1.00 mm, DV: -2.20 mm) of GAD-Cre mice. After three weeks, 100 nL of rabies virus (RV-ENVA-ΔG-EGFP) was injected at the same coordinates to initiate trans-synaptic spread.

Optogenetic circuit targeting

For specific activation of GABAergic projections from the SCN to the DG, a dual-virus strategy was employed. The Cre-recombinase virus rAAV-mDlx-CRE (80 nL) was injected into the SCN (AP: -0.46 mm, ML: ±0.10 mm, DV: -5.95 mm) to restrict expression to GABAergic neurons. In the same surgical session, the Cre-dependent opsin virus rAAV-EF1α-DIO-hChR2-mCherry (150 nL) was injected into the ipsilateral DG (AP: -1.82 mm, ML: ±1.00 mm, DV: -2.20 mm). This approach ensures that only the terminals of SCN GABAergic neurons in the DG express the light-sensitive channelrhodopsin(ChR2).

Optical fiber implantation

For optogenetic terminal stimulation in the DG, chronic implantable optical fibers (numerical aperture: 0.37, fiber core: 0.2 mm, Inper, China) were implanted above the DG, using the same coordinates as the virus injection. This allowed for pathway-specific manipulation of SCN-derived GABAergic terminals. The fibers were secured with dental cement.

Chemogenetic and optogenetic manipulation

Chemogenetics

To activate (hM3Dq) or inhibit (hM4Di) SCN neurons, Clozapine-N-oxide (CNO, from MCE, China) or vehicle was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 3 mg/kg, 30 min before behavioral testing.

Optogenetics

For terminal stimulation of the SCN-DG pathway, mice were connected to a 473 nm blue laser. Light pulses (20 Hz, 10 ms pulse width, 10 mW at fiber tip) were delivered in a randomized, alternating pattern of “Light ON” and “Light OFF” epochs during behavioral tests. Control groups received the same light stimulation but were injected with control virus.

Behavioral test

All behavioral tests (except wheel-running activity) were conducted within a fixed Zeitgeber time window (ZT4–ZT6) to minimize circadian variability across experimental groups. For each test, animals were tested at comparable circadian phases across days, and the order of testing was randomized across groups.

Mechanical threshold test

Periorbital mechanical thresholds were evaluated using Von Frey filaments according to an established protocol [32]. Following individual placement into test cassettes, mice were allowed to habituate for 30 min. Filaments were applied to the periorbital region with gentle pressure until bending, maintained for 1–2 s. A positive response was defined as any of the following behaviors: sudden head withdrawal, escape attempt, scratching, or shaking. The mechanical threshold was determined using an up-down paradigm: following a positive response, a thinner filament was applied, whereas a negative response led to the use of a thicker filament. The threshold was recorded as the lowest force (in g) needed to evoke a positive response. Each mouse underwent three repeated measurements, with 2 min intervals between consecutive stimuli, and the final threshold was calculated as the mean of these replicates.

Hot-plate test

To evaluate thermal nociception, a hot plate test was used [33]. Following a 30-min acclimation to the test room, mice were tested on the preheated plate (54 ± 0.5 °C). The latency to nociceptive responses such as hind paw lick, flick, vocalization, or jump was recorded. Mice were removed immediately upon displaying a response or after a 30-second cut-off to prevent tissue injury.

Novel object recognition (NOR) assessment

Recognition memory was assessed using the NOR [34] in a square open field (30 × 30 × 30 cm). After a 30-min acclimation to the empty arena, mice underwent a 5-minute training session with two same objects, followed by a 5-min test session in which one object was replaced with a novel one. Object exploration was characterized by positioning the nose within 2 cm of the object. The discrimination index, used to quantify novel object preference, was determined by the formula: (Time_novel–Time_familiar) / (Time_novel + Time_familiar).

Y maze test

Spatial working memory was assessed using a Y-maze [35] with three identical arms (6 × 30 × 15 cm) arranged at 120° angles. Individually, mice were positioned in one arm and permitted to explore the maze freely for 5 min. Arm entries were documented, and a spontaneous alternation was characterized by consecutive entries into three distinct arms. The alternation rate, indicating working memory efficiency, was computed as (number of alternations / (total arm entries − 2) × 100%.

Open Field Test (OFT)

An open field test was used to evaluate spontaneous locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior [36, 37]. Each mouse was placed at the center of a 30 cm cubic white arena and allowed to explore freely for 5 min. Their behavior was recorded and analyzed using an automated video tracking system. The total distance traveled and the time spent in the central zone were quantified to evaluate locomotor activity and anxiety levels, respectively.

Wheel-running activity

Circadian rhythms were assessed by monitoring wheel-running activity [38]. Mice were individually housed in cages with running wheels following a 7-day adaptation to a 12-hour light-dark (LD)cycle. Following treatment, they were changed to constant darkness (DD) for 3–5 days to monitor free-running rhythms. Activity was recorded in 1-min bins for subsequent analysis.

Immunofluorescence

Mice underwent transcardial perfusion using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at a fixed Zeitgeber time (ZT6). Brains underwent post-fixation, cryoprotection, and were coronally sectioned at 10–20 μm using a cryostat. For c-Fos induction, mice were subjected to specific behavioral or stimulus paradigms 90 min prior to perfusion. Sections were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Mouse anti-c-Fos (1:400, Servicebio), Rabbit anti-GAD67 (1:100, Biomart), Rabbit anti-CaMKII (1:100, Affinity), Rabbit anti-parvalbumin (PV, 1:500, Huabio), Rabbit anti-somatostatin (SST, 1:50, Upingbio), and rabbit anti-cholecystokinin (CCK, 1:800, Servicebio) were used as primary. The following day, sections were incubated with appropriate fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-goat anti-rabbit, FITC-goat anti-mouse, Flower Cyanine 3 (Cy3)-goat anti-rabbit and Cy3-goat anti-mouse (1:200, all from Servicebio), followed by incubation for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Following this, a 10-minute DAPI staining was performed to label cell nuclei. Finally, a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) was used for image acquisition. For cell counting, at least 4 coronal sections per mouse from the region of interest were analyzed. From these sections, three non-overlapping fields were randomly selected for quantification. c-Fos + and double-labeled cells were manually counted by an experimenter blinded to the group allocation using the ImageJ “Cell Counter” plugin. Cell density (cells/mm²) was calculated based on the area of the region of interest. The mean value of the three fields was calculated to obtain one value per mouse. The statistical unit (n) represents the number of mice.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 and presented as mean ± SEM. Following confirmation of data normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variance using Bartlett’s test, appropriate parametric tests were applied. Intergroup comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons, one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc test for multiple-group comparisons, and two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction when multiple factors were involved. Statistical significance was determined at three levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Behavioral assays assessed distinct functional domains (nociception, recognition memory, spatial working memory, locomotion, anxiety-like behavior, and circadian activity) and were therefore analyzed as independent, hypothesis-driven endpoints rather than as repeated measures of a single outcome. Accordingly, no global correction across all behavioral tests was applied. Where multiple comparisons were performed within a single behavioral assay, appropriate post hoc corrections were used as indicated.

Sample size determination was based on prior studies employing similar behavioral, immunohistochemical, and circuit-level approaches, as well as established conventions in the field. No formal a priori power calculation was performed; however, the chosen sample sizes are comparable to those commonly used in related studies and were sufficient to detect statistically significant effects in the present experiments.

Results

Circadian rhythm disruption and cognitive impairment were induced in NTG-induced migraine mice

To investigate the interaction between migraine, circadian rhythms, and cognitive function, we established a chronic migraine model by intermittent intraperitoneal injection of NTG (Fig. 1A) [31]. Compared to saline-treated controls, NTG-treated mice developed significant and sustained periorbital mechanical allodynia (Fig. 1B) and thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 1C), confirming the successful induction of migraine-related pain hypersensitivity. Under light-dark (LD) cycles, long-term wheel-running monitoring revealed low daily activity in NTG group. In constant darkness (DD), NTG mice exhibited a reduced amplitude and impaired consolidation of their free-running rhythms (Fig. 1D-F), collectively indicating a dysfunction of the central circadian pacemaker. In cognitive behavioral tests, NTG mice showed a significant reduction in novel object preference (Fig. 1G, H), indicating impaired recognition memory [34]. In the Y-maze, their spontaneous alternation rate was also markedly decreased (Fig. 1I, J), suggesting impairments in spatial working memory [35]. To rule out confounding effects of locomotor activity or anxiety, we performed open field tests. While NTG mice traveled a greater total distance (Supplementary Fig. S1A), potentially reflecting a hyperactive state, the time spent in the anxiety-provoking center zone did not differ between groups (Supplementary Fig. S1B). This indicates that the observed cognitive deficits are not attributable to changes in general locomotion or anxiety-like behavior. Collectively, these data demonstrate that the NTG-induced migraine model recapitulates not only the core pain phenotype but also exhibits concurrent circadian rhythm disruption and cognitive impairment.

Decreased proportion of c-Fos–positive GABAergic neurons was observed in the SCN of NTG-induced migraine mice

Given that the SCN serves as the master circadian pacemaker [12], we hypothesized that the circadian disruption observed in our migraine model (Fig. 1D-F) might originate from functional abnormalities within the SCN itself. To directly assess the activation state of the SCN, we performed immunofluorescence analysis using c-Fos expression as an immediate-early gene marker [39]. The findings indicated that compared with saline group, NTG-treated mice exhibited a notable increase in c-Fos-positive cell density within the SCN (Fig. 2A, C), suggesting that the migraine state may be accompanied by an overall elevation in SCN network excitability. To further investigate the cellular basis of this altered activation pattern, we performed double-labeling immunofluorescence experiments using the glutamatergic neuronal marker calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) and the GABAergic neuronal marker glutamate decarboxylase 67 (GAD67) to classify c-Fos + cells. The results demonstrated that despite the general increase in c-Fos expression, the density of c-Fos and GAD67 co-positive cells was significantly decreased (Fig. 2A, D). In contrast, the density of c-Fos and CaMKII co-positive cells showed a non-significant increasing trend (Fig. 2B, E). Importantly, quantitative analysis further revealed that the overall density of GAD67-labeled cells in the SCN was significantly reduced in NTG-treated mice compared with controls (Fig. 2F). Together, these data indicate that NTG treatment is associated with an increase in overall c-Fos expression within the SCN, accompanied by a redistribution in the recruitment of neuronal subpopulations. Specifically, the proportion of c-Fos+ GABAergic neurons is reduced relative to the overall increase in SCN activity, suggesting a redistribution in activity-dependent recruitment of neuronal subpopulations within the SCN network, rather than uniform hyperactivation of all SCN neurons.

Cognitive deficits were exacerbated by chemogenetic inhibition of SCN neurons in NTG-induced migraine mice

We concurrently observed reduced activity of GABAergic neurons in the SCN (Fig. 2) and cognitive behavioral deficits (Fig. 1) in NTG mice. To test whether these neurons contributed causally to the cognitive impairment, we expressed the inhibitory chemogenetic receptor hM4Di in the SCN of NTG mice with established migraine pathology and administered CNO to suppress neuronal activity (Fig. 3A). This manipulation successfully reduced c-Fos levels in the SCN (Fig. 3B). Given that GABAergic neurons dominate the SCN, we verified the cellular specificity of our intervention. Immunofluorescence co-staining confirmed that hM4Di-mCherry was predominantly expressed in GAD67-positive GABAergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 2), indicating that the chemogenetic manipulation primarily targeted and suppressed the GABAergic neuronal population within the SCN. We then assessed the effect of SCN inhibition on nociceptive sensitivity in NTG mice. Neither the mechanical pain threshold (Fig. 3C) nor the thermal pain latency (Fig. 3D) differed from controls, indicating that inhibiting SCN did not exacerbate the pre-existing pain hypersensitivity in the NTG model. This suggests that SCN activity state does not directly regulate the nociceptive symptoms of migraine. However, in NOR test, the discrimination index of NTG group was significantly reduced (Fig. 3E, F), showing that SCN inhibition further impaired their recognition memory. Similarly, in the Y-maze test assessing spatial working memory, SCN-inhibited mice exhibited a significant decrease in spontaneous alternation rate (Fig. 3G, H). These behavioral results demonstrate that directly inhibiting neuronal activity in the SCN, primarily affecting GABAergic neurons, further exacerbates cognitive impairment in migraine mice.

Cognitive and circadian symptoms were alleviated by chemogenetic activation of SCN neurons in NTG-induced migraine mice

Previous experiments indicated that inhibiting SCN neuronal activity exacerbates cognitive impairment, suggesting that SCN functional state is crucial for maintaining cognitive performance. To further investigate the impact of upregulating SCN activity on cognition in NTG mice, we used the excitatory chemogenetic tool hM3Dq to specifically activate SCN neurons (Fig. 4A). Immunofluorescence confirmed that CNO administration significantly enhanced c-Fos signal in the hM3Dq-expressing SCN region (Fig. 4B). To confirm that this activation targeted the same principal neuronal population implicated in the inhibition experiments, we verified its cellular specificity. Immunofluorescence co-staining demonstrated that hM3Dq-mCherry was predominantly expressed in GAD67-positive GABAergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast to the inhibition experiment, activation of SCN completely reversed the NTG-induced cognitive deficits. Mice with SCN hM3Dq expression showed a significant recovery in novel object preference in the NOR test (Fig. 4G, H), and their spontaneous alternation rate in the Y-maze returned to normal levels (Fig. 4I, J). Furthermore, SCN neuronal activation significantly improved the pattern of wheel-running activity, making the originally fragmented rhythm more consolidated and regular across the circadian cycle (Fig. 4E, F). Notably, SCN neuronal activation had no effect on NTG-induced thermal hyperalgesia (Fig. 4C, D) or mechanical allodynia, ruling out the possibility that cognitive improvement was achieved indirectly through pain relief. Therefore, the beneficial effects of SCN activation are attributable to the targeted enhancement of its GABAergic neuronal activity. Together with the inhibition data, these results establish a bidirectional, causal control of cognitive and circadian function by the activity state of SCN GABAergic neurons in migraine.

Selective activation of GABAergic neurons was detected in the dentate gyrus of NTG-induced migraine mice

Having established that the activity of SCN GABAergic neurons bidirectionally controls cognition in migraine, we sought to identify the downstream memory circuits through which this regulation occurs. We focused on the hippocampus, given its central role in memory formation and its known susceptibility to circadian regulation [25, 30]. To investigate whether migraine alters hippocampal network activity, we first assessed the overall neuronal activity levels in the main hippocampal subregions (DG, CA1, CA2, CA3) using immunofluorescence. The study found that NTG mice demonstrated a marked increase in c-Fos expression in the granule cell layer and hilus of the dentate gyrus compared to the control group. The density of c-Fos-positive cells in the hippocampal regions CA1, CA2, and CA3 remained unchanged (Fig. 5A, D). Since the DG is crucial for pattern separation and its function depends on a precise excitatory-inhibitory balance [26], we next dissected the cellular basis of this hyperactivity. Co-staining analysis within the DG showed a substantial increase in the density of c-Fos-positive cells co-localized with the GABAergic neuronal marker GAD67 [40] in the NTG group (Fig. 5B, D). Conversely, the density of c-Fos-positive cells co-localized with the excitatory neuronal marker CaMKII [41] was significantly reduced (Fig. 5C, D). These results suggest that the selective activation of the DG in NTG-induce migraine mice is characterized by a distinct shift in the activation pattern of its principal neuronal populations: a marked increase in activity-dependent recruitment of local inhibitory (GABAergic) interneurons accompanies reduced c-Fos labeling in excitatory granule cells.

A direct GABAergic projection from the SCN to the DG was identified

Based on the above findings, SCN activity bidirectionally regulates cognition, and the hippocampal DG is selectively activated. To further investigate whether the SCN regulates DG activity through direct neural projections, we conducted neural circuit tracing. First, we performed anterograde tracing by injecting AAV-hSyn-mCherry into the SCN (Fig. 6A). Robust mCherry expression was observed at the injection site (Fig. 6B). To ensure anatomical specificity, we delineated SCN boundaries based on atlas-defined morphology and quantified viral spread; mCherry signal was overwhelmingly confined to the SCN with minimal expression detected in adjacent regions (e.g., paraventricular nucleus (PVN)/subparaventricular zone (SPZ)) (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Crucially, we found abundant mCherry+ axon terminals specifically in the DG, but not in other hippocampal subfields (Fig. 6C). Within the DG, SCN-derived axons were distributed in close proximity to GAD67 + inhibitory elements (Fig. 6D), suggesting preferential association with DG inhibitory circuits. To further examine the cellular context of these terminals within the DG, we performed additional immunofluorescence staining for interneuron subtype markers. SCN-derived mCherry-positive terminals were frequently observed in close apposition to somatostatin(SST) and cholecystokinin(CCK) positive interneurons in the DG, whereas such appositions with parvalbumin(PV) positive cells were infrequently observed (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, these anatomical observations do not exclude additional postsynaptic targets within the DG, including granule cells or mossy cells, which will require future cell-type–specific synaptic mapping. To independently verify the SCN as a source of hippocampal input using a non-viral approach, we performed classical retrograde tracing by injecting CTB555 into the DG. Tracer deposition was largely confined to the DG with minimal spread to adjacent hippocampal subfields (Supplementary Fig. 4E), and retrogradely labeled neurons were consistently observed within the bilateral SCN (Supplementary Fig. 4F), further supporting a direct SCN-DG projection. To further test whether SCN GABAergic neurons provide direct input to DG GABAergic neurons, we performed monosynaptic retrograde tracing from DG GABAergic neurons in GAD-Cre mice (Fig. 6E). Starter cells in the DG were successfully labeled with this approach, characterized by their co-expression of TVA-dTomato and RV-EGFP (Fig. 6F). Importantly, retrogradely labeled neurons (RV-EGFP+) were identified in the SCN (Fig. 6G). Immunostaining confirmed that the majority of these presynaptic SCN neurons were GAD67 positive (Fig. 6G). In summary, the convergence of anterograde and monosynaptic retrograde tracing results provides anatomical evidence for the existence of a direct projection from the SCN to the DG, with support for engagement of DG inhibitory circuits.

Optogenetic activation of SCN-derived GABAergic terminals in the DG rescued cognitive deficits in NTG-induced migraine mice

Having anatomically identified a direct GABAergic pathway from the SCN to the DG, we next sought to determine whether this newly discovered circuit functionally mediates the cognitive symptoms of migraine. We employed a projection-specific, cell type-restricted optogenetic strategy (Fig. 7A). In NTG mice, we injected a Cre-recombinase virus (rAAV-mDlx-CRE) into the SCN to selectively target GABAergic neurons and, in the same surgery, injected a Cre-dependent channelrhodopsin (ChR2) virus into the DG along with an implanted optical fiber. This design ensured that only the axon terminals of SCNGABA neurons within the DG expressed the light-sensitive protein (Fig. 7B), allowing for millisecond-precision, selective activation of this specific pathway via blue light delivery to the DG, without affecting other brain regions. During the NOR test, blue-light activation of the SCN-DG pathway in ChR2-expressing NTG mice significantly increased their exploration time for the novel object, restoring their discrimination index to a level comparable to healthy controls (Fig. 7C, D). Similarly, in the Y-maze test, optogenetic activation elevated the spontaneous alternation rate to the normal range (Fig. 7E, F). To rule out non-specific behavioral confounds, we concurrently analyzed open field behavior. Data showed that optogenetic activation did not alter the total distance traveled (Fig. 7G), indicating the absence of general locomotor effects. Critically, it also did not change the time spent in the center zone (Fig. 7H), indicating no change in anxiety-like behavior. Together, these control experiments support the conclusion that the cognitive improvement induced by optogenetic stimulation was associated with manipulation of the SCN→DG pathway rather than non-specific changes in locomotion or anxiety.

Discussion

This study identifies a direct GABAergic pathway from the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) to the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG) and demonstrates its functional involvement in cognitive impairment in an NTG-induced chronic migraine mouse model. We propose that migraine-associated cognitive deficits may arise from altered recruitment of SCN neuronal populations, potentially affecting DG circuit function through this newly identified pathway (Fig. 2). This alteration is accompanied by altered recruitment of both inhibitory interneurons and excitatory neurons within the DG (Fig. 5), consistent with disrupted microcircuit coordination underlying hippocampal memory processing. Critically, both chemogenetic activation of SCN GABAergic neurons and pathway-specific optogenetic activation of this circuit rescued cognitive deficits without affecting nociception (Figs. 4 and 7), highlighting the functional specificity of this pathway and suggesting its potential relevance as a neuromodulatory target.

This finding fills a critical anatomical gap between the SCN and the hippocampal DG. Given that classical circadian output pathways have been described primarily as relays from the SCN through hypothalamic regions such as the subparaventricular zone (SPZ) and related downstream nuclei, the direct SCN-DG projection identified here appears anatomically noncanonical [14, 42]. One possible explanation for its limited prior recognition is that this pathway may be relatively sparse, functionally specialized, or preferentially engaged under specific physiological or pathological conditions, and therefore may have been overlooked in earlier anatomical studies focused on major circadian output routes [43]. In this context, our convergent evidence from anterograde tracing, CTB retrograde labeling, and monosynaptic rabies tracing supports the existence of a direct SCN→DG connection, while suggesting that it may represent a previously underappreciated specialized circadian output pathway rather than a dominant bulk projection.

The coexistence of increased overall neuronal c-Fos expression and reduced c-Fos recruitment among GABAergic neurons in the SCN of migraine mice suggests fundamental changes in network-level coordination rather than uniform changes in neuronal activity. The normal function of the SCN relies on the precise regulation of GABAergic signaling [38, 44], which operates on multiple timescales through both rapid ‘phase’ signals that regulate neuronal firing and sustained ‘tonic’ signals that influence molecular clock phase via calcium-dependent pathways [44, 45]. These signals shape the spatiotemporal organization of neural networks according to neuronal characteristics such as the GABA-dependent reversal potential [20, 46, 47]. Within this framework, our observation of reduced GAD67 labeling in the SCN should be interpreted with caution: rather than indicating neuronal loss or decreased inhibitory transmission, it may reflect dynamic regulation of GAD67 expression associated with altered neuronal activity states, reduced immunoreactivity, or circadian-phase-dependent modulation of GABAergic markers. Recent studies further indicate that GABA released by specific neuronal subpopulations (such as AVP neurons) primarily functions as a “time valve” to limit behavioral output windows, rather than directly driving core molecular clock synchronization [46, 48, 49]. The altered recruitment of neuronal subpopulations observed in our study may disrupt this precise gating mechanism, suggesting impaired integration across multiple levels of circadian signaling: the molecular basis for generating robust rhythms [46], network coordination dynamics [45], and the conversion of intrinsic rhythms into appropriate behavioral timing [49] may all be impaired. A likely consequence is that the SCN loses its ability to output clear, well-timed regulatory signals to downstream brain regions such as the hippocampus [20].

How does this corrupted pacemaker signal specifically impair hippocampal memory? As the hippocampal information gateway, DG performs ‘pattern separation’ by transforming similar inputs into non-overlapping neural representations [50], which relies on the precise balance of local excitation and inhibition [51]. We observed increased c-Fos recruitment of GABAergic interneurons in the DG (Fig. 5), accompanied by reduced c-Fos labeling in CaMKII-positive excitatory neurons. Importantly, c-Fos labeling in DG interneurons should be interpreted as a marker of activity-dependent recruitment rather than a simple readout of increased inhibitory output. In this context, the observed increase may reflect burst firing, disinhibition, or altered temporal engagement of inhibitory circuits, rather than uniform increases in interneuron firing. Such mistimed or desynchronized inhibitory recruitment could interfere with sparse coding and pattern separation in the DG [26], even without implying a simple increase in net inhibitory tone. The newly discovered direct SCN→DG GABAergic projection provides a potential upstream pathway linking circadian dysregulation to DG circuit dysfunction (Fig. 6). Although our anterograde and monosynaptic tracing data support preferential anatomical association of SCN-derived GABAergic inputs with DG inhibitory interneuron populations, we do not exclude the possibility that SCN axons may also contact additional postsynaptic targets, including granule cells or mossy cells. Further cell-type–specific synaptic mapping will be required to resolve the full postsynaptic diversity of this projection. We speculate that under physiological conditions, this projection may provide the DG with a circadian inhibitory input to gate memory encoding [27, 52, 53]. In migraine, however, the regulatory influence originating from the SCN may become altered or desynchronized, potentially changing the constraints on DG interneurons and contributing to aberrant inhibitory engagement within local circuits. Such altered SCN-derived input may therefore contribute to dysregulated interneuron recruitment and impaired information processing in the DG. Our study therefore supports a functional role for this novel SCN→DG GABAergic pathway in migraine-associated cognitive deficits and demonstrates that targeted activation of this circuit can specifically reverse cognitive deficits in migraine.

This study has several limitations that point to future directions. First, experiments were conducted only in male mice. Given the higher prevalence, severity, and clinical heterogeneity of migraine in females, it remains unknown whether the SCN-DG GABAergic circuit identified here operates similarly in female animals or exhibits sex-specific differences. Such differences could critically influence the translational relevance of targeting this pathway for migraine-associated cognitive dysfunction. Accordingly, systematic investigation of this circuit in female models represents an essential direction for future studies. Second, while we have outlined the anatomical and functional framework of the circuit, its molecular and synaptic specifics remain to be elucidated—for example, the GABA receptor subtypes involved, whether transmission exhibits intrinsic circadian rhythmicity disrupted by migraine, and the full postsynaptic target diversity of SCN axons within the DG. Third, all experiments were performed at a fixed circadian phase to minimize variability associated with circadian modulation of SCN activity, and we did not systematically examine whether the strength, timing, or rhythmicity of SCN-DG signaling varies across circadian phases or is altered by migraine, which represents an important direction for future investigation. In addition, although cognitive impairment is a common comorbidity of migraine, its clinical manifestations are heterogeneous and often prominently involve executive and attentional domains. The NTG-induced migraine model and the behavioral paradigms employed in the present study were designed to assess hippocampus-dependent memory functions, particularly those relying on dentate gyrus circuitry. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as elucidating a domain-specific memory phenotype rather than encompassing all forms of migraine-associated cognitive dysfunction. Whether circadian disruption and SCN-derived signals also influence executive or attentional circuits remains an important question for future investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings provide evidence for a novel GABAergic pathway from the suprachiasmatic nucleus to the dentate gyrus that contributes to cognitive impairments associated with migraine. We show that migraine induces a shift in neuronal activity patterns within the SCN, marked by altered recruitment of GABAergic neuronal populations and reduced GAD67 labeling, which may contribute to circadian dysfunction and, via a direct projection, to altered inhibitory circuit engagement in the DG and subsequent memory deficits. The demonstration that targeted activation of this circuit can rescue cognitive function highlights its potential relevance as a neuromodulatory target. This study deepens our understanding of the circuit basis of migraine-associated cognitive dysfunction and provides a framework for future investigation of circuit-based interventions for cognitive symptoms associated with migraine.
