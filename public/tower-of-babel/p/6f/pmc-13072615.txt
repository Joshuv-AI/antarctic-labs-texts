Unpopular patients and compassion fatigue among nurses: a mixed-methods study on emotional burden, workforce well-being, and patient safety
Authors: Shani Fisher, Ayala Blau, Yulia Gendler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo examine the interrelationships among nurses’ perceptions of unpopular patients, compassion fatigue, compassion satisfaction, and the risk of adverse events, informing organizational strategy and safety protocols in acute care hospital settings.DesignMixed methods research using quantitative and qualitative approaches.MethodsA validated online questionnaire was completed by 130 nurses. Measures included demographics, perceptions of unpopular patients, and the Professional Quality of Life Scale assessing burnout, secondary traumatic stress, and compassion satisfaction. The study followed STROBE guidelines.ResultsMost participants were female (72.3%) and aged 41-50. Lower compassion satisfaction and higher burnout or secondary traumatic stress were associated with identifying a greater number of unpopular patient types. Younger nurses and men reported more difficulty, particularly with emotional and logistical care demands. Perceived error probability strongly correlated with socio-behavioral unpopular-patient categories, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress. Nurses who frequently encountered communication-intensive or boundary-challenging patients described heightened cognitive load and increased concern for near-miss situations. Qualitative findings reinforced these trends: participants portrayed unpopular patients as sources of emotional overload, communication conflict, and repeated workflow disruptions that reduced patience, strained attention, and elevated perceived safety risks. Error-reporting patterns further highlighted the interplay between emotional strain, patient mix, and clinical performance, demonstrating how socio-behavioral challenges shape nurses’ sense of safety and vulnerability to errors.ConclusionFindings reveal broader system-level dynamics in which nurses’ perceptions of unpopular patients trigger emotional strain, which in turn elevates the perceived risk of medical error. These dynamics shape nurse well-being and key organizational patient-safety indicators. The study emphasizes the strategic importance of creating supportive practice environments and providing targeted training and resources to enable nurses to manage challenging patient interactions effectively. Such interventions are crucial for mitigating emotional fatigue, enhancing workforce resilience, and ultimately improving patient safety outcomes across the system. Prioritizing nurse well-being must therefore be recognized as a foundational element of organizational sustainability. This study provides actionable insights for nurse leaders and healthcare administrators, highlighting a previously overlooked pathway that links staff emotional experiences with patients to their clinical performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04476-4.Keywords: Unpopular patients, Compassion fatigue, Adverse events, System safety, Nurse leadership, Workforce well-being

Introduction

Nursing teams are integral to healthcare delivery, facing significant workloads, continuous pressure, and direct responsibility for patient safety. Despite advances in healthcare, preventable harm remains a major concern globally for all healthcare leaders. Approximately one in twenty hospitalized patients experiences an adverse event, with about 12% of these cases resulting in permanent disability or death [1]. Medication errors, care management failures, and complications from surgical or invasive procedures are leading contributors to such harm. Moreover, nursing error rates have been estimated at 0.00336 errors per hour of nursing work [1–3]. Evidence consistently reveals a gap between what nurses report and what is observed in practice. A notable study in Ethiopia found that over half of the nursing staff (57.7%) admitted to at least one error within a single year, with nearly a third reporting three or more incidents. Common deviations included incorrect timing (38.6%), followed by failures in assessment and evaluation. In contrast, direct observation research identifies errors in roughly 30.2% of all administrations, with a critical subset (3.3%) categorized as potentially serious [4, 5]. Current literature continues to document error rates often exceeding 30% of total administrations. A comprehensive review of 91 observation-based studies estimated median error rates between 8% and up to 25% in hospital and long-term care settings. However, these figures spike dramatically for intravenous (IV) drugs, reaching rates as high as 48–53% [6]. Recent findings show that while 72.1% of nurses acknowledged committing errors, most frequently involving incorrect dosages (46.9%), less than half of these incidents were officially reported [7]. Furthermore, retrospective analysis indicates that while the vast majority (74.3%) of MAEs result in minimal harm, they are systematically driven by knowledge gaps, high workloads, and environmental stressors [8].

Adverse events emerge from complex interactions of human and systemic factors, including workload pressures, staffing shortages, communication failures, fatigue, burnout, cognitive biases, and insufficient training [9–12]. Increasingly, research highlights the critical role of emotional factors, exhaustion, and compassion fatigue as risk factors for nursing errors.

Safety culture and system-level defenses are essential to mitigate such risks. The Swiss cheese model conceptualizes how active failures and latent systemic weaknesses align to produce adverse events [13]. An emerging focus is the role of staff attitudes toward “unpopular” or “unwanted” patients, whose care may be compromised due to negative perceptions and reduced engagement [14–18].

Compassion fatigue (CF) and related concepts- compassion satisfaction (CS), burnout, and secondary traumatic stress (STS) have been widely examined in health caregiving professions. CF is described as the physical and mental exhaustion and emotional withdrawal experienced by those who care for sick or traumatized people over time. In contrast, burnout arises from prolonged workplace stress, and STS occurs when one is affected by another’s trauma. CS, the positive counterpart of CF, can mitigate its effects [19, 20]. In nursing, CF is considered an occupational hazard due to prolonged exposure to suffering, high emotional investment, and constant patient contact [20]. It manifests physically, emotionally, and spiritually, leading to decreased performance, increased errors, absenteeism, and an intention to leave the profession, ultimately affecting patient safety and quality of care [19].

However, there is a lack of comprehensive research examining the intersection of these factors with nurses’ attitudes toward challenging patients and the subsequent likelihood of professional errors. Specifically, we sought to determine the relationships between nurses’ perceptions of challenging patients and their professional quality of life, the extent to which these perceptions correlate with the perceived probability of professional errors, and how nurses describe the qualitative impact of these interactions on the safety environment. By addressing this critical gap by exploring these interrelated domains within a single investigative framework, offering novel insights to inform interventions, strengthen patient safety culture, and enhance nurse well-being.

Background

Patient safety has long been a foundational goal in healthcare systems worldwide. Nursing professionals, who provide direct and continuous care, work in high-stakes environments that demand vigilance and resilience. Nevertheless, preventable adverse events continue to impact patient outcomes significantly. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), at least one in twenty hospitalized patients worldwide suffers from harm that could have been avoided [21]. Among these, medication errors, procedural mistakes, and failures in care coordination are prominent causes, many of which are linked to nursing practice [1].

The complexity of modern healthcare, including increasing technological sophistication and the involvement of multiple providers, heightens the risk of errors. Adverse events generally arise not from isolated mistakes but from an intricate web of active errors and latent systemic failures. The Swiss cheese model offers a valuable framework to understand this phenomenon, illustrating how “holes” in multiple layers of defense-whether individual errors or organizational weaknesses-align to allow harm to occur [13]. Systemic factors such as staffing shortages, inadequate training, poor communication, and excessive workloads create latent conditions that predispose teams to errors. Concurrently, human factors, including fatigue, burnout, and cognitive biases, exacerbate vulnerability [1].

In peer-reviewed literature, CF has received considerable attention as a detrimental consequence of prolonged exposure to patient suffering. First described in nursing literature in 1992, CF entails emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and diminished capacity for empathy, which may impair clinical judgment and interpersonal care [22, 23]. Empirical studies have linked higher levels of CF with an increased probability of errors, while CS appears protective [22–25]. These findings underscore the importance of addressing both the emotional and clinical dimensions of caregiving to enhance patient safety and staff well-being.

Another emerging area of concern involves attitudes toward “unpopular” or “unwanted” patients, who may be stigmatized due to complex social, behavioral, or medical factors, such as homelessness, substance use, or aggressive behavior [14, 15] Negative staff attitudes may reduce engagement and responsiveness, contributing to poorer care quality and increased risk of adverse events. Within the Swiss cheese model, these attitudes can be viewed as active failures that directly compromise patient safety.

Despite extensive research on each of these domains independently, few studies have examined their interrelationships. Understanding how CF and attitudes toward unpopular patients interact may reveal critical leverage points for enhancing nursing practice, safety culture, and patient outcomes. This study was designed to fill this gap by examining the intersection of these aspects among nursing staff in a medical center.

The study

Aim and objectives

The aim of this study was to examine to define and examine the Unpopular Patient Effect by analyzing the mutual influences between nurse-reported experiences, emotional strain (CF and satisfaction), and patient safety outcomes. The researches seek to elucidate the pathway from subjective nurse experience to clinical risk to inform evidence-based strategies for system safety and workforce sustainability. Secondary objectives include investigating the influence of demographic and professional characteristics on these core variables and identifying factors that moderate the relationship between specific unpopular patient categories and perceived error probability.

Technical terminology used to describe the aim

The study’s aim and objectives were articulated using technical terminology relevant to nursing and patient safety research. Key terms included unpopular patients, compassion fatigue (CF), and patient safety, as well as the validated measurement tool, the Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL-5)- a widely recognized and accepted instrument used to assess the positive and negative impacts of working in caregiving professions, providing a comprehensive view. The ProQOL-5 evaluates three core dimensions: CS, which captures the gratification and fulfillment derived from helping others and positive work relationships; Burnout, which measures emotional exhaustion and cynicism; and Secondary Traumatic Stress (STS), which assesses indirect exposure to stress and trauma resulting from patients’ experiences [26]. The reliability of the scale is robust, exhibiting good to excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) for all three subscales across various studies [27]. The objectives also incorporated methodological terms such as the moderating role of error reporting, perceived error probability, and demographic predictors.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted by a mixed methods approach, using a cross-sectional, quantitative design to examine registered nurses’ attitudes toward unpopular patients, medical errors, and CF, using a structured questionnaire. In addition, two open questions were included in the survey, which employed a qualitative approach. The researches adhered to the STROBE guidelines for cross-sectional observational research [28]. The completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary Appendix 1.

Instrument with validity and reliability

The study questionnaire, developed by the researcher, comprised four sections:

Section A: Demographic information (8 Items) - including age, gender, professional seniority, current role, and employment percentage.

Section B: Attitudes toward unpopular patients (18 Items) - a novel measure whose items were originally generated and subjected to content validation by a multidisciplinary team of subject matter experts. Responses were rated on a 3-point Likert scale (1 = unable to care for this patient and would request reassignment; 2 = reluctant to care but would manage; 3 = no aversion to caring for this patient). The index is calculated by comparing the number of responses 1 and 2 to those who respond 3. Patients perceived as challenging by staff were classified into the following categories: Medical factors (ventilated, unpleasant appearance, isolation, unknown diagnosis, palliative, dependent in nursing care, demented/confused, chronic, in pain); Emotional Factors (violent, suicidal, psychiatric); Socio-behavioral factors (addicted, former employee, communication difficulties, perceived as not truly suffering, opinionated); Logistical factors (hospitalized > 3 weeks, outlying patients, i.e., patients belonging to one ward but admitted in another).

Section C: This section included two open-ended questions: ‘Do you think there is a group or type of patients who are more likely to experience errors during their care?’ and ‘In your opinion, are there other circumstances under which a patient could be classified as unwanted?’

Section D: CF - measured using the validated 30-item Professional Quality of Life Scale (ProQOL-5), which includes three independent factors: burnout, secondary traumatic stress (STS), and CS. Each dimension was calculated separately, and internal consistency reliability was assessed for each subscale. Responses were rated on a 6-point Likert scale (0 = never to 5 = very often), with higher scores indicating greater levels of compassion fatigue. In the present study, Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.7 for burnout, 0.804 for STS, and 0.934 for CS. The internal consistency of all ProQOL-5 measures in this paper was 0.934. The ProQOL-5 has shown good internal consistency in various professional groups, with Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from α = 0.75 to 0.89 for the three subscales of CS, Burnout, and STS [27, 29]. The Hebrew validation study among healthcare providers reported an overall α = 0.72 [30], while another paper found subscale values of 0.87 for CS, 0.82 for STS, and 0.69 for burnout [31]. Similarly, additional research supporting the Hebrew translation demonstrated strong internal consistency, with α = 0.89 for CS, 0.75 for burnout, and 0.83 for STS [32]. The English translated version of the tool is provided in the supplements.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at a regional referral hospital in northern Israel, which serves a population of approximately half a million residents. The Medical Center is part of Clalit Health Services (CHS), the largest healthcare organization in Israel, providing services for approximately 4.4 million enrollees. Participants included registered nurses from all hospital units, departments, institutes, and outpatient clinics. The study cohort comprised a diverse range of nursing roles, spanning from frontline staff to executive leadership, including shift supervisors, team leaders, and chief or deputy nurses. Crucially, all participants, regardless of administrative rank, maintained active clinical involvement and direct patient care responsibilities. The survey was conducted between February and April 2025. Inclusion criteria required active employment as a registered nurse at the medical center and completion of the informed consent process. Exclusion criteria were explicit refusal to participate, as indicated in the screening question, and partial completion of the questionnaire.

Sample size

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 to determine the minimum sample size required to test the study hypotheses (exact two-tailed test, 80% power, α = 0.05, and medium effect size). The results indicated that for Spearman correlations, the required sample size was N = 77; for Mann-Whitney tests, the required sample size was N = 67; and for the regressions, the required sample size was N = 130.

Recruitment and data collection

Data collection was conducted via sector-specific WhatsApp groups from January to March 2025. A secure online questionnaire (Google Forms) was distributed, accompanied by an information sheet explaining the study objectives, the voluntary nature of participation, the right to withdraw at any stage, and assurance of anonymity.

At the beginning of the questionnaire, participants were required to indicate consent through a screening item. Those who declined were excluded from the survey. To ensure single participation per respondent, access was restricted to Google account login; however, no identifying information was collected. Responses were stored in the researcher’s secure Google account and assigned a unique numerical code during data analysis. The final dataset and statistical outputs were stored on a password-protected computer.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 29.0.1.0) and R (version 4.5.0). Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables. Since some of the research variables (e.g., CS and the unpopular patient categories) were not normally distributed, all statistical analyses in this paper are non-parametric, meaning they do not assume a normal distribution. Spearman correlations with 95% confidence intervals were used to examine associations between ProQOL-5 independent factors (CS, Burnout, STS), unpopular patient categories, demographic variables, and error probability. Mann-Whitney U tests with effect sizes (r) compared gender and other demographic groups.

A moderation analysis using R software tested whether error reporting moderated the relationship between unpopular patient categories and error probability. A multiple regression analysis predicted the number of unpopular patient categories from demographic variables. Results were reported with coefficients, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance was set at p < .05 (two-tailed) [33]. By itself, a p-value does not provide a good measure of evidence regarding a model or hypothesis [34]. Qualitative data were derived from two open-ended questions. Interviews were conducted, recorded, and subsequently translated into English by the primary author (SF). The data were analyzed using thematic analysis, strictly adhering to the six-phase framework established by Braun and Clarke [35]. This process involved systematic manual coding to discern emerging themes and explore the conceptual relationships between them, ensuring a nuanced interpretation of the participants’ experiences.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Helsinki Committee of the hospital (Approval No. 0030-24EMC) and by the Nursing Directorate of Clalit Health Services. Completion of the questionnaire indicated that informed consent was obtained.

Results

The characteristics of participants

A total of 130 registered nurses participated in this research. Most of the participants were female (72.3%), with the most frequent age category of 41–50 years (36.9%). Participants were mainly from the Internal Medicine Division (33.8%), Obstetrics and Gynecology (19.2%), and Surgical Division (10.8%). Professional seniority varied, with 25.4% having 11–20 years and 26.2% having 21–30 years of experience. Roles included Chief Nurses/Deputies (41.5%), Shift Supervisors/Team Leaders (32.3%), and staff nurses (26.2%). Most worked full-time (73.8%) and rotating shifts (73.1%). Educationally, 68.5% held a Bachelor’s degree, 31.5% a Master’s degree, and 66.2% had completed a post-basic specialist course. Open-ended questions

Participants were asked two questions: ‘Do you think there is a group or type of patients who are more likely to experience errors during their care?’ and ‘In your opinion, are there other circumstances under which a patient could be classified as unwanted?’. Three themes emerged from the findings.

First theme - patient complexity and vulnerability

This theme reflects situations where patients’ medical, cognitive, or functional conditions create greater challenges and increase the risk of errors. Nurses described these patients as requiring special attention and adaptability.

Medical complexity- patients with multiple chronic conditions or acute needs that demand careful coordination. For example, nurses noted: “Patients with many background illnesses and multiple medications” and “Critical patients with unstable conditions requiring constant monitoring.” Such complexity increases the need for vigilance to avoid mistakes.

Discussion

This mixed-methods study, we examined the interplay between nurses’ perceptions of unpopular patients, compassion fatigue, and safety culture, and how these dynamics impact patient safety. The mixed-methods approach strengthened the research by combining robust quantitative analysis with qualitative observations, offering both statistical rigor and a deeper understanding of the relational and organizational complexities influencing clinical care.

Quantitative findings showed that low CS alongside elevated burnout and STS were strongly associated with encountering more unpopular patient categories, particularly those involving socio-behavioral and logistical complexity. These results align with prior research showing that challenging patient encounters intensify emotional exhaustion and compromise safety outcomes [36–38]. Notably, patient safety competency has been shown to mediate the relationship between burnout and error probability [39], underscoring the vital link between emotional well-being and safe practice.

Demographic analyses revealed that younger and male nurses reported more frequent encounters with unpopular patients, likely reflecting differences in coping strategies, professional maturity, and role expectations. Experienced nurses demonstrated greater resilience in managing emotionally demanding encounters, consistent with literature showing that clinical experience mitigates compassion fatigue [40, 41].

Moderation analysis revealed that error reporting practices significantly shaped the relationship between unpopular patient encounters and perceived error probability. In units with consistent reporting, the association was stronger, suggesting that a transparent safety culture exposes the impact of relational stressors more clearly, while under-reporting conceals these risks. This highlights the importance of cultivating an open safety culture to reveal and address vulnerabilities in care [42].

The qualitative strand deepened these findings by identifying four key domains of relational stress: high medical complexity, behavioral challenges, lack of social support, and cultural or attitudinal barriers. These factors not only increase vulnerability to errors but also strain nurse-patient relationships. Nurses described how these dynamics undermine communication and teamwork-core pillars of patient safety. This echoes the Joint Commission’s recognition of communication failures, often rooted in relational conflicts, as a leading cause of sentinel events [43] Nurses reported that such conflicts, especially with unpopular patients, cause more stress than conflicts with colleagues and can inadvertently lead to patient harm [44, 45].

Gender-related differences further highlight how cultural and professional expectations shape perceptions. Male nurses reported greater strain and a reliance on problem-focused coping strategies, which may be less effective in complex patient encounters. Their minority status and cultural norms discouraging help-seeking amplify vulnerability to stress and error [23, 36, 46–48].

Taken together, these mixed-methods findings offer a compelling understanding: unpopular patients are not only perceived as more vulnerable to errors but also as significant relational stressors that challenge safety culture. By integrating quantitative evidence with qualitative depth, this study reveals that relational stress in nurse-patient interactions erodes communication, undermines teamwork, reduces performance, and increases the probability of errors. These findings underscore the urgent need to address compassion fatigue, strengthen safety culture, and provide targeted support for nurses to safeguard both patient safety and nurse well-being.

Strengths and limitations

A primary strength of this research is the demographic heterogeneity and clinical breadth of the participants. By recruiting from diverse hospital departments and professional backgrounds, the study achieved high sample specificity, ensuring themes were corroborated across varied organizational contexts rather than a single clinical niche. This approach provides a multifaceted ‘360-degree’ view of the phenomenon. However, as a single-center, cross-sectional study, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as their generalizability may be limited, and causal relationships cannot be assumed. Additionally, reliance on self-reported data may have introduced some response bias, and the sensitive nature of the topics could have influenced participants’ openness in sharing their experiences.

Recommendations for further research

Future studies should employ longitudinal or mixed-methods approaches to explore causal pathways between compassion fatigue, patient perceptions, and error outcomes. Special attention should be given to how these dynamics influence nurse well-being, as emotional strain and burnout may not only affect care quality but also compromise the sustainability of the workforce. Comparative research across multiple institutions and cultural contexts could reveal broader patterns and support the development of evidence-based, context-sensitive interventions aimed at strengthening both nurse well-being and patient safety.

Conclusion

This study conclusively validates the existence of the unpopular patient effect, demonstrating a clear and measurable pathway from nurse-reported experience on emotional strain to heightened vulnerability in patient safety. The findings emphasize that relational dynamics, particularly frequent encounters with patients perceived as unpopular, are not isolated interpersonal issues but significant organizational stressors. These stressful interactions are directly shown to contribute to CF, which in turn impairs the cognitive reserves necessary for sound clinical judgment, significantly increasing the perceived error risk. The persistent strain drives adverse outcomes for the nursing workforce, including reduced job satisfaction, increased burnout, and accelerated attrition and disengagement. Individual characteristics like age, experience, and gender are established as key variables that further modulate how these stressors are internalized.

Implications for leadership and management

The study redefines CF not as a personal failure of empathy, but as a critical warning sign of systemic failure to support the nursing workforce. Addressing this is an urgent strategic requirement for leadership. Leaders should formally recognize the unpopular patient effect as a significant human factors risk to high-reliability patient safety. Addressing this requires that institutional policy embed nurse well-being into workplace structures through targeted training focused on managing difficult interactions and preserving empathy. Resources must be allocated to support systems that mitigate emotional strain. Furthermore, strengthening the psychological safety necessary for open, non-punitive error reporting is crucial, as this transparency is essential to identify and correct the system flaws exposed by the unpopular patient effect.

Supporting nurses by strategically addressing the source of their emotional strain is an essential investment. It is required not only for meeting high-reliability standards in patient care but for protecting the longevity, health, and economic effectiveness of the nursing workforce, thereby assuring the overall sustainability of healthcare delivery.
