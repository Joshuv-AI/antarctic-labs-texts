Combined association of genetic predisposition and healthy lifestyle with lung cancer risk: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Zheng Li, Wenhai Fu, Yu Jiang, Yi Feng, Xusen Zou, Yulin Zhao, Peiling Chen, Runchen Wang, Caichen Li, Jianxing He, Wenhua Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolygenic susceptibility and modifiable lifestyle behaviors contribute to lung cancer, but their interaction and combined impact on lung cancer hazard and potential biological correlations remain incompletely characterised. This study aimed to evaluate the independent and combined associations of polygenic risk and healthy lifestyle with lung cancer hazard and to explore potential biological correlations underlying these associations.MethodsWe analyzed data of 254,874 UK Biobank (UKB) participants. A five-component lifestyle score (smoking, alcohol intake, diet, physical activity and sedentary behavior) was summed [0–5] and categorized as unfavorable (0–1 factors), intermediate [2] and favorable [3–5]. An 18-single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) polygenic risk score (PRS) was standardized and categorized into tertiles (low/intermediate/high). Cox proportional hazards models estimated associations with lung cancer hazard; additive interaction was assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI). Mediation analyses of peripheral markers and circulating proteins were exploratory.ResultsAmong 254,874 UKB participants, 2,021 lung cancers occurred over a median of 11.69 years. A favorable lifestyle was associated with lower lung cancer hazard [hazard ratio (HR) 0.34; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.30–0.38; P<0.001], while high PRS was associated with higher hazard (HR 1.62, 95% CI: 1.45–1.81; P<0.001). In the high PRS group, favorable lifestyle remained protective (HR 0.36, 95% CI: 0.30–0.44; P<0.001), whereas intermediate lifestyle was not statistically significant (HR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.68–1.05; P=0.13). The global multiplicative interaction between lifestyle class and PRS category was not significant (P for global interaction =0.49), although selected additive interaction signals were observed, including high PRS combined with intermediate lifestyle (RERI 0.92, 95% CI: 0.31–1.55). Exploratory mediation suggested partial signals for inflammatory markers and proteins (WFDC2, PIGR, PRSS8).ConclusionsThis large-scale prospective study confirms the protective association of a healthy lifestyle with a lower lung cancer hazard. Importantly, in individuals with high genetic susceptibility, moderate lifestyle improvements may not fully offset the elevated hazard, underscoring the need for more comprehensive lifestyle interventions in this high genetic-susceptibility population. Mediation findings provide hypothesis-generating clues for future mechanistic work.

Highlight box

Key findings

• A favorable healthy lifestyle was associated with a substantially lower lung cancer hazard across all polygenic risk score (PRS) categories, including individuals with high genetic susceptibility.

What is known and what is new?

• Genetic susceptibility and lifestyle behaviors are both associated with lung cancer; however, evidence remains limited on how a combined lifestyle score modifies the association between polygenic susceptibility and lung cancer outcomes in large population-based cohorts.

• In UK Biobank, a favorable lifestyle (3–5 healthy factors) was associated with a markedly lower lung cancer hazard across PRS tertiles, including the high PRS group, and we quantified additive interaction patterns between lifestyle category and PRS.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These results support integrating pragmatic lifestyle assessment into prevention strategies alongside genetic risk stratification, and motivate future studies to refine lifestyle scoring (e.g., dose/weighting) and validate potential biological intermediates.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide. In 2022, approximately 2.5 million new cases and over 1.8 million deaths were reported globally, placing a substantial burden on public health systems (1). Despite advances in treatment (2,3), the high societal and healthcare costs underscore the importance of cost-effective prevention strategies.

The etiology of lung cancer is multifactorial, involving a range of behavioral and biological risk factors. Smoking is the most significant known carcinogen (4), but environmental pollution, occupational exposures, and chronic lung diseases also contribute substantially to lung cancer risk (5). Recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified multiple single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) (6) associated with lung cancer risk. Polygenic risk score (PRS) have been shown to effectively capture the cumulative impact of these genetic variants. A study conducted on participants from the UK Biobank (UKB) found (7) that a higher PRS was significantly associated with an increased risk of lung cancer.

Growing evidence indicates that lifestyle behaviors are associated with lung cancer, but most prior studies focused on individual behaviors or did not comprehensively evaluate how lifestyle patterns interact with polygenic susceptibility (8,9). Although genetic and lifestyle factors have been jointly considered in specific contexts such as air pollution (10), the extent to which lifestyle and PRS interact and jointly shape lung cancer hazard has not been fully characterized in large population-based cohorts. Moreover, potential biological correlations linking lifestyle and lung cancer remain incompletely understood.

In this prospective UKB analysis, we developed a five-component lifestyle score (smoking, alcohol consumption, diet, physical activity and sedentary behavior) to evaluate its association with lung cancer incidence across PRS strata. We further performed a sensitivity analysis using a coefficient-weighted lifestyle score, with weights derived from multivariable Cox model coefficients [log hazard ratio (HR)] for the five lifestyle components, to assess robustness to alternative score construction (11). Finally, we conducted exploratory analyses of peripheral markers and circulating proteins to identify candidate biological signals potentially linking lifestyle to lung cancer. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0548/rc).

Methods

Study population

The UKB (12) is a prospective cohort that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 37–73 years through 22 centers between 2006 and 2010. The UKB received approval from the National Health and Social Care Information Governance Committee and the National Health Service (NHS) Northwest Research Ethics Committee (reference: 21/NW/0157). All participants provided informed consent via electronic signature. The current study was approved by the UKB (reference No. 99157). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Among participants with complete healthy lifestyle information and acceptable follow-up (n=334,976), those with any of the following conditions were excluded: (I) withdrawal of informed consent (n=121); (II) cancer at recruitment (n=23,745); (III) genetic information missing (n=56,236). Finally, 254,874 participants were included in the formal analysis (Figure S1).

Baseline cancer exclusion was defined as any malignant cancer diagnosis recorded before or at recruitment, consistent with prior UKB studies, to reduce reverse causation.

Lung cancer assessment

Lung cancer was identified using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision (ICD-10) code C34, with sources including self-reports, hospital admissions data, primary care records, and death registries. Follow-up for lung cancer incidents began from the date of recruitment and continued until the earliest of the following events: study withdrawal, end of the follow-up period (June 1, 2022), lung cancer diagnosis, or death.

PRS calculation

We selected 18 genome-wide significant SNPs to construct PRS (Table S1). PRSs were standardized to a mean of zero and a standard deviation (SD) of 1 by computing the Z score (PRS − mean/SD). We categorized genetic risk into three groups: “low” (the lowest third of the PRS), “intermediate” (the middle third), and “high” (the highest third). Higher PRS scores indicate a greater genetic susceptibility to lung cancer.

Lifestyle factors and healthy lifestyle score

We constructed lifestyle scores for 254,874 participants based on five factors (smoking, physical activity, alcohol intake, diet and sedentary behavior), which were assessed through baseline questionnaires (11). Detailed information on each factor and the corresponding questionnaires is provided in Table S2. Smoking was classified according to the smoking cessation guidelines from the NHS and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) 2015 “Smoking: Reducing and Preventing Tobacco Use” quality standards. Never smoking was categorized as healthy, while current and former smoking were considered unhealthy (15). Moderate alcohol intake was defined according to the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA) and cardiovascular metabolic health dietary priorities as 0–14 grams per day for women and 0–28 grams per day for men. Diet was considered healthy based on adequate intake of at least four out of seven food groups: fruits, vegetables, fish, processed meat, unprocessed red meat, whole grains, and refined grains (16). Physical activity was evaluated using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ-SF). Regular physical activity was defined as meeting the American Heart Association and World Health Organization recommendations: at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity or 75 minutes of vigorous activity per week, or an equivalent combination, or moderate-intensity activity on at least five days a week or vigorous activity on at least one day a week (17). Sedentary behavior based on screen time was estimated using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ), referring to the total self-reported hours spent watching TV and using the computer (excluding computer use at work). A total of 0–4 hours per day was reclassified as low to moderate sedentary behavior (18).

The lifestyle score ranged from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating a healthier lifestyle. The lifestyle scores were then categorized as favorable (3–5 factors), intermediate (2 factors), and unfavorable (0–1 factor) for further analysis.

Peripheral markers

During the baseline assessment [2006–2010], blood biochemical (Category ID 17518) and blood cell count (Category ID 100081) data were obtained from approximately 480,000 participants. Blood cell count samples were analyzed using the Beckman Coulter LH750 instrument, with samples collected in 4 mL ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) vacuum tubes. These two categories provided data on 30 blood biochemical markers and 31 blood cell counts. We used 59 out of these 61 markers, excluding nucleated red blood cell count and percentage because most (>90%) counts or percentages were zero. Blood biochemical markers were categorized as “liver function”, “kidney function”, “endocrine”, “immunometabolic”, or “bone and joint”. Blood cell counts were classified as “white blood cells”, “red blood cells”, or “platelets”.

Metabolic markers were measured using a nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)-based metabolomics platform in randomly selected EDTA plasma samples from approximately 120,000 UKB participants during the baseline assessment. NMR metabolomics (Category ID 220) included 249 metabolic markers (168 directly measured and 81 ratios of these markers). We used the 168 directly measured metabolic markers, categorized as “amino acids”, “apolipoproteins”, “lipoprotein particle size”, “lipoprotein particle concentration”, “fatty acids”, “triglycerides”, “phospholipids”, “cholesteryl esters”, “free cholesterol”, “cholesterol”, “other lipids”, “total lipids”, “ketone bodies”, “glycolysis-related metabolites”, “fluid balance”, and “inflammation”.

Circulating proteins

Non-fasting blood samples were collected at the time of participant recruitment, with plasma prepared and stored at −80 °C. Protein measurements (19) were performed on samples from 54,306 participants enrolled in the UKB Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP) using the Olink Proximity Extension Assay (PEA). The selection of samples included: an initial 5,500 samples pre-selected by UKB-PPP members, followed by 44,502 representative participant samples stratified by age, sex, and recruitment center from the UKB, and the remaining samples selected based on various criteria. For measurement, plasma samples were sent to the Olink Analysis Service Center in Uppsala, Sweden. The relative abundance of 1,465 proteins was quantified using antibodies distributed across four 384-plex panels (inflammation, oncology, cardiometabolic, and neurology) (20). Blood samples were processed on four 384-well plates, each panel of 96 samples including four abundance blocks, and measured using the Olink Explore platform. This platform is based on the highly sensitive and reproducible PEA with low cross-reactivity. The relative concentrations of 1,465 unique proteins were read through next-generation sequencing. Measurements were expressed as Normalized Protein eXpression (NPX) values and log2-transformed. Protein values below the limit of detection (LOD) were replaced by LOD divided by the square root of 2, and each protein’s values were adjusted to a mean of 0 and an SD of 1. Subsequently, protein values underwent an inverse rank normal transformation.

Covariates

Data on covariates were collected at baseline. All models were adjusted for baseline age (ID 21022), sex (ID 31), Townsend deprivation index (ID 189, a measure of area-based socioeconomic deprivation), body mass index (BMI) (ID 21001), and education level (ID 6138) (Model 1).

Statistical analysis

We employed Cox proportional hazards regression models to examine the associations of lifestyle categories and PRS categories with lung cancer hazard. HRs and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Lifestyle class was treated as a categorical variable in interaction analyses. Before examining category-specific additive interaction estimates, we first performed a global test for multiplicative interaction between lifestyle class and PRS category by comparing Cox models with and without the cross-product terms using likelihood-ratio tests. Additive interaction was further assessed using the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI) and its 95% CI: for specific PRS-lifestyle category combinations. To address potential sparse-data and power concerns in interaction analyses, we also regrouped lifestyle into binary categories [favorable (3–5 healthy factors) vs. non-favorable (0–2 healthy factors)] and repeated the global interaction test as a sensitivity analysis.

Furthermore, we stratified the analyses by age, sex, BMI, and tumor type to assess potential effect modification (specific classifications are shown in Table S3). For subgroup analyses, heterogeneity across strata was formally assessed by adding interaction terms between healthy lifestyle score or lifestyle class and the corresponding subgroup variable, with P values for interaction derived from likelihood-ratio tests. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Selected biomarkers, metabolites, and proteins were evaluated as potential mediators through a series of analyses. First, raw data for these variables were standardized using Z-scores. Multiple linear regression models were then applied to assess the associations between a healthy lifestyle and each biomarker, metabolite, or protein. Subsequently, Cox regression models, adjusted for the relevant covariates, were used to examine the relationships between a healthy lifestyle, biomarkers, metabolites, proteins, and lung cancer incidence. Biomarkers, metabolites, or proteins that were significant in both the linear and Cox regression analyses were considered potential mediators for further mediation analysis. The proportion mediated (PM) was estimated using the mediation package, with 95% CIs obtained via non-parametric bootstrap resampling (1,000 resamples). We used a counterfactual framework estimating indirect and direct effects; the PM was calculated as indirect/(indirect + direct). Statistical significance was defined as a false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P<0.05 (21).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were undertaken to evaluate the robustness of the primary findings. First, we additionally adjusted for passive smoking exposure (Model 2), quantified as the self-reported hours per week exposed to tobacco smoke from others at home and outside the home over the past year (UKB Field IDs 1269 and 1279). Second, we constructed a coefficient-weighted healthy lifestyle score by fitting a multivariable Cox model including the five lifestyle components and covariates; the estimated regression coefficients (β, log HRs) were used as weights. The weighted score was rescaled to a 0–5 range and categorised into tertiles (unfavourable/intermediate/favourable).

Patient and public involvement

The study was conducted without involving patients in the formulation and implementation of the research questions, measurement of outcomes, and development of the study plan. Patients were not required to provide their input or offer suggestions regarding the interpretation of the results. There was no plan to disseminate results of the research to study participants.

Results

We included 254,874 UKB participants with complete lifestyle and clinical data (mean age 55.86 years; 51.10% female). Overall, 6.8% had an unfavorable lifestyle (score 0–1), 17.3% had an intermediate lifestyle (score 2), and 75.8% had a favorable lifestyle (score 3–5). Over a median follow-up of 11.69 years [interquartile range (IQR) 8.7–12.8 years], 2,021 participants (0.79%) developed lung cancer (Table 1 and Figure S1).

Association between PRS and lung cancer

The 18-SNP PRS was positively associated with lung cancer hazard when modelled as a continuous standardised variable (HR 1.24, 95% CI: 1.19–1.29; P<0.001). Compared with the low PRS tertile, hazard was higher in the intermediate tertile (HR 1.22, 95% CI: 1.09–1.37) and highest in the high tertile (HR 1.62, 95% CI: 1.45–1.81) (Table S8). Association between lifestyle factors and lung cancer

All five lifestyle components were inversely associated with lung cancer hazard, with the largest association observed for never smoking. Figure 1B illustrates the cumulative hazard of lung cancer by lifestyle score group over the follow-up period. When considering all lifestyle factors combined, participants with scores of 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 had lung cancer HRs of 0.72 (95% CI: 0.55–0.94, P=0.02), 0.56 (95% CI: 0.43–0.72, P<0.001), 0.34 (95% CI: 0.26–0.44, P<0.001), 0.22 (95% CI: 0.16–0.28, P<0.001), and 0.11 (95% CI: 0.08–0.15, P<0.001), respectively, compared to those with a score of 0. Compared with an unhealthy lifestyle, intermediate and favourable lifestyles were associated with 25% (HR 0.75, 95% CI: 0.65–0.85; P<0.001) and 66% (HR 0.34, 95% CI: 0.30–0.38; P<0.001) lower lung cancer hazard, respectively (Figure 2). Additionally, each one-point increase in the healthy lifestyle score corresponded to a 35% lower lung cancer hazard (HR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.62–0.67, P<0.001) (Table S9).

The inverse association between healthy lifestyle and lung cancer hazard was similar across age and sex strata, with no evidence of interaction in formal tests (Figures S2,S3). Results were also broadly consistent across lung cancer subtypes (Figure S4). By contrast, the association varied across BMI strata, with weaker associations among participants with very low or high BMI (Figure S5). Table S9 presents subgroup results when the healthy lifestyle score was modelled as a continuous variable. The corresponding P values for interaction tests are reported in Table S10.

Interaction association of lifestyle, genetic factors, and lung cancer

In PRS-stratified analyses (reference: adverse lifestyle), intermediate and favourable lifestyles were associated with lower lung cancer hazard in the low PRS tertile (HR 0.67, 95% CI: 0.52–0.87 and HR 0.31, 95% CI: 0.24–0.39) and the intermediate PRS tertile (HR 0.70, 95% CI: 0.55–0.89 and HR 0.34, 95% CI: 0.27–0.43).In the high PRS tertile, the favourable lifestyle category remained protective (HR 0.36, 95% CI: 0.30–0.44), whereas the intermediate lifestyle category was not statistically significant (HR 0.85, 95% CI: 0.68–1.05) (Figure S6). When the lifestyle score was modelled continuously, associations did not materially differ across PRS tertiles (Table S8).We observed no evidence of multiplicative interaction. When lifestyle class was treated as a categorical variable, the global test for multiplicative interaction between lifestyle class and PRS category was not statistically significant (P=0.49). Similar results were observed after regrouping lifestyle into binary categories to increase statistical power (P=0.83) (Table S10). In additive interaction analyses using low PRS and favorable lifestyle as the reference group, selected additive interaction signals were observed. In particular, high PRS combined with intermediate lifestyle showed a positive additive interaction (RERI 0.92, 95% CI: 0.31–1.55), whereas other PRS-lifestyle combinations showed weaker or non-significant additive interaction signals (Table S11).

Mediation analysis of peripheral markers and circulating proteins

In exploratory mediation analyses, several peripheral markers and circulating proteins showed partial mediation signals (Tables S4-S7). For blood cell markers, 13 candidates met the prespecified criteria for mediation, with the largest effects for white blood cell count (PM 0.08, 95% CI: 0.05–0.11), mean corpuscular volume (PM 0.06, 95% CI: 0.03–0.09) and neutrophil count (PM 0.05, 95% CI: 0.03–0.07). For biochemical markers, C-reactive protein (PM 0.04, 95% CI: 0.03–0.06) and cystatin C (PM 0.02, 95% CI: 0.01–0.04) showed the largest signals, whereas no significant mediation signals were observed for the metabolic markers assessed. For proteins, WFDC2 (PM 0.28, 95% CI: 0.13–0.99), PIGR (PM 0.28, 95% CI: 0.11–1.47) and PRSS8 (PM 0.26, 95% CI: 0.10–0.85) showed the largest effects.

Sensitivity analysis

After additionally adjusting for passive smoking duration (Model 2), the primary associations were unchanged (Figure S7). Results were also robust when using the coefficient-weighted lifestyle score, which showed a consistent inverse association with lung cancer hazard across lifestyle categories (Figure S8).

Discussion

In this large prospective UKB study, both higher PRS and an unhealthy lifestyle were associated with greater lung cancer hazard. A favorable lifestyle was associated with substantially lower hazard across PRS strata, supporting the importance of lifestyle improvement even among individuals with elevated genetic susceptibility.

Previous large GWAS (13) have identified multiple genetic variants linked to lung cancer. While individual SNPs may have a limited impact on susceptibility, PRS have proven effective in predicting genetic susceptibility for lung cancer (22). In this study, we constructed the PRS using a validated 18-SNP specification derived from genome-wide significant loci and previously applied in UKB lung cancer susceptibility analyses (23), higher PRS was associated with a higher lung cancer hazard, consistent with inherited susceptibility contributing to lung cancer development. Growing evidence suggests that a healthy lifestyle is associated with a reduced hazard of lung cancer, a conclusion that is supported by our study. To the best of our knowledge, this is among the first UKB analyses to integrate a five-component lifestyle pattern with PRS to evaluate their joint associations with incident lung cancer. Previous research has identified relationships between individual lifestyle factors—such as smoking (24), sedentary behavior (25), physical inactivity (26), unhealthy diet (27), and excessive alcohol consumption (28)—and increased lung cancer hazard, but these studies did not fully account for the interrelated nature of lifestyle behaviors and their combined presence in individuals. To comprehensively evaluate the impact of lifestyle on lung cancer hazard, we developed a score based on five key lifestyle factors. Our results demonstrated a clear inverse relationship between lifestyle scores and lung cancer hazard: as healthy lifestyle scores increased, the hazard of lung cancer decreased. Participants were categorized into unhealthy, moderately healthy, and healthy lifestyle groups, with those in the moderately healthy and healthy groups exhibiting significantly lower lung cancer hazard compared to the unhealthy group. This protective trend was consistent across various age groups, sexes, BMI categories, and genetic risk levels, indicating the broad relevance of a healthy lifestyle in reducing lung cancer hazard. However, it is important to note that the protective effect of a healthy lifestyle was diminished in individuals with extreme BMI values (<18.5 or >29.9 kg/m2). The BMI may influence the protective role of a healthy lifestyle on lung cancer hazard through its effects on metabolic function, immune response, or inflammation (29). Thus, when designing lung cancer prevention strategies, individual weight status should be considered. Personalized interventions, such as nutritional support or weight management for individuals with extremely low or high BMI, may be necessary to optimize the long-term protective effects of a healthy lifestyle.

In this study, we integrated lifestyle scores and PRS to examine their joint associations with lung cancer hazard. A favorable lifestyle was associated with lower lung cancer hazard across PRS categories, suggesting that healthy lifestyle patterns may remain relevant even among individuals with elevated genetic susceptibility. These findings are broadly consistent with studies in other cancer types which have also suggested that genetic susceptibility and lifestyle behaviors may jointly contribute to cancer risk (11,30). However, the global multiplicative interaction between lifestyle class and PRS category was not statistically significant, and this finding was consistent after binary regrouping of lifestyle categories. Therefore, our results do not support strong evidence that PRS globally modifies the association between lifestyle and lung cancer hazard on the multiplicative scale. Nevertheless, selected additive interaction signals were observed, particularly for high PRS combined with intermediate lifestyle. Because additive and multiplicative interaction analyses address different epidemiological questions (31), additive-scale risk differences may still be informative for identifying subgroups with a higher absolute-risk burden. This finding should be interpreted cautiously because interaction analyses often have limited statistical power and because lifestyle categories were based on baseline self-reported information.

Mediation analyses identified several candidate circulating signals linking lifestyle and lung cancer hazard. Inflammatory markers, including white blood cell count, neutrophil count, and C-reactive protein, showed mediation signals. Red blood cell indices, including mean corpuscular volume, mean sphered cell volume, mean reticulocyte volume, and mean corpuscular haemoglobin content, also showed mediation signals and may be related to inflammatory responses (32), oxidative stress (33), and tissue hypoxia (34). Proteomic analyses further identified WFDC2, PIGR, and PRSS8, consistent with prior evidence implicating these proteins in lung cancer biology (35). Given the observational design, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than causal.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to integrate five lifestyle factors in exploring the association between lifestyle, genetic risk, and lung cancer, while also investigating the potential biological mechanisms underlying this relationship. The study’s strengths include a large sample size, long follow-up period, and comprehensive clinical biomarker data, which enabled a thorough evaluation of the mechanisms driving the observed associations. Robustness was further supported by sensitivity analyses incorporating passive smoking adjustment and an alternative coefficient-weighted lifestyle score.

However, several limitations should be noted. First, the assessment of lifestyle factors relied primarily on self-reported data, which may introduce information biases, such as recall bias and social desirability bias. Second, as an observational study, although we employed various statistical methods to adjust for confounders, residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. In addition, our primary healthy lifestyle score used dichotomized definitions for each lifestyle component and assigned equal weights across components. This pragmatic approach improves interpretability but may not capture dose/intensity, particularly for smoking intensity and very low/no alcohol intake, and may mask heterogeneity in the relative contributions of individual behaviors. To address this concern, we conducted a coefficient-weighted lifestyle score sensitivity analysis, which yielded directionally consistent findings. Lastly, the data were predominantly derived from a UK population, limiting the generalizability of the findings. While the UKB cohort is broadly representative, lifestyle behaviors and disease risks may vary across different ethnicities and regions, warranting further validation in more diverse populations.

Conclusions

In this large prospective study, we found a significant association between lifestyle improvements and a reduced hazard of lung cancer. Importantly, this protective effect was consistent across different age groups, genders, BMI categories, and levels of genetic susceptibility, suggesting that a healthy lifestyle offers broad potential benefits for lung cancer prevention. However, for individuals with high genetic risk or those with extreme BMI values, more comprehensive lifestyle interventions may be required. These findings provide new insights into lung cancer prevention and offer a scientific basis for developing more effective public health strategies. Future research should aim to further validate these results and design prevention strategies based on lifestyle interventions to reduce the burden of lung cancer.
