Machine learning for risk stratification of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy: Enhancing clinical efficiency in low-resource antenatal care in Tanzania

ABSTRACT
Maternal mortality in Tanzania remains a public health crisis, with Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy (HDP) causing 34% of direct obstetric deaths. In overburdened government clinics, high patient volumes and limited resources often restrict assessments to single-point blood pressure checks, leading to missed diagnoses. This study investigates the potential of machine learning (ML) to move beyond simple threshold detection toward automated risk stratification, aiming to optimize patient flow and prioritize clinical resources for high-risk individuals. We analyzed 337,027 routine records (2023–2024) from Tanzania’s Unified Community System (UCS). Data from multiple visits were aggregated into 187,438 unique client records. HDP was defined by standard clinical thresholds (BP ≥ 140/90 mmHg). We trained five ML models on a balanced subset and validated the top performer on an independent dataset of over 120,000 records to evaluate its utility as a triage tool. XGBoost was the best performing model, achieving 90.1% accuracy and an AUC of 0.95. The model maintained 100% sensitivity, successfully stratifying 12,603 clients into the high-risk category, including those potentially overlooked by traditional checks. While precision was 14% (representing 6.3 false positives per true case), this high-sensitivity screening approach ensures no at-risk client is missed, allowing providers to focus intensive assessment time where it is most needed. ML-driven risk stratification can transform congested ANC workflows by identifying high-risk clients before they escalate to critical states. By automating the initial triage, health facilities can improve operational efficiency and ensure limited specialist time is dedicated to the most vulnerable patients. We recommend that the Ministry of Health strengthens digital data integration to support the deployment of these stratification tools within routine primary care.

Introduction

Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy (HDP) affect an estimated 5–10% of pregnancies in Tanzania [1], contributing to approximately 34% of direct maternal deaths. To address this burden, there is an urgent need for tools that can move beyond passive monitoring toward active risk stratification.

Machine Learning (ML) techniques have recently gained significant attention to predicting maternal health outcomes. Established clinical risk prediction tools such as the fullPIERS and PREP-S models have demonstrated good predictive performance for adverse maternal outcomes in pre-eclampsia and are recommended as adjuncts to clinical assessment in several guidelines [2–7]. However, most existing studies rely on curated research datasets [6,8], that fail to capture data quality challenges inherent in routine Health Management Information Systems (HMIS), including missing values, measurement inconsistencies, and documentation gaps [9]. Recent systematic reviews highlight this parallel rise in ML applications [6,10–12], yet few models have been developed or validated using routine government data from low-resource settings.

Existing HDP models report strong performance with AUC ranging from 0.81 to 0.91 [2,3] and accuracy up to 84% [4]. However, these results typically stem from standardized environments with complete data. Furthermore, most published studies report high precision (often >70%) [4,5], which may not reflect the realities of imbalanced, real-world datasets.

In contrast, this study utilizes routine government Antenatal Care (ANC) data from over 120,000 clients to achieve comparable sensitivity and AUC. Our model results in a lower precision (14%), which is a direct consequence of using “noisy,” passively collected health system data.

In alignment with WHO recommendations advocating for early detection [13], ML-driven stratification offers significant promise for prioritizing care in overburdened systems. The Government of Tanzania (GoT) has already laid the groundwork through the Unified Community System (UCS) [14,15], a digital platform for remote data collection in low-resource settings. However, the potential of this data remains underutilized; many expectant mothers attend fewer than the recommended visits [16–18], and reliance on paper-based backups [18], often prevents the triangulation of data for population-level insights. At the same time, the deployment of ML-based decision-support tools in such contexts requires careful attention to ethical and implementation considerations, including data quality, representativeness, algorithmic bias, transparency of model predictions, and the need to ensure that automated systems augment rather than replace clinical judgment [19–21]. Addressing these challenges is particularly important in low- and middle-income countries where health information systems are evolving, and data completeness can vary substantially across facilities. This study aims to bridge this gap by demonstrating how routine UCS data can be leveraged to stratify maternal risk and improve clinical efficiency within real-world health system constraints.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study utilized routine ANC data from Tanzania’s UCS to develop and validate a machine learning model for HDP risk stratification. The study population comprised all expectant mothers recorded in the UCS between 2020 and 2024 across 23 regions of Tanzania. These regions represent the current coverage of the UCS application, which is being phased into the remaining eight regions of the country.

Data processing and feature engineering

Data were extracted from the UCS online portal and processed through a structured transformation pipeline. Initial cleaning involves converting data types, removing duplicate entries based on unique event identifiers, and correcting implausible physiological values. Using domain expertise, we addressed common digital entry errors (e.g., correcting “14.02” or “1402” to “140.2” mmHg) and removed extreme outliers exceeding three standard deviations from the mean.

Data flattening strategy

We collapsed multiple visits into a single patient record due to extreme temporal sparsity (a median of one visit per client and only 43% of clients having two or more recorded visits). In the data collapse pipeline, we retained the last recorded value for categorical variables and the mean value for numerical variables. This “flattening” approach was critical for two reasons:

Generalizability: It prevented the exclusion of over 50% of the population, thereby reducing selection bias.

Generalizability: It prevented the exclusion of over 50% of the population, thereby reducing selection bias.

Clinical Realism: It mirrors the operational reality in Tanzania, where clinicians must often perform risk assessments based on a single point of contact.

Clinical Realism: It mirrors the operational reality in Tanzania, where clinicians must often perform risk assessments based on a single point of contact.

Feature selection.

We employed a multi-stage hybrid approach to identify predictors. An initial candidate set was established by merging domain expertise with data availability constraints. This set was then statistically screened using Pearson correlation, Chi-square tests, and Mutual Information scores to capture both linear and non-linear associations. While Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) identified a parsimonious subset of three features (systolic BP, diastolic BP, and syphilis), we utilized SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) and clinical consultation to retain a more comprehensive profile. The final model includes proteinuria, BMI, blood glucose, and temperature, ensuring the tool captures a broader biological context relevant to HDP risk.

Missing data handling

We employed listwise deletion (complete case analysis) for the final analytic dataset. We deliberately avoided imputation for two primary reasons:

Non-Random Missingness: In routine health systems, data are often “Missing Not At Random” (MNAR); for instance, diagnostic tests are more likely to be missing in resource-constrained facilities or among patients perceived as low-risk, which would introduce bias if imputed.

After flattening, any client record missing any of the seven core features (systolic/diastolic BP, BMI, blood glucose, proteinuria, temperature, and syphilis) was excluded. Outcome definition

The primary outcome HDP was defined according to the Tanzania’s ANC national guideline as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg [22].

Rationale for algorithmic labeling

In this study, HDP status was derived algorithmically from routine BP measurements rather than relying on documented clinical diagnoses. This decision was necessitated by severe underreporting and data quality concerns in the UCS dataset. Epidemiological estimates indicate that hypertensive disorders of pregnancy affect approximately 5–10% of pregnancies in Tanzania; however, fewer than 0.5% of health records contain a completed diagnosis field due to due to persistent documentation challenges [23]. Consultations with implementation partners identified three primary drivers for this gap:

Data Entry Lag: Clinical events are often not recorded in real-time, leading to omitted diagnoses during retrospective entry.

Data Entry Lag: Clinical events are often not recorded in real-time, leading to omitted diagnoses during retrospective entry.

System Fragmentation: HDP cases are frequently managed in labor wards, but the lack of a floating unique patient identifier and existence of paper registers prevents linking delivery outcomes back to ANC records.

System Fragmentation: HDP cases are frequently managed in labor wards, but the lack of a floating unique patient identifier and existence of paper registers prevents linking delivery outcomes back to ANC records.

Performance Bias: Reluctance to document adverse outcomes due to facility-level performance metrics.

To address these limitations, we employed a rule-based outcome estimation informed by subject-matter experts. This approach classified cases based on clinical signs and onset indicators typical of HDP, providing a “silver standard” ground truth where a gold standard (confirmed clinical diagnosis) was unavailable.

Addressing data leakage and model interpretation

We acknowledge that using BP measurements as both predictors and components of the algorithmic outcome introduces data leakage, which fundamentally shapes the interpretation of our results. Because the outcome is defined by the same BP thresholds that serve as primary predictors, the high AUC and sensitivity achieved do not represent the prediction of future disease. Instead, they demonstrate the model’s capacity for enhanced risk stratification: its ability to identify consistent patterns of BP elevation more reliably than a human clinician performing a single-visit threshold check in a “noisy” environment. This distinction is critical for clinical applications. The model functions as an advanced screening tool for BP-defined risk under real-world conditions of infrequent visits and measurement error. To mitigate the impact of leakage, we:

Incorporated a diverse set of predictive features beyond blood pressure (e.g., BMI, syphilis, proteinuria).

Validated the model on a fully independent dataset.

Conducted a discordant case analysis to demonstrate the model’s ability to “screen in” at-risk cases that might be missed by conventional, manual BP thresholding alone.

While these steps strengthen the model’s utility, the performance metrics must be interpreted strictly within the context of a BP-defined proximal outcome.

ML classification

Several ML classification methods were evaluated to determine their suitability for risk stratification. We compared five widely used algorithms: K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). Rather than selecting a model based on raw accuracy alone (which can be misleading in imbalanced datasets), the optimal model was selected based on its ability to maintain high sensitivity (recall) while processing routine, noisy HMIS data.

Clinical threshold selection for risk stratification

To ensure the model functions as a reliable safety screen, we moved away from default probability thresholds (0.5) or metrics like Youden’s Index that balance sensitivity and specificity equally. Instead, we intentionally calibrated the decision threshold to prioritize 100% sensitivity. While standard optimization (such as F1-score maximization) yielded a threshold of 0.995 with high precision but unacceptably low recall (2%), our approach prioritizes “screening in” all potential HDP cases. This ensures that in a congested facility, no at-risk client is missed, even at the cost of a higher false-positive rate (lower precision), which is clinically managed through standard follow-up BP confirmation.

Model calibration and Precision-Recall analysis.

The agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes was assessed using Platt scaling to obtain calibrated probabilities. We evaluated calibration using: (1) calibration plots comparing predicted vs. observed risk across deciles; (2) the Brier score; and (3) calibration slope and intercept. Given the class imbalance (3.7% HDP prevalence), we prioritized Precision-Recall (PR) curves and Average Precision (AP) over ROC-AUC, as AP provides a more rigorous assessment of the model’s performance on the rare positive class [24].

Sampling strategy and class balancing.

The analytic dataset exhibited severe class imbalance (1,894 HDP cases vs. 48,680 controls). To address this, we employed random under-sampling of the majority class [25] to create a balanced training set (50/50 distribution). This approach was chosen over synthetic methods like SMOTE to ensure the model learned from authentic clinical patterns rather than generated data points, which can exacerbate noise in routine health records. Under-sampling was applied strictly to the training set; the validation set retained the natural 3.7% prevalence to ensure the performance metrics reflect real-world clinical utility.

Model training, testing, and validation.

The dataset was split into 90% for training/testing and 10% for internal validation. We utilized 5-fold cross-validation with hyperparameter tuning (e.g., learning rate and depth for XGBoost; regularization for LR). For final evaluation, an independent validation dataset (N = 120,232) from April–May 2024 was used. We performed a discordant case analysis (using t-tests and chi-square tests) to investigate instances where ML predictions differed from conventional threshold-based detection. Finally, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were calculated to provide model interpretability, identifying the key maternal health indicators driving the stratification.

Feature selection and model development

Bivariate analysis and statistical screening

Initial feature selection was guided by data availability and clinical expertise. Bivariate analysis using Pearson correlation showed strong positive associations between HDP and systolic blood pressure (r = 0.69, p < 0.05) and diastolic blood pressure (r = 0.72, p < 0.05) (Fig 2). Spearman’s rank correlation yielded consistent results (SBP: ρ = 0.67; DBP: ρ = 0.71), confirming the robustness of these primary predictors. Categorical screening via Chi-square tests identified proteinuria (χ² = 1377.02, Cramer’s V = 0.166) and BMI categories (χ² = 1087.67, Cramer’s V = 0.147) as the strongest non-continuous predictors. Mutual Information (MI) scores further confirmed that SBP and DBP were the most informative features, while temperature (MI = 0.008) and syphilis status (MI = 0.001) provided minimal individual predictive gains.

Final analytic dataset construction

From the initial 187,438 client records, 136,864 (73%) were excluded due to the missingness in key features or physiological outliers (>3 SD), resulting in a final analytic dataset of 50,574 records. This sample maintained a natural HDP prevalence of 3.7% (1,894 cases). As detailed in the Methods, a random under-sampling procedure was applied to the training split to create a balanced set of 3,788 records. The final model incorporated seven clinically relevant predictors: SBP, DBP, BMI, blood glucose, proteinuria, temperature, and syphilis.

Model selection and training

We evaluated five ML algorithms using 5-fold cross-validation on the balanced training set (Fig 3). All models demonstrated high accuracy, with Random Forest (99.79%) and XGBoost (99.91%) achieving near-perfect performance across all metrics (Accuracy, Precision, F1, and AUC = 1.00) with zero variance on the cleaned training data (Fig 4). Based on this superior performance and its known robustness in handling the non-linear complexities of routine health data, XGBoost was selected as the optimal model for final deployment and validation.

Model validation and performance on real-world data

Independent validation performance.

The XGBoost model was validated on an independent dataset of 120,232 new records (April–May 2024), maintaining a natural HDP prevalence of 1.4%. As shown in Table 5, the model achieved an overall accuracy of 90.95% (95% CI: 90.8%–91.1%) and an AUC of 0.95 (95% CI: 0.953–0.955), demonstrating exceptionally discriminating power (Fig 6). While cross-validation on balanced data yielded near-perfect precision, the transition to real-world data resulted in a precision of 0.14 (95% CI: 0.130–0.143) and an F1-score of 0.24. This reflects the high-volume, low-prevalence nature of routine health systems and our deliberate calibration toward patient safety.

Clinical threshold optimization and triage utility.

Standard optimization (e.g., F1-score maximization) suggested a high probability threshold (0.995). However, this would have identified only 40 of 1,725 true HDP cases (2% sensitivity), which was deemed clinically unacceptable. By selecting a decision threshold that prioritizes 100% Sensitivity (Recall), the model successfully identified all 1,725 true HDP cases in the validation set (Fig 7). This strategy resulted in a Positive Predictive Rate (PPR) of 0.10, flagging a total of 12,615 records for clinical review. While this introduces a higher volume of false positives (Specificity: 0.91), it ensures that in resource-constrained clinics, the “screen-in” mechanism captures every potential case for manual blood pressure confirmation.

Discussion

ML offers a practical path from single-point blood pressure (BP) thresholding toward automated, data-driven risk stratification in overburdened antenatal care (ANC) settings. Using 337,027 routine UCS records aggregated into 187,438 client-level records, our study demonstrates that an XGBoost model can achieve strong discrimination for HDP (AUC = 0.95) while maintaining maximal sensitivity (100%) on independent validation thereby operationalizing the screening priority of “do not miss” high-risk clients in a context where HDP accounts for ~34% of direct obstetric deaths in Tanzania [2].

Data quality and missingness

Routine ANC data in low-resource settings remain imperfect: paper-based collection, fragmented recording practices, and limited diagnostic testing lead to systematic gaps [11,12,27,28]. In our cohort, missingness was not random: nearly half of excluded women lacked entire classes of clinical tests (proteinuria, glucose), reflecting either test unavailability or clinically appropriate prioritization of urgent care (e.g., late presenters, active labor). This selection mechanism means the most acutely vulnerable women may be underrepresented in training data, producing selection bias and potential inequities in model performance. Improving front-line digitization (tablets, embedded forms) and workforce training would both increase data completeness and improve model generalizability, although cost and maintenance barriers remain [29].

Performance trade-offs and operational implications

XGBoost produced 90.1% accuracy with AUC 0.95 on validation, stratifying 12,603 clients as high-risk; 1,725 (14%) of these met conventional BP-based HDP criteria. The deliberate choice to preserve 100% sensitivity yields a precision of 14% (≈6.3 false positives per true positive). This high-sensitivity design aligns with clinical aims in Tanzania but imposes operational costs: an estimated 6.3 false positive per true postivie in the validation sample, potentially translating to dozens of extra workups per clinician per day in busy clinics [16,23]. Each false positive consumes clinician time, may provoke patient anxiety, and risks alert fatigue with downstream consequences for care quality.

Model validity vs. real-world performance

The divergence between near-perfect development cross-validation and realistic validation performance highlights a familiar translation issue: extensively preprocessed, balanced training sets inflate apparent performance relative to noisy routine data [9,27,30]. Calibration analysis showed systematic overprediction in the validation context; the Brier score (0.08) must be interpreted against low disease prevalence and class imbalance. Thus, the model is best suited for relative risk ranking (triage) rather than precise absolute risk estimates for individual decision-making.

Clinical interpretability and potential added value

SBP and DBP were dominant predictors, consistent with clinical knowledge and explaining the rapid early ROC gains. Similar ML studies have shown that SBP and DBP metrics are among the most influential predictors in models of hypertension and cardiovascular risk [31]. Yet the model also integrated other routinely recorded variables (proteinuria, syphilis status, etc.), producing discordant cases where elevated-but-subthreshold BP combined with ancillary signals led to high-risk classification. ML models are well suited to detect such nonlinear interactions between variables that may not be captured by traditional threshold-based clinical rules [32]. These discordant cases may represent subclinical or emergent risk states that single-point BP thresholds miss, but prospective outcome-anchored validation is required to determine whether they reflect true risk or data noise.

To translate a high-sensitivity model into workable practice, several pragmatic strategies are recommended:

Tiered risk outputs (low/moderate/high) to prioritize immediate review for the highest-risk subgroup.

Limitations

Key limitations include selection bias from non-random missingness excluding clinically important subgroups; reliance on routine BP recordings rather than standardized outcome adjudication; limited temporal depth that prevented sequential/time-series modeling; and the operational uncertainty of the false-positive workload estimates, which must be validated in real-world pilots. Heterogeneity in facility infrastructure and staffing across Tanzania will also affect implementation feasibility.

This work provides proof-of-concept that routine UCS ANC data can power ML-driven triage for HDP with near-zero missed cases, potentially improving prioritization in congested clinics and directing scarce specialist time to the most vulnerable patients. However, the substantial false-positive rate requires implementation approaches that prioritize graded triage, workflow embedding, low-cost confirmatory checks, and prospective recalibration. We recommend Ministry of Health investment in stronger digital data integration, targeted data-quality interventions, and phased pilot deployments to evaluate clinical impact, resource implications, and equity before scale-up dnext steps

Key messages

What is already known on this topic

Maternal mortality in low‑resource settings is driven by hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, yet routine ANC often relies on single blood pressure checks that miss high‑risk cases. Effective risk stratification tools are urgently needed to guide timely interventions.

What this study adds

Using routine ANC data from Tanzania, this study shows that ML can deliver automated risk stratification with 100% sensitivity, ensuring no high‑risk client is missed. The model functions as a practical screening tool to prioritize care in overburdened clinics.

How this study might affect research, practice, or policy

ML‑driven risk stratification could streamline ANC workflows by directing limited specialist time to the most vulnerable patients. Strengthening digital health infrastructure and evaluating real‑world implementation are critical next steps for policy and practice.

Reviewer #2:

Reviewer 2: Recommendation – major review

Abstract

This abstract takes on a critical issue, Tanzania’s persistently high maternal mortality, and does so in a way that feels timely and innovative. Using digital antenatal care records and machine learning to predict hypertensive disorders of pregnancy is a strong framing, and the sheer scale of the dataset (300,000 visits) immediately makes the work stand out. Highlighting the comparison of multiple models before choosing XGBoost also signals methodological care, which is a plus for readers.

At the same time, the way the results are presented feels a little unbalanced. The abstract emphasizes the high accuracy and AUC, but the precision (14%) and F1-score (24%) tell a very different story. Those numbers suggest the model isn’t yet reliable enough for clinical use, and that point is downplayed. A quick acknowledgment that the model favors sensitivity at the cost of precision would help set more realistic expectations about its utility in a low-resource clinical environment.

There’s also some confusion in the dataset description that could use improvement. The authors say they analyzed 300,000 records, but then note only 3,788 were in the training dataset with a 50/50 distribution. That sounds like some balancing or sub-sampling was done, but it isn’t explained. Even a short clarification in the abstract would prevent readers from questioning whether the results are generalizable. Similarly, mentioning how missing values were handled, or whether repeated visits from the same women were included, would add a lot of credibility.

Lastly, the conclusion leans a bit too optimistic. It’s fair to say that AI can support earlier detection and potentially reduce maternal mortality, but the abstract should frame these results as proof-of-concept rather than ready-to-deploy. The mention of data quality is on point, but it could go further, scaling models like this will also require stronger infrastructure, training, and policy support. Balancing optimism with realism would make the abstract much stronger and more trustworthy.

Introduction

The introduction does a good job of framing the study within the broader context of machine learning applications in maternal health. The authors clearly highlight that ML has been used to predict conditions such as pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, and preterm labor, and they link these efforts to the growing availability of digital health data. The reference to WHO recommendations on early detection is particularly useful, since it grounds the study in internationally recognized priorities for improving maternal health outcomes. Overall, the opening establishes that the topic is both relevant and timely.

At the same time, the introduction feels too expansive and could be made more focused. The section on antenatal care reporting provides helpful background but it reads more like a health systems overview than part of a research introduction. It would be stronger if it were condensed and framed in terms of why gaps in ANC reporting matter for early detection of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy. The same is true for the long description of the Unified Community System. While the details are interesting, not all of them are necessary in the introduction. Some information could be moved to the Methods section, while the introduction could highlight more directly how the UCS enables new possibilities for predictive modeling in Tanzania.

The study objectives are well defined but they appear too late in the introduction. Presenting them earlier, right after describing the maternal health burden and the potential role of ML, would give readers a clearer sense of direction. As written, the objectives risk being overshadowed by long background sections. A sharper structure would move from problem (maternal mortality and inadequate ANC data), to opportunity (ML models applied to routine digital data), to the specific research goals. This would keep readers oriented and make the originality of the study stand out more clearly.

Finally, the introduction would be stronger if it more explicitly identified the research gap this study is addressing. The authors mention that ML has been applied to maternal health outcomes, but it is not clear what has been missing from past studies that this paper uniquely contributes. Is it the scale of the dataset, the Tanzanian context, or the integration with a national digital health system? Stating this gap directly would make the novelty of the work more visible. Similarly, acknowledging early on that routine data may have quality issues would strike a more balanced tone and prepare readers for the practical challenges that the study addresses.

Methods

The Methods section provides a good overview of the study design, dataset, and analytic approach. The use of a retrospective cohort design with routine antenatal care data is appropriate given the study objectives, and the description of the Unified Community System adds valuable context on how the data was collected. The attention to data cleaning and preprocessing, including the conversion of variable formats, duplicate removal, and thoughtful aggregation of longitudinal records into cross-sectional format, shows care in preparing the dataset for analysis. Involving obstetricians and public health experts in feature selection is also a strength, as it helps ensure clinical relevance.

At the same time, several aspects of the methods would benefit from additional clarity and detail. For example, the decision to collapse repeated ANC visits into a single record per client may simplify the modeling process, but it also risks losing important temporal information that could influence hypertensive risk. A brief justification for this choice, or at least an acknowledgment of the trade-off, would make the approach more transparent. Similarly, while the authors note that Pearson correlation was used to explore the relationship between predictors and the outcome, correlation may not be the best measure for categorical or non-linear relationships. Providing more detail on why this method was chosen, and whether other feature selection techniques were considered, would strengthen confidence in the modeling pipeline.

The description of the outcome definition is a critical part of this study, and the authors are right to highlight the challenges with underreporting of hypertensive disorders in the dataset. However, the reliance on a single threshold-based calculation to define cases may oversimplify clinical reality. Blood pressure measurements in routine ANC settings can be variable due to equipment quality, staff training, or measurement error. A discussion of how these potential inaccuracies might affect case definition, or whether multiple readings per patient were considered, would make the outcome measure more robust.

Finally, the modeling approach is described in broad strokes but lacks important details for reproducibility. For instance, it is not clear whether cross-validation was used during model training, what hyperparameter tuning was performed, or whether class imbalance was addressed explicitly. Reporting accuracy alone is not sufficient, since accuracy can be inflated in imbalanced datasets; the inclusion of precision, recall, F1-score, and calibration metrics would make the evaluation more complete. The section on model application is a useful addition, showing how the model was tested on new UCS data, but it could go further by explaining how the model’s predictions compared to traditional case definitions and what implications this has for real-world adoption.

Results

The Results section is thorough and presents a wide range of analyses, from descriptive statistics through model validation and feature importance. The descriptive findings are helpful, especially the breakdown of ANC visits before and after collapsing records into individual clients. This makes it clear how much information is lost when visits are aggregated and highlights an important contextual point for interpreting the models. The histogram of gestational age and the correlation heatmap add useful visualization, showing both the distribution of visits and the strength of associations with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy.

At the same time, there are several areas that would benefit from refinement. The correlation results are described as significant, but Pearson correlation is a limited tool for understanding complex relationships between predictors and outcomes, especially when variables are non-linear or categorical. More robust feature selection methods could be mentioned, or at least an acknowledgment that correlation is a preliminary measure. In addition, the decision to balance the dataset by downsampling or resampling to achieve equal risk and no-risk groups should be described in more detail, as this step has a major influence on model performance and interpretability.

The model training and validation results appear strong at first glance, with very high cross-validation scores across all classifiers. However, the near-perfect accuracy shown in Table 2 (100 percent across precision, recall, and F1-score in the small test dataset) raises concerns about overfitting. This becomes evident when the model is applied to new UCS data, where performance drops considerably, especially in precision (14 percent) and F1-score (24 percent). These results deserve a more critical discussion in the Results section itself, not just later in the paper. Presenting calibration curves, confusion matrices, or a breakdown of false positives versus false negatives would make the performance evaluation more transparent.

The feature importance plot is a helpful addition, as it aligns with clinical expectations that systolic and diastolic blood pressure are the strongest predictors. However, the figure could be described in a way that ties more directly to clinical practice. For example, explaining that BMI and proteinuria contribute modestly, while syphilis status and temperature contribute little, would help readers see which features add incremental value beyond standard hypertension screening.

Overall, the Results section demonstrates impressive effort in data analysis and visualization, but it could be improved by providing more nuance around the limitations of the modeling approach. Clearer acknowledgment of the gap between training performance and real-world validation would make the results more credible and prepare the ground for a balanced discussion.

Discussion

The discussion section does a good job of connecting the study results to the bigger picture of maternal health in low-resource settings. The authors clearly explain the challenges of poor data quality, lack of digital tools, and limited trained staff, while also pointing out that Tanzania’s investment in the UCS system is creating opportunities for digital health. It is also a strength that the authors show how the ML model identified predictors like BMI and protein in urine, which are often overlooked in routine care. This makes the case that ML can add value beyond just blood pressure readings.

However, the discussion tends to emphasize the positive findings more than the limitations. The model reached high accuracy but had very low precision, meaning it predicted a large number of women as “at risk” when they were not. This is an important issue because in a health system with limited resources, too many false positives could overwhelm staff and equipment. The authors should discuss this trade-off more directly and explain whether the model in its current form would really help health workers, or if it first needs further refinement.

The limitations section is well written but could be clearer. The authors explain that HDP was not directly recorded in the UCS and that blood pressure values were used instead. This is an important point, but it should also be noted that blood pressure in routine ANC can sometimes be recorded inaccurately, which could affect results. The lack of follow-up data is another key issue, because without clinical confirmation, it is difficult to know how reliable the predictions are outside the dataset.

The recommendations and key messages are helpful but read a bit broad. They could be made stronger by giving more concrete steps, such as improving digital training for health workers, making ANC data entry more standardized, or piloting the ML model in a few facilities before thinking about scaling up. These kinds of practical next steps would make the recommendations more useful to policymakers and health system leaders.

Overall, the discussion is clear and well-structured, but it needs to be more balanced. The authors should talk more openly about the limits of the model, especially the low precision, and give sharper, more realistic recommendations. This would make the paper stronger and show both the promise and the challenges of applying ML to maternal health in Tanzania.

Conclusion

This paper takes on an important problem and shows how machine learning could be used to predict high blood pressure in pregnancy using routine health data in Tanzania. The study makes good use of a large dataset, tests several models, and connects the work to real-world health challenges, which makes it both timely and relevant.

At the same time, the paper needs more clarity and balance. The results show very high accuracy but very low precision, which limits how useful the model would be in practice. The methods also need to be explained in more detail so readers can better understand and trust the findings. If the authors address these issues and provide more realistic recommendations for how the model could be used in low-resource settings, this study could make a strong contribution to maternal health research.

-->Additional Editor Comments (if provided):-->--> -->-->Harnessing Digital Health and Machine Learning for Early Detection of Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy

The three reviews converge on the view that this manuscript addresses an important and timely problem and demonstrates substantial technical effort, improved transparency, and clearer positioning compared to the previous version. Reviewer 2 considers the work close to publishable with minor refinements, particularly around tempering claims, clarifying operational implications of low precision, and contextualizing near-perfect training performance. In contrast, Reviewers 4 and 5 raise significant concerns regarding the use of proxy outcome definitions derived from blood pressure thresholds, the circularity between predictors and labels, severe precision limitations, loss of temporal information, missing data handling, and overstatement of clinical readiness. Collectively, these issues do not invalidate the study’s value as a large-scale, retrospective, proof-of-concept analysis using routine ANC data, but they do require substantial reframing, additional analyses, and stronger methodological transparency. I therefore recommend major revision according to reviewers comments. Addressing these points would significantly strengthen the manuscript’s credibility and alignment with implementation-oriented digital health research.

Reviewer 2 – minor revision

The revised manuscript is much improved and addresses the main concerns from the previous review. The abstract, methods, and results are now better, especially regarding how the dataset was built, how class imbalance was handled, how independent validation was done, and how the model is positioned as a high-sensitivity screening tool rather than a diagnostic system. The authors have improved the methods by clearly describing preprocessing, feature selection, cross-validation, and performance evaluation. The discussion now more openly addresses the impact of low precision in settings with limited resources and offers practical implementation recommendations. A few minor issues remain, such as the need to temper further the interpretation of near-perfect training performance and to more clearly explain the operational burden of false positives, but these do not detract from the overall value of the work.

Abstract

What was done well:

The abstract is much better now. The authors clearly explain the trade-off between sensitivity and precision and describe the model as a high-sensitivity screening tool rather than a diagnostic system. They have clarified the dataset size by distinguishing total ANC visits, collapsed client records, the balanced training subset, and the independent validation dataset. This addresses my earlier concerns about transparency and generalizability. The tone is now more restrained and better suited to a proof-of-concept study.

What remains to be done:

The abstract still focuses too much on accuracy and AUC, which could mislead non-technical readers, given the very low precision. Although the trade-off is mentioned, adding a short phrase that quantifies the operational impact, like the number of false positives per true positive, would make the summary more realistic.

Introduction

What was done well:

The introduction is clearer and better organized. The authors now state the research gap: few ML studies use routine government ANC data at a national level in low-resource settings. Background on ANC reporting and UCS is shortened and moved, and the study objectives are introduced earlier. This improves narrative flow and highlights novelty.

What remains to be done:

The introduction would be improved by a clearer comparison with previous ML-based HDP studies, especially regarding performance metrics such as precision. Right now, the novelty is mostly about the data source and context, not about how the methods differ from existing models. A comparison is needed here.

Methods

What was done well:

This section is the most improved. The authors now give detailed descriptions of data preprocessing, visit collapsing, handling missing data, feature selection, class balancing, cross-validation, hyperparameter tuning, and independent validation. They clearly explain why they collapsed the longitudinal data, based on real-world needs. They also openly discuss the potential for data leakage when using BP-based outcome definitions. The modeling process is now reproducible and defensible.

What remains to be done:

Even with improved transparency, the outcome definition remains limited by using BP thresholds, which are also the top predictors. The authors mention this, but they should make it clearer that the model’s performance does not show true risk prediction. Instead, it should be seen as improved screening in settings with noisy measurements. This point is still unclear. In addition, for the correlation analysis to detect collinearity, it assumes that the continuous variables have a linear relationship. What if this assumption is wrong? The authors need to state how they checked this.

Results

What was done well:

The Results section is now more honest and informative. The authors present confusion matrices and ROC curves, assess calibration, and carefully analyze cases where results differ. They show and discuss the big drop in precision during real-world validation. Feature importance is also explained in clinical terms. This addresses earlier concerns about overfitting and selective reporting.

What remains to be done:

The almost-perfect cross-validation results on the balanced training data are still presented without sufficient caution. Overfitting is mentioned, but the manuscript should clearly say that these results do not prove the model is better. Instead, they are likely due to under-sampling and how the labels were created.

Discussion and Conclusion

What was done well:

The discussion is more balanced and improved. The authors now address the effects of low precision in resource-limited systems and give step-by-step recommendations for piloting, improving data quality, training, and policy integration. The conclusion presents the work as a proof-of-concept needing further validation.

What remains to be done:

The discussion still sometimes overstates the clinical benefits while failing to fully account for the additional burden on the system. Adding a clear statement that using the current model could greatly increase clinical workload would make the paper more credible and better aligned with implementation science.

I strongly believe addressing these concerns will improve the paper.

Reviewer 4 – major revision

This is an important and well-motivated study. However, the use of a proxy HDP label, low precision, and lack of temporal modeling limit the reliability of the model’s predictions. The manuscript would benefit from more rigorous evaluation, expanded discussion of implementation challenges, and clearer framing of its current role as exploratory rather than deployment-ready.

The authors present a retrospective study applying machine learning (ML) to antenatal care (ANC) data collected from Tanzania’s Unified Community System (UCS) to predict hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP). They evaluate several classifiers, selecting XGBoost for deployment, reporting an AUC of 0.95 and 90.95% accuracy on unseen data (n >120,000). The goal is to demonstrate how digital health data can support early detection of HDP in lowresource environments. Both prior reviewers agree that the paper demonstrates relevance, ambition, and technical effort, but also exhibits substantial limitations in clinical labeling, model evaluation, temporal data usage, and real-world implementation claims. This final review consolidates those points, offering a full technical evaluation.

Major Issues

1. Use of Proxy Outcome Definition (HDP) Without Clinical Validation. The outcome label for HDP is derived solely from systolic and diastolic blood pressure thresholds (≥140/90 mmHg), without confirmation from diagnosis fields, medication data, or clinician annotations. This label construction is acknowledged as a proxy but remains fundamentally weak for building clinically trustworthy prediction models. Impact: This creates a risk of label noise, undermines model evaluation, and raises concerns about internal validity. Recommendation: While data limitations are acknowledged, the paper must reframe its scope from “early detection of HDP” to “classification of blood pressure risk states,” and clearly state the limits of proxy labeling in both methods and conclusions.

2. Severe Precision Deficit (14%) With Limited Operational Mitigation Both reviews noted that the model exhibits a 14% precision—indicating that most positive predictions are false positives. The authors attempt to justify this through high recall and potential for early warning, but there is no quantitative or operational analysis of the downstream clinical impact of such a high false positive burden in resourceconstrained settings. Impact: This challenges the model’s practical deployability and user trust. Recommendation: Authors must include a risk-benefit discussion, propose specific triaging protocols, or demonstrate via simulation how false positives would be handled without overwhelming staff or infrastructure.

3. Flattening of Temporal Data is a Structural Limitation. The decision to aggregate longitudinal ANC visit data into static per-patient feature vectors eliminates critical gestational progression patterns, which are central to HDP pathophysiology. This limitation is acknowledged but not sufficiently addressed. Impact: This structurally limits predictive accuracy and masks important trends such as rising BP trajectories or changes in proteinuria over time. Recommendation: Authors should either experiment with temporally-aware models or provide structured temporal feature engineering (e.g., BP slopes, deltas), and fully articulate the implications of the current model’s static nature.

4. Lack of External or Prospective Validation While the model is tested on a large test cohort (n = 120,232), no prospective, geographic, or temporal external validation is performed. Furthermore, predictions are not linked to actual delivery or maternal outcomes. Impact: Limits generalizability and weakens claims about readiness for deployment or policy use. Recommendation: The manuscript should temper claims regarding deployment and emphasize the need for a formal prospective validation study. A pilot phase or follow-up outcome analysis would significantly strengthen future versions.

5. Omission of Missing Data and Data Quality Protocols There is no description of missing data handling, which is critical in LMIC digital health datasets. Features such as glucose, protein in urine, and syphilis status are often missing or inconsistently recorded. Impact: Compromises the transparency, reproducibility, and robustness of the model. Recommendation: Authors must report missingness rates per feature, describe imputation or exclusion methods, and assess model sensitivity to data completeness.

Minor Issues

• Model threshold selection and calibration are not discussed. Threshold optimization (e.g., Youden Index, F1-maximization) under class imbalance should be detailed.

• Feature definitions are ambiguous in several places (e.g., “ANC risk flag,” “partner HIV status”); authors should provide a data dictionary or glossary.

• Use of ROC curves is insufficient under high class imbalance; precision-recall curves should be included.

• SHAP plots are informative but need clearer axis labels and clinical interpretation guidance.

• Terminological ambiguity between “HDP” and “gestational hypertension” must be resolved; either clarify or limit the scope of what the model is predicting.

Figures and Tables

Figures are well constructed but need improvements in labeling. A confusion matrix and precision-recall curve should be included. Tables summarizing missing data rates and class distribution across training, validation, and test sets are missing and should be provided. Methods Structurally adequate but lacking in key details:

• No account of data cleaning or outlier handling

• No missing data methodology

• No description of feature engineering or variable encoding

• Sampling strategy for balancing classes is not defined Study Design Appropriate as a retrospective feasibility study. However, the title and framing imply implementation readiness, which the current evidence does not support. A prospective or hybrid study design would be a more suitable next step.

Any Experiments Missing?

• External or temporal validation experiment

• Sensitivity analysis of label definitions • Subgroup analysis by gestational age or risk tier

• Threshold tuning experiment Statistics Baseline metrics are acceptable, but calibration (e.g., Brier score, reliability diagrams) is not assessed. Confidence intervals for key metrics (AUC, precision, recall) are missing.

Results and Data: Support Conclusions?

Partially. The technical performance is adequate, but the clinical conclusions and implementation claims outpace the underlying evidence.

All Data Accessible? No data or code sharing statement is present. Authors must clarify availability of de-identified data and model artifacts for reproducibility.

References

Sufficient for background and methodology. Authors may consider referencing clinical HDP prediction tools (e.g., fullPIERS, PREP-S) and ethical implications of ML in maternal health. Title

Accurate but overstated. Given the limitations in clinical outcome validation, a more accurate title would replace “Early Detection of Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy” with “Machine Learning Risk Classification of Elevated Blood Pressure in ANC Records.”

Reviewer 5 – reject

Recommendations:

1. The study uses blood pressure measurements to both define HDP and as the primary predictor variables, creating inherent circular reasoning. This undermines the core premise that machine learning adds value beyond simple threshold-based screening. The authors should either reframe this as a digital infrastructure feasibility study, obtain clinically confirmed HDP diagnoses for proper validation, or demonstrate true temporal prediction by using early visit data to predict HDP at later visits.

2. The title and abstract emphasize early detection, but the study design only demonstrates concurrent classification using current blood pressure readings, not prediction before clinical onset. The discordant case analysis shows that false positives have blood pressure just below diagnostic thresholds (136.5 vs 140 mmHg), which represents borderline detection rather than early warning. Authors should either demonstrate genuine early prediction using data from first trimester visits to predict later HDP, or reframe their contribution as "enhanced screening support" rather than early detection.

3. Excluding 73% of records (136,864 of 187,438) due to missing data represents a critical threat to generalizability that is inadequately discussed. Authors must provide a comparison table showing whether excluded records differ systematically from included records, analyze missingness patterns (MCAR vs MAR vs MNAR), and justify why complete-case analysis was chosen over imputation methods. This selection bias could severely limit real-world applicability and must be transparently reported with implications for model deployment.

4. While the authors justify prioritizing sensitivity, the 14% precision means 86% of flagged women do not actually have HDP, and the clinical workflow implications are unexplored. The manuscript needs to include baseline HDP prevalence in Tanzanian populations, describe the clinical pathway for managing flagged cases, estimate resource costs of investigating 12,603 alerts to find 1,725 true cases, and compare performance against existing standard-of-care detection rates. A sensitivity analysis showing model performance at different probability thresholds would also strengthen the clinical applicability discussion.

5. The decision to collapse multiple ANC visits into single records using different aggregation rules (means for BP, maximum for gestational age, most recent for categorical variables) appears arbitrary and destroys potentially valuable temporal patterns. Authors should provide explicit justification for each aggregation method, consider time-series approaches or visit-specific models as alternatives, and at minimum conduct sensitivity analyses using different aggregation strategies. This is particularly important since BP trends over pregnancy are clinically meaningful for HDP risk assessment.

6. The validation dataset (April-May 2024) comes from the same population, facilities, and data collection system as the training data (pre-April 2024), which tests temporal stability rather than true generalizability. Authors should add geographic or facility-level cross-validation, report stratified performance by region and patient demographics, and explicitly discuss how the model would perform in facilities without UCS infrastructure. Without this, claims about scalability and broader implementation remain unsubstantiated.

7. Given that BP dominates the model (SHAP values +7.94 and +7.62) and the outcome is defined by BP thresholds, the manuscript must explicitly demonstrate what additional value machine learning provides over simple clinical protocols. Authors should conduct an ablation study showing model performance when BP features are excluded entirely, compare detection rates against current Tanzanian ANC guidelines, and provide concrete examples of cases where the integration of BMI, proteinuria, and other factors identified at-risk women who would have been missed by BP screening alone. This is essential to justify the added complexity and resource investment of ML deployment.-->--> -->-->[Note: HTML markup is below. Even with improved transparency, the outcome definition remains limited by using BP thresholds, which are also the top predictors. The authors mention this, but they should make it clearer that the model’s performance does not show true risk prediction. Instead, it should be seen as improved screening in settings with noisy measurements. This point is still unclear. In addition, for the correlation analysis to detect collinearity, it assumes that the continuous variables have a linear relationship. What if this assumption is wrong? The authors need to state how they checked this.

Results

What was done well:

The Results section is now more honest and informative. The authors present confusion matrices and ROC curves, assess calibration, and carefully analyze cases where results differ. They show and discuss the big drop in precision during real-world validation. Feature importance is also explained in clinical terms. This addresses earlier concerns about overfitting and selective reporting.

What remains to be done:

The almost-perfect cross-validation results on the balanced training data are still presented without sufficient caution. Overfitting is mentioned, but the manuscript should clearly say that these results do not prove the model is better. Instead, they are likely due to under-sampling and how the labels were created.

Discussion and Conclusion

What was done well:

The discussion is more balanced and improved. The authors now address the effects of low precision in resource-limited systems and give step-by-step recommendations for piloting, improving data quality, training, and policy integration. The conclusion presents the work as a proof-of-concept needing further validation.

What remains to be done:

The discussion still sometimes overstates the clinical benefits while failing to fully account for the additional burden on the system. Adding a clear statement that using the current model could greatly increase clinical workload would make the paper more credible and better aligned with implementation science.

I strongly believe addressing these concerns will improve the paper.

Thank you.

Reviewer 4: Recommendation – major revision

Summary

The authors present a retrospective study applying machine learning (ML) to antenatal care (ANC) data collected from Tanzania’s Unified Community System (UCS) to predict hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP). They evaluate several classifiers, selecting XGBoost for deployment, reporting an AUC of 0.95 and 90.95% accuracy on unseen data (n >120,000). The goal is to demonstrate how digital health data can support early detection of HDP in low resource environments.

Both prior reviewers agree that the paper demonstrates relevance, ambition, and technical effort, but also exhibits substantial limitations in clinical labeling, model evaluation, temporal data usage, and real-world implementation claims. This final review consolidates those points, offering a full technical evaluation.

Major Issues

1. Use of Proxy Outcome Definition (HDP) Without Clinical Validation

The outcome label for HDP is derived solely from systolic and diastolic blood pressure thresholds (≥140/90 mmHg), without confirmation from diagnosis fields, medication data, or clinician annotations. This label construction is acknowledged as a proxy but remains fundamentally weak for building clinically trustworthy prediction models.

Impact: This creates a risk of label noise, undermines model evaluation, and raises concerns about internal validity.

Recommendation: While data limitations are acknowledged, the paper must reframe its scope from “early detection of HDP” to “classification of blood pressure risk states,” and clearly state the limits of proxy labeling in both methods and conclusions.

2. Severe Precision Deficit (14%) With Limited Operational Mitigation

Both reviews noted that the model exhibits a 14% precision—indicating that most positive predictions are false positives. The authors attempt to justify this through high recall and potential for early warning, but there is no quantitative or operational analysis of the downstream clinical impact of such a high false positive burden in resource constrained settings.

Impact: This challenges the model’s practical deployability and user trust.

Recommendation: Authors must include a risk-benefit discussion, propose specific triaging protocols, or demonstrate via simulation how false positives would be handled without overwhelming staff or infrastructure.

3. Flattening of Temporal Data is a Structural Limitation

The decision to aggregate longitudinal ANC visit data into static per-patient feature vectors eliminates critical gestational progression patterns, which are central to HDP pathophysiology. This limitation is acknowledged but not sufficiently addressed.

Impact: This structurally limits predictive accuracy and masks important trends such as rising BP trajectories or changes in proteinuria over time.

Recommendation: Authors should either experiment with temporally-aware models or provide structured temporal feature engineering (e.g., BP slopes, deltas), and fully articulate the implications of the current model’s static nature.

4. Lack of External or Prospective Validation

While the model is tested on a large test cohort (n = 120,232), no prospective, geographic, or temporal external validation is performed. Furthermore, predictions are not linked to actual delivery or maternal outcomes.

Impact: Limits generalizability and weakens claims about readiness for deployment or policy use.

Recommendation: The manuscript should temper claims regarding deployment and emphasize the need for a formal prospective validation study. A pilot phase or follow-up outcome analysis would significantly strengthen future versions.

5. Omission of Missing Data and Data Quality Protocols

There is no description of missing data handling, which is critical in LMIC digital health datasets. Features such as glucose, protein in urine, and syphilis status are often missing or inconsistently recorded.

Impact: Compromises the transparency, reproducibility, and robustness of the model.

Recommendation: Authors must report missingness rates per feature, describe imputation or exclusion methods, and assess model sensitivity to data completeness.

Minor Issues

1. Model threshold selection and calibration are not discussed. Threshold optimization (e.g., Youden Index, F1-maximization) under class imbalance should be detailed.

2. Feature definitions are ambiguous in several places (e.g., “ANC risk flag,” “partner HIV status”); authors should provide a data dictionary or glossary.

3. Use of ROC curves is insufficient under high class imbalance; precision-recall curves should be included.

4. SHAP plots are informative but need clearer axis labels and clinical interpretation guidance.

5. Terminological ambiguity between “HDP” and “gestational hypertension” must be resolved; either clarify or limit the scope of what the model is predicting.

Figures and Tables

Figures are well constructed but need improvements in labeling. A confusion matrix and precision-recall curve should be included. Tables summarizing missing data rates and class distribution across training, validation, and test sets are missing and should be provided.

Methods

1. Structurally adequate but lacking in key details:

2. No account of data cleaning or outlier handling

3. No missing data methodology

4. No description of feature engineering or variable encoding

5. Sampling strategy for balancing classes is not defined

Study Design

Appropriate as a retrospective feasibility study. However, the title and framing imply implementation readiness, which the current evidence does not support. A prospective or hybrid study design would be a more suitable next step.

1. Any Experiments Missing?

2. External or temporal validation experiment

3. Sensitivity analysis of label definitions

4. Subgroup analysis by gestational age or risk tier

5. Threshold tuning experiment

Statistics. Baseline metrics are acceptable, but calibration (e.g., Brier score, reliability diagrams) is not assessed. Confidence intervals for key metrics (AUC, precision, recall) are missing.

Results and Data: Support Conclusions? Partially. The technical performance is adequate, but the clinical conclusions and implementation claims outpace the underlying evidence.

All Data Accessible? No data or code sharing statement is present. Authors must clarify availability of de-identified data and model artifacts for reproducibility.

References. Sufficient for background and methodology. Authors may consider referencing clinical HDP prediction tools (e.g., fullPIERS, PREP-S) and ethical implications of ML in maternal health.

Title. Accurate but overstated. Given the limitations in clinical outcome validation, a more accurate title would replace “Early Detection of Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy” with “Machine Learning Risk Classification of Elevated Blood Pressure in ANC Records

Reviewer 5: Recommendation – reject

Recommendations:

1. The study uses blood pressure measurements to both define HDP and as the primary predictor variables, creating inherent circular reasoning. This undermines the core premise that machine learning adds value beyond simple threshold-based screening. The authors should either reframe this as a digital infrastructure feasibility study, obtain clinically confirmed HDP diagnoses for proper validation, or demonstrate true temporal prediction by using early visit data to predict HDP at later visits.

2. The title and abstract emphasize early detection, but the study design only demonstrates concurrent classification using current blood pressure readings, not prediction before clinical onset. The discordant case analysis shows that false positives have blood pressure just below diagnostic thresholds (136.5 vs 140 mmHg), which represents borderline detection rather than early warning. Authors should either demonstrate genuine early prediction using data from first trimester visits to predict later HDP, or reframe their contribution as "enhanced screening support" rather than early detection.

3. Excluding 73% of records (136,864 of 187,438) due to missing data represents a critical threat to generalizability that is inadequately discussed. Authors must provide a comparison table showing whether excluded records differ systematically from included records, analyze missingness patterns (MCAR vs MAR vs MNAR), and justify why complete-case analysis was chosen over imputation methods. This selection bias could severely limit real-world applicability and must be transparently reported with implications for model deployment.

4. While the authors justify prioritizing sensitivity, the 14% precision means 86% of flagged women do not actually have HDP, and the clinical workflow implications are unexplored. The manuscript needs to include baseline HDP prevalence in Tanzanian populations, describe the clinical pathway for managing flagged cases, estimate resource costs of investigating 12,603 alerts to find 1,725 true cases, and compare performance against existing standard-of-care detection rates. A sensitivity analysis showing model performance at different probability thresholds would also strengthen the clinical applicability discussion.

5. The decision to collapse multiple ANC visits into single records using different aggregation rules (means for BP, maximum for gestational age, most recent for categorical variables) appears arbitrary and destroys potentially valuable temporal patterns. Authors should provide explicit justification for each aggregation method, consider time-series approaches or visit-specific models as alternatives, and at minimum conduct sensitivity analyses using different aggregation strategies. This is particularly important since BP trends over pregnancy are clinically meaningful for HDP risk assessment.

6. The validation dataset (April-May 2024) comes from the same population, facilities, and data collection system as the training data (pre-April 2024), which tests temporal stability rather than true generalizability. Authors should add geographic or facility-level cross-validation, report stratified performance by region and patient demographics, and explicitly discuss how the model would perform in facilities without UCS infrastructure. Without this, claims about scalability and broader implementation remain unsubstantiated.

7. Given that BP dominates the model (SHAP values +7.94 and +7.62) and the outcome is defined by BP thresholds, the manuscript must explicitly demonstrate what additional value machine learning provides over simple clinical protocols. Authors should conduct an ablation study showing model performance when BP features are excluded entirely, compare detection rates against current Tanzanian ANC guidelines, and provide concrete examples of cases where the integration of BMI, proteinuria, and other factors identified at-risk women who would have been missed by BP screening alone. This is essential to justify the added complexity and resource investment of ML deployment.
