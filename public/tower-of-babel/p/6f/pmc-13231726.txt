Influence of comorbidities and patient demographics on Raynaud’s symptom characteristics: implications for diagnosis and management
Authors: Reshma Seomore, Matthew Wells, Ula Tymoszuk, Mithi Ahmed-Richards, Christopher P Denton, Francesco Del Galdo, Sue Farrington, Ariane L Herrick, Michael Hughes, Nick Jeffries-Owen, John D Pauling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAssociations between Raynaud’s phenomenon (RP) and body mass index (BMI), fibromyalgia syndrome (FMS) and migraine have been reported but their influence on the lived experience of RP is unknown.DesignCross-sectional electronic survey.SettingA social media-based awareness campaign organised by Scleroderma & Raynaud’s UK.Participants4141 respondents with RP, including 3279 with primary RP (PRP), 476 with systemic sclerosis-related RP (SSc-RP) and 386 with other systemic autoimmune rheumatic disease-related RP (SARD-RP).InterventionsNot applicable.Main outcome measuresRP symptom characteristics (colour change patterns, pain, numbness, tingling and thumb involvement) and their associations with BMI, FMS and migraine.ResultsIn PRP, low BMI correlated with higher prevalence of cyanosis (55.1% vs 41.2%), hyperaemia (48.2% vs 41.3%), triphasic change (30.4% vs 23.0%) and thumb involvement (36.0% vs 27.0%) compared with high BMI. In PRP, concomitant FMS was associated with higher prevalence of pain (77.7% vs 57.4%), cyanosis (53.8% vs 45.4%), tingling (72.6% vs 62.8%) and thumb involvement (46.2% vs 27.2%) compared with those without FMS. Higher prevalence of pain was reported by FMS respondents with SSc-RP (82.8% vs 69.9%) and SARD-RP (84.1% and 70.9%, respectively). In PRP, migraine was associated with higher prevalence of cyanosis (50.0% vs 44.9%), hyperaemia (50.3% vs 43.7%), pain (69.0% vs 56.2%) and thumb involvement (33.4% vs 27.1%). Migraine was associated with higher prevalence of hyperaemia and pain in SARD-RP.ConclusionBMI, FMS and migraine influence the lived experience of RP, particularly in PRP. FMS is associated with a greater pain burden, whereas low BMI and migraine are associated with prominent vasospastic features. These aetiopathogenic drivers influence RP symptomatology, with implications for management.Keywords: Diagnosis, Classification, Body Mass Index

Introduction

Raynaud’s phenomenon (RP) is a vasospastic disorder of the digital circulation characterised by episodic colour changes in response to cold or emotional stress.1 The classic sequence of blanching (white), cyanosis (blue) and hyperaemia (red) is often accompanied by pain and neurosensory symptoms (numbness and tingling).1 2 These sensory features contribute substantially to functional limitation and impaired quality of life irrespective of aetiology.1,3 RP affects an estimated 3%–5% of the European population, with a higher prevalence among women and those living in colder climates.4 Primary RP (PRP) is generally regarded as a benign condition, although symptoms can be persistent and disabling. The term secondary RP (SRP) is used when there is an underlying cause such as systemic autoimmune rheumatic diseases (SARDs), including systemic sclerosis (SSc), which is typically associated with more severe RP symptoms.3 Secondary forms of RP can be associated with systemic disease involvement, the potential for more severe digital (ischaemic) vascular complications and poorer long-term outcomes.5

In their seminal work on Raynaud’s from almost a century ago, Allen and Brown noted associations between RP symptoms and low weight and chronic pain disorders.6 Vasoconstriction is a key component of thermoregulation, and a low BMI has been linked to increased RP prevalence.7 FMS, characterised by chronic widespread pain and central sensory amplification, is often accompanied by RP symptoms, although microvascular imaging suggests normal morphology and digital perfusion in most patients.8 In SSc, coexistent FMS affects around a quarter (or more) of patients, worsening pain and reducing quality of life.9 10 The co-existence of migraine and RP has resulted in putative shared mechanisms of vascular hyperactivity and autonomic dysregulation.11,13

The influence of BMI, FMS and migraine on actual RP symptom burden across PRP, SSc-RP and other SARD-RP has not been significantly explored. Our objective was to evaluate how BMI, FMS and migraine influence RP symptom characteristics in distinct RP subpopulations.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional, patient-reported survey was conducted (14 November 2024 to 31 March 2025) as part of Be Part of the Answer, a social media-based awareness campaign organised by Scleroderma & Raynaud’s UK (SRUK). The campaign aimed to raise awareness of RP and related autoimmune conditions, while collecting valuable patient-reported information on symptoms, lifestyle factors and comorbidities. The content of the survey was developed by members of the SRUK team (UT, SF), with input from a patient insight partner (MA) and SSc experts (JP, AH, MH, FDG, CD). The 24-item online questionnaire comprised three themes.

Demographics

The survey captured respondent age (5-year brackets from 18 to 24 through ≥85), sex assigned at birth, ethnicity and country of residence.

Lifestyle

Respondents provided information on smoking status, alcohol consumption frequency, tea/coffee consumption frequency, physical exercise activities or sport frequency and self-reported height and weight for BMI calculation.

RP-related features and comorbidities

The survey captured information on family history of RP, self-diagnosed versus physician-diagnosed RP, age at first symptoms and colour change in response to emotional stress or feeling cold. There were questions pertaining to RP symptom characteristics with respect to colour changes (white, blue, red; biphasic or triphasic pattern), associated symptoms (pain, numbness, tingling) and thumb involvement. This was included because thumb involvement is uncommon in PRP and is a recognised indicator of secondary forms such as SSc.1 5 A family history of RP in a first or second-degree relative was recorded.

Classification of RP subtype and comorbidities

Self-report diagnoses of SSc, other SARDs (including systemic lupus erythematosus, mixed connective tissue disease, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, Sjögren’s syndrome, rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis) and associated comorbidities such as FMS and migraine were captured.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are summarised as frequencies and percentages. Associations of FMS and migraine with RP symptom burden were analysed using χ2 test of independence. Associations of BMI category (treated as an ordered categorical variable) with symptom characteristics were examined using the linear-by-linear association test to assess for any linear trends. Statistical significance was defined at p<0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (V.30).

Results

Cohort characteristics

Of 5266 individuals who accessed the survey landing page, 4141 (79%) provided complete responses and were included in the analysis (summarised in table 1). The final cohort comprised 3279 participants with PRP (79.2%), 476 with SSc-RP (11.5%) and 386 with SARD-RP (9.3%) (table 1). Most respondents were female (82.1%), of white ethnicity (95.8%) and the majority were based in the UK (95.4%). Fifty-two (1.3%) respondents did not report either blanching, cyanosis or hyperaemia, yet were included in the analysis. Analyses of FMS and migraine included the full cohort. For BMI analyses, 135 participants with missing or implausible anthropometric data were excluded, leaving 4006 valid cases, and age at onset of RP was available for 3761 participants. Overall, 318 (7.7%) participants reported FMS and 780 (18.8%) reported migraine. BMI distribution was as follows: low (≤19.9) in 15.3%, normal (20.0–24.9) in 49.2% and elevated (≥25.0) in 35.5%.

RP characteristics in different disease subgroups

SRP, particularly SSc was associated with significantly higher levels of pain, cyanosis, thumb involvement and triphasic digital colour changes compared with PRP (table 1). Numbness and blanching were more common in PRP (table 1). Consistent with this, supplementary analyses showed that almost half of individuals with SSc-RP exhibited triphasic colour changes (48.9%), whereas biphasic changes were less frequent (27.5%), highlighting the predominance of triphasic RP in SSc-RP (online supplemental table 1). In PRP, meanwhile, monophasic (a single colour change) RP was the most commonly reported RP characteristic (table 1).

BMI and symptom burden

In PRP, significant linear relationships were observed between increasing BMI category and reducing vasospastic severity, with reducing prevalence of cyanosis (55.1% vs 46.5% vs 41.2% respectively, p<0.001), hyperaemia (48.2% vs 46.3% vs 41.3%, p=0.004), triphasic colour change (30.4% vs 29.7% vs 23.0%, p<0.001) and thumb involvement (36.0% vs 26.4% vs 27.0%, p=0.004) with increasing BMI category (table 2). The inverse was observed for tingling, with prevalence increasing with increasing BMI category (60.1% vs 62.6% vs 65.8%, p=0.021). In SARD-RP, cyanosis (64.5% vs 58.7% vs 52.4%, p=0.084) and triphasic change (41.9% vs 33.3% vs 29.3%, p=0.079) showed weak, non-significant linear trends towards a higher prevalence at lower BMI. Lower BMI was also associated with a numerically, but statistically insignificant, higher levels of pain.

FMS and symptom burden

In PRP, respondents with FMS more frequently reported cyanosis (53.8% vs 45.4%, p=0.022), pain (77.7% vs 57.4%, p<0.001), tingling (72.6% vs 62.8%, p=0.006) and thumb involvement (46.2% vs 27.2%, p<0.001), while numbness (81.2% vs 88.0%, p=0.005) and blanching (86.8% vs 93%) were less frequently reported (table 3). In SSc-RP, FMS was associated with more frequent reports of RP-related pain (82.8% vs 69.9%, p=0.042). Similarly, in SARD-RP, FMS was associated with both higher prevalence of pain (84.1% vs 70.9%, p=0.03) and thumb involvement (50.8% vs 36.8%, p=0.038) (table 3).

Migraine and symptom burden

In PRP, migraine was associated with a higher prevalence of cyanosis (50.0% vs 44.9%, p=0.023), hyperaemia (50.3% vs 43.7%, p=0.003), pain (69.0% vs 56.2%, p<0.001) and thumb involvement (33.4% vs 27.1%, p=0.002) (table 4). There were no significant differences in blanching, triphasic colour change, numbness or tingling. In SSc-RP, migraine was associated only with higher prevalence of blanching (98.2% vs 89.3%, p=0.036). In SARD-RP, migraine was associated with higher prevalence of hyperaemia (65.0% vs 50.2%, p=0.009) and pain (82.5% vs 69.6%, p=0.011).

Family history of RP

A family history of RP affecting a first-degree or second-degree relative differed across subtypes and was most common in PRP (41.2%) compared with SSc-RP (26.5%) and SARD-RP (36%). There was no meaningful association between a family history of RP and the presence of FMS in PRP, SSc-RP or SARD-RP subgroups. In contrast, a stronger familial signal emerged in relation to migraine. Although family history of RP was numerically higher among PRP respondents with migraine (43.7% vs 40.6%) and among those with SSc-RP and migraine (34.5% vs 25.4%), the only statistically significant association was observed in SARD-RP, where migraine was markedly more common in those reporting a family history of RP (50.5% vs 30.7%, p<0.001) (table 4).

Discussion

This survey is one of the largest undertaken to date that explores factors influencing RP characteristics among primary and Secondary RP. Our findings are consistent with an earlier study comparing primary and secondary RP, although pain was lower in our subgroup with PRP compared with earlier work.5 We have identified significant associations between patient demographics and comorbidities, particularly in the context of PRP. Low BMI in PRP is associated with more predominant vasospastic features with a higher prevalence of cyanosis, hyperaemia, triphasic colour change and involvement of the thumb. Enhanced vascular reactivity among people with PRP and a lower BMI may reflect enhanced thermoregulatory sympathetic tone to conserve core temperature. In people with primary RP and a low BMI, treatment strategy may benefit from focussing on core temperature conservation. Traditional vasodilator treatments in people with low BMI are commonly poorly tolerated owing to low basal blood pressure. Across SSc-RP and SARD-RP, BMI did not impact on RP symptom characteristics to the same extent, possibly reflecting the immune-mediated vascular injury and fixed obliterative microangiopathy dominating symptom expression, irrespective of thermoregulatory factors such as low BMI.

FMS appears to be more common in people with both primary and secondary forms of RP than expected by the general population estimate of 5.4%.14 Among people with PRP, FMS is associated with a more polysymptomatic state and a higher prevalence of pain, in particular. These findings align with mechanisms of central sensory sensitisation and symptom amplification. In SRP, which is associated with a fixed immune-mediated vasculopathy, the presence of FMS was chiefly associated with a higher prevalence of pain. Previous studies have suggested normal capillary morphology and preserved digital vascular perfusion among people with PRP and FMS symptoms. In such patients, treatment of the underlying FMS and use of neuromodulatory agents to lessen cold sensitivity might be an effective treatment approach and negate the need for vasodilator drugs that might otherwise result in unwanted vasoactive side effects.

The prevalence of migraine among people with RP in our cohort was broadly consistent with UK estimates of around 3.5%,15 and is linked to more pronounced vasospastic features and pain, particularly in the context of PRP. In PRP, the presence of migraine may be a hitherto neglected clinical feature that indicates an underlying vasospastic tendency. A potential therapeutic challenge in such patients shall be managing the competing effects of interventions targeting each feature with vasodilator treatments sometimes resulting in headaches,16 and newer treatments for migraine such as those targeting calcitonin gene-related peptide being associated with the unmasking of RP symptoms.17 Intriguingly, while RP shows familial clustering across all groups, the strongest association with family history was observed in patients with SARD-RP with coexistent migraine. As migraine also commonly aggregates in families, these results may indicate shared familial or vascular susceptibility contributing to greater vasospastic features in RP.18 19

Our findings have implications for clinical practice and highlight the importance of subtype-specific and multidisciplinary approach to RP management (table 5).

Key strengths of the study include the large sample size and inclusion of major RP subtypes. A further strength of this study is its patient-led design (orchestrated by the UK’s leading patient organisation for people with RP), ensuring that the survey addressed priorities of people with RP. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the impact of BMI and comorbidities on RP symptom characteristics. The electronic anonymised survey design (distributed through the UK national charity for people with RP) resulted in an excellent response rate but the chief limitations of the study was the reliance on self-reported diagnoses, including FMS and migraine (with associated risk of misclassification), absence of objective vascular measures such as nailfold capillaroscopy and possible recruitment bias towards more symptomatic individuals.

Conclusion

BMI, FMS and migraine modify the lived experience of RP. In PRP, the presence of a low BMI, FMS and migraine are each associated with distinct RP symptom characteristics that might have important implications for management of PRP. The influence of these patient characteristics and comorbidities was more limited in SRP. Our findings would support a more nuanced, personalised approach to RP management that takes into consideration historically overlooked factors that may contribute to RP symptoms, such as thermoregulatory responses and pain sensitisation.
