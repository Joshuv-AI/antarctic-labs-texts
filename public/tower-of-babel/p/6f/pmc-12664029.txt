Unbiased Artificial Intelligence: Addressing Bias in Computational Pathology
Authors: Diana Montezuma, Rouven Porz, David Ameisen, Vincenzo L’Imperio, Mircea-Sebastian Serbanescu, Jordi Temprana-Salvador, Norman Zerbe, Nadieh Khalili, Inti Zlobec, European Society of Digital and Integrative Pathology (ESDIP)
Published: 2025

“People generally see what they look for, and hear what they listen for […]”.

Harper Lee, To Kill a Mockingbird

Our perspective, as individuals, is often shaped by our own biases and preconceived notions. Likewise, artificial intelligence (AI) models are shaped by the data they are trained on, inheriting the patterns and biases present within it. Just as human perception is influenced by experience, these models learn from a limited subset of the world, often failing to generalize beyond it. Computational pathology (CPath) is an evolving discipline at the intersection of pathology and computational sciences, encompassing the application of AI to the analysis of pathology images.1 As in other domains of medical imaging, such as radiology and dermatology, CPath is also susceptible to bias, defined, in the context of health care AI, as the systematic or unfair variations in model predictions across different patient populations that may lead to unequal care.2 In the near future, we will increasingly use algorithms in pathology, which, if implemented without oversight, can inadvertently perpetuate, or amplify, existing biases. These applications could then be discriminatory and jeopardize the fair diagnosis and treatment of patients. One might object that biases are entirely human, indeed genuinely human because we are always—consciously, but mostly unconsciously—entangled in biases through our experiences and prejudices toward others.3 However, machine learning models should be objective and generalizable, enabling conclusions that are applicable across diverse contexts. Herein we define bias within the specific context of CPath, provide a concise overview of recent original findings on bias in CPath and make the case for the need for further research in this specific field. Finally, as the European Society of Digital and Integrative Pathology, we propose a comprehensive approach to unbias AI in pathology.

What is Bias in CPath?

Bias in CPath can occur in subtle ways. Although pathology images may not overtly display characteristics associated with specific demographic characteristic groups, the data used to train AI models can still contain underlying biases. Bias, in this sense, refers to systematic unfair preferences within AI systems, potentially resulting in discriminatory outcomes.2 Computational biases in pathology have been broadly categorized into 3 types, according to the source of bias: data bias, development bias, and interaction bias.4 While a detailed description of the multiple types of CPath biases is beyond this commentary’s scope (see [4]), some concepts are important to highlight. Data biases occur when the dataset(s) used to train a CPath model are not diverse, causing the model to favor the majority subgroup, performing less accurately on underrepresented populations. Recently, Vaidya and colleagues reported unequal model performance across demographic characteristic groups using pathology images.5 Also, bias can derive from institution-specific information linked to the images but irrelevant for diagnosis, resulting in misleading associations (institution-specific bias).6,7 This was even highlighted in benchmarking of foundation models (FMs) trained on data from multiple institutes.8 Additionally, development biases can emerge during the models’ design process, where algorithm choices or training practices may favor certain patterns over others, affecting the model’s generalizability. Finally, interaction biases arise when users’ pre-existing expectations or overreliance on AI output can unintentionally reinforce existing model biases (confirmation and automation bias, respectively).9 In essence, bias in CPath arises from various sources, and understanding these biases is essential for fair and reliable CPath.

Bias and Generalization

In CPath, poor generalization is one of the most consequential and underappreciated sources of bias. Most AI models are developed on limited, often monocentric, datasets that fail to reflect the heterogeneity of real-world populations. These models may show high performance upon internal validation but underperform on external data—a phenomenon known as distribution shift.10 This lack of generalizability is not a simple technical footnote, it is an underlying structural cause of bias. Models trained on data from predominantly high-income, majority-ethnic institutions will underperform on data from underrepresented or lower-resource populations, perpetuating health care disparities.11 The FMs promise to overcome these limitations by enabling generalizable feature learning and few-shot adaptation.7 Yet, emerging evidence shows that FMs, particularly trained in a self-supervised fashion, are still susceptible to biases.7,8 Without guidance from domain-relevant data, FMs risk learning representations that amplify existing biases rather than diminishing them.8 Critically, CPath still lacks benchmarking and stratified evaluation standards to assess generalization performance across institutions/demographic characteristic subgroups. This makes it difficult to quantify whether a model is truly fair or merely overfitting to its training cohort. Addressing this gap requires demographic and institutional stratification in evaluation protocols, multi-institutional benchmarks, and fairness-aware training strategies. The FMs should not be blindly trusted; rather, they must be stress-tested for equity.

Why Address Bias in CPath?

In other medical domains, such as radiology, it has been long acknowledged that models can propagate existing biases in the data, causing performance inconsistencies between subgroups.12 Seyyed-Kalantari et al11 have reported that chest X-ray classifiers systematically under-diagnose Black patients, which may result in delayed care. Similarly, Yang Y et al,13 reported systematic underdiagnosis of various pathologies in intersectional subgroups such as Black female patients. This is also true for other imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance imaging and computer tomography scans, where AI models have exhibited diagnostic disparities across different demographic groups. However, this type of research remains largely underexplored in CPath, where similar biases can impact diagnostic accuracy and fairness. Vaidya et al5 showed that state-of-the-art models can exhibit relevant performance disparities across racial and income-linked subgroups. A performance gap between Black and White patients was consistently reported across all tested tasks (3% for breast cancer subtyping, 10.9% for lung cancer subtyping, and 16.0% for mutation prediction in glioma cases). Moreover, they observed that the inequity extended to other demographic characteristic variables, such as median household income and age, with poorer performance in patients from low-income areas.5 Aside from this study, original research on demographic characteristic bias in CPath remains scarce, and most existing studies, albeit also limited, focus on other forms of bias, such as institutional and interaction biases. Recent studies have investigated the use of synthetic images to enhance generalizability in pathology tasks, while mitigating bias. Ketna et al14 have investigated the degradation of Deep Learning systems’ performance in pathology samples, and in chest X-ray and dermatology images. They showed that models trained on data from certain hospitals exhibited reduced accuracy when applied to data from other institutions and that using synthetic images led to improved accuracy and fairness in underrepresented groups.14 Van Booven et al15 have also used synthetic data to improve Gleason grading accuracy in prostate cancer, reducing bias in this setting. Just recently, Rosbach et al16 published the first study to report the existence of confirmation bias in the CPath domain. Addressing bias is not only important due to the possible unfairness, neglecting it can also lead to poorer models: recent investigations have shown that The Cancer Genome Atlas, one of the most widely used datasets in histopathology, may contain site-specific characteristics that may introduce spurious correlations. As such, CPath models can be relying on these site-specific features, rather than the underlying biological characteristics, leading to overoptimistic performance and ultimately compromising their clinical value.17,18 Aside from the above-mentioned studies, and a few other examples, such as,6, 7, 8,18, 19, 20, 21 there persists a considerable gap of original research and comprehensive datasets addressing bias in CPath, namely on biases related to patient-level characteristics. This issue has also been highlighted by Hanna MG et al,4 who urged stakeholders to address bias in pathology data to build more inclusive and equitable AI systems.4 However, overcoming certain aspects of bias may require that more detailed patient-level information is collected, which could conflict with current research ethics standards. To truly understand and mitigate bias, researchers need to be able to compare the data with metadata, such as i.e. race, ethnicity, age, and pre-existing conditions. However, these correlations are often not compatible with true anonymization of patient data. A rethink of the fundamental concepts is warranted and only achievable through more interdisciplinary cooperation, including experts in the field of research ethics.

Overcoming Bias

Addressing bias in CPath demands a systematic, interdisciplinary effort. Paths to overcoming bias can be broadly categorized into 3 major categories (Figure 1): representative data, multidisciplinary approach and computational strategies, detailed below.

Overcoming bias in computational pathology: a summary of suggested approaches.

Representative Data

Bias mitigation begins with multi-institutional datasets, to ensure the inclusion of data from potentially underrepresented groups.5 Having protocols for standardization of laboratory workflows (eg, tissue staining) would further reduce bias and enhance generalizibility.5,18 Public datasets play an important role; however, many currently lack demographic characteristic data. Therefore, improved access to public datasets and importantly, to patient-level metadata, while preserving patient privacy, would allow for person-related potential biases to be considered early during model development stages. Additionally, while representative data can help mitigate bias, in many real-world scenarios, particularly across low-income and middle-income countries, access to diverse data can be limited. Domain generalization, (which aims to improve model robustness to unseen data by learning domain-invariant representations during training) can be a useful strategy to promote equitable performance when certain populations are underrepresented.

Multidisciplinary Approach

Gathering perspectives from multidisciplinary experts at all levels of the AI lifecycle reduces bias.22 Collaborative partnerships between pathologists, AI experts, researchers, as well as ethicists and policymakers should be encouraged. Regulations that balance the needs for data protection and data privacy with bias-auditable research must be established.

Computational Strategies

Data-Level Techniques

Bias already begins at the dataset. Many CPath models are trained on monocentric datasets lacking diversity and perpetuating performance disparities. Corrective action involves balanced sampling, targeted data augmentation, and, in the absence of real-world diversity, synthetic data generation to fill the gaps.14

Bias-Resistant Model Design

Beyond data, models must be explicitly designed to resist bias. Adversarial regularization can suppress the encoding of sensitive attributes; contrastive learning across subgroups promotes shared representations that are not tied to demographic characteristic identifiers. Additional training techniques, which disentangle biologically relevant signals from confounders such as scanner type or institutional metadata.5,23 Fairness-aware loss functions offer a principled way to optimize equity alongside accuracy.

Bias Evaluation Metrics and Post-Hoc Techniques

Benchmarking is a well-established practice in AI24; however, to the best of our knowledge, there are currently no benchmarks specifically designed to evaluate bias in CPath. The evaluation must go beyond aggregate metrics, as bias can remain undetected unless performance is stratified by subgroups, exposing disparities that affect real-world applicability. In segmentation, overlap-based metrics like the Dice Similarity Coefficient penalize small structures more. Because organ sizes differ by sex, identical errors yield lower Dice Similarity Coefficients for women. Thus, bias may stem not only from data or models but also from evaluation metrics themselves. Also, tools such as the Robustness Index8 can quantify model reliance on confounding versus biological features. Furthermore, explainability is crucial, helping to uncover the hidden patterns and to facilitate the understanding of AI decisions by clinicians.

Federated Learning and Collaborative Infrastructure

Federated learning (FL) enables an infrastructure for training AI models without centralizing data. It allows multiple centers to contribute while preserving patient privacy. Through decentralized training and techniques such as differential privacy, FL supports broad collaboration without exposing sensitive data, promoting more inclusive development pipelines.23 Nonetheless, current FL approaches often lack bias mitigation strategies. Common practices such as averaging model weights across sites can inadvertently propagate existing biases. Therefore, FL systems must be fairness-aware by design, explicitly monitoring performance across sites and demographic characteristic subgroups to avoid reinforcing the very disparities they aim to mitigate.

Call to Action

Although this work focuses on specific dimensions of bias in CPath, other important issues (unequal access to CPath tools and constraints of static datasets) also warrant attention. With this Commentary, we aim to make a call to action for greater awareness and original research on bias in the specific field of CPath. Addressing bias in CPath, which is already underway in other medical fields,13 is essential for developing AI models that provide clinically meaningful results across diverse populations. If left unchecked, bias can reinforce existing inequalities in health care, disproportionately affecting underrepresented groups.25 Only by making a collective effort, involving academia, industry, policymakers, and regulators, will we be able to unbias CPath and see beyond what we merely look for.
