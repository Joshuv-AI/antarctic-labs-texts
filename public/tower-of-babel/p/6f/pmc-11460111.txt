Predictors of genital self–image in Iranian women: a cross–sectional study
Authors: Mina Malary, Zeinab Hamzehgardeshi, Afsaneh Keramat, Masoud Yunesian, Maryam Farjamfar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimSeveral factors influence women’s perception of their genitalia. Understanding the elements associated with genital self‑image (GSI) can significantly contribute to enhancing women’s sexual experiences. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify the predictors of GSI in Iranian women.Materials and methodsThis online cross-sectional study was conducted on 722 healthy married reproductive-aged women with records in Amol-Iran health centers during 2022. Participants were selected using a random sampling method from all the healthcare centers. Data were collected through a checklist of socio-demographic and obstetrics information, the Female Genital Self-Image Scale (FGSI), the 6-item Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI-6), the Female Sexual Distress Scale – revised (FSDS-R), and the Multidimensional Sexual Self-Concept Questionnaire (MSSCQ) for assessing sexual esteem and sexual satisfaction. The general linear model was employed to assess the impact of each independent variable (socio-demographic and obstetrics characteristics, sexual function, sexual distress, sexual esteem, and sexual satisfaction) on the dependent variable (GSI).ResultsThe mean score of GSI was 21.70 ± 3.82. Based on the Pearson correlation test, there were significant correlations between GSI and sexual function, sexual esteem, sexual satisfaction, and sexual distress (r = 0.20, r = 0.43, r = 0.42, r = -0.28 respectively; p < 0.001). Using the adjusted general linear model, with the increasing scores of sexual esteem and sexual satisfaction, the GSI score also increased (β = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.27, β = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.22, respectively; p < 0.001). Additionally, age, abnormal vaginal discharge, genital feedback from partners, and satisfaction with body image were identified as other predictors of GSI.ConclusionBased on the study results, it was observed that sexual esteem, sexual satisfaction, and certain socio-demographic variables served as predictors of GSI. Consequently, considering the potential impact of GSI on various aspects of women’s lives, healthcare providers can enhance their counseling techniques to improve GSI by gaining an appropriate understanding of the factors that influence it.Keywords: Genital self-image, Sexual function, Sexual distress, Sexual esteem, Sexual satisfaction

Introduction

Genital self-image (GSI) is recognized as a crucial component of sexual health [1]. It forms an integral part of body image and reflects an individual’s attitudes and emotions towards their own genitalia, as well as their experiences related to it [2]. GSI encompasses the evaluations women make about the overall characteristics of their genitals [3], including factors such as texture and pubic hair distribution, genitalia odor and taste, vaginal tightness, size, and symmetry of labia minora, and genital function [4–6].

Some cultural norms consider female genital parts as taboo organs, leading to feelings of shame and repugnance associated with these body parts [7]. Within such cultural norms, there is a belief that female genitalia should be tight, small, and almost invisible [7]. The proliferation of pornographic content, facilitated by technological advancements, further influences perceptions of idealized genital appearances [8]. As a result, women may be at risk of developing negative GSI due to cultural and social impacts, including media imagery and trends in cosmetic surgeries [8]. The prevalence of negative GSI can lead to an increase in Female Genital Cosmetic Surgery (FGCS) [4], which has become increasingly popular in recent times [9]. Although obtaining exact numbers of FGCS in Iran is challenging due to a lack of comprehensive data, there is a notable demand for FGCS, often driven by the desire to improve sexual function [10]. Sexual functioning contrasts with sexual dysfunction. Sexual dysfunction is a condition that hinders individuals from feeling content or fulfilled during sexual activity [11] and it includes disorders of desire, arousal, orgasm, and sexual pain [12]. For example, the dominant belief in the importance of achieving orgasms during sex places pressure on women, implying that their genitals are inadequate if they do not regularly achieve (multiple) orgasms [13]. As a result, some women who struggle to achieve orgasm or have difficulty experiencing multiple orgasms may feel insecure about their genitals and turn to cosmetic surgery as a solution. According to Goodman et al., there is a temporary increase in sexual arousal and satisfaction following cosmetic surgery, but these levels gradually decrease to the preoperative level after a few months of follow-up. Furthermore, sexual desire, lubrication levels, and orgasm do not show improvement post-surgery [14]. Hence, the effectiveness of FGCS is is questionable as there is insufficient evidence to support its effectiveness and safety [15].

Until now, limited empirical investigations have been conducted to analyze the predictors of GSI, indicating the need for a comprehensive study in this area. Past research has explored the association between GSI and various dimensions of sexual health. Negative GSI has been linked to reduced sexual esteem [6, 8, 16] and higher sexual distress [1, 6]. Additionally, some studies have suggested that females with better GSI tend to experience higher levels of sexual function and sexual satisfaction [8, 17]. However, there are contradictory findings among past researchers. Some studies have not found significant relationships between genital perceptions and different dimensions of sexual experience, including sexual satisfaction and dimensions of sexual functioning like arousal, lubrication, orgasm, and pain [18]. These conflicting results may be attributed to differences in cultural and societal contexts, variations in the evaluated target groups, and discrepancies in the measurement instruments used for the variables.

Raising awareness about the factors influencing women’s perception of their genitalia characteristics, considering regional and cultural contexts, is a vital aspect of planning and implementing effective sexual health counseling sessions. It has been stated in the literature that culture plays a fundamental role in the formation of appearance ideals and also beliefs, attitudes, and values toward sexuality that differ for women from different cultures [19, 20]. Sexuality is an intimate and individual aspect of a person’s life, which can be influenced by a range of societal, religious, ethical, and legal standards and restrictions [21]. Cultural standards portray female genitalia as a forbidden topic that should not be discussed, often associating it with feelings of shame and revulsion [7]. This is maybe more apparent in the Middle East region, especially Iran, which faces many challenges and resistances in communication about sexual matters and sexual education due to traditions and cultural sensitivities [22]. In this cultural context, having and keeping an intact hymen until marriage and in general restrained sexuality is considered a value in women [20, 23]. In addition, religious beliefs and the patriarchal framework of Iranian society provide an important context for many gender disparities between men and women such as different rights of heritage, divorce, witnessing, and blood money [23]. So, it seems these factors interact in complex ways to shape women’s perceptions of genitalia.

Although some components that contribute to poor GSI may be universal; due to differences in cultural values in societies and the characteristics of an ideal sexual body may differ in various countries. Therefore, it is essential to address this aspect to provide appropriate and culturally sensitive support. Given the lack of standard population-based research in the Iranian setting, the present online cross-sectional study was designed to explore the predictors of GSI.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study is part of a protocol study conducted by Malary et al. [24]. The participants were 722 healthy married reproductive-aged women recruited from all the women’s health centers by simple random sampling in Amol, Iran, from March to June 2022. The city of Amol, which is located in the northern part of Iran country, is known for its traditional and conservative culture, where modesty and privacy are highly valued. Moreover, Amol is a region where the majority of the population adheres to Islamic principles, which have a significant impact on gender roles, relationships, and perceptions of sexual body image. In Iranian society, there is an emphasis on modesty and purity, and discussions about sexuality and gsi are often considered taboo. This cultural context may influence women’s perception of their genitals and influence how they view themselves in relation to societal ideals of beauty and femininity. Furthermore, the patriarchal nature of Iranian society may contribute to women feeling pressure to conform to certain standards of attractiveness and femininity, which can impact their self-perception and gsi. These cultural norms and values within the specific area of Amol, Iran, therefore, have the potential to influence how Iranian women perceive their genitals and contribute to variations in genital self-image among different groups of women in this region.

Inclusion criteria of the study included being in reproductive age (between 15 and 49 years), being married, being sexually active, residence in Amol, smartphone access, and providing informed consent to participate. Women with the following criteria were not included in the study: pregnant women, women in the breastfeeding period, women with premature menopause, women with a history of genital surgeries, genital cancers, and mental disorders like depression, anxiety, obsession (all of these criteria were considered based on self-report).

In the current research, the sample size was estimated using the formula for the estimation of a mean in a single population. A 95% confidence level was selected, corresponding to a z-value of 1.96. The mean score of GSI was derived from a previous study by Smith et al. [4], which reported a mean (SD) GSI score of 22.96 (SD = 3.9). Considering a desired precision (d) of 0.03 around the mean, and accounting for potential losses of 10%, a standard sample size of 722 participants was determined.

Sampling

Sampling was carried out after obtaining approval from the ethics committee of Shahroud University of Medical Sciences (Code of ethics: IR.SHMU.REC.1398.097). The target population for this study consisted of married reproductive-aged women who were covered by Amol health centers. Amol has a total of 19 health centers, and all these centers were utilized for sampling purposes. The distribution of married reproductive-aged women across these health centers varied. In the sampling process, probability proportional to size sampling was employed. This method involved selecting a sample of married women in the reproductive age range (15–49 years) based on the number of women covered by each health center. The number of samples selected from each health center was determined based on the number of women covered by that specific center. These individuals were then selected by simple random sampling method to participate in the study. Once eligible participants were identified, the researcher established contact with them via phone. During this telephone arrangement, the study’s objectives and methods were explained to the potential participants, and they were reassured about the confidentiality of their data. For data collection, a questionnaire link was sent via text message to those who expressed their willingness to participate. Participants were required to choose the consent confirmation option before proceeding to complete the questionnaires. If they did not provide consent, they were unable to participate in the study.

Data collection tools

The data for this research were collected using the following data collection tools: a socio-demographic and obstetrics checklist, The Female Genital Self-Image Scale (FGSIS), Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI-6), Female Sexual Distress Scale – revised (FSDS R), and the Multidimensional Sexual Self-Concept Questionnaire for assessing sexual esteem and sexual satisfaction.

The socio-demographic and obstetrics history included information about the following variables: age, duration of marriage, level of education, occupation, family income, number of pregnancies, history of abortion, history of infertility, abnormal vaginal discharge, genital skin lesions, history of sexual abuse, satisfaction with body image, pornography exposure, and genital feedback from sexual partners and healthcare providers.

Genital self-image

GSI was assessed using the well-validated and reliable FGSIS, developed by Herbenick and Reece in 2010. The FGSIS consists of 7 items that measure global female GSI, such as feelings of positivity towards one’s genitals (e.g., “I feel positively about my genitals”). Participants rated their agreement with each statement on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (4). The outcome of FGSIS is a total sum score with a range from 7 to 28 with higher scores indicating a more positive GSI. In a study conducted by Felix et al., the Cronbach’s Alpha for the FGSIS was reported to be 0.81, indicating high reliability [25]. Additionally, the Persian version of the FGSIS demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha and test-retest reliability of 86% [26].

Sexual functioning

Sexual functioning was assessed using the 6-item Female Sexual Functioning Index (FSFI-6). The FSFI-6 is a 6-item, brief, and self-administered instrument derived from the original 19-item FSFI, which is designed to measure female sexual function [27]. The FSFI-6 comprises six domains: desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain. The items related to desire and satisfaction are rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 to 5, while the other items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from 0 to 5. Total scores on the FSFI-6 range from 2 to 30, with lower scores indicating poorer sexual functioning. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of this instrument were confirmed by Maroufizadeh et al., with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.85 [28].

Sexual distress

Sexual distress was assessed using the validated and reliable Female Sexual Distress Scale – revised (FSDS R). The FSDS-R is a self-administered questionnaire that was validated by Derogatis et al. The questionnaire consists of 13 questions in English, and participants respond to each question on a scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always) [29]. The total score on the FSDS-R ranges from 0 to 52, with higher score indicating sexual distress [30, 31]. The original version of the FSDS-R has demonstrated acceptable reliability, with α-Cronbach values ranging from 0.87 to 0.93. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of this tool have been examined and confirmed by Nekoo et al., with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.70 or higher [32].

Sexual esteem and sexual satisfaction

The Multidimensional Sexual Self-Concept Questionnaire (MSSCQ) is a 101-item self-report measure used to assess sexual health. However, the Persian version of this tool was streamlined to 78 items [33]. For our study, we utilized subscales that measured sexual esteem and sexual satisfaction, each containing four and five items, respectively. Participants rated the items on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (not at all characteristic of me) to 5 (very much characteristic of me). Subscale scores were computed by averaging the item scores, with higher scores indicating increased endorsement of the construct for all items [8]. The validity and reliability of the Persian version of this instrument were examined and confirmed by Ziaei et al., who reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.88 and a reliability index exceeding 0.70. Additionally, the Cronbach alphas for sexual esteem and sexual satisfaction were also acceptable (α = 0.88, α = 0.93, respectively) [33]. For our total sample, the Cronbach alpha for sexual esteem was 0.87, and for sexual satisfaction, it was 0.94, both of which were considered acceptable.

Data analysis

For data analysis, the SPSS statistical software (version 25) was used. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean ± SD values, and minimum/maximum values, were used to present the socio-demographic data. At first, we ensured that the assumptions of linearity, independence, homoscedasticity, and normality of residuals were met by visual inspection, measures of Skewness and Kurtosis, diagnostic plots such as scatterplots and residual plots, statistical tests like the Shapiro-Wilk test, etc. These rigorous checks and validations were conducted to ensure the validity and reliability of the linear regression analysis. In the next step, we used univariate analysis for the relation of each variable with GSI. A Pearson linear correlation test was conducted to evaluate the associations between GSI (FGSIS total scores) and the sexual variables (i.e., sexual function, sexual distress, sexual esteem, and sexual satisfaction), as well as age and duration of marriage. To explore the relationship between other socio-demographic characteristics and GSI, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and independent t-test were utilized. Finally, the general linear model was used to predict the effect of each independent variable (demographic/contextual and sexual variables) on the dependent variable (GSI). In the univariate analysis, variables with a P-value of less than 0.25 were considered significant and selected for inclusion in the general linear model. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was set as the level of significance.

Results

In this study, 722 women were included, and data were collected from March to June 2022. Briefly, the participants had a mean age of 31.33 ± 5.59 years, and all of them were married, with a marriage duration ranging from 1 to 30 years (mean = 7.20 ± 5.19 years). Approximately half of the women (46.1%) had experienced pregnancy. Regarding occupation, around half of the women (51.8%) were housewives. Most of the participants had completed a university degree, with 60.7% having a undergraduate degree and 25.8% having a postgraduate degree. In terms of monthly family income, more than half of the women (57.3%) reported that their income was relatively sufficient. In the end, the adjusted general linear model was employed to establish a validated association model between GSI and the variables that were found to be significant (p < 0.25) during univariate analysis in Tables 1 and 2 (including women age, family income adequacy, number of pregnancies, abnormal vaginal discharge, genital skin lesions, sexual esteem, sexual satisfaction, sexual distress, sexual function, history of sexual abuse, satisfaction with body image, genital feedback from sexual partners and negative feedback from healthcare provider). The results of the adjusted general linear model indicated that several variables had a statistically significant relationship with the overall score of GSI (p < 0.05). Specifically, in women with higher sexual esteem (β = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.10 to 0.27; p < 0.001) and higher sexual satisfaction (β = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.06 to 0.22; p < 0.001), GSI was increased, implying a better perception of genitalia. Additionally, higher GSI scores were found among older women (β = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.002 to 0.092; p < 0.041), those who reported higher levels of satisfaction with body image (β = 3.66, 95% CI: 2.94 to 4.37; p < 0.001), and those who received positive genital feedback from sexual partners (β = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.30 to 1.33; p = 0.002). On the other hand, lower (worse) FGSI scores were observed among women receiving negative genital feedback from sexual partners (β = -1.72, 95% CI: -3.09 to -0.34; p = 0.014) and those who reported abnormal vaginal discharge in the last month (β = -0.83, 95% CI: -1.33 to -0.32; p = 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

The findings of the present study revealed significant correlations between the overall score of GSI and various sexual variables. Specifically, there was a positive correlation with sexual satisfaction, sexual esteem, and sexual function, indicating that as sexual satisfaction, sexual esteem, and sexual function scores increased, GSI also improved. On the other hand, there was a significant inverse correlation between the score of GSI and sexual distress, meaning that as the score of sexual distress increased, the score of GSI decreased, indicating a poorer GSI. Moreover, women’s age, abnormal vaginal discharge, genital feedback from sexual partners, and satisfaction with body image were found to be predictors of GSI.

In the present study, two factors related to GSI were identified as sexual function and sexual distress, although they were not statistically significant as predictors of GSI in the adjusted general linear model. It is noteworthy that numerous studies have reported the correlation between GSI and sexual function, and also sexual distress [17, 26, 34, 35]. For instance, Andersen et al. [36] found that a more positive GSI serves as a protective factor against sexual dysfunctions. Similarly, a study conducted with 421 women (18–60 years) in Brazil demonstrated a bidirectional interaction between GSI and sexual function, with evidence of the effect of GSI on sexual function and vice versa [37]. However, there are some conflicting findings in the literature. For instance, Berman et al. reported that GSI was not associated with overall sexual function [18]. These discrepancies in study findings could be attributed to differences in the study populations, assessment tools, and various cultural or contextual factors. Regarding sexual distress, it was reported that a positive GSI was associated with lower levels of sexual distress [18]. According to another study, there was a decrease of 1.24 units in the sexual distress score for each 1-unit increase in the GSI score [1]. On the other hand, while sexual problems and sexual distress can occur independently, the likelihood of them both occurring simultaneously is higher [38]. Therefore, addressing the impact of these two factors in sex counseling sessions is imperative for effectively managing poor GSI.

In our study, sexual satisfaction and sexual esteem emerged as two predictors of GSI, with women having higher (better) sexual satisfaction and sexual esteem scores being at a lower risk for poor GSI. These results are consistent with findings from other studies. For instance, a study conducted on infertile women in Iran also demonstrated a significant relationship between sexual satisfaction and GSI [39]. Another study explored the relationship between female GSI and sexual dissatisfaction, revealing that negative GSI had both a direct effect and an indirect effect (via sexual low self-esteem) on sexual dissatisfaction [40]. Furthermore, research suggests that individuals who maintain a positive GSI, regardless of gender or sexual orientation, are more likely to experience greater sexual esteem [41]. Additionally, Schick et al. found that greater dissatisfaction with genital appearance was associated with higher genital image self-consciousness during physical intimacy, which, in turn, was linked to lower sexual esteem and sexual satisfaction [8]. These findings highlight the significant impact of sexual variables on women’s perception of their genitalia. Sexual variables appear to interact with each other in complex ways, their manifestations varying among individuals based on the cultural context of a society and the values, attitudes, and beliefs that are internalized. Hence, by dispelling taboos and rectifying common misconceptions and myths surrounding sexuality, it is likely that an improvement in one sexual variable could influence and enhance other sexual variables. As such, it is crucial not to underestimate the significant role of sexual factors when managing and caring for women with poor Genital Self-Image (GSI), taking into consideration the prevailing cultural context of society.

in our study, we identified female abnormal vaginal discharge as another predictor factor of GSI scores. Women who reported experiencing abnormal vaginal discharge in the last month obtained lower FGSI scores (indicating poor GSI), highlighting the potential impact of vaginal infections on GSI. This could be due to a plausible association between genital image and gynecological screening behaviors, as it has been observed that some women may skip pelvic exams due to concerns about their genitals being seen by healthcare providers during the examination [42]. Such delays could result in the underdiagnosis and inadequate treatment of conditions like genital infections, ultimately exacerbating poor GSI. This may be more prominent in Iran due to cultural and societal restrictions and strong taboos or stigmas surrounding discussion related to sexual organs, leading to discomfort or reluctance in seeking necessary screenings. However, further research and analysis specific to the Iranian context would be needed to understand the underlying factors contributing to this trend fully. Furthermore, it is important to note that one of the domains of the FGSI scale examines female perception of genital odor. However, it is crucial to consider that women’s perception of malodorous genitalia may be linked to poor GSI only when this odor is not caused by vaginal infections, such as bacterial vaginosis (BV). Therefore, to ensure the accuracy of responses to this question, a physical examination of the pelvis would be necessary to rule out vaginal infections and exclude such cases from the study. Unfortunately, this physical examination was not feasible in our study due to its online nature, which was considered one of the limitations.

We found that genital feedback from sexual partners had a significant impact on women’s perceptions and feelings about their genitals. Specifically, positive feedback was directly correlated with higher GSI scores. On the other hand, negative feedback was inversely associated with GSI scores. This highlights the importance of sexual partners’ feedback in influencing women’s perception of their own genitals. Positive or negative feedback from sexual partners may be particularly more impactful in cultures where open discussions about sexuality are less common, as Positive feedback can validate and reinforce positive feelings about one’s genitals, and negative feedback or criticism could potentially amplify feelings of insecurity or inadequacy about one’s genitals. Qualitative studies have also explored the role of sexual partners in providing feedback to women about their genitals [43–45]. In an examination of psychosocial factors associated with GSI, positive genital feedback was found to be a critical predictor of GSI. However, it is worth noting that the GSI scores of women who received negative feedback from their partners did not differ significantly from those who did not receive such feedback. This could be due to the limited number of women in the negative feedback category, as suggested by Fudge et al. [46]. Therefore, further quantitative studies with a sufficient sample size are required to establish a more definitive relationship between negative feedback and GSI. On the other hand, it is important to consider the cultural nuances and societal expectations when interpreting the impact of genital feedback from sexual partners on women’s perceptions of their genitals. Our finding makes a notable contribution to understanding the psychosocial factors influencing women’s GSI, highlighting the potential value of incorporating partner feedback in interventions aimed at improving GSI in the Iranian setting.

The level of satisfaction with body image, which refers to the perception of one’s whole body, was another significant predictor of FGSI scores in our study. This finding is consistent with past research that has also demonstrated a direct correlation between GSI and body image. Females who have a more negative perception of their overall bodies tend to extend similar feelings towards their genitals [6, 45, 47, 48]. In other words, how a woman feels about her body may influence how she perceives her genitals. Women who have generally negative feelings about themselves are more likely to have negative feelings about their genitals as well. It was shown that society forces women to follow beauty standards. Moreover, Gender roles often characterize beauty as a feminine attribute and impose certain beauty standards upon women. These standards, frequently unrealistic, can contribute to feelings of body dissatisfaction among women [49]. Traditional attitudes toward gender roles may contribute to the development of dysfunctional appearance schemas in women. These dysfunctional beliefs emphasize the importance of appearance in personal achievement, self-concept, and interpersonal relationships, potentially leading to a reduced level of satisfaction with body image [50]. on the other hand, how a woman feels about her body may influence how she perceives her genitals. Women who have generally negative feelings about themselves are more likely to have negative feelings about their genitals as well. Therefore, our findings underscore the importance of challenging societal beauty standards and gender roles and addressing body image satisfaction in interventions aimed at improving FGSI.

In the present study, although age did not show a significant association with GSI in the univariate analysis, it emerged as a predictor of GSI when included in the multiple regression model. Specifically, with advancing age, the GSI score increased, indicating a more positive perception of GSI in older women. This finding aligns with a survey conducted by Rowen et al. (2018) in the US, which also indicated that women of older age were less likely to report poor GSI [16]. However, it is worth noting that most previous studies did not show a clear relationship between age and GSI. The conflicting findings across studies could be attributed to various factors, including differences in the range of age among participants, cultural variations, and the statistical analyses used. Older women may have a more positive perception of their genitals compared to younger women due to reasons such as experience and maturity, changes in priorities, confidence and self-acceptance, and cultural influences. Experience and maturity can lead to a more positive self-image while shifting priorities to internal qualities can enhance perception. Confidence and self-acceptance can develop over time, leading to a positive view of genitals. Cultural norms may also shape perceptions, with some cultures associating aging with wisdom and self-acceptance.

In summary, our finding indicate a strong link between GSI and sexual satisfaction, esteem, and function, which supports several psychological theories. According to these theories, our self-image and self-esteem are greatly influenced by our perception of our physical bodies and our sexual experiences. The inverse relationship between GSI and sexual distress aligns with the understanding that negative sexual experiences can adversely impact one’s self-image. Moreover, the findings of the present study have important implications for policy, practice, and research in women’s health. Our results reveal the importance of policies that encourage positive sexual experiences and a healthy body image. To ensure this, comprehensive sex education should be considered, covering not just the biological aspects of sex, but also the emotional and psychological elements. Policies should also aim to counter damaging societal norms and expectations related to sexual function and physical appearance. Women’s sexual health policies should take into account the predictors of GSI identified in the study. On the other hand, the results of this study could be used to inform therapeutic practices. For example, therapists could work with individuals to improve their sexual satisfaction and esteem as a way of improving their GSI. This could involve cognitive-behavioral techniques to challenge and change negative perceptions of one’s genitalia. Moreover, addressing any sexual distress and promoting positive body image could also be critical components of therapy. Finally, our findings also suggest that future research could benefit from investigating interventions that could improve GSI, and their potential effects on sexual satisfaction, esteem, and function.

Conclusion

The study’s findings highlight sexual esteem, sexual satisfaction, and certain socio-demographic variables as predictors of GSI in Iranian women. Considering the influence of GSI on other aspects of women’s sexual well-being, healthcare providers should prioritize promoting women’s awareness and positive perception of their genitalia. Finally, strengthening women’s self-esteem through sexual health counseling sessions can lead to better sexual experiences.

Some limitations of the study should be acknowledged. Firstly, due to its cross-sectional nature, the study design does not allow for establishing causal relationships among the variables. Future research using longitudinal designs would be beneficial to explore causal associations between GSI and other factors. Additionally, conducting research in the field of sexual health among Iranian women may encounter cultural barriers. Initially, it might be challenging to gain acceptance and participation from potential participants. To address this limitation, the study provided the target group with a link to the online questionnaire to facilitate their involvement. However, there were no specific procedures in place to ensure the quality of the online data collected, such as detecting low attention responses or other measures to control for data quality. While this study tried to comprehensively examine the predictors of GSI, it is important to acknowledge that some factors, such as sexual orientation, were not evaluated. Considering sexual orientation could provide valuable insights into how it may influence GSI and should be considered in future research. Although using the self-reporting method for the inclusion and exclusion criteria of participants was the limitation of the present study, using standard tools for data collection, which enhances the reliability and validity of the results was one of the strengths of the current study. Additionally, the use of random sampling is another strength of this research, as it contributes to a higher degree of generalizability, allowing the findings to be more applicable to the broader population. To further expand our understanding of GSI and its implications for sexual health, future research should explore the concept of GSI in other specific groups, such as men populations, women undergoing genital cosmetic surgery, and women during pregnancy or menopause.
