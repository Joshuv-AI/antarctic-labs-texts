Introduction

Ameloblastic fibroma (AF) was first described by Kruse in 1891 [1]. It is defined by the World Health Organisation (WHO) as a rare, benign, mixed odontogenic tumor composed of mesenchymal tissue resembling the dental papilla with an epithelial component resembling the early developing enamel organ, without dental matrix or hard tissue [2,3]. Included in the group of benign mixed epithelial and mesenchymal odontogenic tumors in the latest WHO classification along with odontoma, primordial odontogenic tumor and dentinogenic ghost cell tumor [3]. It is the least differentiated of this group as it does not produce calcified tissue [1,3]. It accounts for 1.5–4.5 % of odontogenic tumors and has been reported to occur equally in men and women or with a male predilection (1.4:1) [4]. It is most commonly diagnosed in the first and second decade of life with a median age of 15.9 years and is therefore considered a tumor of childhood and adolescence [4,5]. In most cases it presents clinically as a slow growing, asymptomatic enlargement of the posterior mandible. On imaging it is well defined with sclerotic margins, unilocular or multilocular, often associated with a displaced second molar and usually not associated with cortical perforation or root resorption. Depending on the clinical presentation, the differential diagnosis may include dentigerous cyst, keratocyst, ameloblastoma, myxoma [1,4,6]. Recurrence of AF has been reported to be between 16.3 and 43.5% [5,7] and malignant transformation is estimated in 6. 4% [8] and it is believed that 25 % of ameloblastic fibrosarcomas (AFS) originate from an AF [9], so its therapeutic approach is still under debate, with authors recommending conservative treatment by enucleation with or without curettage and others radical treatment by segmental resection or hemimandibulectomy depending on size [1,5,10]. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist [11].

Case presentation

In June 2024, a 15-year-old female patient presented with the complaint of ‘I have an infected tooth’. When questioned, she reported sudden onset of pain in the right posterior mandible first noted one month ago. The past medical history reveals no history of disease. On clinical examination, there was a slight increase in volume with purulent secretion associated with the right retromolar trigone. The panoramic radiograph showed a unilocular radiolucency associated with tooth 48, with resorption of the distal root tip of tooth 47 and tooth 43 retained in the symphyseal region (Image 1). A Computed Axial Tomography scan was requested, which showed a hypodense image of hard tissue extending from the distal root of the 47th to the mandibular angle with bone expansion and perforation of both cortices (Image 2).Based on the clinical and radiographic features, an incisional biopsy was performed and a Nelaton probe was placed as a decompression device. The histopathological diagnosis of incisional biopsy was AF. It was decided to perform enucleation, curettage, peripheral osteotomy, extraction of teeth 48, 47 and 43 in the operating theatre and placement of a mandibular reconstruction plate to avoid pathological fracture of the mandibular angle. The results of this study indicate a slight predilection for males, with the highest prevalence observed in the 10–19 age group (45 %), followed by the 0–9 age group (25 %). These findings align with previous estimates suggesting that 77.7 % of cases are diagnosed before the age of 20 [8]. The mean age of 19.2 years is consistent with the previously reported average of 15.9 years [4]. Notably, 30 % of patients were between their third and sixth decades of life, highlighting the importance of considering this condition as a differential diagnosis even in adult patients (Table 1).

In cases involving the mandible, the prevalence was equally distributed between males (50 %) and females (50 %). The age of the patients ranged from 6 to 60 years, with a mean age of 19.2 years (SD ± 14.36). Clinically, 95 % of the cases were asymptomatic. Lesions were predominantly located in the posterior region of the mandible (80 %), and 90 % were associated with bone expansion. Cortical perforation was observed in 8 cases (40 %), while in half of the cases (50 %), this feature was either unspecified or undetermined. Radiographically, 70 % of the cases exhibited a multilocular pattern, and dental displacement was present in 50 % of the cases. Root resorption was identified in 55 % of the cases.

Regarding treatment modalities, enucleation with curettage was the most commonly reported approach (45 % of cases), followed by enucleation and segmental resection (20 % each). Follow-up data were available for 14 of the 20 cases, with a duration ranging from 6 to 144 months (mean = 33.28 months, SD ±36.19). Recurrence was reported in one patient (5 %), while 65 % of cases showed no recurrence. The remaining six publications did not provide recurrence-related information.

Three cases were identified in the maxilla. The age range for these cases was 4–8 years, and all patients were male.

While a prior report indicated that 28.57 % of patients present with symptoms [12], this study found that 95 % of patients were asymptomatic. The majority of cases (86.9 %) occurred in the mandible, with 80 % located in the posterior region, 5 % in the anterior region, and 15 % involving both anterior and posterior regions. Maxillary cases accounted for 13 % of the total. These findings are consistent with previous reports (82 %, 7.8 %, 10.2 %, and 23 %, respectively) [8]. It has been estimated that 75 % of cases are associated with an impacted tooth [13].

The primary debate regarding the ideal therapeutic approach revolves around the association of ameloblastic fibroma (AF) with ameloblastic fibrosarcoma (AFS). A study of 123 cases estimated a malignant transformation rate in recurrent cases of 11.4 %, with initial presentation age being the only factor significantly associated with malignant transformation. Patients younger than 22 years had a lower likelihood of transformation (3.3 %) compared to those older than 22 years (26.1 %) [1]. It is estimated that one-third of AFS cases develop from AF. In cases where morphological diagnosis is challenging, biomarkers such as Ki-67, Mib-1, p53, and PCNA may be utilized, as they show higher positivity in AFS cases [1,5].

Recurrence rates have been reported to range between 16.3 % and 43.5 % [5,7]. In this study, 26 % of cases did not specify recurrence status, and recurrence was observed in only one patient, occurring two years after initial treatment with enucleation.

Based on histological characteristics, it is possible that some lesions previously reported as AF may represent developing odontomas. There is no evidence to suggest that these are distinct entities, and due to their rarity, lesions with histological features of AF and hard tissue formation are considered developing odontomas. This distinction is crucial to avoid overtreatment, particularly when diagnosed as “ameloblastic fibro-odontoma” or “ameloblastic fibro-dentinoma,” depending on the presence of enamel or dentin [2,3]. These terms are not included in the current classification, as they are considered part of the spectrum of developing odontomas [3].

Molecular analyses of these lesions have identified the presence of the BRAF pV600E mutation, which suggests a closer relationship to AF, as odontomas lack this mutation [14]. Various terms have been proposed to describe AF based on the presence of mineralized tissues. While this study did not consider coalescent lesions, one report indicates that the lesion most frequently associated with AF is the calcifying odontogenic cyst, with 75 % of cases occurring in the posterior mandible [12].

The clinical case presented is consistent with what has been previously reported. The patient's age is within the highest prevalence group, as is the location in the posterior area of the mandible, associated with an impacted and displaced tooth (tooth 48) and root resorption (tooth 47).

Contrary to what was reported, the patient reported sudden onset of pain and a unilocular radiographic pattern.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this case are the early diagnosis and conservative treatment with a focus on quality of life in a young patient, although the response to treatment may not be reproducible in other patients.

Conclusion

Ameloblastic fibroma (AF) is a rare, benign, mixed odontogenic tumor that predominantly affects children and adolescents. It most frequently occurs in the mandible, typically presenting as an asymptomatic enlargement. The clinical case described in this study exhibits the most common features of this tumor, and the findings align with those previously reported in the literature. The management of AF remains a subject of debate, primarily due to the potential risks of recurrence and malignant transformation into ameloblastic fibrosarcoma (AFS).
