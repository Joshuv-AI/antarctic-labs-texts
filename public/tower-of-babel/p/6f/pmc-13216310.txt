snRNA-seq identifies Fmo2+ fibroblasts as drivers of hyperglycemic memory-induced cardiac injury
Authors: Shihan Xu, Chunxiao Ju, Mengmeng Zhu, Yajie Cai, Fengqin Xu, Yanfei Liu, Yue Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite intensive glycemic control, diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) often progresses due to hyperglycemic memory (HGM), yet the specific cardiac cells perpetuating this injury remain unknown. To address this, we performed single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) on hearts from an HGM rat model. Our analysis of 86,120 nuclei revealed HGM-specific inflammatory and epigenetic reprogramming signatures. Fibroblasts emerged as potential mediators, appearing to drive extracellular matrix remodeling via upregulated LAMININ and COLLAGEN signaling. We uncovered a distinct HGM-specific fibroblast subpopulation characterized by oxidative stress and H3K27 demethylation. Integrative analysis prioritized Fmo2 as a key pathogenic candidate, which was further supported via Mendelian randomization and clinical data as a putative causal gene. This study suggests that a pathogenic Fmo2+ fibroblast subpopulation may act as a pathological “memory carrier,” providing novel mechanistic insights and proposing exploratory therapeutic avenues for HGM-induced cardiac damage beyond glycemic control.

Introduction

The global burden of diabetes mellitus (DM) continues to rise. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 463 million people worldwide had DM in 2021, with projections reaching 783 million by 2045, making DM a significant global public health challenge1. Cardiovascular complications are the primary cause of DM-related mortality, accounting for over 80% of deaths among DM patients, with heart failure being particularly prominent2. Studies indicate that even after adjusting for traditional risk factors such as coronary artery disease and hypertension, DM patients still face a significantly elevated risk of developing heart failure3. Furthermore, among patients with established heart failure, the presence of diabetes is an independent predictor of poor prognosis4. This clinical phenomenon prompted the academic community to recognize diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM) as a distinct disease entity. DCM is characterized by structural and functional abnormalities of the myocardium, with primary pathological changes including myocardial inflammation, fibrosis, hypertrophy, and early diastolic dysfunction. These alterations can occur independently of recognized etiologies such as myocardial ischemia and ultimately progress to irreversible systolic dysfunction and heart failure5. Hyperglycemia is widely recognized as a core driver of DCM development, and intensive glycemic control has long been regarded as the cornerstone of preventing diabetic complications. However, extensive clinical research indicates that intensive glycemic control has achieved only limited success in cardiac protection6. Large-scale clinical trials, exemplified by the ADVANCE study, found that while strict glycemic control significantly reduced the risk of diabetic nephropathy, it failed to markedly improve retinopathy or macrovascular complications7. A meta-analysis encompassing eight randomized controlled trials involving 37,229 diabetic patients reached a similar conclusion: intensive glycemic control does not significantly reduce the incidence of heart failure8. Collectively, these studies reveal a dilemma: even when blood glucose levels return to normal, cardiac damage caused by early hyperglycemia may persist a phenomenon termed “hyperglycemic memory” (HGM)9. Although extensive research indicates that HGM can promote cardiac damage through multiple molecular pathways, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated10. Therefore, in-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms of HGM not only helps reveal the irreversible pathological changes induced by hyperglycemia in the early stages of DM but also provides new insights for identifying novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

In recent years, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology has provided a revolutionary tool for deciphering the cellular composition and states of organs such as the heart, significantly deepening our understanding of cardiac homeostasis and disease11,12. Multiple studies have now employed scRNA-seq to reveal alterations in the cellular landscape, transcriptional profiles, and communication patterns of DCM, providing high-resolution insights into its pathological processes and laying the groundwork for identifying novel therapeutic targets13–15. However, these pioneering efforts have focused exclusively on cardiac cellular changes under sustained hyperglycemia, leaving the cellular landscape of cardiac injury induced by HGM largely unexplored. Furthermore, previous studies on HGM have primarily relied on whole-tissue signaling or analyses at specific molecular levels16, failing to identify which distinct cell subpopulations persist as “memory carriers” to drive pathological cardiac remodeling even after normalization of blood glucose levels. Therefore, this study will employ snRNA-seq technology to address this critical gap, aiming to systematically decipher the cellular and molecular landscape of HGM at the single-cell level. This approach will identify key cell subpopulations driving pathological progression and their core regulatory factors.

In this study, we first established a DM rat model using a high-fat diet (HFD) combined with streptozotocin (STZ), followed by the construction of an HGM model through rigorous insulin therapy. Utilizing snRNA-seq technology from the 10x Genomics platform, we systematically mapped the single-cell nuclear transcriptome landscape of rat hearts under HGM conditions. Results indicate that fibroblasts play a pivotal role in HGM-induced cardiac damage. Further analysis identified a specifically enriched Fmo2+ fibroblast subpopulation exhibiting oxidative stress and H3K27 demethylation signatures, which acts as a key pathological “memory carrier” driving the long-term progression of cardiac damage.

Results

Enhanced glycemic control fails to reverse diabetes-induced cardiac dysfunction and pathological remodeling

This study established a DM rat model by combining HFD with intraperitoneal STZ injection. A subset of DM rats subsequently received daily subcutaneous insulin to normalize blood glucose levels, thereby generating HGM model (Fig. 1A). Circulating glucose monitoring initiated two weeks after STZ administration revealed that DM rats maintained persistently elevated blood glucose levels throughout the experiment, whereas HGM rats exhibited decreased glucose levels after two weeks of insulin intervention, returning to ranges indistinguishable from the Con group (Fig. 1B). Fasting blood glucose measurements at the endpoint further confirmed significantly elevated levels in the DM group compared to the Con group, while the HGM group showed no statistically significant difference from the Con group (Fig. 1C). Echocardiographic assessment revealed marked systolic dysfunction in both DM and HGM groups relative to Con, as evidenced by significantly reduced ejection fraction (EF) and fractional shortening (FS) (Fig. 1F–H). Notably, despite effective glycemic normalization in the HGM group, the degree of systolic dysfunction was comparable to that observed in DM rats, indicating that insulin-mediated glucose control did not ameliorate cardiac systolic impairment. To elucidate the histological basis of functional impairment, pathological analysis of cardiac tissue was performed. H&E, WGA, and Masson trichrome staining consistently revealed marked pathological remodeling in both DM and HGM groups, characterized by significant myocardial interstitial fibrosis and cardiomyocyte hypertrophy (Fig. 1I). Quantitative analysis showed that both groups exhibited significantly higher fibrosis area and cardiomyocyte area compared to the Con group, though no significant intergroup difference existed between DM and HGM groups (Fig. 1J-K). Single-cell nuclear transcriptome profiling of cardiac damage induced by HGM

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying HGM-induced cardiac damage at single-cell resolution, this study employed single-cell nuclear RNA sequencing to construct single-cell nuclear transcriptome maps of hearts from HGM and DM rats. Following rigorous quality control, a total of 86,120 high-quality nuclei were utilized for subsequent analysis. Through unsupervised clustering and expression of cell-specific marker genes17, eight major cardiac cell types were successfully identified and annotated: cardiomyocytes (Ldb3, Fh12, Myoz2, Rbm20, Myh6), fibroblasts (Dcn, Col1a1, Fbn1, Col5a1, Gsn), endothelial cells (Flt1, Flt4, Hmcn1, Pgm5, Cgnl1), macrophages (Cd74, Cd163, Mrc1, Cx3cr1, Adgre1), T cells (Skap1, Ptprc, Ccl5, Cd3g, Arhgdib), pericytes (Pdgfrb, Abcc9, Rgs5, Plcl1, Adgrl3), mesothelial cells (Wfdc21, Gpm6a, Msln, Sema3c), and neuronal cells (Scn7a, Cdh19, Grik2, Csmd1, Ncam2) (Fig. 2A-B). No clustering bias was observed in cell distribution across samples or groups (Fig. S1). To investigate HGM-related alterations in cardiac cell composition, cluster differential abundance across groups was assessed by sampling cell neighborhoods. Results revealed significant bidirectional distribution patterns in key cell populations, including cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells, where cell subpopulations enriched in HGM coexisted with those enriched in controls. This finding indicates that HGM-induced cellular composition remodeling involves not merely quantitative changes but drives heterogeneous shifts within cell subpopulations (Fig. 3C). Notably, the DM group exhibited similar patterns of cellular abundance alterations, suggesting that once established, the cardiac cell heterogeneity reshaping induced by high-glucose exposure persists as a durable injury. To elucidate the transcriptional characteristics of these changes, differential gene expression analysis and GSEA were further performed. Results indicated that cardiomyocytes exhibited the most pronounced transcriptional alterations in response to both HGM and DM stimulation, followed by fibroblasts (Fig. 2D). GSEA analysis revealed that the transcriptional signature of the HGM group primarily manifested as abnormal epigenetic regulation, catabolic reprogramming, and activation of inflammation-related signaling pathways. Among these, the histone lysine demethylation pathway showed the most significant enrichment, suggesting that HGM may profoundly influence cardiac transcriptional programs through epigenetic modifications. In contrast, the DM group’s transcriptional signature centered on metabolic stress responses, notably showing significant enrichment in fatty acid catabolic process pathways (Fig. 2E, F). Thus, despite similarities in cardiac cell composition between HGM and DM, their molecular regulatory mechanisms exhibit distinct differences: HGM emphasizes epigenetic reprogramming and inflammation-driven mechanisms, while DM centers on energy metabolism dysfunction. Furthermore, notably, both HGM and DM groups exhibited negative enrichment in pathways related to extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis (Fig. S2). Specifically, in HGM, the regulation of fibroblast growth factor receptor signaling pathway was significantly downregulated, whereas in DM, collagen biosynthetic processes were markedly suppressed (Fig. 2F). Fibroblasts play a key role in intercellular communication during cardiac damage induced by HGM

To further explore the intercellular communication network driving HGM-induced cardiac damage, CellChat analysis was performed to characterize communication patterns among cardiac cell types. Under HGM conditions, the heart exhibited a highly interactive intercellular network primarily centered on fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and cardiomyocytes (Fig. 3A). Fibroblasts emerged as the strongest signal emitters across the entire network, whereas cardiomyocytes exhibited the strongest signal reception activity (Fig. 3B). Signaling pathway analysis revealed distinct differences in emission and reception patterns among cell types. Notably, LAMININ and COLLAGEN pathways were markedly upregulated, both closely associated with ECM remodeling (Fig. 3C). Fibroblasts served as the main signal senders for both pathways. Interestingly, T cells showed the strongest signal reception for the COLLAGEN pathway, implying their potential involvement in ECM remodeling–related immune regulation (Fig. 3D, Fig. S3). Higher-order clustering of the signaling network using non-negative matrix factorization identified three functional communication modules: Module 1 (ECM remodeling and fibrosis, dominated by LAMININ, COLLAGEN, and THBS signaling); Module 2 (tissue repair, dominated by EPHB, NRG, and EPHA signaling); and Module 3 (endothelial barrier integrity and inflammatory regulation, dominated by JAM, PECAM1, and CDH5). Fibroblasts primarily contributed to ECM remodeling and fibrosis signals in Module 1, whereas cardiomyocytes mainly participated in inflammatory and endothelial barrier regulation in Module 3 (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these findings delineate a pathological communication network in HGM-induced cardiac damage that centers on fibroblasts and is dominated by ECM remodeling signals (particularly LAMININ and COLLAGEN). This network not only directly affects cardiomyocytes but may also bridge fibrotic remodeling with immune activation via fibroblast-T cell interactions, ultimately driving HGM-related cardiac damage.

Key fibroblast subpopulations and their core genes in HGM-induced cardiac damage

To further elucidate the role of fibroblasts in HGM-induced cardiac damage, we performed subclustering analysis of fibroblasts, identifying five discrete subpopulations (Fig. 4A). Analysis of subpopulation composition revealed significant group-specific differences (Fig. 4B). In the Con group, FB2 was the predominant subpopulation. In the HGM group, the proportion of FB2 decreased markedly, whereas FB1 and FB4 increased significantly. Notably, FB4 was exclusively present in the HGM group, indicating a potential key role in HGM-mediated cardiac pathology. In the DM group, FB2 further declined, while FB1 expanded to become the dominant subpopulation. To characterize the functional properties of these fibroblast subpopulations, we performed GSVA, which revealed distinct biological features among the five subpopulations (Fig. 4C). FB2, dominant in the Con group, was enriched in pathways related to negative regulation of myoblast proliferation, fibroblast growth factor chemotaxis, and endothelial cell chemotaxis, suggesting a critical role in maintaining cardiac structural homeostasis. FB1, predominant in the DM group, was enriched in Wnt signaling-mediated cardiomyocyte fate determination, circadian rhythm-related processes, and antimicrobial peptide production, implying that FB1 may contribute to diabetic myocardial remodeling through modulation of cardiomyocyte differentiation and immune responses. Importantly, FB4, unique to the HGM group, exhibited enrichment in pathways associated with negative regulation of cellular response to oxidative stress, NADPH oxidation, and histone H3K27 demethylation. This finding is consistent with the GSEA-based enrichment of the “histone lysine demethylation” pathway, suggesting that FB4-mediated H3K27 demethylation may constitute a central pathological mechanism in HGM-induced cardiac damage. Marker genes of FB4, including Fmo2 and Adamts12, further corroborate its role in oxidative stress response and pro-fibrotic processes (Fig. 4D). Pseudotime trajectory analysis using monocle3 revealed a progressive differentiation continuum among FB2, FB3, FB1, and FB4 (Fig. 4E). FB2 predominantly occupied the early phase of the trajectory, FB1 and FB3 were localized to intermediate-to-late phases, while FB4 was positioned at the terminal end of the differentiation branch, suggesting that these subpopulations assume distinct functional roles at different stages of disease progression. Gene module analysis further identified that FB4 specifically enriched and activated modules 9, 13, and 21 (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these analyses highlight the functional heterogeneity of fibroblast subpopulations and position FB4 as a terminal, potentially pathological state in HGM-induced cardiac damage. Finally, to identify the core regulatory genes driving the FB4 pathological phenotype, we employed a multi-modal integrative strategy. hdWGCNA on the FB4 subcluster identified six key modules (Figs. S4, S5). We then intersected three datasets: (1) FB4-specific differentially expressed genes, (2) genes from the FB4-specific activated pseudotime modules (9, 13, 21), and (3) the top 20 hub genes from the six hdWGCNA modules. This approach converged on nine core genes: Fmo2, Sox5, Lama2, Prex2, Dapk1, Arhgap24, Meis1, Pid1, and Gucy1a1 (Fig. 4 G). Integrating clinical data with genetic causal inference reveals the potential pathogenic role of Fmo2

To further identify potential key pathogenic genes from core candidate genes, this study first utilized the publicly available GEO dataset GSE26887 to analyze microarray expression data from left ventricular myocardial biopsies of 7 DCM patients and 5 controls. Results revealed significant alterations in the expression of FMO2, SOX5, PREX2, DAPK1, MEIS1, and GUCY1A1 in cardiac tissues from DCM patients (Fig. 5), while ARHGAP24, PID1, and LAMA2 showed no significant changes (Fig. S6). To further identify potential key disease-causing genes, a Two-Sample MR analysis was conducted. Based on IVs’ selection criteria, 11 cis-eQTLs, including Meis1, Fmo2, and Dapk1, were incorporated as IVs for MR analysis (Table S1). Analysis results revealed a causal association between Fmo2 and non-ischemic cardiomyopathy (IVW, OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.02–1.73, P = 0.04) (Fig. 6). This suggests that genetically predicted elevated Fmo2 expression may represent a key molecular link in HGM-induced cardiac damage, holding potential clinical translational value. Discussion

The concept of HGM provides a critical theoretical framework to explain the clinical observation that intensified glycemic control often yields only limited reductions in heart failure risk10. Previous studies have implicated mechanisms such as advanced glycation end-product accumulation, persistent oxidative stress, microRNA-mediated regulation, and epigenetic modifications in the establishment and maintenance of HGM18–21. However, HGM-induced cardiac damage arises from complex pathophysiological interactions across multiple cell types, including cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. Key questions remain unresolved: which specific cell subpopulations mediate these molecular processes, and how do they sustain cardiac damage even after normalization of glucose levels? Addressing these questions requires a detailed characterization of the cellular and molecular basis of HGM at single-cell resolution. In this study, we systematically mapped cardiac cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communication networks under HGM conditions using single-nucleus RNA sequencing for the first time, revealing the central role of fibroblasts in HGM-induced cardiac damage. Notably, we identified a fibroblast subpopulation characterized by Fmo2 expression, oxidative stress signatures, and H3K27 demethylation, which acts as a key pathological “memory carrier” driving the persistent progression of cardiac damage. These findings provide novel cellular and molecular insights into the mechanisms underlying sustained cardiac damage following glycemic normalization and offer a foundation for developing precision therapeutic strategies targeting HGM-associated cardiac damage.

Fibroblasts are the primary cell type in the cardiac interstitium, playing a crucial role in maintaining cardiac structural integrity and regulating extracellular matrix homeostasis22. Myocardial interstitial fibrosis is a major pathological feature of DCM23. Previous studies have conclusively demonstrated that under sustained hyperglycemic conditions, fibroblasts form a self-amplifying pro-fibrotic pathological circuit through the synergistic actions of the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway24, the AGEs-RAGE axis25, inflammatory mediators26, and calcium-sensitive receptors27, making them the core driver cells of cardiac structural remodeling. However, the specific mechanisms by which fibroblasts contribute to cardiac damage under HGM conditions remain unclear. This study reveals for the first time that fibroblasts are also a key cell population driving cardiac damage in HGM, yet their mechanism differs significantly from that in DCM. This process is primarily mediated by a fibroblast subpopulation exhibiting oxidative activation and histone H3K27 demethylation. Previous studies on HGM provide crucial support for this finding. Multiple studies indicate that persistent oxidative stress plays a central role in long-term vascular complications induced by HGM. Lee et al. discovered that under HGM conditions, ROS production and transglutaminase 2 activation form a vicious cycle in vascular endothelial cells, leading to sustained high expression of inflammatory adhesion molecules and apoptosis28. Furthermore, persistent oxidative stress can induce retinal microvascular dysfunction29. Jeon et al. further revealed that ROS activation persists in multiple organs including the retina, lungs, and kidneys in HGM mice, and can be partially reversed by C-peptide intervention20. This study extends these findings, suggesting that oxidative stress is also a core pathological mechanism in HGM-induced cardiac damage, mediated by fibroblasts. Beyond oxidative stress, histone H3K27 demethylation also represents a key molecular hallmark of fibroblast-driven cardiac remodeling in HGM. Histone methylation modifications, as a crucial epigenetic mechanism mediating HGM’s pathological effects, have been validated across diverse HGM models21. For instance, in aortic endothelial cells, HGM induces histone H3K4 monomethylation and H3K9 demethylation in the NF-κB p65 promoter region, thereby driving sustained expression of inflammatory genes30,31. In cardiomyocytes, HGM leads to reduced H3K9me3 levels in the IL-6 promoter region32. Our findings indicate that H3K27 demethylation represents a key molecular hallmark of HGM-activated fibroblasts. This epigenetic modification relieves transcriptional repression33, potentially promoting sustained activation and expression of pro-fibrotic genes. Thus, oxidative stress and H3K27 demethylation may synergistically drive the progressive cardiac damage mediated by HGM-activated fibroblasts. Furthermore, it is noteworthy that dysregulated intercellular communication plays a pivotal role in the cardiac fibrosis process of DCM13, yet the intercellular communication characteristics of the specific pathological stage known as HGM remain poorly understood. This study is the first to utilize intercellular communication analysis to map the cellular interaction network of cardiac damage during HGM, revealing a pathological communication pattern centered on fibroblast-myocyte interactions. A defining feature of this pattern is the sustained activation of fibroblast-derived LAMININ and COLLAGEN signaling. Previous studies have confirmed that fibroblasts interact with cardiomyocytes through pathways such as TGF-β, angiotensin II, IL-6, and mechanical stimulation, thereby driving pathological fibrosis progression and cardiac dysfunction34. The observed fibroblast–myocyte interactions in this study suggest that, beyond classical collagen deposition, the contributions of basement membrane and laminin-associated matrix remodeling to HGM-induced cardiac remodeling may have been previously underestimated. Collectively, our findings precisely localize the pathological effects of HGM to cardiac fibroblasts, identifying a subset marked by oxidative stress and H3K27 demethylation. Moreover, these fibroblasts orchestrate a pathological signaling network centered on LAMININ and COLLAGEN pathways, thereby sustaining cardiac fibrosis. This study provides novel cellular and molecular insights into the mechanisms underlying persistent cardiac damage despite normalization of blood glucose levels.

Another significant finding of this study is that Fmo2 was identified as a potential pathogenic gene mediating HGM-induced cardiac damage in the aforementioned specific fibroblast subpopulation. Fmo2 belongs to the flavin monooxygenase family, which is highly conserved from bacteria to humans35. Its classical function primarily involves the oxidative metabolism of exogenous compounds and drugs, as well as cellular detoxification reactions36,37. Its enzymatic activity depends on molecular oxygen, flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH)38,39. Recent studies have expanded the biological significance of Fmo2, revealing its regulatory role in multiple pathophysiological processes, including lifespan extension40, suppression of cardiac fibrosis41, and prevention of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease42. These findings suggest that Fmo2 possesses key regulatory functions beyond detoxification metabolism in maintaining homeostasis and influencing the progression of chronic diseases. This study reveals that the Fmo2+ fibroblast subpopulation exhibits distinct “NADPH oxidation” molecular signatures, consistent with its enzyme activity’s NADPH-dependent biochemical properties. This suggests Fmo2 may play a pivotal regulatory role in oxidative stress within fibroblasts. Previous research further supports that Fmo2 upregulation in cardiac fibroblasts constitutes a crucial molecular event for maintaining oxidative stress and promoting fibrotic activation states. Alex et al. discovered that the matrix retention phenotype in diabetic cardiomyopathy fibroblasts is associated with induction of the oxidative Fmo2 gene43. Tuleta et al. similarly observed that Fmo2 expression in diabetic mouse cardiac fibroblasts showed synchronized upregulation with matrix cell marker genes, matrix cross-linking enzymes, and other oxidative response-related genes44. Notably, studies indicate that Fmo2 exerts a protective role in the fibrotic remodeling process following cardiac injury. Cheng et al. discovered that Fmo2 deficiency represents a conserved molecular hallmark of cardiac injury after myocardial infarction (MI), exerting anti-fibrotic activity via the CYP2J3-SMURF2 axis. Restoration of Fmo2 expression significantly ameliorates fibrotic remodeling after MI across species ranging from rodents to non-human primates41. Consistent with this, Liu et al. also revealed Fmo2’s cardioprotective function by inhibiting the unfolded protein response pathway through catalyzing disulfide bond formation, thereby mitigating cardiomyocyte apoptosis during MI45. Although Fmo2 exhibits cardioprotective effects in MI, it is important to note that MI and HGM exhibit fundamental differences in their pathophysiological mechanisms. MI is characterized by massive cardiomyocyte necrosis following acute coronary artery occlusion, accompanied by acute inflammation and oxidative stress surges, initiating compensatory cardiac fibrosis aimed at scar repair46,47. In contrast, HGM represents a chronic, diffuse pathological state where cardiac injury stems from persistent oxidative stress and epigenetic reprogramming induced by early hyperglycemic exposure, primarily manifesting as interstitial cardiac fibrosis10. Thus, it is likely that this fundamental difference in pathophysiological background determines the opposed functions of Fmo2 in different disease models. In summary, under HGM conditions, Fmo2 may no longer function solely as an oxidative metabolic enzyme but instead serves as a key molecule driving chronic oxidative stress and sustained fibroblast activation. This discovery not only reveals a novel molecular mechanism for HGM-induced cardiac damage but also provides a potential target for precise interventions against HGM-related cardiac fibrosis.

Although this study provides novel cellular and molecular insights into the mechanisms of HGM-induced cardiac damage, several limitations remain. First, our current conclusions rely predominantly on transcriptomic data and causal inference derived from Mendelian randomization. While these analyses suggest that Fmo2+ fibroblasts might act as pathological memory carriers in HGM-induced cardiac damage, direct validation at the histological, protein, and functional levels is currently lacking. Specifically, without spatial transcriptomics or histological staining, it remains unclear exactly where this specific fibroblast population is anatomically located within the heart. Furthermore, we have not yet isolated and purified these Fmo2+ fibroblasts to directly assess the impact of altered Fmo2 expression on their specific cellular function and metabolism in vitro. Second, the study has not yet elucidated the upstream driving mechanisms and downstream effect pathways governing the interactions among Fmo2, oxidative stress, and H3K27 demethylation. Third, insulin intervention was the sole method employed for glycemic control, without comparing potential differences in HGM-induced cardiac injury outcomes using other clinically common drugs (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors). Therefore, future research must prioritize the spatial mapping of these cells, the development of targeted isolation protocols for in vitro functional and metabolic validation, multi-omics analyses integrating proteomics, and validation in larger prospective clinical cohorts to substantiate these transcriptomic observations and expand their translational potential.

This study systematically constructed a single-nucleus transcriptome map of HGM-induced cardiac damage using snRNA-seq technology. For the first time, it indicated that fibroblasts may play a pivotal role in HGM-induced cardiac damage. Among them, a specific fibroblast subpopulation characterized by high Fmo2 expression, oxidative activation, and H3K27 demethylation may act as a “memory carrier” potentially associated with the pathological effects of HGM. These findings provide novel cellular and molecular insights into HGM and suggest that the Fmo2+ fibroblast subpopulation could represent a potential precision therapeutic target for mitigating HGM-induced cardiac damage. This work may provide a basis for exploring strategies beyond glycemic control for intervention in DCM.

Methods

Animal experiments

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats aged 4-5 weeks were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (SCXK(JING)2021-0006). Rats were housed in SPF-grade animal facilities with access to sterile water and feed, maintained at 24°C with a 12/12-hour light-dark cycle. After a 1-week acclimation period, rats were fed either standard diet (Xietong Bio-Engineering Co., Ltd., China, Lot No. 2507060508) or a 45% kcal high-fat diet (HFD; Research Diets, USA, Cat. No. D12451) for 5 weeks before modeling. A single intraperitoneal injection of STZ(30 mg/kg, dissolved in 0.1 mmol/L citrate buffer, pH = 4.5) and fed a HFD to establish diabetic rat models. Control rats received an equal volume of citrate buffer (0.1 mol/L sodium citrate, pH = 4.5) and were fed a standard diet. Blood glucose levels were measured using an ACCU-CHEK meter (Roche, USA). Successful establishment of the diabetic model was confirmed when three consecutive random blood glucose measurements exceeded 16.7 mmol/L48. Two weeks after successful modeling, rats were randomly assigned to the DM group and the HGM group. The HGM group received daily subcutaneous injections of intermediate-acting insulin at 1.5 UI/100 g. On the first day of treatment, blood glucose was monitored every 6 hours over a 12-hour period to ensure it dropped to a near-normal range (approximately 8–11 mmol/L). Subsequently, blood glucose levels were measured every two weeks (from week 9 to week 23) to confirm that the HGM rats consistently maintained near-normal glucose levels of 8–11 mmol/L. After 18 weeks of STZ infusion, rats were fasted for 12 hours and deeply anesthetized via an intraperitoneal injection of 2.5% tribromoethanol (300 mg/kg). Upon confirmation of deep anesthesia and loss of reflexes, the rats were euthanized by exsanguination. Cardiac tissue was harvested for further analysis. All animal experiments in this study follow the Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals (China) and are approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Xiyuan Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Approval No. 2025XLC034-2).

Echocardiography

Transesophageal echocardiographic measurements were performed according to methods described in previous studies49, using a Mindray S7 SCI ultrasound device (Mindray, China). Rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (2% in O2, 1 L/min). Left ventricular wall thickness and chamber dimensions were determined in the parasternal short-axis view (M-mode), from which left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (FS) were derived. All measurements were performed by an observer blinded to the recording identity. Measurements were obtained during six consecutive cardiac cycles.

Histological analysis

Whole rat hearts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 25 °C for 24 h. Following fixation, tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned along the longitudinal axis at 4 µm thickness. Paraffin-embedded sections were dewaxed with xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol concentrations. The dewaxed and rehydrated sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E, Wuhan Servicebio Co., Ltd., China), Masson’s trichrome (Nanjing SenBeiJia Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China), and Alexa Fluor 488-labeled wheat germ agglutinin (WGA, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), respectively. Collagen content and myocardial hypertrophy were assessed according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Isolation of the nuclei from heart tissue

Heart tissue was cut into small pieces approximately 0.1 cm in size. A mixture of 1 ml lysis buffer and 1 ml PBS was added. The tissue was washed twice with PBS, and the supernatant was discarded. Subsequently, 2 ml of pre-chilled lysis buffer was added, and the sample was pre-lysed on ice for 2 minutes using a Dounce homogenizer. The sample was incubated on ice for 6 min, followed by the addition of 2 ml pre-chilled 4% BSA dilution. The mixture was centrifuged at 4 °C and 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 2 ml lysis buffer (containing 4% BSA), and incubated on ice for 3 minutes. Debris was removed using the Medtronic kit (Order No. 130-109-398), followed by centrifugation (4 °C, 300 g, 5 min). Nuclei were resuspended in nuclear suspension buffer (1× PBS, 2% BSA, 0.1% RNase inhibitor, Roche), filtered through a 20 μm cell strainer, and counted.

Droplet-based snRNA-seq using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform

Nuclei were suspended in PBS containing 0.04% BSA. snRNA-seq libraries were prepared using Chromium Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kits v3.1 (10x Genomics) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The target nuclei recovery for each library was 6000. Prior to sequencing, the quality of the constructed libraries was assessed. Fragment size distribution was evaluated using an Agilent dsDNA 915 Reagent Kit, and library concentration was quantified using a VAHTS Library Quantification Kit for Illumina. The generated libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Clustering analysis

Cell Ranger was used for alignment, filtering, barcode counting, and UMI counting to generate feature barcode matrices and global gene expression. The R package Seurat (v 5.3.0) was employed for dimensionality reduction, visualization, and analysis of snRNA sequencing data. To ensure data quality, nuclei meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) Number of expressed genes <300 or >6000; (2) Mitochondrial gene proportion >5%. Additionally, scDblFinder (v 1.22.0) was employed to identify and remove potential doublets. Following these criteria, 86,120 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. UMI counts for each cell were log-normalized using NormalizeData(), and the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected via FindVariableFeatures(). Data were linearly scaled using ScaleData(). Following PCA analysis on highly variable genes, batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. The top 20 dimensions were selected for subsequent analysis via ElbowPlot. Cells were then clustered using the Leiden algorithm. Cluster stability was assessed with clustree (v 0.5.1), and a resolution of 0.3 was ultimately selected. Finally, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) visualization was performed using RunUMAP().

Analysis of cellular differential abundance

By sampling cell neighborhoods from k-nearest neighbor (k-NN) graphs, miloR (v 1.6.0) was used to detect the enrichment of each group within neighborhoods and assesse cluster differential abundance between different groups50. The buildGraph() function in miloR was executed with the following parameters: k = 15, d = 15, followed by makeNhoods() and calcNhoodDistance(). Differential abundance was tested using testNhoods() with a generalized linear model. Significance was determined by the spatial FDR calculated by miloR based on the differential abundance P-value and neighborhood relationships.

Differential gene expression and GSEA/GSVA analysis

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between different groups, this study employed a pseudo-bulk analysis strategy51. Specifically, the raw count matrices of target cell clusters within each sample were first aggregated to generate an expression matrix at the sample level. Subsequently, the aggregated data underwent normalization, and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.44.0) to obtain a list of DEGs. For gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), we ranked all genes based on their log2 fold change values derived from the DESeq2 analysis and conducted enrichment calculations using GSEABase (v1.66.0). To assess the functional states of distinct fibroblast subpopulations, we computed the mean gene expression values for each subpopulation using Seurat. Genes with total expression values greater than zero were selected for analysis, and the top 1000 genes with the highest standard deviations were retained for gene set variation analysis (GSVA). Pathway activity scores for each subpopulation were calculated using GSVA (v1.52.3) based on the “gsva” algorithm. This unsupervised method quantified gene set enrichment signals to reveal functional differences among fibroblast subpopulations. Both GSEA and GSVA analyses utilized the C5 (Gene Ontology Biological Process) collection from MSigDB v7.5.1 as reference gene sets.

Cell-cell interaction analysis

To decipher the holistic communication landscape between cells, we employed CellChat (v 1.6.1) and utilized its built-in high-quality ligand-receptor interaction database to conduct a systematic analysis of signaling relationships among cell populations52.

Pseudotime and trajectory analysis

Pseudotime and trajectory analyses on single-cell data were performed using Monocle3 (v1.4.26). First, the learn_graph function was used to construct latent differentiation trajectories to capture potential branching and discontinuous evolutionary processes. Subsequently, the order_cells function was applied to define pseudotime based on selected starting nodes to infer cellular developmental progression. Finally, differentially expressed genes were identified along the resulting trajectories to reveal key dynamic transcriptional changes.

High dimensional WGCNA

This study employed hdWGCNA (v0.2.26) to analyze fibroblast subpopulation data and uncover potential biological functions and regulatory features53. The fibroblast expression matrix was preprocessed using standard Seurat workflows, including quality control, normalization, and low-quality gene filtering, to ensure data reliability. A soft-thresholding power of 2 was selected based on the scale-free topology fit index and mean connectivity criteria. Subsequently, a weighted gene co-expression network was constructed through the hdWGCNA analysis workflow. Modules of highly co-expressed genes were identified using hierarchical clustering with dynamic tree cutting, each representing genes sharing similar expression patterns and potential functional associations.

Two-sample MR

This study conducted a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis to explore the potential causal effects of specific gene expression on disease risk. Cis-expression Quantitative Trait Loci (cis-eQTLs) were extracted from cardiac tissue and whole blood in the GTEx v10 database and used as genetic instrumental variables (IVs). IVs were stringently selected based on the following criteria: (1) significant association with target gene expression (P < 1 × 10⁻⁶); (2) location within 1 Mb of the transcription start or stop site; (3) linkage disequilibrium pruning to ensure variant independence (R² <0.2); and (4) sufficient instrument strength (F statistic >10) to minimize weak instrument bias. Outcome summary statistics were obtained from the FinnGen R12 database, including 13,917 non-ischemic cardiomyopathy cases and 402,343 controls. The MR analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package (v0.6.22). For genes instrumented by a single SNP, causal estimates were derived using the Wald ratio method54, while inverse variance weighting (IVW) served as the primary approach for multiple-SNP instruments55. Weighted median and MR-Egger analyses were further conducted to assess robustness. The IVW method integrated the effects of multiple variants using inverse-variance weighting, yielding a pooled causal estimate with improved precision.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.4.1) and R (v4.2.3). Data were presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). unless otherwise specified. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess data normality. For normally distributed data, two-tailed Student’s t-tests (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for multiple groups were conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal–Wallis test (multiple groups) was applied, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
