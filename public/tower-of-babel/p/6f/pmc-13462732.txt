Thermally activated paint-sludge as potential sustainable adsorbent for pollutant removal with insights from isotherms kinetics thermodynamics and response surface methodology
Authors: Ali El-Rayyes, Martins Titus, Amnah Mohammed Alsuhaibani, Moamen S Refat, Ezekiel Foloronsho Sodiya, Kholoud K Alzahrani, Edwin Andrew Ofudje
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe continuous rise the release of untreated paint industry wastewater containing dyes, organic pollutants, and heavy metals poses serious environmental and public health concerns, most especially in developing countries where treatment infrastructure remains inadequate. This study examines the potential of thermally activated sludge (TAS) derived from paint industry waste as cost-effective and sustainable adsorbent for the uptake of pollutants from industrial effluents. Comprehensive adsorption analyses through isotherm, kinetic, thermodynamic, and response surface methodology (RSM) modelling were performed to elucidate the adsorption mechanism and optimize operational parameters. Thermally activated sludge was synthesized via pyrolyzing paint sludge at 700 °C and compared with unactivated sludge to explain the effect of thermal modification on adsorption performance. Comprehensive characterization through XRD, FTIR, SEM–EDS, BET, and TGA confirmed that thermal activation enhanced the porosity, surface heterogeneity, mineral composition, and functional groups of the sludge adsorbent. Batch adsorption experiments were performed by varying contact time, pH, temperature, adsorbent dosage, and pollutant concentration. TAS displayed superior removal performance compare to US, achieving substantial reductions in BOD, COD, TSS, TDS, oil and grease, nitrate, and heavy metals including Pb, Cd, and Cr. Maximum adsorption efficiency 86.9% was attained under alkaline conditions. Equilibrium studies revealed that the Freundlich isotherm best explained the adsorption process (R² = 0.998 for TAS), signifying multilayer adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces, while the Langmuir maximum adsorption capacity increased from 58.82 mg g⁻¹ for US to 77.21 mg g⁻¹ for TAS. Kinetic assessment revealed that adsorption onto TAS followed the pseudo-second-order model (R² = 0.995), indicating chemisorption involvement, whereas intraparticle diffusion contributed to the overall adsorption mechanism. Examinations of the thermodynamic data confirmed that the adsorption process was spontaneous and endothermic, with TAS displaying stronger adsorbate–adsorbent interactions compare to US. RSM study identified pH and adsorbent dosage as the most influential operational variables and demonstrated significant interaction effects between process parameters. Thermal activation improved adsorption efficiency, adsorption capacity, and process stability, highlighting the potential of converting industrial sludge waste into a value-added material for sustainable wastewater treatment applications.Keywords: Activated sludge, Adsorption mechanism, Heavy metal, Paint industry, OptimizationSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Significant environmental problems have arisen as a result of paint manufacturing industry’s explosive growth, especially with relation to the release of untreated or insufficiently treated effluent into the aquatic bodies. A complex mixture of organic solvents, resins, pigments, and heavy metals like lead (Pb), cadmium (Cd), and chromium (Cr) are commonly found in paint effluents (Viktoryová et al.,1). For, instance, the global paint industry is driven by paints’ crucial dual purpose of providing both protection and aesthetic enhancement. Paints operate as protective barriers, insulating diverse substrates from corrosion, UV radiation, and biological deterioration, ensuring the longevity of infrastructural, automotive, and industrial assets2. Paints provide an aesthetic role by adding decorative value and supporting branding efforts in the architectural and consumer product sectors, resulting in continuing market expansion. While the entire paint industry, which includes all formulations, is significantly larger, the rapid growth of the water-based segment highlights its important role in sectors like construction and automotive, fuelled by rising demand for sustainable products3.

Environmental contaminants coming from industrial paint operations and the domestic use of these paints represent a severe threat to human health worldwide, pushing up rates of disease and premature mortality. Because of their toxicity and endurance, these contaminants from paints effluent pose major threats to aquatic life, water quality, agricultural practices, and human health. They frequently surpass permissible limits for parameters including pH and electrical conductivity4, 5. These exposures can cause a wide range of serious illnesses, such as respiratory conditions like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), fetal abnormalities and reproductive toxicities, cancers caused by DNA mutations, neurodegenerative diseases like Alzheimer’s along with developmental delays in children, and cardiovascular events like heart attacks and strokes6, 7, 8. These effects are underpinned by essential biological pathways, oxidative stress that destroys cells, chronic systemic inflammation, endocrine system disturbances, and direct genetic alterations, that magnify vulnerability across populations6, 9. Addressing this issue requires urgent investment in effective monitoring and treatment systems more public education campaigns, and stricter regulatory frameworks to reduce exposures and long-term health consequences10.

However, the environmental effects of industrial effluents are made worse in Nigeria and other developing nations by a lack of wastewater treatment facilities and the setbacks from some of the already well-know traditional methods. For instance, due to their low removal efficiency, traditional physical treatment procedures for paint pollutants, such as filtration and sedimentation, have proven ineffective in eradicating dissolved ionic contaminants like heavy metals and volatile organic compounds (VOCs)11. Chemical techniques including precipitation, membrane filtration, and coagulation have been widely used; however, they require significant reagent inputs and generate hazardous sludge as a secondary waste, complicating disposal and raising operational responsibilities12. Biological treatments, such as bioremediation, provide more environmentally friendly alternatives, but they are often slow to treat and susceptible to inhibition or failure when exposed to the high toxicity and complex synthetic chemistries found in industrial paint waste streams13. Generally, many of these traditional techniques simply transfer or concentrate pollutants rather than removing them completely, and they lack mechanisms for material recovery, which is a significant disadvantage in light of circular economy principles that emphasize resource reuse and waste minimization14.

Adsorption has become well-known as a successful tertiary treatment technique for eliminating dyes, heavy metals, and persistent organic contaminants15. Adsorption is preferred over other traditional techniques due to its ease of use, ability to remove contaminants even at low concentrations, and low or even zero production of secondary waste16. Adsorption systems, particularly those based on commercial activated carbon is reported to be very effective towards effluents treatment, however, they have the difficulty of adsorbent saturation, requiring costly and high energy needs which often limits its usage17, 18. As a result, research is being done on sustainable, affordable, effective, and environmentally alternative materials that can reduce pollution.

One result of wastewater treatment procedures that shows promise as an adsorbent candidate is activated sludge. Sludge is a partially solid slurry mixture that usually consists of a liquid (usually water) combined with suspended solids. It results from a number of commercial, industrial, and municipal processes19. Thermal treatment improves the sludge’s porosity, surface area, and functional groups, which raises the adsorption capacity20. By turning a waste product into a useful resource, this method not only enhances contaminant removal performance but also complies with the principles of the circular economy, resolving issues with effluent treatment and sludge disposal18. Recently, some studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of sludge-derived adsorbents for the removal of complex industrial pollutants, with increasing emphasis on activation strategies, surface modification, and process optimization to enhance adsorption efficiency and reusability21, 22 (Cui et al.,23). Similar multi-mechanistic adsorption behavior involving electrostatic interaction, pore diffusion, hydrogen bonding, and surface complexation has been reported for biomass-derived adsorbents used in wastewater remediation24, 25.

With adsorption capacities similar to those of commercial activated carbons, a number of studies have shown that thermally activated sludge may efficiently adsorb a variety of contaminants, such as heavy metals, dyes, medications, and inorganic anions26. The sustainability and cost-effectiveness of sludge-based adsorbents are further supported by their strong regeneration and reuse potential18. Thermally activated sludge has gained attention as a viable alternative to commercial activated carbon due to its competitive adsorption performance and significantly lower cost. When compare with emerging and conventional adsorbents, sludge-derived materials relevance towards pollutants removal can be examined. For instance, commercial activated carbon usually exhibits good adsorption capacities depending on the pollutant system, whereas biochar materials generally show lower capacities because of their relatively lower surface area and activation level27. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), known for their remarkably high surface areas and tunable porosity, have exhibited superior adsorption performance in some cases; nevertheless, their application is often constrained by high synthesis cost, limited stability in aqueous systems, and scalability challenges28. Unlike high-performance engineered adsorbents, sludge-derived materials are produced from industrial waste streams, require relatively simple processing, and align with circular economy principles.

Despite the rising body of research on sludge-derived adsorbents, some gaps remain. Many studies rely on the use of external precursor for the production of adsorbent and synthetic wastewater systems that do not adequately represent the complexity of real industrial effluents. Also, little attention has been given to integrating mechanistic modeling, thermodynamic evaluation, and statistical optimization within a unified framework. Thus, this study aims to bridge these gaps by utilizing real paint industry sludge and wastewater as well as combining adsorption isotherm, kinetic, thermodynamic, and Response Surface Methodology (RSM) analyses. Additionally, a direct comparison between unactivated and thermally activated sludge is conducted to elucidate the effect of thermal modification on adsorption performance. This integrated approach provides both practical relevance and deeper mechanistic insight, distinguishing this work from previous studies. The study clarifies the underlying adsorption mechanisms and optimizes important parameters like pH, adsorbent dosage, contact time, and initial pollutant concentration by using adsorption isotherm models, kinetic models, thermodynamic, and response surface methodology evaluations. Comprehensive material characterizations shed light on the chemical functionalities and surface morphology that affect adsorption behaviour.

Materials and methods

Materials

The effluent employed in this study was collected as a composite wastewater stream during the cleaning of equipment used in the manufacturing of water-based paints, including emulsion and satin variations. The wastewater samples were collected in pre-cleaned high-density polyethylene (HDPE) containers and transported immediately to the laboratory. All adsorption experiments were conducted within 48 h of sample collection to minimize physicochemical changes in the effluent composition. Prior to use, the wastewater was homogenized by manual agitation to ensure uniform dispersion of suspended constituents A representative volume was drawn directly from a well-mixed storage tank at the effluent treatment facility using an 18-liter dispenser, ensuring sample integrity and consistency after permission was sorted and obtained. To preserve its physicochemical properties, the sample was held at ambient laboratory temperatures (~ 27.5 °C) and protected from direct sunlight. Visual investigation indicated a turbid, dark purple coloration with a strong paint-like odour and slight fermented characteristics. To study concentration-dependent impacts, the effluent was used in its undiluted state.

Importantly, no preliminary physical treatment, such as filtration or sedimentation, was used to preserve the sample’s true heterogeneity, which is representative of real-world situations. Prior to each experimental application, the effluent was manually stirred to restore homogeneity, and chemical parameters such as pH and temperature were modified only as needed to meet specific procedural criteria. This thorough sample and handling approach assures that future analyses replicate actual operational settings, increasing the findings’ relevance to practical wastewater treatment scenarios.

Preparation of adsorbent

The sludge sample was obtained from the effluent treatment facility of a paint manufacturing firm in Lagos, Nigeria, which focusses on the production of water-based decorative paints, such as emulsion, satin, and wall putty. The sample was collected from a dewatered, stacked matrix on a sand substrate, ready for disposal. At the collection site, a part was placed straight into a knotted high-density polyethylene (HDPE) bag, with no chemical preservation. The material was transferred to the laboratory and kept at ambient temperature of 27.5 ℃. It received a multi-stage pre-treatment, commencing with 12 h of sun-drying to minimise moisture content. It was then baked in an oven at 80 °C for two hours to guarantee that the material was consistent and dry. The baked sludge was ground and sieved to obtain a uniform particle size fraction of ≤ 250 μm. Thermal activation was achieved in a muffle furnace at a heating rate of 10 °C min⁻¹ until 700 °C was attained, after which the sample was maintained at this temperature for 2 h. The resulting sample was then allowed to cool gradually inside the furnace to avoid thermal shock and maintain structural integrity. The obtained product was pulverised and referred to as thermally activated sludge (TAS), while the portion not treated with elevated temperature was labelled as unactivated sludge (US).

Adsorbent characterization

The adsorbent, derived from sludge, was thoroughly characterised to determine its physical and chemical characteristics relevant to adsorption efficacy.

X-ray Diffraction (XRD) was used to determine the phase composition and crystallinity of the adsorbent. Measurements were taken with a Rigaku ULTIMA-IV diffractometer (Rigaku Corporation, Japan) using Cu-Kα radiation (wavelength 1.54 Å). Phase identification was based on a comparison with the International Centre for Diffraction Data (ICDD) PDF-4 + database (2024), which analysed peak locations, intensities, and full width at half maximum (FWHM) to characterise crystalline phases present. Surface functional groups were identified using Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy. Spectra were obtained for duplicate samples before and after adsorption using a PerkinElmer FT-IR spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA). The characteristic absorption bands were allocated to functional groups, and the fluctuation in peak intensities and locations was investigated to infer chemical alterations involved with the adsorption process. Scanning Electron Microscopy and Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy were employed to analyse surface and elemental composition. The samples were prepared by carbon coating to improve conductivity and then mounted on aluminium stubs using conductive carbon tape. Micrographs were taken with a JEOL JSM-6610LV scanning electron microscope (JEOL Ltd., Japan) at 20 kV accelerating voltage. The point of zero charge (pHpzc) of the sludge was measured using the pH-drift method. Briefly, NaCl solutions (25 mL of 0.01 M) were adjusted to initial pH values in the range of 2 to 8 and 5 mg of the sludge was introduced to each solution and agitated for 3 h at 150 rpm. The final pH values were then measured, and the difference between the initial and final pH was determine as the pHpzc. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA 4000, PerkinElmer, Haarlem, Netherlands) was performed under a nitrogen atmosphere from room temperature up to 1000 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min to assess the thermal stability of the adsorbent. Surface area and pore structure analyses were performed at 77 K after degassing samples at 150 °C for 4 h using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) instrument.

Adsorbate characterization

Prior to treatment trials, the paint industry wastewater was thoroughly characterized to determine its baseline pollutant burden. While ancillary parameters, such as pH, oil and grease, total suspended solids (TSS), chemical oxygen demand (COD), nutrient content (nitrogen, phosphate), and heavy metal concentrations, were determined using established standard protocols as previously documented by Imohiosen et al.29, primary pigment levels were assayed spectrophotometrically using UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan) at pH-optimized wavelengths derived from custom calibration curves. This allowed for the delineation of the composite organic, particulate, and inorganic fractions derived from resins, solvents, and pigments. Calibration curves were plotted to evaluate the initial concentration of the paint effluent.

Adsorption experiments

A series of batch adsorption studies were carried out by stirring known volumes of each adsorbent with wastewater samples in Erlenmeyer flasks with an orbital shaker to ensure uniform mixing. To completely determine the best conditions for maximal pollution removal, a wide range of important operational parameters were purposefully altered. The study looked at the effects of solution pH ranging from acidic to basic (2–10), adsorbent dosage from 0.2 to 0.8 g/L, temperatures from, 25 °C to 75 °C, and contact periods ranging from 30 to 180 min. UV-visible spectrophotometry was first used to characterize the effluent in order to determine the initial concentration of the wastewater and referred to as C0. Solution pH adjustment was achieved using 0.1 M HCl or 0.1 M NaOH prior to adsorption experiments. Spectrophotometric measurements of adsorbate concentrations were made at wavelengths specific to pH levels: 242 nm at pH 2, 266 nm at pH 7, and 276 nm at pH 10. To guarantee accuracy and consistency, this pH-dependent calibration procedure was strictly followed in all ensuring adsorption tests. 50 mL aliquots of the derived effluent were placed in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks and 0.2 g of the adsorbent was contacted with it for batch adsorption studies. The adsorption mixtures were agitated at 150 rpm throughout the batch adsorption experiments to ensure adequate solid–liquid contact. At predetermined time intervals, samples were withdrawn with syringe, and centrifuged and prior to UV–Visible spectrophotometric analysis using UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan).

Reusability experiments

The reusability potential of the adsorbent was performed via repeated adsorption–desorption experiments conducted over five consecutive cycles under identical operational conditions. After each adsorption experiment, the used adsorbent was separated from the solution and subjected to a desorption process to regenerate its active adsorption sites. Briefly, desorption was carried out using 0.1 M ethanol as the eluent. For regeneration studies, the spent adsorbent was contacted with 50 mL of 0.1 M ethanol solution and agitated at 150 rpm for 2 h prior to washing and drying. Ethanol was selected due to its ability to effectively disrupt the interactions between the adsorbate molecules and the active functional groups of the adsorbent, thereby promoting efficient desorption. Following the desorption step, the adsorbent was carefully washed several times with distilled/deionized water to eliminate any residual ethanol and detached contaminants remaining on the surface. The washed material was then dried in a hot-air oven at a temperature of 70 °C until a constant weight was achieved, ensuring complete removal of moisture before reuse. The regenerated adsorbent was subsequently reused in the next adsorption experiment under the same experimental conditions, including adsorbent dosage, contact time, solution pH, temperature, initial concentration, and agitation speed. This adsorption–desorption procedure was repeated for five successive cycles to assess the regeneration efficiency, operational stability, and durability of the adsorbent material.

Results and discussion

Characterizations of sludge adsorbent

The obtained X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns for thermally activated sludge before and after adsorption as illustrated in Fig. 1 display distinct differences in crystalline order and surface structure. Before exposure to effluent, TAS exhibited a series of well-defined diffraction peaks, most especially 2θ between approximately 28° and 32°, with additional peaks seen at higher angles (~ 35–55° 2θ). These reflections are indicative of the presence of crystalline mineral phases, likely including aluminosilicate, quartz (SiO₂), titanite (CaTiSiO₅), standard rutile (TiO₂), and metal oxide components inherent to sludge ash matrices, which are often associated with thermally treated sludge materials.

X-ray diffraction patterns of thermally activated sludge (TAS) before and after adsorption of paint industry effluent, showing changes in crystallinity and surface structural characteristics after pollutant uptake.

After the sorption of the effluent, the XRD pattern displays a noticeable decrease in the intensity of most of the initial observed characteristic peaks and an increase in the diffuse background signal. However, it was noticed that the positions of the principal diffraction peaks remain mostly unchanged between the two patterns, signifying that the bulk crystallographic structure of TAS was preserved through the adsorption process. Nevertheless, the decrease in peak sharpness post-adsorption signifies a higher degree of surface disorder and possible surface site or pore blockage by adsorbed constituents from the effluent matrix30. Such attenuation is in alignment with increased amorphous character or surface contamination, as adsorbed organic and inorganic species reduce the scattering contrast of crystalline domains31.

The absence of significant shifts or new diffraction reflections in existing peaks after the adsorption process suggests that adsorption did not transform the host lattice structure or induce the formation of new crystalline phases. This behavior is consistent with previous reports on sludge-derived adsorbents where the crystalline framework remained intact after interaction with contaminants32. The elevated baseline observed in the XRD scan after adsorption indicates the presence of a disordered surface layer on the adsorbent. This kind of increase in diffuse background intensity are usually associated with amorphous or poorly ordered phases in sludge-derived biochars and activated carbons, where broad diffraction features depict structural disorder within the carbon matrix33. This behavior is usually attributed to the accumulation of adsorbed inorganic and organic species on the adsorbent surface, which can attenuate or mask diffraction signals by reducing the scattering contrast of crystalline domains33. Similar increases in amorphous features in adsorption studies using sludge-derived biochars and activated carbons have been attributed to organic accumulation and surface functional group interactions following adsorption processes34.

Fourier Transform Infrared spectroscopy was performed to examine changes in surface functional groups of thermally activated sludge (TAS) induced by effluent adsorption (Fig. 2). The FT-IR spectrum of TAS displays key bands corresponding to a variety of functional groups inherent to sludge organic matter and mineral components that enable contaminant uptake. A broad O-H stretching band at 3240–3560 cm⁻¹ arises from surface hydroxyls and adsorbed water, promoting hydrogen bonding25]. Weak aliphatic C-H stretches were seen at 2920–2850 cm⁻¹ which suggest residual organics, while C = O and H-O-H bends were observed near 1740–1650 cm⁻¹ which confirm polar oxygen groups35. Stretches ascribed to the vibrations of Si-O-Si, C-O and P-O were observed around 1130–1025 cm⁻¹), whereas M-O (such as Fe-O, Al-O) vibrations were noticed below 800 cm⁻¹ which provide sites for electrostatic attraction, coordination, and complexation36.

FT-IR spectra of thermally activated sludge (TAS) before and after adsorption of paint industry effluent, showing the involvement of surface functional groups during the adsorption process.

Post-adsorption FT-IR study of TAS spectrum shows clear shifts confirming pollutant interactions. Specifically, broad –OH band was seen at 3375–3660 cm⁻¹) and with an increase in intensity and slight band narrowing, signifying consumption or reorganization of surface hydroxyl groups due to interaction with effluent components. The initial bands observed at 1740–1650 cm⁻¹ shifts to near 1720–1630 cm⁻¹, which is characteristic of complexation between organic pollutants and surface functional groups. Changes in the vibrations of Si-O-Si, C-O and P-O were also observed with new peaks showing at 1120–1015 cm⁻¹ region, imply the involvement of mineral-associated functional groups such as silicates, phosphates in the adsorption process.

The morphology outlook of the surface and microstructural characteristics of thermally activated sludge before and after effluent adsorption were examined with scanning electron microscopy (SEM) as illustrated in Fig. 3 which reveal pronounced changes in texture and surface features, providing direct evidence of effluent uptake at the solid–liquid interface. Before the adsorption (Fig. 3a), the TAS surface displays a heterogeneous microstructure with a rough, porous and irregularly shaped particles topography. The surface contains fragments and aggregated clusters, interspersed with microvoids and exposed edges. Such morphological features are typical of thermally treated biosolids, where thermal activation partially removes volatile organic components and exposes underlying mineral phases, resulting in a high density of surface irregularities and accessible adsorption sites. The observed surface roughness and porosity aligned with enhanced surface area and adsorption potential reported in similar sludge-derived materials37. After the uptake of effluent (Fig. 3b), significant changes in surface morphology were observed. The roughness and fractured previously observed on adsorbent surface became more compact and smoother, and some of the distinct particulate features visible in the SEM prior to adsorption process were obscured. This obvious decline in visible pores and cavities indicates that effluent constituents have accumulated within surface depressions and pore networks, effectively coating or filling the active sites on the TAS surface. This morphological “masking” is often ascribed to the attachment of contaminants molecules on active sites and within micro- and mesopores, causing surface coverage that diminishes textural heterogeneity38.

SEM micrographs of thermally activated sludge (TAS): (a) before adsorption and (b) after adsorption of paint effluent, illustrating changes in surface morphology and pore structure following contaminant uptake.

The chemical compositions of unactivated sludge and thermally activated sludge were assessed by EDS, and the results obtained are presented in Fig. 4. The results show that inorganic mineral elements dominated both samples, with calcium (Ca) being the most abundant constituent. After calcination, a noticeably increased the relative Ca content from 36.73 at% in US to 45.07 at% in TAS was observed which can be attributed to the decomposition and volatilization of organic matter during heating, which concentrates the inorganic ash fraction. This elevated Ca content suggests the stabilization or formation of Ca-bearing mineral phases such as carbonates, oxides, or phosphates, which can improve the alkalinity and potential adsorption capacity of the thermally treated sludge. Titanium (Ti) remained the second most abundant element but decreased from 26.32 to 20.11 at% after activation. Similarly, silicon (Si), and aluminum (Al) which are often associated with aluminosilicate and clay minerals, displayed only minor changes, signifying that the silicate framework of the sludge remained largely intact during thermal treatment. Other elements detected are magnesium (Mg) exhibited a noticeable decrease (4.11 to 2.93 at%), carbon (from 5.27 to 3.13 at%), while sulfur increased significantly from 1.55 to 4.03 at%, suggesting the concentration or formation of sulfate species during heating. Trace elements such as Zn, Na, Fe, and Sr were found in the raw sample and exhibited slight decreases after activation, while V was no longer detected in TAS. The EDS result is in alignment with the findings from XRD analysis where phases like aluminosilicate, quartz, titanite, rutile, and metal oxide components inherent were detected.

Elemental composition profiles of US and TAS, highlighting the effect of thermal activation on the mineral composition of the adsorbent.

Figure 5 illustrates the thermal behaviour curve of the sludge which shows that thermal decomposition proceeds through three distinct stages, which are characteristic of sewage sludge–derived materials. A minor weight loss was seen at the temperature below 200 °C which corresponds to the evaporation of physically adsorbed moisture and light volatile compounds present in the sludge matrix39. The moderately small mass reduction in this region signifies that the sludge had low residual moisture content prior to analysis, indicating effective thermal pretreatment. Region between 250 and 450 °C is the most significant mass loss. A pronounced DTG peak centered near ~ 380–400 °C was observed which indicates rapid devolatilization of organic constituents. This stage can be assigned to the thermal degradation of lipids, proteins, carbohydrates, and microbial cellular components present in the sludge40. The sharp reduction in mass from nearly 100% to about 20% affirms that organic matter constitutes the dominant degradable fraction of the sludge. Since only a single dominant DTG peak was seen, it signifies relatively homogeneous organic composition after thermal activation and this feature aligned with previous studies where it was reported that the primary pyrolysis zone of sewage sludge lies within the 200–500 °C range, where most volatile organic compounds are released and pore structures begin to develop41. Above the temperature of 450 °C, the TG curve becomes relatively stable, indicating only small mass loss up to 900 °C. Within this region, slow decomposition of refractory organics, fixed carbon rearrangement, and mineral transformations like the decomposition of phosphates and carbonates. The residual mass of about 15–20% represents the inorganic ash fraction inherent in sewage sludge.

Thermogravimetric (TG/DTG) analysis of paint sludge illustrating thermal decomposition stages, mass loss behavior, and thermal stability of the sludge-derived adsorbent.

Figure 6a illustrates the pore size distribution curves of US and TAS as a function of pore diameter. Significant variances are evident between the two samples with the untreated sludge displaying a narrower pore distribution with a sharp maximum centered at 2.1 nm and a rapid decline at larger pore diameters, signifying limited pore heterogeneity and relatively constrained pore accessibility. On the other hand, TAS displays a broader and more extended pore size distribution with a peak shifted toward larger pore diameters (2.8–3.0 nm). This broader distribution and higher pore volume retention across the mesoporous region suggest that thermal treatment facilitate pore widening and enhanced mesostructural development. The observed increased mesoporosity with TAS can be ascribed to the volatilization of organic fractions and decomposition of unstable components during thermal activation, which generated additional voids and interconnected pore channels42. Such enhanced pore structure is has been reported beneficial for adsorption since mesopores facilitate the diffusion and transport of large pollutant molecules into the internal adsorption sites43.

Pore structural features and adsorption-related textural properties indicating: (a) pore size distribution curves of US and TAS, (b) adsorption isotherm plot of US and TAS and (c) cumulative pore volume and differential pore volume distribution of TAS.

Figure 6b displays the adsorption isotherm-related plot, which reflects adsorption affinity and pore filling behavior. Both US and TAS shows increasing adsorption trends with increasing relative pressure, suggesting progressive multilayer adsorption and capillary condensation within mesoporous structures. However, the adsorption response of TAS varies from that of US. TAS exhibits comparatively lower values of 1/[W((P₀/P)−1)] across the investigated pressure range, signifying improved adsorption efficiency and enhanced pore accessibility after thermal treatment44. In addition, the smoother and more gradual increase observed for TAS indicates a more uniform pore network and improved adsorbate diffusion behavior. Whereas, US displays steeper increments and higher values, suggesting less efficient adsorption and possible diffusion limitations because of narrower or partially blocked pores. This further confirms that thermal treatment enhances the physicochemical properties of the sludge by increasing accessible adsorption sites and facilitating adsorbate transport within the pore system.

Figure 6c shows the cumulative pore volume and differential pore volume distribution as a function of pore diameter with the TAS possesses substantially higher pore volume development relative to US across the studied pore diameter range. After thermal activation, an increase in cumulative pore volume was observed within the 1.4–1.7 nm range, suggesting enhanced development of accessible microporous channels that may facilitate adsorbate diffusion45. Concurrently, the differential pore volume distribution (dV(d) curve displays a sharp rise for TAS within the same pore diameter interval, confirming that thermal treatment enhanced pore connectivity and most likely generates additional active pore structure. Inversely, US reveals lower cumulative pore volume and weaker pore development, suggesting a denser and less porous structure. The computed surface area for US and TAS are 65.1 m² g⁻¹ and 95.4 m² g⁻¹ respectively, which could be advantageous for adsorption applications because of greater accessibility of active adsorption sites for pollutant sequestration.

Physiochemical analysis on raw paint effluent using US and TAS

The physicochemical characteristics of raw paint effluent and treated samples using thermally activated sludge (TAS) and unactivated sludge (US) are presented in Table 1. Across most of the parameters tested, the results show considerable improvement in effluent quality after adsorption, with TAS consistently outperforming US. The values of temperature didn’t change much with TAS-treated samples obtained at 28.5 °C and US-treated effluent at 27.9 °C which are well below the WHO limit of 40 °C. This suggests that the treatment process did not significantly influence thermal characteristics and that temperature was not a limiting factor in the treatment process. The raw paint effluent showed an alkaline pH of 9.8, exceeding the WHO permissible range (6.5–8.5). After treatment, the pH dropped to 7.2 for TAS and 8.7 for US. The declined toward neutrality in pH after treatment with both adsorbents signifies effective interaction between functional groups on the activated sludge surface and the alkaline constituents of the effluent. Similar reports have been documented in recent adsorption studies where thermally modified sludge showed improved buffering capacity because of the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups46. Electrical conductivity (EC) reduced noticeably from 950 µS/cm in the raw effluent to 637 µS/cm after and 350 µS/cm after US and TAS treatment respectively. The observed greater reduction with TAS (≈ 63%) compared with US (≈ 33%) indicates improved removal of dissolved ionic species which could be attributed to the thermal activation of the sludge that increase pore development and surface area, thus enhancing ion adsorption capacity, which explains the superior performance of TAS47. For TDS analysis, a substantial decline in value was observed which drops from 575 mg/L in the raw sample to 207 mg/L (US) and 135 mg/L (TAS). The better removal efficiency of TAS suggests enhanced adsorption of dissolved inorganic and organic constituents which is consistence with previous studies which reported that thermally treated sludge exhibits increased surface functionality and microporosity, which facilitates dissolved pollutant uptake48.

TSS values reduced drastically from 75 mg/L in the raw effluent to 35 mg/L and 20 mg/L after treatment with US and TAS respectively with TAS achieving higher removal efficiency, although, both treatments met the WHO limit (< 100 mg/L). The decline in TSS value can be as a result of particle entrapment within sludge flocs and surface adsorption mechanisms. The COD values was also observed to have declined significantly from 310 mg/L in the raw effluent to 103 mg/L and 80 mg/L after US and TAS treatment respectively, bringing both within the WHO permissible limit (< 250 mg/L). The presence of high COD in paint effluent is often attributed to the presence of solvents, resins, and pigments. The greater removal achieved by TAS indicates more effective removal of oxidizable organic matter, which is in alignment with reports that activated sludge–based adsorbents can achieve substantial COD reduction in industrial effluents49. A combined coagulation–adsorption systems by Hu et al.50, have reported up to 68.46% COD removal and with enhanced transmittance of the wastewater from 23% to 89%.

The value of the BOD after treatment with US and TAS reduced from 90 mg/L in the untreated effluent to 22 mg/L (US) and 12 mg/L (TAS) corresponding to removal efficiencies of approximately 76%, and 87% respectively. The noticeable decline suggests effective elimination of biodegradable organic compounds and lower BOD values signify reduced risk of oxygen depletion in receiving water bodies. The treatment of the paint effluent with US and TAS caused a dramatically reduction of oil and grease concentration from 15 mg/L in the raw effluent to 0.55 mg/L and 0.32 mg/L with both well below the WHO limit of 10 mg/L. The high removal efficiency (> 96%) suggests strong hydrophobic interactions and pore trapping within the sludge matrix. Paint effluents typically contain oily binders and additives, and the results demonstrate that sludge-based adsorbents are highly effective for their removal.

Analysis of the content of heavy metals in the paint effluent before and after treatment with US and TAS show significant reduction after treatment. For instance, Pb reduced from 0.05 mg/L to 0.03 mg/L with US and non-detectable levels with TAS, Cd declined from 0.02 mg/L to 0.008 mg/L (US) and 0.003 mg/L (TAS), and Cr decreased from 0.12 mg/L to 0.03 mg/L with US and non-detectable levels with TAS. The improvement observed with heavy metal removal by TAS could be as a result of the increased surface functional groups and porosity created during thermal activation. Recent studies have reported similar findings that thermally activated sludge exhibits high affinity for heavy metals in industrial wastewater32, 46.

Nitrate and chloride concentration after treatment with US dropped from 60 mg/L and 34.6 mg/L in the raw effluent to 45.1 mg/L and 33.6 mg/L, whereas with TAS, the value declined to 38.3 mg/L and 23.4 mg/L. It was observed that TAS successfully reduced nitrate below the regulatory threshold, while US achieved only marginal compliance. The removal likely took place via adsorption and partial biological assimilation within the sludge matrix. The small removal observed with chloride ions is likely due to their soluble nature and weakly adsorbed compared with heavy metals and organic pollutants. Generally, the post-treatment effluent from TAS met WHO discharge standards for all parameters measured, signifying its suitability for safe discharge and potential reuse applications. While US also enhanced effluent quality, its performance was comparatively lower, most especially for COD, BOD, EC, heavy metals, and nitrate.

Equilibrium adsorption study

Contact time plays a vital role in evaluating the adsorption performance of adsorbents because it influences the interaction between the available active sites on the adsorbent surface and the adsorbate molecules. Herein, adsorption efficiency rose with increasing contact time for both adsorbents until equilibrium was reached as shown in Fig. 7a. In the case of the unactivated sludge, the percentage removal rose from 35.6% at 30 min to 65.4% at 150 min after which a slight decline and stabilization were noticed. Also, for the thermally activated sludge, the percentage removal displays an increase from 44.5% at 30 min to 85.3% at 180 min, suggesting a higher adsorption capacity compared to US. The observed initial rapid adsorption for both adsorbents could be as a result of the abundance of vacant active adsorption sites and high concentration gradient between the adsorbent surface and the adsorbate51, 52. But with an increase in the contact time, these empty active sites become gradually occupied with contaminants, resulting into slower adsorption rates until equilibrium is established51, 52. Related behavior pattern has been documented in adsorption studies using sludge-derived adsorbents, where rapid uptake took place at the initial because of surface adsorption followed by slower intraparticle diffusion processes53. The enhanced surface area, pore structure, and the number of active functional groups on the sludge surface could be responsible for the higher adsorption capacity observed for TAS compared with US as the thermal activation transformed the sludge into porous carbon-like materials, thus improving adsorption efficiency54.

Effect of operational parameters on adsorption efficiency of paint effluent using US and TAS: (a) contact time, (b) temperature, (c) solution pH, and (d) initial effluent concentration.

Adsorption processes are often affected significantly by temperature which influences the mobility of adsorbate molecules and the interaction between adsorbent surfaces and adsorbate. As depicted in Fig. 7b, the adsorption percentage rose from 45.6% at 25 °C to 65.4% at 45 °C, followed by a decrease to 59.4% at 75 °C for US. Also, TAS displayed an increase from 53.6% at 25 °C to 77.4% at 55 °C, after which adsorption efficiency decreased at higher temperatures. The observed rise in adsorption with temperature has been reported to be attributed to enhanced mass transfer and increased pore accessibility55. But, the reduction in adsorption efficiency at elevated temperatures may be because of the weakening of adsorptive forces between adsorbate molecules and the adsorbent surface, as well as possible structural changes in the adsorbent material causing desorption of already adsorbed pollutant molecules or destabilization of adsorbent–adsorbate interactions56.

One of the most critical parameters influencing adsorption process is the solution pH because it impacts both the ionization state of pollutants in solution as well as the surface charge of the adsorbent. The results as presented in Fig. 7c reveal that adsorption efficiency rose with increasing pH for both adsorbents with US showing an increase in removal efficiency from 24.9% at pH 2 to 65.7% at pH 10, while TAS rose from 24.5% at pH 2 to 86.9% at pH 10. The low uptake observed within acidic pH may be because of high concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) which compete with contaminants molecules for available adsorption sites on the sludge surface56, 57. However, with an increase in solution pH value, the surface of the adsorbent becomes more negatively charged, facilitating electrostatic attraction between the positively charged dye molecules present in paint effluent and the adsorbent surface53. Furthermore, alkaline conditions may enhance the deprotonation of functional groups including hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on the sludge surface, which increases the number of negatively charged adsorption sites and explains the highest adsorption efficiency noticed at pH 10, particularly for TAS. The driving force for mass transfer between the adsorbent surface and the adsorbate in solution is usually influenced by the initial effluent concentration of the contaminant. The findings reveal that adsorption efficiency rose with increasing effluent concentration for both adsorbents as illustrated in Fig. 7d. In the case of US, the removal efficiency rose from 51.5% at 25 mg/L to 69.2% at 125 mg/L, while TAS surged from 65.4% to 85.3% over the same concentration range. This pattern indicates that higher contaminant concentration provides a stronger driving forces for mass transfer, which improves the diffusion of dye molecules toward the adsorbent surface55.

Mechanism of the adsorption process

The uptake of paint effluent by unactivated sludge (US) and thermally activated sludge (TAS) is best explained as a multi-step and multi-mechanistic process, rather than being governed by a single dominant mechanism. The integration of kinetic, isotherm, thermodynamic, and characterization results provides a coherent framework for understanding the adsorption pathway. First, the good agreement of TAS isothermal data with the Freundlich model suggests that adsorption occurs predominantly on heterogeneous surface sites, whereas Langmuir model perfectly described the data for US inferring homogeneous surface. Kinetic assessment showed that the pseudo-second-order model provided the best fit to towards TAS data indicating chemisorption, while the pseudo-first-order model best explained the US and suggesting that the adsorption rate is controlled by the availability and occupation of active sites through physisorption mechanism. Thermodynamic parameters corroborates this with the enthalpy change (ΔH° ≈ 46.37 kJ/mol for TAS) lies within the range typically associated with strong interactions, including surface complexation and ion exchange, while the lower value observed for US (≈ 18.26 kJ/mol) indicates weaker, predominantly physical interactions. This distinction confirms that thermal activation enhances the interaction strength between adsorbent and adsorbate. The intraparticle diffusion model and SEM observations indicate that mass transfer limitations and pore-filling mechanisms also play a significant role. The non-zero intercept in the diffusion plots confirms that intraparticle diffusion is not the sole rate-limiting step, and adsorption proceeds through multiple sequential stages, including boundary layer diffusion and internal pore diffusion. FTIR analysis further supports the involvement of surface functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl moieties, which participate in hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and possible complexation with pollutant species. Based on these combined observations, the adsorption mechanism can be described as follows:

Modified biochars exhibited adsorption capacities between 20 and 800 mg/g, signifying that chemical or metal modification substantially improve their adsorption properties. The modification of the surface often leads to the introduction of additional functional groups and improves pore development, thus increasing affinity toward metals, dyes, and other emerging contaminants. The wide adsorption range noticed for modified biochars indicates that their performance strongly depends on the feedstock type and modification method employed. Sludge-derived adsorbents/biochars showed comparatively smaller adsorption capacities of 10–400 mg/g. Though their performance is generally smaller compare to CAC and MOFs, sludge-derived adsorbents provide important economic and environmental advantages because they convert waste sludge into useful adsorbent materials. Their adsorption efficiency is influenced by factors such as activation temperature, sludge composition, and activation method. Consequently, optimization of preparation conditions can significantly improve their adsorption performance. From this study, the thermally activated sludge developed achieved an adsorption capacity of 77.21 mg/g for paint effluent removal. This value falls within the reported range for sludge-derived adsorbents and reveals that thermally activated sludge can serve as an effective low-cost adsorbent for wastewater treatment. Although its adsorption capacity is lower relative to those observed in CAC and MOFs, the material offers advantages in terms of waste valorization, affordability, and sustainable sludge management.

Effect of reuse

Figure 13 displays the variation in PD (%) with increasing numbers of reuse cycles for the two sample groups, namely US and TAS. The findings display a small gradual decline in PD (%) for both US and TAS samples as the number of reuse cycles increases. Though, TAS consistently exhibits higher PD (%) values than US throughout all reuse cycles, indicating superior stability and reusability performance. In the case of the US sample, PD (%) declines from 71.2% during the first reuse cycle to 63.4% after the fifth cycle. Likewise, the TAS sample exhibits a decreasing trend, with PD (%) reducing from nearly 79.3% in the first cycle to around 71.2% by the fifth cycle. This reduction signifies a progressive loss in performance, most likely because of active site blockage, structural degradation, or reduced functional efficiency upon repeated usage. Despite this decrease, TAS maintains significantly higher PD (%) values relative to US at every reuse stage which indicates better retention of activity and enhanced operational durability.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that thermally activated sludge calcined at 700 °C is a highly efficient adsorbent for the treatment of paint industry wastewater. Thermal activation enhances sludge crystallinity, surface area, and surface chemical functionality, generating a porous and heterogeneous adsorbent structure with abundant active sites that favor efficient monolayer adsorption. Complementary kinetic analysis reveals that intra-particle pore diffusion acts as the rate-limiting step, while chemisorption drives the overall uptake of pollutants, highlighting the critical role of adsorbent accessibility and porosity in achieving optimal treatment outcomes. Importantly, the integration of Response Surface Methodology (RSM) provided valuable insights into process optimization and variable interactions. The quadratic model highlighted pH as the most influential factor, along with significant interaction effects between pH and adsorbent dosage. Although the model exhibited moderate predictive capability, it successfully identified optimal operational conditions that maximized adsorption efficiency. The regeneration study confirmed that the thermally activated sludge can be reused for multiple cycles with minimal loss in adsorption efficiency, supporting its practical applicability.These findings underscore the importance of multivariable optimization in complex wastewater systems. By integrating sludge-based adsorption into industrial wastewater systems, manufacturing sectors can achieve effective pollutant mitigation while promoting resource recovery and environmental stewardship.
