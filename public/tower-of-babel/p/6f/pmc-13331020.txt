Physical activity promotion among HIV providers in Colorado: an explanatory sequential mixed-methods study
Authors: Grace L. Kulik, Ashley Turner, Rachael Brink Akay, Kelly K. O’Brien, Evelyn Iriarte, Kristine M. Erlandson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe purpose of this study was to characterise physical activity promotion and explore strategies to increase physical activity promotion among HIV providers at an outpatient infectious disease clinic.MethodsWe conducted a mixed-methods study with an explanatory sequential design using a questionnaire and semi-structured interviews. We recruited providers with an independent caseload of people living with HIV (PLHIV) in an outpatient HIV clinic. We administered a questionnaire asking about physical activity promotion with patients, followed by a semi-structured interview with a subsample of participants. We analysed questionnaire responses descriptively and interview transcripts were analysed using a conventional content analysis.ResultsSeventeen providers participated in the study. Questionnaire responses (n=17) indicated that 88% of providers felt that physical activity promotion was a worthwhile intervention, but 65% did not feel that there was sufficient compensation from insurance companies or clinic resources to promote physical activity. In interviews (n=8), providers reported they felt that more structured physical activity advice for patients would be most beneficial.ConclusionsUnderstanding the extent of physical activity promotion among providers, as well as barriers and facilitators of this behaviour, may inform implementation of strategies to maximise health promotion. Providers generally promoted physical activity but discussed the need for structured physical activity interventions and described the current systems-level barriers for activity promotion. The incorporation of an in-clinic specialist was proposed as a potential strategy to better facilitate initiation and sustained adherence to physical activity with PLHIV but should be explored in future studies.

Introduction

People living with HIV (PLHIV) are prone to an accelerated development of ageing-related comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, metabolic abnormalities and frailty.14 This accelerated development of comorbidities often results in a greater accumulation of functional impairments with ageing, creating an urgent need for interventions to attenuate this process.3 Physical activity is a safe and effective intervention to mitigate consequences and improve function, both among PLHIV and in the general population.58

Despite evidence supporting the importance of physical activity, PLHIV report high rates of sedentary behaviour compared with the general population, with only 50% meeting recommended physical activity guidelines.9 10 Prior qualitative studies that have explored attitudes towards exercise or physical activity among PLHIV identified individual and environmental factors influencing physical activity, such as stigma when attending a gym, boredom with exercise, lack of support and lack of specific recommendations from providers.91113 In the general adult population, physical activity recommendations from physicians are associated with increased physical activity and have been reported as a motivating factor for patients.1417 However, only one-third of patients in the general population report receiving physical activity advice from their primary care providers, and this number is unknown among PLHIV.1820

Whereas physical activity encompasses all bodily movement, exercise can be thought of as a subset of physical activity that is structured and intentionally focused on improving one or more components of physical fitness.21 Exercise counselling and prescriptions are often used as a way to increase overall physical activity and physical function in the general population and among those with HIV.2224 Providers often face barriers that impede consistent counselling of any physical activity recommendations, including priority of other health concerns, lack of knowledge or confidence with writing exercise prescriptions (especially among infectious disease providers who serve as primary care providers for PLHIV) and limited time during clinic encounters.25 26 Known patient barriers may also dissuade providers from giving physical activity recommendations, such as being unhoused, having low income or working multiple jobs.27 Strategies to overcome these provider-reported barriers are needed to ultimately initiate and sustain physical activity in PLHIV. To our knowledge, no studies to date have explored attitudes and behaviours of physical activity promotion from primary care providers among PLHIV. Furthermore, using a mixed-methods approach can provide deeper context beyond just a quantitative characterisation of this behaviour among providers. Understanding the extent of physical activity promotion, as well as barriers and facilitators of promotion, may inform the design of future trials to maximise physical activity uptake and long-term adherence. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to (1) characterise current physical activity promotion behaviours from HIV providers with PLHIV in an outpatient infectious disease clinic and (2) identify strategies that would be most beneficial in facilitating greater physical activity promotion with PLHIV.

Materials and methods

We conducted an explanatory sequential mixed-methods design, in which quantitative data collection and analysis preceded and informed the qualitative phase.28 This design was selected to first characterise providers’ physical activity promotion behaviours and perceived barriers using questionnaire data and subsequently to generate an in-depth understanding of these findings through semi-structured interviews. The quantitative results directly informed the development of the interview guide, allowing for targeted exploration of key gaps and patterns identified in the survey.28 Consistent with this approach, the study followed a quantitative-qualitative sequence, with emphasis placed on the qualitative data to contextualise and expand on the quantitative findings. Another point of integration within this mixed-methods study was the merging of quantitative and qualitative data using a joint display table. Joint displays represent integration of data,29 in which findings were juxtaposed and meta-inferences, or insights, were revealed.30

Participants and recruitment

We recruited from all eligible healthcare providers (n=28 providers) in the University of Colorado Health Infectious Disease Clinic. The clinic is an infectious disease group practice serving over 2000 patients, who have a mean age of 50 years.31 To be included in the study, healthcare providers had to have an independent caseload of patients with HIV and work at least one half-day in the clinic per week. All included providers were trained in the specialty of infectious diseases and provided primary care for those with HIV. Providers were excluded if providing physical activity counselling and/or medical advice (ie, medical assistants (MAs)) was outside their scope of practice or if they were medical students or residents without an independent caseload.

Data collection

Questionnaire: we administered an electronic questionnaire through Research Electronic Data Capture that was modified from a survey by Rethorn et al that examined physical activity practices and promotion behaviour among physical therapists (online supplemental material 1).32 33 This questionnaire aimed to assess the three central elements (capability, opportunity and motivation) of the Behaviour Change Wheel framework.34

Interview: on completion of the questionnaire, participating providers could indicate interest in participating in a subsequent semi-structured interview. Results from the questionnaire informed the semi-structured interview guide, specifically on current physical activity promotion behaviours and strategies to better facilitate physical activity promotion (online supplemental material 2). Qualitative interviews were conducted online via Zoom with participants between March and April 2024. Interviews were 20–30 min in duration. All interviews were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and de-identified for analysis.

Participation was voluntary without impact on employment. Participants were given a US$15 Amazon gift card for completion of the questionnaire and a US$30 Amazon gift card for completion of the interview.

Quantitative analysis

We used descriptive analysis to describe the results of the questionnaire. The raw frequency (n) and percentage of providers (%) who selected each response for each question was calculated and reported in the results. To protect the confidentiality of the providers in the sample, age was reported in categories by decade and the providers were not asked to report their race.

Interview analysis

We used a conventional content analysis approach, employing inductive coding.35 After immersing themselves in the data by performing an initial read of the transcripts, two researchers (RBA and AT) coded the transcripts. Each researcher coded the first transcript independently, after which the researchers met to discuss the findings and reconcile the codes. Once a consensus was reached, the researchers shared the initial codebook with the entire research team to ensure agreement before they returned to the data and continued the same pattern for the following three transcripts. After meeting to discuss the fourth transcript, each researcher independently coded two of the remaining transcripts. The final transcripts were reconciled. The previously coded transcripts were reviewed and reconciled with each new code created. The final codes were reviewed and categorised during a meeting with the entire research team. The interview transcripts were assigned identification codes (ie, P.1, P.2) to maintain confidentiality of the providers in the presentation of interview quotes. NVivo V.11.0 software was used for data management and coding.

Sample size estimation

The sample size for this study was based on a convenience sample of eligible providers within the clinic (n=28), of whom 17 completed the questionnaire and eight participated as key informants in semi-structured interviews. Consistent with prior qualitative research in HIV and exercise, we anticipated that a sample of approximately 15–20 survey respondents and a smaller key informant subsample (5–10 informants) would be sufficient to generate meaningful insights into provider health promotion behaviours.36 37 For the qualitative component, data saturation served as the stopping criterion for recruitment and analysis, defined as the point at which no new codes or concepts emerged; this was achieved in the final two interviews.38 39

To enhance qualitative credibility, we incorporated strategies aligned with best practices for rigour, including inclusion of providers from diverse clinical roles (eg, physicians and allied health professionals) to capture a range of perspectives within the clinical team. We also actively identified and examined negative or divergent cases that did not align with dominant patterns, using these instances to refine code definitions and thematic interpretations. Regular team-based discussions, iterative codebook development and consensus meetings further supported analytic rigour, credibility and consistency of the findings.

Results

Participant characteristics

Of 28 providers at the Infectious Diseases clinic, 17 consented and completed the questionnaire. Most respondents were female, under 50 years of age and had a clinical doctorate (Doctor of Medicine/Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine). Of the 17 providers, 12 were physicians and five were allied health professionals (nurse practitioners, physician assistants or social workers). Further details are provided in table 1. Eight of the 17 (47%) providers reported having continuing education beyond their medical training in a health promotion focus (ie, physical activity, smoking cessation), and eight of 17 (47%) providers reported taking continuing education in a behaviour change focus (ie, theories of behaviour change, motivational interviewing). Eight of 17 (47%) providers reported that they consider themselves to be much more active than what they perceive the average American to be; five (29%) reported being slightly more active, three (17%) reported being slightly less active and one (6%) provider reported being much less active than the average American population. No providers routinely used a screening tool for physical activity (ie, Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire, Recent Physical Activity Questionnaire). Providers reported providing physical activity counselling primarily for patients with a body mass index >30 kg/m2, diabetes or all patients. The specific breakdown of patients for whom providers reported providing physical activity counselling can be found in online supplemental figure 1.

Quantitative data

Fifteen of 17 (88%) providers somewhat or strongly agreed that delivering physical activity promotion with their patients was worthwhile while 11 of 17 (65%) providers agreed that other patient concerns were typically prioritised over delivering physical activity promotion (figure 1). Eighty-two percent of providers strongly or somewhat disagreed that insurance companies provide sufficient support or compensation for time spent promoting physical activity, and 65% disagreed with the statement that the clinic provided all necessary resources to deliver this intervention. Qualitative data

Among the eight providers who participated in the semi-structured interviews, six primary themes were identified from the qualitative analysis and three were deemed to address study objectives. These themes were integrated with questionnaire findings and selected for presentation in the manuscript. The three themes discussed here are the provider’s current physical activity promotion behaviour, the need for structured physical activity promotion and the system-level capacity to assess and promote physical activity. Additional themes from the interviews that are beyond the scope of the study that pertain broadly to providers reflecting on patient perceptions of physical activity can be found in online supplemental table 1.

Provider’s current physical activity promotion behaviour

Providers described how they addressed physical activity in clinic visits with PLHIV. All interviewed providers reported consistently having conversations about physical activity with most of their patients. Even though physical activity promotion was occasionally viewed as an extra step, they viewed it as an important component of care. Providers reported that physical activity promotion typically only occurs during a ‘wellness preventative visit’ or a standard HIV check-up, not visits to address acute medical issues. One provider stated that they felt it was important to promote physical activity across the age spectrum:

So, for all my follow-up patients, no matter the age, I do talk about physical activity, at least every visit or every other visit or something of that sort, because I see problems across the spectrum. Whether it’s a 20-year-old or a 70-year-old, it’s the same problem. So, I talk about diet and physical activity to all my patients. P.4

Several providers expressed that the discussion of physical activity and the advice or recommendations given are dependent on the type of patient. For example, one provider said that they prefer to refer patients with chronic conditions to more structured or supervised programmes.

I have another 30-40% of people who are like older, who have chronic issues, multiple chronic issues and all of that. For them, I think a program might be helpful to just give some direction. P.4

Another provider noted that they try to meet patients where they are at, because some physical activity is better than none to start with.

I always say it’s 150 minutes of moderate-intensity and or 75 minutes of high-intensity exercise per week. I tried to meet people where they’re at. So, if somebody’s saying I’m having a really hard time. So, I talked about like, well, can you get up, maybe like, five to 10 minutes earlier, and start stretching? And we talked about committing to small increments. P.5

Providers intentionally tailored conversations towards specific groups of patients. For example, one provider stated that they really emphasise physical activity for patients going on antipsychotic medications, as they tend to induce weight gain or patients with depression.

I think I address it when I put people on the antipsychotic medications because they tend to cause weight gain, and you know, it’s harder to get it off once you get it on. And then so, or just even some of the, if they’re, if they tend to be overweight, any medications, even antidepressants, I address it as well, but definitely with the antipsychotics. And then a lot of people, or most of my patients that have treatment-resistant depression, I address physical movement as a strategy for depression and to get out of depression. And then, you know, when they, of course, if they bring it up, part of their self-esteem issues, it comes up a lot. P.2

Need for structured physical activity promotion

Providers discussed how patients need a structured physical activity programme or intervention as opposed to just being told to ‘stay active’. When asked about whether or not they currently print out or use an exercise handout or instruction sheet, one provider noted that a handout was perceived as a hassle to print during the session and would likely not be used:

No, because that means I have to find the printer and do the printer and make sure the printer works, which most of the many times does not. And yeah, and it usually ends up on the bus. P.3

Providers indicated that having a ‘health coach’ service available for patients would be an effective intervention to increase physical activity. Some providers felt that having a health coach to educate on physical activity in addition to diet and nutrition would be the most beneficial.

I definitely think if there are structured sessions like that, where there is some diet and exercise and all of this kind of a lifestyle, a coach kind of a person in there who can talk about everything because diet is a huge issue. I mean, exercise is one component, but people don’t even know what to eat and what not to eat. P.4

Another topic that emerged was how having an ongoing clinical exercise trial at the University was successful for a handful of patients. Contrastingly, physical therapy was mentioned as a potential starting point for structured physical activity in the form of rehabilitation but had a significant number of barriers. Barriers to physical therapy included lack of referral uptake, lack of insurance coverage for rehabilitation services and high co-pays with limited sessions even when insurance did cover. Additionally, although it may be used as a starting point, providers felt that physical therapy did not equate to physical activity or a structured exercise programme.

I have a half dozen people in physical therapy, and it’s just that they’re only addressing the knee pain, the back pain. They’re not really doing an exercise program. P.2

Although physical therapists may provide return-to-sport exercise programmes, most insurance companies in the USA will not reimburse for time that is spent on a structured exercise programme. Therefore, physical therapists are required to focus on a specific injury complaint. While therapeutic exercise is a billable code and often used during rehabilitation, structured and progressive exercise is often only provided to sport or tactical athletes.

I would say most of my physical therapy referrals are based on an issue as opposed to a well patient that just wants to start exercise. P.7

System-level capacity to assess and promote physical activity

In regard to assessing physical activity, one provider stated that it was important to keep it simple so that an MA could perform the assessment during the routine measurement of vital signs.

I think that’s the tricky thing is a lot of these physical activity questions end up with like 10 questions of how many days of activity, what activity do you do? And we don’t want it to be something the MAs don’t do. It’s gotta be something super simple that they’re potentially able to incorporate. P.2

One provider expressed concern over the time it would take to perform a physical activity screening assessment. A screening assessment would be more troublesome than beneficial if it posed scheduling problems:

It’s all about scheduling, right? So, if I know that all my patients will be in a room 10 minutes after the hour, after their scheduled time, then I’m okay with that. What I don’t want to do is come to the clinic and sit around. P.1

Several providers reported that having a ‘health coach’ service centralised or located within the clinic would facilitate the greatest uptake in utilisation compared with external referrals. Centralised care is particularly beneficial for this population, which faces unique barriers when seeking outside care, such as stigma. Providers brought up unsuccessful examples of previous times that they have referred to other specialists.

I have referred to nutritionists or dieticians, which has helped in a couple of patients, but in general, it’s just like another place they must go to. And, you know, our patient population has had so much stigma and felt awful about the medical field in general. It’s not as easy to get them to go someplace else. P.3

Providers noticed a lack of follow-up on referrals, even to specialists for physical function-related impairments, such as physical therapy.

So, if things could be located in that area, I think it is workable, but every time I start referring people out, it just falls off and it becomes really hard to actually implement. P.4

Mixed-methods analysis

Two distinct points of mixed-methods analysis were conducted to build on and integrate the study findings. First, questionnaire data were analysed to identify areas requiring further exploration. These findings informed the development of the interview guide, which was designed to clarify and expand on information gained from the questionnaire data. After interview data were collected and analysed to identify codes and themes, both the questionnaire and interview data questions were populated in a joint display table (table 2). Evaluating the data in parallel resulted in meta-inferences and added depth to the understanding of providers’ view on addressing physical activity in PLHIV. To illustrate, the questionnaire data identified that providers place value in discussing physical activity with their patients and questions were included in the interview to delve further into this concept. Comparing the quantitative and qualitative data resulted in the understanding that while providers value addressing physical activity in PLHIV, there are several limitations that hinder the consistent application in practice including systems-level challenges, time and availability of resources. Insights from the mixed-methods analysis can be used to create targeted clinical interventions.

Discussion

Findings from this study show that this sample of HIV providers felt that physical activity was an important intervention, but there were not enough resources available through insurance reimbursement or the clinic to facilitate physical activity promotion. Qualitative interviews were integrated with questionnaire findings and identified the clinic resources that would be most beneficial to facilitate physical activity promotion. Additionally, providers felt that providing options for a more structured physical activity intervention, beyond general guidance, would be most effective. An in-clinic health coach was proposed as an ideal option to incorporate into the existing clinic infrastructure. An in-clinic health coach would assess each patients’ current activity habits and intervene by providing education on physical activity, exercise prescriptions, setting personalised goals and performing intermittent follow-ups.

Among the cohort of HIV providers in the present study, the vast majority agreed or strongly agreed on the importance of physical activity promotion. This finding is in line with several international studies that examined physical activity promotion activity among primary care providers.4042 Approximately >90% of primary care providers in England and Scotland, respectively, agreed or strongly agreed with the statement that physical activity promotion is important in their practice.40 41 Most providers in our study agreed that physical activity promotion would help patients become more physically active, consistent with other studies where 84% of providers perceived physical activity counselling as ‘very effective’ and 92% of physicians agreed that they could be effective in increasing physical activity.41 42 A systematic review of physical activity promotion among primary care providers had mixed results,43 and a separate study found that approximately half of the physicians estimated <25% patient adherence to physical activity prescriptions.44 Finally, while our questionnaire did not specifically ask about barriers, 82% of our respondents did not feel that there was sufficient compensation from the clinic or insurance companies to promote physical activity. Lack of financial reimbursement for physical activity promotion was also reported to be a barrier from pooled studies in the systematic review.43

During the semi-structured interviews, providers in our study emphasised a need for more structured physical activity guidance to provide patients. This theme parallels previous findings from qualitative studies of patients’ perspectives on physical activity promotion and counselling. Patients reported wanting tangible physical activity recommendations and that more structured guidance improved individual self-efficacy, which better facilitated engagement in physical activity.45 46 The level of support from the primary care provider was an important aspect, as patients were dissatisfied when providers were ‘instructing without supporting’.45

Providers in our study suggested that having an exercise specialist or physical therapist within the clinic would likely be the most beneficial for patients. This finding expands on themes from several qualitative studies, which identified that referral for focused physical activity guidance would be the most beneficial.46 47 One study identified potential benefits of a ‘physical exercise coordinator’ who could review patients’ current limitations, exercises they enjoy and provide structured advice.46 Similarly, healthcare providers in Germany expressed frustration at the lack of established referral options for individuals specialised in the promotion of physical activity.47 For patients with chronic pain, recovering from a recent hospitalisation or injury, or have significant balance deficits, an in-clinic physical therapist would be another viable option, as physical activity promotion also falls within the scope of practice for physical therapists. Physical therapy has been successfully integrated in other outpatient HIV clinics,37 48 49 but may be limited by insurance policies surrounding referral justifications for patients who only require physical activity education and behaviour change strategies.

The desire for services within the HIV clinic may be a theme unique to PLHIV. In prior studies, PLHIV reported that embedding services within the HIV clinic is preferred compared with being referred to a specialist outside of the clinic.50 51 In a study by Davis et al, 61% of participants living with HIV expressed that they would only see an ageing specialist if the provider was co-located inside the HIV clinic.52 Qualitative studies describing patient perspectives after receiving care within the HIV clinic further underscore these findings, with PLHIV reporting high satisfaction in clinic models that offer centralised, multidisciplinary services.51 53 54

Although this study was not originally designed to capture themes according to a behavioural theory framework, the findings can be conceptually interpreted through the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour model.34 Providers reported the need for more structured guidance aligns with the ‘capability’ domain, as it reflects gaps in knowledge to perform physical activity counselling. The reported lack of time, referral pathways and insurance reimbursement fall within the ‘opportunity’ domain. Encouragingly, providers recognised the importance and value of physical activity for PLHIV, suggesting high ‘motivation’ to engage in PA promotion when feasible. Although the motivation domain may be met, finding strategies or interventions to enhance the capability and opportunity domains may improve overall PA promotion among HIV providers.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample was limited to a single outpatient infectious disease clinic in Colorado. Providers in Colorado may be more prone to physical activity promotion given the high physical activity levels of the state, which also may limit generalisability and transferability of the results to other states.55 Second, we had a small sample size of providers, and the providers who consented to the interview were those who reported consistent physical activity promotion. Therefore, we were unable to obtain the perspectives of providers who did not routinely discuss physical activity. Finally, we did not interview patients in this study, although we have previously explored exercise barriers and facilitators for this clinic population.11

In conclusion, within this clinic-based sample of HIV providers, physical activity was viewed as an important component of healthcare for people ageing with HIV. The majority of providers valued physical activity promotion in their own practice but expressed the need for more structured guidance and options for in-clinic referrals for more individualised physical activity and nutrition advice. Our findings suggest that provider-level resources and structured referral pathways may be important considerations for facilitating physical activity promotion in HIV primary care. Although exploratory, the results provide preliminary insights that may inform the design of future implementation trials for provider-level physical activity promotion interventions.
