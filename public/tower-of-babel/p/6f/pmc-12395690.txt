Gender differences regarding interest in opioid agonist treatment with hydromorphone: a cross-sectional study of syringe service program participants
Authors: Vitaliy Lasiychuk, Megan Ghiroli, Teresa López-Castro, Aaron D. Fox
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the United States, only 15–20% of people with opioid use disorder (OUD) receive medications for OUD annually. Injectable opioid agonist treatment with hydromorphone (iOAT-H) is a novel approach that reduces non-prescribed opioid use. However, interest in iOAT-H among women, who face unique challenges in OUD treatment, may be different than men. This study evaluates women’s attitudes toward iOAT-H, and gender disparities in OUD-related risks, exploring potential gender-specific treatment needs. We hypothesized that women would be less interested in iOAT-H than men given the disproportionate stigma women face in accessing OUD treatment.MethodsThis is a secondary analysis of a cross-sectional study of urban syringe service program participants. Participants self-reported interest in iOAT-H, which was assessed on a 4-point scale with 3 or 4 considered “interested.” Participants also provided self-reported data on preferences for OUD treatment and their OUD-related risks, including frequency of drug use and injection practices. Descriptive statistics and comparative analyses were employed to explore gender differences.ResultsOf 108 participants, 31% were women and 69% were men with a mean age of 43.0 (SD ± 10.8). Almost all participants had severe OUD (98.1%) and had prior OUD care (96.3%). Most women (68%) and men (64%) reported interest in iOAT-H. For OUD treatment preferences, most women chose iOAT-H (56%), while men most commonly endorsed methadone treatment (42%) as their preferred option. Women had more OUD-related risks than men including injecting more times per day (5 vs. 3, p > 0.01) and injecting in public more often (24 vs. 7, p = 0.05) in the past 30 days.ConclusionInterest in iOAT-H was high among women and men, but we found gender differences in OUD-related risks. This may reflect unmet needs among women who would potentially enter treatment with iOAT-H, but additional work is necessary to examine specific risks such as mental health, trauma exposure, and creating safe treatment spaces for women. Nonetheless, iOAT-H appeared acceptable to women in our sample, and many treatment-experienced people who inject drugs would prefer iOAT-H to other available OUD treatments.

Background

New options for opioid use disorder (OUD) treatment are needed in the United States (US). In 2023, there were between 5.7 and 17.7 million people in the US with OUD [1, 2]. Most do not engage in treatment with only approximately 13% receiving medications for OUD (MOUD) and 22% receiving any OUD treatment annually [3, 4]. Food and Drug Administration-approved MOUDs, methadone, naltrexone, and buprenorphine [5], reduce risks for infectious disease, incarceration, and overdose [6]; however, only 43−57% of those who start treatment continue it for one year or more, and overdose mortality increases dramatically when MOUD is discontinued [6–8]. Some US data suggest that more than half of those starting MOUD discontinue treatment within 30 days [9]. In 2024, there were 54,743 opioid-related deaths with synthetic opioids, primarily fentanyl, accounting for 48,422 of these deaths [10]. In the context of the current dangerous illegal drug supply, MOUD that surpasses available options in reducing non-prescribed opioid use and retaining people with severe OUD in treatment is greatly needed.

Injectable opioid agonist treatment (iOAT) is available in several European countries and Canada, and clinical trials have been conducted in Australia, but iOAT is not available in the US [11]. Patients receive high-potency short-acting opioids (e.g., diacetylmorphine or hydromorphone) for self-injection under the supervision of medical staff. Two reviews including 10 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found iOAT with diacetylmorphine (iOAT-D) to be more effective than conventional MOUD in reducing non-prescribed opioid use among persons with severe OUD [12, 13]. A single RCT of iOAT with hydromorphone (iOAT-H) demonstrated non-inferiority to iOAT-D in opioid use outcomes with fewer severe adverse events (e.g., seizures) [14–16]. Hydromorphone may be the more feasible MOUD in the US of these two, because it is a schedule II drug that is already used for severe pain, while diacetylmorphine is a schedule I drug, meaning it has no approved uses. Thus, there are safety and feasibility reasons to advance research on iOAT-H.

Prior research has established interest in iOAT-H among people who inject drugs (PWID) in the US and Australia, but subgroups of PWID may have different attitudes toward iOAT-H [17–19]. Women with OUD, who are more likely than men to have a history of trauma [20–22], and comorbid psychiatric conditions such as anxiety, depression, and mood disorders [23, 24], have different OUD treatment needs than men. Women are more likely than men to report chronic pain and tend to progress from opioid use to addiction more rapidly [24–26]. Women may also face more stigma when identified with substance use disorders, which could impact their willingness to attend OUD treatment programs [27–29]. For example, in two qualitative studies, one with participants from a prior Canadian iOAT trial and the other with patients receiving iOAT in Vancouver, British Columbia, women reported discomfort with being observed by medical staff while injecting (i.e., loss of privacy) [30, 31]. Therefore, while most OUD treatment in the US has been delivered in similar ways to women and men, the gender differences highlighted above suggest a potential need for gender-specific approaches, especially in planning for iOAT in the US.

This study is a secondary analysis of cross-sectional data that assessed attitudes toward iOAT-H among syringe services program (SSP) participants in New York City (NYC). We sought to determine whether attitudes toward iOAT-H differed between women and men, hypothesizing that women would be less interested in iOAT-H than men given the disproportionate stigma women face in accessing OUD treatment. In this study we focused solely on women and men because of sample size limitations that failed to encompass a broader spectrum of gender identity. Our main objectives were to describe differences based on gender in: (1) participants’ substance use and co-morbidities, which may reflect different treatment needs; and (2) preferences for OUD treatment and interest in iOAT-H.

Methods

Study procedures

PWID were recruited from 3 SSPs in New York City between November 2020 to August 2021. The eligibility criteria were: (1) age of ≥ 18 years, (2) self-report of having OUD, (3) had injected opioids in the past, and (4) received services at 1 of the 3 partner SSPs. Staff members posted fliers about the study at the SSP and referred potential participants to the researchers for eligibility screening and informed consent. Due to COVID-19 social distancing precautions, most interviews were done over the phone, and participants who did not have a phone could use one at the SSPs. Qualtrics (Provo, UT) was used to collect survey data. After completing the interview, participants received $20 in cash and a $5.50 round-trip transit pass. The study was reviewed by the Albert Einstein College of Medicine’s Institutional Review Board and deemed exempt research.

Measures

Sociodemographic data

We used a two-step process to collect “sex at birth” and “gender identity.” Participants reported their sex as “Male” or “Female,” and options for gender were “Male,” “Female,” “Transgender,” or “Gender nonconforming.” Participants were not given the option of “non-binary” or a free text option to report a different gender identity. When reporting specific data points we use the terms male and female, but otherwise, we use the terms man and woman to refer to gender identity. To report race/ethnicity, patients selected one of the following options: “White,” “Black/African American,” “Indian (American),” “Alaska Native,” “Pacific Islander,” “Other Asian,” and “Some other race.” Participants were also asked if they identified as Hispanic. Other demographic characteristics included: employment status (dichotomous, yes/no), insurance coverage (Medicaid, Medicare, private insurance, military health benefits, or other), housing status (categorized as stable vs. unstable housing), and prior incarceration (dichotomous, yes/no).

Opioid use disorder

We defined OUD based on the DSM-5 criteria. Participants who answered “yes” to 6 or more of the 11 criteria were categorized as having severe OUD. Participants were also asked (dichotomous, yes/no), if they had ever injected drugs, injected drugs in the past 30 days, and if they had injected opioids in the past 30 days.

Substance use-related measures

Participants were asked how many days within the past 30 days they had used the following drugs: cannabis, cocaine or crack, heroin or fentanyl, methadone, buprenorphine, other opiates/analgesics, benzodiazepines, and amphetamines [32]. The main route of administration was reported for each substance. Non-fatal overdoses were measured by asking, “Throughout your life, have you ever experienced an overdose (by overdose we mean being unresponsive or unable to be woken up, waking up in a hospital or ambulance, collapsing or losing consciousness, having difficulty breathing, or having blue skin due to heroin, fentanyl, or another opioid)?” [33] Participants answering yes reported how many times in their lifetime they had overdosed.

Injection practices

Participants were asked the number of months in the past year that they injected opioids, how many days in the past week that they injected opioids at least once, and how many times per day they injected opioids in the past week. They were also asked if they had ever injected drugs in public, and how many times in the past 30 days they had injected in public.

Treatment history

Participants reported if they had ever received the following OUD services: “Detox”, “Outpatient program with groups or one-on-one counseling”, “Inpatient or residential program”, “Methadone treatment program”, “Buprenorphine or suboxone treatment program”, “Naltrexone”, or “Mutual aid group (AA or NA)”. Patients also reported the number of times they had received each service, and all services were summed as the total number of past “care episodes.” A separate item asked participants to report the number of OUD treatment episodes they received in the past 5 years. We considered participants to be “treatment experienced” if they reported having 2 or more treatment episodes in the past 5 years (consistent with the eligibility criteria of North American iOAT trials) [15]. Participants also reported whether they had experienced each of 10 potential barriers to OUD treatment, meaning reasons that participants had not received OUD treatment in the past. Potential barriers were established through consultation with SSP staff members, several who had lived experience of OUD and different types of OUD treatment. Examples include: inability to pay, travel/time conflicts, poor treatment by medical staff in the past, not wanting to be seen at the treatment site by others, and lack of child care (see Appendix 1a for additional details). Participants reported (dichotomous, yes/no) whether each potential reason applied to them.

Interest in iOAT

Researchers read a description of iOAT-H to participants, which included information on hydromorphone and what treatment would entail (see Appendix 1b for additional details). Participants’ interest in iOAT-H was then gauged by asking, “If it were offered to you, how interested would you be in starting treatment with injection hydromorphone (Dilaudid)?” Those who answered, “Very Interested” and “Somewhat Interested,” were considered interested. Other answers were considered not interested.

Preference for future treatment

We inquired about OUD treatment preference by asking, “If you had the choice of getting any opioid treatment would you choose to get: [OUD treatment option].” Listed options included: “buprenorphine,” “methadone,” “naltrexone (Vivitrol),” “injectable hydromorphone,” “a program without medication,” and “I’m not interested in any treatment.” Of the participants who indicated they preferred injectable hydromorphone, we further inquired about the reason for their preference. We listed several statements that expressed possible reasons participants may have had for their hydromorphone preference. Possible reasons were established through consultation with SSP staff. Answers to each statement were recorded as “strongly disagree,” “disagree,” “undecided or unsure,” “agree,” or “strongly agree” with “strongly agree” or “agree” categorized together and other answers categorized as disagree.

Statistical analysis

We used descriptive statistics to summarize participants’ characteristics based on gender. Specifically, we report means and standard deviations for normally distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) for skewed continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Next, we determined the number of participants who were interested in iOAT-H, and the number that expressed preference for iOAT-H. We then compared sociodemographic and clinical characteristics among participants based on gender. Chi square testing was used for categorical variables, and T-test or Mann-Whitney U tests were used for continuous variables. We also constructed a multivariable logistic regression model with gender as the main independent variable and interest in iOAT-H as the dependent variable. We selected covariates, which included age, current methadone use, and times injecting opioids per day, a priori based on clinical knowledge regarding the population and potential confounds that could affect interest in iOAT-H. We compared reasons for OUD treatment preferences and previous barriers to OUD treatment between women and men using chi square testing. All analyses were performed using Stata software (version16.1, College Station, TX).

Results

Sample description

Of 134 screened SSP participants, 108 enrolled and completed the questionnaire. Most questionnaires were administered in English (87%) and conducted remotely via telephone (70%). Participants were predominantly male (69%), identified as Hispanic (68%), with a mean age of 43.0 years (standard deviation [SD]: ±10.8). Most participants were unstably housed (78%) and reported having health insurance coverage (89%), including 86% with Medicaid and 11% with Medicare.

While both women and men frequently met criteria for OUD severe (100% vs. 97%, respectively), currently injected heroin or fentanyl (94% vs. 87%), injected opioids daily (median days per week: 7 vs. 7) and had previously used methadone treatment (88% vs. 72%), there were several significant differences between participants based on gender (see Table 1). Women (vs. men) reported injecting heroin or fentanyl more times per day in the past week (median: 5 vs. 3, p > 0.01), injecting in public more times in the past month (median: 24 vs. 7, p = 0.05), and a greater proportion had received OUD treatment in the past 5 years (76% vs. 52%, p = 0.02). Women reported a greater mean number of overdoses than men (4.1 vs. 2.3), but these differences were not significant.

Interest in iOAT

Most participants were interested in iOAT-H, and there were no significant differences between women and men (68% vs. 64%) based on chi-square testing. In multivariable regression with gender as the main independent variable and age, current methadone use, and times injecting opioids per day as covariates, differences remained non-significant.

OUD treatment preference

In reporting preference for OUD treatment, women most commonly reported that they would prefer iOAT-H (56%), while men most commonly preferred methadone treatment (42%), but differences in preferences were not statistically significant (see Fig. 1).

Barriers to OUD treatment

All participants had received some past OUD treatment, and there were no significant differences in the reported number of prior treatment episodes based on gender (see Table 1). Women reported a greater median number of treatment episodes in the past 5 years (3 vs. 2), but these differences were not statistically significant. Past barriers that participants had experienced in seeking addiction treatment were similar between women and men with the most common reported barrier being costs. However, significantly more women than men (50% vs. 30%, p = 0.05) reported not having sufficient support as a barrier to addiction treatment (see Fig. 3).

Discussion

Our sample of urban SSP participants had severe OUD, attempted treatment numerous times previously, and most currently injected opioids daily. Women and men both reported high interest in iOAT-H. Women most endorsed iOAT-H as their preferred OUD treatment (56%), while men most endorsed methadone treatment (42%), but these differences were not statistically significant. Women did report more OUD-related risks, including injecting more frequently and injecting in public more often, in comparison to men. Thus, differences in the sample based on gender may be important for developing iOAT-H programs.

We found high interest in iOAT among both women and men, but prior results from similar studies in other geographic areas differed somewhat. A study of SSP participants in Australia found that men were significantly more likely than women to perceive iOAT as a good OUD treatment for them (58% vs. 45%), but the authors did not explain potential reasons for this difference [19]. Meanwhile, another study of people with OUD in Baltimore found that 65% of women were interested in iOAT with diacetylmorphine, which was not significantly different than men [34]. We had hypothesized that women would have less interest in iOAT than men, due to the impact of stigma surrounding OUD treatment, but together these studies suggest that a substantial proportion of women with severe OUD would be interested in iOAT if it were made available.

Other studies of women who inject drugs are consistent with our findings of greater OUD-related risks among women in comparison to men. In our study, women reported more prior overdoses, prior OUD treatment episodes, as well as a greater median number of methadone treatment episodes than men, though not all differences were statistically significant. In the Canadian SALOME trial of iOAT-H, women at baseline had more addiction-related symptoms, worse psychological health, more crack cocaine use, and a greater proportion were living with HIV and reported participating in sex work in comparison to men [35]. In the earlier Canadian NAOMI trial of iOAT-D, women at baseline also more commonly had HIV and Hepatitis C Virus infection, greater cocaine use, more reported sex work, and more prior suicide attempts in comparison to men; however, women did not have more prior addiction treatment episodes [36]. Women at SSPs also may be at a disproportionally higher risk of OUD-related harm. Studies of a cohort from an SSP in Sweden found that women who inject drugs were more likely to share syringes and paraphernalia than men [37, 38]. One US study found that women at SSPs were more likely to develop wounds and skin infections [39]. Another US survey of PWID who used a mail-delivery SSP demonstrated that women had higher rates of HCV and lower odds of using safe sources for injection supplies than men [40]. In samples recruited outside of SSPs, women who inject drugs compared to men were also found to have higher risk injection behaviors [41, 42], higher standardized mortality ratios [43, 44], and higher reported rates of sexually transmitted infections [45, 46]. Therefore, our findings are consistent with others suggesting that women who inject drugs may have greater vulnerabilities than men.

Our study methods could not elucidate why OUD-related risks were greater in women than men. We did not ask questions regarding past trauma, interpersonal violence, and mental health comorbidities, but the literature shows that women with OUD are more vulnerable than men in these domains. Women with OUD experience greater stigma when identified with OUD than men and often lack support from family and partners to enter treatment [27, 47]. This was more commonly reported as a barrier to addiction treatment among women in our study than men. Women with OUD also have faced trauma at higher rates than men prior to OUD [20, 21]. A Canadian study found that women who use drugs are affected by interpersonal violence disproportionately and were at an increased risk of sexual violence [48]. Women with OUD suffer interpersonal violence 3–5 at times higher rates than women without OUD [46]. Thus, future iOAT programs will need to account for additional vulnerabilities among women with OUD.

Gender-specific treatment considerations are warranted, especially given that women are less likely than men to enter substance use treatment programs [49]. In general, women-centered substance use disorder treatment programs have reduced barriers to entry, had better outcomes than standard care, and had higher adherence to continued care, especially when paired with domestic counseling and child care [21, 49]. Childcare is often cited as a consideration for women in OUD treatment, however a lack of childcare was not reported as a barrier in our sample. Also, women with OUD report more difficulties maintaining employment and housing than men [50]; however, addressing these issues may be difficult in the context of efficiently delivering iOAT.

Our study does have several limitations. While our survey had different items for sex assigned at birth and gender identity, the use of the same terminology of “male” and “female” for sex and gender may have been confusing for some participants. Our gender identity category did not give the option of “non-binary” or free-text of a different gender identity, which limits inferences about other gender groups. Although participants were given a description of iOAT-H and had the opportunity to ask questions regarding iOAT-H, this treatment is unavailable in the US, and participants were unlikely to have direct experience with iOAT-H to inform their opinions. Though interest in iOAT-H appeared high, reported interest could be influenced by social desirability bias or the way that our description of iOAT-H was phrased. We also cannot know whether participants who expressed interest in iOAT-H would actually start treatment if it were made available to them. Many of the measures were self-reported and therefore subject to recall bias. We did not ask detailed questions about sex work, which is common among women receiving iOAT. Also, our sample only included participants at three SSPs in NYC and therefore may not be generalizable to other contexts in the US. There was an error in how American Indian/Alaskan Native demographic data was collected in the survey. Lastly, we had a small sample of female participants, which also may affect the generalizability of findings to the broader population with women who inject drugs.

Conclusions

In a cross-sectional study of SSP participants’ attitudes toward iOAT-H, we found interest in iOAT was high regardless of gender, but there were differences between women and men in OUD-related risks. This may reflect unmet needs among women who would potentially enter treatment with iOAT-H, if it becomes available, but additional work will be necessary to examine specific mental health needs, prior exposure to trauma, and preferences for creating a safe treatment space for women. Nonetheless, iOAT-H appears to be an acceptable approach to OUD treatment among women in our sample, and many treatment-experienced PWID would prefer the option of iOAT-H to other OUD treatments that are currently available.
