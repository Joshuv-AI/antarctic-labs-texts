Heterogeneity of patient preferences for long-acting HIV treatment among people with HIV in Kenya
Authors: Enrique M. Saldarriaga, Brett Hauber, Jacinda Tran, Anne Kaggiah, Catherine Maina, Naum Jepkosgei, Elizabeth Mwangi, Douglas Barthold, John Kinuthia, Susan M. Graham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong-acting ART (LA-ART) regimens available or in development are emerging alternatives to daily oral HIV treatment, designed to improve adherence. We explored preference heterogeneity for LA-ART among people living with HIV (PLWH) in Kenya and investigated their association with sociodemographic and psychosocial characteristics.MethodsWe designed a discrete choice experiment (DCE) to elicit preferences for LA-ART attributes among 700 PLWH recruited from three clinics in Nairobi Kenya (January 2023—February 2024). Each participant answered 17 choice-tasks, each with 2 hypothetical LA-ART alternatives and current daily oral therapy. Each LA-ART profile was defined by mode (long-acting pills, injections under the skin, injections into the muscle, and implants), pain, frequency, location of treatment, pre-treatment time undetectable, pre-treatment negative reaction testing, and late-dose leeway. Participants answered demographic and psychosocial questions and provided consent to link their clinical data. We fitted a latent-class model to the DCE data, assigned class membership to each participant, and estimated a multinomial logit model to explore associations between participant characteristics and class membership.ResultsWe found three classes in our DCE data and labeled them based on which hypothetical LA-ART formulation they preferred over their current daily oral therapy: LA-Implant (41% of the sample), LA-Injection-or-Oral (subcutaneous or intramuscular injection; 38%) and LA-Oral (21%) classes. All classes preferred “clinic” of over “home” for treatment location. Compared to LA-Oral participants, the other two classes had more experience with and less aversion to injections and had started ART more recently. LA-Implant participants were more likely to have housing insecurity. LA-Injection-or-Oral participants were less likely to be a female sex worker and to have a monthly income; and more likely to be a man who has sex with men or male sex worker, to have private insurance, and to have easier access to a clinic.InterpretationResults provide a deeper understanding of the LA-ART preference landscape. Understanding PLWH preferences for these therapies is crucial to increasing their acceptance and uptake as regimens become available and could aid in the development of preference-informed interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12879-026-13927-x.

Introduction

The Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) reported that 1.4 million people (adults and children) were living with HIV (PLWH) in Kenya in 2023, of whom 17,000 were newly infected [1]. Despite important advances to control the HIV epidemic, sub-Saharan African is one of the regions with the highest incidence of HIV, accounting for 38% of all new infections in 2022 [2]. HIV transmission remains an ongoing public health concern largely due to challenges with sustained access to HIV testing and treatment, particularly among marginalized populations [3, 4]. Among PLWH in Kenya, 94% are taking antiretroviral therapy (ART) and 89% are virally suppressed, [2] with important differences in ART uptake and adherence across sociodemographic groups, including gender and age [5, 6]. Important barriers to sustained ART adherence include privacy concerns, stigma and its repercussions, treatment side effects, mental health (particularly depression), and housing insecurity, among other individual, social, and structural barriers [7–10]. No individual product or intervention can eliminate all barriers, but new, innovative therapeutics, such as long-acting ART (LA-ART) regimens now available or in development, offer an alternative that could increase ART adherence and viral suppression rates, particularly for patients for whom daily oral pills (the current standard of care) have presented challenges [11].

LA-ART has emerged as a promising alternative, poised to overcome barriers associated with traditional daily oral ART uptake and adherence [12]. Cabotegravir and rilpivirine, a monthly or bi-monthly injectable regimen for HIV treatment with demonstrated safety and efficacy, [13–15] is the first LA-ART to gain regulatory approval [16]. Additional treatment modes, such as implants and long-acting tablets at varying dosing frequencies, are under development for the treatment of HIV [12, 17–19]. The success of these treatments to increase the proportion of PLWH who are virally suppressed in Kenya and reduce the spread and burden of HIV will depend on the attributes of potential LA-ART regimens and their acceptability among potential users [20]. Therefore, it is crucial to understand what drives preferences for different LA-ART formulations among PLWH in Kenya, and how these preferences vary across demographic groups, to inform future delivery strategies [21, 22].

Discrete choice experiments (DCE) are used to quantify stakeholder preferences for goods or services based on their characteristics (also known as attributes) [23, 24]. DCE participants compare the attributes of two or more hypothetical alternatives and choose their preferred option. The choice data, representing participants’ stated preferences, are then modeled as a function of the attributes of each alternative in the corresponding choice task [25]. Our research team designed, pilot-tested and conducted a DCE among PLWH in the United States (US) to elicit preferences for key attributes of potential LA-ART regimens [26–29]. We found compelling evidence that, on average, potential US users would prefer LA-ART regimens over their current daily oral treatment and identified distinct groups of patients, differentiated by which mode of administration they preferred [30, 31]. Our team adapted and piloted the DCE for Kenyan participants, based on interviews with Kenyan key informants with expertise in HIV clinical care, peer education, research, or policy [32–34]. We found that participants in Kenya also generally preferred hypothetical LA-ART regimens over their current daily therapy and that mode of administration was the most important treatment attribute [34]. However, average preference estimates may obscure important differences across patients. For example, an overall preference for a particular treatment mode may reflect either broad appeal across most participants or strong preferences concentrated within a subset of individuals.

This study extends the previously published aggregate analysis by examining preference heterogeneity, identifying distinct preference groups within the population, and assessing how participant characteristics are associated with membership in those groups. The analysis aimed to identify preference groups (i.e., classes) within our sample and assess associations between participants characteristics and class membership, This evidence can be leveraged to inform the design of LA-ART options or interventions to support LA-ART that better reflect the needs of populations with diverse preferences in the future.

Methods

Previous publications from our research team have described in detail the design, pilot testing and administration of the DCE survey in the U.S. [26–29]. as well as its adaptation to Kenya [32–34]. Here we present the most important features.

Population and setting

We recruited participants from the Kenyatta National Hospital (KNH) Comprehensive Care Clinic and two Sex Workers Outreach Program (SWOP) clinics in Nairobi, Kenya between January 2023 and February 2024. These clinics provide free HIV care to PLWH, with medications supplied by Kenya Medical Supplies Authority. The KNH Comprehensive Care Clinic serves patients from the general population of PLWH, while the SWOP clinics serve key populations, including male and female sex workers and men who have sex with men (MSM).

Our research team coordinated with clinic staff to conduct outreach in person or via telephone, and participants were recruited on-site during routine clinic visits. Eligibility criteria included having received an HIV diagnosis and being 18 years of age or older or an emancipated minor, enrolled in care at one of the research sites, fluent in English or Kiswahili, and able to provide informed consent. We excluded patients unable to consent or participate fully in all study procedures due to poor understanding, cognitive impairment, or other medical conditions. The final sample included 700 participants, all of whom had been prescribed a daily oral ART regimen. The sample size was informed by published guidelines [35] and by prior information obtained during pilot testing [27, 33].

The University of Washington (UW, STUDY00013593) and Kenyatta National Hospital-University of Nairobi Ethics and Research committee (P458/06/2021) reviewed and approved the study protocol and informed consent documents. All participants provided informed consent electronically.

DCE design

We developed a three-alternative DCE composed of two unlabeled hypothetical LA-ART profiles and a current-therapy (opt-out) alternative. The initial US DCE was developed based on key informant interviews [26] and pilot tested with 50 participants in western Washington State and Atlanta, Georgia [27]. We adapted the US DCE to the Kenyan context based on interviews with key informants, including clinicians, researchers, policy makers, and peer supporters [32]. The resulting survey was pilot-tested with 50 participants [33]. The survey deployed in Kenya had several updates compared to the US DCE, aimed to improve its understanding in the Kenyan context. These included updated photo examples of the treatment modes and terminology such as substituting “possible” for “hypothetical,” “chemist” for “pharmacy,” and “clinic” for “clinic or doctor’s office.” Further details on the DCE adaptation may be found in published and forthcoming work from our research team [32, 33]. The DCE and its associated survey questions were translated into Kiswahili and offered to participants in the language of their choosing.

Within a choice set, each hypothetical LA-ART profile was defined by seven attributes: treatment type (long-acting oral [LA-Oral] pills, subcutaneous injections, intramuscular injections, implants), location of administration (home, clinic, chemist), frequency of dosing (1 week, 1, 2, 3, 6, and 12 months), pain level (none, mild, moderate), pre-treatment time undetectable (i.e., the length of time that viral suppression would be required before LA-ART initiation: none, 3, and 6 months), pre-treatment negative reaction testing to ensure no severe adverse drug reactions prior to LA-ART initiation (needed or not), and late-dose leeway (i.e., flexibility in timing next dose before breakthrough viremia: short or long period). The opt-out alternative represented the participant’s current daily oral ART regimen, which could be any number of oral pills taken at least once daily. Participants were asked to assume equal effectiveness, safety, and cost of both hypothetical LA-ART options and their current therapy [30]. To ensure the hypothetical LA-ART profiles were realistic, we restricted some attribute-level combinations based on key informant interviews. Appendix 1 shows all feasible combinations [26, 32].

DCE components, survey, and data collection

Potential participants were screened for eligibility and subsequently provided informed consent using REDCap [36, 37]. Following consent, participants were asked to complete the DCE survey, which was administered using SurveyEngine (SurveyEngine GmbH, Berlin, Germany) on tablets in a private area within the clinics [38]. Participants received an introduction to the study objectives, a description of LA-ART, and were shown two videos that illustrated the format of the choice tasks, attributes, and levels of each attribute [33]. After introducing each DCE attribute, participants were asked comprehension questions to ensure accurate understanding. Pilot testing confirmed good understanding of the choice tasks, attributes, and levels [33]. Each participant answered 17 choice tasks. In addition to the DCE, an accompanying survey collected data on sociodemographic, health-related, and clinical characteristics of participants. This survey was developed for the US [30, 31] and adapted for Kenya [32, 33]. The DCE and accompanying survey can be found in Appendix 2. While each participant completed the DCE and survey, research staff remained nearby in case assistance was needed. We ensured the quality of the DCE data through a post hoc assessment of response quality where we examined invariant selection of DCE alternatives and LA-ART administration modes and within-participant variability in selections across tasks (Appendix 3).

Individual-level characteristics

We examined individual-level characteristics as potential predictors of membership in the preference-defined latent classes. Candidate variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature and included demographic, clinical, treatment experience, socioeconomic, mental health, and healthcare access characteristics. Examples included adherence-related measures (e.g., adherence to ART as prescribed, missed ART doses, and difficulty taking daily oral pills), stigma and social support measures, prior regimens, and socioeconomic indicators. The final multivariable model retained variables that contributed meaningfully to the characterization of latent-class membership while maintaining model parsimony. The absence of an independent association between a candidate variable and latent-class membership is not necessarily evidence that the variable is unrelated to LA-ART preferences. Rather, it indicates that the data we obtained did not meaningfully distinguish membership in the preference-defined latent classes.

Participant characteristics retained in the final model (Table 1) were grouped into three categories: demographic characteristics including gender, age, clinic-recorded key population status (female sex workers, male sex workers or MSM, and not key population), and having a steady sexual or romantic partner; clinical and health related characteristics including number of years taking ART, experience receiving injections for medical treatment, and aversion to injections; and, socioeconomic characteristics—including a dichotomization of the score of social support (higher indicates more social support) into above or below the median, having a stable place to live, monthly income, having private insurance, and how easy it is to get to an HIV clinic.

Data analysis

Preference heterogeneity was evaluated using a latent class analysis (LCA). The aim of the LCA is to partition the sample into distinct preference groups (i.e., classes), where the preferences within the class are similar but systematically different between classes [39, 40]. We followed a model-selection process in which we estimated models with 2 to 10 classes and evaluated model fit using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted AIC. To avoid overfitting and to preserve meaningful sample partition, the final model was selected based on both model fit and whether additional classes yielded substantively distinct and interpretable preference segments. The three-class solution was ultimately chosen because it provided a meaningful representation of preference heterogeneity while maintaining parsimony. Because pain is conditional on mode of administration (e.g., LA-Oral generates no pain) and consistent with our previous studies, we modeled mode and pain as a composite attribute so that only feasible combinations were included (Appendix 1). In addition, we collapsed the two injection modes (subcutaneous and intramuscular) into a single mode because these modes are associated with the same levels of pain and frequency of administration [30, 41]. We conducted the analysis using Stata statistical software (version 14.2, StataCorp LLC) [42].

After identifying the number of classes, we calculated each participant’s probability of belonging to each class and assigned them to the class with the highest likelihood. We then used a multivariable, multinomial logit model to quantify associations between participant characteristics and class membership. The multinomial logit model estimates adjusted relative risk-ratios (RRR), which are ratios of the proportion of participants with a given characteristic in any class relative to the proportion in the reference class, adjusted to other variables in the model [43]. Thus, we defined each class by its distinct LA-ART preferences and identified participants characteristics associated with class membership. To improve model and interpretability, categories with 10 or fewer total observations were excluded from presentation of the multinomial model results.

We conducted sensitivity analyses excluding participants who showed evidence of invariant response in the DCE (Appendix 3).

Results

Latent class analysis identified three distinct preference groups within the study population. Statistical fit improved with each additional class, but partitions beyond three classes subdivided existing groups without yielding substantively distinct preference patterns or policy-relevant segments. Thus, we selected the three-class model because it provided the most meaningful and interpretable representation of preference heterogeneity within the sample. The lowest probability of belonging to a class was 94%, indicating a low likelihood of misclassification across classes. The three classes identified were mainly differentiated by their preference for treatment administration mode. The characteristics of participants in each class are shown in Table 1.

Figure 1 presents LA-ART preferences across the three classes. Two classes, which we refer to as “LA-Implant” (41% of the sample) and “LA-Injection-or-Oral” (38%), preferred LA-ART over their current ART therapy, regardless of LA-ART administration mode. The LA-ART preference over current therapy is shown by the high disutility (i.e., negative preference weight) of the current treatment alternative in each class. LA-Implant class participants preferred implants over other LA-ART modes, regardless of pain level or frequency of administration. LA-Injection-or-Oral participants showed the strongest preference for injectable formulations and relatively high preference for LA-Oral therapy, suggesting receptivity to multiple LA-ART modalities; implants were consistently least preferred in this class. These participants were relatively indifferent regarding late-dose leeway, negative reaction testing, and time undetectable. The third class, labeled “LA-Oral” (21% of the sample) preferred their current treatment over most LA-ART alternatives, but their preference for LA-Oral was the highest. Participants in the third class showed strong preference for clinic as the treatment location and were indifferent regarding frequency, late-dose leeway, negative reaction testing, and time undetectable. Across all three classes, the most important attribute was the combination of treatment mode and pain. The second most important attribute was treatment frequency for the LA-Implant and LA-Injection-or-Oral, and administration location for the LA-Oral class (Fig. 2). Coefficients greater than one indicate a larger proportion of individuals in a class have that characteristic compared to participants in the reference class, LA-Oral. Participants from the LA-Implant class were more likely than those in the LA-Oral class to have taken ART for fewer than 10 years (RRR for 6–10 years 2.27, 95% confidence interval [95%CI] 1.28–4.03), to have more experience receiving injections (RRR 3.74, 95%CI 1.71–8.17), and to report not having a stable place to live (RRR 1.97, 95%CI 1.11–3.48). They were also less likely to be averse to injections (RRR 0.09, 95%CI 0.06–0.15). Participants from the LA-Injection-or-Oral class, compared to those in LA-Oral class, were more likely to be MSM or male sex workers (RRR 2.51, 95%CI1.04-6.04), to have more experience receiving injections (RRR 4.12, 95%CI 1.88–9.03), to report no monthly income (RRR 3.02, 95%CI 1.29–7.10), and to have private insurance (RRR 2.54, 1.03–6.25). They were also less likely to be female sex workers (RRR 0.30, 95%CI 0.16–0.57), to be averse to injections (RRR 0.11, 95%CI 0.07–0.19), and to have difficulties getting to a clinic (RRR 0.50, 95%CI 0.28–0.91).

Discussion

We explored preference heterogeneity for long-acting HIV treatment among PLWH in Kenya and identified three distinct preference groups that were primarily differentiated by treatment mode. These findings show that average preferences reported in our previously published aggregated analysis [34] mask substantial heterogeneity. Most participants preferred LA-ART over their current treatment, with participants clustering into three distinct groups characterized by differing preferences for treatment modes: an LA-Implant class (41%), an LA-Injection-or-Oral class (38%), and an LA-Oral class (21%). These results suggest that no single LA-ART product is likely to meet the needs of all patients and that maintaining a range of treatment options may be important for maximizing uptake.

The main implication of our findings is that most (79%) of our sample would likely achieve a higher utility by switching to any LA option than remaining with their current therapy. The remaining 21% would be better off only if switching to LA-Oral were possible. These findings are aligned with several previous qualitative and quantitative studies that found high acceptability for LA-ART products in sub-Saharan Africa [44–47]. Unlike our study, which directly compared preferences across multiple LA-ART modes, prior studies focused exclusively on injectable formulations. A consistent theme across this literature is the appeal of LA-ART due to reduced pill burden, fewer clinic visits, and less risk of HIV status disclosure, which in turn may increase privacy and reduce exposure to stigmatizing experiences [46–48]. These motivations are not limited to injectable formulations but apply to all LA-ART formulations. However, different modes may vary in how they support these outcomes. For example, although implants eliminate daily pill-taking, their visibility under the skin may pose challenges for individuals concerned about unintended disclosure [49]. By assessing preferences across multiple modes, our findings highlight how common acceptability drivers (including lower treatment burden and greater privacy) interact with mode-specific features to shape distinct preference patterns.

While participants in the LA-Implant and LA-Injection-or-Oral class were divided by their preferred mode of administration, their preferences are similar in important ways. First, both classes consistently preferred implants or injections over LA-Oral, regardless of the level of pain or frequency of administration, suggesting a willingness to trade off discomfort or more pain for the perceived benefits of these treatments. Second, these participants had been taking ART for relatively fewer years and were both more experienced with and less afraid of injections than their counterparts in the LA-Oral class (Table 2). While these characteristics differ somewhat from those identified in prior studies of LA-ART acceptability in the Africa region, they reflect a similar underlying openness to switching from daily oral therapy, particularly among individuals who are not strongly attached to their current ART delivery method, perceive daily pill-taking as burdensome, and are motivated by the potential for increased privacy and reduced stigma [45, 46, 50]. Additionally, evidence from North America shows that willingness to switch to an injectable LA-ART option is higher among individuals who are not heterosexual, [51, 52] who have recently initiated ART, [31] and who are not averse to injections [53, 54].

In addition to these similarities between the LA-Implant and LA-Injection-or-Oral classes, each class also had its own distinct set of associated characteristics. Participants from the LA-Implant class were more likely to report housing insecurity (Table 2). This may relate to medication storage; the lack of a safe place to store the medicines has been linked to a higher willingness to switch to LA-ART alternatives [44, 47, 55]. Further, only participants in the LA-Injection-or-Oral class were more likely to report easy access to an HIV clinic, suggesting that this mode of delivery may be especially attractive to these individuals. Nonetheless, all three classes preferred administration at a clinic over home. Despite the inconvenience associated with more trips to a healthcare facility for a weekly or monthly LA-Oral regimen by previous studies, [45, 46, 56] our participants may have preferred a clinic location for their treatment to avoid privacy and storage issues associated with treatment at home, consistent with a previous LA-ART acceptability study in Kenya [44]. This finding echoes a well-documented theme that perception of sustainable access to healthcare services might incentivize uptake of novel therapies [44, 57, 58].

LA-Injection-or-Oral participants were also more likely to report not having a monthly income, which differs from previous studies in low-income settings where higher income has been linked to greater LA-ART acceptability [45]. However, previous studies in African countries have found an inverse relationship between willingness to pay and income or poverty, highlighting the complex link between perceived value of health innovations and economic status [59–61]. In addition, the LA-Injection-or-Oral participants were more likely to have private insurance, which may help explain their greater openness to novel therapeutics, as observed in previous research that links insurance coverage and healthcare utilization in African countries [62]. Of note, all participants in this study received ART free of charge from their clinic. With recent reductions in support for global HIV programming, willingness to pay and insurance support will become increasingly important to evaluate in order to support good patient outcomes while ensuring program sustainability.

The third group, LA-Oral, was distinguished by a preference for oral medications over injections or implants, whether continuing their current daily oral ART regimen or starting LA-Oral treatment. These participants represented a fifth of the sample and were more likely to be members of a key population (sex workers or MSM) and have relatively longer experience with ART, which may have influenced their preference for a more familiar alternative (i.e., pills) [31, 46]. Contrary to LA-Implant and LA-Injection-or-Oral participants for whom frequency of administration was more important than location, LA-Oral participants deemed location as more important than frequency.

In conducting this study, we used the same experimental design, attributes, and levels as the DCE administered in the US, [30, 31] allowing us to directly compare the results from both preference heterogeneity analyses. The results from our study are broadly consistent with the US LCA: both analyses identified three classes mainly differentiated by the preferences for mode of administration [31]. However, the proportion of the sample who preferred LA-ART over their current therapy was 64% in the US, but 79% in Kenya. Moreover, preference segmentation in Kenya was largely driven by differences in treatment mode, however, not all classes were exclusively tied to a single formulation. Specifically, participants in the LA-Injection-or-Oral class expressed relatively favorable preferences for both LA injectable and oral therapies. This pattern suggests that some patients may be willing to consider multiple LA-ART options rather than a single preferred formulation. Further, US participants showed a clear preference for receiving their treatment at home over other alternatives, while Kenya participants showed clear preference for clinic. This difference may reflect the broader role that HIV care clinics play in Kenya, providing critical social support from providers, trained peers and clinic-based support groups, while offering a trusted and confidential environment that minimizes concerns about medication storage and inadvertent HIV status disclosure associated with home- or pharmacy-based care [32, 33]. These factors may contribute to the stronger preference for clinic-based administration observed in Kenya relative to the US. In terms of participant characteristics associated with each class, both studies found that aversion to injections and good adherence to current daily oral ART were associated with a decreased likelihood of choosing an LA-ART alternative over current daily oral treatment.

Strengths of our study include its novelty. To our knowledge, this is the first study in sub-Saharan Africa to examine preferences for a wide array of LA-ART formulations among PLWH and how individual characteristics are associated with preferences. Further, our statistical analysis adhered to established standards for analyzing conjoint data, including selection of the latent-class solution based on both statistical fit and the substantive interpretability of the resulting preference groups [25]. This study has limitations, however. First, some attribute combinations were intentionally restricted to reflect realistic LA-ART delivery scenarios. Consequently, certain levels, such as weekly dosing and home administration, were partially confounded, limiting our ability to completely disentangle preferences for those individual attribute levels. Second, the class assignment was based on the highest probability for each participant. While this approach simplifies interpretation, it introduces some misclassification risk because, in reality, class membership is probabilistic, not deterministic [63]. We found that the lowest probability of belonging to a class was 94%, indicating low likelihood of misclassification across classes. Third, while we explored associations between participant characteristics and class membership, these should be interpreted as correlations rather than causal explanations. Factors influencing preferences may be unobserved or confounded by variables not included in our model. Fourth, some covariates were insufficiently informative or lacked statistical power due to small subgroup sizes, limiting our ability to detect more nuanced associations. Finally, although the LCA model identifies meaningful patterns of preference heterogeneity, it does not fully explain the underlying drivers of these differences, and unexplained heterogeneity remains.

Conclusions

As LA-ART becomes more widely available, understanding patient preferences and taking them into consideration during the development of formulations and interventions to support scale-up will be important for optimizing LA-ART regimen impact, especially in lower resource settings such as Kenya [64, 65]. Our study demonstrates that PLWH in Kenya exhibit heterogeneous preferences based on treatment mode, with distinct groups favoring implants, injections, or oral options. These findings and the identification of different demographic and health characteristics associated with distinct preferences underscore the potential for an array of LA-ART formulations to meet the diverse needs of patients. Given the significant barriers to access, including cost, stigma, and challenges with integrating these products into resource-constrained healthcare systems, addressing these barriers while attending to patient preferences. Future efforts should focus on developing treatment strategies that incorporate both daily and long-acting options and align these options with patient preferences as much as possible, with the overall goal of optimizing ART adherence, viral suppression, and other treatment outcomes.
