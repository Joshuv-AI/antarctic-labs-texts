RNA G-quadruplex structure recognition by chimeric peptides
Authors: Xi Mou, Jiuming Wang, Yanruisheng Shao, Yu Li, Chun Kit Kwok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractG-quadruplexes (G4s) are non-canonical nucleic acids involved in gene regulation and have attracted interest as potential therapeutic targets. However, developing ligands that selectively recognize specific G4 structures remains challenging. Here, we report the rational design of chimeric peptides that combine a de novo selected peptide (Pep11) with a native peptide (Rhau18) derived from the endogenous DHX36 protein. The chimeric peptides bound RNA G4s (rG4s) with nanomolar affinity, representing a 10-fold enhancement over the individual modules, while retaining selectivity for rG4s over DNA G-quadruplexes (dG4s) and non-G4 RNA structures. These chimeric peptides inhibit telomerase activity in vitro and suppress the translation of an rG4-containing reporter gene in living cells. Live-cell imaging further supports their ability to recognize rG4 structures in a cellular context. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of the peptide–rG4 complexes were consistent with the experimental observations and suggested that peptide fusion promoted a more extensive and persistent peptide–rG4 interface, providing a possible structural basis for the enhanced binding affinity. Overall, our study suggests that chimeric peptide design provides a useful strategy for developing selective rG4-targeting ligands and modulating functional nucleic acid structures.

Introduction

G-quadruplexes (G4s) are non-canonical nucleic acid structures formed by guanine-rich sequences that fold into stable, four-stranded conformations through Hoogsteen hydrogen bonding. These structures are further stabilized with monovalent cations (K+ > Na+ > Li+) under physiological conditions [1, 2]. G4 structures function as dynamic regulatory elements, modulating processes such as replication, transcription, and translation by transitioning between folded and unfolded states [3, 4]. For example, the RNA G-quadruplex (rG4) found in the telomerase RNA component (hTERC) is involved in telomerase assembly. Stabilization of the hTERC rG4 structure can impede telomerase activity and hinder telomere elongation in cancer cells [5, 6]. The widespread occurrence of rG4s in transcriptomes underscores their broad biological significance and highlights their potential as therapeutic targets for diseases, including cancer and neurodegenerative disorders [7, 8].

Despite increasing interest in rG4-targeting ligands, selective recognition of rG4 structures remains difficult. Small molecules such as pyridostatin and BRACO-19 are commonly used to stabilize G4 structures, but their selectivity is often limited, which may lead to off-target effects and cytotoxicity, thereby restricting their broader use [9, 10]. These limitations have encouraged the development of alternative strategies, including peptide-based binders. Compared to many small molecules, peptides offer good biocompatibility and can be engineered to engage nucleic acid structures through defined molecular interactions. In particular, protein-derived G4-binding peptides provide useful templates for modular ligand design and potential cellular applications [11]. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of protein-derived or engineered peptides for G4 recognition, including RHAU/DHX36-derived peptides and affinity-selected bicyclic peptides [12–14]. However, most reported peptide-based G4 ligands have focused on DNA G-quadruplexes (dG4s) or known G4-binding protein motifs, whereas generalizable strategies for selective RNA G4 recognition and functional modulation remain comparatively underdeveloped.

To discover novel G4-binding peptides, we previously established a G4–messenger RNA (mRNA) display-seq platform and identified a de novo peptide, Pep11, which preferentially binds rG4s [15]. Although the tandem and cyclic versions of Pep11 improved binding properties, further optimization was required to achieve biological efficacy. Inspired by multivalent interactions in nature [16, 17], we reasoned that combining distinct binding modules might synergistically enhance affinity and specificity. Parallel studies on endogenous G4-interacting proteins have highlighted the DHX36-derived peptide Rhau18 as a natural G4 binder, providing a foundation for engineered constructs [13].

In this study, we designed chimeric peptides by fusing the de novo selected peptide Pep11 with the endogenous G4-binding peptide Rhau18. This design was guided by the modular organization of natural RNA-binding proteins, in which different interaction surfaces can contribute to target recognition. Using hTERC rG4 as a representative target (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S1), we found that the chimeric peptides bound to the rG4 structure with nanomolar affinity and showed improved binding compared to either individual module. The binding data, together with selectivity assays, suggest that the fusion of Pep11 and Rhau18 can enhance rG4 recognition. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations further provided a structural interpretation of the experimental observations and highlighted several residues that may participate in peptide–rG4 interactions. Overall, these results indicate that modular peptide engineering may provide a useful route for developing ligands that target specific nucleic acid structures.

Materials and methods

Materials

All oligonucleotides (oligos) utilized in this study were listed in Supplementary Table S1. The DNA and RNA oligonucleotides were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies Inc (USA), Huzhou Hippo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China) and Genewiz Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). The oligos were dissolved in ultrapure, nuclease-free distilled water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and stored at −20°C until use. N-Methyl-mesoporphyrin IX (NMM) was obtained from Frontier Scientific (USA). BRACO-19 was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (USA). The psiCHECK-2 vector and Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay kit used in reporter gene assays were purchased from Promega (USA). SYBR Gold and Hoechst 33342 were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. The peptides were synthesized by SynPeptide Co., Ltd. (China). The peptides were dissolved in ultrapure, nuclease-free distilled water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and stored at −80°C prior to the experiment.

Methods

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays with fluorescent oligos

Before the reaction, the RNA or DNA samples were heated at 90°C for 5 min, then incubated at 4°C overnight to allow refolding. Samples containing 5 nM 6-carboxyfluorescein (FAM)-labeled DNA or RNA, 1 mM MgCl2, 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, and varying concentrations of peptide were prepared and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The samples were then subjected to polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) using 10% nondenaturing gels (37.5:1 acrylamide:bis-acrylamide). The PAGE gels were prepared using a 0.5× Tris–borate–EDTA (TBE) running buffer. After running at 4°C with a consistent current of 30 mA for 30 min, gel images were collected using Cytiva Amersham Typhoon biomolecular imagers, and analyzed using ImageJ.

MST binding assay

Before the reaction, the RNA or DNA samples were heated at 90°C for 5 min, immediately transferred to 25°C, and incubated for 15 min to allow refolding. For MST measurements, 25 nM FAM-labeled RNA was mixed with serially diluted peptides in buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM KCl, and 1 mM MgCl₂. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 30 min before measurement. Samples were loaded into standard capillaries and analyzed on a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper Technologies) at 25°C using the blue fluorescence channel and the binding affinity mode. The binding data were analyzed using the NanoTemper MST analysis software. Subsequently, the binding curves were generated using a one-site specific binding model in Prism 10.

Circular dichroism spectroscopy

Before measurement, hTERC rG4 was prepared in reaction buffer containing 10 mM lithium cacodylate (LiCac, pH 7.0) and 1 mM KCl. Two sample preparation conditions were used for circular dichroism (CD) measurements. For the pre-folded RNA condition, hTERC rG4 was first heated at 90°C for 3 min, immediately transferred to 25°C, and incubated for 15 min to allow refolding. Reaction samples containing 5 μM oligonucleotide alone or 5 μM oligonucleotide with peptide were then prepared in the same reaction buffer and incubated at 4°C overnight before CD measurement. For the thermodynamic refolding condition, hTERC rG4 and peptides were mixed before thermal annealing. The mixtures were heated at 95°C for 5 min, followed by slow cooling to room temperature at a rate of 0.5°C/min in the presence of the peptides before CD measurement. CD spectra were acquired from 220 to 310 nm at 2 nm intervals. The corresponding buffer blank contained the same reaction buffer, including 10 mM LiCac (pH 7.0) and 1 mM KCl, but no oligonucleotide or peptide, and was measured under identical instrumental conditions. The raw spectra were corrected by subtracting the corresponding buffer blank and then converted to mean residue ellipticity. The spectra were smoothed over a 10 nm window for visualization. Data processing and analysis were performed using Spectra Manager™ Suite, Microsoft Excel, and Prism 10.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays with unlabeled oligos

Before the reaction, the RNA or DNA samples were heated at 90°C for 3 min, immediately transferred to 25°C, and incubated for 15 min to allow refolding. Reaction mixtures containing 100 nM RNA, 150 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 25 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), and varying concentrations of peptide were prepared and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The samples were then loaded into 10% nondenaturing PAGE (37.5:1 acrylamide:bis-acrylamide). The PAGE gels were prepared with 0.5× TBE running buffer. After running at 4°C with a consistent current of 30 mA for 30 min, the gels were stained with NMM and SYBR Gold and then detected using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System.

Ultraviolet melting assay

Before the measurement, hTERC rG4 was prepared in a reaction buffer containing 10 mM LiCac (pH 7.0) and 1 mM KCl and heated at 90°C for 3 min, immediately transferred to 25°C, and incubated for 15 min to allow refolding. Reaction samples containing 5 μM oligos or 5 μM oligos with peptide were then prepared in the same reaction buffer and incubated at 4°C overnight before measurement. The samples were transferred into cuvettes sealed with Teflon tape to minimize evaporation. Ultraviolet (UV) melting profiles were recorded by monitoring absorbance at 295 nm from 5°C to 95°C, with data collected at 0.5°C intervals. The corresponding buffer blank contained the same reaction buffer, including 10 mM LiCac (pH 7.0) and 1 mM KCl, but no oligonucleotide or peptide, and was measured under identical instrumental conditions. The absorbance traces were corrected by subtracting the corresponding buffer blank and then smoothed using a 5°C window before derivative analysis. Melting temperatures (Tms) were determined from the first derivative of absorbance with respect to temperature, dAbs/dT. Data processing was performed using Microsoft Excel for data organization and analysis and Prism 10 for graph generation.

Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) melting assay

The hTERC oligo was labeled with 5′ FAM and 3′ Cy3 and prepared at a final concentration of 0.2 µM. The peptides were added at a final concentration of 0.2 µM to achieve a 1:1 rG4-to-peptide ratio. For RNA competition experiments, transfer RNA (tRNA) was included at a final concentration of 2 µM as a structured biological RNA competitor. All samples were prepared in buffer containing 1 mM KCl and 10 mM LiCac. The rG4 was heated at 90°C for 5 min, immediately transferred to 25°C, and incubated for 15 min to allow refolding. After addition of peptides, samples were equilibrated at 25°C for 30 min before thermal melting analysis. Fluorescence signals were acquired using a CFX96 Touch Real-Time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) Detection System. The thermal protocol consisted of an initial 30-min hold at 25°C, followed by a gradual temperature increase to 100°C at a rate of 0.5°C/min. For each experimental condition, triplicate reactions were performed in a 25 µl volume. The collected fluorescence data were blank-corrected and smoothed over a 5°C window before calculation of the first derivative with respect to temperature. The resulting derivative curves were normalized for visualization, and the Tm was defined as the temperature corresponding to the maximum of the first-derivative curve. Final plots were generated using Prism 10.

Telomerase repeat amplification protocol assay

Telomerase activity was measured using the TRAPEZE® gel-based telomerase detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with minor modifications. The telomerase repeat amplification protocol (TRAP) assay consists of three main steps: (i) telomerase-mediated addition of telomeric repeats to the telomerase substrate, (ii) PCR amplification of the telomerase extension products, and (iii) detection of the amplified products by gel electrophoresis. HeLa cell extracts were first prepared using the CHAPS lysis buffer provided in the kit. For the standard TRAP assay, cell extracts equivalent to 500 cells were mixed with different concentrations of peptides or BRACO-19 in Tris–HCl buffer containing 25 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM KCl, and 1 mM MgCl₂, pH 7.5. The mixtures were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The reaction mixtures were then added to the TRAP master mix containing TRAP buffer, dNTPs, primers including the telomerase substrate (TS) primer, reverse primer (RP), and K1 primer, the TSK1 DNA template, and Taq DNA polymerase. The samples were incubated at 30°C for 30 min to allow telomerase-mediated extension, followed by 30 cycles of PCR amplification.

To assess whether the peptides interfered with the PCR amplification step, a PCR-stage control experiment was performed. In this control, telomerase extension was first allowed to proceed in the absence of peptides or ligand at 30°C for 30 min. The peptide was then added immediately before the PCR amplification step, and the samples were subjected to the same 30-cycle PCR program.

The PCR products were generated using the TS and RP primers, producing a 6-bp incremental ladder starting from 50 bp. The TSK1 template was amplified by the TS and K1 primers to generate a 36-bp internal control for monitoring PCR inhibition. Reaction products were separated on an 8% native PAGE gel and stained with SYBR Gold.

Cell imaging

One day before transfection, HeLa cells were seeded in 3.5 cm confocal dishes (NEST, China). Cy5-labeled hTERC rG4 WT or MUT was heated at 90°C for 5 min, immediately transferred to 25°C, and incubated for 15 min to allow refolding before transfection. The annealed RNA was co-transfected with FAM-labeled peptides using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the BRACO-19 treatment group, BRACO-19 was added at a final concentration of 20 μM during transfection. Four hours after transfection, the cells were stained with Hoechst 33342 at a final concentration of 5 μg/mL for 15 min to visualize nuclei. Confocal images were acquired using a Leica TCS SPE laser scanning confocal microscope (Germany). Pearson’s correlation coefficients between the RNA and peptide fluorescence signals were calculated from 10 whole-field images per group using ImageJ. Statistical analysis was performed using ordinary one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Šídák’s multiple-comparisons test in Prism 10.

Reporter gene assay

The wildtype/mutant hTERC rG4 sequences were cloned into the 5′ untranslated region (UTR) of the Renilla luciferase in the psiCHECK-2 vector (Promega, USA) [15]. For transfection, 1.5 × 104 HeLa cells were seeded in a 96-well black-wall optical plate (SPL Life Sciences, Korea) and allowed to adhere for 20 h. Subsequently, each well was transfected with 30 ng of the respective reporter plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). After 4 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the peptides (Rhau18–Pep11, Pep11–Rhau18, Rhau18, Pep11, or Pep71) at final concentrations of 0, 100, and 200 nM. After 24 h of incubation, luciferase activities were assessed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA) on a Molecular Devices SpectraMax ID3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA). The Renilla luciferase signal was normalized to the corresponding firefly luciferase signal for each well. All experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates (n ≥ 3). Data from at least three independent replicates (n ≥ 3) are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance (P <.05) was determined by a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test.

Total RNA extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

After the 24-h peptide treatment described above, total RNA was isolated from HeLa cells using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA). RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically (NanoDrop OneC). Next, complementary DNA was synthesized from 100 ng of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Master Mix (Takara, Japan) in a 20 µl reaction. Quantitative PCR was performed using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA) and specific primers on a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the results are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments.

Structure prediction and structure docking

The three-dimensional structures of the hTERC rG4 and the peptides (Pep11, Rhau18, Pep11–Rhau18, and Rhau18–Pep11) were predicted individually using the AlphaFold3 server (https://alphafoldserver.com/) [18] with default parameters and a fixed random seed 42. The top-ranked model among the five predictions with the highest predicted TM (pTM) score for each molecule was selected for subsequent docking studies. As the pTM score reflects the confidence in the model’s prediction quality, this selection strategy enabled us to use the prediction with better quality. Protein–RNA docking was performed using the HDOCK server [19], and the top-ranked model according to the HDOCK score for each complex was analyzed. Interactions between the peptides and the rG4 were assessed by calculating the minimal distance between all atoms in each peptide residue and each RNA nucleotide.

MD simulation and kinetics analysis

MD simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024.2 (https://www.gromacs.org/) with GPU acceleration. The initial coordinate and topology files were prepared using CHARMM-GUI (https://www.charmm-gui.org/) Solution Builder, employing the CHARMM force field. The complexes were solvated in a rectangular periodic box with dimensions of ∼56 × 56 × 56 Å³, using the TIP3P water model. A minimum solvent buffer of 10 Å was maintained between the solute and the box boundaries. The system was made electrically neutral and then filled with a salt solution matching normal physiological conditions (0.15 M KCl) by automatically placing 27 potassium (K⁺) ions and 14 chloride (Cl⁻) ions.

Energy minimization was first performed using the steepest descent algorithm to remove any bad atomic contacts and to relax the surrounding solvent molecules. This step avoids unrealistic steric clashes that can arise when building the initial model [20]. After minimization, the system was gradually equilibrated while keeping the heavy atoms of the solute weakly restrained in their initial positions. Equilibration was carried out in the isothermal–isobaric (NPT) ensemble at 303.15 K and 1 bar, which matched the temperature and pressure used in our experiments and is commonly employed in biomolecular simulations [21]. Temperature was controlled with a Langevin thermostat, and pressure was maintained with a Parrinello–Rahman barostat, two standard algorithms known to provide stable sampling of NPT conditions [22]. During this stage, the solvent and ions were allowed to move freely and reorganize around the restrained solute. It ensured that the final state of the system has a stable density and a physically valid distribution of water molecules and ions before the actual measurement data can be collected.

Production MD simulations were then performed for 500 ns in the NPT ensemble at 303.15 K and 1 bar, using the same thermostat and barostat settings as in the equilibration steps. A 2 fs integration time step was used, with bonds involving hydrogen atoms constrained by the LINCS algorithm. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald method under periodic boundary conditions.

Short-range electrostatic interactions and van der Waals interactions were smoothly truncated at 1.2 nm using a switching function, consistent with common practice in simulations of similar biomolecular systems [21]. System coordinates were saved every 10 ps, providing sufficient temporal resolution to analyze both structural changes and slower dynamical processes over the 500-ns trajectory.

Post-simulation analyses were performed on the 500 ns MD trajectories to quantify peptide–RNA interfacial interactions. Interface buried surface area (BSA) and RNA-interface solvent-accessible surface area (SASA)/BSA-related area metrics were calculated frame-by-frame to evaluate the compactness of the peptide–RNA interfaces. Intermolecular contacts were defined in each frame of the 500-ns trajectory as peptide–RNA atom pairs separated by <4 Å. The trajectory comprised 50 000 frames in total, enabling characterization of the temporal evolution of these contacts. Hydrogen bonds were calculated along the MD trajectories using gmx hbond in GROMACS according to its standard geometric criteria. The distributions of hydrogen-bond numbers were compared among the complexes.

To identify dominant interaction hotspots, hydrogen-bond occupancies were calculated for individual peptide residues as the proportion of frames in which a given peptide residue formed at least one hydrogen bond with the RNA. For the peptide optimization analysis, contact-count distributions were additionally compared between the parent construct and sequence-modified variants. Apparent binding curves were generated by plotting fraction bound against peptide concentration and fitting the data with a sigmoidal model for comparative analysis.

Results

Chimeric peptides exhibit nanomolar affinity and high selectivity for rG4 structures

To investigate the effect of multivalency on G4 recognition, two chimeric peptide constructs were engineered by fusing the endogenous protein DHX36-derived Rhau18 and the de novo Pep11 discovered through G4–mRNA display-seq in different orientations (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Figs S1 and S2, and Supplementary Table S2). The rational design of the chimeric peptides is based on two core principles: modular domain combination and structural pre-organization for synergistic binding. We hypothesized that covalently fusing these two peptides would create a composite interface with enhanced affinity and specificity. We first evaluated the binding affinity of these constructs for the rG4 target by MST. Both constructs demonstrated nanomolar-level binding affinities for hTERC rG4 WT. For comparative analysis, the same binding assay was performed on Tandem Pep11, Cyclic Pep11, Rhau18, and Pep11 (Supplementary Table S2 and Supplementary Figs S3–S6). The results indicated that Rhau18–Pep11 (Kd = 285 ± 20 nM) and Pep11–Rhau18 (Kd = 99 ± 6 nM) possessed stronger binding affinity than Tandem Pep11 (Kd = 461 ± 103 nM) and Cyclic Pep11 (Kd = 408 ± 45 nM), and exhibited an ∼10-fold enhancement compared to the individual modules Rhau18 (Kd = 1.52 ± 0.29 μM) and Pep11 (Kd = 2.23 ± 0.39 μM) (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S7). To verify the structural specificity of the binding, a mutant hTERC rG4 construct (MUT) was designed (Supplementary Table S1). Both chimeric peptides showed markedly reduced binding to the MUT sequence (Kd = 4.17 ± 1.14 μM for Rhau18–Pep11; Kd = 2.82 ± 0.97 μM for Pep11–Rhau18) (Supplementary Fig. S8), confirming that the interaction between the chimeric peptides and the target is rG4 structure specific. We also synthesized two control chimeras that combine Rhau18 with a scrambled version of Pep11, denoted Rhau18–scrPep11 and scrPep11–Rhau18. The scrambled chimeras showed a smaller affinity enhancement than that observed for Rhau18–Pep11 (Kd = 447 ± 37 nM) and Pep11–Rhau18 (Kd = 203 ± 14 nM), supporting a sequence- and topology-dependent contribution beyond the effect of increased peptide size and charge (Supplementary Figs S9–S11). The binding specificity was further investigated using MST and electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) against a panel of G4-forming sequences and RNA sequences, including APP rG4 (parallel) [23], MALAT1 rG4 (parallel) [24], TERRA rG4 (parallel) [25], c-kit1 dG4 (parallel) [26], HRAS dG4 (antiparallel) [27], hTelo dG4 (hybrid) [28] and non-G4s like RNA hairpin, and polyrA18 sequence (Supplementary Tables S1 and S3). Among these, only APP rG4, MALAT1 rG4 and TERRA rG4 exhibited substantial binding affinity for the chimeric peptides, reinforcing their specificity for rG4 targets (Fig. 1D and E). Collectively, these results illustrate that the chimeric peptides exhibit enhanced binding affinity and structural specificity toward rG4 targets. These findings highlight the potential of multivalent peptide design for the selective targeting of biologically relevant G4 structures.

Chimeric peptides stabilize the rG4 structures

To further characterize the chimeric peptide–rG4 interaction, we next performed a suite of biophysical assays to assess the structural impact on the rG4. We first asked whether chimeric peptides binding to the rG4 structure would change the rG4 conformation. To test this, we employed CD spectroscopy, which is widely used to monitor the secondary structural features of nucleic acids, including G4 topology, by measuring the differential absorption of left- and right-handed circularly polarized light [29]. The CD data revealed that both hTERC rG4 alone and the complexes displayed parallel G4 topologies (Fig. 2A), suggesting that complex formation does not disrupt rG4 structure. Notably, this characteristic parallel G4 signature remained completely unchanged even after subjecting the mixtures to a heating/cooling step (thermodynamic conditions) (Supplementary Fig. S12A). Together, these consistent results indicate that complex formation does not disrupt the rG4 structure. To further assess whether G4-associated features remained detectable during interactions with the peptides, we employed EMSA with N-methyl mesoporphyrin IX (NMM) and SYBR Gold staining. NMM preferentially fluoresces upon association with G4 structures, whereas SYBR Gold stains nucleic acids more generally. The NMM-stained EMSA revealed that fluorescence signals were retained in the peptide–rG4 shifted bands, suggesting that the peptide-bound RNA remained NMM-accessible and maintained G4-associated features (Fig. 2B).

Biophysical analysis reveals rG4 thermal stabilization upon Rhau18–Pep11 and Pep11–Rhau18 binding. (A) CD spectrum of the hTERC rG4 alone and in complex with Rhau18–Pep11 or Pep11–Rhau18. All spectra exhibit a characteristic positive peak at ∼265 nm and a negative peak at ∼240 nm, indicative of a parallel G4 topology. The spectral profiles remain largely unchanged upon peptide binding, indicating that the secondary structure of the rG4 is preserved. (B) EMSA of hTERC rG4 and Rhau18–Pep11 or Pep11–Rhau18 with NMM and SYBR Gold staining. The persistence of a retarded band with similar mobility across conditions, coupled with maintained NMM fluorescence, suggests that G4-associated features remain detectable in the peptide-bound RNA. (C) UV melting for hTERC rG4 alone and its complexes with Rhau18–Pep11 and Pep11–Rhau18. A characteristic hypochromic shift at 295 nm was observed for all samples, confirming the formation of rG4 structures. The Tms of the complexes formed with Rhau18–Pep11, Pep11–Rhau18 were significantly higher than that of the hTERC rG4 alone, indicating enhanced rG4 thermal stabilization caused by the binding of peptides. (D) FRET melting curves for hTERC rG4 in the presence of Rhau18–Pep11, Pep11–Rhau18, Rhau18, or Pep11. Tm values were determined from the peak positions of the first-derivative curves. The increase in Tm corroborates the UV melting results, demonstrating peptide-mediated thermal stabilization of the hTERC rG4 structure.

To evaluate the effect of chimeric peptide binding on the thermal stability of rG4, UV melting experiments were performed under a low-K⁺ condition (1 mM K⁺), in which the rG4 structure is relatively less stable, thereby allowing peptide-induced stabilization to be more readily detected (Fig. 2C). G4 formation was assessed by monitoring the characteristic hypochromic transition at 295 nm, a recognized spectroscopic signature of G4 folding. The Tm was used as a quantitative indicator of G4 thermal stability [30]. The UV melting profiles were consistent with G4 formation in all tested samples. Compared with rG4 alone, the Tm values of the chimeric peptide–rG4 complexes were significantly increased from 53.8 ± 1.3°C to 70.3 ± 1.2°C and 68.8 ± 1.2°C, respectively, indicating substantial stabilization of rG4 by these peptides. In comparison, incubation of rG4 with the individual modules, Pep11 or Rhau18, resulted in only modest increases in Tm to 57.8 ± 1.3°C and 54.7 ± 1.2°C, respectively (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Fig. S12B). These data indicate that the chimeric constructs stabilize rG4 more effectively than individual modules. In parallel, FRET melting assays were performed to monitor G4 unfolding and evaluate peptide-induced stabilization. The melting curves of hTERC rG4 were recorded in the presence or absence of the peptides at a 1:1 rG4-to-peptide ratio. Consistent with the UV melting data, both chimeric peptides increased the Tm of the hTERC rG4 compared with rG4 alone or rG4 incubated with individual modules (Fig. 2D). We also introduced 2 µM of tRNA as a structured biological RNA competitor in the FRET melting assay. The presence of tRNA had little effect on peptide-mediated rG4 stabilization, supporting the stabilizing effect of the chimeric peptides under competitive RNA conditions (Supplementary Fig. S12C and D). Collectively, these UV and FRET melting assays demonstrated that the chimeric peptides stabilize the rG4 structure and enhance its thermal stability upon binding.

Chimeric peptides suppress telomerase activity in cell lysate

Given the stabilization of hTERC rG4 by the chimeric peptides, we next examined whether this interaction was associated with changes in telomerase activity in vitro. hTERC provides the RNA template for telomerase-mediated addition of telomeric repeats to chromosome ends, and stabilization of G4 structures within hTERC has been reported to interfere with telomerase function by limiting access to, or utilization of, the RNA template [31, 32]. We therefore hypothesized that the chimeric peptides Rhau18–Pep11 and Pep11–Rhau18 may reduce telomerase activity by stabilizing the hTERC rG4 structure. Telomerase activity was evaluated using the TRAP assay with telomerase-positive HeLa cell extracts, followed by gel electrophoresis analysis [33] (Fig. 3A). Treating with increasing concentrations of Rhau18–Pep11 or Pep11–Rhau18 (0–1250 nM) led to a dose-dependent decrease in TRAP product intensity (Fig. 3B), indicating reduced apparent telomerase activity under these assay conditions. The known G4-stabilizing ligand BRACO-19 [34] was included as a positive control and showed comparable inhibition (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the non-G4 binding peptide Pep71 and the individual modules Rhau18 or Pep11 showed no detectable inhibition of TRAP product formation under the same conditions (Supplementary Figs S13 and S14), suggesting that the observed decrease was specific to the chimeric constructs rather than a nonspecific effect of peptide treatment.

The peptides were pre-incubated with telomerase-positive extracts from HeLa cells prior to the TRAP assay. The TSK1 template was amplified with TS and K1 primers as a 36 bp internal control. Telomerase-mediated elongation products were amplified by the TS primer and the RP to generate 50 bp + 6n (where n = 0, 1, 2, …). And the PCR products were detected using gel electrophoresis. (B) TRAP assay results demonstrate dose-dependent suppression of telomerase activity in HeLa cell lysates treated with Rhau18–Pep11 or Pep11–Rhau18. Because the TRAP assay includes a PCR amplification step and can be affected by G4-binding ligands, we performed an additional PCR-stage control. In this experiment, the peptides were added only after the telomerase extension reaction, immediately before PCR amplification. Under these conditions, the chimeric peptides did not reduce the amplified TRAP signal, suggesting that the reduced product intensity observed in the standard TRAP assay was not due to PCR inhibition or interference with product detection (Supplementary Fig. S15). Together with the CD, UV melting, and FRET melting data showing stabilization of the hTERC rG4, these results suggest that the chimeric peptides reduce telomerase activity, potentially through an effect associated with hTERC rG4 stabilization. These findings are consistent with a functional role of the hTERC rG4 and suggest that structure-targeting chimeric peptides can be useful tools for studying rG4-associated telomerase regulation.

Chimeric peptides recognize the hTERC rG4 structure in living cells

To directly visualize and substantiate the interaction between the chimeric peptides and the rG4 within the complex intracellular milieu, we conducted live-cell confocal imaging experiments. FAM-labeled Rhau18–Pep11 or Pep11–Rhau18 was co-transfected with Cy5-labeled hTERC rG4 WT or MUT constructs (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2, and Supplementary Figs S16 and S17). Confocal imaging showed overlap between the green fluorescence from FAM-labeled Rhau18–Pep11 and the red fluorescence from Cy5-labeled hTERC rG4 WT in several subcellular regions (Fig. 4A, upper panel). In comparison, the hTERC rG4 MUT control showed much weaker overlap under the same imaging conditions, suggesting that the observed association is related to the rG4 structure (Fig. 4A, lower panel). A similar imaging and analysis procedure was applied to Pep11–Rhau18, which gave a comparable colocalization pattern (Fig. 4B).

Both Rhau18–Pep11 and Pep11–Rhau18 showed higher colocalization with WT rG4 than with the corresponding individual peptide modules or the MUT rG4 construct. These results are consistent with preferential recognition of the WT rG4 structure by the chimeric peptides in cells. Although further work is needed to define the exact intracellular distribution and binding state of these complexes, the imaging data suggest that the chimeric peptides can be used to probe rG4 structures in a cellular context.

Chimeric peptides suppress rG4-mediated gene activity in cells

Having confirmed that the chimeric peptides interact with rG4 structures in live cells, we further explored their impact on rG4-mediated gene expression. The rG4 structures located in the 5′ UTR of mRNAs are known to repress translation by impeding ribosomal scanning [35]. We hypothesized that our chimeric peptides would stabilize the 5′ UTR rG4 structure in a reporter system, thereby enhancing its translation-inhibitory effect.

To test this hypothesis, we conducted a dual luciferase reporter gene assay in HeLa cells. The hTERC rG4 WT or rG4 MUT motif was inserted into the 5′ UTR of the Renilla luciferase gene, while the firefly luciferase gene served as an internal control for normalization (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table S1). In the absence of any rG4-targeting peptides, the construct containing the hTERC rG4 WT motif exhibited a normalized luciferase activity of 87.2% ± 7.4% relative to the construct containing the disrupted rG4 MUT motif, confirming the intrinsic translational repressive activity of the rG4 structure. Treatment with increasing concentrations (0–200 nM) of Rhau18–Pep11 or Pep11–Rhau18 resulted in a dose-dependent further reduction to 85.5% ± 7.4% and 81.6% ± 3.9% respectively in normalized luciferase activity specifically for the WT construct, with no effect on the MUT construct (Fig. 5B). This highlights the specificity of the chimeric peptides in targeting functional rG4 structures. The individual modules (Rhau18 and Pep11) showed modest effects, underscoring the efficacy of the chimeric design. The negative control peptide Pep71 confirmed that the effect is a result of specific rG4 recognition (Fig. 5B).

To determine whether this inhibition occurs at the mRNA level via rG4, we performed reverse transcription quantitative PCR. The results showed no significant changes in the mRNA levels of the reporter gene across all treatment conditions (Supplementary Fig. S21 and Supplementary Table S1), indicating that the inhibition occurs at the translational level, but not at the transcriptional level. In conclusion, these findings reveal that the chimeric peptides specifically target and stabilize the rG4 structure in the 5′ UTR, thus effectively suppressing downstream gene translation.

Computational modeling of chimeric peptide–G4 complexes

To gain further insight into the recognition of rG4s by chimeric peptides, we next performed computational modeling to obtain a tentative structural view of how the chimeric peptides might interact with and stabilize rG4s. Structures of hTERC rG4, the individual peptides, and the fused peptides were predicted using AlphaFold 3, and the highest-confidence models were then used for protein–RNA docking to generate representative peptide–rG4 complex models (Fig. 6A–C and Supplementary Fig. S22). We further analyzed the hTERC rG4 & Rhau18–Pep11 complex by MD simulation and extracted representative frames for visualization of peptide–RNA contacts, which were compared with the docked complexes of hTERC rG4 with Pep11 or Rhau18 alone (Fig. 6D–I and Supplementary Fig. S23). Residue–nucleotide contacts were estimated using a 4 Å heavy-atom distance cutoff. The models suggest that the fused peptide retains interaction features associated with both parental motifs and forms an extended contact interface with hTERC rG4. For example, residues W13, K17, and R26 in the Rhau18–Pep11 construct show repeated contacts with the RNA, whereas Rhau18 alone displays additional contacts involving the I10–G11 region. Although these models are intended only as qualitative references, they are consistent with the experimentally observed improvement in rG4 stabilization by the fused constructs.

To further assess the possible effects of peptide fusion and motif arrangement on rG4 recognition, we performed explicit-solvent MD simulations of the hTERC rG4 in complex with the individual peptides and the two chimeric constructs. Based on the simulation models, both chimeric peptides tended to display a larger and more persistent modeled interface with the rG4 than the individual peptides, with Pep11–Rhau18 showing the strongest overall trend among the systems examined. These results are consistent with the experimental observations and suggest that covalent linkage of the two recognition motifs may promote a more favorable binding configuration and greater overall stabilization.

Structural descriptors extracted from the MD trajectories showed a similar overall pattern. Compared with the individual peptide systems, the chimeric peptides generally exhibited larger BSA, greater numbers of residue–nucleotide contacts, and more peptide–rG4 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 7A–D), consistent with the formation of a broader and more persistent intermolecular interface. Pep11–Rhau18 displayed the most pronounced interface-related features, whereas Rhau18–Pep11 showed an intermediate pattern but still generally exceeded the individual peptide modules. Analysis of the solvent-accessible surface area of the RNA interface revealed a comparable tendency. Although these descriptors do not directly quantify binding affinity, they support the possibility that peptide fusion enhances rG4 recognition by enabling a more extensive and stable binding interface.

To identify residues potentially involved in rG4 recognition, we first analyzed residue-level hydrogen-bond occupancies in the MD trajectories of the Rhau18–Pep11 complex. W13, K17, and R26 showed relatively frequent hydrogen-bonding contacts with the rG4 and were positioned close to the RNA surface in representative simulation snapshots (Fig. 7E). MD-based computational alanine scanning predicted that substitution of any single residue would have only a limited effect on peptide–rG4 binding, whereas simultaneous substitution of all three residues would weaken the interaction (Fig. 7F). Consistent with this prediction, the individual W13A, K17A, and R26A substitutions produced little change in the experimentally measured binding affinity, while the combined W13A/K17A/R26A mutation resulted in a clear reduction in affinity (Fig. 7G and Supplementary Figs S24–S27). These results suggest that W13, K17, and R26 contribute collectively to the Rhau18–Pep11–rG4 interface, as the loss of an individual contact may be compensated by the remaining interaction network.

An analogous analysis was performed for Pep11–Rhau18. Several residues in the Pep11-derived region, including H7, R8, and R9, showed relatively high hydrogen-bond occupancies and sustained association with the rG4 throughout the trajectories (Supplementary Fig. S28). Computational alanine scanning indicated that substitution of these residues could nevertheless favor the overall peptide–rG4 interaction, despite the loss of their local contacts (Supplementary Fig. S28). In agreement with this prediction, individual alanine substitution of H7, R8, or R9, as well as simultaneous substitution of all three residues, increased the experimentally measured binding affinity of Pep11–Rhau18 (Supplementary Figs S28–S32). These results indicate that frequently engaged residues do not necessarily make favorable net contributions to binding, as their effects may also reflect local conformational constraints or suboptimal interfacial arrangements. Together, the computational and experimental findings show that the contributions of individual residues depend strongly on sequence context and motif arrangement, while also identifying positions whose modification may either weaken or further improve rG4 binding.

Discussion

The rational design of molecules that selectively target rG4s remains challenging, as many reported ligands show limited discrimination among different nucleic acid structures or only moderate binding affinity [36–39]. Here, we explored a peptide-based modular design strategy inspired by natural RNA-binding proteins. By combining the DHX36-derived G4-binding peptide Rhau18 with the de novo selected rG4-binding peptide Pep11, we generated two chimeric peptides with improved binding to rG4 structures. Compared with the individual peptide modules, the chimeric peptides showed an ∼10-fold enhancement in affinity toward hTERC rG4. Together with the scrambled-chimera controls and MD analyses, these results suggest that the improved binding is not simply a consequence of increased peptide size or charge, but likely involves sequence- and orientation-dependent interactions between the peptide modules and the rG4 structure. Importantly, the linker-free design adopted in this study provides a simple and modular strategy that preserves the intrinsic binding properties of both peptide modules without introducing additional structural complexity. At the same time, the relatively modest affinity enhancement over the scrambled chimeras suggests that direct fusion alone may not fully exploit cooperative multivalent recognition, indicating that further optimization of the spatial arrangement between the two binding modules may be beneficial.

Recent studies have developed several peptide- or protein-based tools for targeting G4 structures, mainly through rational engineering or screening-based discovery. For instance, Zheng et al. developed an artificial G4 probe (G4P) based on the Rhau23, enabling genome-wide mapping of dG4s in living cells via chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) [40]. Ngo et al. further improved the stability and binding properties of Rhau23 through cyclization, generating c-Rhau23 with enhanced proteolytic resistance and nanomolar affinity for parallel dG4s [41]. These studies demonstrated the usefulness of engineered peptide probes, although their applications have mainly focused on dG4s. In parallel, screening-based methodologies have proven powerful for de novo peptide discovery. Galli et al. used phage display to isolate a nanobody (SG4), which binds dG4s with low-nanomolar affinity and can be expressed intracellularly for live-cell G4 imaging [42]. Liu et al. identified bicyclic peptides (b-G4pep3) with submicromolar affinity and selectivity for dG4s over duplex DNA [12]. Compared with these dG4-focused examples, rG4-targeting peptides remain less developed. Our previous G4–mRNA display-seq platform allowed the selection of short peptides, such as Pep11, that preferentially bind rG4s over dG4s and non-G4 structures [15]. The present work builds on this selected peptide by testing whether fusion with a natural G4-binding module can further improve rG4 recognition.

The results also point to several areas that require further optimization. For potential cellular or in vivo applications, properties such as delivery efficiency, biostability, intracellular distribution, and biodistribution will need to be improved. Previous studies have used cell-penetrating peptides or nanocarrier-based delivery systems to enhance the cellular uptake of peptide-based molecules [43–45], and similar approaches may be useful for chimeric rG4-binding peptides. Intracellular expression of the chimeric peptides may also provide a complementary strategy to evaluate their specificity, competition with endogenous G4-binding proteins, and effects on rG4-mediated gene regulation in cells [40]. In addition, the modular design used here could be further expanded by incorporating other G4-binding motifs, such as RGG/RG-rich domains, to generate variants with different binding preferences or cellular properties [46–48]. To gain a deeper understanding of how these chimeric peptides recognize and bind to rG4 structures, high-resolution structural techniques like cryo-electron microscopy or X-ray crystallography are also indispensable. Such detailed structural insights are crucial for elucidating the molecular interactions and will provide a solid foundation for designing more effective next-generation chimeric peptide molecules.

More broadly, this work suggests that combining two nucleic acid-binding motifs can be a useful strategy for improving rG4 recognition. The approach is not limited to Rhau18 and Pep11, and future designs could pair modules with complementary interaction features, such as aromatic stacking, electrostatic contacts, or groove/loop recognition. Such combinations may help tune peptide binding toward different RNA or DNA structural targets. However, further systematic testing of linker design, module orientation, peptide stability, and target selectivity will be needed before general design rules can be established.

In summary, we designed chimeric peptides by fusing a de novo selected rG4-binding peptide, Pep11, with the natural G4-interacting peptide Rhau18. These chimeras bind hTERC rG4 with higher affinity than the individual modules, stabilize the rG4 structure in vitro, and show functional effects in telomerase and reporter assays. MD analyses and alanine-mutagenesis experiments further support a model in which multiple peptide residues contribute to rG4 recognition. Overall, our results indicate that modular peptide engineering is a robust and practical strategy for developing rG4-targeting ligands and provide a scientific basis for further optimization of peptide-based tools for studying functional nucleic acid structures.
