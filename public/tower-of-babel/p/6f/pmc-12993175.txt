Mutual interactions between HG-LG gellan gum and tea polyphenols: Concentration-dependent structural modulation and enhanced antioxidant stability
Authors: Hongxia Lu, Tong Chang, Ming Geng, Lulu Qi, Decong Xu, Mengting Zhang, Yu Zhu, Guilan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tea polyphenols (TPs) provide health benefits but are unstable during the thermal and pH variations during food processing and storage. In this study, we developed a composite hydrogel system using low- and high-acyl gellan gums (HG–LG, 1:1 w/w) for efficient TP protective matrix. The characteristics were systematically evaluated. Incorporating TPs enhanced the gel texture and water retention but increasing the TP content led to a decrease in the storage and loss moduli and texture parameters. Analyses confirmed the formation of a stable, hydrogen bonded 3D network. Molecular dynamics simulations further revealed that TPs, in particular epigallocatechin gallate, interact strongly with gellan gum through multiple hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, stabilizing the compounds and enhancing thermal stability. The gels retained over 90% DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging activity following prolonged heating, demonstrating enhanced stability. Thus, the HG–LG composite hydrogel is an effective, thermally stable carrier for TPs in functional food applications.

Introduction

Tea polyphenols (TPs) are naturally occurring plant polyphenols abundant in all parts of tea plants (Yan et al., 2020). Polyphenols have drawn significant interest from researchers owing to their potential health benefits, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, anticancer, cardioprotective, antidiabetic, and antimicrobial properties. Furthermore, their applications in food processing and preservation and as functional ingredients are well recognized (Klisurova et al., 2019). However, TPs are unstable with respect to pH, temperature, light irradiation, and oxidation, which significantly affects their bioavailability.

Therefore, several strategies to enhance and preserve the stability of TPs have been developed (Lei et al., 2019; Yan et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2022). One strategy for improving TP stability and controlling release is loading into a gel matrix (Liu et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2023). The utilized substrates are composed of natural biopolymer polysaccharides (Wang et al., 2023), which are abundant, biocompatible, and safe materials that can form stable structures and interact effectively with bioactive substances, making them an ideal medium for loading (Manzoor et al., 2022). Further, the release characteristics of TPs can be controlled by embedding them in a polysaccharide matrix, which also protects them from environmental stressors (Zhang et al., 2023). While common polysaccharide matrices such as alginate and chitosan have been extensively studied for bioactive loading matrix, they exhibit inherent limitations regarding mechanical strength, gastric stability, or compatibility with specific compounds (Damiri et al., 2024). Gellan gum, particularly through blends of its high-acyl (HG) and low-acyl (LG) variants, offers tunable rheological and textural properties that can address some of these challenges. However, its potential as a composite protective matrix for polyphenols remains underexplored.

Gellan gum is a safe and nontoxic microbial extracellular polysaccharide known for its exceptional thermal stability and high resistance to acid, microbial, and enzymatic degradation (Wu et al., 2018; Xu et al., 2019). Crucially, gellan gum hydrogels can load and protect active substances. Gellan is classified based on its acyl group content into low-acyl (LG) and high-acyl (HG) types. HG forms soft, elastic gels, whereas LG produces translucent, rigid gels. Both HG and LG have limited applications in food systems (Bajaj et al., 2007; Fialho et al., 2008). To address this, researchers have explored various HG–LG complexes and their combination with other hydrophilic colloids to form composite gel systems (Banerjee et al., 2013; Sow et al., 2019). These composite systems show improved rheological and processing properties, as well as favorable structural characteristics for food applications. In our previous study, the properties of a TP–LG gel and their pH dependence were examined (Zhang et al., 2023), revealing that hydrogen bonds between TPs and LG chains resulted in a compact and robust network structure. Thus, TPs improved both the textural properties and water-holding capacity of a TP–LG complex, enhancing structural stability; consequently, TPs and gellan gum are promising composite components (Banerjee et al., 2013). However, the characteristics of TP–HG–LG mixed gels and the effect of mixing on the stability of TPs remain unexplored. The selection of an appropriate HG:LG ratio is critical for optimizing gel performance. Previous studies on mixed gellan systems suggest that a 1:1 HG-LG ratio promotes the formation of an interpenetrating polymer network (IPN), combining the high strength of LG gels with the deformability and superior water-holding capacity of HG gels (Huang et al., 2003; Mao et al., 2000). Furthermore, such mixed gels have been reported to exhibit acid-induced strengthening (Bradbeer et al., 2014), a property that could potentially enhance the protection of sensitive compounds during gastric transit. Based on these premises, we hypothesized that this specific 1:1 ratio would create a dense and stable micro-environment conducive to protecting TP from thermal and oxidative degradation. In particular, the dynamic evolution of this mixed gel network under thermal and mechanical stresses, which is crucial to its functional performance in processed foods, has not yet been systematically investigated in the presence of TP.

To address this knowledge gap, we propose a multi-scale characterization approach. By integrating rheological analyses (such as strain sweeps and static and dynamic rheological tests) with thermogravimetric profiling, we aim to elucidate how TP-HG-LG interactions affect the gel resilience to mechanical and thermal stress. This methodological framework goes beyond conventional texture and color evaluations, providing mechanistic insights into network stability under simulated processing conditions.

In particular, we focused on adding various concentrations of TP to an HG–LG matrix and evaluating the resulting gel properties by analyzing the viscoelastic behavior, thermal stability, microstructural, structural, and morphological characteristics, along with textural and color changes. The objective was to elucidate the structure–property–function relationships between gel characteristics, TP interactions, and mixed polysaccharide behavior. The findings of this work contribute to a mechanistic understanding of how TP concentration modulates the structure of mixed HG-LG gels, and how this matrix, in turn, confers thermal and oxidative protection to TP. These insights provide a foundation for designing polysaccharide-based protective matrices suitable for thermally processed functional foods. Furthermore, the results highlight the potential of the 1:1 HG-LG ratio as a stable carrier for improving the retention and stability of TPs in complex food systems.

Materials and methods

Materials

LG, HG were acquired from CP Kelco Company. Tea polyphenols (TP) extract was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biological Co., Ltd. (China). According to the manufacturer, the total polyphenol content was ≥98%. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis revealed the following major catechin profile (percentage of total extract): epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) 40.46%, epicatechin gallate (ECG) 19.8%, gallocatechin gallate (GCG) 4.49%, epicatechin (EC) 2.98%, and gallocatechin (GC) 0.36%. Other components such as gallic acid, caffeine, and residual moisture constitute the remaining mass. Analytical-grade tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane, gallic acid, and sodium chloride were obtained from China Medicinal Group Reagent Co., Ltd.

Preparation of TP–HG–LG systems

HG and LG, in a 1:1 mass ratio (mass fraction: 1%), were accurately weighed, added to deionized water (100 mL). The mixture was then transferred to a beaker and stirred using a magnetic stirrer at 300 rpm in a constant-temperature water bath at 90 °C for 30 min. Complete dissolution was confirmed by visual inspection against a light source, ensuring the solution was transparent and free of visible particulates. After the colloids had completely dissolved, different proportions of TPs (0.0%, 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3%, 0.4%, and 0.5%) were added to the system and mixed thoroughly. The total amount of the produced solution was maintained at 100 mL by adding deionized water heated to 90 °C to compensate for evaporative losses. The solution was transferred to silicone molds while hot. After lowering the temperature, the molds were refrigerated at 4 °C for 24 h before analysis.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The prepared samples were freeze-dried and positioned on a guide station using conductive glue. Subsequently, the sample surface was coated with a gold membrane and analyzed by SEM operated at 20.0 kV (Zeiss Evo MA15).

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The freeze-dried samples were analyzed by FTIR spectroscopy (Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS 50, USA) equipped with a attenuated total reflectance (ATR; KRS-5) probe. Samples were scanned between 400 and 4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 using the ATR method (Boni et al., 2016).

TP–HG–LG gel properties

Textural properties

A texture analyzer (TA.XT Plus100C, Stable Micro System, UK) equipped with a P50 probe was used to evaluate the texture profile of the manufactured TP–HG–LG gels (Wang et al., 2021). The gels were manufactured in cylindrical shapes with a height of 23 mm and diameter of 25 mm. Texture profile analysis (TPA) was conducted under the following conditions: preload speed of 1.0 mm/s, compression speed of 1.0 mm/s, recovery speed of 1.0 mm/s, 50% deformation, and trigger force of 5 g.

Water-holding capacity (WHC)

The WHC of each sample was determined using an ultrahigh-speed centrifuge (Xin et al., 2024). A 1.5-mL plastic centrifuge tube was filled with the produced material (1 mL), and the tube was centrifuged for 30 min at 10,000 rpm. After removing the top layer of water, the WHC was calculated using Eq. (1).(1)WHC=W2−W1W1−W0×100%

Here, W0 is the mass of the centrifuge tube [g], W1 is the total mass of the TP–HG–LG gel and tube before centrifugation [g], and W2 is the total mass of the TP–HG–LG gel and tube after centrifugation, [g].

Swelling capacity

The TP–HG–LG gel samples were cut into 1 cm3 cubes for freeze-drying and subsequently immersed in deionized water. Remaining surface moisture was absorbed using filter paper until a constant weight was reached, and the equilibrium swelling ratio (SR) was determined using Eq. (2).(2)SR=Mt−M0Mt

Here, Mt. is the weight of the composite gel after swelling [g],and M0 is the dry weight of the initial composite gel [g].

Color difference analysis

A colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) was used to measure the color differences at five randomly selected points on the surfaces of TP–HG–LG gel samples, which were sliced into 1 cm3 cubes (Mirani & Goli, 2021). The colors were quantified using the lightness (L*), red–green (a*), and blue–yellow (b*) coordinates. The device was calibrated with a standard white tile before recording these values. The overall color difference (∆E) was calculated using Eq. (3).(3)ΔE=ΔL∗2+Δa∗2+Δb∗21/2

Thermogravimetric analysis

TGA analysis was conducted using a simultaneous thermal analyzer (SDT 650, TA, USA). Approximately 3–5 mg of samples with different tea polyphenol (TP) contents were heated from 30 to 550 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere at a flow rate of 20 mL/min. To comprehensively evaluate the thermal behavior, a segmented heating program was employed: the temperature was increased at 10 °C/min from 30 to 190 °C, then at 5 °C/min from 190 to 280 °C, and finally at 10 °C/min from 280 to 550 °C.

Rheological behavior

A rheometer (Discovery HAAKE RS6000, Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., USA) was used to assess the gel strength of the various substances. A 35-mm diameter parallel plate geometry was utilized and a 1-mm gap height was set. At 25 °C and 0.1% strain (in the linear viscoelastic region), the frequency sweep mode was applied in the range of 0.1–10 Hz. At shear rates of 0.1–100 s−1, the apparent viscosity was recorded. All measurements were performed in the linear viscoelastic region, which was evaluated by strain sweeps in the range of 0.1–100%.

Antioxidant properties

Antioxidant activity was assessed using DPPH and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity assays (Wang & Jee, 2023). For the DPPH assay, a DPPH stock solution (0.2 mM) was prepared in ethanol and then diluted to obtain an absorbance of 0.7–0.9 at 517 nm before use. Subsequently, the sample solution (1 mL), which refers to the solution obtained after dissolving the gel, was combined with DPPH reagent (2 mL) dissolved in ethanol. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 30 °C in the dark. Next, absorbance was measured at 517 nm using distilled water as the reference. The scavenging rate of DPPH radicals was calculated by subtracting the absorbance of the blank sample from that of the sample and multiplying the result by 100% (Eq. (4)).(4)DPPH radical scavenging activity=A0−A1A0×100%

Here, A0 is the absorbance value of the control group, and A1 is the absorbance value of the sample.

In addition, the •OH scavenging activity assay involved mixing the sample solution (1 mL), ferrous sulfate (1 mL, 9 mM), salicylic acid–ethanol solution (1 mL, 9 mM), and hydrogen peroxide solution (1 mL, 8.8 mM). This mixture was incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark, and the absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 517 nm, using distilled water as the blank. The •OH scavenging rate was calculated using Eq. (5).(5)⋅OHscavenging activity=1−A5−A4A3

Here, A3 is the absorbance value of the sample blank, A4 is the absorbance value of deionized water used instead of salicylic acid, and A5 is the absorbance value of the sample set.

The DPPH and •OH scavenging activities of the TP–HG–LG gels were determined using solutions with five different pH values (3, 5, 7, 9, and 11) prepared from 1 M solutions of HCl and NaOH. The TP–HG–LG sample containing 0.3% TPs was immersed in a constant-temperature water bath heated to 50, 60, 70, and 80 °C for 10 h. After heat treatment, the initial volume was supplemented with deionized water at the corresponding temperature, and the corresponding scavenging activities were calculated using Eqs. (4) and (5).

Molecular dynamics (MD)

A model of gellan gum (twelve monosaccharides) was constructed using the CHARMM-GUI (Jo et al., 2008). Gellan gum is composed of repeating units, formed of two glucose, one rhamnose, and one glucuronic acid. The initial structures of catechin (CAT) and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) were obtained from the PubChem database and geometrically optimized using the GAFF force field in Avogadro (Hanwell et al., 2012). The initial structure of the water molecules was modeled using SPC216, with topological parameters and charges assigned using TIP3P under the AMBER14 force field. CAT and EGCG molecule were optimized using density functional theory in ORCA (Neese, 2018) using the r2SCAN-3c method. Subsequently, single-point energy calculations were performed at the B3LYP/G D3 def2-TZVP level of theory with the def2/J RIJCOSX approximation. The molecular topology was created using the GAFF2 force field using the Antechamber, and RESP2 charges for CAT and EGCG and MMFF94 charges for gellan gum were fitted using Multiwfn (Lu & Chen, 2012; Zhang & Lu, 2021). All computations were performed using GROMACS 21.6. A 10 × 10 × 10 nm3 periodic cube box was constructed using gmx editconf, with a gellan gum chain added to the center of the box and 10 CAT or EGCG molecules randomly added to the box. Subsequently, 16734 water molecules and counterions (3 Na+ ions) were added to the system. Energy minimization (1000.0 kJ/mol/nm) was performed using the steepest-descent method; subsequently, conjugate gradient optimization (100.0 kJ/mol/nm) was used for a second round of energy minimization. The system was equilibrated using the conventional NVT ensemble (298.15 k, 1 ns) and constant pressure and temperature NPT ensemble (1 ns) (Zhu et al., 2024). The linear constraint solver method was used for all the essential constraints in the system. The simulated temperature (298.15 K) and pressure (1.0 bar) were attained using a velocity rescaling thermostat and a Parrinello–Rahman barostat, with time constants of 0.1 and 2 ps, respectively. After pre-equilibrium, MD simulations of 200 ns were conducted for all simulation systems. Long-range interactions were calculated using the Particle–Mesh–Ewald technique, with a 10-Å cutoff for the van der Waals interaction. The time increment was 2 fs, and a snapshot was captured every 1.0 ps. After MD simulation, the trajectory was examined using the GROMACS21.6 software package and visually examined using VMD (Humphrey et al., 1996).

Data processing and analysis

All experiments were independently repeated three times, and data are expressed as the mean ± SD. Graphs were created with GraphPad Prism 9 software and Origin 2024. Statistical comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test using SPSS 26.0. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Sem

Fig. 1 shows the microstructure of the freeze-dried gels composed of HG and LG with varying TP contents. The HG–LG gel exhibited a loose and irregular sheet-like network structure with large pores and thin walls. At low concentrations of 0.1% and 0.2% TP (Fig. b and c), the gel network gradually became denser, with an increase in pore number, and a transition toward a more regular honeycomb-like morphology. As TP concentration increased to 0.3% (d), the gel microstructure displayed the most dense and uniform state, characterized by small, evenly distributed pores, thick and continuous walls, and a clearly defined network skeleton. This enhancement can be attributed to the molecular interactions between TP and the gellan gum chains.The phenomenon can be attributed to the polyhydroxyl structure of TPs, which facilitates hydrogen bonding with high-acyl and low-acyl polysaccharides and increases the physical entanglement between these components. This improves gel stability and induces changes in internal structures of the gel particles (Biao et al., 2019). However, when TP concentration was further increased to 0.5% (f), the network structure reverted to a looser state, with enlarged and irregular pores, and localized collapse and fusion of the structure. This phenomenon is likely due to the self-aggregation of excessive TP molecules, which disrupted the homogeneity of the original gel network and compromised its structural integrity. TPA

Fig. 3 displays the TPA results revealing the mechanical characteristics of the gels. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, the gel hardness decreased with the increase in TP concentration from 0.1% to 0.5%. The HG–LG gel without TPs had the highest hardness (576.01 g), whereas the TP–HG–LG gel with 0.5% TPs had the lowest (472.17 g). Compared to the TP–HG–LG gel, the HG–LG gel exhibited higher hardness, springiness, and chewiness. The TP–HG–LG gel was found to be more brittle than the HG–LG gel, suggesting a weakening effect induced by TP addition. The discrete bigel phase acts as an “active filler,” strengthening the gel structuring through HG–LG interactions and forming a more stable interpenetrating gel network. However, the introduction of TPs appears to disrupt this dense network because the TP molecules interpenetrate and interfere with the HG–LG gel structure. Consequently, the original dense structure thus becomes relatively weak, decreasing gel hardness, elasticity, and chewiness. The TP–HG–LG gel is soft and elastic, and its minimum elasticity reached 0.86 (p < 0.05). This observation is consistent with those of previous studies (Han et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2019), demonstrating that an increase in the polysaccharide content increases the steric barrier between molecules, reducing the possibility of intrachain ionic bonding.Fig. 3Effects of TP content on the (a) hardness, (b) chewiness, (c) cohesiveness, and (d) elasticity of the TP–HG–LG gels. Compared to the HG–LG gel without TPs, the TP–HG–LG gels exhibited lower adhesiveness. Therefore, the observed difference in cohesion between the TP–HG–LG gels is not significant.

Swelling analysis

Swelling is a measure of the increase in volume resulting from the adsorption of solvent, offering insights into the physical stability of polymeric systems (Wang et al., 2023). Fig. 5 shows the swelling kinetic curves of the HG–LG gel and TP–HG–LG gel samples with varying TP concentrations. The swelling rate of the gels increased rapidly within the first 2 h, followed by a plateau as the swelling equilibrium was reached. At equilibrium, the swelling rate of the HG–LG gel was above 99.2%, while that of the TP–HG–LG composite gels was below 99.0%. In addition, the swelling degree exhibited a gradual decrease with the increase in TP content, suggesting a potential inhibitory effect of elevated TP concentrations on gel swelling. This trend may be associated with the enhanced non-covalent interactions, particularly hydrogen bonding, between TP molecules and the HG–LG gel network with the increase of TP concentration; such enhanced interactions might reduce intermolecular spacing and potentially facilitate the formation of a relatively denser gel network structure, which could in turn hinder molecular mobility within the composite gel system. Furthermore, the hydrogen bonding interactions among TPs, HG–LG polymers and water molecules, together with the van der Waals interactions in the gel system, may be moderately attenuated at higher TP concentrations, which could further contribute to the slight reduction in gel swelling observed in the experiment.Fig. 5Effect of TP content on the swelling degree of TP–HG–LG gels.Fig. 5

TGA

The thermal stability of the HG-LG composite gels and those incorporated with TP was evaluated using TGA. The TGA and corresponding differential thermogravimetric (DTG) curves are presented in Fig. 7. The weight loss profile of the HG-LG-TP composite gels from 30 to 500 °C can be generally divided into two distinct stages. The initial weight loss observed at approximately 100 °C is primarily attributed to the evaporation of free and bound water within the samples. The main phase of thermal decomposition occurred between 220 °C and 300 °C, which is associated with the disruption of intermolecular hydrogen bonds and the thermal degradation of the polysaccharide backbones.Fig. 7TGA (a) and DTG (b) profiles of the HG-LG gel and the composite gels containing various TP contents of 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3%, 0.4%, and 0.5%.Fig. 7

TGA (a) and DTG (b) profiles of the HG-LG gel and the composite gels containing various TP contents of 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3%, 0.4%, and 0.5%.

Pure TP exhibited rapid weight loss starting at approximately 220 °C and was nearly completely degraded by 550 °C with negligible residue, indicating its inherently low thermal stability. In contrast, the pristine HG-LG matrix demonstrated superior stability, retaining approximately 25% of its initial mass at 550 °C. Notably, all HG-LG-TP composite gels exhibited a significantly higher residual mass ratio at 550 °C compared to both pure TP and the HG-LG matrix. This enhanced thermal stability confirms that the gellan gum network effectively protects TP from thermal degradation. Furthermore, the increased residual mass suggests the formation of strong intermolecular interactions—such as hydrogen bonds of varied types and lengths between the polyphenols and the HG-LG matrix, thereby reinforcing the overall structural integrity of the complex. This observation aligns with previous reports indicating that polyphenol–polysaccharide interactions can significantly improve thermal stability (Xu et al., 2024).

The DTG curves provided further insight into the degradation kinetics. All composite samples exhibited a prominent weight loss peak between 220 °C and 260 °C, corresponding to the primary cleavage of the gellan gum backbone. The maximum decomposition temperatures (Tmax) were recorded at 239 °C for the pure HG-LG gel and 228 °C for pure TP. Upon the incorporation of TP, the Tmax of the HG-LG-TP composites progressively shifted to higher temperatures, reaching 242 °C at a TP concentration of 0.5%. This positive shift indicates a synergistic enhancement in thermal stability facilitated by the interactions between TP and the HG-LG matrix, which corroborates the FTIR results. Finally, between 300 °C and 400 °C, the composites experienced minimal weight loss with the DTG curves approaching the baseline, signifying the completion of the primary degradation phase and the transition into sample carbonization.

Rheological properties

The rheological properties of the composite gels were characterized using small-amplitude oscillatory shear measurements, including amplitude sweep, frequency sweep, and steady-state flow tests. As shown in Fig. 8(Ι), the effect of the TP concentration on the mechanical strength of TP-HG-LG composite gels was significant. All samples consistently exhibited solid-like behavior, as shown by the storage modulus (G′) being larger than the loss modulus (G“) within the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). All samples exhibited a relatively broad LVR (up to 1% strain), indicating stable gel structures under small deformations. Similarly, the yield stress (intersection of G′ and G") increased with the increase in TP content. Fig. 8(ΙΙ) presents the frequency sweep results. Within the LVR, all gels had high mechanical strength, and G′ showed minimal frequency dependence (0.1–10 Hz). For all formulations, G′ remained greater than G“, reflecting predominantly elastic behavior. At lower TP concentrations, both the G′ and G" values of TP-HG-LG gels were slightly higher than those of the control HG-LG gel. However, as the TP concentration increased, although the LVR broadened, both G′ and G“ values decreased at small deformations and low frequencies (0.1–10 Hz). This observation appears contradictory to the amplitude sweep results, in which a higher TP content enhanced the gel strength. This apparent discrepancy may be attributed to the dual role of TP in the gel network. At low TP concentrations, a limited amount of TP maybe act as a cross-linker, interacting with gellan chains via hydrogen bonding and resulting in a denser, strengthened gel network. In contrast, excessive TP addition may promote the aggregation of TP molecules, disrupting intermolecular cross-linking between gellan gum chains. Alternatively, the competitive binding of TP with gellan gum may compromise the original gel network integrity, yielding a looser microstructure. Consequently, although higher TP concentrations increased the LVR in the strain sweep tests, they reduced the elasticity (G′ and G") at small deformations and low frequencies, as observed in the frequency sweep experiments. As presented in Fig. 8(ΙΙΙ), all gels exhibited shear-thinning behavior. With increasing TP concentration, both HG-LG and TP-HG-LG gels showed a similar trend in apparent viscosity. Notably, when the TP concentration was below 0.3%, the apparent viscosity of TP-HG-LG gels was only marginally higher than that of HG-LG gels, suggesting that TP concentration did not affect the rheological properties of TP-HG-LG gels significantly. This suggests that TP addition has a negligible effect on the overall viscosity, indicating that viscosity is not the dominant rheological feature of HG–LG gels, consistent with previous reports.

Effect of pH

The DPPH and •OH radical scavenging activity decreased with the increase in pH for all gels, with increasing pH for TPs and the TP–HG–LG gels, but the DPPH radical scavenging activity of the TP–HG–LG gels was higher than that of a TP solution at the same pH. As the pH increased to 11, the DPPH radical scavenging rate decreased to 43.38%, significantly different from that (49.32%) at pH 3. However, for the TP–HG–LG gels, the DPPH scavenging rate decreased by only 15.61% on increasing the pH from 3 to 11. Thus, the presence of TPs increases the antioxidant activity, consistent with previous findings (Cohen et al., 2008). These results suggest that polyphenol–polysaccharide interactions yield gels that protect polyphenol activity, even at a pH conditions similar to that of gastric fluid (Valli & Clark, 2010). Thus, the TP–HG–LG gels have enhanced polyphenol bioavailability and high antioxidant ability.

Effect of temperature

As shown in Fig. 9b and d, the antioxidant activity of the TPs and TP–HG–LG was be strongly affected by temperature. From 25 to 80 °C, the DPPH scavenging rate of TPs and the TP–HG–LG gels decreased from 93% to 87.72% and from 94.5% to 90.04%, respectively. However, the TP–HG–LG complex gel showed stronger DPPH free radical scavenging activity than the TPs at the same temperature, and this difference became more pronounced with the increase in temperature. These results demonstrate that the lower the TP stability, the greater the degrees of crosslinking and entanglement of the TP–HG–LG gel during heating, which forms a more stable structure with higher thermal stability than that of pure TPs. Thus, the thermal stability of the TP–HG–LG gels was enhanced compared to that of the TPs, as also reported by Jin et al. (2020). Hence, the interaction between TPs and the HG–LG gel matrix significantly affects the thermal stability and antioxidant activity of the composite gel, consistent with the FTIR and TGA results. These findings confirm that TPs effectively enhance the antioxidant stability of the TP–HG–LG gels at higher temperatures and extreme pH values.

Conclusions

A TP–HG–LG composite hydrogel was developed using gellan-gum-based polysaccharides as the matrix and TPs for functional enhancement. Comprehensive evaluation of the physicomechanical and functional properties was conducted. FTIR analysis confirmed the formation of a stable network through hydrogen bonding between the TPs and HG–LG. MD simulations further revealed that TPs, particularly EGCG, form strong hydrogen-bonding and electrostatic interactions with hydroxyl and carboxyl groups on the gellan gum chains, with EGCG capable of forming up to nine stable hydrogen bonds. These interactions, supported by a binding energy of −908.356 kJ/mol, substantially enhance the microstructural stability of the composite. The synergistic effect attributed to the entanglement between HG and LG chains endows the composite hydrogel with significantly superior rheological stability and structural toughness compared to single component systems. Consistent with these findings, TGA demonstrated that despite partial disruption of the gel's macroscopic 3D network, reflected in reduced hardness, elasticity, and chewiness, the microstructural thermal stability of the composite was significantly because of the enhanced hydrogen-bond network. This structural reinforcement effectively preserves the antioxidant activity of TPs, even at high temperatures. Further, with the increase in the TP content above 0.3%, the steric hindrance of TP molecules increased, interfering with HG-LG chain entanglement and resulting in a progressive decrease in the gel's macroscopic structural integrity, as confirmed by multiple analytical approaches. Rheological tests indicated a pronounced decrease in storage modulus, and TPA revealed reductions in gel hardness, elasticity, and chewiness. SEM observations revealed a transformation toward a layered, porous matrix. MD simulations indicated strong TP–gellan gum aggregation, and excessive TP loading resulted in local over-aggregation, impairing gel continuity and uniformity and further decreasing the mechanical strength. Nevertheless, the TGA results revealed that TP inclusion enhanced the thermal stability of the gel by promoting hydrogen bond formation with the matrix polysaccharides. This dual effect, mechanical weakening on a macroscopic scale but molecular stabilization, was further highlighted by differences between static (TPA) and dynamic (rheology and TGA) assessments, underscoring the dependence of the gel's properties on the mode and extent of applied stress.

Additionally, increasing the TP content darkened the gel color and slightly decreased swelling capacity, although water retention consistently remained above 95%. Under varying temperature and pH conditions, the TP-encapsulated gels showed significantly higher DPPH and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity compared to free TPs, indicating improved protection conferred by the gel matrix. MD simulations supported these observations, demonstrating that stable TP–GG complexes embed phenolic hydroxyl groups within the gel network, shielding them from external stressors (such as high temperature and extreme pH) and, thus, preserving antioxidant activity.

In summary, TP incorporation into the hydrogel system results in a concentration- and condition-dependent dual effect: at the microscopic level, TPs enhance thermal stability and antioxidant retention through strong hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions; at the macroscopic level, excessive TPs may compromise gel mechanical strength by disrupting polysaccharide chain entanglement. Ultimately, this study demonstrates that a TP concentration of 0.3% serves as the optimal formulation for developing an effective, thermally stable carrier for functional food applications. This specific concentration achieves the best balance between maintaining the macroscopic structural integrity of the gel matrix and providing antioxidant environmental protection. By integrating experimental analyses with molecular simulations, this study clarifies the multi-scale interaction mechanisms between GG and TPs, providing theoretical and practical guidance for the design of TP-fortified composite gels with tailored functional properties. Nevertheless, this study primarily focused on a pure gel matrix, and aspects such as the gel's performance in complex food systems, its sensory attributes, and long-term storage stability were not addressed. Therefore, future studies should focus on incorporating the composite gel into real food matrices, evaluating its physicochemical stability during storage, and conducting in vitro digestion studies to assess its potential for practical applications in functional food development.
