Pre-operative single-level ultrasound-guided superficial parasternal intercostal plane block and pulmonary recovery after cardiac surgery with sternotomy: a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Pierre Huette, Camille Daumin, Rémi Fontaine, Thomas Lefebvre, Paul Tarpin, Christophe Beyls, Brigitte Gubler, Yazine Mahjoub, Adrien Ollier, Osama Abou-Arab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract

Editor,

After cardiac surgery with sternotomy, parasternal blocks are increasingly recommended for pain management, with PROSPECT guidelines endorsing them as part of multimodal analgesia.1 However, their impact on postoperative pulmonary recovery has not been specifically evaluated.

We conducted a prospective, single-centre, randomised, single-blind trial with blinded endpoint evaluation (PROBE design) at Amiens University Hospital, France (NCT05515809). Adults undergoing elective cardiac surgery via full sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass were randomised 1:1. The intervention group received pre-operative, single-level, bilateral ultrasound-guided superficial parasternal intercostal plane (SPIP) blocks using ropivacaine 0.2% (20 ml per side), injected between the pectoralis major and external intercostal muscles approximately 2 cm lateral to the sternum at the 4th–5th intercostal space, after general anaesthesia but before skin incision.2 The control group received standard care without locoregional anaesthesia. All patients received protocol guided multimodal analgesia (paracetamol, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, nefopam and patient-controlled morphine).

The primary outcome was forced vital capacity (FVC, % predicted) measured by spirometry at 24 h after surgical closure. A sample size of 84 patients (42 per arm) was calculated to detect a 10% absolute difference in FVC decline (90% power, two-sided α = 0.05).3 Analyses used intention-to-treat with multiple imputation for missing data; secondary endpoints were corrected with Holm's method.

From 1 April 2023 to 30 June 2024, data from 82 patients were analysed (41 per group). Baseline characteristics were well-balanced; mean age was 65.1 ± 8.8 years (68.3% male) (Figure 1, Supplemental Digital Content)

As shown in Fig. 1 FVC declined sharply in both groups at 24 and 48 h before recovering toward baseline at 2 months. At 24 h after surgical closure, FVCs were 43.4 ± 18.2% (intervention) vs. 34.1 ± 13.1% (non-intervention) of the pre-operative levels. The adjusted FVC difference from baseline was +7.41 (95% confidence interval, −1.72 to 16.54)% higher in the intervention group, which was not statistically significant (P = 0.11; P = 1 after Holm correction). None of the secondary spirometric parameter differed significantly after adjustment (Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content). Pain scores at rest at 24 h were numerically lower in the intervention group (NRS 2.0 [1.0–3.0] vs. 3.5 [2.0–4.0]; P = 0.03), but this did not survive multiple testing correction (P = 0.57). Pulmonary complications, morphine consumption, mechanical ventilation duration and length of stay were similar between groups. No block-related adverse events were observed (Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content)

Our negative primary outcome aligns with Demarquette et al., who found that parasternal blocks did not improve quality of recovery at 24 h after sternotomy.4 Several factors likely explain our findings. First, with well-implemented contemporary multimodal analgesia in place, the incremental analgesic benefit of a single-shot block is modest – pain scores were already low in both groups. Second, post-sternotomy respiratory dysfunction is multifactorial: cardiopulmonary bypass effects, phrenic nerve injury, pleural effusions, chest wall oedema, opioid-induced respiratory depression, pain from sites not covered by parasternal blocks (mediastinal drains), and sedation-analgesia mismatch all contribute beyond what regional analgesia alone can address.5 Third, our blocks were given pre-operatively as single-shot injections, and FVC was measured at 24 h, a time point that may fall outside the expected analgesic window of ropivacaine in this compartment.6 Whether postoperative or continuous SPIP techniques would yield different results on early respiratory outcomes remains an open and clinically relevant question.7

In routine cardiac surgical patients managed with an effective multimodal analgesia protocol, a pre-operative single-level single-shot SPIP block did not improve functional pulmonary recovery at 24 h after surgical closure. Future research should evaluate postoperative and continuous block strategies, earlier respiratory endpoints and patients with pre-existing respiratory compromise.
