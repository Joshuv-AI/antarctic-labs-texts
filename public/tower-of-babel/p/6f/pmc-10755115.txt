Integrated environmental, lifestyle, and epigenetic risk prediction of primary gastric neoplasia using the longitudinally monitored cohorts
Authors: Genki Usui, Keisuke Matsusaka, Kie Kyon Huang, Feng Zhu, Tomohiro Shinozaki, Masaki Fukuyo, Bahityar Rahmutulla, Norikazu Yogi, Tomoka Okada, Mizuki Minami, Motoaki Seki, Eiji Sakai, Kazutoshi Fujibayashi, Stephen Kin Kwok Tsao, Christopher Khor, Tiing Leong Ang, Hiroyuki Abe, Hisahiro Matsubara, Masashi Fukayama, Toshiaki Gunji, Nobuyuki Matsuhashi, Teppei Morikawa, Tetsuo Ushiku, Khay Guan Yeoh, Patrick Tan, Atsushi Kaneda

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDNA methylation accumulates in non-malignant gastric mucosa after exposure to pathogens. To elucidate how environmental, methylation, and lifestyle factors interplay to influence primary gastric neoplasia (GN) risk, we analyzed longitudinally monitored cohorts in Japan and Singapore.MethodsAsymptomatic subjects who underwent a gastric mucosal biopsy on the health check-up were enrolled. We analyzed the association between clinical factors and GN development using Cox hazard models. We further conducted comprehensive methylation analysis on selected tissues, including (i) mucosae from subjects developing GN later, (ii) mucosae from subjects not developing GN later, and (iii) GN tissues and surrounding mucosae. We also use the methylation data of mucosa collected in Singapore. The association between methylation and GN risk, as well as lifestyle and methylation, were analyzed.FindingsAmong 4234 subjects, GN was developed in 77 subjects. GN incidence was correlated with age, drinking, smoking, and Helicobacter pylori (HP) status. Accumulation of methylation in biopsied gastric mucosae was predictive of higher future GN risk and shorter duration to GN incidence. Whereas methylation levels were associated with HP positivity, lifestyle, and morphological alterations, DNA methylation remained an independent GN risk factor through multivariable analyses. Pro-carcinogenic epigenetic alterations initiated by HP exposure were amplified by unfavorable but modifiable lifestyle choices. Adding DNA methylation to the model with clinical factors improved the predictive ability for the GN risk.InterpretationThe integration of environmental, lifestyle, and epigenetic information can provide increased resolution in the stratification of primary GN risk.FundingThe funds are listed in Acknowledgements section.Keywords: DNA methylation, H. pylori, Epstein–Barr virus, Smoking, Alcohol, Gastric cancer

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Aberrant DNA methylation accumulates at the promoter CpG islands in non-malignant gastric mucosa after exposure to environmental pathogens such as Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) and Epstein–Barr virus. The silencing of tumor suppressor genes due to the aberrant DNA methylation accumulated at the promoter CpG island is closely related to carcinogenesis. In addition, aberrant DNA methylation is associated with various cancer hallmarks such as altered expression of cancer driver and passenger genes, shortened telomeres, and chromosomal alterations. A previous cohort study revealed that aberrant DNA methylation accumulates in the gastric mucosa of patients with gastric neoplasia (GN) after surgical resection, and the methylation levels in the gastric mucosae reflect the risk of developing secondary gastric cancer.

Added value of this study

Accumulation of DNA methylation in gastric mucosae, observed several years before primary GN development, was predictive of higher primary GN risk and shorter duration to disease presentation, drawing parallels to recent reports of somatic mutations or copy number aberrations accumulating in non-neoplastic tissues. Methylation levels were associated with H. pylori positivity, lifestyles, e.g. drinking or smoking, and histopathological alterations; however, in multivariable analyses, DNA methylation remained an independent GN risk factor. Pro-carcinogenic epigenetic alterations initiated by H. pylori exposure were amplified by unfavorable but modifiable lifestyle choices. Primary GN risk increases as a result of a combination of multiple environmental factors and lifestyles, and the integrated information on environmental factors, lifestyle habits, and DNA methylation accurately reflects the risk of primary GN.

Implications of all the available evidence

The integration of environmental factors, lifestyle, and DNA methylation makes it possible to accurately estimate the primary GN risk for each individual. Risk estimation for primary GN using DNA methylation carries significant potential from a healthcare economic perspective because they stratify GN risk, even in subjects with H. pylori, enabling optimal prevention strategies. Our results emphasize that subjects with H. pylori should avoid both heavy drinking and smoking, especially those with DNA methylation, to avoid further DNA methylation of the gastric mucosa and increased GN risk.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally.1 GC development is influenced by both lifestyle and environmental factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, and infection by Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) and Epstein–Barr virus (EBV).2 H. pylori, a gram-negative bacterium found in the stomachs of approximately half of the global population,3,4 is a cardinal GC risk factor.3, 4, 5, 6 Chronic inflammation caused by H. pylori infection causes increased DNA methylation in the promoter CpG islands of multiple genes,7 including tumor suppressor genes.8,9 EBV, a human herpesvirus typically acquired in childhood, contributes to GC in 7–16% of cases.10,11 EBV is an epimutagen that induces de novo DNA methylation in a large proportion of the genome and epigenomic abnormalities induced by EBV infection are closely related to carcinogenesis.12,13

Epigenetic alterations in gastric tumors have been extensively reported.14, 15, 16 Notably, we and others have shown previously that aberrant epigenetic alterations can also accumulate in non-malignant tissues,17, 18, 19, 20 which may influence the risk of gastric neoplasia (GN).18, 19, 20, 21 Molecularly, aberrant DNA methylation is associated with various cancer hallmarks such as altered expression of cancer driver and passenger genes, shortened telomeres, and chromosomal alterations.20,21 Recently, it was reported that DNA methylation aberrations in the gastric mucosa of patients with GN after surgical resection are predictive of the risk of developing secondary GN.22,23 However, it remains unclear if DNA methylation alterations in the gastric mucosa of healthy individuals, observed sometimes years before GN development, are predictive of future malignancy and how environmental and lifestyle factors interplay with DNA methylation.

Here, we hypothesized that incorporating DNA methylation information, combined with clinical and lifestyle factors, would provide increased resolution in risk-stratifying populations for the prediction of primary GN including dysplasia/intraepithelial neoplasia (D/IEN) and carcinoma. To test this, we performed an integrated analysis of clinical factors and DNA methylation data of gastric tissues from a large longitudinally monitored cohort in Japan, with validation in a separate cohort from Singapore.

Methods

Additional protocols and detailed procedures are described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section.

Study population and check-up cohort study design

This longitudinal cohort study was conducted at NTT Medical Center, Tokyo. The hospital is conducting an Early Disease Detection and Prevention Study on healthy subjects.24, 25, 26 This health check-up program includes medical interviews, physical and physiological examinations, blood examinations, esophagogastroduodenoscopy, and unenhanced low-dose computed tomography for cancer screening. Healthy individuals who participated in the health check-up program and whose gastric mucosal tissues were collected via esophagogastroduodenoscopy between March 2006 and November 2018 were included in the present study (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Figure S1). Subject follow-up concluded in March 2019. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) history of GN, (ii) diagnosis of GN at the time of the health check-up or within six months after the health check-up, and (iii) no follow-up esophagogastroduodenoscopy examination more than six months after the first health check-up. In this study, the start of follow-up was defined as the date when the gastric mucosal biopsy was performed during the health check-up. The end of follow-up was defined as the date of GN diagnosis, censoring (defined as follows), and March 2019, whichever came first. If endoscopic follow-up was not conducted, patients were censored at the date of the last endoscopic examination.

Clinical data collected from the check-up cohort

In the health check-up program, participants were asked questions pertaining to age, biological sex, lifestyles, family histories of various diseases, and medical and treatment histories by professional interviewers. Alcohol consumption was evaluated in terms of volume per week. Subjects were categorized into four groups: non-drinkers (<40 g/week), light drinkers (40–140 g/week), moderate drinkers (140–280 g/week), and heavy drinkers (≥280 g/week).27 Smoking habits were assessed based on pack-years (packs of cigarettes per day multiplied by years of smoking). Subjects were categorized into three groups: non-smokers (<1 pack-year), light smokers (1–40 pack-years), and heavy smokers (≥40 pack-years).28 Smokers were also asked whether they had quit smoking and were categorized into four groups: former light smokers, current light smokers, former heavy smokers, and current heavy smokers. Regarding the consideration of sex/gender in our study design, we adopted biological sex and the data for biological sex was collected based on the self-reporting.

Gastric atrophy was evaluated by endoscopy specialists certified by the Japan Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society. The subjects were categorized into three groups according to their degree of atrophy: non-atrophy, closed-type atrophy, and open-type atrophy.29,30 H. pylori serum antibody titer was measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Eiken Chemical, Tokyo, Japan). The antibody titer threshold for H. pylori seropositivity was set at 10 U/mL.25 Patients were classified as H. pylori-infected if their H. pylori serum antibody test was positive or if gastric atrophy could be confirmed by endoscopy. Subjects with a negative H. pylori serum antibody test result and without endoscopic atrophy were characterized as participants not infected with H. pylori. The eradication of H. pylori was determined by a board-certified gastroenterologist from the Japanese Society of Gastroenterology based on the history of eradication determined by interview, endoscopic findings, and the results of the urea breath test.

To select the marker genes of each epigenotype, we also collected GN tissues (EBV CpG island methylator phenotype (EBV-CIMP) or high methylation epigenotype (HME)) (n = 20) and surrounding mucosae (n = 17) at the University of Tokyo Hospital (See Supplementary Materials and Methods for more detailed information on sample collection).

Clinical data and sample collection from the Gastric Cancer Epidemiology Program (GCEP) cohort

From the GCEP, a large prospective cohort study in Singapore,31 we selected 2565 of these subjects (Supplementary Figure S2a and Supplementary Materials and Methods). We analyzed the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip data of gastric mucosae biopsied from the antrum (n = 137) (frozen specimens) collected from the GCEP cohort20 (Accession number: GSE 103186) (Supplementary Figure S2b).

Infinium methylation assay

DNA methylation analysis was performed using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 164 extracted DNA samples (See Supplementary Materials and Methods). Infinium data of gastric tissues collected in our study are available in the GEO datasets (Accession number: GSE199898).

Identification of the epigenotypes for GN cases

To identify the epigenotypes for GN cases in this study, we conducted the unsupervised two-way hierarchical clustering analysis using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip data. We identified marker genes for each epigenotype using the Infinium data from our GN cases (n = 48) and non-GN non-H. pylori mucosae (n = 9) (See Supplementary Materials and Methods section). To validate whether these are appropriate markers for the classification of epigenotypes, we analyzed the Infinium HumanMethylation450 BeadChip data of GNs (frozen samples, n = 395) registered in The Cancer Genome Atlas Program (TCGA, https://cancergenome.nih.gov/) database.16,32 In this analysis, the gene order was predetermined, and a one-way hierarchical clustering analysis was conducted, specifically targeting markers present in both the Infinium HumanMethylation450 and Infinium MethylationEPIC platforms (Supplementary Figure S5).

Gene ontology (GO) analysis

Gene annotation enrichment analysis was performed for GO (biological process, cellular component, and molecular function) using the Functional Annotation tool Metascape (http://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1).33

Histopathology, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and in situ hybridization (ISH)

GN lesions were diagnosed according to WHO criteria.34 D/IEN was classified as low grade D/IEN and high grade D/IEN.35 Preneoplastic lesions in the mucosa including pathological atrophy and intestinal metaplasia were diagnosed according to the updated Sydney System.36 The biopsied mucosae were categorized into four stages: marked, moderate, mild, and normal, based on the presence and severity of atrophy and intestinal metaplasia. Cases with marked, moderate, mild, or no pathological atrophy/intestinal metaplasia were assigned pathological atrophy/intestinal metaplasia scores of 3, 2, 1, or 0, respectively. In addition, cases with open-type, closed-type, or no endoscopic atrophy were assigned endoscopic atrophy scores of 2, 1, or 0, respectively. The morphological abnormality score was calculated by summing these three scores. Cases with a morphological abnormality score of 2 or more were classified into the severe group, while those with a score of 1 or 0 were classified into the mild group. These pathological assessments were performed independently by two pathologists certified by the Japanese Society of Pathology. IHC and ISH were performed using the BenchMark ULTRA automated staining system (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland).

Microsatellite instability (MSI) assay

The MSI status of GN cases was analyzed using the MSI Analysis System, Version 1.2 (Promega, Charbonnières-les-Bains, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. GN samples were defined as MSI-H if two or more of the five microsatellite markers (NR-21, BAT-26, BAT-25, NR-24, and Mono-27) were altered; otherwise, they were defined as microsatellite stable.

Statistical analysis

Detailed statistical analysis methods and models are described in the Supplementary Materials and Methods section and Supplementary Table S1.

Clinical data analysis of all subjects in the check-up cohort

The relationship between the clinical factors and the incidence of GN development was analyzed using data from 4234 subjects who underwent a health check-up. GN incidence probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test according to clinical factors. The rates of GN development for each clinical factor were summarized using the person-years method and compared among clinical factors through hazard ratios (HRs) estimated by the univariable and multivariable Cox hazard models. To address confounding bias, potential confounders were included as explanatory variables in the multivariate Cox hazard model. P-values were calculated using the Wald test. Schoenfeld residuals were calculated to assess whether the proportional hazard assumption was met in these multivariable Cox models, and P-values were calculated using the Chi-squared test.

The clinical data from the check-up cohort (n = 4234) were combined with data from the GCEP cohort (n = 2565) to investigate the relationship between environmental factors and the incidence of GN. GN incidence probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the log-rank test according to the combination of H. pylori status, alcohol consumption, and smoking.

Data analysis focused on methylation analysis subjects in the check-up cohort

The relationship between clinicopathologic factors or DNA methylation levels and GN incidence was analyzed using data from 68 subjects for whom methylation analysis was performed on gastric mucosal biopsies collected during the health check-up. GN incidence probabilities were estimated using the weighted Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the robust score test to identify differences in GN incidence by clinicopathological factors and H. pylori status. GN incidence probabilities were also compared by the average β-values of common markers among all subjects and selected subjects with high-risk factors. Additionally, GN incidence probabilities were compared by the β-value of each candidate marker among selected subjects with high-risk factors. The weighted analyses were performed corresponding to the sampling probabilities of patients with future GN, subjects with H. pylori and no future GN, and subjects with neither H. pylori nor future GN.

To compare the predictive performance of clinical factors and DNA methylation (mean β-value of common markers) for GN development, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated using univariable logistic regression models with each factor as an explanatory variable and GN development as the outcome variable. To further investigate the potential improvement in the predictive performance of primary GN development by incorporating DNA methylation alongside clinical factors, we generated ROC curves using multivariable logistic regression models.

Comparison of DNA methylation levels of common markers

To investigate the associations between the β-values of common markers and clinicopathological factors or epigenotypes, we utilized the β-values of all 156 common markers across all samples. P-values were estimated using the Wald test with generalized linear models, treating the β-values as the outcome variable. In all linear regression models, 156 β-values per person were used as the unit of analysis, and intrapersonal correlations were accounted for using generalized estimating equations with individuals as clusters. Because the linear regression models were fitted through generalized estimating equations with robust variance estimator clustering outcomes within each person, the homoscedasticity and normality assumptions were not necessary to obtain valid confidence intervals for regression coefficients.

Comparison of DNA methylation levels of the markers for the classification of epigenotypes

To validate whether the selected markers for each epigenotype identified using the methylation data from the 48 GN samples in our cohort are appropriate markers for the classification of epigenotypes, we utilized the Infinium HumanMethylation450 data from the 395 GC tissue samples in the cohort. We examined the methylation status of the 411 markers that are present in both the Infinium HumanMethylation450 and Infinium MethylationEPIC platforms among the 585 identified markers. The β-values of EBV markers (n = 180), HME markers (n = 114), and common markers (n = 117) were compared between EBV-CIMP, HME, and low methylation epigenotype (LME). We utilized the β-values of all markers for each epigenotype across all samples. P-values were estimated using the Wald test with generalized linear models, treating the β-values as the outcome variable. In all linear regression models, 180 β-values per person were used as the unit of analysis for EBV markers, 114 β-values for HME markers, and 117 β-values for common markers. Intrapersonal correlations were accounted for using generalized estimating equations, with individuals considered as clusters. An independent correlation structure was assumed for intrapersonal correlations, although this assumption need not be correct.

Others

To investigate the associations between DNA methylation accumulated in the gastric mucosal tissue at the check-up and GN incidence, we utilized the β-values of all 156 common markers across all sample (patients with future GN (n = 21) and subjects with H. pylori and no future GN (n = 38)). The odds ratios (ORs) for GN development among the DNA methylation levels were estimated using univariate and multivariable logistic regression models. To address confounding bias, potential confounders were included as explanatory variables in the multivariate logistic regression models. In the logistic regression models, 156 observations per person were used as the unit of analysis, in which only the β-value data varied within the person. Intrapersonal correlations were considered using generalized estimating equations, and an independent correlation structure was assumed for intrapersonal correlations.

To evaluate whether methylation accumulates further in the presence of H. pylori eradication and continued smoking, we selected the 14 patients whose gastric mucosa was sampled at two different time points after H. pylori eradication. P-values were estimated using the Wald test with generalized linear models, treating the difference in paired β-values of common markers between the two collections as the outcome variable and smoking status (continuous smokers or former smokers who quit before the additional collection) as the explanatory variable. In this model, 156 values per person were used as the unit of analysis, and intrapersonal correlations were accounted for using generalized estimating equations with individuals as clusters. An independent correlation structure was assumed for intrapersonal correlations, although this assumption need not be correct.

To investigate associations between clinicopathological factors and GN development (biopsy from patients with future GN vs. biopsy from subjects with no future GN), Welch’s t-test or Fisher’s exact test was performed. To examine associations between clinicopathological factors, including alcohol consumption (heavy drinkers vs. non-drinkers) and smoking (heavy smokers vs. non-smokers), and the β-values of the common markers (biopsy from subjects with no future GN), we conducted a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. To assess whether differences in β-value are associated with the time to onset of GN development, we compared the correlation between the average β-values of common markers in biopsies from patients with future GN and the time interval until GN development and calculated the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (R). Fisher’s exact test was used to assess associations between lifestyle and morphological abnormality (severe vs. mild). To evaluate the correlation among the initial elevation in β-values of common markers due to H. pylori infection alone, the accelerated increase in β-values due to the addition of drinking, and the accelerated increase due to the addition of smoking, we calculated the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient (R), and P-values were calculated using t-test. The association among genes with initiated methylation by H. pylori, genes accelerated methylation by alcohol consumption, and genes accelerated methylation by smoking were investigated using Fisher’s exact test.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, URL) and open-source software Cluster 3.0.37 The functions “lm.cluster” and “glm.cluster” were used to solve the generalized estimating equations.

Results

Association between clinical factors and GN development

Among 6459 healthy individuals who participated in the health check-up program and whose gastric mucosal tissues were collected via esophagogastroduodenoscopy, 4234 asymptomatic subjects were enrolled in the study after excluding 114 subjects with a history of GN, 144 subjects diagnosed with GN at or within 6 months of the health check-up, and 1967 subjects who had not undergone esophagogastroduodenoscopy more than 6 months after their initial health check-up (Fig. 1a, Supplementary Figure S1). Clinical characteristics of the subjects are presented in Supplementary Table S2. The median follow-up was 4.2 years (interquartile range: 2.1–7.2 years). During this period, 77 subjects developed primary GN, such as D/IEN, tubular adenocarcinoma, poorly cohesive carcinomas including signet ring cell carcinoma and other subtypes, mixed adenocarcinoma, gastric adenocarcinoma with lymphoid stroma, and hepatoid adenocarcinoma and its related entities (Supplementary Table S3). The incidence rates of patients who developed GN stratified by clinical factors are shown in Fig. 2a–f. GN incidence was higher in older subjects, males, moderate or heavy drinkers, heavy smokers, and those positive for H. pylori or GN family history. The multivariable Cox model revealed that age (adjusted HR: 2.22, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.73–2.85; P < 0.001 for ten years of age, Wald test), alcohol consumption (adjusted HR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.11–4.10, P = 0.02 for heavy vs. non-drinkers), smoking (adjusted HR: 2.10, 95% CI: 1.05–4.22, P = 0.04 for light vs. non-smokers; adjusted HR: 2.95, 95% CI: 1.36–6.40, P = 0.006 for heavy vs. non-smokers, Wald test), H. pylori infection (adjusted HR: 7.23, 95% CI: 1.77–29.59, P = 0.006, Wald test) were associated independently with GN development (Table 1). The HRs of GN development remained largely unchanged in models that excluded the potential mediators (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Additionally, the adjusted HR of smokers compared with non-smokers increased as follows: former light, former heavy, current light, and current heavy smokers (Supplementary Table S6). Schoenfeld residuals were evaluated for the multivariate Cox hazard models to confirm that the proportional hazards assumption was satisfied (Supplementary Figure S3a and b).

Identification of DNA methylation epigenotypes and marker genes

We first analyzed the GN methylomes to confirm previously-reported DNA methylation epigenotypes reported in gastric tumors—specifically the EBV-CIMP, HME, or LME.14 Unsupervised two-way hierarchical clustering of GNs and non-GN non-H. pylori mucosae (Supplementary Figure S4) were performed, confirming the three methylation subtypes: EBV-CIMP, HME, and LME. As previously reported, all GN cases demonstrating positivity for EBER-ISH were classified as EBV-CIMP, and GN cases showing MSI-high or the methylation at the promoter of MLH1 were classified as HME.14,16

We identified 271 EBV markers methylated in EBV GN (EBV-CIMP), 158 HME markers methylated in EBV GN and HME, and 156 common markers methylated in all GNs but unmethylated in non-GN non-H. pylori mucosae (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Table S7, and Supplementary Materials and Methods section). We confirmed that these marker sets are suitable for the classification of epigenotypes, using public data registered in TCGA (GNs, n = 395)16,32 (Supplementary Figure S5). GO terms enriched in common markers were involved in pattern specification, regionalization, cell morphogenesis, cell fate, and stem cell development (Fig. 3b). Common markers were also enriched in genes previously reported to act as tumor suppressors, including USP6,38 NRG1,39 and MDGA240 (Supplementary Table S8).

DNA methylation analysis using GN tissues and surrounding mucosae

We investigated if the EBV, HME, and common markers are also methylated in surrounding mucosae concurrently present with the GN tissues (Supplementary Figure S6). In patients with EBV GN, EBV, HME, and common markers were highly methylated in GN tissues, but only common markers were mildly methylated in the gastric mucosae surrounding EBV GN. Notably, methylation β-values (See Supplementary Materials and Methods section) of common markers in mucosae surrounding EBV GN were higher compared to non-GN non-H. pylori mucosae (mean: 0.24 vs. 0.10, P = 3 × 10−12, Wald test) (Fig. 3c). In patients with HME, HME markers were highly methylated only in GN tissues, while common markers were highly methylated in both GN tissues and surrounding mucosae (Supplementary Figure S6). In patients with LME, common markers were highly methylated in surrounding mucosae, except in those GN cases associating with rarer subtypes, such as hepatoid adenocarcinoma and related entities and H. pylori-negative GN. These findings suggest that common markers exhibit conserved methylation in non-malignant gastric mucosae adjacent to GNs, regardless of GN epigenotype.

DNA methylation analysis using biopsied mucosal tissues

We proceeded to analyze methylation levels of the 156 common marker genes in gastric mucosal biopsies collected from subjects at their health check-up. Specifically, we compared patients with future GN to subjects with no future GN, either with or without H. pylori infection (Fig. 3d). Common markers were highly methylated in mucosal specimens from patients with future GN, with EBV and HME markers showing low methylation levels. As shown in Fig. 3e, β-values of biopsies from patients with future GN were significantly higher than subjects with H. pylori and no future GN (mean: 0.38 vs. 0.22, P = 8 × 10−9, Wald test) and also subjects with neither H. pylori nor future GN (mean: 0.38 vs. 0.10, P = 5 × 10−35, Wald test). Notably, while β-values of biopsies from subjects with H. pylori and no future GN were significantly higher than those of subjects with neither H. pylori nor future GN (mean: 0.22 vs. 0.10, P = 2 × 10−9, Wald test), we also noted a large variation in β-values in the former, possibly reflecting the influence of lifestyle and other clinicopathologic factors (See later sections).

To validate these differences, we analyzed methylation data of gastric biopsies collected for the GCEP,20 a cohort study of subjects at risk for GN in Singapore. Similar to the Japanese cohort, β-values of common markers of gastric biopsies from subjects with H. pylori who later developed early gastric neoplasia (EGN) were significantly higher than those from subjects with H. pylori who did not later develop EGN (mean: 0.45 vs. 0.32, P = 5 × 10−9, Wald test) (Fig. 3f).

To assess if differences in β-value are associated with the time to onset of GN development, we compared the correlation between β-values of biopsies from patients with future GN and the time interval until GN development (Fig. 3g). The correlation coefficient between these two variables was −0.42, indicating a negative correlation between the methylation level in gastric mucosal biopsies and the subsequent time to GN development. Taken collectively, these results suggest that DNA methylation alterations in gastric mucosae are predictive of future GN development and inversely correlated with the duration to malignancy.

Relative to subjects with H. pylori and no future GN, gastric biopsies from patients with future GN were also significantly associated with morphological abnormalities (Fig. 3d). The morphological abnormalities are all well-established risk markers for GN,29,31 and indeed, weighted Kaplan–Meier curves showed that endoscopic atrophy and pathological atrophy associated with higher GN incidence (Fig. 4a and b). No statistically significant correlation was observed between the presence of intestinal metaplasia and the incidence of GN (Fig. 4c). We then assessed relationships between DNA methylation alterations in gastric biopsies with these morphological risk factors.

Endoscopic atrophy

Mucosal biopsies from subjects with open-type atrophy, who have a high incidence of GN,27 had significantly higher β-values of common markers than those with closed-type atrophy in both patients with future GN (open vs. closed: mean β-value: 0.39 vs. 0.31, P = 0.001, Wald test) and also subjects with no future GN (open vs. closed: mean β-value: 0.25 vs. 0.16, P = 0.008, Wald test). Mucosal biopsies with no atrophy showed even lower methylation levels (mean β-value: 0.10) (Fig. 4d). Overall, the β-values of mucosal biopsies from patients with future GN were higher than subjects with no future GN, regardless if the latter had open-type (GN vs. non-GN: mean: 0.39 vs. 0.25, P = 1 × 10−5, Wald test) or closed-type atrophy (GN vs. non-GN: mean: 0.31 vs. 0.16, P = 1 × 10−7, Wald test).

Pathological atrophy

Among patients with future GN, mucosal biopsies with marked or moderate pathological atrophy had significantly higher β-values than normal mucosae (marked vs. normal: mean β-value: 0.48 vs. 0.28, P = 1 × 10−5; moderate vs. normal: 0.41 vs. 0.28, P = 6 × 10−7, Wald test). Similarly, higher β-values in marked, moderate, or mild atrophy were also observed in subjects with no future GN (marked vs. normal: mean: 0.35 vs. 0.16, P = 1 × 10−8; moderate vs. normal: 0.30 vs. 0.16, P = 4 × 10−5; mild vs. normal: 0.26 vs. 0.16, P = 0.001, Wald test) (Fig. 4e). Among the subjects with marked, moderate, or no atrophy, β-values were significantly higher in patients with future GN than subjects with no future GN: 0.48 vs. 0.35 for marked pathological atrophy (P = 0.008, Wald test), 0.41 vs. 0.30 for moderate pathological atrophy (P = 0.001, Wald test), and 0.28 vs. 0.16 for normal mucosae (P = 9 × 10−9, Wald test). These trends were replicated in the GCEP cohort, where gastric mucosae with pathological atrophy had significantly higher β-values of common markers than normal mucosae (mean β-value: 0.42 vs. 0.25, P = 2 × 10−21, Wald test) (Fig. 4e).

Intestinal metaplasia

β-values were significantly higher in mucosae with marked, moderate, or mild intestinal metaplasia than normal mucosae, both in patients with future GN (marked vs. normal: mean: 0.46 vs. 0.27, P = 9 × 10−11; moderate vs. normal: 0.40 vs. 0.27, P = 3 × 10−4; mild vs. none: 0.38 vs. 0.27, P = 9 × 10−3, Wald test) and also in subjects with no future GN (marked vs. normal: mean: 0.33 vs. 0.12, P = 6 × 10−14; moderate vs. normal: 0.31 vs. 0.12, P = 9 × 10−8; mild vs. normal: 0.30 vs. 0.12, P = 9 × 10−6, Wald test) (Fig. 4f). Among the subjects with marked, moderate, or no intestinal metaplasia, β-values were significantly higher in patients with future GN than in subjects with no future GN: 0.46 vs. 0.33 for marked intestinal metaplasia (P = 6 × 10−4, Wald test), 0.40 vs. 0.31 for moderate intestinal metaplasia (P = 0.048, Wald test) and 0.27 vs. 0.12 for normal mucosae (P = 4 × 10−17, Wald test). Similar findings were observed in the GCEP cohort, where gastric mucosae with intestinal metaplasia had significantly higher β-values of common markers than normal mucosae (mean β-value: 0.38 vs. 0.19, P = 3 × 10−50, Wald test) (Fig. 4f).

DNA methylation is an independent GN risk factor

Table 2 showed that DNA methylation was significantly associated with GN development after adjusting for morphological factors and biopsy site (adjusted OR: 2.14, 95% CI: 1.67–2.73, P < 0.001 for 0.1 increase in β-value, Wald test). Extending this analysis, DNA methylation remained significantly associated with GN development even after adjusting for morphological factors, biopsy site, age, sex, drinking, smoking, H. pylori, and family history of GN (adjusted OR: 1.96, 95% CI: 1.48–2.60, P < 0.001 for 0.1 increase in β-value, Wald test). The OR of DNA methylation associated with GN development remained relatively consistent in models that excluded a potential mediator (Supplementary Table S9). Weighted Kaplan–Meier curves exhibiting the association between high DNA methylation levels and GN incidence are shown in Fig. 4g.

Lifestyle is also significantly associated with a higher proportion of morphological abnormalities such as atrophy and intestinal metaplasia (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Figure S7a). To formally explore associations between environmental/lifestyle factors and DNA methylation alterations, we thus stratified subjects by age, H. pylori infection, alcohol consumption, and smoking habits. The mean β-values of gastric mucosae increased with age (0.16 for those ≤50 years; 0.20 for 51–60 years; 0.24 for >60 years), H. pylori status (0.10 for non-infection; 0.22 for infection), alcohol consumption (0.16 for non-drinkers; 0.19 for light drinkers; 0.23 for moderate drinkers; 0.27 for heavy drinkers), and smoking amount (0.16 for non-smokers; 0.21 for light smokers; 0.30 for heavy smokers) (Fig. 5b–e).

A multivariable linear regression model showed that heavy drinking (adjusted regression coefficient: 0.10, 95% CI: 0.02–0.17, P = 0.009, heavy vs. non-drinkers, Wald test) and heavy smoking (adjusted regression coefficient: 0.09, 95% CI: 0.002–0.18, P = 0.046, heavy vs. non-smokers, Wald test) associated with DNA methylation even after adjusting for clinical factors such as age, H. pylori status, and biopsy site (Table 3). In GCEP, we confirmed a significant association between heavy drinking and DNA methylation (adjusted regression coefficient: 0.12, 95% CI: 0.01–0.23, P = 0.04, heavy vs. non-drinkers, Wald test) (Table 3 and Supplementary Figure S7b and c). These results indicate that unfavorable lifestyles may increase GN risk by enhancing levels of DNA methylation in gastric mucosae.

Notably, for certain subjects treated to eradicate H. pylori, repeat longitudinal collection of mucosal tissues allowed us to assess relationships, in smokers, between smoking cessation and changes in DNA methylation (n = 14). Cardinal role of H. pylori environmental factor in DNA methylation

To assess the critical role of H. pylori or lifestyle factors in initiating these methylation alterations, we investigated DNA methylation changes compared to subjects with neither H. pylori nor future GN, who neither had neither heavy drinking nor smoking (Fig. 5g–i and Supplementary Figure S8a–c). Subjects with H. pylori, who neither had neither heavy drinking nor smoking showed elevation of β-values by >0.1 in 37 genes among the common markers (n = 156), while non-H. pylori drinkers showed β-value elevation in no genes and non-H. pylori smokers in 6 genes only.

We next assessed the role of lifestyle factors in accelerating DNA methylation that was initiated by H. pylori. We investigated DNA methylation changes compared to subjects with H. pylori and neither heavy drinking nor smoking (Fig. 5j–k and Supplementary Figure S8d and e). Heavy drinkers with H. pylori showed β-value elevation by >0.1 in 50 genes, and heavy smokers with H. pylori in 52 genes. These results are consistent with models where H. pylori infection is obligately required to initiate methylation changes, which can then be enhanced by lifestyle choices.

We then assessed the correlation among the methylation targets by H. pylori and lifestyle factors (Supplementary Figure S9a–c). The initial elevation in β-values due to H. pylori infection alone was not correlated with the accelerated increase in β-values due to the addition of drinking (P = 0.9, t-test) or smoking (P = 0.1, t-test), whereas a positive correlation was observed between the accelerated increase in β-values by drinking and smoking (R = 0.27, P = 7 × 10−4, t-test). To ask if drinking and smoking target similar or distinct methylation alterations, we examined overlaps among genes methylated by each factor (Supplementary Figure S9d and e). Genes with accelerated methylation by drinking and smoking significantly overlapped by 24 genes (P = 0.01, Fisher’s exact test), while genes with initiated methylation by H. pylori did not significantly overlap with genes accelerated by lifestyle (P = 0.1 for drinking, P = 0.3 for smoking, Fisher’s exact test). Gene annotation enrichment analysis showed different GO terms for gene sets exhibiting accelerated methylation by drinking and smoking, respectively (Supplementary Figure S9f). These results suggest that each lifestyle factor may target distinct gene sets and pathways, though their targets partially and significantly overlap.

Improved risk estimation of primary GN by combining environmental, lifestyle factors, and DNA methylation

Finally, we tested if DNA methylation can refine the stratification of primary GN risk beyond environmental and lifestyle factors (Fig. 6). Reassuringly, eradication status significantly stratified GN risk among subjects with H. pylori (P = 0.01, robust score test) (Fig. 6a). Notably, we observed marked differences in GN incidence when subjects with H. pylori were classified into high (average β-value of 156 common markers ≥0.25) and low methylation groups (P = 2 × 10−4, robust score test) (Fig. 6b). Similarly, DNA methylation levels also significantly stratified GN risk of subjects with high-risk lifestyles, such as drinkers, smokers, those older than 60 years, and men (Fig. 6c–f).

To assess the predictive performance of DNA methylation and clinical factors for GN development, ROC curves were generated using univariable logistic regression models (Fig. 6g). We observed that the area under the curve (AUC) value of the univariable model incorporating DNA methylation as an explanatory variable was highest. To further investigate the potential improvement in the predictive performance of GN development by incorporating DNA methylation alongside clinical factors, we generated ROC curves using multivariable logistic regression models (Fig. 6h). By adding lifestyle factors as explanatory variables to the logistic regression model, which already included H. pylori status as an explanatory variable, the AUC values improved from 0.73 to 0.81. Additionally, incorporating clinical factors (age and sex) as explanatory variables further increased the AUC value to 0.87. Moreover, when DNA methylation was included as an additional explanatory variable, the AUC increased to 0.92. These findings suggest that integrating DNA methylation data with clinical factors can enhance the discriminative performance to predict the risk of developing primary GN.

To identify the best candidate markers to stratify primary GN risk for each subgroup with high-risk lifestyle/environmental factors, we compared GN incidence according to the β-value of each common marker (n = 156), and selected three markers with the lowest P-values for each subgroup, e.g. NKX1-1 and SLC32A1. Weighted Kaplan–Meier curves showed that DNA methylation levels of these three candidate markers were strongly associated with high GN incidence among subjects at high risk of developing GN (Supplementary Figure S10a–e).

Discussion

We revealed that aberrant DNA methylation has been compared in gastric mucosae before and after GN development, in a large longitudinal cohort. The methylation levels in gastric mucosae reflect the risk for future GN development and a shorter interval to incidence, drawing parallels to recent reports of somatic mutations41,42 or copy number aberrations43 accumulating in non-neoplastic tissues. The incidence of GN correlated with increasing methylation levels, suggesting that DNA methylation may be used as a quantitative marker to stratify the GN risk. We also found that H. pylori infection is required to initiate these methylation changes, which are then accelerated by lifestyle factors. Methylation levels are significantly associated with GN risk in a general asymptomatic population and even within subgroups with high-risk factors. Collectively, our findings highlight the utility of integrating environmental, lifestyle, and epigenetic information to inform GN precision prevention.

In high-risk areas for GN, such as Japan,1 asymptomatic individuals, particularly those infected with H. pylori, often undergo regular endoscopic examinations. However, the frequency of these examinations is currently not standardized, and the decision is left to the discretion of the clinician and patient.44 This approach results in frequent endoscopic examinations even for individuals at low risk of developing primary GN, leading to increased patient burden and healthcare costs. By stratifying the risk of developing primary GN based on environmental factors, lifestyle habits, and DNA methylation levels in the gastric mucosa, it becomes possible to optimize the frequency of endoscopies for each patient. Implementing an individualized approach to endoscopic screening, tailored to an individual's risk profile, holds the potential to achieve early detection of GN, reduce patient burden, and optimize the allocation of healthcare resources.

While DNA methylation was detected in the surrounding mucosa in many cases including GN cases showing MSI-high or the methylation at the promoter of MLH1 were classified as HME, DNA methylation accumulation was not markedly observed in gastric mucosa of certain rare subtypes such as H. pylori-negative GN and some EBV GNs. These may reflect differences in the carcinogenic and epigenetic mechanisms underlying these subtypes. EBV infection induced aberrant DNA methylation within a very short period,12 and other epigenetic alterations caused by EBV infection may contribute to GN development.45, 46, 47, 48 The observation of low DNA methylation levels in the mucosa of patients with EBV GN implies that the determination of methylation levels alone might lead to an underestimation of the risk of cancer incidence for the rare GN subtypes.

Lifestyles are associated with the accumulation of DNA methylation only in subjects with H. pylori, indicating that the methylation changes require H. pylori infection for initiation, then lifestyle choices for acceleration. These suggest that subjects with H. pylori should avoid both heavy drinking and smoking to prevent from accelerating DNA methylation in the gastric mucosa and increasing their GN risk. Although a few studies have examined the relationship between lifestyles and DNA methylation in the gastric mucosa,49,50 the mechanisms by which lifestyles promote the accumulation of DNA methylation in subjects with H. pylori are not fully clarified. Considering that drinkers and smokers tended to show morphological changes in gastric mucosa, lifestyles might perhaps affect chronic inflammation that promotes methylation due to H. pylori infection.51

Current smokers tended to have a higher risk for GN development compared to past smokers, which is consistent with previous epidemiological studies.52,53 Our study also showed that increases in DNA methylation over time in subjects who quit smoking were significantly attenuated compared to continuous smokers. Methylation levels, however, remained high after smoking cessation and did not decrease to levels of non-smokers. These suggest the importance of smoking cessation for the prevention of GN development and the need for continuous surveillance even after smoking cessation, from both epidemiological and molecular abnormality perspectives.

Methylation levels accurately reflect primary GN risk. While unfavorable lifestyles accelerated the DNA methylation accumulation, they synergistically accelerated GN risk in subjects with H. pylori (Supplementary Figure S11a–c). Whereas primary GN risk increases as a result of a combination of multiple environmental factors, DNA methylation is considered a simple and useful quantitative marker to estimate GN risk. We also found that target genes methylated by each environmental factor may overlap in part; however, common markers are not necessarily methylated similarly, suggesting that the best markers for stratifying GN risk may differ for each subgroup classified by exposure to environmental factors. Indeed, candidate markers for stratifying risk of primary GN overlapped among subgroups, whereas candidate markers unique to each subgroup were also identified. These results highlight the potential for integrated GN risk estimation based on lifestyle and DNA methylation.

There are some limitations in this study. First, as mentioned in other studies, this health check-up cohort is composed mainly of NTT Group employees, and therefore has a high proportion of males.24, 25, 26 Furthermore, subjects who undergo health check-ups including endoscopy often have a higher risk of developing GN, such as those with H. pylori infection. Additionally, in this study, 1967 subjects who did not undergo follow-up endoscopy were excluded from the analysis, and these excluded populations may have included many subjects with a low risk of developing GN. For these reasons, the population analyzed in this study is at higher risk of developing GN than the general Japanese population. Second, there was variation in the length of the follow-up period for enrolled subjects, particularly for those who were enrolled later in the study, resulting in shorter follow-up periods for some individuals. The shorter follow-up period for certain subjects implies that some subjects with no GN subjects may still have the potential to develop GN in the future. This factor may lead to a misestimation of the association between DNA methylation accumulation and GN risk. Third, due to the lack of genomic analysis of germline variation in our study, we were unable to exclude patients with hereditary cancer predisposition syndromes, who develop GN through a distinct mechanism compared to patients with typical GN. However, in our study, all 21 patients with future GN who underwent methylation analysis of their biopsied mucosal samples collected at the time of health check-up had a history of H. pylori infection. Therefore, the potential impact of not excluding patients with hereditary cancer syndromes from the analysis is believed to be limited. Fourth, in our study, the biopsy sites of gastric mucosa were not uniform across all cases. We addressed this issue by adjusting for the biopsy site in our multivariate analysis, and confirmed that DNA methylation levels were associated with lifestyles and high GN risk even after adjustment for the biopsy site. Among the 21 patients with future GN, 9 patients had gastric mucosa biopsied at the same regions (upper, middle, or lower) where GN subsequently developed. However, the DNA methylation levels of the biopsied gastric mucosa at the time of the check-up in these 9 patients were not significantly different from those in the other 12 patients (Supplementary Figure S12). Based on these results, we concluded that the influence of the distance between the GN development site and the biopsy site on DNA methylation levels is limited in our study. Fifth, the sample size of this study was sufficient to identify DNA methylation as a predictive marker for GN development, but might not be large enough for risk estimation of rare GN subtypes. Sixth, we could not examine the association between the type of alcohol and DNA methylation accumulation although the type of alcohol consumed may affect DNA methylation accumulation. Seventh, the information regarding socioeconomic status, which is a potential confounding factor in the relationship between lifestyle and GN, was not gathered in this cohort. As a result, adjustment for socioeconomic status could not be carried out. Finally, considering that our cohorts are largely composed of Asians, it should be necessary to conduct further studies using larger and more diverse populations.

In summary, a significant accumulation of DNA methylation was detected in biopsied mucosa several years before GN development, reflecting a higher risk for future GN development and a shorter duration to GN incidence. The risk for primary GN can be integratively estimated through environmental, lifestyle, and DNA methylation factorization.
