Evaluation of the role of post-partum ultrasound of cesarean delivery scar on subsequent pregnancy outcomes: a preliminary study
Authors: Marina Pekar Zlotin, Ron Maymon, Nofar Ben-Basat, Michal Well, Yaakov Melcer, Eric Jauniaux
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background The aim of this study was to evaluate the association between postpartum ultrasound assessment of the cesarean scar and obstetric outcomes in subsequent pregnancies.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at a tertiary referral center between 2020 and 2022. Women who underwent postpartum transvaginal ultrasound assessment of the cesarean scar were compared with a historical reference group (2017–2019) with a previous cesarean delivery who did not undergo scar evaluation between pregnancies. Cesarean scar defects (CSD) were defined according to Delphi consensus criteria. Maternal and neonatal outcomes, including placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), uterine rupture, dehiscence and preterm birth, were compared between groups.ResultsDuring the study period, 481 women underwent postpartum ultrasound assessment of the cesarean scar. Of these, 141 women subsequently conceived and delivered at our institution and were included in the study group. These patients were compared with 393 women in a historical reference group. Among the 105 patients with complete ultrasound assessment according to the Delphi criteria, 30 (28.6%) were diagnosed with a cesarean scar defect, while 75 (71.4%) had no detectable defect. Patients in the study group were significantly older (p = 0.02) and had significantly (p < 0.001) higher gravidity and parity than those in the reference group. The study group delivered at a significantly (p < 0.001) earlier gestational age. Major obstetric complications, including placenta accreta spectrum (PAS), uterine rupture, or dehiscence, were significantly more common in the study group than in the reference group (8/131 (5.8%) vs. 7/393 (1.7%); p = 0.029). The rate of preterm birth was also significantly higher in the study group compared to the reference group (16/138 (11.5%) vs. 20/393 (4.8%); p = 0.009).ConclusionImplementation of a post-partum ultrasound follow-up program for patients delivered by cesarean section enables standardized identification of cesarean scar defects before a subsequent pregnancy. In this cohort, women with a cesarean scar defect had higher rates of obstetric complications in subsequent pregnancies than women without a detectable defect. Standardized postpartum ultrasound evaluation may support risk stratification in future pregnancies. However, the study was not designed to evaluate the impact of ultrasound findings on clinical management, and the findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations.

Introduction

The rate of cesarean delivery has increased markedly over the past decades worldwide, rising from approximately 7% in 1990 to more than 20% today. However, this increase is heterogeneous across regions, with substantially higher rates reported in high- and middle-income countries compared with low-income settings [1, 2].

This increase has led to a corresponding rise in complications during subsequent pregnancies. Recent studies have indicated that patients with a history of CD often experience long-term gynecological complications, secondary infertility, higher risk of early pregnancy failure and obstetric complications [1, 2].

The main risk factor for these complications is the development of a lower uterine segment (LUS) cesarean scar defect (CSD), also called a uterine niche or isthmocele; CSDs have been linked to abnormal uterine bleeding and pelvic pain, particularly postmenstrual spotting, as shown in a randomized controlled trial assessing hysteroscopic niche resection [3]. Obstetric complications in subsequent pregnancies include conditions such as cesarean scar ectopic pregnancy (CSEP) [4], placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) [5], and uterine rupture [6], which are often associated with high rates of perinatal maternal morbidity.

In 2019, the European Niche Taskforce proposed standardized criteria for CSD identification using a modified Delphi method. They defined a CSD as an indentation of at least 2 mm at the scar site and recommended consistent ultrasound-based evaluation protocols [2, 7]. These criteria have since been validated in prospective studies [2] and are used in clinical practice to report on the uterine anatomical remodeling following CD. Although cesarean sections are the most common major surgical procedure performed worldwide, postoperative follow-up does not include an evaluation of the uterine scar and with the routine 6-week postpartum consultation is typically limited to the assessment of the skin scar healing and breast-feeding [8]. This contrasts with other surgical specialties, where postoperative imaging is often standard practice. Given the well-established association between CSD and obstetric complications in subsequent pregnancies, early identification of impaired myometrium healing may provide the opportunity for risk stratification before the start of next pregnancy.

Recent expert consensus highlights the importance of structured early detection of postpartum complications, supporting the potential role of targeted ultrasound follow-up after CD [8, 9]. Corso et al. [8]. highlighted that while enhanced recovery protocols for elective CD emphasize early discharge, pain control, and return to function, they often lack formalized long-term assessment pathways. Within this context, the routine postpartum consultation, when combined with an ultrasound examination, could provide a valuable window for early identification and management of obstetric and gynecologic complications resulting from uterine healing abnormalities. Although cesarean scar defects have been associated with adverse gynecologic and obstetric outcomes, it remains unclear whether their identification during the postpartum period translates into improved outcomes in subsequent pregnancies [11].

The aim of our study was to evaluate the association between postpartum ultrasound assessment of the cesarean scar using the Delphi criteria and obstetric outcomes in subsequent pregnancies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort study at a specialized post-cesarean clinic at Shamir Medical Center (Tel Aviv, Israel). During the study period (2020–2022), 4,422 cesarean deliveries were performed at our institution. All women who underwent cesarean delivery received a routine recommendation in their discharge documentation to attend a dedicated post-cesarean follow-up clinic between 6 weeks and 12 months postpartum. This recommendation was applied universally and was not based on symptoms, obstetric risk factors, or physician selection. Attendance was voluntary. A total of 481 women attended the clinic and underwent a postpartum ultrasound examination of the cesarean scar. The study is reported according to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. Of these 481 women, 141 subsequently conceived and delivered at our institution and were included in the study group. The process of cohort derivation is summarized in Fig. 3 The ultrasound assessment was not performed based on gynecological symptoms such as abnormal uterine bleeding or pelvic pain. Of these, 141 had a subsequent pregnancy and delivery and were included in the study group. The reference group included 393 patients who had a subsequent pregnancy and delivery after a CD, between 2017 and 2019, and did not have a post-partum evaluation of their CD scar.

Inclusion criteria were patients who had a postpartum transvaginal scan (TVS) examination of their Lower Uterine Segment after a CD, and who had a subsequent pregnancy and delivery. Exclusion criteria included unknown pregnancy outcomes, early or late miscarriage, or subsequent delivery at another medical center.

Maternal and neonatal outcomes were systematically assessed. Neonatal outcomes included birthweight, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min, and umbilical cord blood gas values. Maternal obstetric outcomes included uterine rupture during trial of labor, uterine dehiscence, gestational age at delivery, and intraoperative suspicion of placenta accreta spectrum (PAS). PAS was diagnosed based on prenatal ultrasound findings suggestive of abnormal placental invasion together with intraoperative findings, including difficult placental separation and/or abnormal placental adherence. Histopathological confirmation was not routinely available.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical committee approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shamir Medical Center, Tel Aviv, Israel (Approval number: ASF-36-24), prior to the initiation of the study. The IRB waived retrospective patient consent, as all medical records were reviewed within the same institution and all data were fully anonymized prior to analysis.

Ultrasound assessment

All patients in the study group had a TVS examination using Voluson E8 system (GE Healthcare, Zipf, Austria), by an experienced operator between 6 weeks and 12 months after CD. When a CSD was identified, we used the Delphi criteria7 including length (the largest length and the length at the CSD base); depth (deepest point of both the main CSD and any branches); RMT (at the thinnest point measured perpendicular to the serosa, excluding fibrosis), adjacent myometrial thickness (AMT), near the thickest area of myometrium close to the CSD; the distance between the CSD and vesicovaginal (VV) fold (from the top of the main CSD to the VV fold); and distance between the CSD and external os, measured parallel to the cervical canal from the most distal point of the CSD to the external os.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL). Descriptive parameters are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Univariate analysis was performed using Student’s t-tests for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to assess associations between the study group and major obstetric complications. The low number of patients with major obstetric complications in the study group (n = 8), did not allow for the construction of a statistically stable logistic regression model required for a multivariable analysis. Analyses were performed using available-case data. Therefore, denominators may vary across variables and outcomes according to data availability.

Results

Patient characteristics

During the study period, 4,422 cesarean deliveries were performed at our institution. Of these, 481 women attended the dedicated post-cesarean clinic and underwent postpartum ultrasound assessment. Among them, 141 subsequently conceived and delivered at our institution and were included in the study group. The derivation of both study cohorts is summarized in Fig. 3. Unless otherwise specified, analyses were performed using available-case data and denominators vary according to data availability for individual variables Among the 452 women with available data, the median interval between cesarean delivery and postpartum ultrasound assessment was 5.0 months (IQR 4.0–6.5 months). Fifty-six women (12.4%) underwent ultrasound assessment more than 12 months after cesarean delivery. Among the 134 patients in the study group with available follow-up, the majority (n = 105) had undergone one previous CD. Fifteen had two prior CDs, ten had three, two had four, and two had five. One additional patient had a prior cesarean combined with a myomectomy. No cases in either group required a peripartum hysterectomy.

Clinical and sonographic characteristics of patients with major obstetric complications in the study group

CD scar assessment data were available for 134 patients in the study group. Among them, 105 had complete ultrasound measurements allowing evaluation according to the Delphi criteria. Of these, 30 (28.6%) were diagnosed with a cesarean scar defect (CSD), while 75 (71.4%) had no CSD. The corresponding obstetric complication rates were 13.3% and 4%, respectively.

CSD ultrasound morphology

Among the 30 patients diagnosed with a CSD, complete sonographic measurements were available for 26. The mean maternal age in this subgroup was 29.3 years (range: 21–39). The average total uterine length was 74.9 mm (SD ± 10.5; range: 56–87.5 mm). The mean CSD depth was 3.6 mm (SD ± 1.5; range: 2–5.3 mm), and the CSD base width averaged 6.0 mm (SD ± 1.9; range: 4–10 mm). Residual myometrial thickness (RMT), measured at the thinnest point above the CSD, had a mean of 5.3 mm (SD ± 2.4; range: 2.0–8.8 mm). The average distance from the fundus to the CSD was 34.5 mm (range: 18–51 mm), and the distance from the CSD to the external cervical os was 40.1 mm (range: 20–57 mm). Regarding uterine position, the majority (73%) had an anterior uterus, while 27% had a posterior uterus. Adhesions, as reflected by a negative sliding sign, were observed in 7 of the 26 cases (26.9%).

The historical reference group included 393 patients with a subsequent pregnancy and delivery between 2017 and 2019, without scar evaluation. In this group, obstetric complications occurred in eight patients (2.04%). Patients in the study group were significantly (p = 0.02) older at the time of delivery and had higher gravidity and parity (both p < 0.001). They delivered at an earlier gestational age (38.37 vs. 39.02 weeks, p < 0.001) and had lower mean birthweight (p = 0.04). The incidence of preterm birth was notably higher in the study group (11.5% vs. 4.8%, p = 0.009), with a corresponding odds ratio of 2.45 (95% CI: 1.23–4.87; p = 0.0089), indicating a significantly increased risk. Umbilical cord arterial pH < 7.1 was less frequent in the study group (6.56% vs. 19.44%, p = 0.0013), while no significant differences were observed in 1- or 5-minute Apgar scores. Discussion

As far as we are aware, our study is the first to have evaluated whether ultrasound evaluation of LUS scar healing process at the time of the routine postpartum consultation could improve obstetric outcomes in subsequent pregnancies. We found that postnatal identification of a CSD was associated with a higher rate of obstetric complications and preterm birth rates; however, there was no difference in the overall obstetric outcome between the study group and historical reference group. Interestingly, the rate of umbilical cord arterial pH < 7.1 was lower in the study group despite higher rates of obstetric complications. This finding should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively small number of events and may reflect differences in clinical management, timing of delivery, or residual confounding between the cohorts.

The implementation of the Delphi-based sonographic CSD assessment has provided a standardized, reproducible framework for evaluating CSD dimensions, such as residual myometrial thickness (RMT), adjacent myometrial thickness (AMT), and CSD depth parameters, which have been shown to correlate with both gynecologic and obstetric risks [7]. Feldman et al. demonstrated that thin RMT and deep CSD are significantly associated with abnormal uterine bleeding and poorer healing profiles [2].

Overall, we found that the post-partum detection of a CSD did not reduce obstetric morbidity in our population. Our findings are consistent with those summarized in a recent systematic review, which reported increased obstetric risks in women with CSD and stressed the urgent need for prospective cohort studies using uniform assessment protocols [11]. The observed differences in obstetric outcomes between the study and reference groups may also reflect baseline differences between the cohorts and temporal changes in clinical practice, rather than an effect of postpartum scar evaluation itself. The composite obstetric complication outcome included PAS, uterine rupture, and uterine dehiscence. Because The number of events for each individual complication was small and the individual complication analyses did not reach statistical significance, the composite outcome should be interpreted cautiously. These complications differ in their pathophysiology and clinical presentation, and the observed association may not apply equally to each individual outcome. The absence of an apparent benefit associated with postpartum scar evaluation in our cohort may be related to the high level of care available in our institution, including 24/7 access to surgical teams with expertise in managing complex cesarean deliveries. Our data agree with those of Timor-Tritsch et al., who showed that early detection of CSD-related complications, such as CSEP and early PAS, is possible. However, intervention strategies remain controversial, and outcomes often depend on access to emergency delivery by expert surgical teams and timing [12]. It was recently highlighted that in patients with a history of multiple CDs, ultrasound imaging can play a key role not only in identifying patients with a high probability of PAS at birth but also in preoperative assessment of the surgical risks associated with the remodeling of the LUS and the need for a specialist multidisciplinary team (MDT).

Overall, these findings highlight the role of integrating CSD morphology, dimensions, and clinical context when evaluating whether and how to intervene, as well as the level of antenatal and perinatal care that may be required during subsequent pregnancies [13]. Ivan et al. have recently shown that early postnatal sonographic evaluation of uterine scar healing, particularly the niche shape and RMT, is significantly associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes such as PAS and preterm delivery [14].

Beyond the diagnostic value shown in our cohort data, the routine identification and characterization of CSDs may have broader implications for health systems with more limited resources or settings without access to MDT and surgeons experienced in complex CD. In such environments, where emergent management of complications such as uterine rupture or PAS may be less feasible, postpartum or early pregnancy detection of high-risk scar morphologies, including deep CSDs with markedly reduced RMT (< 1 mm), could become a practical, low-cost triage tool. Establishing a structured follow-up protocol using standardized sonographic criteria may enable clinicians to stratify patients by long-term risk, optimize counseling, and timing of subsequent delivery. This low-cost strategy may also be beneficial in lower-resource settings for identifying women who would benefit from referral to specialized centers or enhanced surveillance in future pregnancies.

There are currently no epidemiologic data on the link between the morphology of a CD scar in the postpartum period and the subsequent risk of obstetric and gynecologic complications. Detection of a cesarean scar defect may have potential clinical implications even in the absence of a standardized intervention pathway. Identification of a significant defect may prompt counseling regarding future pregnancy risks, recommendation for early ultrasound assessment in a subsequent pregnancy to exclude cesarean scar pregnancy, closer antenatal surveillance in selected cases, or referral to specialized centers when severe scar abnormalities are identified. However, no standardized management protocol based on postpartum ultrasound findings was implemented during the study period, and our study was not designed to evaluate whether such interventions influence subsequent pregnancy outcomes.We believe that incorporating ultrasound imaging into routine postpartum CD follow-up will offer an opportunity to identify patients at increased risk for obstetric complications in subsequent pregnancy and will give us a better understanding of the scar healing process in individual patients. Recent international expert consensus further supports structured early detection of postpartum complications, reinforcing the potential value of postpartum uterine scar evaluation as part of comprehensive postpartum care for patients delivered by cesarean Sects. [8, 9].

The potential benefits of CSD repair, particularly in symptomatic women or those with thin RMT, have only been shown in small cohorts. In a study by Goldenberg et al., 60% of patients who underwent laparoscopic CSD repair conceived within 6 months, and none experienced rupture or PAS in subsequent pregnancy, suggesting that surgical repair could restore uterine integrity and improve outcome in subsequent pregnancies [15]. However, surgical corrections may be associated with complications such as hemorrhage, adhesion formation, and may potentially increase the risk of rupture dehiscence in subsequent pregnancies, as the repair often requires removing a large part of the LUS [12, 16, 17], further increasing the risk of LUS.

The main strength of our study is in its original design for the follow-up of patients delivered by cesarean section beyond the routine evaluation of the external skin incision scar. Including a simple TVS of the CD scar in the 6-week post-partum visit allows for detailed evaluation of the uterine scarification process and targeted counseling for patients presenting with a CSD.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design may have introduced selection bias due to differences in baseline characteristics between the study and reference group, including maternal age, gravidity, parity and number of previous cesarean deliverieswhich are known risk factors for placenta accreta spectrum, uterine rupture, dehiscence, and other adverse obstetric outcomes. Second, we were unable to stratify outcomes according to specific CSD size (e.g., RMT < 2 mm vs. > 2 mm), which may limit interpretation of obstetric risk according to CSD morphology. In addition, women with early or late miscarriage were excluded from the analysis. As cesarean scar defects have been associated with early pregnancy failure, this exclusion may have introduced selection bias and could have led to underestimation of the full reproductive impact of cesarean scar defects. The diagnosis of PAS was based on prenatal ultrasound findings and intraoperative clinical assessment without histopathological confirmation, which may limit diagnostic accuracy. In addition, the wide time interval between cesarean delivery and ultrasound assessment (6 weeks to 12 months postpartum) may have introduced assessment bias, as cesarean scar morphology can change over time due to progressive healing and myometrial remodeling. Due to the limited number of major obstetric complications in the study group, multivariable adjustment for potential confounders was not statistically feasible and the findings should therefore be interpreted primarily as descriptive associations. Finally, patient recruitment began during the COVID-19 pandemic, and changes in clinical practice during this period may have influenced gestational age at delivery. Importantly, our study did not evaluate whether ultrasound findings influenced clinical decision-making during subsequent pregnancies; In addition, we did not evaluate whether postpartum ultrasound findings influenced reproductive decision-making before a subsequent pregnancy. Ultrasound assessment may have affected decisions regarding future conception, contraception, sterilization, or mode of delivery planning, potentially influencing which patients subsequently conceived and were included in the study cohort. Therefore, the results should not be interpreted as evidence of the clinical effectiveness of postpartum ultrasound screening.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort study, postpartum ultrasound detection of cesarean scar defects was associated with higher rates of obstetric complications in subsequent pregnancies. Standardized ultrasound evaluation based on the Delphi criteria enables postpartum identification of CSDs and may support risk stratification and patient triage in future pregnancies. However, the study was not designed to evaluate whether ultrasound findings influenced clinical management, and the relatively small number of adverse events limits the strength of the conclusions. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations. Further multicenter studies using standardized transvaginal ultrasound protocols are required to better define the relationship between cesarean scar characteristics—including location, morphology, and size—and subsequent obstetric and gynecologic outcomes.
