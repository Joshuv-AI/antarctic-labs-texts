Perioperative sleep disturbances in total knee arthroplasty: incidence, mechanisms, and management strategies
Authors: Zhengming Zhu, Zheng Zhu, Chen Peng, Kehao Wang, Run Fang, Lingchao Kong, Rende Ning
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTotal knee arthroplasty (TKA) is an effective treatment for end-stage knee osteoarthritis (KOA). Previous studies have found that KOA patients commonly experience sleep disorders during the perioperative period of TKA, which can significantly affect postoperative recovery, pain management, and quality of life. Sleep disturbances during the perioperative period of TKA are a complex issue influenced by multiple factors. Although clinical interventions have shown promising efficacy, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and require further research. This article reviews the specific causes of sleep disturbances in TKA patients during the perioperative period, aiming to provide a reference for personalized sleep regulation interventions to improve postoperative outcomes. The article begins by searching public databases using selected keywords to identify recent studies on the management of sleep dysfunction during the perioperative period of TKA. Through a summary of the relevant literature, the review focuses on the incidence, influencing factors, interventions, and potential mechanisms of sleep dysfunction during the perioperative period of TKA. It aims to optimize perioperative management and support the future development of high-quality clinical intervention randomized controlled trials, ultimately contributing to the optimization of postoperative sleep management strategies and enhancing rapid recovery and long-term satisfaction in TKA patients.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis, Total knee arthroplasty, Sleep disturbances, Perioperative management, Pain

Introduction

With the increasing aging population, degenerative joint diseases have become a major focus of clinical research. The knee joint, as one of the most important joints in the human body, must bear immense pressure while providing flexibility, making it a common site for osteoarthritis (OA) [1, 2]. Once knee osteoarthritis (KOA) occurs, it can cause significant pain, economic burden, and severely impact the patient’s quality of life [3, 4]. Therefore, improving and treating KOA has become a key area of current clinical research. Clinically, KOA is mainly treated with conservative or surgical methods. Conservative treatment is primarily employed in the initial phase of KOA, including modalities such as shortwave therapy [5], oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [6], chondroitin sulfate or amino polysaccharide medications [7], as well as intra-articular injections of corticosteroids or hyaluronic acid to alleviate synovial inflammation [8]. In terms of surgical treatment, arthroscopic repair can be used for early cartilage damage, and arthroscopic microfracture can be used for treatment if the damage involves subchondral bone [9]. In cases of severe joint deformity and significantly restricted range of motion, total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is required as the final treatment [10]. In recent years, with advancements in surgical techniques, improved implant designs, and the ongoing application of rehabilitation concepts and multimodal pain management, patient satisfaction with TKA has steadily increased. However, a considerable number of patients still experience anxiety and tension during the entire treatment process, which seriously affects the treatment effect. Through the in-depth development of clinical follow-up work, a previously neglected factor—sleep status has become the focus of clinical attention, that is, the above patients often have severe sleep dysfunction, which may be an important factor affecting the therapeutic effect.

Sleep is a vital physiological process for maintaining physical and mental health. A normal "sleep–wake" cycle periodically regulates body activity and food intake while also participating in the regulation of normal physiological functions such as body temperature, heart rate, muscle tone, and hormone secretion [11]. Sleep is essential for promoting memory formation, toxin clearance, muscle repair, growth, and immune function [12, 13]. However, clinical follow-up studies on KOA patients have found that most of them suffer from varying degrees of sleep disturbances [14, 15]. Specifically, these issues manifest as reduced sleep onset time, shorter sleep duration, and decreased sleep quality, leading to disruptions in the "sleep–wake cycle," failing to meet the body’s physiological sleep needs. Chronic sleep dysfunction can severely impact the physical and mental health of patients, leading to anxiety, depression, pain sensitization, paranoia, hallucinations, and interpersonal relationship issues [16, 17]. In severe cases, it can also lead to a decline in immune function, exacerbation of inflammatory responses, and poor recovery after TKA, as well as the occurrence of related complications [18]. Some studies also suggest that sleep dysfunction throughout the treatment process can worsen pain symptoms, and uncontrolled pain can, in turn, exacerbate sleep dysfunction, thereby significantly affecting treatment outcomes [19, 20].

In view of this, we focus on TKA patients as the study population, with an emphasis on analyzing research related to sleep dysfunction during the perioperative period. This review aims to summarize the factors influencing sleep dysfunction, intervention measures, and underlying mechanisms to optimize perioperative management, improve and enhance treatment outcomes for KOA patients, reduce the incidence of perioperative sleep dysfunction in TKA, and ultimately provide valuable insights for improving clinical treatment satisfaction.

Epidemiology and duration

It is estimated that prior to undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA), 31% of patients experience difficulty falling asleep, 18% report poor sleep quality, and approximately 81% experience at least one episode of difficulty maintaining sleep per week [21, 22]. Postoperatively, more than 25% of patients sleep for less than 6 h per night, and 67% report that their sleep quality either remains the same or worsens compared to preoperative levels [23–26]. Several observational studies on sleep changes after TKA indicate that sleep dysfunction is most severe during the first 6 weeks after surgery, gradually improving within 6 months to 1 year but often not fully returning to normal levels. Some patients still experience moderate to severe sleep dysfunction one year postoperatively [19, 24, 26, 27].

Causes of sleep disorders

Perioperative sleep quality is the result of multiple factors. In addition to pre-existing sleep disorders, it is also closely related to secondary factors (such as pain or drug effects) and related factors such as anesthesia and surgical stress. Given the high incidence of perioperative sleep disorders in patients undergoing total knee arthroplasty (TKA), accurate identification of the above risk factors can help surgeons guide patients to perform postoperative rehabilitation in a more targeted manner, and improve their short-term recovery thru early intervention.

Primary sleep disorders

Primary sleep disorders refer to independent sleep abnormalities not induced by other medical, psychiatric, or environmental factors, typically caused by physiological or neurological mechanisms. Common sleep disorders in the elderly include insomnia, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and restless leg syndrome (RLS). Most KOA patients are middle-aged or elderly, and due to physiological aging, they are often affected by various sleep disorders during the perioperative period of TKA. These sleep disorders can either occur independently or in combination, forming a complex pattern that exacerbates the sleep issues throughout the treatment process.

InsomniaInsomnia is diagnosed when a person experiences dissatisfaction with sleep quality, difficulty falling asleep, frequent awakenings, and increased nighttime awakenings. It is the most common manifestation of sleep disorders. Studies have shown that patients with long-term insomnia symptoms often experience exacerbation of these symptoms after undergoing TKA, with longer-lasting sleep disturbances [28]. These may be related to postoperative pain, medication interference, and the anxiety or stress associated with surgery. Changes in the sleep environment and the lack of an independent sleep setting during hospitalization can also worsen insomnia symptoms [21, 29].

Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)OSA occurs due to repeated collapse of the airway during sleep, leading to hypoxemia, sleep fragmentation, and daytime sleepiness. The prevalence of OSA is higher in men, the elderly, and obese individuals [30]. Studies have found that the incidence of OSA in TKA patients is as high as 70% [31]. The chronic hypoxic state caused by OSA may increase pain sensitivity and exacerbate postoperative pain, making it more difficult for patients to fall asleep [32]. Additionally, the use of opioids in multimodal pain management may cause respiratory suppression and reduce upper airway muscle tone, leading to more severe apneas and hypoxemia, and thus further impairing sleep quality in OSA patients [32, 33].

The prevalence of RLS in the elderly is between 9 and 20% [34]. RLS often worsens at night, severely affecting sleep quality. For TKA patients with RLS, the uncontrollable urge to move their legs not only aggravates the severity of sleep disturbances but also significantly affects early postoperative recovery, contributing to complications such as increased wound bleeding [35, 36].

Historically, due to a lack of understanding of the underlying causes of primary sleep disorders in KOA patients, perioperative management during TKA has lacked effective screening and intervention for these conditions, potentially leading to decreased clinical treatment satisfaction in patients with these comorbidities. The coexistence of multiple primary sleep disorders, such as insomnia, OSA, and RLS, further complicates postoperative recovery. Thus, it is crucial to prioritize preoperative diagnosis and clinical intervention for sleep disorders in perioperative TKA management.

Secondary sleep disorders

Research has shown that decreased sleep quality and efficiency are associated with increased pain sensitivity [37]. In addition to primary sleep disorders, chronic pain from KOA is a significant contributor to sleep dysfunction [38]. Early-stage KOA pain is typically intermittent, worsening with weight-bearing activities and alleviating with rest. As the condition progresses, late-stage KOA patients often experience rest pain, often described as "night pain"[22]. Moreover, excessive negative cognitive processing of pain—pain catastrophizing—is also closely linked to decreased sleep quality during the perioperative period of TKA. Research shows that in the early postoperative period, KOA patients with heightened anxiety about pain have a higher incidence of sleep disorders [39, 40]. Furthermore, KOA patients with insomnia demonstrate more features of central sensitization in quantitative sensory testing (QST), suggesting that sleep dysfunction in KOA patients may be induced by pain perception enhancement through central sensitization mechanisms [41]. A prospective study on KOA patients with insomnia showed that improvement in sleep quality also alleviated knee pain symptoms and effectively reduced interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) levels [42]. Previous studies have found that IL-6 and TNF-α are closely related to the progression of KOA [43, 44]. These findings suggest that sufficient sleep may effectively slow the progression of KOA and provide a new therapeutic approach for its treatment.

Anesthesia

During TKA surgery, patients undergo anesthesia. General anesthesia typically has a strong central sedative effect, which may suppress rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and negatively impact postoperative sleep recovery [23, 45, 46]. Research has shown that general anesthesia is highly correlated with worsened postoperative apnea and hypoventilation indices in OSA patients [47]. General anesthetics, such as propofol and sevoflurane, may suppress upper airway muscle activity, leading to airway collapse and exacerbating apnea and hypoxemia, which in turn severely affects sleep quality. For KOA patients with preexisting OSA, general anesthesia not only significantly reduces postoperative sleep quality but also worsens postoperative hypoxemia and increases the frequency of nighttime awakenings. In contrast, spinal or epidural anesthesia can avoid airway intubation and reduce the sedative effects of anesthetics on the central nervous system, potentially helping to mitigate REM sleep suppression and improve sleep quality. For KOA patients with preexisting sleep disorders, using spinal or epidural anesthesia during TKA may help reduce the impact of anesthesia on sleep function.

Surgical stress

TKA surgery is a significant procedure that involves not only soft tissue dissection but also bone resection and ligament release to achieve proper knee alignment and movement. Therefore, attention must be paid to the inflammation response triggered by surgical trauma. The release of inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6 and C-reactive protein (CRP), may exacerbate sleep disturbances [48–50]. Previous studies have shown that sleep disruption increases IL-6 levels [51], and IL-6 is associated with peripheral nociceptor sensitization as well as central/spinal sensitization during glial cell activation [52], which contributes to increased pain severity and sensitivity. These studies suggest that inflammatory mediators may regulate pain sensitivity in KOA patients, and the inflammatory "storm" triggered by surgical stress could further enhance pain sensitivity, severely impairing postoperative sleep quality. Decreased sleep quality can then amplify pain perception, as sleep deprivation reduces endogenous pain inhibition and increases pain sensitivity [37]. This process creates a "sleep deprivation—pain sensitization" vicious cycle. This mechanism may be a key factor in the long-term sleep dysfunction and persistent pain experienced by some TKA patients after surgery, and interrupting this cycle presents a new approach to perioperative TKA management.

Sleep quality monitoring and intervention during the perioperative period of TKA

Improving sleep quality can help alleviate pain, promote immune function recovery, enhance tissue repair, reduce anxiety and depression, minimize drug dependence, and decrease side effects. Therefore, actively monitoring sleep quality during the perioperative period of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) and providing treatment for patients with sleep disorders is crucial for promoting postoperative recovery and improving quality of life.

Sleep quality monitoring methods

Objective monitoring

Objective sleep data, such as polysomnography (PSG), is the gold standard for monitoring the "sleep–wake" activity [53]. PSG allows clinicians to assess sleep stages, breathing events, and other sleep phenomena by measuring electroencephalogram (EEG), electromyogram (EMG), blood oxygen saturation, airflow sensors, and other sensors. However, the use of multiple instruments increases the difficulty of data collection and comprehensive judgment [16]. Recently developed portable wearable devices show high sensitivity for sleep monitoring, though their accuracy in detecting wakefulness is still not on par with polysomnography [16, 54]. With the advent of AI-driven digital technologies, high-precision, highly sensitive sleep monitoring devices will facilitate and popularize sleep monitoring practices.

Subjective monitoring

Subjective sleep quality monitoring is typically performed using questionnaires, which have been widely applied in studies on KOA patients during the perioperative period of TKA. These include: (1) Epworth Sleepiness Scale: This scale includes 8 questions designed to assess daytime sleepiness [55]. (2) Insomnia Severity Index (ISI): Comprising 7 questions, this tool mainly assesses the patient’s dissatisfaction with and concerns about their current sleep patterns [56]. (3) Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI): A more complex scale with 19 items, it evaluates subjective sleep quality, sleep onset time, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, the use of sleep aids, and daytime dysfunction [57]. (4) Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS): This system focuses on two sleep-related domains: sleep impairment, which measures alertness, drowsiness, fatigue, and sleep-related dysfunction during wakefulness; and Sleep Disturbance, which assesses sleep quality, depth, and restorative effects [58].

Subjective understanding and feelings can influence the accuracy of these scales, despite providing quantifiable data on the patient’s sleep condition. In some cases, they may not align with objective monitoring data, but their flexibility and simplicity provide effective data support for clinical sleep quality evaluation.

Interventions for sleep treatment

Pharmacological treatment

Sedative-hypnotic drugsSedative-hypnotic medications are commonly used to treat anxiety, insomnia, and other sleep disorders and can significantly improve insomnia symptoms. Benzodiazepines enhance the inhibitory effect of gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the brain to produce sedative effects [59] and may cause respiratory depression when combined with opioids. Long-term use can also lead to drug dependence and tolerance [60, 61]. Non-benzodiazepine drugs, such as zolpidem, are effective in reducing nighttime awakenings and improving fatigue, but they carry the risk of hallucinations [62, 63]. Although these drugs can improve insomnia to some extent, studies have indicated that they increase the incidence of postoperative complications such as falls and periprosthetic fractures in TKA patients. Therefore, medication use should be adjusted according to the patient’s condition.

Antidepressants and AntihistaminesAntidepressants, such as trazodone, are used in treating insomnia and depression, but their clinical use has been limited to non-surgical patients and has not been widely applied in TKA patients. Antihistamines, such as diphenhydramine, which are used for sedation and anxiety relief, have been employed to treat insomnia during the perioperative period. However, studies suggest that they may increase the risk of postoperative falls [64]. The effects and potential complications of these drugs in the perioperative period of TKA require further large-scale clinical studies and long-term follow-up.

MelatoninMelatonin is commonly used to regulate circadian rhythms and improve sleep quality in patients with insomnia [65]. While melatonin does not produce side effects like hallucinations, cognitive impairment, or delirium, its application in KOA patients remains controversial. A randomized controlled trial of 140 TKA patients found that melatonin supplementation did not significantly improve subjective sleep quality (PSQI) during the first 6 weeks post-surgery [66, 67], suggesting its limited efficacy in improving sleep quality after TKA.

Non-pharmacological interventions

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I)CBT-I has been shown in multiple studies to be highly effective in improving insomnia symptoms [68]. The core idea of CBT-I is to alter patients’ cognitive and behavioral patterns related to sleep, breaking the vicious cycle of sleep disturbance and improving sleep quality. However, CBT-I typically requires about 8 weeks to show results [69]. With the increasing application of accelerated rehabilitation concepts during the perioperative period of TKA, patients may not be able to complete CBT-I within this time frame, limiting its clinical use.

Light therapyTKA patients may experience circadian rhythm disturbances due to pain, anesthetic drugs, or changes in the hospital environment. Light therapy can help restore the normal sleep–wake cycle by exposing patients to bright light, mimicking daytime conditions, and improving sleep onset time and quality. Additionally, light therapy stimulates melatonin secretion, alleviating postoperative anxiety and improving sleep quality [70].

AcupunctureAcupuncture involves inserting fine needles into specific body points, while moxibustion uses heat to stimulate acupuncture points. Both have been shown to be effective in treating sleep disorders [72, 73]. However, the invasive nature of acupuncture may limit patient acceptance in clinical settings. To reduce anxiety, minimize invasiveness, and improve efficacy, auricular point acupressure has been explored as a less invasive alternative [74]. The ear is considered a reflex zone of the body, and stimulating specific ear points can regulate the autonomic nervous system, endocrine system, and immune system. Clinical trials have shown that ear acupressure enhances GABAergic activity, reduces anxiety, regulates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, decreases cortisol levels, and promotes parasympathetic nervous system activity, which helps patients fall asleep faster and reduces night awakenings [75–77]. However, further high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to fully elucidate the underlying physiological mechanisms.

Conclusion

With more and more KOA patients receiving TKA treatment, the potential link between sleep and rapid recovery during the perioperative period has attracted widespread attention. However, the complexity of sleep problems is affected by a variety of factors. Although there are some treatment methods in clinical practice, each method has certain limitations. At present, there is a lack of a safe and effective way to solve the sleep disorders during the perioperative period of TKA. In addition, the effects of these interventions on long-term sleep quality and functional recovery have not been fully clarified, and further verification is still needed through multicenter, large-sample prospective studies in the future.

However, it is undeniable that more and more clinicians are aware of the impact of perioperative sleep on the recovery of TKA patients. We believe that with the in-depth development of clinical research, such as carrying out psychological education to enhance patients’ medical compliance and psychological resilience, targeted assessment, and personalized sleep intervention, it will help reduce the incidence of sleep dysfunction during the perioperative period of TKA so as to provide more powerful support for improving the speed of postoperative rehabilitation and treatment satisfaction of patients (Fig. 1).
