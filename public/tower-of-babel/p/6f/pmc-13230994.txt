Optimizing conductive thermoset composites for bipolar plates using statistical mixture design
Authors: Salah M S Al-Mufti, S J A Rizvi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA key challenge in developing composite bipolar plates (BPs) lies in optimizing the composition and synergy of filler and matrix materials. Random filler ratios often lead to suboptimal properties, limiting performance. In this study, we address this gap by employing an I-optimal mixture design, incorporating ANOVA analysis, to systematically optimize 21 formulations. This approach enabled the development of a low-cost, high-performance thermoset composite using novolac type phenol formaldehyde resin (NPFR) reinforced with expanded graphite (EG), carbon black (CB), graphite (G), and short carbon fibers (sCF). Model analysis (R2 = 0.97) revealed synergistic EG/sCF interactions enhancing conductivity, while CB/G disrupted networks. The combination of NPFR and sCF improved flexural strength, while higher G content may have reduced structural cohesion, despite the low R2 value (0.54). Increasing NPFR and CB decreased water absorption (WA) by reducing hydrophilicity. Antagonistic EG–G, EG–sCF, and G–sCF interactions further suppressed WA, as validated by quadratic modeling (R2 = 0.99). The optimum composite, fabricated via compression molding, exhibited in-plane conductivity of 69.1 S/cm, flexural strength of 29.6 MPa, and water absorption of 1.2%. The results reveal inherent trade-offs between electrical conductivity, mechanical integrity, and durability under high conductive filler loading. Moreover, this study provides a fundamental basis for understanding filler interactions, enabling the rational design of composite bipolar plates for PEM fuel cell applications.Keywords: Fuel cell, Bipolar plate, Conductive polymer composites, Expanded graphite, I-optimal mixture approachSubject terms: Engineering, Chemical engineering, Electrical and electronic engineering, Energy infrastructure, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) are crucial for sustainable energy, offering high power density, fast response, and zero-emission operation1–3. A key component in PEMFCs is the bipolar plate (BP), which governs electrical conduction, fluid distribution, and thermal management4–8. BP materials must meet strict DOE standards, including high electrical conductivity, mechanical strength, corrosion resistance, and low-cost manufacturability (see Table 1). Traditional graphite BPs offer good conductivity but are brittle and costly. Metallic alternatives are durable but prone to corrosion9–11. Composite BPs, combining conductive fillers and polymers, are lightweight and corrosion-resistant, but balancing properties to meet DOE targets remains challenging12–14.

The performance of composite BPs strongly depends on the type, distribution, and proportion of conductive fillers and polymer matrices15. The most common fillers used in composite BPs are carbon-based materials, such as graphite (G), carbon black (CB), expanded graphite (EG), graphene, carbon nanotubes (CNTs), and carbon fibers (CF), among others16. Composite bipolar plates typically incorporate two main types of polymer matrices: thermosetting and thermoplastic. Thermosetting polymers, such as phenolic resin (PF) and epoxy resin (EP), offer excellent thermal stability and mechanical strength. On the other hand, thermoplastic polymers like polypropylene (PP), polyethylene (PE), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), and polystyrene (PS) are favored for their ease of processing and recyclability17. Suboptimal filler ratios can disrupt conductivity and mechanical stability, often due to poor percolation or agglomeration. Excessive filler improves conductivity but weakens strength, while poor nanofiller dispersion creates voids, degrading gas barrier properties18,19. Various studies have investigated nanoparticles and fiber reinforcements to improve mechanical and thermal properties of polymer composites in different manufacturing processes and environments20–22.

Numerous studies have explored the impact of single and multiple fillers on composite BPs, yet critical limitations remain1,2,23–32. Hanapi et al.33 fabricated chopped CF-reinforced EP/G BPs using one-step compression molding (CM). Optimizing EG and CCF content achieved good conductivity (22.7 S/cm) and low porosity (9.84%). However, the obtained conductivity remains below DOE targets for PBs. Yan et al.34 developed EP/G composite BPs enhanced with 1 wt% nano-copper, increasing conductivity by 75.2%. The composite met DOE 2025 targets but had a 26.84% drop in flexural strength. However, nano copper makes it costly and impractical for commercial use. Chen et al.35 enhanced composite BPs by adding fumed silica, improving conductivity and flexural strength. However, its water absorption could cause swelling if not controlled. According to a study36, short CF–reinforced PP/EP composite BPs were prepared via melt mixing and CM. Selective CF localization enabled conductivity up to 16.7 S/cm at 70 wt%, though still below DOE targets. Radzuan et al.37 developed CF/EP composites using CM. Conductivity was negligible below 80 wt% CF due to insufficient conductive networks, but at 80 wt%, through-plane and in-plane conductivity reached 6.34 and 4.26 S/cm, respectively. CB and CNT were introduced to boost conductivity further.

To meet DOE targets, the effects of conductive filler type, their interactions, and composite component ratios within the polymer matrix must be systematically studied. The mixture design (MD) approach offers a structured method to optimize filler compositions by systematically varying proportions and assessing their effects on key properties. MD is a statistical approach that can be used to optimize the composition of materials with multiple components. Introduced in the early 1930s by agronomists38 and further developed by statistician Scheffé in 195839, mixture designs have since found applications in various industries, including civil engineering40–44, pharmaceuticals45–49, food50–54, and the development of composite materials55–59. It reduces experimental waste and enables reliable, efficient development of fuel cell composites55,60.

Current studies on composite BPs prioritize random filler ratios to enhance properties or factorial design for process parameters (temperature, pressure, and time). Fatema et al.61 used Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to optimize processing parameters for thermoset-based BPs, achieving 107.4 S/cm conductivity at 187 °C, 119 bar, and 5 min. Selamat et al.62 enhanced conductivity and flexural strength in graphite–PP composites through optimized CM. Roncaglia et al.63 applied a full-factorial design to maximize conductivity in G/EP composites. Kahveci et al.64 used RSM to optimize hydrogen fuel cell flow and voltage. Fatma et al.65 found optimal PEMFC performance at 81.2° contact angle, 1.97 dm³/min flow rate, and 1.69 μm surface roughness. King et al.66 achieved 38.31 S/cm through-plane conductivity in PP composites using CB, graphite, and CNTs. Tariq et al.67 used full factorial design and RSM in PP–G composites with MWCNT, CB, and EG, predicting conductivity and flexural strength with 83.9% and 93.4% accuracy.

Random filler selection in composite bipolar plates often results in suboptimal electrical and mechanical performance, limiting rational material design. While prior studies have focused on individual fillers or single-property optimization, systematic multi-property optimization considering electrical conductivity, flexural strength, and water absorption simultaneously remains limited, and the synergistic interactions among multi-scale carbon fillers are not well understood.

In this work, a mixture design–based optimization framework is proposed for EG/CB/G/sCF/novolac phenolic resin composite bipolar plates. This approach enables quantitative evaluation of filler interactions, reveals inherent trade-offs between conductivity, mechanical integrity, and durability, and provides a fundamental basis for future studies aimed at understanding filler–filler and filler–matrix interactions, while approaching DOE-relevant performance metrics and reducing experimental redundancy and material waste.

Experimental section

Materials

Novolac type phenol formaldehyde resin (NPFR) used as a polymer matrix was obtained in the powder form from Kakar Trade Chem Limited, Delhi, India. The gel time @ 145 °C, flow @ 125 °C angle 60, ash content, melting point, and hexamine content of the resin were 43 s, 41 mm, nil, 89 °C, and 11%, respectively. Natural flake graphite (NFG) (purity, 99.9 wt%; average flake size, 560 μm) used to prepare EG was obtained from Tirupati Specialty Graphite Private Limited, Bhubaneswar, Odisha, India. The carbon black (CB) (grade REGAL 400R, surface area ~ 75 m2/g, OAN ~ 113 mL/100 g) was supplied by DQ Enterprises, Delhi, India. Graphite fine powder (purity 99.9%) was obtained from Central Drug House (CDH), Delhi, India. Unidirectional carbon fiber (CF) was obtained from Carbon Light Private Limited, Ghaziabad, Utter Pradesh, India. The Carbon containing, tensile strength, thermal conductivity, and electric resistivity was 93%, 4900 MPa, 0.0224 Cal/(cm s °C), and 1.6 × 10− 3 Ω cm, respectively. Note that the unidirectional carbon fiber was manually cut to a target length of 3 mm using a marked template for accuracy, and confirmed that the maximum length did not exceed approximately 3.5 mm. The sulphuric acid (H2SO4, 98%) and nitric acid (HNO3, 69%) were supplied by Gulmarg Chemical & Scientific Works, Aligarh, India. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Preparation method

Preparation of EG

Graphite flakes (100 g per batch) were mixed with a 3:1 (v/v) H2SO4 (98%)/HNO₃ (69%) solution (2000 mL), and the mixture was allowed to react for 48 hours at room temperature (RT) with intermittent stirring. Following the intercalation step, the graphite was filtered and washed with deionized water until the pH reached 7 (confirmed by a pH meter) and then dried in a convection oven at 80°C for 24 hours. Portions (2 g) of the treated graphite were rapidly transferred into a crucible and placed in a muffle furnace stabilized at 800°C (± 30°C) for 1 minute. During the thermal shock treatment, the graphite flakes expanded significantly, forming the final product, ‘expanded graphite’ (EG). This procedure was repeated on multiple batches to obtain a total yield of approximately 4 kg of EG, which was used as the primary filler in composite preparation. Formulation of composites

The ranges of the various ingredients in the composite blend formulation for the development of the composite bipolar plate were determined based on preliminary experiments and an extensive review of the available literature. The study utilized a mixture composite bipolar plate (independent variables) composed of NPFR (A: 25–45%), EG (B: 35–60%), CB (C: 5–10%), G (D: 5–10%), and sCF (E: 5–10%). EG has been chosen as the primary filler for the composite, accounting for 35–60 wt% of the blend, owing to its outstanding characteristics, including high electrical conductivity, low gas permeability, lightweight nature, flexibility, chemical stability, and cost-effectiveness. The incorporation of graphite enhances the electrical conductivity and mechanical strength of the composite, contributing to the overall structural integrity and durability of the bipolar plate. CB is further introduced to augment electrical conductivity, as its highly conductive properties bridge gaps in the conductive network, optimizing electrical performance. Additionally, short CF reinforce the structural integrity of the composite, enhancing the overall strength of the bipolar plate. The selection of co-fillers content ranges (5–10 wt%) was guided by targeted property requirements and supported by previous studies27,70–78.

The composite bipolar plate formulation was meticulously crafted using an I-optimal mixture design approach, employing the Design-Expert software. This strategic formulation sought to achieve a composite bipolar plate possessing the desired performance characteristics, encompassing in-plane electrical conductivity (IPC), through-plane electrical conductivity (TPC), flexural strength (FS), and water absorption (WA). In this study, the I-optimal mixture design resulted in 21 experimental runs. These runs encompassed 15 model terms, 3 replicates to gauge pure error, and 3 lack-of-fit points, as detailed in Table 2. Multiple regression analysis was used to assess the fit and significance of linear, quadratic, special cubic, and cubic models.

Preparation of composites

The quantities of NPFR, EG, CB, G, and sCF were weighed for each experimental run as determined by the I-optimal mixture design (refer to Table 2). These pre-weighed components were mixed to achieve a perfectly homogeneous blend of the carbon fillers and NPFR. This uniform mixture was then carefully introduced into the cavities of a three-part mold. The assembled mold was placed between the plates of a compression molding machine with a temperature set at 180 °C ± 5 °C. Subsequently, a gradual load was applied, reaching a maximum of 7.9 MPa, which was the upper limit of the machine, for a duration of 10 min. Following the completion of the molding process, the composite plates were allowed to cool before being removed from the mold. These plates were then precisely cut into dimensions suitable for the subsequent measurement of various properties, as depicted in Fig. 3 and flowchart in Fig. 2b.

Results and discussion

Primary filler: expanded graphite

Expanded graphite (EG) served as the primary filler in the composite formulation due to its favorable electrical properties and porous morphology. SEM analysis (Fig. 4a–d) revealed a typical vermicular structure, with loose and porous features resulting from the exfoliation of graphite layers during thermal expansion. At lower magnification (100×), EG displayed a worm-like morphology, while higher magnifications (500× to 10000×) revealed abundant pores, visually estimated to be in the range of 2–15 μm. These pores, including both crevice-like and web-like structures, suggest enhanced surface area and interconnectivity. Similar porous structures have been reported by Tao et al.87 using microwave-irradiated EG. The expansion process disrupts the graphite crystal structure, leading to a significant volume increase due to the rapid evaporation of intercalants17,88.

SEM images of the expanded graphite at different magnifications: (a) ×100, (b) ×1000, (c) ×3000, and (d) ×10,000. The observed worm-like structure and layer separation indicate successful expansion following intercalation and thermal treatment. In images (a) and (b), the yellow arrows highlight crevice-like pores, while in (c) and (d), the red arrows indicate web-like pores, contributing to the increased surface area and structural interconnectivity.

This expanded morphology is known to enhance filler dispersion and interfacial bonding in polymer matrices. To evaluate its electrical potential, the in-plane electrical conductivity of EG was found to be 265.7 S/cm, confirming its suitability as a highly conductive filler.

Effect of ingredients ratio on the properties of the prepared composites

In-plane electrical conductivity

This variation in electrical conductivity underscores the intricate relationship between the composite’s constituent ingredients and its conductive properties. It was observed that the highest in-plane conductivity of the composites was achieved when a lower proportion of NPFR, CB, and Gr were combined with a higher concentration of sCF within the composite matrix. This indicates that the reduced quadratic model can be used to accurately map the design space.

The regression model (Eq. (8)) was simplified by removing non-significant terms, and the resulting model adequately represented the relationship between in-plane conductivity and the actual values of the ingredients.

Equation (8) shows that the linear terms of EG, CB, G, and sCF have a positive influence on in-plane conductivity, while the linear term of NPFR has a negative influence. The high coefficient of G (18.88) indicates that G contributes the most to electrical conductivity compared to the other ingredients. The negative coefficients of the quadratic terms EG*CB, EG*G, and G*sCF indicate that the interactions between these ingredient pairs decrease the in-plane electrical conductivity values. These interactions appear to reduce the composite’s electrical conductivity, highlighting the importance of understanding and optimizing these combinations to improve performance.

The peak in-plane electrical conductivity was achieved when the composition consisted of NPFR/EG/CB/G/sCF in the proportions of 25/54.7/5/5.3/10 wt%. Conversely, the lowest in-plane electrical conductivity was observed when the composition had the highest percentage of the resin, denoted as NPFR/EG/CB/G/sCF at 45/38.3/5/6.7/5 wt%. This variation in conductivity highlights the significant impact of ingredient proportions on the resulting electrical properties of the composite.

Moreover, as the sCF content reached its maximum level (10 wt%), a noticeable increase in in-plane electrical conductivity was observed. Conversely, augmenting the quantities of CB and G resulted in a decline in conductivity. This discernible effect is vividly depicted in Fig. 6a. These observations point to the pivotal role of sCF, in collaboration with EG, as the primary driver of enhanced electrical conductivity within the composite. This is due to the inherently superior conductivity of sCF when compared to CB and G. Additionally, CB and G have a disruptive effect on the conductive network established by sCF, further emphasizing the significance of sCF in governing electrical conductivity. Although CB and G show positive linear effects on conductivity in Eq. Through-plane electrical conductivity

The through-plane electrical conductivity of the composites ranged from 0.88 to 5.76 S/cm, as detailed in Table 2, underscoring the intricate relationship between constituent ingredients and conductivity. Notably, the levels of NPFR, CB, and Gr were found to intricately influence conductivity, with the highest values attained when these components were in lower proportions, coupled with a higher concentration of sCF. The composite composition yielding optimal conductivity featured 54.7 wt% of EG. Importantly, this implies that composite with higher in-plane conductivity also exhibited the highest through-plane conductivity.

Understanding the flexural strength proves to be a challenging task due to its intricate dependence on the composition of ingredients. Notably, the composite with the lowest flexural strength (21.03 MPa) was observed in the sample crafted with minimal proportions of NPFR (25 wt%), EG (52.72 wt%), CB (8.08 wt%), G (6.79 wt%), and sCF (7.41 wt%) in the mixture. Conversely, the highest flexural strength value (56.99 MPa) was attained in the composite derived from a mixture comprising (35.98 wt%) of NPFR, (37.37 wt%) of EG, (10 wt%) of CB, (8.08 wt%) of G, and (6.79 wt%) of sCF. The decrease in flexural strength when using a lower amount of NPFR in the composite can be attributed to insufficient binding and inadequate matrix formation. Insufficient NPFR may lead to poor binding between composite components, reducing cohesion and strength. Additionally, a lower amount of NPFR can result in an incomplete matrix formation, impacting the composite’s ability to distribute and bear loads effectively. These findings suggest that the interplay between the various ingredients, rather than the mere quantities of individual components, plays a crucial role in determining the flexural strength of the composites. A study by Witpathomwong et al. showed that a polybenzoxazine-based composite with 2 wt% CNTs achieved a flexural strength of 41.5 MPa, meeting the U.S. Department of Energy’s target for bipolar plates. However, while CNTs significantly enhanced mechanical and thermal properties, their use increases the overall cost of the composite, which may limit large-scale commercial adoption90.

It is also worth noting that the relatively low moulding pressure used during composites preparation may have influenced some of the observed composite properties, particularly density and flexural strength. The moderate level of compaction likely resulted in limited filler–matrix interlocking and increased porosity, which may have contributed to the trends observed in mechanical performance. This factor should be taken into account when interpreting the results.

Statistical analysis and model validation

To validate the significance of the experimental findings, a detailed statistical analysis was conducted using multiple regression models, including linear, quadratic, special cubic, and reduced quadratic formulations. The optimal model for each response variable was selected based on rigorous statistical criteria: sequential p-value, lack-of-fit p-value, coefficient of determination (R2), adjusted R2, predicted R2, and adequate precision ratio (APR).

In-plane electrical conductivity was best described by a reduced quadratic model, exhibiting exceptional significance (sequential p-value < 0.0001), high explanatory power (R2 = 0.9095), and robust predictive capability (predicted R2 = 0.7002). Through-plane electrical conductivity followed a linear model (sequential p-value = 0.0032; R2 = 0.6098), indicating a statistically significant but moderate correlation. Flexural strength also adhered to a linear model (p-value = 0.0101; R2 = 0.5437), confirming statistical significance despite moderate fit. Water absorption demonstrated the highest reliability via a reduced quadratic model (sequential p-value < 0.0001; R2 = 0.9945), underscoring the robustness of experimental data.

These results confirm the statistical validity and reliability of the experimental outcomes, and the corresponding models have been integrated into the revised manuscript.

Optimization

The focus of this study was on optimizing the performance of the polymer composite plate, and as a result, specific optimization criteria were devised (Table 4). The optimization targets were to: (i) maximize the in-plane electrical conductivity, (ii) maximize the through-plane electrical conductivity, and (iii) Keep flexural strength within the desired range (minimum value above DOE target). The final optimized formulation was found to be 25 wt% for NPFR, 55 wt% for EG, 5 wt% for CB, 5 wt% for G, and 10 wt% for sCF. The obtained desirability for the achieved combination was 0.95 (see Table 5). The predicted values of predictive models for the IPC, TPC, FS, and WA are 65.77 S/cm, 4.11 S/cm, 26.37 MPa, and 1.26%, respectively. The model recommends minimizing the content of CB and G to a minimum of 5 wt% and maximizing CF to a maximum of 10 wt% to optimize the performance of the polymer composite plate (see Fig. 10). This implies that sCF proves more effective in enhancing the electrical conductivity of the composite compared to CB and G. Augmenting the quantity of sCF in the composite holds the potential to further enhance electrical conductivity without compromising flexural strength.

Comparative obtain results with commercial bipolar plates

In this section, a comparative analysis was conducted to evaluate the performance of the C-2 bipolar plate design (see Fig. 11a–d) against various commercial alternatives, including VC-BMC-1025 from Verdecell (USA), Victor Reinz (USA), and Schunk (Germany). Comparing these values, the C-2 design demonstrates a distinct advantage with its significantly lower bulk density (1.2 g/cm3) compared to the other plates. While its in-plane electrical conductivity (69 S/cm) is slightly lower than Schunk (111 S/cm), it remains competitive, especially when considering the considerably lower through-plane electrical conductivity (5.8 S/cm) of C-2 compared to the other commercial plates. In terms of flexural strength, C-2 falls within an acceptable range for practical applications (29.6 MPa), despite being slightly lower than Victor Reinz and Schunk. Notably, C-2 exhibits minimal water absorption (1.2%) compared to the absence of data for the commercial plates (see Table 6). The relatively low flexural strength compared to commercial plates can be attributed to the pressure limitation during molding (maximum 7.9 MPa). Higher compaction pressures used in industrial processes typically enhance mechanical properties by improving filler dispersion and reducing porosity. Despite this, our composite showed competitive conductivity and acceptable mechanical performance.

Limitations

The study was limited by the maximum molding pressure of 7.9 MPa, which may have restricted filler compaction and contributed to a relatively low bulk density of 1.2 g/cm3. This limitation could also explain the low R2 value (0.56) in the flexural strength model, potentially due to internal voids and uneven filler distribution affecting mechanical consistency. The use of carbon black and graphite is limited to below 5 wt% to prevent conductivity saturation, potentially restricting further performance gains. Additionally, the dispersion of short carbon fibers within the matrix remains a challenge, as poor dispersion can lead to non-uniform electrical and mechanical properties. Finally, the cost and availability of high-performance fillers, such as short carbon fibers, may limit the economic feasibility of large-scale production.

Conclusions and future remarks

In summary, we developed a low-cost, high-performance thermoset composite using a statistical I-optimal mixture design, achieving properties that closely align with DOE targets for PEMFC bipolar plates. Our approach minimized the number of experiments, reduced material waste, and enhanced composite performance through optimized filler interactions.

Our findings suggest that the composition of thermoset-based composites critically influences their electrical, mechanical, and hygroscopic properties. While sCF consistently improved conductivity, higher contents of CB and G (above 5 wt%) led to a decline, likely due to negative interaction effects and agglomeration disrupting the conductive network. Flexural strength was strongly dependent on resin content, and higher NPFR levels effectively reduced water absorption, enhancing moisture resistance. While the optimized composite achieved a good balance of properties, its mechanical strength remains modest compared to commercial standards.

To extend this work, the carbon black and graphite contents should be limited to below 5 wt% to avoid conductivity saturation. Increasing short carbon fiber content beyond 10 wt% could further improve electrical performance. Additionally, applying molding pressures above 7.9 MPa may further enhance the density and overall performance of the composites, depending on equipment capabilities. These findings offer a solid foundation for the development of tailored composites for advanced energy applications such as fuel cells and electrolyzers. In particular, the prepared composites —especially C-2— demonstrate strong potential for industrial applications, including bipolar plates, lightweight conductive components, and energy storage systems. While the present study focuses on materials-level optimization of composite bipolar plates, the proposed mixture design framework provides a robust foundation for future investigations of cell-level performance metrics, including areal specific resistance, corrosion resistance, and gas permeability under realistic PEMFC operating conditions.
