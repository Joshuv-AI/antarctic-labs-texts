Arthroscopic Centralization of Meniscal Allograft Transplantation by Pullout Repair
Authors: Jiang Guo, Haocheng Du, Xin Guo, Jinzhu Liu, Yue Xu, Xiaocheng Jiang, Peng Zhang, Xintao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtrusion of meniscal allografts is common after meniscal allograft transplantation. This can reduce or even eliminate the meniscus’s ability to protect the cartilage. In this article, we describe a technique for centralizing the transplanted meniscus. The technique effectively addresses meniscal extrusion after meniscal allograft transplantation. It is cost-effective and easy to perform, and it provides a viable treatment option.

The meniscus plays a vital role in evenly distributing loads across the knee and ensuring its stability.1 Meniscal extrusion is defined as protrusion of the peripheral margin of the meniscus beyond the edge of the tibial plateau by 3 mm or more.2, 3, 4 When meniscal extrusion occurs as a result of various factors, the function of both the medial and lateral meniscus is compromised to varying degrees, resulting in a weakened load-bearing mechanism. Consequently, there is an increased concentration of load-bearing pressure on the articular cartilage, leading to accelerated degeneration.

To address this issue, in 2012 Koga et al.5 introduced the technique of meniscus centralization, aiming to restore and maintain meniscal function by centralizing the midbody of the meniscus onto the rim of the tibial plateau, effectively repairing or preventing meniscal extrusion. Through meniscus centralization techniques, the meniscus can be partially restored inward and maintained in a stable position, thereby achieving the goal of recovering and maintaining meniscal function.6,7 Subsequent reports have shown favorable clinical and radiographic outcomes using this technique.8,9

Extrusion of meniscal allografts after meniscal allograft transplantation (MAT) is prevalent, with reported incidences ranging from 30% to 80%.10 This technical note describes the technique of meniscus centralization in MAT, which effectively addresses extrusion concerns and thereby reinstates the chondroprotective impact of MAT.

Surgical Technique

Indications

Centralization of the meniscus and MAT are performed in cases of irreparable meniscal tears or meniscal deficiency. Moreover, this meniscus centralization technique is applicable to cases of extrusion of the midbody of the meniscus.

Patient Setup

The patient is placed in the supine position under spinal or general anesthesia, and a tourniquet is applied to the thigh. Anteromedial and anterolateral portals are made to perform the diagnostic arthroscopy. Before MAT and centralization are performed, the meniscal graft is made based on 3-dimensional computed tomography reconstruction measurements and the articular cartilage is evaluated and treated if needed.

Meniscus Centralization in MAT

Arthroscopy-assisted MAT is performed by our modified bone plug method with anatomic meniscal root reinsertion (Video 1).11 A 4-mm-diameter spherical anterior and posterior bone plug is cored from the meniscal allograft to permit sound bone-to-bone healing with the tibia. Two Ethibond sutures (No. 5; Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) are placed via the whipstitch technique in the anterior and posterior horns of the meniscal graft for later traction and spreading (Fig 1). Moreover, a FiberWire suture (No. 2; Arthrex, Naples, FL) is placed with a simple stitch at the midbody of the meniscus for centralization (Fig 1). The meniscal remnant is shaved until the meniscus-capsular zone is reached. A tibial guide, 2.0-mm guide drill, and 4.5-mm core drill are then used to locate and make the attachment points of the meniscal posterior and anterior horns (Figs 2 and 3). After the meniscal allograft is positioned in the articular cavity, the suture grasper is inserted through 2 tunnels to retrieve the 2 Ethibond sutures (Fig 4). Suturing is started from posterior to anterior to firmly attach the graft to the joint capsule by the outside-in and all-inside techniques (Fig 5).

Before all the sutures are tied, centralization of the meniscus is performed by the transtibial pull-through technique. The centralization position is confirmed by the tibial guide and a 2.0-mm guide drill, just proximal to the lateral meniscus and 1 cm anterior to the popliteal hiatus, with an arthroscopic view from the anteromedial portal. Then, a 4.5-mm tunnel is made by the core drill (Fig 6). The FiberWire is retrieved from the tunnel and fixed with a 4.75-mm SwiveLock device (Arthrex). Next, the sutures from the anterior and posterior tunnels are sutured to each other, leaving both meniscal horns anchored. Finally, the transplanted meniscus is evaluated for stability and matching (Table 1, Fig 7). After surgery, the patient undergoes routine postoperative rehabilitation.

Discussion

MAT is typically indicated for patients experiencing symptomatic meniscal deficiency in the absence of significant degenerative changes, provided that any coexisting conditions such as osteochondral defects, instability, or malalignment can be surgically addressed.12 The ideal candidates for MAT are skeletally mature individuals younger than 45 to 55 years who have undergone partial or total meniscectomy.13

Theoretically, MAT can relieve degeneration of the knee. However, studies in recent years have confirmed that the curative effect is not satisfactory. A recent meta-analysis reported that graft extrusion after MAT could not be completely avoided and that approximately half of the transplanted menisci exhibited evidence of major graft extrusion greater than 3 mm after arthroscopic-assisted MAT using bony fixation.10 In addition, it has been reported that a non-extruded meniscal allograft exhibits comparable chondroprotective properties to the natural meniscus whereas an allograft with extrusion exceeding 3 mm completely loses its ability to safeguard cartilage.14

The occurrence of extrusion in transplanted menisci can be attributed to several factors, including donor/patient size mismatch and surgical technique. The increased graft extrusion and incidence of major extrusion after medial MAT are often linked to limitations of the arthroscopically assisted bone plug technique. Challenges in accurately determining the anatomic positions of the anterior and posterior horns—resulting from obstructed visualization and the absence of definitive landmarks—frequently result in improper horn positioning. In contrast, the keyhole technique for lateral MAT enables more precise horn positioning through a bony trough, thereby reducing extrusion risks. These findings underscore the critical role of surgical technique in graft positioning and extrusion outcomes.15, 16, 17 To minimize extrusion and restore native biomechanics, optimization of graft size, graft quality, and meniscal root positioning is essential.18

Usually, the arthroscopic centralization technique includes knotless anchors, a JuggerKnot soft anchor (Arthrex, Naples, FL), all-inside suture anchor repair, and a dual-tunnel suture pullout technique. Consequently, as described in this article, we have developed a procedure to centralize and stabilize the midbody of the grafted lateral meniscus to prevent extrusion after MAT, which has some advantages over the existing procedures (Table 2): (1) Meniscal extrusion after MAT is reduced by the pullout repair centralization technique itself regardless of suture anchor placement, thereby reducing the difficulty of anchor implantation from the midlateral portal. (2) This technique is performed without knot tying, thus reducing impingement and cartilage injury. (3) This technique is cost-effective and easy to perform in contrast to other centralization techniques. (4) This technique eliminates the possibility of loose bodies in the joint because no rigid material is used. It should be noted that this is a preliminary report, and further follow-up is needed to investigate the long-term effects of the centralization technique applied to meniscal allografts in terms of clinical and radiologic outcomes. Nevertheless, centralization of the grafted meniscal repair by an arthroscopic tibial pullout technique can possibly reduce meniscal extrusion, maintain meniscal function, and eventually prevent progression of osteoarthritis.
