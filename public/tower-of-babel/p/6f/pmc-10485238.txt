Elusive long‐standing intraoral foreign body: A rare encounter
Authors: Chandini R, Saranya R, Murali Balasubramaniam, Ranjini Rajkumar, Safal Dhungel, Snehashish Ghosh
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Key Clinical MessageForeign body ingestion/aspiration can occur during orthodontic procedures that may be of minimal risk or lead to acute medical and life‐threatening emergencies. The present report describes a rare case of retained orthodontic wire fragment in the masticator space for nearly two decades identified as an incidental radiographic finding, highlighting the importance of optimum care in the dental clinic at all times be it surgical or nonsurgical intervention.

INTRODUCTION

Dental‐related objects rank second in the list of frequently ingested or aspirated foreign bodies in adults 1 such as endodontic instruments, prosthesis, extracted teeth, and orthodontic components 2 like bands, brackets, buccal tubes, removable appliances, appliance fragments, archwire fragments, transpalatal arches, sectional archwires, expansion appliance keys, coil springs, retainers, and quadhelices. 3 Patient position on the dental chair during treatment such as the supine or semirecumbent position is an important risk factor for these incidents. 4 Though orthodontic components retained in the airway system or gastrointestinal tract are periodically reported in literature, cases where they get embedded in the oral soft tissues are extremely rare. In the present article, we report an unusual case of a long‐standing arch wire fragment impacted into the left masseter muscle that was discovered incidentally on radiographic imaging.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 33‐year‐old female presented to the dental clinic with a chief complaint of dull throbbing pain in the posterior upper left jaw region for several years. Severity of the pain increased during chewing. Patient has a medical history of hypothyroidism for the past 3 years and is under medication for the same, and she is also allergic to azithromycin. On clinical examination, no significant extraoral or intraoral findings were noted. Imaging using orthopantomogram (OPG) was advised to rule out any caries involvement. The OPG image incidentally revealed a thin linear radiopacity in relation to the anterior border of the ramus (Figure 1A). Skull posteroanterior view appeared to show the same (Figure 1B). Further, imaging using computed tomography (CT) with contrast was advised to specify the location of the foreign body in relation to the jaw bone and the surrounding soft tissues. The three‐dimensional formatted images of CT scan showed a linear density in the masticator space (Figure 1C).

The initial differential diagnosis was a broken needle, possibly associated with a nerve block given to perform wisdom tooth extraction, underwent by the patient twelve years ago. Patient was informed about the imaging finding, and she reported that the clinical surgeon who performed her third molar extraction made sure she was comfortable during the entire procedure, which was uneventful with no postoperative complications. She then revealed on further questioning that she underwent fixed orthodontic treatment seventeen years ago due to mild crowding in her upper anteriors. This led to the hypothesis that the retained foreign object could also be an archwire fragment from her previous treatment.

An informed consent was obtained, and patient was prepared for surgical removal of the foreign body under local anesthesia. A single linear incision was placed over the anterior border of ramus at the left coronoid notch to raise a full‐thickness mucoperiosteal flap. The lateral and medial surfaces of the ramus were partially exposed to get a clear view of the masseter at the retromolar area. The site was visually examined and palpated for the foreign body, but it could not be identified. After a laborious search by the surgeon through the fiber of the masseter, the foreign body was recovered slightly superior to the retromolar area. It was a wire fragment of orthodontic origin as suspected measuring approximately 14 mm in length (Figure 1D). The wound was irrigated with saline and povidone iodine. Hemostasis was ensured and wound was sutured with 3–0 black silk. On postoperative follow‐up, 2 weeks later, wound healing was uneventful, and the patient stated that she was relieved of her chronic pain during mastication. An OPG was taken for patient reassurance to confirm the absence of foreign body (Figure 1E).

DISCUSSION

In the branch of orthodontics, wire fragments are the most commonly ingested component. This can take place when the orthodontist is cutting the distal end of a newly placed arch wire in order to prevent mucosal injury to the patient, during which the cut fragment can fall into the oropharynx. To prevent this, the cutting ends of the distal cutters should be visually checked for trapped wires after every cut. The wire can also break after the patient is discharged due to daily life activities like mastication. 5 , 6 Imaging studies are necessary for locating and eliminating a foreign body and evaluating the possible complications. Conventional radiography is easily available and is also an economical method to which foreign bodies are highly sensitive. If the retained material is not located by conventional radiography, ultrasound and CT can be performed in depicting foreign bodies in superficial and deeper locations, respectively. 7 In the reported case, the foreign body was an incidental finding in radiographic imaging.

It was located deep into the soft tissues with fibrosis surrounding it, and hence was invisible to the naked eye and could not be palpated either. Only with the help of radiographic imaging, the surgeon was able to access the exact location, highlighting the importance of imaging techniques in such cases. Foreign bodies that get embedded tend to induce a reparative granuloma formation enclosing them, which makes their detection difficult during surgery. 8 This is why identification and removal of the foreign body in the present case was extremely challenging for the surgeon. The sharp wire could have possibly punctured through the oral mucosa and migrated to the masseter muscle. Impacted foreign bodies can migrate from their site of entry to a distant site. Migration of foreign bodies like staples and fish bones within oral and cervical space has been reported in literature previously. 9 Bradford et al. reported a very similar case of retained arch wire fragment in the pterygomandibular space discovered as an incidental radiographic finding. 10

In the present case, retrieval of the object was taxing for the surgeon, and irritation and damage of tissues during the effort taken were extremely stressful to the patient as well. Hence, prevention is the best approach in our increasingly litigious world as foreign body impaction can not only cause physical and emotional distress to the patient but can ruin the reputation of dental practitioners as well.

The orthodontist should evaluate the orthodontic appliances to detect any wire breakage or loss of attachment before the patient leaves the dental office. If the patient feels any object in the oral cavity, he should resist the swallowing reflex and spit it out immediately. In case of aspiration/ingestion of foreign object, the patient should be referred for chest/abdominal radiography. Periodical radiographic evaluation during orthodontic treatment is necessary for early detection and management of foreign objects.

CONCLUSION

This is a case report on an unusual incident of a retained orthodontic wire fragment in the masticator space for a long period of time. It emphasizes the importance of regular follow‐up and vigilant examination of orthodontic patients with vague symptoms by the clinicians when carrying out dental procedures to prevent inhalation and ingestion of foreign bodies.
