Acceptance of pain neuroscience education in chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions: qualitative systematic review
Authors: Lukas Kühn, Eileen Wengemuth, Lara Kleist, Kyung Eun (Anna) Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.Six factors influence the acceptance of pain neuroscience education: clear communication, biomedical beliefs, personal relevance, tailored setting, strong clinician relationships, provider confidence, and role clarity.

1. Introduction

Globally, it is estimated that 1.3 billion people suffer from musculoskeletal pain conditions.42 There is considerable variation in the prevalence of different types of musculoskeletal pain, showing high prevalence in low back pain (36.8%), followed by other musculoskeletal disorders (21.5%), osteoarthritis (19.3%), neck pain (18.4%), gout (2.6%), and rheumatoid arthritis (1.3%).42 In the United States, 20.5% of the population report chronic pain conditions, of which back pain, hip, knee, and foot pain represent the most common disorders.60

Contemporary management strategies of chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions combine physical therapy and exercise with psychological treatment, complemented by a pharmacological management of somatic and psychological symptoms.1 Regarding psychological interventions, these can include aspects of acceptance and commitment therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, and pain education.1 Within the pain education approach, the concept of pain neuroscience education (PNE) has emerged as the dominating concept of teaching people about the causes and biology of their chronic pain (Table 1).

Pain neuroscience education encompasses a range of interventions aimed at educating individuals about the origins of pain, its biopsychosocial etiology, and the underlying biological mechanisms.32 While no universally accepted standard exists for the key educational components or delivery formats of PNE interventions, these typically include explanations of pain neurophysiology. Common topics covered are nociception and nociceptive pathways, neural structures such as neurons and synapses, the action potential, spinal inhibition and facilitation, peripheral and central sensitization, and neuroplasticity.25 The communication of this educational content is often conveyed through simplified scientific language, supported by visual aids such as diagrams, illustrations, booklets, or metaphors.26,32 A widely recognized example of a PNE resource is the “Explain Pain” booklet authored by Butler and Moseley.7

The use of PNE has been shown to have beneficial effects on various chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions, including chronic low back pain,10,15,28,53, 59 fibromyalgia,22,44 chronic fatigue syndrome,22 and hip and knee osteoarthritis.49 The treatment outcomes most notably influenced by PNE include pain perception,27,28,31,44,49 understanding of pain neurobiology,27 physical function,27,44,48 and disability.10,22,27,28,31,59 Furthermore, PNE has demonstrated positive effects on pain-related psychological factors, such as kinesiophobia,37 pain catastrophizing,37 anxiety,44 and depression.44 When combined with other multimodal interventions, such as exercise or manual therapy, PNE has been shown to produce the most promising results,15,31,38,44 a combination often referred to as PNE+.46 At the current state of knowledge, the optimal delivery format for PNE consists of oral educational sessions, incorporating reinforcement strategies, with a recommended total of 4 to 8 sessions delivered over 7 to 12 weeks.22,28

In practice, the biopsychosocial management approach of musculoskeletal pain conditions underlies numerous challenges, including clinical unpreparedness, misconceptions of guideline recommendations, limited resources, lacking funding models, or cultural aspects affecting clinical management expectations.35 However, to this date, a systematic synthesis and comprehensive display of empirically available determinants affecting the use and acceptance of PNE is pending. Thus, this review aimed to identify and explain determinants affecting the use and acceptance of PNE in clinical practice. As these determinants are inherently complex, context-dependent, and shaped by clinicians' or patients' experiences, perceptions, and organizational environments, qualitative primary studies represent the most appropriate evidence base for illustrating individual and contextual mechanisms to PNE acceptance. Restricting the review to qualitative primary research ensures methodological coherence and allows for a richer, more explanatory synthesis of findings.

2.2. Inclusion criteria

2.2.1. Participants/population

In this review, we included all qualitative studies conducted with healthcare providers involved in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain. This can involve all relevant physician specialty groups (ie, family physicians, orthopedic specialists, neurologists, or psychiatrists) and care providers of psychology, nursing, physiotherapy, sports therapy, occupational therapy, osteopathy, or chiropractic care. Eligible articles must address healthcare providers' experiences, perspectives, and attitudes toward PNE use, and/or its acceptance and applicability in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions.

Moreover, qualitative studies were included focusing on patient experiences, perspectives, and attitudes toward PNE use, acceptance, and applicability. Eligibility of studies investigating patient perspectives was given if participants were diagnosed with a chronic musculoskeletal health condition, such as chronic low back pain, chronic pain in the joints of the upper or lower limb (ie, frozen shoulder, epicondylitis lateralis), fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteopenia, gout, or juvenile arthritis. On the other hand, ineligible were patient groups diagnosed with chronic cancer pain, chronic postsurgical pain, and chronic pregnancy-related pain, as these groups are typically not targeted to PNE content. Studies involving children and adolescents were excluded as PNE for pediatric populations represents a conceptually distinct educational domain, characterized by different cognitive, developmental, and contextual requirements. Including these populations would have introduced substantial heterogeneity and limited the applicability of synthesized results to adult clinical practice.

2.2.2. Phenomena of interest

We included qualitative studies in which perspectives on educational interventions about the biology and physiology of pain were targeted. Within these educational interventions, an improved understanding of pain origins, biopsychosocial aspects of its etiology, and underlying biological functions had to be central concepts of information provision. In that respect, eligible educational interventions facilitate a shift of patients' perspectives on pain, representing an indication of tissue damage toward pain representing a personal need to protect the body. Following this understanding of PNE, we excluded studies targeting perspectives on multifaceted and multidisciplinary pain management interventions (ie, a combination of exercise approaches, manual therapy, psychological interventions, education, pharmacological interventions, coping interventions such as relaxation, imagery, or self-management skills) when PNE was embedded as one of multiple therapeutic components. In these contexts, participants' views could not be analytically isolated to PNE itself, and inclusion would have risked compromising construct specificity by conflating PNE-related determinants with those attributable to other elements of the intervention.

2.2.3. Context

We included qualitative studies investigating patient and healthcare provider perspectives in musculoskeletal care settings in primary, secondary, or tertiary care. We excluded settings of palliative and intensive care units and emergency departments as we expected the concept of PNE to be subordinate to these contexts.

2.2.4. Types of studies

This review included only original qualitative research comprising interview studies, focus group studies, observational ethnographic studies, or distinctly reported qualitative results of mixed-methods studies. Other forms of narrative literature, such as opinion articles, editorials, or expert articles, were excluded from analysis. This limitation of eligible research was determined to ensure methodological rigor in the exploration of opinions, motives, and social norms regarding the acceptance, use, and applicability of PNE in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions.

2.3. Search strategy

The search strategy was performed on MEDLINE (PubMed) (February 21st, 2024), Web of Science (February 21st, 2024), and PsycInfo (February 23rd, 2024). The initial search was performed on MEDLINE (PubMed) by applying predefined keywords, MESH terms, and search strings. This initial search was used to screen titles, abstracts, and index terms of retrieved articles for additional key themes relevant to building a comprehensive search strategy for our review's targeted population and phenomenon of interest. The final search and its results are illustrated in Appendix 1 (see Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/PR9/A394). The search strategy was developed by L. Kühn, L. Kleist, and E. Wengemuth, and A. Choi. L. Kühn performed the final search.

2.4. Study selection

For each database, retrieved articles were downloaded to RIS files, imported into EndNote 20 (Clarivate), and checked for duplicates. After duplicate removal, the final RIS file was imported to the online application SUMARI software (Joanna Briggs Institute), which was used to perform the title and abstract screening as well as the full-text screening. E. Wengemuth and L. Kühn independently conducted the consensus-guided title and abstract screening. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion between the 2 authors. In cases of hardship, third-party consultation (A.C.) made decisions on article inclusion. The full-text screening followed the same procedure.

2.5. Assessment of methodological quality

A. Choi, E. Wengemuth, L. Kühn, and LK independently assessed included articles for methodological quality following the JBI critical appraisal tool for qualitative research, which comprises 10 quality criteria.2 Two researchers independently assessed each article. Discrepancies were discussed between the 2 assessors, and mutual agreement was reached. Before the quality assessment, AC, EW, LaK, and LK predetermined 4 criteria of the critical appraisal tool to be mandatory for the final article inclusion of this review. These criteria included congruity between the research methodology and the research questions (Q2: “Is there congruity between research methodology and research questions or objectives?”), congruity between the research methodology and the representation of data (Q4: “Is there congruity between research methodology and the representation and analysis of data?”), an adequate representation of participant voices (Q8: “Are participants, and their voices, adequately represented?”), and a plausibility of conclusions flowing from data analysis (Q10: “Do the conclusions drawn in the research report flow from the analysis or interpretation of the data?”). The methodological quality assessment results were transferred to a narrative table format using the JBI SUMARI software.

2.6. Data extraction

The characteristics of the included articles were extracted by EW and LK using the JBI SUMARI data extraction tool. In that regard, data on methods used for data collection and analysis, geographical location, investigated phenomena of interest, aspects of the context and setting of each study, participant characteristics, and identified key themes were extracted and organized in a narrative table format. LK revised the final data extraction table of study characteristics to ensure a homogenous phrasing of similar concepts.

2.7. Data synthesis

Review findings were synthesized using JBI SUMARI, in which the JBI meta-aggregation approach for qualitative evidence synthesis was applied.2 In that respect, the findings were rated according to their quality and trustworthiness and were considered unequivocal, credible, or not supported. Unequivocal findings were illustrated by a direct quotation of a participant, which was considered to thoroughly support the finding. Credible findings were illustrated by a participant's voice which, however, prompted open questions about whether the illustration comprehensively reflects the finding. Not supported findings lacked participant illustrations and were excluded from analysis.

In a subsequent step, the findings were organized separately into themes and categories. These themes and categories then underwent meta-aggregation and synthesized results derived from them.

2.8. Assessing confidence in the findings

To appraise certainty in synthesized findings, we graded according to the ConQual approach.33 This approach allows for drawing conclusions on the dependability and credibility of each synthesized finding and was presented in a summary of findings table, highlighting on which the rationale of each ConQual score was calculated.

3.4.1. Synthesized finding 1: basic didactic principles such as metaphors, multichannels, repetition, and plain messages support the delivery of pain neuroscience education

To make PNE content understandable and internalize PNE messages, health care professionals (HCPs) should take enough time and use basic didactic principles such as metaphors, repetition, and easily understandable messages. A standard mode of PNE delivery is through video. However, this format can be perceived as tedious and exhausting. This seems to be especially prevalent in video formats in which messages are difficult to understand, and comics and cartoons illustrate protagonists. On the other hand, easily understandable messages that are convincingly presented as evidence-based help the trustworthiness of PNE videos. Independent of the delivery format, longer PNE sessions can be perceived as overwhelming.“Other than looking at those videos that we had to watch, they were a bit tedious (…). Well, I mean, we covered it all in with [the Physiotherapist]. We sort of talked about it, that one about, I don't know, deflecting pain, imagine pain, the worst and I don't know that one. I actually didn't even get all the way through that.” [Patient]3“Too much, too quick.” [Patient]50

On the other hand, HCPs find metaphors helpful for explaining chronic pain mechanisms, such as comparing pain with a car's petrol light, making it easier for patients to understand. Patients use metaphors, like comparing chronic pain with a faulty car or smoke alarm, to communicate and help others understand their pain. Generally, patients value the language of PNE, as it offers terms to express their pain experiences better. 3.4.2. Synthesized finding 2: persisting beliefs in biomedical explanations of pain can lead to skepticism and misunderstandings of pain neuroscience education

Persisting beliefs in biomedical explanations of chronic pain represent major barriers to an in-depth understanding and acceptance of the PNE concept. Understanding that pain originates from the brain rather than physical structures can lead to the perception that pain is “invented” or not real, although the person still feels the pain as genuine. The idea that pain can result from a heightened nervous system response may cause resistance, as some individuals find it hard to accept or understand. Explaining pain through the PNE approach may lead to patients feeling that their pain is not being taken seriously, as it can be perceived as psychological or imagined.“I do not agree that it is caused here (he points to his head), I can hardly understand this. I can't believe this, maybe the doctor is right, but I look at it differently” [Patient]36

“I do not agree that it is caused here (he points to his head), I can hardly understand this. I can't believe this, maybe the doctor is right, but I look at it differently” [Patient]36

Patients often found educational content on exercises aimed at desensitizing pain (such as meditation and breathing exercises) to be unhelpful, frustrating, and not in line with their practical approach to pain management. To better accept PNE, patients required more precise explanations about the link between chronic pain and mental health, emphasizing the emotional toll of chronic pain. Despite that, many patients appreciate that thoughts, beliefs, and emotions can influence their pain experience. HCPs experience that chronic pain patients often have comorbid depression, which potentially hinders the acceptance of PNE, as the emotional burden may overshadow the focus on pain management strategies. Moreover, some patients express hesitation or misconceptions about psychological assessments, often thinking they are only for those who are severely depressed or “crazy.”“That's often the story with chronic people that you end up, you know they're so depressed and low that you feel a bit like the Dementors in Harry Potter, you feel like your soul is being sucked out of you because you're constantly trying to kind of buffer them up … So, you've got chronic problems and they're depressed … they can't play golf, they can't do their shopping, they can't drive very far, they're not working … you cry with people sometimes, it's though … with him I did get a bit frustrated … he seemed a bit needy.” [Physiotherapist]56

HCPs note that patients sometimes perceive concepts such as central sensitization as a form of illness, mainly when explained regarding faulty body systems such as “wiring.” This can lead to confusion or concern about something being wrong with them. Moreover, strong beliefs in biological causes of chronic pain may prevent patients from accepting concepts of PNE, which suggests a more complex, multifactorial origin of pain. In that respect, patients find it helpful to shift the focus from blaming specific body parts (such as an arm) to understanding pain stemming from the central nervous system, offering a new perspective on managing pain. Without medical examinations or precise physical diagnoses, patients often struggle to accept that their chronic pain is not linked to a physical problem, despite reassurances that there is nothing seriously wrong.

Irrespective of this, religious beliefs and practices, such as using herbs or seeking spiritual healing, can play a role in how patients experience and manage chronic pain.“The tricky thing is, that if you say there's something wrong with the wiring, the inhibitory wiring, it doesn't function as it should, but it can get better, that you still call it a kind of illness. “O, so I have faulty wiring, so something is wrong”. 4. Discussion

In this qualitative evidence synthesis, we aimed to explore determinants of the use and acceptance of PNE in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions. We identified 6 main syntheses of findings relevant to the use and acceptance of PNE.

Regarding synthesized finding 1, research highlights both the power and complexity of using metaphors in PNE. Gallagher et al.11 found that metaphors can effectively reduce pain catastrophizing and improve pain knowledge, with benefits persisting at a three-month follow-up in a single-blinded randomized controlled trial. However, Louw et al.26 suggest that while the overarching goal of helping patients reconceptualise pain is essential, no specific metaphor seems more effective than others, indicating that the overall message may matter more than the metaphor itself. By contrast, Johnson, Hudson, and Ryan16 critically discussed in a debate article the potential harm of metaphors that link chronic pain too closely to a stand-alone patho-anatomical argumentation, such as “pain is damage.” Arguing that these metaphor types may reinforce fear and misunderstanding, they advocate for a shift toward constructive metaphors such as “pain is protection” to foster better understanding, engagement, and well-being.16 While metaphors are valuable tools in pain education, these studies emphasize that their design and delivery require thoughtful considerations, as they require a process of making careful (metaphor) choices that resonate with patients' lived experiences, avoiding examples that oversimplify or imply tissue damage and evaluating how patients interpret metaphors to be able to identify or prevent misunderstandings.

Within synthesized finding 1, growing scientific evidence supports the effectiveness of delivering PNE through multiple channels, such as in-person sessions, video formats, booklets, and online applications, often referred to as blended-learning approaches. In a notable randomized controlled trial involving 120 participants with chronic spinal pain, the authors demonstrated that blended-learning PNE, which combined online modules with in-person sessions, led to significant improvements in kinesiophobia and illness perceptions.29 However, the effect sizes were small to moderate, suggesting that blended-learning PNE should not be considered as a stand-alone treatment.29 Furthermore, a randomized controlled trial comparing multimedia-based pain management education with traditional face-to-face sessions in patients with chronic low back pain revealed that both methods effectively reduced pain intensity and pain catastrophizing. The multimedia approach, which included video content and online resources, was particularly beneficial for patients with limited access to in-person sessions.47

Scientific research supports the importance of repetition in PNE for individuals with chronic pain. Repeated exposure to accurate pain information helps reinforce learning, challenge maladaptive beliefs, and promote lasting behavioural changes. A meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found that longer durations of PNE were associated with greater reductions in pain intensity and kinesiophobia, particularly in adults.23

Concerning the synthesized finding 2, academic literature supports the idea that persistent adherence to biomedical explanations of pain may contribute to skepticism and misunderstandings of PNE. In a survey study of Gibbs et al.,12 the authors found that clinicians' biomedical attitudes significantly influenced their decision-making for patients with chronic low back pain, often leading to more conservative management approaches, potentially hindering the acceptance of alternative educational models such as PNE. Although evidence is limited, research suggests that simulation-based learning combined with debriefing and reflective discussion may help clinicians to gradually shift toward a more biopsychosocial understanding of chronic pain.5

In addition, results of an exploratory online survey revealed that individuals with persistent pain were more likely to express negative attitudes toward PNE concepts on social media compared with healthcare professionals.55 This highlights how it is particularly challenging for individuals suffering from persistent pain experiences to accept the biopsychosocial aspects of PNE messages. To support a biopsychosocial understanding of chronic pain in affected individuals, it seems important to validate pain experiences58 and to account for health literacy competencies36 to deliver appropriate PNE content to individuals.

Accounting for synthesized finding 3, a recent systematic review by Tatikola et al.52 also emphasizes that personalizing PNE to the individual's context and needs can further optimize clinical outcomes. In addition, the review highlighted that PNE is more effective when delivered in one-to-one oral sessions and associated with reinforcement elements, emphasizing the importance of personalized and engaging delivery methods.52

Regarding synthesized finding 4, potential benefits of individual or group-based learning settings for delivering PNE have especially been empirically investigated for group-based interventions. Those have shown positive outcomes in various chronic pain conditions, including fibromyalgia6 or chronic low back pain.39 Outcomes that significantly improved compared with the control groups were the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire,6 pain intensity,39 and disability.39 By contrast, individualized PNE can allow tailored interventions that consider personal factors such as educational backgrounds or cultural contexts. In a recently published study protocol for a randomized controlled trial, Salazar-Méndez et al.43 aim to compare the clinical and psychosocial effectiveness of in-group vs individually administered PNE sessions in patients with chronic low back pain. Group-based interventions may offer benefits such as social support and shared learning experiences, enhancing motivation and adherence. On the other hand, individualized PNE may allow for personalization, potentially leading to more targeted and relevant interventions.

In synthesized finding 5 of this evidence synthesis, it is emphasized that a good clinician–patient relationship is a crucial prerequisite for accepting PNE interventions. To the best of our knowledge, no experimental study designs currently support this relationship. However, a study published by Losin et al.24 demonstrated that patients who felt a sense of similarity and trust toward their clinician reported lower pain levels during simulated medical procedures. This effect was particularly prevalent in patients with high levels of anxiety.24 Moreover, a functional MRI study has shown that patients' trust in their clinician can affect brain activity related to pain processing.4 When patients interacted with clinicians they trusted, there was reduced neural activity in pain-related brain regions during painful stimuli, indicating that trust can modulate pain at the neural level.4 Following this rationale, establishing trust and rapport is a promising approach to enhance patient engagement, improve learning outcomes, and eventually reduce pain perception.

In synthesized finding 6, a lack of confidence, unclear responsibilities, and a professional culture focused on biomedical aspects of care were identified as possible barriers keeping HCPs from delivering PNE. This finding is prevalent in a variety of biopsychosocial treatment approaches. For instance, a qualitative systematic review by Synnott et al.51 investigated physiotherapists' perceptions regarding assessing and managing cognitive, psychological, and social factors influencing recovery in individuals with low back pain. The authors concluded that while physiotherapists recognize certain psychosocial factors in low back pain management, their ability to address them effectively seems to be encumbered by limited recognition, stigmatization tendencies, and insufficient training.51 These findings additionally support the need for enhanced education and training to equip certain occupational groups with the skills necessary to manage the multifaceted nature of chronic pain conditions.

4.1. Strengths and limitations

This review demonstrates several methodological strengths that enhance the trustworthiness and relevance of its findings. First, we adhered to the JBI methodology for qualitative meta-aggregation. This ensured a systematic and transparent study appraisal, data extraction, and synthesis. The rigorous assessment of methodological quality using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool—independently conducted by 2 reviewers—reinforced the credibility of the included studies. Furthermore, an accompanying published protocol contributed to the transparency and accountability of the review process.

Another notable strength is the inclusion of both patient and healthcare provider perspectives. This dual-perspective approach supports a more holistic understanding of how PNE is perceived and applied in clinical contexts. Moreover, the strict inclusion criteria, which focused specifically on educational interventions targeting a reconceptualization of pain from a biopsychosocial perspective, allowed for a high level of specificity in addressing the review questions. Applying the ConQual approach further strengthened the confidence in the synthesized findings by transparently evaluating their credibility and dependability.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting this review's findings. This review did not include grey literature such as expert commentaries, editorials, or unpublished reports. As a result, potentially rich sources of experiential knowledge and emerging clinical insights may have been overlooked, which could limit the breadth of perspectives captured. The exclusion of specific patient populations, including pediatric, postsurgical, cancer-related, and pregnancy-related chronic pain groups, further narrows the scope of applicability. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings may be limited to the adult population living with musculoskeletal pain in nonpalliative and nonemergency care contexts. Moreover, the transferability of our findings is limited by the fact that the included studies predominantly reflected perspectives of physiotherapists, exercise physiologists, and general practitioners, which may not fully capture the views, implementation contexts, or educational practices of other healthcare professions involved in pain management.

While meta-aggregation offers a structured and policy-relevant synthesis of findings, it does not pursue theory development to the same extent as other approaches, such as meta-ethnography.45 Thus, this review prioritizes documenting our original interpretations over generating novel theoretical insights. Finally, although studies were drawn from diverse healthcare settings and geographical regions, contextual differences in healthcare delivery, professional roles, and cultural understandings of pain may introduce heterogeneity. When transferring the findings to specific clinical or cultural contexts, these factors should be considered.

Considering future directions, further work is needed to develop and evaluate PNE approaches tailored to individuals with strongly ingrained biomedical beliefs, as well as to investigate how clinician learning strategies may facilitate shifts toward a biopsychosocial framework. Comparative evaluations of individual vs group-based delivery formats, supported by implementation-focused methodologies, would help to determine how contextual factors influence PNE engagement and feasibility. Moreover, experimental studies are needed to examine the role of the clinician–patient relationship as a mechanistic contributor to PNE delivery. Given that the existing qualitative evidence predominantly reflects the perspectives of physiotherapists, exercise physiologists, and general practitioners, future studies should capture the views of a broader range of healthcare professions to enhance the applicability of findings across clinical settings.

5. Conclusion

In this review, the qualitative evidence—rated as low to very low in certainty—provides only tentative insights into factors that may influence the use and acceptance of PNE. Several studies described that communication approaches such as metaphors, repetition, and personally relevant explanations might support understanding, although the consistency and impact of these strategies remain uncertain. Persistent biomedical beliefs were reported as a potential source of skepticism toward PNE, yet the extent to which addressing these beliefs affects engagement is unclear. Preferences regarding delivery format and the perceived quality of the clinician–patient relationship were also noted as possible influences. On the provider-level, including limited confidence, role ambiguity, and adherence to biomedical models, were mentioned as potential barriers to integrating PNE into routine practice. Overall, these themes should be interpreted with caution, and further rigorous research is needed to clarify the mechanisms that influence the implementation of PNE into clinical contexts.
