Identifying Strengths and Weaknesses in Health Literacy among Medical Students: A Systematic Review
Authors: Alvin Baralin, Emna Boulares, Sabrina Chen Yin Tan, Young-jeon Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited health literacy is a global public health concern linked with increased healthcare expenses and poor health outcomes. Health literacy (HL) is crucial for medical students as patients and future medical practitioners. This systematic review aims to provide a global understanding of the state of HL among medical students, identify strengths and weaknesses across HL domains, and determine different variables associated with HL.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted using the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration number CRD42024608968. The CINAHL, PubMed, Scopus, Medline, and Web of Science were searched for cross-sectional studies that reported the HL of medical students from 2013 to February 2025. The AXIS tool was used to assess the quality of the included studies. The results are presented using narrative analysis.ResultsThirteen studies met the inclusion criteria. These studies collectively included 3,647 medical students, approximately 44% female and 29% male. Different HL assessment tools were used, resulting in variations in the findings. Medical students demonstrated proficiency in locating and comprehending health information but faced challenges in managing their own health, feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers, and appraising health information. Symptoms of depression, social support, and the amount of time spent using the internet were associated with HL. All studies included revealed a very low risk of bias.ConclusionThe health literacy of medical students ranged from moderate to high. The strengths identified both in the general context and in the digital context were finding and understanding health information. However, areas needing improvement were identified in its practical application, particularly in self-management and critical appraisal of health information. These findings indicate the potential benefit of incorporating targeted health literacy training into medical education to enhance critical thinking, practical application, and communication skills in support of patient-centered care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07388-0.Keywords: Health Literacy, E-Health Literacy, Digital Health Literacy, Medical Students, Systematic Review, Determinants of Health, Health Promotion, Medical Education, Preventive Medicine

Background

Health literacy (HL) is the capacity to look for, comprehend, appraise, and act on health information and services to make better health decisions [1, 2]. When used with online health resources and digital technology, this capacity is called e-health literacy (e-HL) or digital health literacy [3], which has significantly advanced modern healthcare administration.

Limited health literacy remains a significant concern for public health worldwide. It has been linked with increased healthcare expenses [4–6] and adverse health outcomes [7, 8], highlighting the importance of healthcare providers in helping mitigate these challenges. Healthcare providers with high levels of health literacy may assist patients in effectively managing their health [9, 10].

However, the majority of healthcare professionals have difficulty identifying patients with limited HL [11, 12] and overestimate their HL levels [13]. In addition, the unfamiliarity and miscomprehension of the term "health literacy" and its domains [10, 14, 15] may restrict the impact of healthcare interventions.

HL is relevant to medical students as patients and future medical practitioners. Medical students constitute an essential pipeline for the future healthcare workforce. They are expected to protect the health and welfare of individuals and communities while also serving as a supporting resource for the public health system [16]. Several studies suggest that medical students who receive HL-specific training have increased their health literacy [17]. High HL levels are required to manage the complexities and changes within the health systems and services [18].

The understanding of health literacy has evolved over the past few years. The early functional view of HL focused primarily on reading and comprehending basic information, such as understanding prescription instructions and appointment reminders [19, 20]. Nutbeam’s model broadened HL beyond basic skills to include individuals’ ability to communicate, make informed decisions, and critically evaluate health information [21]. The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasized HL as a key determinant of health, grounded in the ability to access, understand, and apply information in everyday contexts [22]. In response to the growing digitization of healthcare, digital HL frameworks have emerged, such as Norman and Skinner’s model [3], which identifies six interrelated literacies necessary for engaging with digital health resources. Similarly, the WHO’s Global Strategy on Digital Health [23] highlights digital HL as crucial for enabling equitable access to health services and informed participation in digital health systems. These developments underscore HL as a dynamic and context-dependent competency essential for navigating modern healthcare environments.

Health literacy is globally acknowledged as a key factor influencing health outcomes [24]. Nevertheless, reviews across different populations, including children and adolescents [25, 26], older adults [27], university students [28], migrants [29], and men [30], consistently indicate insufficient levels. Although various individual cross-sectional studies have examined the health literacy of medical students [31, 32] and a systematic review has explored health literacy in mixed populations [28] that may include them, none have focused exclusively on this population. Given their future role as healthcare providers, a dedicated systematic review is warranted to synthesize and contextualize these individual findings. This systematic review contributes to the field not only by examining the overall HL rate but also by identifying strengths and weaknesses across different HL domains. Moreover, it addresses important yet underexplored factors such as demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, digital access, and academic standing that may influence HL in this population.

This systematic review aims to fill this gap by providing an extensive overview of HL among medical students, highlighting the strengths and weaknesses across HL domains, and assessing the factors associated with HL. By synthesizing current evidence, this review serves as a basis for subsequent investigations and useful perspectives for medical instructors and policymakers to improve health literacy training in medical education.

Methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statements [33]. The protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) with the registration number CRD42024608968.

Research Question

This review was guided by the PICo framework:

Population (P): Medical students.

Interest (I): The state of health literacy, including strengths and weaknesses across specific health literacy domains, and associated factors (such as sociodemographic, socioeconomic, digital access, and academic factors)

Context (Co): Assessment of health literacy within the academic and educational environments of medical students

On the basis of this framework, the review seeks to answer the following question:

What is the state of health literacy among medical students, including the strengths and weaknesses across specific health literacy domains, and what sociodemographic, socioeconomic, digital access, and academic factors are associated with health literacy in this population?

Search strategy

Systematic searches were conducted in five electronic databases: CINAHL, PubMed, Scopus, Medline, and Web of Science. All databases were searched from 2013 until February 2025. In 2013, the WHO Regional Office for Europe reported that HL is a significant determinant of overall health, more important than income, employment status, educational level, or racial or ethnic background [34]. Information on the search strategies is presented in Appendix 1.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The included literature comprised (a) cross-sectional studies that measure the overall HL levels and/or HL domains among medical students who are enrolled in medical education to qualify as medical practitioners [35, 36], (b) research published in peer-reviewed journals from 2013—February 2025, (c) English-language studies, and (d) studies available in full text. The inclusion of only English-language studies was based on resource limitations for translation and to ensure data accuracy. Full-text studies were selected to ensure access to all essential data for the review.

To ensure methodological rigor, only peer-reviewed published articles were included. Gray literature, such as theses, conference proceedings, and unpublished reports, was excluded. Citation tracking was not performed, as the search strategy was designed to be comprehensive across the five major databases.

Additionally, studies with mixed study populations (i.e., studies that included all health sciences students) were included only if the findings related to the medical student population were presented independently. The corresponding authors were contacted to request this information for studies without disaggregated results for medical students. Studies were included if disaggregated data were provided; otherwise, they were excluded.

Other exclusion criteria include, but are not limited to, the measurement of specific HLs, such as oral HL and mental HL.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

One reviewer (AB) initially screened the titles and abstracts of all identified articles. Full-text screening was then conducted independently by two reviewers (EB and SCT). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus, and if disagreements persisted, a third reviewer (YJS) was consulted to reach a final decision.

All references were managed using EndNote (version 21.5, Clarivate™) for citation management, duplicate removal, and full-text screening. Data extraction was carried out independently by EB and SCT using a standardized Microsoft Excel form. Extracted information included the study title, author(s), year, reference, participants' gender and age, survey locations, theoretical frameworks, health literacy (HL) measurement tools, HL levels, and findings related to HL domain scores and influencing factors. Any inconsistencies during the data extraction process were resolved through discussion and consensus. If unresolved, AB and YJS were involved to ensure consistency and accuracy throughout the procedure.

Quality Assessment

The Appraisal Tool for Cross-Sectional Studies (AXIS) was used to assess the quality of the included studies [37, 38]. Two authors (EB and SCT) independently evaluated the quality of each study. If disagreements arose, the third author (AB) reviewed the issue and facilitated a consensus. The scoring system was adopted from previous studies [28, 39, 40]. This scoring system classified studies on the basis of their responses: those correctly answering at least 19 out of 20 questions had a very low risk of bias, scores of 17 or 18 indicated a low risk, scores of 15 or 16 signified a moderate risk, and scores of 14 or fewer reflected a high risk of bias.

Analysis and Reporting

The results were reported using narrative synthesis due to the diversity of studies in the field [41].

Initially, an inductive thematic analysis was conducted to synthesize the extracted data. Two independent reviewers (EB and SCT) identified the initial codes, which were then applied to relevant segments of the data. Similar codes were then grouped into themes based on patterns that emerged inductively from the data itself. Any discrepancies between the reviewers during the coding process were resolved through a thorough discussion, and if necessary, a third reviewer (YJS) was consulted to reach a consensus. These results were then reviewed and categorized into final themes to provide a narrative framework to integrate the conclusions cohesively.

Results

Systematic searches in CINAHL, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane yielded 826 records. After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 653 records were reviewed. Of 653, 32 were identified for full-text review based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Nineteen studies were further excluded after full-text review. Consequently, the thirteen studies listed in Table 1 were included in this systematic review. Figure 1 presents a flow diagram summarizing the study selection process.

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection process

These studies were conducted in Kazakhstan, Australia, China, Thailand, Iran, Nepal, Pakistan, Tasmania, and Vietnam. Eleven studies were performed at single university institutions, and two were conducted across multiple medical universities. These studies collectively included 3,647 medical students. Among the 46% of studies that reported gender-specific data, approximately 44% were female and 29% were male. The participants’ ages ranged widely from less than 20 years to more than 35 years. In addition, eight studies measured general HL, whereas five measured e-HL.

Various theoretical frameworks have been employed to help explain and contextualize health literacy. For general HL, these include but are not limited to Nutbeam's Health Literacy Model [45, 48, 52, 54], Sørensen et al.'s model [51], and Healthy People 2030 [42, 47]. For e-HL, Norman and Skinner's model [43, 46, 49] and the WHO framework [44, 50] were used. These theoretical models have contributed to shaping the conceptual basis and structure of HL instruments reported in the included studies. These instruments were validated within the context of each specific country.

General Health Literacy

The general health literacy levels of medical students varied across the studies reviewed. Three studies from Australia [45, 53, 54] and Nepal [52] utilized the Health Literacy Questionnaire 44 (HLQ-44), which presents mean scores for each HL domain but not an overall mean score. Lane et al.'s study reported mean scores ranging from 3.00 to 4.41, indicating moderately high HL [45], Mullan et al.'s scores ranging from 3.06 to 4.48, described as high [54], Budhathoki et al. reported scores ranging from 2.88 to 3.73, interpreted as high [52], and Mather et al.'s scores ranged from 3.00 to 4.10, also reflecting high levels of HL [53]. Kayupova et al. [42] assessed general HL in Kazakhstan using the HLS19-Q12 tool, with 94.45% of students achieving a mean score of 87.60%, categorized as high. In China, Zhong et al. [48] employed the c-HALT-8 tool, with a mean score of 25.70 (4.52). In Iran, Barati et al. [51] and Pourfridoni et al. [47] used the HELIA tool and reported mean scores of 125.88 (15.87) and 131.60 (13.43), respectively; both indicative of high or excellent levels of HL based on the scoring interpretations used in their studies.

Four reviewed studies—Budhathoki et al. [52], Lane et al. [45], Mather et al. [53], and Mullan et al. [54]—reported mean scores for multiple HL domains. Medical students presented the highest scores in terms of their understanding of health information well enough to know what to do (Lane et al. 4.41 (0.49), Budhathoki et al. 3.73 (0.59), Mather et al. 4.10 (0.53), Mullan et al. 4.48 (0.53)), ability to find good health information (Lane et al. 4.11 (0.59), Budhathoki et al. 3.56 (0.64), Mullan et al. 4.45 (0.52)), and ability to actively engage with healthcare providers (Lane et al. 3.77 (0.61), Budhathoki et al. 3.52 (0.73), Mather et al. 3.93 (0.71), Mullan et al. 4.24 (0.56)).

In contrast, the scores reflecting feelings of being understood and supported by healthcare providers were one of the lowest across all four studies (Lane et al. 3.10 (0.65); Budhathoki et al.: 3.03 (0.50); Mather et al. 3.10 (0.51); Mullan et al. 3.06 (0.65)). Other weak areas include appraising health information (Lane et al. 3.00 (0.48); Budhathoki et al. 2.91 (0.42); Mather et al. 3.00 (0.48); Mullan et al.: 3.19 (0.47)) and actively managing health (Lane et al. 3.01 (0.52); Budhathoki et al. 2.89 (0.41); Mather et al. 3.07 (0.43); Mullan et al. 3.19 (0.52)).

e-Health Literacy

Several instruments have measured e-HL from various studies with varying results. Three studies used the eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS). In Thailand, the mean e-HL among medical students was 33.35 (3.28), categorized as high [49]. Iranian medical students had a mean score of 27.0 (6.06), considered a moderate level [50], whereas Chinese medical students scored 31.9 (5.9) [55]. Moreover, two studies utilized the Digital Health Literacy Instrument (DHLI). In Vietnam, Sen et al. [46] reported a mean e-HL score of 55.03 (7.85). In contrast, Farooq et al. [44] in the Pakistan domain-specific scores ranging from 8.25 to 9.98, describing these results as representing a commendable degree of proficiency with domain levels ranging from ‘desirable’ to ‘very desirable’. Finally, Kayupova et al. [42] assessed e-HL among the same participants using the Health Literacy Survey 2019 – Digital Health Literacy (HLS19-DIGI), reporting a mean score of 86.26 (21.79).

Two of the five studies provided the mean scores per domain. In the study by Tanasombatkul et al. [49], the strengths included knowing where to find health resources on the internet with a mean of 4.39 (0.59), how to find helpful health resources on the internet (4.32/0.56), and the ability to tell high-quality health resources from low-quality health resources on the internet (4.25/0.64). On the other hand, Farooq et al. [44] identified key strengths in operational skills (9.98/1.81), protection privacy (9.98/1.64), and information searching (8.77/1.67).

However, weaknesses were evident in specific domains. Both studies identified challenges in evaluating the reliability and credibility of health resources from the internet with means of 3.97 (0.64) [49] and 8.25 (1.79) [44]). Tanasombatkul et al. [49] also reported low confidence in using information from the internet to make health decisions (3.98/0.59). Additionally, Farooq et al. [44] highlighted difficulties in determining the relevance of online health data (8.41/2.10).

Factors Associated with Health Literacy

The studies by Zhong et al. [48] and Tanasombatkul et al. [49] identified significant variables associated with health literacy. Zhong et al. [48] reported a significant relationship between HL using c-HALT-8, depressive symptoms, and social support (p < 0.05, β −0.121, SE0.033). Among the sources of social support—family, friends, and teachers—only family support showed a significant negative relationship with depressive symptoms. In addition, the study by Tanasombatkul et al. [49] revealed that e-HL using eHEALS is significantly associated with the duration of internet use (coefficient = 0.23; 95% CI [0.04, 0.42]; p_value = 0.014).

Quality Assessment

The quality assessment, performed with the AXIS tool, revealed that all studies had a very low risk of bias (see Table 2). Overall, the studies demonstrated strong methodological rigor, with minor concerns related to nonresponse and incomplete nonresponder reporting.

Discussion

This systematic review evaluated health literacy among medical students, identified strengths and weaknesses across HLs, and examined key predictors. A comprehensive search across multiple databases resulted in thirteen studies involving 3,647 medical students. The results revealed that general HL, including e-HL among medical students, ranged from moderate to high. Several HL domains were identified as strengths, such as finding and understanding health information, whereas areas of concern included managing one’s health and appraising health information. Additionally, two studies reported that depressive symptoms and social support, as well as duration of internet use, were associated with HL.

Before delving deeper into the interpretation of the findings, it is essential to acknowledge several limitations. First, this review was restricted to studies published in English with full-text access, which may have narrowed the comprehensiveness of the evidence. Second, the review may be affected by publication bias, since studies reporting positive or statistically significant findings tend to have a higher likelihood of being published. Third, the heterogeneity observed in the variability of population characteristics, sample sizes, measurement tools, and outcomes is an important limitation. These variations influence the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the lack of access to disaggregated data from mixed-population studies may have limited the inclusion of diverse perspectives. This could have enhanced the depth of the analysis.

The reviewed studies also presented several limitations. This review included thirteen studies from a limited number of countries, with the majority being single-institution studies that rely on self-reported data, which is prone to bias. The cross-sectional designs restricted causal relationships, while small sampling issues and selection bias may have reduced the representativeness of the results. Online surveys and email recruitment excluded students with limited internet access or lower digital literacy, introducing selection bias. Additionally, variations in measurement tools complicated comparisons between studies. The COVID-19 pandemic has also disrupted recruitment processes and study timelines, contributing to the use of smaller samples and reduced participation rates.

Despite these limitations, this review provides important insights into the current state of health literacy among medical students. This study presents a focused synthesis of the strengths and weaknesses of various HL domains within this population. The reviewed studies revealed that medical students generally have moderate to high levels of health literacy, including e-HL. Their ability to search for and understand health information in general and digital contexts is foundational for future healthcare practitioners. As medical education and healthcare settings evolve, the increasing demand for e-health tools highlights the importance of digital health literacy. Compared with university students from other academic disciplines, medical students exhibit greater general HL [28] and superior e-HL skills [48]. This difference may be explained by formal training with experienced health professionals, better access to reliable information, familiarity with medical terminology, and greater exposure to health-related knowledge and practices [56].

Although the results were largely positive, several HL domains revealed critical weaknesses. Specifically, the ability to appraise health information and evaluate online health resources was lacking, with medical students demonstrating limited critical assessment and application of health information for decision-making. This finding indicates that while medical students can find and understand basic health information, they often struggle to assess its trustworthiness or relevance critically. Such skills are vital for their future roles as healthcare professionals, as they must effectively evaluate the credibility of health sources. The inability to appraise health information is critical for academic success [57] and may also influence the quality of future clinical care. They may be less equipped to communicate accurate information effectively to patients, potentially compromising care outcomes and contributing to public misinformation. This gap likely arises from the complex nature of health information, which requires advanced critical thinking skills to assess properly [57], and an insufficient focus on HL development within medical curricula [45].

Medical students also face challenges managing their health, indicating difficulties translating health information into health maintenance or improvement strategies. This may reflect a gap between knowing and practicing theories, hindering effective self-management behaviors. As a result, they may engage in poor self-care practices [58], face an increased risk of burnout [59], and possibly long-term physical or mental health issues [60]. It could also impair their ability to model healthy behaviors for patients and communities. Studies suggest that medical students tend to disregard or deny health problems, make self-diagnoses, and dislike going for care [61, 62]. In addition, the heavy workloads of medical school may prevent them from leading healthy lifestyles [63].

Building on these concerns, the domain of feeling understood and supported by healthcare providers reveals possible communication and interpersonal skills challenges. These are essential for delivering quality patient-centered care. Effective communication and empathy are fundamental to achieving trust, collaborative behavior, and understanding patients' needs [64]. When patients feel heard, respected, and supported, they are more inclined to follow prescribed treatments, share important health details, and actively participate in shared decision-making [65]. In contrast, deficits in these areas may lead to misunderstanding, decreased satisfaction with care, and poorer health outcomes, especially among patients with low health literacy or complex health needs [10]. Nevertheless, medical students face difficulties with such development because of emotional distress from burnout and symptoms of depression, which, in turn, reduce the level of empathy [66].

In addition, focusing on clinical skills during training could further decrease empathy, as it places greater importance on clinical skills than interpersonal skills [67]. These factors may reduce students' ability to connect with patients and compromise their preparedness for delivering holistic, patient-centered care.

These concerns have implications in the digital health space, as the scores for e-HL indicate modest competencies. Deficits in using and evaluating internet-based health-related resources could hinder readiness for digital healthcare delivery, which is now a salient feature of current practice. In a study by Marsilio et al. [68], 38% of medical students and residents across medical fields expressed concerns regarding the use of digital health information in making decisions due to limited exposure to personal and academic settings. Insufficient training concerning health technologies, negative attitudes toward health informatics, and a lack of evidence-based assessment strategies may hinder medical students’ ability to use digital technology. These factors can impair their ability to provide effective care [69, 70].

These challenges may be further exacerbated by socioeconomic differences, educational frameworks, and health system structures that significantly influence HL outcomes. These influences should be carefully considered when comparing contexts [28]. For example, countries with limited resources or fragmented health systems may not offer real-world applications for students to develop self-management and appraisal skills. These skills are crucial for effectively navigating and appraising health information.

Even though most medical students understand HL as an obstacle to ideal health outcomes, the majority do not realize that it is also a social determinant of health [71]. This oversight may stem from their exposure to the broader social factors influencing health during their education. Understanding HL as a social determinant is vital for assisting future healthcare professionals in addressing the broader systemic issues that impact patient health outcomes.

Implications for Practice

This study underscores the need for curriculum enhancements in medical education. It emphasizes the practical application of HL skills through problem-based learning, service-learning initiatives, and reflective practices to strengthen clinical reasoning and interpersonal competencies. Additionally, improving digital health literacy is essential, as proficiency in assessing and utilizing online health resources is increasingly critical in modern healthcare. To address this gap, integrating digital literacy modules into medical curricula can equip medical students with the necessary skills to effectively use e-health tools, telemedicine platforms, and clinical decision-support systems. Establishing centralized university websites that provide vetted, accessible health information, modeled after pandemic-era information hubs, can facilitate health promotion. Medical educators could implement mandatory workshops on critical appraisals of health information and digital literacy, integrate health literacy competencies into clinical rotation assessments, and utilize standardized patient encounters that simulate real-world evaluations of online health information. Such structured interventions could more effectively equip students to apply HL skills in clinical practice. Furthermore, policymakers should consider incorporating HL training as a mandatory component of medical education accreditation standards. Such an initiative would ensure that future healthcare professionals are effectively trained to navigate complex health challenges and provide high-quality patient care.

Implications for Future Research

Future research should explore the longitudinal effects of HL interventions on medical students and evaluate the effectiveness of specific curricular changes. Comparative studies across diverse linguistic, cultural, and socioeconomic contexts are needed to identify the most effective curriculum designs. Additionally, exploring the influence of cultural, environmental, and socioeconomic factors on HL will provide a broader perspective on the factors influencing it. These research directions will help inform the development of evidence-based practices for incorporating HL into medical education.

Conclusion

This systematic review revealed that medical students generally exhibit moderate to high levels of overall health literacy. The main strength observed, both in general and in digital contexts, was the ability to locate and comprehend health information. However, critical weaknesses remain in their ability to appraise health information, manage their health and interpersonal skills, and lack confidence in online information. The findings emphasize the need for curricular enhancements that focus on building critical thinking and the practical application of health information. Enhancing interpersonal skills is also essential for preparing students for holistic and patient-centered care. Additionally, the growing importance of digital health literacy calls for the need to integrate e-HL training into medical curricula to meet the demands of next-generation health practitioners in modern healthcare. Socioeconomic and cultural factors should be considered to ensure that HL interventions are contextually relevant and equitable. This review contributes to the literature by offering a targeted synthesis of health literacy among medical students, a group often underrepresented in broader HL research. It also identifies specific areas in need of development. Future research should investigate longitudinal changes and predictors of HL, and examine its role as a social determinant of health to guide curriculum design and policy development.
