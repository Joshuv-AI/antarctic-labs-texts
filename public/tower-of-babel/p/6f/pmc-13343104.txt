Patient perspectives to inform the implementation of tobacco use treatment in Lebanon’s safety net primary care system: a qualitative study
Authors: Sara Tohme, Dima Bteddini, Maya Romani, Tamar Kabakian-Khasholian, Lama El Kadi, Ruba Abla, Catherine El Ashkar, Magda Montague, Ryan Theis, Jihan Khaywa, Mona H Osman, Kenneth Ward, Donna R Shelley, Rima Nakkash, Ramzi G Salloum, Jennifer H LeLaurin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo inform the adaptation of an existing tobacco use treatment (TUT) programme into a contextually appropriate service for Lebanon’s safety-net primary care system.DesignIn-person focus group discussions with an in-depth thematic analysis.SettingTwo primary healthcare centres in Beirut, LebanonParticipants61 individuals who smoked cigarettes (n=36) and waterpipe (n=25).ResultsThe analysis revealed four primary themes: 1) smoking as a coping mechanism for national crisis, 2) lack of perceived fit of the programme with the Lebanese context, 3) differing perceptions of harm and addiction by tobacco product, 4) perceived effectiveness of programme components. Participants reported that smoking helped them cope with stress resulting from the national crisis and felt that patient education material did not reflect local norms around tobacco use. Participants’ views on the relative harm and addictiveness of cigarettes and waterpipe were often discussed in the context of differing use patterns. Addressing mental health, using competent and non-judgmental counsellors, and sharing real-life success stories were recommended to improve engagement in TUT.ConclusionsTobacco is embedded within the Lebanese social and cultural life. Our findings inform implementation efforts by identifying contextual needs specific to the Lebanese population that smokes. Tailoring TUTs can increase acceptability, effectiveness and sustainability of smoking cessation efforts in Lebanon and similar low-resource contexts.

Introduction

Smoking remains the leading preventable cause of death globally.1 2 Although considerable progress has been achieved in curbing the tobacco epidemic in many parts of the world, smoking rates remain high in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).3 4 The WHO endorses the integration of smoking cessation advice into primary care settings, easily accessible and free telephone-based counselling, and low-cost pharmacotherapy as components of population-level tobacco use treatment (TUT).5 6 These treatments are highly cost-effective5 6; however, their evidence base primarily comes from high-income countries (HICs), and there is limited evidence of their effectiveness in LMICs.7 8 TUTs developed in HICs often fail to account for the differences in health system infrastructure and sociocultural norms present in LMIC settings.3 9

In Lebanon, the tobacco use burden is exceptionally high, with over 70% of individuals reporting current smoking of cigarettes or waterpipe, with a prevalence of 48.6% among males and 21.5% among females.1012 This has contributed to escalating rates of cancer, cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases, which together exceed 60% of all deaths in Lebanon.13 14 Lebanon also reports the highest age-standardised lung cancer incidence rate among females in the Eastern Mediterranean region (approximately 13.4 per 100,000) and the second highest among males (25–37 per 100,000), with an average of 950 new cases diagnosed annually.15 16 Access to and uptake of evidence-based cessation services remain limited due to a combination of structural and resource-related barriers. Lebanon lacks a systematic, nationally integrated smoking cessation programme, and cessation support is not routinely offered in primary care settings. Access to behavioural counselling and pharmacotherapy also remains limited, fragmented and unaffordable.17 Political instability and economic collapse have further strained the country’s public health infrastructure, reducing its capacity to deliver preventive services, including tobacco control.1820

As prior research on population-based TUT has predominantly involved patients in HICs, there is limited understanding of patient perceptions and attitudes in LMICs.21 Being the ‘ultimate stakeholders’ in their healthcare decisions, patients play a pivotal role in the successful implementation of TUT programmes. It is essential to understand how patients perceive these TUT programmes and their readiness to accept and adopt them. Engaging patients can help identify key barriers to engagement and implementation, prioritise locally relevant modifications and foster their buy-in. Such stakeholder-informed adaptation is crucial to ensure that TUT services are not only effective but also contextually relevant, feasible, sustainable and scalable in LMIC settings like Lebanon.2224 This study aims to contribute to that effort by identifying patient-informed strategies and recommendations to inform the adaptation and implementation of population-based TUTs in Lebanon and other LMICs.

Methodology

We conducted in-person focus groups in May 2023 for preparing for the Project PHOENICS (PHOne ENabled Implementation of Cessation Support) trial, which evaluates the comparative effectiveness of a multi-component evidence-based TUT programme in Lebanon’s safety net primary care network (NCT05628389).25 The current study aims to inform adaptations to the TUT programme in use by the American University of Beirut Medical Centre, which is modelled after behavioural TUTs used in HICs, into a contextually appropriate phone counselling service using patient feedback. The programme includes eight sessions of counselling with a certified Tobacco Treatment Specialist, nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) and patient education materials. This programme, while designed for the general public and targeting individuals seeking to quit cigarette or waterpipe smoking, was developed within an academic medical setting. As such, adaptation is necessary to ensure alignment with the needs of the safety net primary care network, which serves more than 50% of the population. The American University of Beirut Institutional Review Board (SBS-2022-043) approved this study, and the findings were reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist (see online supplemental file 2).

Conceptual framework

This study was organised by the Exploration, Preparation, Implementation, Sustainment (EPIS) framework, which is used to inform the process of implementing evidence-based interventions and factors that influence the implementation process and outcomes.26 This study focuses on the second phase of this framework, the Preparation phase, to identify the needs, context, and determinants influencing the implementation of population-based TUT in Lebanon. We used three EPIS domains: outer context factors (eg, service environment), innovation factors (characteristics of intervention), and inner context factors (eg, individual characteristics).27

Participants and recruitment

We used purposive sampling to recruit patients from two primary healthcare centres in Beirut, Lebanon’s capital and largest city. The two centres were selected based on their high patient volume and the diverse patient populations they serve, including individuals from underserved communities and various socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds. Patients were considered eligible if they received care at the primary healthcare centres, were aged ≥18 years, and smoked cigarettes daily (≥2 cigarettes per day) or waterpipe tobacco (≥1 session per week) regularly. Participants were enrolled in person. Enrolled participants signed a written consent and completed a survey on their demographics and tobacco use history (see online supplemental file 3). We recruited a total of 63 individuals, of which 61 attended focus groups. We conducted five focus groups with individuals who primarily smoked cigarettes and four with individuals who primarily used waterpipe. The focus groups ranged from five to nine participants in size. Participants were compensated $10 for their participation.

Procedure

Two team members (RA and JK), a researcher with prior experience in qualitative data collection and a smoking cessation counsellor, respectively, facilitated the discussions. During the focus groups, facilitators provided participants with information detailing the reasons for the research, introduced the pre-existing TUT programme, and shared patient education materials. Participants were asked about their perceptions of the health effects of smoking, experiences with quitting smoking, thoughts and experiences regarding TUT programmes, and suggestions for modifying the programme. The focus group discussion guide and patient education materials are presented in online supplemental file 1. Focus groups were conducted in Arabic, in a private room in one of the primary healthcare centres, and lasted between 90 min and 2 hours. Focus groups were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in Arabic, translated into English, and back-translated to ensure accuracy.

Data analysis

In the first stage of analysis, we used rapid qualitative analysis procedures to summarise transcripts and inform adaptations to the programme prior to the launch of the implementation trial.28 29 Summaries generated during the rapid analysis stage informed the development of the initial codebook used for a second stage of in-depth thematic analysis.30 The analysis team was led by a team member with expertise in qualitative research (JHL). It included master’s-level research coordinators and a graduate student with training in qualitative analysis (DB, CA, LK and RA). The analysis approach was guided by the EPIS framework and involved iterative stages of deductive and inductive coding, enabling the identification and description of emerging themes.31 This combined deductive and inductive approach allowed for a comprehensive exploration of attitudes, barriers and facilitators beyond predetermined constructs. Two team members independently coded each transcript. The coding team met regularly to resolve discrepancies by consensus, discuss salient topic codes, and refine the coding framework. Data were analysed using NVivo 14.32 Saturation was reached after analysing five focus groups, after which no additional codebook revisions were made.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Findings

Participants

We conducted nine focus groups with 61 participants (table 1). Five focus groups included individuals who self-identified as primarily using cigarettes (n=36) and four included individuals who primarily used waterpipe (n=25). Five (8.2%) participants were dual users of both products and six 9.8% used other nicotine products (e-cigarettes, IQOS). Most participants had completed at least high school or vocational education (59.0%) and were employed full-time (52.5%). About half (50.8%) were uncertain about their readiness to quit, and a similar proportion had previously attempted cessation (52.5%).

Themes

Five main themes emerged: 1) smoking as a coping mechanism for national crisis, 2) lack of perceived fit of the programme with the Lebanese context, 3) differing perceptions of harm and addiction by tobacco product, 4) perceived effectiveness of programme components, and 5) participant recommendations. Participant recommendations to address barriers

Outer context (service environment)

Smoking as a coping mechanism for national crisis

Participants discussed how macro-level socio-economic factors impacted individual mental health and served as key barriers to smoking cessation. They explained that the economic crisis in Lebanon and widespread unemployment had brought psychosocial stressors into their lives, and smoking helped them cope with problems such as anxiety and depression. Participants also explained that in the context of the economic collapse, smoking was one of the few affordable means of coping, given the low cost of tobacco products in Lebanon.

One participant conceded, “We have poor mental health, and the general situation is hopeless. I have nothing to do to distract myself from the cigarette, so I smoke it for fun.” Some participants added that their prior cessation attempts failed due to exposure to stressful situations, further diminishing their confidence in quitting in the current circumstances. As one participant stated, “If it weren't for the tragic situation in the country, I could quit smoking without anyone advising me (to quit)”.

Innovation factors

Perceived fit of the programme with the Lebanese context

When reviewing the existing patient education materials, participants noted that some of the content was inappropriate for the situation in Lebanon. They expressed that the avoidance-based recommendations in the programme materials were incompatible with local social norms surrounding tobacco use in Lebanon. They noted the high prevalence of smoking in Lebanon made it nearly impossible to avoid exposure to others who smoke when trying to quit. Participants also explained that tobacco use is deeply embedded in Lebanese social gatherings and hospitality norms, and it would not be appropriate to ask others in their social circle to refrain from smoking. One participant who used waterpipe explained: “Avoiding people who smoke is difficult, as sometimes you are forced to welcome a visitor who smokes, or to visit someone who smokes; I cannot tell them not to smoke because I'm trying to stop smoking.” Other strategies for managing triggers presented in the patient education materials, such as taking showers or exercising, were perceived as impractical given Lebanon’s electricity crisis and chronic power outages, which frequently limit access to hot water, good lighting, and elevator use. Participants also cited poor urban infrastructure and high levels of air pollution as barriers to safe outdoor exercise. Some participants also thought telephone-based counselling did not fit the Lebanese reality. Although some participants saw potential benefits in remote counselling due to its convenience and accessibility, especially given transportation costs, several noted that the unreliable mobile communication network would be a barrier.

Inner context (individual characteristics)

Perceptions of harm and addiction by tobacco product

While most participants acknowledged that the use of both cigarettes and waterpipe led to poor health outcomes, their views differed regarding which product was more harmful. This was often linked to differences in the typical duration and frequency of use of each product. While one participant who used waterpipe stated directly that “(waterpipe) is more harmful”, others felt that the limited frequency of waterpipe use made it less harmful than daily use of cigarettes. Waterpipe is usually available in restaurants, cafés and can be smoked at home but cigarettes are easier to carry around and use everywhere. For instance, one participant who smokes waterpipe noted, "They say (waterpipe) is more harmful, but I think smoking (waterpipe) is for half an hour or an hour, but smoking cigarettes happens all the time. So, I think that the (waterpipe) smoking effects are less harmful than smoking cigarettes.” Some participants contextualised potential harms, arguing that if waterpipe use relieves stress and is not addictive, it may be ‘more beneficial than harmful’. Participants also distinguished between addiction and habit in relation to the use of different products of tobacco. Some participants perceived cigarettes as being inherently more addictive and harder to quit than waterpipe smoking, while others argued that waterpipe smoking was equally, if not more, addictive than cigarettes. One participant insisted that their use of cigarettes was merely ‘a habit and not an addiction’. Even among those who claimed they were not addicted, many confessed to suffering from significant withdrawal symptoms on cessation. One participant who smoked waterpipe shared: “When I stopped smoking, I suffered a lot. Although I was not addicted, I had symptoms like people who take drugs.”

Perceived effectiveness of the intervention

Participants identified multiple factors that affected their perception of programme effectiveness, including limited familiarity with TUT programmes and a lack of trust in the supporting evidence base. Most participants were unaware of TUT programmes or had limited exposure to them. During discussions of the evidence base for phone counselling and NRT, participants inquired whether the supporting studies had been conducted in Lebanon. When informed that the trials were from other countries, participants expressed scepticism about the transferability of the evidence to the Lebanese context, with one participant stating, “theoretical and practical are different,” and another explaining, “in other (countries), they do not have our problems. They have social security and health insurance.” In contrast to those expressing doubt, some participants showed excitement for access to new treatment, as one participant shared, “I may participate, why not? I love to try, honestly. I know about research studies at the American University of Beirut, so I always like to try new experiences. I try it and see if it will help me; I surely have no problem”.

Participant recommendations

Program content and delivery

Given the widespread depression and anxiety resulting from the nation’s economic collapse, participants emphasised the importance of addressing the relationship between mental health and tobacco use within the programme. They noted that personalised guidance from counsellors was essential to encourage participants to complete the full programme and emphasised the importance of the counsellor’s empathetic communication style. They stressed the need for the counsellor to be positive, non-judgmental and clear in explaining information. Participants also suggested modifications to educational materials to better suit their needs and social reality, including tips and guidance on how to manage triggers related to constant exposure to smoking and peer pressure. Some participants suggested video calls and viewed them more engaging than regular phone calls, as they offer a degree of face-to-face interaction, although others noted that poor internet connectivity could pose a barrier.

Reach and retention

Participants cited several ways to enhance programme reach and retention. They suggested sharing positive experiences and testimonials from individuals who had successfully quit through the programme to encourage participation. Participants also identified several existing programme components as facilitators, which could be further emphasised to enhance engagement. They believed the programme’s cost-free nature had the potential to increase reach by eliminating the financial constraint, particularly in light of ongoing economic hardship. Additionally, they viewed physician referral to be the most trusted and influential way into the programme, with some individuals noting that a doctor’s recommendation legitimised the programme’s value. Finally, continued participation in the programme was seen as contingent on the psychological benefits derived from participation. Several noted that beyond information and advice, the programme should provide emotional and mental health support to sustain their engagement.

Discussion

This study provides important insights into adapting TUT programmes for low-resource contexts. Our findings reveal that interest and confidence in smoking cessation in Lebanon are hindered by the country’s ongoing socio-economic crisis. Participants identified multiple barriers to cessation, including external stressors and social norms surrounding tobacco use. This study is among the few to explore varying perceptions of people who smoke based on product type in an LMIC setting. While most participants viewed cigarettes as addictive, waterpipe smoking was often framed as a social or leisure activity. Our findings provide patient-centred recommendations for enhancing the effectiveness of TUT programmes in resource-restricted settings.

Lebanon’s economic crisis and its impact on mental health emerged as significant barriers to tobacco cessation. The country’s economic collapse and political instability have resulted in socioeconomic disruption characterised by high inflation, social unrest, poverty and financial hardship. These cataclysmic conditions have profoundly affected mental health, increasing anxiety, stress and depression.3335 In our focus groups, smoking was commonly referred to as a mechanism for coping with daily psychological distress. Tobacco products in Lebanon remain highly affordable, even post-crisis, with cigarette prices ranging from approximately $0.35 to $2.60 USD per pack and waterpipe tobacco (mu‘assal) ranging from about $0.40 to $2.70 USD per package.36 With low income and a lack of affordable alternatives for stress management, participants relied on smoking to alleviate external pressures, making cessation attempts especially challenging. This finding aligns with existing literature that links stress to impulsive decision-making and reduced control over quit attempts, increasing relapse vulnerability in both high- and low-resource settings.37 38 In Lebanon, these dynamics transcend individual struggles to population-level crises and instability, leading to a collective large-scale prevalence of mental health issues, fatalism, and general apathy about quitting. This intricate cyclical interplay between crisis, mental health and tobacco use is similarly documented in other LMICs. The WHO World Health Survey, a large cross-sectional, community-based study of over 200 000 adults, showed a strong relationship between perceived stress and smoking across 41 LMICs spanning Africa, Asia, the Americas, and Europe.39 In such countries, smoking tends to serve as a coping mechanism for population-wide stress, reinforcing a pattern of dependence and relapse. While TUT programmes typically include strategies for stress management and trigger control, these approaches may yield limited efficacy when mental health problems operate at this broader, societal scale. Participants in this study recommended the inclusion of mental health support with TUT programmes, suggesting that combining TUTs with population-level psychosocial and stress-management strategies, such as mindfulness or cognitive behavioural therapy, could improve resilience, cessation outcomes, and programme sustainability.4042 Notably, the focus groups were conducted before the 2024 War in Lebanon, a contextual shift that has likely exacerbated stress and anxiety on an even larger scale. Our findings highlight that the normalisation of smoking in Lebanese society is a key barrier to cessation. Despite a legal minimum smoking age of 18,43 cigarette-smoking participants in this study reported a mean smoking initiation age of 17.6 years. Widespread tobacco use, cultural norms, hospitality traditions, and wide availability contribute to the entrenchment of tobacco use within everyday life in the Eastern Mediterranean region.44 This cultural underpinning is not necessarily present in high-income countries. In many countries in Western Europe and North America, smoking is no longer regarded as a social norm. Instead, the strong tobacco control policies, widespread public health campaigns and shifts towards health and wellness have contributed to the denormalisation of tobacco use.45 46 This divergence in cultural norms plays an important role in designing and implementing TUT programmes in LMICs, necessitating contextual adaptation to reflect local beliefs, social practices and structural realities rather than solely replicating models developed in high-income settings.

To our knowledge, our study is the first to compare the differences in perceptions between cigarette and waterpipe smoking in Lebanon. Many participants perceived waterpipe smoking as less harmful than cigarette smoking, framing their assessments of harm around the duration and frequency of use rather than the toxicological properties of the substances themselves. While participants also generally acknowledged the addictive properties of both products, many discussed ‘addiction’ in relation to cigarette use and ‘habit’ when discussing waterpipe. This aligns with prior work in LMICs showing that lay understandings of tobacco risk are frequently shaped by patterns of consumption and personal experience rather than medical evidence,47 48 which shows that even intermittent waterpipe use leads to substantial toxicant exposure comparable to or exceeding that of cigarettes.49 Research conducted in other LMICs, including the Eastern Mediterranean Region and Vietnam, also shows that people who smoke demonstrate divergent perceptions regarding the harm and addictiveness of cigarettes compared with waterpipe tobacco.48 50 Accordingly, programmes developed to address cigarette smoking may be ineffective for waterpipe smoking. A 2023 Cochrane review of waterpipe-specific TUTs identified five studies that included face-to-face behavioural interventions. Four interventions were based on best practice cessation interventions for people who smoke combustible cigarettes, while only one study adapted the intervention to waterpipe users. A meta-analysis of these studies showed low‐certainty evidence that behavioural interventions increase the chances of achieving waterpipe smoking abstinence.51 Some reasons noted by the review pertain to the indirectness of TUTs to waterpipe smoking, imprecision of results, risk of bias, small sample sizes, short follow-up periods and limited evidence. A study in Lebanon found that 61.9% of primary care physicians believe that the techniques currently used for cigarette smoking cessation are not entirely appropriate or applicable for waterpipe cessation, indicating a lack of consensus on the transferability of cessation methods.52 With varying perceptions among smokers using cigarettes or waterpipes, providing targeted treatment is needed for a better rigorous understanding of effective and sustainable TUTs for each tobacco product.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, social desirability bias may have influenced participant feedback, although we encouraged open and honest discussions. Second, the focus groups were mixed gender, preventing a comparative analysis of perceptions among men and women. Third, although we aimed to target centres that serve diverse patient populations, participants were recruited from only two centres, which might limit the transferability of our findings.

Conclusion

Our findings emphasise the necessity of tailoring TUT to align with the lived realities of people who smoke in Lebanon and other LMICs. Multicomponent approaches that incorporate culturally relevant education, accessible and patient-centred counselling modalities and targeted support to address both cigarette and waterpipe smoking are essential to strengthen motivation, promote adherence and ensure long-term cessation success.
