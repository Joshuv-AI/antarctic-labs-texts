An interactive anatomy tool to boost clinical reasoning and knowledge retention for preclinical medical students: a preliminary evaluation
Authors: Pooja Rao, Tejas Ravindra, Kun Li, Gail Elliott, Grace Pinhal-Enfield
Published: 2026

Introduction

Anatomy is consistently regarded as one of the most demanding components of the preclinical medical curriculum, owing to its extensive content, complex spatial relationships, and direct relevance to clinical reasoning and practice. The progressive compression of instructional time has further intensified these challenges, requiring students to acquire foundational knowledge and apply it diagnostically within increasingly constrained timeframes. Over the past two decades, medical curricula worldwide have continued to condense the basic sciences, particularly anatomy, with reported reductions in dedicated anatomy instruction time by as much as 50–80% compared with earlier curricular models [1–3]. This restructuring places greater demands on learners to rapidly develop spatial competence and integrate anatomical knowledge with clinical reasoning in a more condensed time period. When instruction relies predominantly on traditional, two-dimensional lecture formats, students may struggle to translate memorized structures into clinically meaningful understanding and application. In contrast, active, student-centered strategies, including drawing exercises, mobile applications, and structured activities, such as neural pathway simulations and functional application exercises, have been associated with greater engagement and improved educational outcomes [4–7]. For example, Narayan et al. [7] demonstrated significantly improved learning among students who participated in a facilitated group where instructors connected cortical function to clinical relevance. Such findings underscore the value of diverse instructional methodologies in supporting anatomy learning within compressed curricula. Therefore, while high-quality static images and expert instruction remain highly effective in anatomy education, there is increasing interest in tools that support active and self-directed learning. Computer-based learning (CBL) tools and three-dimensional visualization platforms further complement lectures and human donor dissection. Although not substitutes for hands-on anatomical study, these technologies can enhance spatial reasoning, promote self-directed learning, and reinforce clinical integration [8–10]. Contemporary platforms, including Anatomage, Biodigital Human, and Zygote Body, illustrate the capacity of interactive 3D resources to increase flexibility and immersion in neuroanatomy education [11–15]. However, commercially available platforms present limitations for institutional adoption. They are costly, require external licensing, and offer limited adaptability to institutional curricular priorities. Yet, as medical education increasingly emphasizes early clinical exposure, competency-based progression, and longitudinal skill development, there is a growing need for institutionally developed tools that can be iteratively refined, customized, and aligned with evolving educational objectives.

Innovation and purpose

Although existing platforms provide valuable anatomical visualization, few explicitly model the functional consequences of peripheral nerve lesions, an essential component of developing diagnostic reasoning. The present project was conceived to address this gap while simultaneously creating a sustainable, institutionally controlled educational resource that can be modified in alignment with evolving curricular goals. Building on contemporary advances in interactive anatomy education, the first author, a third-year medical student, developed a web-based platform that enables learners to simulate nerve lesions within a three-dimensional environment and observe the resulting motor, sensory, and reflex deficits in real time. A central feature of the platform is its dynamic lesion simulation, which demonstrates how specific nerve injuries translate into characteristic clinical findings, such as weakness or sensory loss. An accompanying lesion map organizes injuries according to anatomical location in the upper limb, reinforcing spatial understanding and clinical correlation. By directly linking anatomical structures to their functional and clinical consequences, the tool supports a deeper conceptualization of lesion localization. The interactive design permits learners to progress at their own pace, revisit complex concepts, and visualize neuroanatomical pathways. As an internally developed resource, the platform can be iteratively refined by faculty and students to reflect curricular revisions, expand on additional anatomical regions, and align with emerging clinical priorities, offering long-term flexibility for student learning and engagement.

Rationale for upper limb focus

Upper limb nerve lesions were selected as the initial focus of the application for several reasons. First, feedback from prior cohorts of second- and third-year medical students consistently identified this topic as particularly challenging. The brachial plexus and its terminal branches require advanced spatial reasoning to understand how lesion location translates into specific motor, sensory, and reflex deficits. Second, upper limb neuropathies are frequently assessed on licensing examinations, including the USMLE, and are commonly encountered early in clinical training, making the topic a strong target for educational innovation. Third, the anatomical complexity of the upper limb, with its layered motor and sensory distributions, provided an ideal context for evaluating the simulation’s ability to clarify structure-function relationships before extending the platform to additional anatomical regions.

Materials and methods

Ethics and participants

The study was approved by the Human Research Protection Program (IRB #Pro2025001729). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. First-year medical students enrolled in the anatomy course at Robert Wood Johnson Medical School, Rutgers University, were invited to participate via email, which emphasized the voluntary nature of the participation. A total of 35 first-year medical students participated in the study. Upon arrival, students were randomly allocated to one of two instructional rooms, with room assignment determining instructional condition. This resulted in allocation to either the 3D Simulation Group (n = 18) or the 2D Review Group (n = 17). All participants completed the instructional session; however, complete pre- and post-test data were available for 33 participants (3D Simulation Group: n = 18; 2D Review Group: n = 15), who were included in the primary analysis of knowledge gains.

Study design

This single-session pilot study examined the effectiveness of a three-dimensional (3D) interactive anatomy application compared with traditional PowerPoint-based didactic instruction for teaching upper limb nerve lesions (2D). The 2D Review Group participated in a PowerPoint-based session in which slides were described and advanced by a student facilitator. The facilitator’s role was limited to navigation of pre-existing course materials, and no additional instruction, explanation, or teaching beyond the slide content was provided. Participants in this group engaged with the material while completing the same guided worksheet as the 3D Simulation Group. Both groups engaged with identical content and worksheets within a structured, time-matched session, differing only in the mode of content interaction (static slides versus interactive simulation). All participants had previously completed the required components of the anatomy curriculum, including lectures, laboratory sessions, and small-group activities, before enrolling in this optional study.

The study was designed to determine whether interactive simulation could enhance the integration of foundational anatomical knowledge with clinical reasoning during the preclinical phase of medical education. The 3D simulation tool was developed in response to a recurring learning challenge identified through informal discussions and feedback from second- and third-year medical students. Learners consistently reported difficulty translating their understanding of upper limb nerve anatomy into accurate lesion localization and clinical interpretation. Although traditional lecture slides provided foundational content, students described persistent challenges in visualizing three-dimensional spatial relationships and linking anatomical structures to clinical presentations. This feedback informed the development of the workshop intervention, which specifically targeted upper limb nerve lesions to strengthen clinical integration of anatomy knowledge.

Application design and development

A distinguishing aspect of this project is that the three-dimensional (3D) simulation application was fully conceptualized, designed, and developed by a medical student (first author), drawing on direct experience within the preclinical anatomy curriculum. This student-led development process ensured that the platform was intentionally structured to address commonly reported learning challenges, particularly the difficulty of reconstructing three-dimensional nerve pathways from two-dimensional lectures. The web-based platform was developed using Cursor [16], an AI-assisted coding interface, which facilitated the creation of customized interactive features, allowing users to manipulate upper limb anatomy and observe motor, sensory, and reflex deficits in real time following specific nerve injuries (see Fig. 1). The platform incorporates fully manipulable three-dimensional anatomical models, allowing users to rotate, zoom, and isolate structures within a spatially accurate environment. Nerve pathways are visualized in three dimensions, enabling learners to explore anatomical relationships from multiple orientations and observe how lesion location alters functional outcomes. Anatomical models were constructed and animated in Blender [17], incorporating labeled three-dimensional structures that identify lesion sites and dynamically simulate the associated clinical manifestations (See Fig. 2). Under the standard usage of educational purposes, three-dimensional anatomical models were obtained from publicly available resources and supplemented with a commercially licensed écorché model created by Lashko [18]. The model includes a fully textured skeleton and major muscles with individually named anatomical structures, allowing isolation and animation of specific muscle groups.

Content validation and iteration

Educational content was derived from validated resources, including the University of British Columbia Anatomy Tools [19], and instructor-approved lecture materials from our school. All content was reviewed by RWJMS anatomy faculty to ensure anatomical and clinical accuracy. To enhance usability and instructional clarity, the prototype application underwent pilot testing with third-year medical students. Feedback regarding model visualization, interface design, and the accuracy of functional mapping informed iterative refinements, resulting in improved navigation and clearer representation of lesion localization in relation to corresponding clinical manifestations (see Fig. 3).

The study

First-year medical students were randomly allocated by room to either a 2D Review Group or a 3D Simulation Group. The 2D Review Group participated in a PowerPoint-based review (PPT) session derived from existing course materials, focusing on upper limb nerve lesions and their associated clinical deficits. The 3D Simulation Group engaged with a custom, interactive web-based platform that facilitated dynamic exploration of nerve pathways, lesion localization, and corresponding motor, sensory, and reflex impairments. To ensure instructional consistency, both groups completed identical guided worksheets during the session. The guided worksheet was designed to promote active application of anatomical knowledge and included structured prompts requiring students to identify nerve pathways, localize lesion sites, and predict associated motor, sensory, and reflex deficits. Questions were aligned with session learning objectives and required learners to integrate anatomical structure with clinical presentation.

Before the activity, participants completed a baseline survey administered through Qualtrics [20]. The survey assessed prior anatomy coursework beyond the standard medical curriculum, typical study strategies, previous exposure to interactive or three-dimensional anatomy tools, and self-reported confidence in identifying upper limb nerves and applying anatomical knowledge to clinical scenarios, measured using 5-point Likert scales. Baseline knowledge was evaluated using a five-item multiple-choice quiz derived from course lecture content.

Following the activity, participants completed the same five-item multiple-choice quiz to assess changes in clinical anatomy knowledge. The post-activity survey also included six intervention-specific items (see Table 1), comprising Likert-scale questions evaluating clarity, visual effectiveness, clinical relevance, and perceived usefulness relative to customary study methods, as well as open-ended questions soliciting qualitative feedback regarding helpful features, areas of difficulty, and suggestions for improvement.

Post-session Survey Items

Each session lasted one hour. Approximately ten minutes were allocated at the outset for completion of the pre-test and ten minutes at the conclusion for the post-test, leaving approximately forty minutes for engagement with the assigned instructional materials. This standardized structure ensured equivalent exposure across groups. During the activity period, the 2D Review Group reviewed nerve lesion content using PowerPoint slides, whereas the 3D Simulation Group explored the same material through the interactive platform. Immediately following the session, all participants completed the post-test and provided structured feedback regarding their learning experience.

Data analyses

Quantitative and qualitative data were analyzed to evaluate both educational effectiveness and learner experience associated with the 3D simulation. Quantitative measures included pre- and post-activity quiz scores, per-item confidence ratings, overall confidence assessments, and self-reported prior anatomy background. The primary analytic focus was to compare knowledge gains across instructional approaches.

For each participant, a change in score was calculated as the difference between the pre-test and post-test scores. Given the modest sample size and the absence of clear normality in change of score distributions, a Mann-Whitney U test was employed to compare learning gains between groups. This non-parametric approach was used because it does not assume normality and allows for the evaluation of whether the distribution of score change differs across instructional approaches. All statistical tests were two-tailed, as no directional hypothesis was specified a priori. Effect size was calculated using r, defined as the absolute value of the standardized test statistic (z) divided by the square root of the total sample size. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional benchmarks (0.1 = small; 0.3 = medium; 0.5 = large).

Open-ended survey responses were analyzed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) following Braun and Clarke [21, 22]. Responses to four free-text prompts were initially reviewed separately for the 3D Simulation Group (n = 18) and 2D Review Group (n = 17). Codes were generated inductively from participant responses and iteratively refined into broader interpretive themes. Themes were subsequently compared across instructional conditions to identify convergent and divergent patterns related to perceived helpful features, areas of confusion, suggested improvements, and future educational priorities. Consistent with the reflexive interpretive nature of this qualitative approach, the emphasis was on meaning generation and thematic interpretation rather than coder agreement metrics.

Results

A total of 35 students participated in the study. Of these, 33 participants had complete pre- and post-test data and were included in the knowledge gain analysis (3D Simulation Group: n = 18; 2D Review Group: n = 15). Survey response rates differed slightly, with 18 responses from the 3D Simulation Group and 17 from the 2D Review Group. Differences in sample size across analyses reflect incomplete pre- and post-test data for two participants.

The 3D Simulation Group demonstrated a higher median change score (median = 30.00, IQR = 60.00) compared with the 2D Review Group (median = 20.00, IQR = 10.00). Following the intervention, both groups showed similar post-test performance (3D Simulation Group: mean = 80.00; 2D Review Group: mean = 81.33). Within this single-session context, students in the simulation group reached a similar level of performance despite lower initial scores.

Open-ended survey responses

Open-ended survey responses were analyzed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) to examine learner perceptions across instructional conditions. Responses from the 3D Simulation Group (n = 18) and 2D Review Group (n = 17) were initially analyzed separately and then compared across conditions to identify convergent and divergent themes. Themes were organized according to the four survey prompts, allowing comparison of perceived strengths, areas of confusion, suggested improvements, and future topic priorities across instructional conditions.

Participants in the 3D Simulation Group most frequently emphasized visual-spatial support, interactivity, and usability as helpful features, whereas participants in the 2D Review Group more often highlighted practice questions, structured organization, and visual clarity. Participants in the 3D Simulation Group most commonly identified technical friction and organizational issues as barriers, while PowerPoint participants primarily noted pacing concerns. Suggested improvements included technical refinements, expanded anatomical content, and additional practice opportunities, with lower limb anatomy emerging as the most frequently requested future topic across both groups (Table 4).

Discussion

The present study evaluated whether a student-developed three-dimensional (3D) interactive simulation application could enhance integration of anatomical knowledge and clinical reasoning beyond a traditional PowerPoint-based review within a compressed preclinical curriculum. Although both instructional approaches were associated with improved post-session performance, several findings warrant closer consideration.

First, while the differences in learning gains between groups did not reach statistical significance, students in the 3D Simulation Group showed a larger median change score and achieved post-test performance similar to peers in the 2D Review Group, despite entering with lower baseline scores. The 2D Review Group began with higher pre-test mean scores, yet both groups showed similar post-test levels. Because baseline group differences were present and the analyses were unadjusted, this convergence should be interpreted cautiously and cannot be attributed solely to the intervention. However, the larger mean change score observed in the 3D Simulation Group (30.00 vs. 16.00) may reflect both the instructional modality and a greater opportunity for improvement among students with lower initial knowledge. In the context of compressed anatomy curricula, where rapid integration of structure and function is essential, this pattern remains educationally relevant as an area for further investigation. Although the calculated effect size was small, the direction of change aligns with the conceptual premise underpinning the application’s development: that interactive, spatially dynamic modeling may particularly benefit students struggling to reconstruct three-dimensional relationships from two-dimensional lectures. Future investigations with larger sample sizes should consider stratified randomization based on baseline performance or the use of analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with pre-test scores included as a covariate to better account for initial group differences.

Item-level responses were largely comparable between instructional modalities. Following correction for multiple comparisons, no items demonstrated statistically significant differences, and observed effect sizes were small at most, indicating minimal practical differences in reported perceptions. However, qualitative findings provided important nuance and highlighted differing learner priorities by instructional modality. Participants in the 3D Simulation Group emphasized visual-spatial support, interactivity, and intuitive design, whereas participants in the 2D Review Group more frequently highlighted the value of structured summaries and practice questions. Notably, participants described the simulation as supporting visual-spatial understanding and interactive exploration of lesion concepts. This distinction, moving from memorization of distributions to a conceptual understanding of lesion localization, represents an important threshold in the development of diagnostic reasoning. While traditional lectures efficiently convey content, they may not consistently facilitate dynamic visualization of how lesion levels alter function. The ability to manipulate anatomical structures and immediately observe functional consequences appears to have supported conceptual integration rather than superficial recall. It is important to note that high-quality static images, particularly when delivered by experienced instructors, can effectively support anatomy learning. The present study does not directly compare interactive simulation with expert-led instruction, and therefore cannot determine the relative contribution of interactivity, three-dimensional visualization, or instructional expertise. Rather, the findings suggest that interactive, self-directed tools may offer complementary value by enabling learners to actively explore spatial relationships and simulate clinical scenarios.

These findings align with established principles of effective medical education. Prior evidence suggests that active manipulation of three-dimensional models can enhance spatial understanding and promote deeper conceptual learning. In contrast, the present findings also highlight the continued value of structured review materials and guided reinforcement for some learners.

An important distinction highlighted by the present findings is the role of the platform as a self-directed learning tool. Unlike static slide-based review, which may rely on passive recognition in the absence of animation or instructor guidance, the interactive simulation enables learners to actively manipulate anatomical structures, explore spatial relationships, and iteratively test their understanding through lesion simulation. This form of engagement may support deeper conceptual processing, particularly during independent study, where opportunities for guided explanation are limited. Importantly, while enhanced slide-based instruction incorporating animation and annotation may offer similar benefits, the present study suggests that interactive, self-directed tools provide a complementary approach to reinforcing anatomical understanding outside of formal teaching sessions.

Minor technical barriers (e.g., slider responsiveness, setup time) were also identified but were logistical rather than pedagogical in nature. These issues are readily addressable through iterative refinement and do not diminish the perceived educational value of the tool. Indeed, students frequently recommended expansion to additional anatomical regions and integration of practice questions, reflecting learner enthusiasm for mastery-oriented enhancements. There is also a distinguishing contribution of this project: its student-led development model. The platform was conceptualized and built by a medical student using Cursor, an AI-assisted coding environment, in combination with Blender for 3D modeling. This approach demonstrates that, with faculty mentorship and structured feedback, learners can translate lived curricular challenges into targeted educational innovations. In contrast to commercially licensed platforms that are costly and less adaptable, internally developed tools offer flexibility, sustainability, and alignment with evolving institutional priorities. This model also reinforces professional identity formation by positioning students not only as consumers of curriculum but as contributors to its evolution.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study employed a single-session design, precluding evaluation of long-term knowledge retention or transfer to summative assessments. Participants were assigned through room-level random allocation rather than individual stratified randomization, which may introduce group-level confounding and contribute to baseline group differences. The modest sample size further limited statistical power and prevented more robust adjustment for these differences. Future studies should incorporate stratified randomization or analytic approaches such as ANCOVA to better control for initial knowledge variation. Additionally, participation was voluntary and ungraded, which may limit generalizability to required coursework contexts. Accordingly, this study should be interpreted as an initial step toward evaluating student-led, AI-assisted educational innovation rather than a definitive assessment of instructional effectiveness.

An additional consideration is the role of the guided worksheet, which was completed by both groups. The worksheet was designed to promote active engagement and application of anatomical knowledge, and may have contributed to improvements in post-test performance independent of instructional modality. As such, the observed learning gains likely reflect the combined effects of structured practice and content delivery, and the present design does not allow isolation of the worksheet’s independent contribution. Finally, the 2D Review condition involved a student facilitator advancing slides, whereas the 3D Simulation Group engaged in a more self-directed format. Although facilitation in the 2D condition was limited to navigation of pre-existing content without additional instruction, this difference in delivery represents a potential confounding factor. Importantly, the present design does not allow isolation of the independent effects of three-dimensional visualization and interactivity. As such, observed benefits likely reflect the combined influence of both elements. Future studies should standardize delivery formats across conditions to more precisely isolate the effect of instructional modality.

Future directions

Future research should extend this work longitudinally across multiple cohorts, incorporate delayed post-testing to assess retention, and evaluate integration within graded curricular components. Expanding the platform to additional modules, including lower limb and cranial nerve lesions, will permit examination of scalability and curricular impact. Embedding the simulation as a structured supplement within the Cells to Structure course may further clarify its additive value relative to existing instructional modalities. Taken together, these preliminary findings suggest that interactive 3D neuroanatomy simulations offer meaningful added value in promoting clinical integration and conceptual understanding within preclinical medical education. Interpretation of these quantitative findings remains limited by the exploratory five-item knowledge assessment, which offers only a narrow measure of immediate performance change.

Moreover, the feasibility of student-led, AI-assisted development highlights a replicable institutional model for addressing peer-identified learning gaps through internally sustained educational innovation.

As an institutionally developed initiative, this platform is intended for sustained curricular integration. In contrast to externally licensed commercial products, it can be directly modified and expanded by faculty and students to reflect evolving instructional priorities, incorporate additional anatomical regions (e.g., lower limb, cranial nerves), and align with competency-based milestones as medical education advances. The overarching objective is to establish a faculty-endorsed simulation resource within the RWJMS curriculum that meaningfully bridges foundational anatomy instruction with the development of clinical reasoning skills. This study serves as an initial evaluation of the value of developing in-house applications to address student-identified areas that would benefit from application-based support.

Conclusion

In the context of increasingly compressed preclinical curricula, medical educators must identify approaches that convey anatomy knowledge and promote meaningful clinical integration. This study provides preliminary evidence that a student-developed, three-dimensional interactive simulation application can support the integration of structure and function in upper limb neuroanatomy. Although quantitative differences in learning gains between the 3D Simulation and 2D Review Groups did not reach statistical significance, the convergence of post-test performance, despite lower baseline scores in the 3D Simulation Group, suggests that interactive modeling may offer a useful complementary approach for learners with lower baseline knowledge. Qualitative findings further indicate that learners perceived the simulation as visually supportive, interactive, and conducive to deeper conceptual understanding. Importantly, the ability to simulate nerve lesions and observe corresponding motor, sensory, and reflex deficits appeared to support reasoning beyond simple memorization. In a discipline where spatial reconstruction and lesion localization are persistent challenges, such tools may offer complementary pedagogical value by supporting active, self-directed engagement with anatomical content.
