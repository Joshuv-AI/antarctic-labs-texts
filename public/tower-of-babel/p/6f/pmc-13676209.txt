Genetically determined very low lipoprotein(a) is not associated with type 2 diabetes and glycemic traits
Authors: Moa P. Lee, Paulina Baca, Fernando Rivas, Alice Zhang, Jesus Alegre‑Diaz, Jaime Berumen, Pablo Kuri-Morales, Kari E. North, Carlos J. Rodriguez, Til Stürmer, Ron C. Hoogeveen, Christie M. Ballantyne, Mariaelisa Graff, Christy L. Avery
Published: 2027

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLipoprotein (a) [Lp(a)] is an independent atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk factor, with levels largely determined by variation in the LPA gene. Despite interests in therapeutic Lp(a) lowering, the phenotypic effects of very low Lp(a) are incompletely characterized, with some studies suggesting increased type 2 diabetes risk.ObjectivesEstimate associations between very low Lp(a), inferred by LPA loss of function variant rs41272114, with type 2 diabetes and glycemic biomarkers.MethodsA multi-population study of European, Hispanic/Latino, and South Asian populations from five cohort studies was assembled. Effects of the null allele rs41272114 (CC, wild-type; T, null allele) on type 2 diabetes and glycemic biomarkers (fasting glucose, fasting insulin, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein ratio, and HOMA-IR) were estimated using multivariable-adjusted and cohort-specific linear or logistic regression models that were combined using inverse variance meta-analysis.ResultsOf n=519,892 participants, the T null allele was common (n=49,841 CT and n=2,039 TT) and n=38,320 participants had type 2 diabetes (prevalence range: 4.7, 29.5%). Participants with the TT genotype had a median Lp(a) ranging 1.5-1.9 nmol/L compared to median Lp(a) ranging 15.8-30.0 nmol/L in participants with the CC genotype. We did not observe increased odds of type 2 diabetes by rs412722114 genotype (ORCTvs.CC = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94, 1.00; ORTTvs.CC = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.89, 1.15). Mean levels of glycemic biomarkers also did not differ by rs41272114 genotype.ConclusionsIn a large multi-population study with high burdens of type 2 diabetes, life-long very low Lp(a) did not increase type 2 diabetes risk.

Introduction

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] is a highly heritable, causal, and independent atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk factor that is elevated in approximately 1.5 billion people globally [1,2]. About 70-90% of variation in Lp(a) concentrations is explained by genetic variability in the LPA locus, which encodes apolipoprotein (a) [apo(a)] [3]. Structurally, Lp(a) consists of an LDL-like particle covalently bound to apo(a) by a disulfide bond to apoB of the LDL particle [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Acknowledging the strength of evidence linking Lp(a) with ASCVD, national and international guidelines recommend Lp(a) measurement either in individuals with moderate to high ASCVD risk or in everyone at least once for improved risk stratification [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. Although no therapeutics are currently available that meaningfully lower Lp(a), very potent Lp(a)-targeted therapeutics are under evaluation. These therapeutics can reduce Lp(a) by ∼70-99% [[12], [13], [14]].

Despite considerable interest in therapeutic Lp(a) lowering, less is known about the phenotypic consequences of very low Lp(a). For example, several studies have suggested a potential increased risk of type 2 diabetes at very low Lp(a) levels [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Much of the evidence linking low Lp(a) with type 2 diabetes comes from genetic-epidemiology studies, which have demonstrated up to a 45% increase in type 2 diabetes risk among participants with very low Lp(a) [15]. However, more recent genetic studies suggest no increased risk in type 2 diabetes associated with low Lp(a) [16,20]. These inconsistencies may reflect heterogeneity in study design and study populations, as well as the use of genetic instruments that do not precisely isolate the effects of very low Lp(a). Even when appropriate instruments are available, statistical power is limited as the relevant variants are relatively rare. LPA null alleles, therefore, offer a largely unexplored avenue to better understand the phenotypic consequences of very low Lp(a). The most common null allele identified to-date, rs41272114, induces alternative splicing and premature termination, producing apo(a) that lacks the domain needed for covalent binding to apoB, abrogating LDL-apo(a) binding and results in lifelong near-0 levels of Lp(a) [21]. However, this variant remains relatively infrequent in European ancestral populations, the population forming the bulk of the Lp(a) evidence base. Interestingly, rs41272114 is more frequent in Hispanic/Latino populations, populations that also have high burdens of type 2 diabetes [22,23].

Thus, we conducted a multi-population study of European, Hispanic/Latino, and South Asian populations, prioritized for null-allele frequency, large sample size, and diabetes burden, to assess the effect of LPA null allele rs41272114 on type 2 diabetes and glycemic biomarkers. By leveraging genetic diversity across populations, our study enables a robust examination of whether very low Lp(a) is associated with type 2 diabetes and glycemic traits.

Methods

Participating Studies

We identified four longitudinal studies in which the rs41272114 T allele was common [minor allele frequency (MAF) > 5%] [Cameron County Hispanic Cohort (CCHC), [24] Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos (HCHS/SOL), [25] and Mexico City Prospective Study (MCPS) [26] ] or the sample size was large enough to enable examination of phenotypic effects even though the rs41272114 T allele was <5% (UK Biobank European and South Asian participants) [27]. Lp(a) was directly measured in two studies (UK Biobank and HCHS/SOL) and was used to estimate effects of the rs41272114 genotype on Lp(a). The Institutional Review Boards at participating institutions approved each study. Informed consent was obtained by the study investigators and updated at each visit.

UK Biobank. The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study of 502,649 individuals aged 40 to 69 recruited at 22 centers in England, Wales, and Scotland [27,28]. Phenotypic data were measured using interviewer-administered questionnaires and blood samples collected at baseline (2006-10). The LPA null allele rs41272114 was imputed with high accuracy using the merged UK10K and 1000 Genomes reference panel (r2=1) [29]. We restricted to unrelated participants (kinship coefficient < 0.0884 indicative of second-degree relationships, n=427,041) of European and South Asian ancestry (n=9,351) as rs41272114 was too rare in the other UK Biobank ancestral populations (minor allele frequency <0.0001).

Mexico City Prospective Study. The MCPS is a prospective cohort study of n=155,434 residents aged ≥ 35 years recruited from two urban districts of Mexico City [26]. Participants provided information on disease history via interviewer administered questionnaire and blood samples at baseline (1998-2004). The rs41272114 allele was genotyped on the Illumina Global Screening Array v2 beadchip [30] and analyses were restricted to unrelated participants (kinship coefficient < 0.0884, n=74,781).

Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos. The HCHS/SOL is a prospective cohort study of n=16,415 self-identified Hispanic/Latinos adults aged 18-74 from four US communities (Bronx, New York; Chicago, Illinois; Miami, Florida; San Diego, California) [[31], [32], [33]]. Phenotypic data was collected through baseline examinations including structured interviews, physical exams, anthropometry and blood samples (2008-2011). rs41272114 was genotyped on the Infinium Expanded Multi-Ethnic Genotyping Array (MEGA) [34]. Analyses were restricted to participants who self-identified as having a Mexican background (n=4,523).

Cameron County Hispanic Cohort. The CCHC is a community-based cohort study of 5,020 (n=3,641 to 4,196 for the final analytic cohorts across the outcomes) Hispanic/Latino participants residing in Cameron and Webb counties on the Texas-Mexico border [35]. Participants were assessed through interviewer-administered questionnaires and clinical examinations and blood samples were collected at baseline (2004). The rs41272114 genotype was measured using the MEGA-EX Array panel [36].

Phenotypic Data

Fasting glucose, triglycerides (TG), high density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) were measured by enzymatic assays (Beckman Coulter or Roche) [37]. In MCPS, TG and HDL-C were measured using the Nightingale Health Ltd high-throughput targeted NMR-metabolomics platform [38]. Glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured in buffy coat samples using validated high-performance liquid chromatography [39]. The triglyceride-to-high-density lipoprotein (TG/HDL) ratio, as an indirect measure of insulin resistance, was calculated by dividing TG (mmol/L) by HDL-C (mmol/L) [40]. Homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting glucose (mg/dL) * fasting insulin (mU/L) /405 [41]. Type 2 diabetes prevalence was defined using a combination of medical history, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, and medication use. Lp(a) in nmol/L was measured using either the Randox assay (UK Biobank) or the Roche assay (HCHS/SOL). For Lp(a) values below assay-specific limits of detection, we imputed a random value between 0 and the limit of detection, assuming a uniform distribution.

Statistical Analysis

We estimated the associations between the rs41272114 genotype (CC, CT, TT), using a codominant genetic model, with CC non-null allele carriers as the referent group. For continuous phenotypes, to ensure normality and consistency of distributions across studies, we applied a rank-based inverse normal transformation. Generalized linear models with an identity link for continuous phenotypes and a logit link for categorical phenotypes were used to estimate associations with rs41272114. Confounders included age, age [2], sex, ancestral principal components, assessment center (when applicable), genotyping array (UK Biobank), and fasting status (for the TG/HDL ratio) [42]. When there were fewer than five participants in a genotype group, study-specific results were not presented. For HCHS/SOL, sampling weights were applied. Study-specific results were combined using inverse variance fixed-effects meta-analysis [43]. Analyses were performed using R version 4.1.0 software (The R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Discussion

In this large study of 51,880 LPA null allele (rs41272114) carriers, including 2,039 individuals homozygous for the T minor allele with 326 type 2 diabetes cases among them, risk estimates consistently aligned with the null, providing strong evidence against an increased diabetes risk associated with very low Lp(a). This observation was consistent across populations. We also saw no evidence of associations between the T rs41272114 minor allele with increased levels of glycemic and insulin resistance biomarkers. In sum, these results suggest that life-long very low Lp(a) does not increase type 2 diabetes.

The relationship between Lp(a) and type 2 diabetes has long been debated, with several observational studies suggesting an inverse relationship between Lp(a) levels and type 2 diabetes [15,18,19,44]. In a meta-analysis of four observational studies, individuals in the lowest quintile of Lp(a) (mean = 3.3 mg/dL) had higher risk of type 2 diabetes (hazard ratio = 1.28; 95% CI: 1.14, 1.43) compared with those in the highest quintile (mean = 62.9 mg/dL) [19]. These observational studies have limitations including residual confounding and reverse causation, motivating genetic studies that may be less susceptible to these threats to validity. Specifically, observational findings should be interpreted in the context of prior evidence supporting reverse causation, whereby diabetes, particularly the insulin-resistant, hyperinsulinemic state of type 2 diabetes, may lower circulating Lp(a) rather than low Lp(a) increasing diabetes risk [45]. Prior studies have reported lower Lp(a) concentrations in type 2 diabetes compared with nondiabetic controls, inverse associations between insulin levels and Lp(a), and experimental evidence that insulin may suppress apo(a) synthesis. This phenotype-specific effect is further supported by observations that Lp(a) levels in type 1 diabetes are generally similar to those in controls but may increase with poor glycemic control or renal involvement. Thus, inverse associations between Lp(a) and incident diabetes should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect diabetes-related changes in Lp(a) metabolism.

Results from genetic studies examining associations between Lp(a) and type 2 diabetes, however, have also been inconsistent. A study of 109,440 participants from the Copenhagen General Population Study used a two-variant genetic instrument to define low Lp(a). This study reported no association with type 2 diabetes [20]. However, the genetic instrument did not specifically capture exposure to very low Lp(a) levels, as the median Lp(a) level among participants classified as having genetically low Lp(a) was 14 nmol/L, which is comparable to median Lp(a) levels observed in UK Biobank participants of European ancestry [15,17]. In contrast, Gudbjartsson et al., 2019 examined both measured Lp(a) levels and two loss of function LPA null alleles (rs41272114 and rs143431368) in an Icelandic population, reporting that null allele homozygotes had 1.45 (95% CI: 1.05, 1.99) times the odds of type 2 diabetes compared with others [15]. However, despite a very large sample size (n=143,087), only 37 participants with type 2 diabetes also carried two copies of the loss of function variants. Thus, there is considerable uncertainty around this estimate, underscoring the need for replication.

No studies to the best of our knowledge have attempted to replicate Gudbjartsson’s finding for type 2 diabetes using LPA null alleles that completely capture a very low Lp(a) phenotype. This evidence gap likely reflects the rarity of LPA null allele variants in the European populations that largely compose the Lp(a) evidence base. Indeed, by assembling a multi-population cohort, we identified more than 2,000 participants carrying two copies of the null allele, of whom 326 participants had prevalent diabetes. This reflects an eight-fold increase compared to Gudbjartsson. Future efforts will include an examination of the role of very low Lp(a) in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, [46] an expanded consortium will likely be required to investigate possible roles of very low Lp(a) with clinical phenotypes, including long-term bleeding risk in patients with coronary artery disease [47]. Efforts linking biobank and genotypic data may provide a way forward, particularly if these biobanks are ancestrally diverse.

Although our results robustly suggest that lifelong low Lp(a) does not increase type 2 diabetes risk, it remains unclear whether these results can be generalized to experimental trials of Lp(a) reducing agents. For instance, the ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trial of alirocumab among patients with acute coronary syndrome demonstrated no overall effect on type 2 diabetes (hazard ratio = 0.95, 95% CI: 0.85, 1.05). However, a 10 mg/dL reduction in Lp(a) corresponded to a 7% increase in type 2 diabetes risk [48]. Such inconsistencies between genetic studies and experimental lipid-lowering trials examining type 2 diabetes risk have been reported in prior studies [49]. While genetic studies accurately anticipated the increased risk of type 2 diabetes associated with statin therapy, [50,51] results have been less consistent for PCSK9 inhibitors or ezetimibe [[52], [53], [54]]. These discrepancies may be driven by imperfect alignment between genetic instruments and pharmacologic mechanisms as well as variation across study populations [49,55]. While the rs41272114 LPA null allele may serve as a proxy for the mechanism of action of muvalaplin or similar agents that inhibit apo(a)-LDL binding, our results may not apply to other Lp(a)-targeting therapies such as antisense oligonucleotides or small interfering RNAs.

Despite multiple strengths, including our large and diverse cohort and a highly specific genetic instrument for very low Lp(a), our study has several limitations. It should be acknowledged that, while the exposure to very low Lp(a) based on the LPA genotype is lifelong, the observed genotype-phenotype association at a single point in time (i.e., at study enrollment) reflects the inherent selection process by survival. This may introduce selection bias, as individuals with diabetes who die before study enrollment are systematically excluded, potentially distorting genotype-phenotype relationships if survival differs across genotypes. Further, heterogeneity in age at enrollment may introduce additional complexity, as older participants have had more time to develop type 2 diabetes, and the associations we observe may therefore reflect different points along the underlying lifetime risk curve. Such selective survival and age-based truncation of the risk period may complicate the interpretation of our estimates as true lifetime risk. While we observed consistent results across the studies with participants across a relatively broad age range (mean age: 38-56) that were included in our analysis, which may offer some reassurance, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Also, we had modest statistical power for assessing associations between TT carriers and some glycemic phenotypes, including fasting insulin. Although individual glycemic biomarkers are not fully interchangeable and capture distinct aspects of glucose homeostasis, they share substantial clinical overlap. When considering the pattern of results across all available glycemic biomarkers, there was little evidence to suggest that very low Lp(a) meaningfully influences glycemic traits. The consistency of null findings across several correlated endpoints may lender additional support for the broader inference despite limited power for some individual biomarkers. Lastly, we could not assess the effects of other null alleles, including rs143431368, as these variants were too rare in the populations included herein (MAF< 0.5%). Examining rs143431368 – a predicted splice acceptor variant leading to reduced production of functional apo(a) protein – is of considerable interest, as the mechanism by which it causes very low Lp(a) differs from the mechanism underlying rs41272114, a splice donor variant in the intron of apo(a) KIV type 8 leading to a truncated form of apo(a) that is unable to bind covalently to apoB-100 to form Lp(a) [21,56]. While the rs41272114 LPA null allele may approximate the mechanism of action of agents such as muvalaplin that inhibit apo(a)-LDL binding, our findings may not extend to other Lp(a) targeting strategies, including antisense oligonucleotides or small interfering RNAs, which reduce Lp(a) through distinct biological mechanisms.

Conclusions

This study provides robust evidence of no effect of the LPA null allele rs41272114 on type 2 diabetes risk and glycemic biomarkers. Our findings add to a growing evidence base suggesting that lowering Lp(a) may provide cardiovascular benefit without increasing the risk of type 2 diabetes.
