Clemastine Restores Myelination Protein Expression in S16 Schwann Cells by Enhancing AMPK Activation and Reducing H2O2-Induced Oxidative Stress
Authors: Chawon Yun, So Young Lee, Jun Hong Won, Ga Hee Kim, Tae Hyun Kim, Jung Il Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeripheral nerve injury and oxidative stress can severely impair Schwann cell function by disrupting the expression of key myelin proteins, promoting intracellular lipid accumulation, and damaging mitochondrial integrity. These pathological changes are central to various neurodegenerative disorders and chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy, yet effective therapeutic approaches remain limited. Clemastine, an FDA-approved antihistamine with known remyelination-enhancing effects in the central nervous system, has not been thoroughly explored for its protective role in peripheral myelinating cells under oxidative stress. In this study, we investigated the time-dependent protective effects of Clemastine in S16 Schwann cells exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as a model of oxidative injury. Treatment with Clemastine significantly increased the expression of myelin-related proteins such as myelin protein zero (MPZ), alongside in increase in AMPK phosphorylation at Thr172. However, co-treatment with H2O2 ensued oxidative damage, leading to reduced pAMPK(T172) and MPZ expression, elevated ROS levels, and increased lipid accumulation. These results suggest that oxidative stress can attenuate Clemastine’s effects in association with disrupted redox balance and energy metabolism. Subsequent treatment with Metformin (Met), a pharmacological activator of AMPK, was associated with partial recovery from H2O2-induced oxidative damage. Overall, our findings support the potential of a combinatorial approach using Clemastine and Met to promote myelin-related protein expression and lipid metabolic balance in Schwann cells under oxidative stress, rather than establishing a definitive synergistic or causal mechanism.Keywords: Schwann cells, Clemastine, AMPK signaling, Myelin proteins, Oxidative stress

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral nerve is critically dependent on the function and survival of Schwann cells, the principal glial cells of the peripheral nervous system (PNS) (Oliveira et al., 2023). These cells not only form the myelin sheath necessary for rapid nerve conduction but also play indispensable roles in axonal regeneration and metabolic support following nerve injury (Bosch-Queralt et al., 2023). However, Schwann cells are highly susceptible to various forms of cellular stress, which can compromise their myelinating capacity and contribute to peripheral neuropathy. Oxidative damage is one of the most prominent stressors associated with peripheral nerve degeneration, particularly in clinical settings involving trauma or cancer treatment (Areti et al., 2014).

Among oxidative insults, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is frequently employed in experimental models as a direct donor of reactive oxygen species (ROS), capable of simulating redox imbalance in vitro (Miguel et al., 2009). Excessive ROS generation in Schwann cells leads to a cascade of pathological events, including mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid accumulation, and disruption of energy metabolism (Abd Razak et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2020). These changes ultimately impair cell survival and differentiation, and are closely associated with the downregulation of myelin-related proteins such as myelin protein zero (MPZ) and myelin basic protein (MBP). Furthermore, increased intracellular lipid accumulation and mitochondrial membrane depolarization under oxidative conditions have been implicated in the progressive demyelination observed in peripheral neuropathies (Barnes-Vélez et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2020). Despite the central role of these mechanisms in Schwann cell dysfunction, effective strategies to prevent or reverse such damage remain limited.

Recent studies have highlighted the potential of pharmacological agents to enhance glial cell resilience against oxidative injury (Bachtell et al., 2017; Trofin et al., 2025). Clemastine, an FDA-approved first-generation H1 histamine receptor antagonist, has gained attention for its ability to promote remyelination in central nervous system (CNS) disorders, notably through the differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (Jiang et al., 2023). However, its protective capacity in peripheral glia under oxidative stress conditions has not been fully elucidated. Current evidence suggests that Clemastine may modulate key stress-response pathways, including those involving AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a master regulator of energy sensing and mitochondrial function (Garcia and Shaw, 2017; Yamazaki and Ohno, 2025).

AMPK activation is known to promote mitochondrial biogenesis, reduce oxidative damage, and enhance autophagic clearance, thereby supporting cellular adaptation under metabolic stress (Salminen and Kaarniranta, 2012; Srivastava et al., 2012). These properties position AMPK as a promising therapeutic target for protecting Schwann cells from oxidative insults. Notably, Clemastine has been reported to influence AMPK signaling, although this effect remains poorly defined in peripheral myelinating cells (Yamazaki and Ohno, 2025). In parallel, Metformin (Met), a widely used antidiabetic drug and known AMPK activator, has shown promise in improving cellular outcomes under oxidative conditions in various cell types (Apostolova et al., 2020; González-Casanova et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2016).

In this study, we established an in vitro oxidative stress model in S16 Schwann cells using low-dose H2O2 exposure to mimic sublethal redox imbalance. We investigated whether Clemastine could confer protective effects by preserving myelin protein expression and attenuating H2O2-induced ROS accumulation, lipid dysregulation. Furthermore, we explored whether co-treatment with Met could enhance or restore the protective effects of Clemastine through AMPK activation. This study aims to provide mechanistic insights into the therapeutic potential of Clemastine and AMPK modulation in maintaining Schwann cell function under oxidative stress.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Reagents and antibodies

Clemastine, Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), poly-L-lysine (PLL), Metformin (Met), dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Anti-Myelin protein zero (MPZ), β-Actin antibodies were from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Anti-pAMPK(T172), AMPK were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, Massachusetts, USA). For total RNA isolation, PURY RNA Plus RNA MiniPrep was prepared from GenDEPOT (Baker, TX, USA). Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR (qPCR) were performed using the Bio-Rad iScript cDNA kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) and SmartGene Sybr Green Q-PCR Master Mix (SMART GENE, Korea), respectively. ProEX™ CETi protein extract solution was obtained from TransLab Biosciences (Daejeon, Korea). CCK cell viability assay kit was purchased from DongIn BioTech (Seoul, Korea). Both Clemastine and Metformin were dissolved in DMSO for all treatments in this study, and vehicle controls received equivalent volumes of DMSO to match the treatment groups.

Cell culture

Immortalized rat Schwann cells (S16) were purchased from ATCC (Bethesda, MD, USA). Cells were seeded onto PLL coated culture dishes and maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2, at 37°C, and were maintained to >80% confluency before harvesting.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was measured using the D-Plus CCK cell viability assay kit from DongIn (Seoul, Korea) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were treated with various concentration of Clemastine for 24 h or H2O2 for 1 hr with additional incubation for 24 h. The absorbance of the final solution was measured at 450 nm using a Molecular Devices SpectraMax M2e Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

RNA isolation and gene expression analysis

Total RNA was isolated using the PURY RNA Plus kit according to manufacturer’s instructions. Single-stranded cDNAs were synthesized from 500 ng of total RNA in a 40µL reaction volume using an amfiRivert Platinum One cDNA Synthesis Platinum Master Mix (GenDEPOT). Quantitative PCR analysis was performed using cDNA corresponding to 20 ng of RNA input with Power SYBRTM Green PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the protocol of the manufacturer. qPCR was performed using the following program: denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 20 s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 20 s, with a subsequent melting curve analysis generated using the CFX Opus Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). Relative gene expression was analyzed using the Livak method as described before (Yun et al., 2018). A normalized expression ratio (2−ΔΔCt) was calculated for each target gene, representing the fold difference in mRNA expression in the treatment groups when compared with that in the vehicle group. Western blot analysis

Culture medium was removed from cells, and wash once in 1x DPBS. They were lysed in N-PER™ Neuronal Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher) with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail buffer (GenDEPOT, P3300-001). Lysates were centrifuged at 13,000×rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Cleared lysates were used to determine total protein amount (BCA Protein Assay, Takara, T9300A). Proteins were separated in polyacrylamide SDS gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane. A total of 5-20 μg protein was applied on each lane of the gels. After blocking with 1% BSA at room temperature for 1h, the membrane was incubated with primary antibodies including anti-MPZ, AMPK, pAMPK(T172) and β-actin at 4°C overnight. The secondary antibodies were applied at room temperature for 1 h after three times 10 min washes with TBST. Detections were performed on iBright (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) using ECL Substrate kit (Thermo scientific).

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded on a cover-glass in 6-well culture plate and treated with Clemastine, H2O2 and Metformin. Samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min, and permeabilized using 0.1% TritonX-100 for 5 min, incubated with 10% BSA in PBS for 1 hr for blocking. Thereafter, the cells were incubated with anti-MPZ and pAMPK antibody overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with FITC-conjugated goat anti rabbit IgG (Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories, West Grove, PA, USA). Fluorescence images were captured using an Evos (Invitrogen) microscope with x400 magnification.

Lipid droplet staining (Oil Red O)

To measure cellular lipid deposition, Oil Red O staining (Sigma-Aldrich) was performed as previously described (Yun et al., 2024). To quantify Oil Red O content, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each sample. After 5 min, the sample density was measured at 510 nm using a MULTISKAN GO reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The difference in optical density was measured by normalization to total protein.

ROS assay (DCFDA staining)

Intracellular oxidative stress was analyzed using the ROS sensitive probe 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluoroscein diacetate (DCFDA, Sigma-Aldrich). To measure intracellular ROS, cells were incubated with 20 μM of DCFDA containing phenol-red free complete media for 2 h and fluorescence intensity was analyzed using GloMax fluorescence reader (Promega). The fluorescence value obtained was normalized to the total protein.

Statistical analysis

All data analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v5.0, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Significant difference between groups was determined using t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

RESULTS

Determination of optimal Clemastine concentration and time-dependent activation of AMPK signaling in S16 Schwann cells

To determine an appropriate working concentration of Clemastine for use in S16 rat Schwann cell assays, we began by evaluating its effects on S16 cell viability and cellular morphology. As shown in Fig. 1A, treatment with Clemastine of 5 µM or higher led to a clear reduction in cell viability, as assessed by CCK-8 assay. In addition, cells exposed to higher concentrations exhibited altered morphology, including cellular shrinkage and a loss of the typical bipolar structure of Schwann cells (Fig. 1B). These observations suggest that prolonged exposure to high levels of Clemastine impairs Schwann cell integrity. In contrast, treatment with 1 µM Clemastine preserved cellular morphology and maintained high viability, identifying this concentration as the optimal, non-toxic dose for further mechanistic experiments.

Cell viability is presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using Student’s t-test. ***p<0.0005 vs. Vehicle group. Next, to evaluate the temporal effects of Clemastine on Schwann cell signaling and myelination-related protein expression, we treated S16 cells with 1 µM Clemastine for different durations (0, 24, 48 h) and assessed protein levels by Western blotting. As shown in Fig. 1C and 1D, Clemastine induced a progressive increase in the protein expression of pAMPK(T172), total AMPK, and MPZ. These results suggest that Clemastine is associated with increased AMPK signaling and enhanced expression of myelin-associated marker in a time-dependent manner. The gradual increase in both pAMPK(T172) and total AMPK suggests a potential involvement of metabolic or stress-response signaling pathways related to AMPK in Clemastine-treated cells.

Immunofluorescence analysis of Clemastine-induced AMPK activation and myelin-related protein expression in S16 Schwann cells

Immunofluorescence staining revealed that Clemastine treatment markedly increased the expression of both pAMPK(T172) and MPZ in S16 Schwann cells (Fig. 2A, 2B). Compared with vehicle-treated cells, Clemastine-treated cells displayed a pronounced nuclear accumulation of pAMPK, consistent with its role as a transcriptional regulator linking energy status to myelination. MPZ immunoreactivity was also enhanced, showing a distinct cytoplasmic distribution pattern that aligns with its synthesis and trafficking prior to membrane incorporation. These results support the notion that Clemastine is associated with Schwann cell differentiation and increased myelin-related protein expression, highlighting its potential relevance for peripheral nerve repair.

Establishment of an oxidative stress model and its inhibitory effect on Clemastine-induced AMPK and MPZ expression in S16 Schwann cells

Oxidative stress is a critical driver of peripheral nerve degeneration (Areti et al., 2014), and to mimic this condition in vitro, we exposed S16 Schwann cells to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at varying concentrations (0-50 µM) for one hour. Cell viability assessed 24 h post-treatment showed no significant cytotoxicity up to 5 µM H2O2 (Fig. 3A). At higher concentrations (≥10 µM), however, cells exhibited morphological features of stress, such as rounding and membrane blebbing, along with reduced adherence, indicative of early cytotoxic effects (Fig. 3B). Based on these findings, 5 µM H2O2 was selected as a sublethal oxidative stress condition for downstream assays.

(B) Representative phase-contrast microscopic images of S16 cells following H2O2 exposure. (C) Western blot analysis of S16 cells treated with Clemastine (1 µM) with H2O2 (5 µM). Expression levels of pAMPK(T172), total AMPK, and MPZ were evaluated to assess the impact of oxidative stress on Clemastine-induced signaling. (D) Densitometric quantification of protein bands normalized to β-Actin. Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test vs Clemastine only from Cle/H2O2 co-treated samples. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001, ns; not significant.

To explore whether oxidative stress influences the promyelinating activity of Clemastine, we treated S16 cells with Clemastine (1 µM) alone or in combination with H2O2 (5 µM), and evaluated the expression of myelination-related markers and AMPK activation. As shown in Fig. 3C, co-treatment with H2O2 substantially attenuated expression of pAMPK(T172), MPZ compared to Clemastine alone, suggesting that oxidative stress is associated with reduced AMPK activation and myelin-related protein expression.

Densitometric analysis of Western blot bands (Fig. 3D) confirmed that pAMPK, total AMPK, and MPZ expression levels were significantly reduced under oxidative conditions, even in the presence of Clemastine. This indicates that oxidative may interfere with Clemastine-associated Schwann cell differentiation, potentially involving AMPK-related signaling pathways. These results highlight the vulnerability of Schwann cells to oxidative challenges and suggest that redox balance is a critical determinant of pharmacological efficacy in nerve repair strategies.

Immunofluorescence analysis showing oxidative stress-mediated suppression of Clemastine-induced AMPK activation and MPZ expression

Immunofluorescence staining further supported this observation, showing reduced signal intensity for both pAMPK(T172) and MPZ in the Clemastine/H2O2 co-treated group compared to Clemastine-only treatment (Fig. 4A, 4B). These findings suggest that oxidative stress impairs Clemastine-mediated Schwann cell activation and myelin-related protein expression, possibly by disrupting AMPK signaling. Interestingly, closer examination revealed that MPZ staining was largely diminished in the cytoplasmic region but retained as a perinuclear signal consistent with the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) which was absent in the vehicle control group. Given that MPZ undergoes folding and post-translational processing in the ER before being transported to the plasma membrane (Volpi et al., 2016), this pattern likely reflects oxidative stress-induced retention or misfolding of MPZ within the ER, rather than its proper trafficking to the myelin membrane.

Images were captured at 400× magnification.

AMPK activation by Metformin restores Clemastine-induced myelin-related gene and protein expression under oxidative stress

To further explore whether AMPK activation via Metformin (Met) could rescue the oxidative stress-impaired myelination response, we performed a combinatorial treatment experiment using Clemastine, H2O2, and Met in S16 Schwann cells. The qPCR analysis was conducted under four experimental conditions: vehicle control, Clemastine alone, Clemastine/H2O2, and Clemastine/H2O2/Met. We first evaluated the mRNA expression of key myelin-associated genes, including MBP, MPZ and MAG which are known downstream targets of Schwann cell differentiation and myelination pathways, as well as myelin- regulatory transcription factors EGR2(KROX20), SOX10, dedifferentiation and Schwann cell activation and remyelination marker ERB2 (Fig. 5A). qPCR analysis revealed that Clemastine treatment alone significantly upregulated the expression of these genes compared with vehicle control. Notably, co-treatment with H2O2 markedly suppressed this upregulation, reflecting an inhibitory effect of oxidative stress on transcriptional programs associated with myelination. Strikingly, the addition of Met to the Clemastine/H2O2 condition partially restored the expression of all target genes toward Clemastine-only levels, suggesting that AMPK activation may counteract oxidative stress-induced transcriptional suppression.

At the protein level, Western blot analysis showed that pAMPK and MPZ, both of which were suppressed in the Clemastine/H2O2 condition, dose-dependently restored by Met treatment (Fig. 5B). These findings were further supported by densitometric analysis, confirming that AMPK activation supporting the association between AMPK activation and recovery of myelin-related gene and protein expression in Schwann cells.

To further clarify the temporal relationship between AMPK activation and myelin protein expression, we examined pAMPK and MPZ over a 12-h period (Fig. 5C). The results revealed that pAMPK levels steadily increased following Met treatment (red dotted line), whereas MPZ expression did not show a significant change within the 12-h time window. These data indicate that AMPK activation temporally precedes detectable changes in MPZ expression, supporting the notion that pAMPK upregulation occurs earlier than myelin-related protein induction without implying a direct causal requirement within this time frame.

Metformin restores Clemastine-induced pAMPK and myelin protein expression at the cellular level

To corroborate the biochemical findings, we performed immunofluorescence staining for pAMPK and MPZ in S16 cells treated with Clemastine/H2O2, with or without Met. As shown in Fig. 6A and 6B, cells treated with Clemastine/H2O2 exhibited weak staining for both pAMPK and MPZ, consistent with impaired activation of the myelination program. In contrast, pAMPK displayed enhanced nuclear and cytoplasmic staining in the Met-treated group. Notably, MPZ staining under oxidative stress was largely diminished in the cytoplasm and instead retained in a perinuclear pattern, as shown in Fig. 4B. In contrast, the addition of Met not only restored the overall fluorescence intensity of both markers but also reduced ER retention of MPZ, resulting in a more widespread cytoplasmic distribution and continuous, membrane-associated signal. These results reinforce the association between AMPK activation via Met and the recovery of myelin-related signaling pathways suppressed by oxidative stress, thereby supporting Clemastine-associated myelin-related responses under adverse cellular conditions.

Enhanced AMPK activation by Metformin inhibits ROS and lipid accumulation from Clemastine- H2O2 co-treated S16 Schwann cell

To explore whether AMPK activation by Met alleviates oxidative stress-induced dysfunctions, we examined ROS production and lipid accumulation in Schwann cells exposed to Clemastine and H2O2. As shown in Fig. 7A, DCFDA staining demonstrated a robust increase in ROS under combined treatment, indicating redox imbalance. Addition of Met significantly reduced ROS, consistent with an association between Met treatment, reduced ROS levels, and AMPK activation.

Metformin (2 mM) treatment in S16 Schwann cells exposed to Clemastine (1 µM) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 5 µM) resulted in (A) reduced intracellular ROS levels, as determined by DCFDA fluorescence staining. (B) Oil Red O staining demonstrated increased intracellular lipid accumulation (lipid droplet deposition) following Clemastine/H2O2 co-treatment, which was significantly attenuated by Met addition. (C) Western blot analysis of lipid metabolism- related proteins revealed that Clemastine/H2O2 co-treatment modestly increased PPARα expression while elevating FASN levels. Met treatment recovered both PPARα and MPZ but reduced FASN expression. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: ***p<0.001, ns; not significant.

Previous studies have demonstrated that peripheral nerve injury is accompanied by dynamic changes in lipid metabolism, particularly through the upregulation of lipoprotein lipase (LPL), which is partly derived from infiltrating macrophages and may facilitate reutilization of lipids from degenerating myelin (Huey et al., 2002). These findings highlight that lipid turnover is a crucial component of the nerve injury response and repair process. In this context, we sought to determine whether Clemastine-mediated promyelinating activity is affected by oxidative stress-induced lipid accumulation, and whether AMPK activation by Met could counteract such lipid dysregulation in Schwann cells. First, we evaluated intracellular lipid accumulation as a secondary marker of oxidative injury using Oil Red O staining. As expected, Clemastine/H2O2 co-treatment led to enhanced lipid accumulation. This effect was notably reversed by Met treatment (Fig. 7B), reinforcing the idea that AMPK activation not only reduces ROS levels but also mitigates aberrant lipid deposition in Schwann cells.

To further elucidate whether AMPK activation by Met modulates lipid metabolic pathways in Schwann cells, we assessed the expression of PPARα, a key regulator of fatty acid oxidation, and fatty acid synthase (FASN), a central enzyme in lipogenesis (Chakravarthy et al., 2007; Kersten, 2014). As shown in Fig. 7C, co-treatment with H2O2 after Clemastine pre-treatment significantly reduced PPARα and MPZ expression and upregulated FASN, reflecting a metabolic shift toward lipid accumulation under oxidative stress. Notably, the addition of Met partially restored PPARα and MPZ levels while suppressing FASN expression, that AMPK activation is associated with attenuation of oxidative stress–induced lipid metabolic imbalance, rather than definitively reprogramming lipid metabolism.

DISCUSSION

Our study demonstrates that Clemastine is associated with enhanced Schwann cell myelin-related protein expression and increased AMPK signaling, and that these beneficial effects are significantly compromised under oxidative stress. Initial findings confirmed that 1 µM Clemastine represents an optimal, non-toxic concentration that induces a time-dependent increase in AMPK phosphorylation and the expression of MPZ in S16 Schwann cells (Fig. 1, 2). This effect was observed through both Western blot and immunofluorescence analyses, suggesting that Clemastine’s promyelinating activity may be associated with AMPK-related signaling pathways. This is consistent with prior reports that identify AMPK as a central regulator of Schwann cell energy metabolism and myelination processes (Beirowski et al., 2014; Long and Zierath, 2006).

In Fig. 1D, both pAMPK(T172) and total AMPK levels increased relative to β-actin, yet the pAMPK/AMPK ratio showed no marked rise at 48 h. This suggests that Clemastine enhances total AMPK abundance while modestly sustaining phosphorylation, collectively suggesting an increase in AMPK signaling capacity. Thus, AMPK activation reflects the combined effects of total protein upregulation and sustained phosphorylation, consistent with evidence that increased total AMPK can sensitize cells to stress even with subtle phosphorylation changes (Hardie, 2015).

However, exposure to H2O2 significantly attenuated Clemastine-induced pAMPK(T172) and MPZ expression in Fig. 3 and Fig. 4, indicating that oxidative stress interferes with Clemastine-associated promyelinating responses. These observations are supported by both protein and mRNA analyses, as well as by immunofluorescence imaging (Fig. 5, 6). Notably, oxidative stress also suppressed transcription of several key myelination genes and regulatory factors, including MPZ, MBP, MAG, SOX10, and EGR2 (KROX20). This repression of promyelinating gene networks suggests that oxidative stress may interfere with upstream transcriptional regulation, potentially via redox-sensitive pathways or by damaging key signaling intermediates. These findings are consistent with earlier studies demonstrating that oxidative stress impairs Schwann cell differentiation and myelin maintenance (Gonçalves et al., 2017; Kim et al., 2019).

In addition to the overall reduction in expression, we observed that oxidative stress altered the intracellular localization of MPZ, leading to diminished cytoplasmic signal and retention of the protein in a perinuclear pattern consistent with the ER (Fig. 4B). This suggests that H2O2 may affect MPZ transcription as well as intracellular trafficking and folding, resulting in ER accumulation. Remarkably, Met treatment alleviated this retention phenotype, restoring cytoplasmic distribution and membrane-associated MPZ expression (Fig. 6B). This recovery pattern further supports an association between AMPK activation and improvement of oxidative stress–related protein processing abnormalities, in addition to transcriptional recovery.

Our findings extend beyond confirming that oxidative stress impairs Schwann cell differentiation by showing that Clemastine’s AMPK-dependent promyelinating effects are particularly vulnerable to redox imbalance, yet can be recovered through Met co-treatment. While previous studies have described oxidative stress as a broad inhibitor of Schwann cell myelination (Kim et al., 2019; Lehmann et al., 2012), our work highlights a potential dual role of AMPK, first as a metabolic regulator associated with myelin gene expression, and second as a redox-sensitive node whose activation may support Schwann cell differentiation under oxidative challenge. This dual positioning of AMPK suggests that metabolic modulation could serve as an actionable strategy even when canonical promyelinating pathways are compromised.

In our results, Met treatment restored pAMPK and MPZ expression at both the transcript and protein levels under oxidative conditions (Fig. 5). This was further confirmed by immunofluorescence, which revealed recovery of both cytoplasmic and nuclear pAMPK signal intensity and re-establishment of typical MPZ expression (Fig. 6). These results suggest that AMPK may function as an important integrative node linking metabolic regulation, redox homeostasis, and differentiation signaling in Schwann cells. The ability of Met to restore Clemastine function at the level of AMPK and MPZ highlights the feasibility of combinatorial therapies for enhancing myelin repair under oxidative stress.

In contrast to prior reports where AMPK activation was primarily linked to energy metabolism (Jansen et al., 2020; Long and Zierath, 2006), our data indicate that AMPK also orchestrates a broader protective program integrating transcriptional control, antioxidant defense, and lipid metabolic balance. The observation that Met not only restored MPZ but also reduced ROS levels implies that AMPK activation may influence oxidative stress levels, rather than acting solely downstream of survival signaling. This distinction is critical because it positions AMPK as a therapeutic convergence point for both metabolic and oxidative injury in Schwann cells.

To further explore the downstream cellular consequences of AMPK activation, we next examined whether Met reduces oxidative stress burden and lipid dysregulation in Clemastine/H2O2-treated Schwann cells. The results shown in Fig. 7 demonstrate that Met significantly attenuated intracellular ROS levels and suppressed lipid droplet accumulation, both of which were elevated under oxidative stress.

In line with these observations, Fig. 7C provides evidence that AMPK activation by Met is associated with alterations in Schwann cell lipid metabolic profiles. Specifically, oxidative stress induced by Clemastine/H2O2 markedly reduced the expression of PPARα, a central transcription factor regulating fatty acid oxidation, while simultaneously upregulating fatty acid synthase (FASN), a key enzyme of lipogenesis. This pattern reflects a metabolic shift favoring lipid accumulation under stress. Notably, the addition of Met reversed this trend by restoring PPARα expression and suppressing FASN expression to levels even below those of the vehicle-treated group, suggesting that AMPK activation promotes a metabolic environment conducive to oxidative metabolism rather than lipogenesis. This shift may contribute to reduced lipid accumulation and support a cellular state permissive for myelin-related processes.

Our overall results align with existing literature showing that AMPK serves as a central regulator of both antioxidant defense and lipid metabolism (Jansen et al., 2020; Long and Zierath, 2006). By reducing ROS generation and limiting lipid accumulation, AMPK activation creates a cellular environment more permissive for differentiation and myelination. In this context, the cytoprotective effects of Met appear to complement the promyelinating activity of Clemastine, suggesting a complementary approach, while Clemastine initiates myelin-related gene expression and AMPK signaling, Met reinforces these effects by stabilizing intracellular redox and metabolic states.

Taken together, these insights allow us to propose a model in which Clemastine and Met exert complementary effects. Clemastine primes the myelination program, while Met fortifies the metabolic and redox environment required for its execution. Unlike previous single-agent approaches, this combination addresses both transcriptional activation and cellular homeostasis, offering a more resilient strategy for remyelination in oxidative conditions such as peripheral nerve injury or chemotherapy-induced neuropathy.

Based on the experimental findings, the proposed regulatory framework is schematically summarized in Fig. 8. Under basal conditions, Clemastine enhances AMPK phosphorylation and supports proper trafficking of MPZ, thereby promoting myelin-related gene and protein expression in Schwann cells. In contrast, oxidative stress induced by H2O2 suppresses pAMPK and MPZ expression, increases ROS accumulation, and disrupts lipid homeostasis, collectively attenuating Clemastine-mediated promyelinating effects. Activation of AMPK by Met restores pAMPK signaling, reduces oxidative and lipid stress, and alleviates MPZ retention, synergistically reinforcing Clemastine-associated myelin-related responses.

Schematic illustration of the protective functions of Clemastine in Schwann cells under oxidative stress. Clemastine enhances phosphorylation of AMPK (pAMPK), promotes the release of MPZ from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and increases the expression of myelin-related genes and proteins. Under normal conditions, Clemastine facilitates myelin restoration by supporting AMPK signaling and proper trafficking of MPZ, leading to improved myelin protein synthesis. However, exposure to H2O2 disrupts redox homeostasis, reduces pAMPK and MPZ levels, and induces ROS accumulation and lipid dysregulation, thereby counteracting Clemastine’s effects. Subsequent activation of AMPK by Metformin restores pAMPK activity, mitigates oxidative damage, and synergistically enhances the Clemastine-mediated recovery of myelin gene and protein expression.

Our findings further suggest that Met-driven recovery of pAMPK and MPZ is closely linked with reductions in ROS burden and normalization of lipid metabolic signaling, including suppression of FASN and regulation of PPARα. This implies a mechanistic framework in which AMPK activation suppresses lipogenesis, rebalances fatty acid oxidation, and enhances antioxidant capacity, thereby alleviating oxidative and lipotoxic stress. Importantly, this suggests that reduced ROS and lipid peroxidation are not merely correlates but potential contributors to improved myelin-related outcomes. Future work will need to clarify this causal relationship, which may open new avenues for targeting metabolic and redox pathways to promote myelin repair in peripheral neuropathies.
