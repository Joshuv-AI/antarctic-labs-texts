Sodium transport and redox regulation in Saccharomyces cerevisiae under osmotic stress depending on oxygen availability
Authors: A Shirvanyan, K Trchounian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the molecular mechanisms behind the differential responses of Saccharomyces cerevisiae industrial strains (ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13007) to osmotic stress. We observed that, in contrast to ATCC 9804 strain, sodium flux in ATCC 13,007 is not N, N’-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCCD)-sensitive under osmotic stress, suggesting a distinct ion homeostasis mechanism. Under aerobic conditions, osmotic stress increased reduced SH groups by 45% in ATCC 9804 and 34% in ATCC 13,007. In contrast, under microaerophilic conditions, both strains experienced a 50% reduction in thiol groups. Notably, ATCC 13,007 exhibited a 1.5-fold increase in catalase (CAT) activity under aerobic stress compared to standard conditions, while ATCC 9804 showed enhanced CAT activity due to SH group binding. Additionally, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was doubled during aerobic growth in both strains, with ATCC 13,007 showing a 1.5-fold higher SOD activity under osmotic stress. The results demonstrate that S. cerevisiae adapts to osmotic stress differently under aerobic and microaerophilic conditions, with aerobic conditions promoting Pma-Ena-Trk interplay, reduced thiol levels and increased catalase activity, while microaerophilic conditions demonstrate Pma-Nha-Trk interplay and shifts redox balance towards oxidized thiol groups and enhance superoxide dismutase activity. Understanding these mechanisms can aid in developing stress-resistant yeast strains for industrial applications.Keywords: Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Sodium flux, Thiol groups, Catalase, Superoxide dismutase, Osmotic stressSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biophysics, Microbiology

Introduction

Based on initial sugar concentrations used for alcoholic fermentation by yeast the process can be implemented in 3 states: normal gravity (NG, less than 180 g/L), high gravity (HG, 180–240 g/L) and very high gravity (VHG, more than 250 g/L). When external osmotic pressure increases, to restore cellular osmotic balance, glycerol accumulation in the cells is required1. As a result, to adjust to the high osmolarity environment, cells may employ a decision to distribute glucose intracellularly between ethanol production and glycerol accumulation, reducing overall ethanol yield. Furthermore, its overproduction resulted in a 50% decrease in growth rate and a shift in the transcription of over 40% of genes2. Therefore, improving yeast tolerance to high osmolarity and high concentration of alcohol is decisive for successful fermentation and alcohol production.

In S. cerevisiae, osmotic stress induces the high-osmolarity glycerol (HOG) pathway via activation of upstream sensors and effectors. Hog1 protein activates a cytoplasmic response that acts on glycerol production and ion transporters, metabolism, and genes expression3,4. Yeast mutants, that lacking HOG pathway, are highly sensitive to osmotic stress while mutations causing uncontrolled HOG pathway are lethal1. Other major functional groups of osmo-stimulated genes include proteins involved in protection against oxidative damage and protein denaturation, such as genes encoding antioxidants (e.g. CTT1, GRE3, TRX2, TTR1) and chaperones (e.g. HSP12, HSP26, HSP42, HSP104). The transcriptional general stress response factors Msn2/Msn4, which are transcriptional regulators of these antioxidant and chaperone genes, are under the control of Hog15.

Another significant role in osmotic stress response plays the calcineurin (calcium activated Ser/Thr protein phosphatase) signaling pathway6–8. In S. cerevisiae it is activated during exposure to various environmental stress conditions, such as hypotonic and cold stress, hyperosmotic and salt stress, H2O2 generation etc8,9. The primary target of calcineurin is the Crz1 zinc-finger transcription factor. In response to environmental stress, calcineurin dephosphorylates Crz1, allowing it to enter the nucleus and bind to Calcineurin Dependent Response Elements (CDREs) with the sequence 5′-GNGGC(G/T) CA-3′. These CDREs are located in the promoters of genes responsive to calcineurin, such as the Na+-ATPase ENA1. Around 160 genes, including those of antioxidant enzymes, are largely dependent on calcineurin6,10. Moreover, early reports suggested that the calcineurin is required for K+ transporter-Trk1, to properly discriminate between K+ and Na+ under salt stress11.

One of the main players of stress response is plasma membrane monovalent cations, especially potassium and sodium, exchange systems and transporters. In contrast to potassium, sodium, because of its high toxicity, exported out of cell to keep intracellular levels low via active sodium transporters like Ena1 and Nha11. While the exterior sodium level in K+-starved cells is 100 times higher than the K+ concentration, the intracellular level of sodium is still relatively low. Significant amount of sodium enters when the extracellular Na+/K+ ratio rises over around 700:1, and corresponding amounts of K+ must exit to keep the cells electroneutral12. The metabolic response to Na+ is highly coordinated and even more regulated than the reaction to high potassium in the medium13. At acidic intracellular pH the Nha1 antiporter can extrude sodium, whereas at higher pH, the Ena1 transporter is principally responsible for sodium extrusion. Loss or increase of Nha1 or Ena1 function has been shown to influence cytosolic pH, membrane potential, Trk1-dependent potassium uptake and to be involved in the initial adaptation to both osmotic and pH stress. Results show that expression of both, the nha1 and ena1, genes is specifically and markedly increased in response to osmotic and pH stress via the action of several signaling pathways, including HOG and calcineurin pathways11,14. There is extremely scarce research-based data studying sodium flux changes and relationship with cell energy exchange and key antioxidant enzymes at osmotic stress conditions and its role in adaptation of yeast.

S. cerevisiae is a facultative anaerobe, preferentially fermenting microorganism, which widely used in various biotechnological processes due to its culture simplicity, rapid growth, safe status and high cell concentration culture. However, some of these features depend on the availability of oxygen during growth15. Additionally, most of the industries perform in semi-aerobic conditions to enhance yeast biomass production16. Therefore, it is important to study the impact of oxygen on yeast metabolism and adaptation pathways to the different environmental factors.

It is indisputable that antioxidative compounds and enzymes, e.g., glutathione (GSH), CAT, SOD, thioredoxin and glutathione peroxidase (TPx and Gpx) play significant roles in stress resistance mechanisms, such as antioxidant defense against reactive oxygen species (ROS) and detoxification. A dozen studies described the role of antioxidant enzymes in aerobic conditions17–19. In contrast, data related to their roles, activation and operation mechanisms in actual fermentative conditions are almost inexistent.

Yeast cells, adapted in different stress environments, are required to enhance productivity of fermented foods and biofuels, however, the interactions between cellular maintenance, growth and stress responses remain unexplored. The exact mechanism of adaptation has not been fully understood. There is not much information available concerning membrane transporters, metabolism, and adaptation pathways of S. cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 strains under osmotic stress conditions. Thus, the following research aimed to investigate their stress-resistance mechanisms, including sodium flux and antioxidant enzymes activity changes and their regulation mechanisms under osmotic stress conditions. Results will not only play a fundamental role but also can be applied for enhancement of fermentation processes of making alcoholic beverages, food products, and bioethanol.

Results

ATCC 9804 strain was isolated from palm wine or from Makgeolli (Korean traditional rice wine)20,21, while another strain, ATCC 13,007 is ale brewing strain, isolated from Irish beer22,23, and has ability to produce extracellular glucoamylase24. Despite the paucity of data in the literature, it was shown that wine and beer yeast strains differ by fermentation capacity, time, temperature, as well as alcohol resistance, flavor and aroma profile, nutrient requirement and flocculation properties16,25,26. Nutrient requirements of ATCC 13,007 and 9804 strains depending on growth conditions.

Earlier we found that strains differ in growth properties and fermentation capacity under strains conditions. Thus, we have shown that the specific growth rate (SGR) of yeast differs in aerobic and microaerophilic conditions: in aerobic conditions regardless of pH (5-7.2) SGR of both strains decreased by 40% under osmotic stress compared to standard conditions. In contrast, strictly weak basic conditions are needed for yeast growth and biomass production in oxygen limiting and osmotic stress conditions, where the specific growth rate of ATCC 9804 is suppressed ~ 85% and in ATCC 13,007 ~ 60% compared to standard conditions27. Metabolite profiling HPLC data shows ethanol production reaches its maximum rate (82.1 ± 2.5 mM (ATCC 9804); 117.3 ± 3.5 mM (ATCC 13007)) in 24–32 h under standard microaerophilic growth conditions. The maximum ethanol yield (mM) per glucose (mM) was 0.3 and 0.7 for ATCC 9804 and, 0.4 and 1.1 for ATCC 13,007 during aerobic and microaerophilic growth respectively under standard conditions.

Marginal glycerol production was observed in aerobic conditions with a production rate of 4 µmol per hour and 1.3 µmol per hour in S. cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 respectfully, whereas, in standard conditions during microaerophilic growth, it reached up to 98 µmol per hour and 175 µmol per hour for same strains27,28. Under standard conditions the maximal acetate production (12.2 mM) was observed during oxygen limitation conditions in both strains, which was ~ 10-fold higher compared to its levels during aerobic growth. However, during aerobic growth under osmotic stress conditions acetic acid production rate increases and reaches up to 15.4 mM. Any fermentation product was not observed in medium during yeast growth under oxygen limiting and osmotic stress conditions27,28. Thus, the results demonstrate that the strains exhibit differences in metabolic properties, suggesting the presence of distinct adaptation mechanisms, which is the focus of our study.

Changes in extracellular medium osmolality during growth under standard condition or osmotic stress condition

In natural environments and industry, yeasts are constantly subjected to variations in external osmolarity. Osmotic pressure can be generated because of high or low solute concentrations, which lead to osmotic stress characterized by the loss or an influx of water into the cell and subsequent violation of cell turgor. This can seriously impair cellular functions29. To understand how osmotic pressure changes during aerobic or microaerophilic growth of S. cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 strains under standard conditions, as well as in the osmotic stress conditions, medium osmolarity was monitored (see Fig. 1).

Osmotic pressure changes during growth of S. cerevisiae ATCC 9804 (a) and ATCC13007 (b) strains under standard condition or osmotic stress conditions.

Results show that the osmolarity of the medium was higher by 100 mOsm/kg in both strains at the initial phase of growth under osmotic stress compared with standard conditions. During 3 h of aerobic growth at standard conditions the medium osmolarity is slightly reduced, following subsequently increase of medium osmolality. After 24 h of aerobic growth under standard conditions the medium osmolarity is equal to the same parameter under osmotic stress and remains stable at the next stages of growth. A similar nature of osmolarity changes was observed during the microaerophilic growth as well. At 24 h of microaerophilic growth of ATCC 9804 strain under standard conditions medium osmolarity was ~ 1.3 fold higher in comparison with aerobic conditions.

During 3–6 h growth medium osmolarity rises, but from the 6 h of aerobic growth under osmotic stress medium osmolality decreased and stays almost stable after 24 h. Remarkably, medium osmolarity stays higher by 100 mOsm/kg at 96 h aerobic growth of ATCC 9804 strain under osmotic stress compared to the same parameter under standard conditions. Conversely, the medium obtains almost the same osmotic properties in both standard condition and osmotic stress conditions at 96 h growth of ATCC 13,007 strain. In both strains changes in osmolality were not observed during microaerophilic growth under osmotic stress.

Analyses of sodium flux in S. Cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 under standard condition or osmotic stress condition

Osmolarity fluctuations in the environment have a profound impact on ion balance, making the study of sodium flux a crucial component in understanding how yeast cells adapt to changing conditions and maintain their internal stability4,30.

Earlier we discuss potassium and proton flux changes during fluctuations from standard condition to osmotic stress conditions27. Our results show (see Fig. 2) that both strains exhibit increased sodium flux rate in both aerobic and microaerophilic growth under osmotic stress compared to standard conditions. However, oxygen limitation leads to decreased sodium flux under osmotic stress conditions compared to aerobic growth in the same conditions. Thus, sodium flux rate increases by 2․6-fold and 4.1-fold during aerobic growth, and by 1.5-fold and 1.1-fold in ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007, respectively, during microaerophilic growth under osmotic stress compared to standard conditions. It has been shown31,32 that the amount of dissolved oxygen in medium can affect not only the cell growth and nutrient uptake, but also the adaptation of the cells against different environmental factors. Thus, it was shown, that the transcriptional repressors of hypoxic genes - Mot3 and Rox1 are involved in the adaptive response to the hyperosmotic stress, with an important function in the adjustment of ergosterol biosynthesis. Both factors, together with the Hog1 kinase, are essential to downregulate the expression of specific ERG genes involved in the biosynthesis of ergosterol. Therefore, total sterol levels decrease under osmotic stress, which is needed to efficiently adapt to hyperosmotic conditions31. Additionally, it was shown that proper mitochondrial function is a critical factor for yeast adaptation to osmotic stress32.

Comparison of ΔJNa+ in S. cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 intact cells within aerobic (a) or microaerophilic (b) growth under standard condition or osmotic stress conditions. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001, ns - nonsignificant.

Sodium flux was DCCD-dependent upon addition of glucose to the experimental assay during aerobic growth of both strains under standard conditions. However, these phenomena weren’t observed during addition of glycerol. The complete opposite picture was observed when the ATCC 9804 cells are previously grown under osmotic stress conditions, then glucose or glycerol is added to the experimental medium: in this instance increase in the osmolarity of the experimental assay led to DCCD-dependent sodium flux. In contrast to ATCC 9804 strain, regardless of the availability of oxygen in growth media in ATCC 13,007 under osmotic stress conditions sodium flux was not DCCD-dependent.

The results show that sodium flux increases under osmotic stress, with a greater rise in aerobic conditions. Sodium flux in ATCC 9804 is influenced by glucose and DCCD under standard conditions, but this dependency shifts under osmotic stress, whereas ATCC 13,007 sodium flux remains independent of DCCD regardless of stress.

The changes in the number of SH-groups and malondialdehyde (MDA) level in S. Cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 cell extracts under standard condition or osmotic stress condition

An extensive regulation of redox state is essential to yeast cells’ response to osmotic stress. Our study explores the dynamic changes in reduced and oxidized thiol groups with respect to the osmolarity-induced ion balance, providing insights into their critical function in the cellular response to osmotic stress. These redox modifications offer important new insights on how yeast adapts to changing environmental conditions.

Our result shown (see Fig. 3A) that after 24 h growth, the total number of oxidized groups remains constant under all conditions studied․ In contrast, in both studied strains, shifting from aerobic to microaerophilic growth under standard conditions, an increase in the number of reduced SH groups is observed. During aerobic growth under osmotic stress conditions, the number of reduced SH groups increased by 45% and 34% in ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 respectively. In microaerophilic conditions, the osmotic stress leads to the reduction of the number of thiol groups by approximately 50% in both strains.

The changes in the number of SH-groups (a) and malondialdehyde (MDA) level alterations (b) in S. cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 cell extracts under standard condition or osmotic stress conditions. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001, ns - nonsignificant.

The reduction of total number of accessible SH groups was correlated with increase in MDA level (Fig. 3B): the results indicate that under stress conditions, the concentration of MDA is significantly elevated ~ 2 fold in both strains compared to the standard conditions. Thus, the amount of MDA increases under standard conditions - during the transition from microaerophilic to aerobic, and under osmotic stress conditions - during the transition from aerobic to microaerophilic growth conditions․

The study reveals that the number of reduced SH groups in S. cerevisiae increases under osmotic stress in aerobic conditions but decreases under microaerophilic stress. MDA levels are significantly higher under stress conditions compared to standard conditions. These findings highlight the impact of osmotic stress on the redox state of yeast cells and suggest a correlation between oxidative stress and altered thiol group levels.

CAT and SOD activity changes in S. Cerevisiae ATCC 9804 and ATCC 13,007 strains depending on growth conditions

One of the targets of osmotic stress is the oxidative-reductive processes in cells, and a significant role in the cellular response and adaptation to it is played by antioxidant enzymes, which regulate the disrupted oxidative-reductive balance. Thus, here we investigate the effect of redox processes in the cell on the activities of yeast antioxidant enzymes CAT and SOD, and the role of these enzymes in cellular adaptation.

During aerobic growth of ATCC 9804 strain, no significant change in catalase activity is observed from shift of standard condition to osmotic stress conditions, unlike ATCC 13,007, in which the activity increases ~ 1.5 fold (see Fig. 4). In ATCC 9804 strain the binding of available SH groups with specific modifier-NEM, during both aerobic and microaerophilic growth, stimulates the activity of the enzyme under standard conditions. In contrast no change in enzyme activity was observed because of the binding of available SH groups in the ATCC 13,007 strain. The role of SH groups in the regulation of CAT activity increases in osmotic conditions, and the binding of available SH groups in cells grown in both aerobic and microaerophilic conditions leads to a decrease in enzyme activity by 24% in ATCC 9804 strain and by 28% in the ATCC 13,007 strain. In the case of microaerophilic growth of ATCC 9804 and 13,007 strains under standard conditions the CAT activity decreases by 2 and 1.5-fold, respectively (see Fig. 4B). During microaerophilic growth under osmotic stress conditions, the activity of the CAT enzyme in the ATCC 9804 strain increases by 63%.

In both strains, SOD is doubly active during aerobic growth under standard conditions in comparison with microaerophilic conditions (see Fig. 5). Under standard conditions, the binding of available SH groups correlates with an increase in SOD enzyme activity by ~ 50% (ATCC 9804) and 22% (ATCC 13007) in aerobic, and by 90% (ATCC 9804) and ~ 60% (ATCC 13007) in microaerophilic conditions.

Remarkably, during aerobic growth of both strains under osmotic stress conditions the binding of SH groups significantly increases the SOD activity in comparison with microaerophilic conditions (see Fig. 5A).

The study demonstrates that catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities in S. cerevisiae are differentially regulated under various growth conditions and suggest that osmotic stress impacts antioxidant enzyme regulation, with variations observed between strains and growth conditions, emphasizing the complex interplay between oxidative stress and enzyme activity.

Discussion

During 3 h of aerobic growth at standard conditions the reduction of medium osmolarity is due to glucose assimilation and absence of significant production and accumulation of some substances in the extracellular medium is not observed․ Subsequently, medium osmotic pressure rises, which can be conditioned with production of TCA cycle organic acids and glycerol because of fermentation occurring due to Crabtee effect33.

At 24 h of microaerophilic growth of ATCC 9804 strain under standard conditions medium osmolarity was ~ 1.3 fold higher in comparison with aerobic conditions, which can be due to predominantly fermentative nature of metabolism, because of which high amount of glycerol is produced and accumulated in the external environment. In the case of ATCC 13,007 strain, osmolarity changes have the same tendency in both aerobic and microaerophilic growth under standard conditions. This can be due to glycerol production inhibition during aerobic growth of strains under standard conditions as mentioned earlier27.

During 3–6 h aerobic growth under osmotic stress conditions the increase in medium osmolality may be conditioned with activation of cellular respiration due to which different acids accumulated in the medium. For example, our earlier published research indicates the accumulation of succinate under these conditions which concentration in medium was 2 times higher compared standard condition27.

In both strains constant medium osmolality throughout microaerophilic growth under osmotic stress conditions may be due to lethargic consumption of glycerol under oxygen-limiting conditions and its non-fermentable nature in fully anaerobic conditions. Fermentation products have not been reported during cultivation of S. cerevisiae wild-type strains growing on glycerol in microaerophilic or anaerobic conditions27,34,35.

Oxygen limitation leads to decreased sodium flux under osmotic stress conditions compared to aerobic growth in the same conditions which was correlated with higher potassium flux rates established during microaerophilic growth where it exceeds ~ 2.5 fold in ATCC 9804 and ~ 3.4 fold in ATCC 13,007, compared to aerobic growth in the same conditions, as we shown earlier27.

Thus, results show that Na+/K+ exchange plays a decisive role in the mechanisms of adaptation to stress conditions in aerobic conditions, and K+/H+ exchange in microaerophilic conditions. These results are in good conformity from the evolutionary theory of life origin and development of ion transporters36,37. For instance, Benito et al. showed38 that fungal K+- or Na+-ATPases evolved from an ancestral K+-ATPase through processes of gene duplication to maintain a better adaptation to Na+. They demonstrated that Schizosaccharomyces pombe is the only known fungus in which this duplication did not occur and in which there is a lack of Na+-ATPase activity. This duplication was constant for the yeast as they were constantly exposed to sodium-rich seawater or similar fluid, and the ancestral P-type K+-ATPase evolved into the present Na+-ATPase38.

Increased sodium flux rate confirms earlier found mechanism, which suggest39 that osmotic stress associated HOG pathway stimulates cation transporters at the plasma and vacuolar membranes to reinstall normal ionic strength and osmolarity in the cytosol is important for an efficient transcriptional response.

The addition of glucose to the experimental assay induces DCCD-sensitive sodium flux in both strains during aerobic growth under standard conditions, whereas glycerol addition does not elicit the same response. This suggests that sodium flux changes are not linked to proton ATPases like Pma1 or mitochondrial FOF1-ATPase in rapid adaptation to stress under aerobic conditions, emphasizing the role of Na+/K+ exchange systems, such as Na+-ATPase and potassium channels (Ena and Trk, respectively).

Conversely, when ATCC 9804 cells previously grown under osmotic stress conditions are exposed to glycerol (an increase in assay osmolarity) results in DCCD-sensitive sodium efflux, which was correlated with potassium influx27, indicating the involvement of K+/H+ exchange systems, such as Nha, Trk and Pma1, in stress adaptation under oxygen-limiting conditions for this strain. Notably, in contrast to ATCC 9804 strain, sodium flux in ATCC 13,007 under osmotic stress conditions remains insensitive to DCCD, irrespective of oxygen availability during growth, suggesting strain-specific differences in stress response mechanisms.

The results indicate that the total number of oxidized SH (non-accessible SH groups) remained constant across all examined conditions (see Fig. 3A). This consistency suggests a potential influence from constitutive protein disulfide bonds within the cells. An increase in the number of reduced SH groups under oxygen limiting standard conditions is probably due to the intense transport and accumulation of SH groups containing amino acids inside the cells, as well as the stimulation of glutathione synthesis. The same phenomena were found for another yeast, Candida utilis, in which NaCl-induced osmotic stress stimulates glutathione biosynthesis up to 70%40. The reduction of total number of accessible SH groups during growth under oxygen limiting and osmotic stress conditions can be conditioned with the absence of the preferred carbon source, glucose, and the sluggish assimilation of glycerol, as mentioned above. As a result, to meet the energy requirements amino acids serve as an energy source. It was shown that the growth of yeasts on glycerol is dependent on the presence of medium supplements such as amino acids and nucleobases35,41, but exactly in which pathway these compounds influence and why stimulate growth on those conditions remains unclear. It was also known that during the oxidation of 1 reduced glutathione molecule the Gibbs free energy is 58 kJ42, which would be sufficient for the synthesis of 2 mol of ATP43. Thus, under microaerophilic stress conditions, the decrease in the amount of reduced sulfhydryl groups may be an important mechanism for the regulation of the bioenergetic charge of the cell and thus for the survival of the yeast under these conditions.

The reduction of total number of accessible SH groups was correlated with increase in MDA level. This suggests a predominance of oxidizing processes within the cell under osmotic stress conditions and direct linkage between osmotic and oxidative stress. Increased lipid peroxidation may also be due to produced ethanol effect on cell membrane structures. Thus, it was shown44 that the adaptation to the high ethanol concentrations was facilitated by modifications in H+-ATPase activity and the lipid composition of the plasma membrane.

In ATCC 9804 the binding of available SH groups during both aerobic and microaerophilic growth stimulates the activity of the CAT under standard conditions. In contrast, no change in CAT activity was observed because of the binding of available SH groups in the ATCC 13,007 strain, which indicates that the enzyme activity in the ATCC 13,007 strain does not depend on the number of SH groups under standard conditions.

Remarkably, during aerobic growth of both strains under osmotic stress conditions the binding of SH groups significantly increases the SOD activity in comparison with microaerophilic conditions. This indicates that SH groups have a strict role in the regulation mechanisms of SOD activity in aerobic conditions, which clearly differs from the characteristic shown in microaerophilic conditions.

The main role of antioxidant enzymes under oxygen limited conditions remains unclear and a growing number of studies focused on it. New findings suggest that in addition to its primary function as an antioxidant enzyme, SOD also functions as a nuclear transcription factor, an RNA binding protein, a synthetic lethal interactor, and a signal modulator in the metabolism of glucose45.

The overarching/working model of observed results is presented in Fig. 6. The model outlines the two working systems’ differences in adaptation to osmotic stress under aerobic or microaerophilic conditions. Thus, the model proposes that under aerobic conditions, in response to osmotic stress, the Na+-ATPase (ENA), Trk and Pma systems interact, as a result, sodium is pumped out of the cell and potassium and protons enter the cell. These protons participate in neutralizing reactive oxygen species, particularly superoxide radicals and alongside with malondialdehyde level regulates the oxidative-reduction balance of the cell by modulating the ratio of reduced (SH) to oxidized (S-S) thiol groups favor the reduced form. This plays a crucial role in regulating the activity of antioxidant enzymes, specifically, this involves the suppression of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and the stimulation of catalase (CAT) activity, thereby ensuring an appropriate stress response and adaptation. Under microaerophilic conditions, the Pma operates in the opposite direction, hydrolyzing ATP and extruding protons from the cell. The energy derived from ATP hydrolysis is utilized to transport sodium to ensure vital processes, such as sodium transport via Na+/H+ antiporter system (Nha) as potassium uptake via Trk systems under energy-limited conditions. As a result of proton extrusion by the Pma, the intracellular redox balance is disrupted, and the ratio of reduced (SH) to oxidized (S-S) thiol groups shift towards the latter. Due to the oxidation of existing SH groups, also impairs the regulation of antioxidant enzymes; catalase activity is suppressed while superoxide dismutase activity is stimulated. This adjustment is essential for neutralizing superoxide anions generated from incomplete oxidation under low partial pressure of oxygen conditions.

Working model of sodium flux, redox balance and antioxidant enzymes activities regulation involved in osmotic stress response of S. cerevisiae cells depending on the availability of oxygen during growth. The model illustrates how S. cerevisiae adapts to osmotic stress under aerobic versus microaerophilic conditions. Aerobically, ENA systems pump sodium out, allowing protons and potassium influx via PMA and Trk, respectfully. Protons neutralize ROS and maintain a highly reduced thiol level, which upregulates CAT and downregulates SOD activity, promoting stress adaptation. Under microaerophilic conditions, PMA hydrolyzes ATP to export protons, using the energy for Na+ and K+ transport via NHA and Trk, respectfully. This proton extrusion disrupts the redox balance, increasing oxidized thiol groups level, which downregulates CAT activity, and upregulates SOD activity to ensure proper osmotic stress response. These results improve our knowledge of the complex reactions in the metabolism, bioenergetics, and physiology of yeast adaptation and importance of redox dynamics, osmolarity, ion flux, and antioxidant enzyme activity regulation as key components of for survival.

The results have potential applications in industrial processes where osmotic stress is prevalent, such as bioethanol production, brewing, and biotechnology. A deeper understanding of these adaptive responses depending on oxygen availability offers multiple strategies for enhancing yeast robustness in industrial contexts. Thus, one promising strategy involves optimizing dissolved oxygen levels during fermentation. By adjusting oxygen concentration, it is possible to induce specific stress responses that improve yeast tolerance to both osmotic and oxidative stress. Obtained results show that controlled adjustments in oxygen levels may enable the precise regulation of redox balance and ion homeostasis mechanisms-two processes that are essential for preserving cellular viability and optimizing production efficiency in stressful environments. Furthermore, advanced genetic engineering approaches could be applied to regulate ion transport systems, such as the Pma-Ena-Trk interplay under aerobic and the Pma-Nha-Trk interplay in microaerophilic environments, which could improve ionic equilibrium and stress tolerance. Furthermore, modulating the activity of essential antioxidant enzymes like SOD and CAT via redox balancing, in addition to overexpressing them, may reinforce yeast resistance to various stress factors.

Methods

Yeast strain, media, and culture conditions

S. cerevisiae ATCC 13,007 (Prototroph, pof + STA, diploid, European Nucleotide Archive (ENA): ERR5358805) and ATCC 9804 (GenBank: Z95939.1; ENA: ERR5285363) wild-type strains (American Type Cell collection, USA) were purchased from Microbial Depository Center of Armbiotechnology Scientific and Production Center of National Academy of Sciences of RA.

Yeast extract-peptone-dextrose (YPD) medium (g L− 1: 10:20:20)46 was used to get yeast liquid cultures. Media were inoculated with 1.5% (v/v) overnight culture containing 109 cells and grown in YPD medium and further considered as a standard physiological condition (hereafter standard conditions)47 and incubated at microaerophilic (Vtare/Vmedium = 1.2, without shaking) or aerobic condition (Vtare/Vmedium = 2.5, 130 rpm rotary shaking48 at 30◦C. To induce osmotic stress conditions the strains were similarly cultured under aerobic or microaerophilic condition in glucose-free YP (10 g L− 1 yeast extract, 20 g L− 1 peptone) medium supplemented with glycerol (5 g L− 1) as a carbon source27. These conditions and glycerol concentration were chosen based on metabolite analysis, which demonstrated that, at the stationary growth phase, the fermentation broth is devoid of glucose and contains glycerol. The presence of glycerol creates elevated osmotic pressure, thereby affecting yeast physiology16,27. Medium pH was adjusted to 7.2 using K2HPO446,49,50. Cell growth was monitored at 600 nm using Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Germany).

Osmolarity determination

Medium osmolarity changes have been tracked at 0, 3, 6, 24, 48, 72 h grown yeast liquid cultures. Samples taken were centrifuged at 3000 x g for 15 min, after osmotic pressure of 100 µL cell-free supernatant were determined by using the K7400-S Semi-Micro Osmometer (Knauer, Germany) based on their freezing point. Osmometer previously calibrated with standards (A01240–300 mOsm per kg; A01241-1–400 mOsm per kg and A01250–850 mOsm per kg (Knauer, Germany))51,52. Results were expressed in milliosmole (mOsm per kg).

Sodium flux analysis

Early stationary phase culture was harvested using Sorvall LYNX 6000 Superspeed Centrifuge (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 3500xg for 15 min. After double washing with distilled water pellet was resuspended in the experimental assay (150 mM Tris–HCl containing 0.4 mM MgSO4, 1 mM NaCl, 1 mM KCl, pH 7). Na+ ion fluxes (∆JNa+) by whole cells were determined as described27,53 upon addition of glucose (20 g L− 1 ) or glycerol (5 g L− 1) via potentiometric method by using HI 5222 pH-ISE-ORP Meter (Hanna Instruments, USA) equipped with Na+ - selective FC300B electrode (Hanna Instruments, Portugal)54, which was calibrated with different concentration (5µM-100mM) of NaCl containing experimental solution at pH 7, 25 °C and ∆JNa+ was determined using the calibration curve. In whole cells incubated with specific inhibitor of mitochondrial FOF1-ATPase – N, N’- dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCCD; 0.5 mM), the same procedure was performed. Results were expressed in mmol Na+ / min per 109 cells per mL.

SH groups determination

Early stationary growth phase cells were harvested by centrifugation and washed with distilled water, after cells were resuspended in ‘’breaking medium’’ (0.65 M sorbitol, 0.1 mM Na-EDTA, pH 6.5), then breaked using glass beads as described27,55.

Accessible SH-groups were determined by the reaction of cell extract with Ellmann’s reagent (5, 5’-dithiobis-2-nitrobenzoic acid) using glutathione as a standard56,57. 100 µg protein containing cell extracts were treated with the reagent till the latter was fully reacted and the optical density of the mixture became constant (~ 1.5–2 h). Samples, treated with 0.5 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM), a thiol-group specific modifier, were measured by the same principle57. Protein was measured by the method of Lowry using bovine serum albumin as a standard58. All assays were done at 30 °C. Accessible SH groups were determined as the difference of total and NEM treated samples. SH groups level was determined according to Beer–Lambert law and expressed in nmol/L per mg protein.

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA) concentration

Malondialdehyde level was determined in cell extracts obtained from early stationary phase grown cells as described above. Firstly, protein free cell extract was prepared by protein precipitation via trichloroacetic acid (TCA). Then samples were centrifuged and the product of fatty acid breakage - MDA content in protein free supernatant was determined using the reaction with thiobarbituric acid59. Results were expressed in µM/mg protein.

Antioxidative enzymes activity assays

CAT (1.11.1.6) activity was determined by the photometric measurement of the H2O2 consumption by the early stationary grown phase cell extracts containing catalase at 240 nm as described60,61. SOD (1.15.1.1) activity determination was carried out by Beauchamp and Fridovich method62,63 based on formation of blue-colored formazan because of reaction between the substrate, nitro blue tetrazolium chloride (NBT) and riboflavin produced superoxide radicals in the presence of methionine and EDTA (500 µg/ mL). The SOD present in the sample (early stationary grown phase cell extracts) acts on the superoxide radicals and thereby reduces the net amount of superoxide anions dropping the formazan production. A decline of 50% in the formation of formazan is taken as one unit of SOD. In parallel the samples incubated with 0.5 mM NEM were included. Specific activity of SOD was expressed in Units per mg protein.

Chemicals and data processing

Glucose, peptone, agar, yeast extract, Tris (Carl Roth GmbH, Germany), BSA, ATP (Sigma Aldrich, Germany), NBT, Riboflavin, Methionine and the other reagents and chemicals of analytical grade were used. Each data point represented was averaged from independent triplicate cultures: the standard deviation was not more than 3%. Statistical analysis was performed by 2-way ANOVA Tukey’s statistical test using GraphPad Prism Data Analysis and Scientific Graphing Software (version 8.0.2) (GraphPad Software Inc., USA; https://www.graphpad.com)64.
