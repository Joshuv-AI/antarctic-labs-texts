Increased bone mass but delayed mineralization: in vivo and in vitro study for zoledronate in bone regeneration
Authors: Rongchang Wang, Chaowei Liu, Wenwei Wei, Yanjun Lin, Lin Zhou, Jiang Chen, Dong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBisphosphonates (BPs) are widely used to inhibit excessive osteoclast activity. However, the potential to compromise bone defect healing has limited their broader application. To better understand the influence of BPs on bone regeneration, we established a bone grafting model with Zoledronate administration, aiming to deepen the understanding of bone remodeling and mineralization processes.MethodsA bone grafting model was established in the distal femurs of male Sprague–Dawley rats. The experimental group received systemic administration of Zoledronate (ZOL, 0.2 mg/kg, administered twice). Histological analysis and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were employed to assess osteoblastic and macrophage activity, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining was used to evaluate osteoclastogenesis. Mineralization was assessed through Micro-CT analysis, Raman spectroscopy, and back-scatter scanning electron microscopy (BSE-SEM). Additionally, the in vitro effects of ZOL on osteoblast and osteoclast activity were investigated to further elucidate its impact on bone regeneration.ResultsIn vivo, the ZOL group showed increased bone mass, as observed in histological and radiological assessments. However, Micro-CT, Raman spectroscopy, and BSE-SEM detection revealed lower mineralization levels in ZOL group's regenerated bone. Acid-etched SEM analysis showed abnormal osteocyte characteristics in ZOL-group’s regenerated bone. Simultaneously, elevated osteopontin (OPN), F4/80 expression along with reduced TRAP expressing was found in the grafting region of ZOL group. In vitro, ZOL did not negatively impact osteogenetic activity (ALP, BMP4, OCN expression) at the tested concentrations (0.02–0.5 g/ml) but significantly impaired mineralization and inhibited osteoclast formation, even at the lowest concentration.ConclusionsThis study highlights a less recognized negative effect of ZOL on bone mineralization during bone regeneration. More research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-04906-2.Keywords: Zoledronate, Mineralization, Bone regeneration, Osteoclasts, Scanning electron microscopy

Introduction

Bisphosphonates (BPs) have long been established as potent inducers of osteoclast apoptosis [1, 2] and have been widely used for decades to treat diseases characterized by high bone turnover, such as osteoporosis and tumor-induced hypercalcemia [3]. It is accepted that BPs exert their anti-resorption effects by selectively binding to and being adsorbed onto mineral surfaces in bone: the dual phosphate groups of BPs confer a high affinity for mineral binding [4], while the R2 side chain, particularly in nitrogen-containing BPs, plays a crucial role in promoting osteoclast apoptosis via the mevalonate pathway [5].

Recently, BPs have garnered attention as potential agents for bone regeneration in tissue engineering. BPs application has consistently resulted in significant increases in bone mass [6–8], some studies have reported negative effects when grafts are pretreated with BPs [9]. Excessive suppression of osteoclastic activity has been observed, leading to adverse outcomes such as osteonecrosis or delayed healing [10]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying bisphosphonate-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (BRONJ) remain unclear, reduced vascularization may play a key role. Osteoclasts are known to mediate angiogenesis through the secretion of PDGF-BB, and ZOL has been shown to inhibit this process, reducing vascular tube formation in vitro [11].

With deeper research into bone biology, it is recognized that bone regeneration and maturation are a complex process involving not only osteoblasts/osteocytes but also macrophages (osteomacs) and osteoclasts (OCs) [12]. Depletion or inhibition of osteoclasts and their progenitors (macrophages) can induce abnormal bone-related phenotypes. For instance, mice lacking osteoclasts by M-CSFr gene knockout exhibit reduced bone length and abnormal tooth eruption [13]. The depletion of macrophages has been shown to impair fracture healing [12]. The close relationship between osteoblastic and osteoclastic cells has also been elucidated in the field of bone tissue engineering [14]. In animal models of ectopic bone formation, highly osteoinductive materials have been observed to stimulate the activity of macrophages and osteoclasts [15].

Clinically, the timing of dental implant loading post-surgery is critical, both the local bone quantity and quality in the grafting area should be considered. However, despite ongoing research, the influence of BPs on bone quality and the role of osteoclasts in bone mineralization remain poorly understood and sometimes contradictory [16–18]. To address these gaps, Zoledronate (ZOL), a third-generation BP, was utilized in this study. A defect grafting model was constructed to investigate its impact on local bone architecture and mineralization. In addition to assessing osteoblasts, this study examined ZOL's effects on macrophages and osteoclasts to better understand BPs' role in bone regeneration.

Materials and methods

Surgery and drug administration

Thirty-eight male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (7–8 weeks, weighing 250–260 g) provided by the Fujian Medical University Laboratory Animal Center were used in this study. The rats were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine hydrochloride (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (5 mg/kg). A trepan was used to create a defect 4 mm in diameter and 5 mm in depth in both femurs. The defect edge site was defined as 2 mm away from the distal epiphyseal growth plate, as described by similar studies [19, 20]. Deproteinized bovine bone (DBBs) (Gegreen, RUISHENG BIOTECHNOLOGY, China) were placed into the defect, and absorbable membranes (kindly donated by NEO MODULUS BIOTECHNOLOGY, China) were used to cover the surface of the defect (Fig. 1). To prevent infection, the rats were intraperitoneally injected with penicillin (100 mg/kg, every 24 h, three times).

Bone defect grafting model on the rat’s distal femurs. A The defect was 4 mm in diameter and 5 mm in depth. B The DBBs were transferred into the defect. C A membrane was placed over the cavity to ensure stability

Zoledronate powder (Macklin, China) was dissolved with sodium hydroxide solution, and the pH of the stock solution was adjusted to 7.0–7.1. The working solution was diluting the stock solution with sterile normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride). ZOL was injected intraperitoneally twice (0.2 mg/kg, once every 48 h). The time point of treatment could be seen in the process flowchart [Supplement Material 3].

Vickers hardness test

Eight male SD rats (4 weeks old, weighing 100–110 g) provided by Fujian Medical University were used. ZOL was injected intraperitoneally (0.2 mg/kg). The animals were sacrificed after 18 days. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h, the muscle and soft tissues were removed from the femurs. The samples were subsequently cut via a saw (IsoMet HS Pro, Buehler, USA) to isolate the diaphysis (middle of the femur) in transverse sections. The sections underwent acid-etching and back-scattered SEM detection (for detailed procedures, see the Supplemental Material 2). Finally, the polished blocks were used for Vickers hardness testing.

Ethics statement

This study was performed in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals of the National Institutes of Health. All procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare & Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University (Fujian, China). The ethical approval number is FJMU IACUC 2020-0027.

Sample collection

All the rats were sacrificed with an overdose of pentobarbital (200 mg/kg) at 4 days and at 1, 2, 4, and 8 weeks for detection. The femurs were isolated and transferred immediately to 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h. The samples were decalcified with 20% EDTA at 37°C for 2–3 weeks for histological analyses. The Micro-CT and Raman tests have no conflicts with the other tests, they are tested before other testing. The precise sample allocation can be found in the table [Supplement Material 4].

Acid-etched and back-scattered SEM and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) analysis

For the SEM and back-scattered electron microscopy (BSE SEM) detection process, refer to the detailed protocol [Supplementary Material 4] [21]. For CLSM analysis, the protocol was similar to that used for acid-etched SEM, with the key difference being the substitution of collagen dissolution with collagen immunofluorescent labeling. Collagen was stained using TRITC-phalloidin (37°C, 1 hour). After washing with PBS, the tissue samples were analyzed using CLSM (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

Micro-CT analysis

The samples were scanned at a thickness of nine microns in a Micro-CT scanner (Skyscan 1176, Bruker, Germany). The scanned data were analyzed via CTAn software (Bruker, Germany) for bone histomorphometric analysis. The ROI was defined as the space in the defect shown in the transverse sections (columnar region filled with DBB; see supplemental file 1). The bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone grayscale index were calculated to evaluate the quality and mineralization level of the new bone. Other bone parameters were simultaneously produced by the CTan software [22].

Raman spectroscopy analysis

After the surface soft tissue was removed, Raman spectra were collected in the wavenumber range of 100–2000 cm⁻¹ via a Renishaw Raman microscope (Invia Reflex, Renishaw, England) using 785nm laster. Samples were analyzed without decalcification. Three examination points were randomly selected from the defect cortical bone area, avoiding the site where DBB was exposed. The data were processed with Origin 2016 (Microcal, USA). Band fitting was performed via Gaussian polynomials, and the integrated areas of selected bands were measured for band intensity [23, 24]. Mineral-to-matrix ratios were calculated by dividing the phosphate band (959 cm−1) by the amide I band (1662 cm−1).

Histological analysis

The decalcified samples were transversely cut. After dehydration, the samples were hyalinized in normal butanol and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 4 µm thickness were subjected to Masson staining (Maxim, China). Osteoclasts were identified with a tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) Staining Kit (KeyGEN, China). The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the area of the defect. Using Adobe Photoshop CC 2019 (Adobe, USA), trabecular and TRAP+ cells were isolated from the images to calculate the TRAP+ cell density and the ratio of the mean total cross-sectional bone area to the mean total cross-sectional tissue area (B.Ar/T.Ar).

Immunohistochemical protocols

Heat-mediated antigen retrieval was employed. Sections were heated in a water bath at 100°C for 15 minutes, then washed with 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with H₂O₂ to prevent false-positive results. Non-specific antigen sites were blocked using diluted goat serum.. After incubation with primary antibodies against OPN, RUNX2, OCN, and F4/80 (Affinity, China, 1:200) for 12 h at 4°C, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at 37°C for 60 min. A streptavidin-biotinylated horseradish peroxidase complex (HRP) was added, and a diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (Maxim, China) was added to visualize the sections using a microscope.

Osteogenic lingeage cell isolation and culture

To harvest osteoblasts (OBs), cranial pieces from neonatal Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (2–3 days old) were digested with collagenase II (Solarbio, China) at 37°C for 1–2 hours. The cells were then isolated by centrifugation and cultured until passage 3 (P3) for subsequent analysis. The cells were cultured in α-MEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, China).

Cell proliferation, ALP activity and calcium nodule induction

Osteoblasts (OBs) were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 4×10³ cells/well. After 24 hours of culture, the cells were treated with varying concentrations of zoledronate (ZOL) (0, 0.1, 0.5, 1.0, or 2.0 µg/ml). The treated cells were subsequently incubated for different time intervals with CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Japan) for 1 hour to assess cellular proliferation. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured to quantify the proliferation levels.

For visual detection of proliferation, crystal violet staining was performed. OBs were seeded at a low density of 2×10⁴ cells/well and at a high density of 5×10⁴ cells/well into 24-well plates. To evaluate osteogenic activity, OBs were seeded at a density of 2×10⁴ cells/well in 12-well plates. Once cells reached approximately 80% confluence, the medium was replaced with osteogenic induction medium (10 mM β-glycerophosphate, 50 µg/ml ascorbic acid, and 10 nM dexamethasone). The osteogenic induction process was terminated after 24 days, and ZOL was administered at concentrations of 0, 0.02, 0.1, or 0.5 µg/ml for 7 days.

For the assessment of alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity, OBs were seeded into 24-well plates at densities of 2×10⁴ cells/well for low density and 5×10⁴ cells/well for high density. ALP activity staining kits (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were used to evaluate osteogenic differentiation. (Note: osteogenic induction medium was not applied for ALP activity assessment). The medium was refreshed every three days. Stained sections were imaged using a stereomicroscope (Stemi 508, ZEISS, Germany).

Bone marrow macrophage isolation and OC induction

Bone marrow cells were isolated from the tibia and femur of male SD rats (8–9 weeks). After a 21-gauge spinal needle penetrated the bone marrow, the bone marrow was flushed out with PBS, and the liquid was filtered through a 40 µm filter. After centrifugation, the cells were collected and incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. Primate macrophages were induced with M-CSF (20 ng/ml) (PeproTech, USA) for 48 h. After removing the nonadherent cells, the remaining macrophages were subjected to further detection. For osteoclast (OC) induction, we used M-CSF and RANKL (Amizona Scientific, USA) to induce monocyte differentiation into osteoclasts. BMMs were seeded (1x104 cells per well) in 48-well plates. The cells were cultured with M-CSF (40 ng/ml) for 12 h. After removing the nonadherent cells, M-CSF (30 ng/ml) was added to the wells. RANKL (40 ng/ml) was added on the third day of culture for 48 h. ZOL (0.5 µg/ml) was added simultaneously with RANKL. At the end of culture, the cells were fixed and stained for TRAP. OC was detected on the fifth day and reached its peak on the seventh day. TRAP + cells with more than two nuclei were considered a mature OCs. Pictures were acquired with a stereomicroscope (Stemi 508, ZEISS, Germany). The number of OCs was calculated via Photoshop 2019 (Adobe, USA).

Immunofluorescence and live-cell fluorescence detection

Osteoblasts obtained from digested neonatal cranial tissue as described previously were used for the P3 generation. Primate macrophages from rat bone marrow previously induced with M-CSF were also detected for their characteristics. After being washed with 4% PBS, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Triton X-100 (0.5%) was added to the samples for 20 min. Then, 5% BSA was added to block nonspecific binding. Actin Red (KeyGEN, China), which was used for tracking cytoskeleton staining, was added to the actin filaments, which were then incubated for 30 min and directly observed under a fluorescence microscope. Rabbit anti-rat BMP4 (Affinity, USA), rabbit anti-rat OCN (Affinity, USA), and rabbit anti-rat F4/80 (Affinity, USA) were added to 10% goat serum and incubated overnight. FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (KeyGEN, China) was added to the sections, which were subsequently incubated in the dark at 37°C for 1 hour. DAPI (KeyGEN, China) was subsequently used as a nuclear stain. The fluorescence of living lysosomal cells in macrophages was detected with LysoTracker Green (KeyGen, China). Images were acquired via CLSM (FV3000, Olympus, Japan).

Statistical analysis

The images from BSE-SEM, CLSM, ALP, crystal violet staining and Alizarin red staining were transferred to binary grayscale images via Photoshop 2019 (Adobe, USA) and analyzed via CTan software: generally, the grayscale index of images was regarded as the signal intensity, which was transferred into quantitative data. The data were found to follow a normal distribution via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. For in vivo data, the statistical analysis of the bone volume fraction (BV/TV) data was performed via one-way ANOVA, and Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test was used for multiple group comparisons. All other data were tested via an independent samples t test for paired group comparisons. For in vitro experimental data, Tukey's HSD test was used for multiple group comparisons. The analysis of variance was performed via SPSS 23.0 software (IBM, USA). Graphs were made with Origin 2016 software (Microcal, USA). A value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Micro-CT analysis revealed that deproteinized bovine bones (DBBs) were surrounded by newly formed bone (Fig. 2A, original image; Supplementary Material 1). By applying an appropriate threshold value, trabeculae were distinguished from the DBBs and bone marrow (Fig. 2A, binary image). Bone formation in the defect region was more pronounced in the ZOL group, with the proportion of new bone increasing between 2 and 4 weeks, unlike the control group, where it declined after 2 weeks (Fig. 2B).

The measured bone thickness (Tb.Th) and degree of separation (Tb.Sp) showed that ZOL increased Tb.Th and decreased Tb.Sp, suggesting a relatively low bone turnover rate (Fig. 2D, E). However, bone density in the region of interest (ROI) was significantly lower in the ZOL group compared to the control group at 4 and 8 weeks (Fig. 2C). Pseudocolor imaging further illustrated that in the intramedullary area, abundant but low-density calluses formed around the grafts in the ZOL group, persisting even at 8 weeks (Fig. 2H). This immature bone invaded the cortical area and appeared to disrupt bone–graft integration (Fig. 2H, red arrow). Raman spectroscopy also confirmed that ZOL induced lower mineralization levels on the surface of the cortical defect area (Fig. 2I).

Histology: compromised graft–host integration and delayed maturation

H&E staining revealed a prominent presence of both osteoblasts and osteoclasts in the ZOL-treated group at 2 weeks (Fig. 3A). Consistent with the radiological findings, histological analysis demonstrated that ZOL enhanced bone generation (Fig. 3B, C, E). However, in the ZOL group, hyperplastic calluses invaded the cortex, whereas in the control group, direct contact between the DBBs and cortical bone was observed (Fig. 3B, C, dotted red square). At 8 weeks, a distinct healing line between the new and old bone in the ZOL group indicated poor bone–bone binding (Fig. 3C, yellow arrow).

Immunohistochemical results showed increased OPN and OCN expression in the ZOL group, while RUNX2 expression was lower (Fig. 3D, G, H, I). This might be the reason that OPN and OCN is expressed in conjunction with active osteogenic activity [25], while RUNX2 controls bone maturation and terminates bone regeneration [26].

ZOL enhanced macrophage activity but suppressed osteoclastogenesis in vivo

Considering the close relationship between macrophages, osteoclasts, and osteogenesis, we analyzed the activity of macrophages (F4/80-positive cells) and osteoclasts (TRAP-positive cells). Both groups showed high F4/80 expression in the early phase, which rapidly declined in subsequent days. In the ZOL group, macrophages initially concentrated around the defect region and gradually dispersed into the graft gap (Fig. 4A).

The effect of ZOL on osteoclasts was more complex than on macrophages. The ZOL group exhibited more osteoclasts at 2 and 4 weeks (Fig. 4A). However, their distribution differed between the groups. In the ZOL group, osteoclasts were primarily located on the lateral wall of the defect region, while in the control group, they were more evenly distributed (Fig. 4A, see week 2 images). Regarding the balance between bone formation and resorption, Ob.N/Oc.N parameters indicated that ZOL disrupted normal bone remodeling, a pattern typically seen when osteoclast activity is suppressed (Fig. 4C, D-F) [27, 28].

Undecalcified tissue detection: Abnormal lacunocanalicular network and hardness

Over the period from 4 to 8 weeks, the overall scale of the canaliculi network decreased. Comparing the two groups, the ZOL group exhibited a higher number of osteocytes. Notably, when focusing on the canaliculi extending from the lacunae, the control group demonstrated a greater number of canals, potentially indicating a more active and mature cell-to-cell connection (Fig. 5A). BSE-SEM was employed to quantify osteocyte numbers, revealing fewer lacunae (osteocytes) but greater mineralization in the newly formed bone of the control group (Fig. 5B, D, E).

Since acid etching removes mineral content and exposes collagen, Rhodamine Phalloidin was used to stain the bone collagen, as higher collagen content generally indicates less mature bone. Beyond the observed differences in collagen richness in the woven bone areas, the cortical area also displayed higher mineralization in the control group (Fig. 5C, F). Additional analysis through another bone regeneration model demonstrated a reduction in the transverse hardness of the femur bone under ZOL treatment (Fig. 5G).

In vitro part

ZOL suppressed calcium nodule formation but did not negatively affect osteogenic marker activity

ALP staining was employed to assess the impact of ZOL on osteogenetic activity. The staining intensity appeared to correlate strongly with cell proliferation rather than ALP viability. When cells were seeded at low density, staining intensity showed a positive correlation with the final cell density (Fig. 6C, M). By seeding at high density, this proliferation variable was controlled, resulting in a significant increase in ALP staining intensity at ZOL stimulation, indicating enhanced ALP activity at the set concentration (0.1 and 0.5 µg, Fig. 6C-F, K-L). The inhibition of proliferation was further confirmed by the osteoblast CCK-8 assay after 7 days of culture (Fig. 6P), and Cell morphology indicative of apoptosis was observed in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6H). Regarding osteogenetic activity, ZOL did not downregulate the expression of BMP4 or OCN; in fact, their expression was elevated after three days of high-dose ZOL stimulation (Fig. 6A-B, I, J).

A-B, I, J ZOL upregulated the expression of BMP4 and OCN in osteoblasts at typical concentrations (*P < 0.05). C, E, K, L After seeding at a low density, ALP staining revealed that the amount of colored sediment in the culture well decreased with ZOL stimulation in a dose-dependent manner. At a higher density, the trend reversed. D, F, P ZOL decreased cell proliferation in a time- and concentration-dependent manner. G, O ZOL strongly hindered calcium nodule formation at all concentrations. H ZOL altered the cellular morphology and induced apoptosis. 1x = 0.02 µg/ml, 5x = 0.1 µg/ml, 25x = 0.5 µg/ml

In contrast to the expression of osteogenetic proteins, calcium nodule formation was significantly inhibited. Even at low concentrations, calcium nodules were scarcely detectable (Fig. 6G, O). Compared with the expression of typical osteogenetic proteins, the mineralization phase appeared more sensitive and vulnerable to ZOL stimulation.

Osteoclastic lineage culture: ZOL induced macrophage apoptosis and interfered with osteoclast fusion

After 3 days of culture with M-CSF, the isolated monocytes differentiated into macrophages, characterized by abundant lysosomes and typical F4/80 membrane markers (Fig. 7A, B). Under ZOL stimulation, these macrophages exhibited high levels of annexin V, an apoptosis marker (Fig. 7C, F). Notably, the fusion of preosteoclasts into mature osteoclasts was significantly disrupted, with few multinucleated cells observed even at low doses of ZOL (Fig. 7D, F)

Discussion

In this study, ZOL was found to induce compromised mineralization and delayed osseoconsolidation in the bone grafting model. Similarly, BPs-induced impaired fracture healing and compromised new bone quality, characterized by thickening of calluses, have also been reported by other researchers in classic defect healing models [10, 29–31]. However, in contrast to our findings on bone hardness, other studies using torsional tests have suggested that BPs do not negatively impact bone strength in fracture models [32]. We hypothesize that the increased number of calluses at the fracture site may compensates for the impaired bone maturation. In the current study, the new bone in the grafting region was too thin for Vickers hardness testing, so the cortical bone of juvenile rats was used as a substitute. Future studies could employ more precise techniques, such as microindentation or nanoindentation hardness testing, to characterize the newly formed bone in the grafting region.

In this study, BPs appeared to induce opposite effect on osteoclasts in vitro and in vivo. For in vitro part, consistent with the previous studies demonstrating the apoptosis-inducing effects of BPs on osteoclasts, this study proved that even relatively low concentrations of ZOL significantly hindered osteoclast formation. However, the in vivo situation seems more complicated. Interestingly, other studies have also reported an increase in osteoclast numbers after BPs stimulation in rodent models [30, 33, 34]. On the basis of clinical data, Robert et al. reported giant but nonfunctional osteoclast formation in the ilium of a patient receiving long-term BPs therapy [35]. Some researchers suggest that these stimulated osteoclasts are either undergoing apoptosis or are nonfunctional due to detachment from bone[36, 37]. In the present study, rather than simply counting osteoclasts number, we assessed the osteoblast/osteoclast ratio to better represent bone remodelling status. In the further experiments, the detection of osteoclasts’ ultrastructure and typical apoptotic makers could be applied to figure out its cellular activity in BPs stimulation.

Compared with the yearly dose used to treat osteoporosis in humans (0.1 mg/kg), we selected a relatively higher dose to better represent potential drug accumulation effects, as most patients suffering from BRONJ have a long history of drug administration [38]. The drug dose indeed had differential impacts on osteogenesis in both in vivo and in vitro studies. In this study, ZOL inhibited osteoblast proliferation in a dose- and time-dependent manner. Other researchers have observed that in vitro osteogenic activity increased in the concentration from 0.05 mg–0.5 mg/kg, but no difference between 0.5 mg–1 mg/kg [39]. In vivo, however, high-dose does not always lead to negative effects; for example, some studies report that short-term high-dose ZOL enhances jaw crystallinity (0.6 mg/kg) [40], and others highlight its chondroprotective effects [41]. The relationship between drug dose and bone characteristic still needs further investigation.

Importantly, we observed lower bone density under ZOL stimulation, which may contradict prior findings [42, 43]. Evaluating differences in methodologies might be one of the causes of these differences. Compared with clinical evaluations of bone density, which usually rely on dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and evaluate the whole organ, Micro-CT can focus on the average trabecular density (TMD) [44]. To gain a more precise view of trabecular mineralization level, we used BSE-SEM and collagen richness detection in nondecalcified samples, with results consistent with the Micro-CT data.

Osteocytes are known to play a crucial role in secondary bone mineralization [45, 46]. The lacunar-canalicular network (LCN) serves as a critical pathway for osteocytes to mediate mineralization [47]. This study demonstrated that ZOL induces an immature canaliculus of osteocytes. Whether this degenerated LCN is due to BPs’ direct impact on osteocytes or a consequence of osteoclast inhibition remains unclear. Although the underlying mechanism for this jeopardized mineralization is still unclear, it should be clear that BPs could directly obstruct calcium apatite crystal formation. By mimicking organic pyrophosphoric acid (PPi) but not hydrolysis with pyrophosphatase, Bps can inhibit abnormal ectopic mineralization at high concentrations [48–53]. Nevertheless, considering the dose used in our study, it is far below the concentration that directly inhibits crystal formation. For BPs, the R2 side chain determines the antiresorptive ability, which makes zoledronate 10,000 times greater than that of nitrogen-free BPs [54]. Clinically, non-nitrogen-containing BPs like etidronate are preferred to prevent ectopic mineralization and avoid severe osteonecrosis [49]. Therefore, instead of chemical pathways inhibiting calcium apatite crystal formation, the biological process by impacting the osteoblast/osteoclasts should be cast with more attention for the delayed mineralization.

The role of osteoclasts in bone maturation has garnered increasing attention over the past decades. In the clinic, osteopetrosis is a rare genetic disorder characterized by the abnormal thickening and hardening of bones. Osteoclast-related gene knockout (M-CSF receptor, OPG receptor, TRAP, Snx10) mice shares similar osteopetrotic phenotype, and some has the characteristics of jeopardized mineralization in the cortex [55–57]. Other evidence from in vitro studies, such as Cecilia et al.'s work, reveals the potential positive role of TRAP in skeletal mineralization [58]. Although osteoclasts could modulate osteogenesis both in vivo and in vitro [59, 60], the underlying mechanism is still unclear. More evidence is needed to elucidate the relationship between osteoclasts and bone maturation.

In addition to local bone quantity, its quality should also be considered, especially when assessing the loading requirement for implants. Our study revealed a decrease in the physical strength of ZOL-treated bone, suggesting that the timing for implant loading may need to be delayed in BP-treated patients. Although increased bone mass has been reported following the application of anti-resorptive drug-modified allografts [61] or implants [62], this strategy should be employed cautiously, as compromised mineralization and impaired bonding strength may occur.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the negative effect of zoledronate on bone remodeling. The delayed mineralization and impaired intracortical healing indicate that more caution should be taken when using this drug for bone regeneration. Further studies are essential to investigate the interaction between mineralization and osteoclasts activity.
