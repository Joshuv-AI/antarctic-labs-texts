Effects of Limited English Proficiency on Outcomes Following Burn Injury
Authors: Andrea Rosmery Iturralde Carrillo, Larissa Epstein, Carina Franco, Jennie Caluya, Natalie Coca, Krista L Haines, Jason Heard, Soman Sen, Tina Palmieri, Kathleen S Romanowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudies indicate that surgical patients with limited English proficiency (LEP) experience longer hospital stays and higher emergency department (ED) recidivism. Burn care requires complex coordination for both inpatient and outpatient services, regardless of language preference. This study hypothesized that burn patients with LEP would have longer hospital stays, fewer follow-ups, and more ED visits. Following IRB approval, a retrospective chart review analyzed burn patients admitted between January 2018 and December 2019. Collected data included demographics, burn characteristics, outcomes, preferred language, and follow-up care. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS (version 9.4), employing chi-square, Fisher exact, Spearman correlation, Wilcoxon two-sample, and Kruskal-Wallis tests. A total of 751 patients (median age: 46 years, IQR: 26) were included, with 555 (73%) males and a median total body surface area (TBSA) burn of 6.5% (IQR: 12). Among them, 15% had inhalation injuries, and 5% died. Sixty-one patients (8%) identified a preferred language other than English. LEP and English-speaking patients showed no significant differences in burn size (5.5% vs 6.6%, P = .32), mortality (3.3% vs 4.7%, P = 1), length of stay (8 vs 9 days, P = .43), or discharge to home (86.9% vs 75.9%, P = .44). However, LEP patients were more likely to have scheduled follow-ups (93.4% vs 80.2%, P = .04), attend visits (78.7% vs 50.7%, P = .0005), and revisit the ED (19.7% vs 10.5%, P = .03). These findings highlight disparities in discharge education and outpatient care, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to improve post-burn recovery in LEP populations.Keywords: low English proficiency, burn, outcomes, follow-up

INTRODUCTION

The United States is a country of diverse cultures and languages. According to the US Census Bureau, the population in 2020 was 331 449 281, and 21.7% of the population speak languages other than English at home. Additionally, over 8% of the US population has limited English proficiency (LEP).1,2 LEP refers to individuals who do not speak English as their primary language and who have a limited ability to read, speak, write, or understand English.3 Effective communication is essential to establishing a doctor-patient relationship and delivering high-quality care. However, language differences can be a significant barrier, directly affecting doctor-patient communication and creating a gap that contributes to disparities.4

Burn injuries remain a considerable public health concern. According to the American Burn Association, there are 30 115 burn hospital admissions per year and 6844 cases involve concomitant trauma injury.5 Burn patients often require prolonged hospitalizations, multiple operations with very precise postoperative management, and long-term follow-up following hospital discharge, all of which require effective communication between healthcare providers and patients to ensure optimal wound care and the best possible postoperative recovery. Patient outcomes could be affected in patients with LEP who face difficulties completing appropriate medical treatment due to miscommunication or misunderstanding, resulting in poor short-term and long-term outcomes.4,6

While many hospitals provide trained interpreters for LEP patients, studies have shown that medical teams often underutilize these resources.7 LEP is associated with worse healthcare and outcomes overall.7 In the surgical field, the evidence of LEP’s impact is contradictory as shown in studies by Santana et al. and Gamboa et al. which suggest that there were no significant differences in outcomes for LEP patients. In contrast, Liu et al. report worse outcomes for patients with LEP that led to delays in surgery and more complications such as multidrug-resistant organism infections and anemia. These conflicting findings highlight the need for further investigation into the impact of LEP on surgical care, particularly among burn patients, to better understand the disparities that LEP patients face.8–10

Despite the possible association between LEP and poor outcomes, few studies have examined its effect on post-burn outcomes. A study of pediatric patients found no difference in outcomes for patients with LEP, but more research was needed to fully understand the issues faced by patients with LEP.11 In our current study, we aim to examine outcomes in our adult patients with burn injuries who have LEP. We hypothesize that LEP patients will have different injury characteristics and, due to communication difficulties, patients with LEP will have longer hospital stays, fewer follow-up visits, and more ED visits than those with English proficiency.

METHODS

Following institutional review board approval, a retrospective chart review was conducted using the electronic medical records (EMR) of all adult patients admitted to a regional American Burn Association verified burn center from January 1, 2018, to December 31, 2019. Patients with nonburn injuries, such as desquamating skin conditions, were excluded. Additionally, prisoners were also excluded from the study population.

Data were collected on patient demographics, burn size (%TBSA), days on a ventilator, length of stay (LOS), Intensive Care Unit (ICU) days, discharge disposition, primary payer source, follow-up appointments (recommendation for, scheduling of, and attendance), Emergency Department (ED) visits related to the burn injury, readmissions, homelessness, and comorbidities. We collected information on follow-up appointments, ED visits, and readmissions within 30 days of discharge. ED visits and readmissions were identified using recorded visits at our institution or patient-self-reported visits at outside institutions as written in the medical record or documented phone conversations.

To determine LEP among our patients, we defined LEP as a preferred language other than English for our retrospective analysis. The preferred language was collected from the information section in the EMR, which is populated during a patient’s first encounter with our institution. We further categorized our population as patients with LEP and English as a primary language (EPL). Patients who died prior to determining their preferred language were excluded.

SAS statistical software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), was used to analyze the data. A Shapiro-Wilk test for normality was used to determine if continuous variables had a normal distribution. None of the continuous variables in this study had a parametric distribution. Therefore, a Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to analyze continuous variables. Chi-square testing was used to determine significant differences in proportions and categorical data, except for small cell sizes. When there were small cell sizes for a particular data point, the Fisher exact test or the Monte Carlo estimate for the Fisher exact test was used. Univariate analysis was done on all variables in relation to English proficiency. Multivariate regression analysis with backward elimination was performed to assess factors associated with English proficiency. All variables significant on univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis. All mean values are represented as mean ± standard deviation, and all median values are represented as median (interquartile range). Statistical significance was determined by a P-value <.05.

RESULTS

There were 751 patients included and 61 (8.12%) had LEP. Demographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 42 years (IQR: 22) for the LEP group and 46.5 years (IQR: 26) for the EPL group (P = .6461). There was no difference in the gender distribution between patients with LEP and their EPL counterparts. The LEP group was 63.93% male, while the EPL group was 73.62% male. There was no statistically significant difference in homelessness between the 2 groups (3 [4.92%] vs 96 [13.91%], P = .18). Regarding race, the LEP group comprised 21.31% Asian, 4.92% Caucasian, and 73.77% other race, with no individuals identifying as American Indian/Alaskan Native, Black, or Native Hawaiian. The LEP group also had a higher percentage of Hispanic or Latino individuals (68.85% vs 14.78%) and patients covered by Medicaid insurance (62.3% vs 48.55%). Race, ethnicity, and type of insurance were considered statistically significant with a P < .05.

In looking at outcomes following burn injury, we found that there were no differences in ICU days (2 days [IQR: 16] vs 3 days [IQR 15]), ventilator days (0 days [IQR: 0] vs 0 days [IQR: 0]), or LOS (8 days [IQR: 16] vs 9 days [IQR: 18]) when the LEP group is compared to the EPL group (Table 5). The mortality rate was similar between the groups, with no statistically significant difference (LEP: 3.28% vs EPL: 4.78%; P = 1) (Table 6). Regarding discharge disposition, most patients in both groups were discharged to their homes (LEP: 86.895 vs EPL: 76.38%). The readmission rate in the LEP group (11.48%) was twice that of the EPL group (5.65%), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (P = .09). A follow-up appointment was scheduled for the patient prior to discharge more frequently in the LEP group (93.44% vs 80.2%, P = .04). Additionally, clinic follow-up attendance was significantly higher in the LEP group (78.69%) compared to the EPL group (50.51%) (P = .0004). Despite the higher rate of scheduled and attended follow-ups in the LEP group, ED visits were more frequent in the LEP group (19.67%) compared to the EPL group (10.6%) (P = .03) (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

This study is the first to examine the impact of LEP on outcomes in adult burn patients, uncovering a previously underrecognized pattern of healthcare utilization. Unlike prior research in surgical populations, burn care uniquely depends on precise ongoing communication across inpatient and outpatient settings. Contrary to our hypothesis, LEP patients did not have longer hospital stays, higher mortality, or more severe burns. However, they were more likely to have scheduled follow-ups, attend clinic visits, and return to the ED after discharge. These findings suggest that while clinical outcomes were similar, communication gaps—particularly at discharge—may contribute to increased healthcare use.

There is limited literature on LEP in burn care, though several studies have assessed its impact in other surgical settings. Stokes et al. found associations between LEP and race/ethnicity, which aligns with our findings.11 However, our study also identified a significant difference in insurance status: Medicaid covered 62.3% of LEP patients. Consistent with Stolarski et al.’s bariatric surgery study, comorbidities did not differ significantly between groups.12 We did observe a higher prevalence of substance use disorder among EPL patients—an area warranting further exploration for potential cultural differences in reporting or diagnosis.

When comparing our adult burn findings to pediatric data, we also found no significant differences in LOS or readmission rates between LEP and EPL groups—consistent with the study by Santana et al. in children with burn injuries.8 While LEP patients had twice the readmission rate, this was not statistically significant. Nevertheless, it emphasizes the importance of providing discharge instructions in a patient’s preferred language to reduce readmissions and optimize resource use. Our findings contrast with studies by Luan-Erfe et al., Lui et al., and Stokes et al., which reported longer LOS in LEP patients.10,11,13 This discrepancy may stem from our smaller sample size.

We found higher rates of follow-up scheduling and clinic attendance among LEP patients—contrary to the findings from Santana et al., Luan-Erfe et al., and Liu et al.8,10,13 This may reflect institutional awareness of the challenges LEP patients face (eg, navigating English-only phone systems), prompting providers to take extra steps in ensuring continuity of care. Despite increased follow-ups, LEP patients still had higher numbers of post-discharge ED visits, potentially due to misunderstanding care plans or hesitancy to communicate over the phone. Similar patterns were reported by Liu et al. and Stolarski et al.10,12

The emotional and cognitive burden of navigating healthcare in a second language—especially under stress—can impact both health behaviors and perceived well-being.14 Interpreter services have been shown to improve quality of care, reduce readmissions, enhance postoperative outcomes, and address mental health care needs.10 While our study found no differences in hospital outcomes or mortality, in line with most prior studies, these data reinforce the critical role of communication in high-quality care.8,9,11,12,15,16

LIMITATIONS

This study’s primary is its novel focus on LEP in adult burn patients, highlighting underreported healthcare utilization trends. However, the small sample size of LEP patients may have limited our ability to detect statistically significant differences. As a retrospective chart review, the study is subject to limitations in data inaccuracy and completeness. Information from patient encounters outside our institution’s EMR network may not have been captured, potentially underestimating healthcare utilization. Future multicenter, prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and guide interventions to improve care for LEP patients.

CONCLUSION

Burn injury management is complex and requires coordinated efforts from physicians and patients. Beyond the immediate medical needs, burns carry significant emotional and logistical challenges for patients and families. Successful recovery depends on the patient’s active participation. Clear post-discharge education—delivered in the patient’s preferred language—is essential, particularly regarding infection prevention and wound care. Interpreter services play a key role in bridging communication gaps and ensuring effective follow-up. We must make further efforts to create effective communication and cultivate stronger relationships with our patients. Optimizing emergency care and follow-up strategies will help reduce long-term complications and enhance healthcare outcomes for LEP patients.
