Microglial IL-27 modulates depression-like behaviors induced by postnatal immune activation
Authors: Xinghui Qiao, Naigang Li, Yuan Yao, Xinyue Zhang, Yanrong Wang, Xuan Zhang, Tiantian Zhao, Dong Wu, Shangming Liu, Jingyi Du, Dongshuang Wang, Can Diao, Liyan Wang, Fenglin Cao, Wenjuan Zhou, Aijun Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryEarly-life inflammation increases the risk of mental disorders later in life by altering the long-term microglial capacity for neuronal spine engulfment, highlighting a tightly regulated neuroimmune interaction. However, how local immune signals modulate microglial function remains unclear. Here, we show that microglia-associated IL-27-IL-27Rα signaling alleviates depression-like behaviors induced by postnatal immune activation (PIA). At the cellular level, IL-27 treatment suppresses excessive microglial phagocytic activity, thereby preserving synaptic density and preventing synaptic loss. Notably, its beneficial effects extend beyond the PIA model, as IL-27 also ameliorates behavioral deficits in prenatal stress-exposed mice. Importantly, animal safety evaluations support the tolerability of IL-27 administration. These effects are mediated, in part, through the STAT1-Trem2-dependent mechanism. Collectively, these findings support IL-27 as a protective immunoregulatory factor and highlight its therapeutic potential for mood disorders associated with neurodevelopmental immune dysregulation.

Introduction

Postnatal immune activation (PIA) refers to a non-specific systemic adaptive response triggered by infectious or non-infectious stimuli during early life.1 Such events can disrupt normal brain development, with long-lasting effects on physiological and behavioral systems throughout the lifespan2,3 and an increased risk of psychoneurological disorders later in life.4

Microglia, the brain’s resident immune cells, have garnered significant attention as pivotal regulators of neural circuit patterning and synaptic refinement during early development and as modulators of brain homeostasis throughout life. In response to inflammatory stimulation, microglia shift to a reactive state, exhibiting increased migration and phagocytic activity. While transient activation is protective, excessive or prolonged activation can impair brain development, with effects persisting into adulthood.5,6 Notably, heightened microglial activity has been strongly associated with depression, as evidenced by autopsy studies of individuals with a history of suicide.7,8,9,10,11 This highlights the critical role of microglial dysregulation in mood disorders.4,12 However, the cell-intrinsic signaling pathways and cell-extrinsic cues that govern the developmental programming of microglia remain poorly understood.

Cytokines, key immune effectors, play a significant role in modulating neuronal development, synaptic plasticity, and behavior.5 Neuroimmune dysfunction underlies a wide range of pathologies, from sickness behavior to neurodevelopmental and psychiatric conditions. Among these, interleukin (IL)-17 has been implicated in synaptic plasticity, anxiety, short-term memory, and sociability,13 and IL-1β is involved in anxiety and depression.14 As the resident macrophages of the brain parenchyma, microglia are highly responsive to cytokine signaling and a diverse array of peripheral cues. Investigations into the precise roles of microglia and their responses to immune challenges have predominantly focused on T helper 1 (Th1) and T helper 17 (Th17)-mediated pathways.15 IL-27 has been shown to regulate Th1 and Th17 cell responses, promoting Th1 differentiation while suppressing Th17 activity.16 And alterations in Th17 cell activity are strongly linked to depression.17 In our study, IL-27 was identified as a significantly dysregulated cytokine in the PIA model, and given its known role in modulating microglial activity, we selected it for further investigation.17,18 Furthermore, studies have demonstrated its neuroprotective effects in conditions such as cerebral hemorrhage and multiple sclerosis, as well as its ability to mitigate neuropathic pain following peripheral nerve injury.19 However, the role of this cytokine in neurodevelopment and its implications for neurodevelopmental disorders remain poorly understood.

Given the strong association between neuroimmune dysfunction and psychiatric disorders,13,20,21,22,23 we investigated the potential role of IL-27 in modulating early-life neuroimmune interactions. We examined several emotion-related brain regions in our model and found that the hippocampus exhibited the most prominent changes, consistent with its established role in emotional regulation and early postnatal remodeling. We, therefore, focused on how IL-27 in the hippocampus influences the behavioral and cellular consequences of PIA. We found that IL-27 administration attenuated depression- and anxiety-like behaviors in adult mice exposed to PIA and modulated microglial phagocytic activity via Trem2 signaling. In addition, we conducted complementary experiments in a prenatal stress model (PNS) and performed preliminary safety assessments, aiming to explore the broader relevance and translational potential of IL-27. These findings suggest that IL-27 may be a promising immunoregulatory target for mood disorders associated with early-life immune stress.

Results

IL-27 is upregulated in microglia of PIA mice

In our study, we established the PIA model (Figure S1A) and assessed depression- and anxiety-like behaviors in mice using various behavioral tests, including the sucrose preference test (SPT), tail suspension test (TST), forced swim test (FST), open field test (OFT), and elevated plus maze (EPM) (Figures S1B–S1D). Mice in the PIA group exhibited significant behavioral deficits in adulthood compared with controls. Immunofluorescence staining of hippocampal sections indicated elevated protein levels of inflammatory markers such as IL-1β and TNF-α in the PIA group relative to the control group (Figures S1E–S1G). Quantitative reverse-transcription PCR analysis also indicated elevated mRNA levels of inflammatory markers, such as IL-1β and TNF-α, in the PIA group relative to the control group, both in the hippocampus and in the cerebral cortex; however, the elevated trend in IL-6 expression was more pronounced in the cerebral cortex (Figure S1I). In addition, a time-course analysis showed that hippocampal IL-6 mRNA expression followed an early peak-rapid decline pattern, with a peak at 12 h and a clear decrease by 24 h, highlighting region-specific regulation of the inflammatory response (Figure S1J). Furthermore, a marked increase in IBA1 expression was observed (Figures S1F and S1H), indicating pronounced microglial activation and confirming the successful construction of the PIA model.

After completion of behavioral testing in adult mice, hippocampal tissues were collected for total RNA extraction and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to identify persistent transcriptional alterations associated with PIA (Figure 1A). RNA-seq revealed a significant elevation of IL-27 in PIA mice (Figure 1B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis showed enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway (Figure 1C). Our previous study also detected a significant increase in the expression level of IL-27 in microglia of adolescent mice in a mouse model of depression.24 Considering the regulatory role of IL-27 in autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, including multiple sclerosis (MS), we investigated its expression and role in the PIA model. IL-27 mRNA expression in the hippocampus was significantly upregulated at 12 h after PIA induction, followed by a gradual decline, yet remained elevated compared with baseline up to day 7 (Figure 1D). Immunohistochemistry and western blot confirmed a significant increase in IL-27 protein levels 24 h post-PIA (Figures 1E and 1F). We also detected the expression of IL-27 in other brain regions, including the dorsal hippocampus (dHC), ventral hippocampus (vHC), medial prefrontal cortex, and nucleus accumbens. To identify the cellular source and potential targets of IL-27 following PIA, we performed co-immunofluorescence staining of IL-27 and its receptor (IL-27R) with cell-type markers. IL-27 was primarily expressed in microglia and, to a lesser extent, in astrocytes, with a significantly higher proportion of IL-27+ microglia observed in the PIA group compared with controls (Figures 1G, 1H, and 1M). Consistent with previous reports identifying astrocytes as a cellular source of IL-27,25 we observed IL-27 immunoreactivity in astrocytes. Although a slight increase was noted in the PIA group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (Figures 1H and 1M). In contrast, neurons exhibit minimal IL-27 expression (Figures 1I–1M), suggesting that glial cells—particularly microglia—are the primary source of IL-27 after PIA.

Previous reports have shown high IL-27 receptor (IL-27R) expression in monocytes and macrophages.18,26 To determine which brain cells might respond to IL-27 following PIA, we assessed IL-27R co-localization with cell-type markers. Our findings demonstrated a significant co-localization of IL-27R with glial markers. Specifically, co-labeling of IL-27R and IBA1 increased approximately 10-fold in the PIA group compared with the control group (Figures 1J–1N), while there was no significant increase in co-labeling of IL-27R with GFAP or NeuN (Figures 1K, 1L, and 1N).

To further support our findings, sequencing analysis of primary microglia treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in vitro revealed an increase in IL-27 expression (Figures S2A and S2B), and these findings were confirmed in subsequent experiments showing consistent changes in mRNA and protein levels corresponding to LPS manipulation (Figures S2C–S2I). Collectively, these findings indicate that IL-27 is predominantly secreted by activated microglia and exerts its effects via IL-27R expressed on microglia, positioning microglial IL-27 as a potential therapeutic target in PIA-induced neuroinflammation and depression.

IL-27 functionally regulates PIA-induced behavioral abnormalities in mice

To elucidate the specific role of IL-27 in PIA-associated depression-like behaviors, we conducted gain- and loss-of-function experiments using recombinant IL-27 (rIL-27) and a neutralizing antibody, respectively. In the gain-of-function arm, we performed a series of behavioral tests in PIA mice following short-term stereotactic injection of rIL-27 (Figure 2A). PIA mice exhibited significant depression- and anxiety-like behaviors in adulthood, which were markedly attenuated by neonatal IL-27 treatment administered once daily from postnatal day 3 to day 5 (P3–P5). For depression-like behaviors, IL-27 substantially decreased the immobility time in both TST and FST and increased sucrose consumption in SPT (Figure 2B). For anxiety-like behaviors, IL-27 did not affect locomotor activity. However, it reversed PIA-induced reductions in center time in the OFT (Figure 2C) and increased time spent in the open arms in the EPM (Figure 2D), suggesting that the observed emotional improvement was not driven by increased locomotor activity. To further evaluate the endogenous role of IL-27 in PIA, as a loss-of-function approach, we neutralized circulating IL-27 using an anti-IL-27 antibody (targeting IL-27 p28) at the onset of PIA modeling (Figure S3A). Mice treated with anti-IL-27 displayed more severe symptoms of depression and anxiety than those in the PIA group, highlighting the protective role of endogenous IL-27 in PIA. For depression-like behaviors, IL-27 neutralization significantly prolonged the immobility time in the TST and also reduced sucrose consumption, indicating an exacerbation of depression-like behaviors (Figure S3B). Anxiety-like behaviors were also aggravated, as shown by further reductions in center time during the OFT (Figure S3C) and in open-arm exploration in the EPM (Figure S3D). These results suggest that endogenous IL-27 plays a critical modulatory role in limiting the severity of PIA-induced behavioral deficits.

Building on our observation that IL-27 alleviates behavioral abnormalities in the PIA model, we next examined whether sustained IL-27 expression could yield prolonged therapeutic benefits. To this end, we overexpressed IL-27 using an AAV9-F4/80-IL-27-GFP vector designed to preferentially target microglia, which was injected into the lateral ventricle27,28 (Figure 2E). Robust GFP expression was observed in the hippocampus at postnatal day 19 and predominantly co-localized with IBA1+ microglia, with minimal expression in neurons or astrocytes (Figures S4A–S4E). qPCR analysis of fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted GFP+ cells revealed robust enrichment of microglia-specific markers, and flow cytometry further showed that ∼97% of GFP+ cells were CD11b+CD45ˆlow microglia (Figures S4F–S4I). Consistently, IL-27 expression was significantly increased in FACS-sorted microglia at the mRNA level, and this microglia-predominant upregulation was further confirmed at the protein level by western blot using magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS)-isolated microglia, astrocytes, and neurons (Figures S4J–S4M). Consistent with these cell-type-resolved data, IL-27 protein levels in tissue lysates were also significantly increased (Figures 2F and 2G), confirming successful overexpression. Behavioral tests showed that IL-27 overexpression recapitulated the antidepressant and anxiolytic effects observed with recombinant IL-27 (Figures 2H–2J), further supporting its therapeutic potential in PIA-induced behavioral abnormalities. To further strengthen microglia-specific validation, we employed a Cx3cr1-Cre-dependent AAV-DIO strategy to manipulate IL-27 signaling in microglia selectively. Microglia-restricted targeting was confirmed by immunostaining and immunoblotting, and both gain- and loss-of-function manipulations in microglia resulted in corresponding behavioral alterations in the PIA model (Figure S5). These results demonstrate both the efficacy and durability of IL-27-mediated behavioral rescue, supporting its potential for further translational development.

IL-27 rescues PIA-induced morphological changes in the brain

To determine whether IL-27 influences structural brain alterations induced by PIA, we performed histological and ultrastructural analyses. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Nissl staining revealed preserved hippocampal cytoarchitecture across groups, with no significant changes in neuronal density (Figures S4N and S4O). However, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed reduced synapse number in PIA mice, which was rescued by IL-27 treatment (Figures 3A and 3B), along with reduced lysosome accumulation (Figures 3C and 3D). Microglia are known to participate in synaptic remodeling by pruning synapses during postnatal development, and dysregulated synaptic pruning has been associated with various brain disorders.29,30,31 Therefore, we explored the potential link between microglial susceptibility and synaptic pruning. IBA1 immunostaining revealed significant microglial activation in PIA mice, which was attenuated by IL-27 treatment (Figure 3G). Subsequently, quantitative morphometric analysis demonstrated that IL-27 reversed PIA-induced changes in microglial number, longest path, endpoint voxels, and branch, which are associated with microglial reactivity (Figures 3H and 3I). These findings suggest that IL-27 mitigates PIA-induced synaptic and dendritic abnormalities, likely by suppressing aberrant microglial activation and preserving microglia-neuron interactions essential for synaptic homeostasis.

IL-27 modulates microglial phagocytic activity in vivo and in vitro

To explore the molecular mechanism by which IL-27 modulates PIA-induced behavioral deficits, we performed RNA-seq on hippocampal tissue from control, PIA-, and IL-27-treated mice. Differential gene expression analysis, with thresholds set at |log2(fold change)| > 1 and a q value ≤0.05, identified 1,102 DEGs in pairwise comparisons (Figure 4A). Volcano plot analysis revealed substantial differential gene expression between the IL-27 and PIA groups (Figure 4B). Enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms showed enrichment of synapse-associated functions (Figure 4C). Based on sequencing results and the critical role of microglia in synaptic remodeling,32,33 we next investigated how IL-27 regulates microglial phagocytosis in the context of PIA. To assess the impact of IL-27 on synaptic integrity, we measured the expression of postsynaptic density protein (PSD-95) in the CA1 and dentate gyrus regions using immunofluorescence. IL-27 treatment significantly increased PSD-95 levels in PIA mice (Figures 4F–4H). Further analysis of synaptic proteins, including the postsynaptic marker PSD-95 and the presynaptic marker synaptophysin (Syn), indicated a significant increase in the hippocampus following IL-27 treatment (Figures 4I and 4J). This suggested that IL-27 positively influences synaptic protein expression. Three-dimensional reconstructions demonstrated extensive co-localization of immunoreactive PSD-95 dots (red) with IBA1-labeled microglial processes (green) in the hippocampus of PIA mice. This synaptic-microglial interaction was notably reversed following IL-27 treatment (Figures 4K and 4L). To further validate these findings, microglia were isolated from mice using flow cytometry (Figure 4M) and analyzed via qPCR. The analysis showed a significant decrease in the expression of phagocytic markers and genes, including NPC1, CD14, CD68, C1qa, C1qb, Mertk, and Tim4, in the hippocampal tissues of IL-27-treated mice compared with PIA mice (Figures 4N and 4O). Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed upregulation of glutamatergic synapse-associated gene signatures following IL-27 treatment (Figure 4P), suggesting that reduced microglial phagocytic activity may contribute to the preservation of excitatory synaptic signaling. Further functional validation is needed to confirm the physiological relevance of these transcriptomic changes.

These results collectively suggested that early inflammation activation enhances microglial synaptic phagocytosis, but this increase is mitigated by subsequent IL-27 treatment, indicating a potential therapeutic role for IL-27 in regulating microglial activity and preserving synaptic integrity.

To explore the role of IL-27 in microglial morphology and function in vitro, morphological changes in BV2 and primary microglial cells were observed under a microscope, and identity was verified by IBA1 immunostaining in parallel. Under basal conditions, BV2 cells typically exhibit a spindle-shaped or elongated morphology, whereas primary microglia display small, elongated cell bodies or multiple ramified processes. Upon LPS stimulation or activation, BV2 cells generally adopt a more rounded morphology with enlarged cell bodies and reduced processes, while primary microglia show marked cell body enlargement, shortened or retracted processes, and in some cases, a classic amoeboid morphology. Notably, IL-27 treatment partially restored these cells to their baseline morphology (Figure S6A). To assess functional consequences on phagocytic activity, we used 1-μm fluorescent latex beads to simulate engulfment. Immunofluorescence analysis showed LPS significantly increased bead uptake in both BV2 and primary microglia, whereas IL-27 treatment markedly attenuated phagocytic events (Figures S6B and S6D). Orthogonal views and 3D confocal reconstruction further verified that bead signals were enclosed within the IBA1+ cellular volume, supporting true internalization and strengthening the reliability of the phagocytosis readout (Figure S6C).

To investigate whether IL-27 modulates synaptic integrity indirectly via microglia, we first treated primary microglial cells with LPS, IL-27, or vehicle control for 24 h before co-culture. This approach ensured that only microglia were exposed to stimuli, thereby maintaining a single-variable system. Following treatment, microglia were washed and then co-cultured with untreated primary neurons. In co-cultures, synaptic density and the abundance of synaptic proteins (PSD95 and Syn) were diminished relative to neurons cultured alone. These deficits were further exacerbated in co-cultures with LPS-activated microglia but were significantly restored in cultures containing IL-27-pretreated microglia. Synaptic recovery was confirmed by immunofluorescence and western blotting (Figures S6E–S6H). At the molecular level, the expression of phagocytic genes, including C1qa, C1qb, Tim4, and CD68, was downregulated in IL-27-treated microglia (Figure S6I). Furthermore, to evaluate whether changes in synaptic structure were accompanied by alterations in neuronal function, we performed live-cell calcium imaging using the calcium-sensitive dye Fluo-4 AM. Upon potassium chloride (KCl) stimulation, neurons co-cultured with LPS-activated microglia exhibited significantly suppressed calcium responses, as evidenced by reduced peak F/F0 values and decreased area under the curve (Figures S7A–S7D). In contrast, neurons co-cultured with IL-27-pretreated microglia showed a partial restoration of calcium signaling, suggesting that IL-27 partially preserves neuronal excitability and synaptic activity. Consistent with these in vitro findings, in vivo fiber photometry revealed robust, state-dependent calcium dynamics in hippocampal neurons during the TST, with higher ΔF/F during struggling than immobility (Figures S7E–S7I). Notably, LPS markedly blunted TST-evoked hippocampal calcium activity, whereas IL-27 treatment restored both the overall ΔF/F signal and peak-aligned calcium transients toward control levels (Figures S7J–S7O).

Taken together, these in vitro findings demonstrate that IL-27 protects synaptic integrity at both structural and functional levels by modulating microglial activation state and phagocytic capacity.

IL-27 exerts neuroprotective effects via Trem2 signaling

Among them, Trem2 exhibited a notable upregulation following IL-27 treatment, suggesting a potential involvement in its downstream effects. Moreover, as STAT1 is a key downstream transcription factor in IL-27 signaling, transcription factor binding-site prediction indicated a high-probability STAT1-binding motif within the Trem2 promoter region (Figure 5A), supporting Trem2 as a critical downstream effector of the IL-27–STAT1 axis. Western blot analyses confirmed IL-27-induced Trem2 expression in the hippocampus of PIA mice (Figures 5B and 5C). Trem2, known for its roles in degenerative diseases, obesity, atherosclerosis, and tumors, influences microglial functions, including phagocytosis, proliferation, survival, and inflammatory response.34,35,36 Immunofluorescence confirmed the expression of Trem2 in both microglia and astrocytes in vivo (Figure S8B). However, qPCR analysis of isolated CNS cell types revealed that microglia expressed significantly higher levels of Trem2 mRNA compared with neurons and astrocytes (Figure S8D). In primary microglia, immunofluorescence, qPCR, and western blot analyses consistently showed that IL-27 treatment enhanced Trem2 expression (Figures S8C and S8E–S8G). To determine whether IL-27 exerts its neuroprotective effects through the Trem2 pathway, we employed small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting Trem2 to reduce its expression in vitro, with knockdown efficiency confirmed by western blotting and immunofluorescence (Figures S9A–S9D). To examine in vivo relevance, siRNA-Trem2 or siRNA-control was injected into P2 mice, followed by IL-27 treatment and behavioral assessment (Figures 5D and 5E). We found that Trem2 knockdown abolished the behavioral improvements. For depression-like behaviors, Trem2 inhibition significantly increased the immobility time of TST and FST and decreased the proportion of sugar-water intake (Figure 5F). Regarding anxiety-like behaviors, the Trem2 knockdown reduced center dwell time in OFT (Figure 5G). In EPM, Trem2 knockdown significantly reduced the time to enter the open arms (Figure 5H). These results suggest that microglial Trem2 is required for IL-27-mediated alleviation of depression- and anxiety-like behaviors.

Immunostaining of IBA1 in the hippocampus revealed that IL-27 suppressed microglial activation after PIA, whereas this effect was reversed by Trem2 knockdown (Figure 5I). To determine whether Trem2 mediates IL-27’s effects on microglial phagocytosis, we performed a 3D reconstruction of PSD-95 and IBA1 co-localization. Trem2 knockdown reversed synaptic engulfment that had been reduced by IL-27 treatment (Figures 5J and 5K), indicating that Trem2 is involved in modulating IL-27-mediated microglial phagocytic activity.

It has been reported that Trem2 affects the complement system and thus reduces synaptic pruning by binding to C1q in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) models.37 We further explored the relationship between Trem2-mediated downstream alterations and complement system. Immunofluorescence analysis showed an increased percentage of PSD-95 puncta co-localized with C3 in Trem2-knockdown mice compared with IL-27-treated controls (Figures 5L and 5M). Western blotting further demonstrated upregulation of C1qa and C3 proteins following Trem2 inhibition (Figures 5N and 5O), suggesting that Trem2 may limit complement cascade activation. While prior studies suggest Trem2 directly interacts with C1qa, further studies are required to confirm this interaction in the context of IL-27 signaling. Importantly, knockdown of Trem2 alone after PIA modeling resulted in an increase in depression- and anxiety-like symptoms in mice (Figures S9E–S9H), reinforcing the notion that Trem2 plays a key role in regulating neuroimmune responses. Collectively, these findings imply that the IL-27-mediated effects might be exerted through the modulation of Trem2, which in turn influences complement-mediated phagocytosis, thereby increasing synaptic density in the PIA mouse model.

STAT1 and STAT3, acting as transcription factors,39 are identified as crucial mediators for IL-27 in regulating the transcription of target genes. We next determined whether IL-27 regulates Trem2 through these transcription factors. Indeed, IL-27 treatment augmented Trem2 expression in primary microglia, which could be abrogated by pharmacological inhibition of the activity of STAT1 (fludarabine, 5 μM). However, IL-27 upregulation of Trem2 was largely unaffected in response to STAT3 inhibition (stattic, 5 μM) (Figures 6C and 6E). Moreover, IL-27 failed to upregulate Trem2 in IL-27Rα-deficient microglia (Figures 6D and 6E), confirming pathway specificity. To verify direct transcriptional regulation, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation qPCR (ChIP-qPCR) in BV2 cells and observed increased STAT1 binding at the Trem2 promoter following IL-27 exposure (Figure 6F), supporting a direct regulatory interaction. Chen et al. have reported that in ischemic brain damage, the PI3K/Akt pathway was identified as a downstream target of Trem2.40 We further examined whether Trem2 plays a similar role in our model. Consistently, IL-27 treatment enhanced phosphorylation of PI3K and Akt in microglia compared with LPS treatment alone (Figures 6G and 6H), suggesting functional engagement of this axis downstream of IL-27-STAT1-Trem2 signaling. Conclusively, our results demonstrated that IL-27 targeting to microglia and subsequent phosphorylation of STAT1 in the nucleus can modulate Trem2 expression (Figure 6I). This mechanism may ultimately contribute to the amelioration of mood disorders induced by PIA in mice.

IL-27 is associated with early-life adversity models and adolescent depression

To strengthen the translational relevance of our study, we incorporated an additional animal model that mimics early-life adversity. LPS-induced immune activation models provide a well-established and reproducible approach to studying inflammation-driven behavioral changes. We also employed a PNS paradigm based on restraint stress (Figure 7A). Behaviorally, treatment with IL-27 improved depression and anxiety symptoms in PNS-exposed offspring. Specifically, IL-27 increased sucrose preference in the SPT and reduced immobility time in the TST and FST (Figure 7B). IL-27 treatment attenuated the reduction in OFT center dwell time caused by PNS (Figure 7C). Moreover, in the EPM, IL-27-treated mice spent significantly more time in the open arms than the PNS group (Figure 7D). Western blot analysis revealed that PNS exposure led to a marked reduction in synaptic protein levels, including PSD95 and Syn, in the hippocampus. IL-27 treatment significantly restored the expression of both markers (Figures 7E and 7F). In parallel, electron microscopic analysis revealed significant synaptic loss in PNS-exposed offspring, demonstrating structural deficits at the ultrastructural level. Notably, IL-27 treatment effectively mitigated these synaptic deficits (Figures 7G and 7H). To bridge our preclinical findings with potential clinical implications, we measured plasma IL-27 levels in adolescents with major depressive disorder (MDD) and healthy controls (Figure 7I). Participant characteristics are summarized in Table S2. qPCR results showed that IL-27 mRNA levels were significantly higher in MDD patients (Figure 7J) and positively correlated with PHQ-9 and HAMD scores (Figure 7K). Linear regression analysis confirmed the group difference (B = −1.21, SE = 0.48, p = 0.012) and further indicated that IL-27 levels were associated with depression severity (B = 0.07, SE = 0.04, p = 0.049; B = 0.18, SE = 0.09, p = 0.055). This is consistent with the results in animal models.

Consistent behavioral and synaptic improvements across PIA-induced and PNS-based paradigms, together with elevated IL-27 levels in adolescents with MDD and their correlation with depression severity, underscore the translational potential of IL-27 as a therapeutic target for early-life adversity-induced mood disorders.

Given the promising therapeutic potential of IL-27 observed in both behavioral and structural outcomes, it is essential to assess its safety profile prior to translational consideration. To evaluate the long-term safety of IL-27, we performed a comprehensive toxicological analysis, including physiological monitoring, hematological and biochemical profiling, and histopathological examination (Figure S10A). Behavioral analysis demonstrated that IL-27 treatment did not significantly alter depression- or anxiety-like behaviors compared with untreated controls, as evidenced by comparable performance in SPT, TST, FST, EPM, and OFT (Figures S10B–S10D). These results indicate that IL-27 maintains normal affective behavior profiles, preserving emotional homeostasis at the administered dosage. Gross anatomical inspection and histopathological assessment of major organs showed no structural abnormalities or signs of toxicity (Figure S10E). Longitudinal monitoring indicated no significant changes in body weight or food intake (Figures S10F–S10G), indicating no adverse effects on growth or metabolism. Furthermore, hematological analysis demonstrated normal blood cell counts and coagulation function (Figures S10H and S10I), and serum biochemistry confirmed that liver, kidney function markers, lipids, ions, cardiac enzymes, and diabetes testing programs remained within physiological ranges (Figure S10J).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that IL-27 not only improves behavioral and structural outcomes in a translationally relevant model of prenatal stress but also exhibits a favorable safety profile, supporting its therapeutic potential for mood disorders linked to early-life adversity.

Discussion

IL-27 is a pleiotropic cytokine involved in diverse immune and tissue responses, including infections, cellular stress, neuronal diseases, and tumors.41 It exhibits complex immunomodulatory properties, balancing activating and inhibitory effects in both innate and adaptive immunity. However, its specific role and mechanisms in mental disorders remain poorly understood. Our study provides evidence that IL-27 exhibits both antidepressant and anxiolytic effects by modulating microglial phagocytic activity in the brain. Notably, we have identified that IL-27 suppresses microglial phagocytosis through the STAT1/Trem2 signaling pathway. This discovery not only highlights the therapeutic potential of IL-27 in neuropsychiatric treatment but also paves the way for further research into its role in neuroimmune interactions.

Numerous studies have confirmed that IL-27 and its receptor are altered in innate immune cell types, including microglia, macrophages, and dendritic cells, during disease. For example, in models of MS and neuropathic pain, IL-27 expression has been observed in astrocytes and resident immune cells of the central and peripheral nervous systems.25,42 In contrast, our study, using the PIA model, revealed that IL-27 and its receptor are primarily expressed in microglia rather than astrocytes, with minimal expression in neurons. This cell-type specificity underscores the distinct immunological context of early-life neuroinflammation and highlights microglial IL-27 signaling as a potential therapeutic axis.

Microglia are central regulators of neuroimmune homeostasis, particularly during brain development.43,44 In the PIA model, an imbalance in microglial phagocytosis is a key feature. Our data demonstrate that IL-27 modulates microglial phagocytic activity and restores the morphology, quantity, and phagocytosis markers of microglia altered by early-life inflammation. Although previous studies have reported on the involvement of inflammatory factors in neuroimmune-related research, our study links IL-27 with microglial phagocytosis and explores its role in mood disorders. To functionally verify this effect, we employed recombinant IL-27 protein and a neutralizing antibody to examine its bidirectional modulation and further validated the findings using an AAV9-mediated overexpression approach. Moreover, Wang et al.45 reported that IL-27Ra−/- mice exhibited exacerbated adipose tissue inflammation, further supporting the protective role of IL-27 in immune regulation. In future studies, the use of IL-27 knockout mice will enable a better systematic evaluation of the functional roles of IL-27 across different disease stages, brain regions, and cell types in the PIA model, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of IL-27’s immunomodulatory and protective functions in inflammation-associated mood disorders.

Although the PIA model captured acute inflammation-driven behavioral pathology, we further validated the relevance of our findings using a PNS model that more closely mirrors early-life adversity. The consistent effects of IL-27 across both models reinforce its significance as a modulator of microglial function and behavioral outcomes. Importantly, we employed the restraint-stress PNS paradigm. This model is procedurally straightforward, yields stable and reproducible outcomes, and reliably induces anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. For context, we also note the unpredictable variable stress PNS paradigm developed by the Tracy Bale group46 as an alternative approach; by combining multiple stressors, it emphasizes contextual complexity but generally requires tighter control of experimental variables and larger sample sizes. The two paradigms are not hierarchically superior but serve complementary purposes depending on study goals. Our principal conclusions were derived from, and primarily supported by, the PNS model. Future studies employing additional experimental paradigms will be required to further substantiate and expand the translational relevance of these findings.

Trem2 is recognized as essential for microglial-mediated synaptic elimination during brain development,47 yet its role in synaptic pruning within the context of PIA is not well understood. Prior studies indicate that Trem2 deficiency leads to excessive synaptic pruning and heightened phagocytosis in inflammatory models.48 In AD, Trem2 complexes inhibit complement-mediated synapse elimination. Our transcriptomic analysis identified Trem2 as a target of IL-27 signaling, with subsequent experiments confirming that IL-27 restores Trem2 expression and reduces microglial overphagocytosis. Previous literature has established that Trem2 triggers STAT1 signaling in T cells.49 Notably, we demonstrate that IL-27 activates STAT1, which binds to the Trem2 promoter and transcriptionally regulates its expression. Although p-STAT1 levels were also elevated by LPS, ChIP analysis revealed that Trem2 expression did not significantly increase, suggesting that IL-27R engagement is necessary for STAT1-mediated Trem2 transcription. This implies that alternative regulatory pathways may underlie LPS-induced Trem2 downregulation.

Beyond the role in phagocytosis, neuroinflammation is a critical factor in the development of mood disorders such as depression and anxiety in humans and rodents.50 Inflammation early in life leads to glial cell activation and subsequently accelerates cytokine and synaptic defect production.51 The impact of IL-27, a cytokine itself, on PIA-induced inflammation merits further exploration. Studies of animal models of disease and cultured cells have shown that multiple signaling pathways contribute to the anti-inflammatory effects of IL-27.52,53 In particular, IL-27 inhibits T cell differentiation and function while inducing macrophages and activated CD4+ T cells to secrete the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10.54,55 Within the CNS, IL-10 moderates neuroinflammation by reducing the activity of proinflammatory cells and reactive astrocytes, and by promoting a neuroprotective microglial phenotype.56,57,58 In our transcriptomic analysis, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment revealed that IL-27 treatment significantly downregulated genes involved in immune and inflammatory responses, particularly pathways related to cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. Thus, it is postulated that IL-27 plays a significant anti-inflammatory role in the context of PIA.

In summary, our study demonstrates that IL-27 modulates microglial activation and phagocytic activity, thereby alleviating depression- and anxiety-like behaviors induced by PIA and PNS model. By restoring synaptic integrity through immune-neural interaction, IL-27 emerges as a key regulator of neuroimmune homeostasis. These findings highlight IL-27 as a potential immunotherapeutic target for mood disorders associated with perinatal immune dysregulation and provide mechanistic insights into microglia-mediated synaptic remodeling in affective pathology.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that warrant follow-up. First, our work relies on PIA and PNS paradigms; validation across additional early-life adversity and infection/inflammation models is needed to establish generalizability and robustness. Second, while our data suggest that Trem2 may regulate phagocytosis via the complement system, direct evidence of interactions with specific complement components is lacking. In particular, whether Trem2 binds C1q to competitively limit synaptic tagging remains to be tested. Given that C1q is predominantly secreted by microglia, interpreting changes in C1q localization requires strict control of microglial cell numbers. However, our current model does not permit precise regulation of microglial populations across treatment groups. Future studies using conditional or inducible manipulations will be essential to dissect the Trem2-complement axis and its causal role in synaptic remodeling. Finally, our clinical sample size is limited; we plan to expand the cohort and pursue multi-center replication to enhance external validity and translational relevance.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

All animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Shandong University (ECSBMSSDU2022-2-173). C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Cx3cr1-Cre mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences and maintained on a C57BL/6J background. Sexually mature male and female mice, aged 10–16 weeks and 8–10 weeks, respectively, were used as breeders. To generate litters, two female mice were mated with one male mouse in our animal facility. Pregnant females were housed individually 1–4 days before parturition, and male mice were removed after 10 days of mating. All animals were housed in ventilated cages under standard laboratory conditions in a specific pathogen-free facility with a 12 h light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. Only male offspring were used for subsequent experiments. Because only male offspring were included, the potential influence of sex on the experimental outcomes was not assessed, which represents a limitation of the study.

Human participants

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing of Shandong University (approval number 2024-R-139). The clinical ethics number is KYLL-2024-0432. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians before sample collection.

This study included 18 adolescent patients with MDD and 18 healthy controls matched 1:1 by age and sex; each group included 6 males and 12 females. The age range of participants was 10–20 years. Additional demographic and clinical characteristics, including socioeconomic status, Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) scores, and 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) scores, are summarized in Table S2. Sex distribution did not differ significantly between the MDD and healthy control groups. Ancestry, race, ethnicity, and gender identity were not collected. Given the limited sample size, sex-stratified analyses were not performed; therefore, the potential influence of sex or gender on study outcomes could not be assessed.

Adolescent patients with MDD were recruited from the Department of Psychiatry at the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. Healthy control subjects were recruited through local community postings and media advertisements. The inclusion criteria for MDD patients were as follows: (1) Meet the diagnostic criteria for MDD using a Structured Clinical Interview by two neuropsychiatrists according to the Diagnostic Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). (2) HAMD-24 score >20. (3) First onset of depression, with no prior treatment with antidepressants or antipsychotics. The exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of severe somatic or organic brain diseases. (2) Diagnosis of other psychiatric disorders according to DSM-5, including intellectual disability, substance dependence or abuse, and bipolar disorder. For healthy controls, individuals with a prior diagnosis of neurological, psychiatric, or psychological disorders were excluded based on questionnaire screening. All participants completed the HAMD and PHQ-9 scales.

Cell lines and primary cell cultures

Primary microglia and astrocyte cultures

Primary mixed glial cultures were prepared from the cerebral cortex of C57BL/6J mouse pups at P2–P3. The sex of neonatal donor pups used for primary glial cultures was not determined, and cells were prepared from pooled pups. Briefly, cortical tissues were dissociated and cultured in 75-cm2 flasks (Beaver). After approximately 10–12 days in culture, primary microglia were selectively collected by shaking the flasks at 200 rpm and 37°C for 4 h. The remaining adherent cells were predominantly primary astrocytes. The purity of the isolated microglia was nearly 100%, as confirmed by immunofluorescence staining (Figure S2A). Cells were then seeded onto culture plates or flasks pre-coated with poly-L-lysine (PLL; Sigma, USA; #P3143) according to established protocols.59

BV2 cell line

BV2 mouse microglial cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China; #CL-0493). According to the information provided by the supplier, the cell line was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling.

Culture conditions and mycoplasma testing

Primary glial cultures and BV2 cells were maintained in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA; #11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA; #10099141C) and antibiotics at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Culture medium was refreshed every 3 days for primary glial cultures or as needed for BV2 cells. Primary glial cultures and BV2 cells were screened for mycoplasma contamination using the MycoAlert Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza) and were confirmed to be negative.

Postnatal immune activation

The PIA model was adapted from previous methodologies with minor modifications.2,60,61,62,63,64,65,66 Neonatal C57BL/6J and Cx3cr1-Cre mice received intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of LPS (Sigma, L-2880, Escherichia coli, serotype O26; 50 μg/kg) or pyrogen-free saline on P3–P5 using a 0.3-mL insulin syringe (BD). Pups were toe-clipped for identification. Only male offspring were used in this study. Offspring were weaned at P21, and male mice were group-housed five per cage, with littermates kept together and only animals from different litters assigned to the same experimental condition co-housed. Mice were initially allocated in equal numbers to each group. The animal exclusion criteria included occasional handling-related exclusions and the mandatory removal of animals exhibiting spontaneous weight loss or poor general condition unrelated to the experimental treatment, as required by institutional welfare guidelines. To eliminate the impact of potential batch differences in the behavioral data analysis, behavioral data were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs), with cohort included as a random effect, which effectively controlled for any variability introduced by different testing batches.

Prenatal stress model

This experimental protocol was developed by carefully modifying established methodologies in the field.67,68,69,70,71 Two female and one male C57BL/6J mice were placed in one cage for mating. Vaginal plugs were checked daily, and the day of plug detection was designated as embryonic day 0.5 (E0.5). Upon confirmation of pregnancy, dams were randomly allocated to one of three experimental groups: Control, PNS, or PNS with rIL-27 treatment, and subsequently housed individually in standard laboratory cages. PNS was applied from E9.5 to E18.5 using restraint stress: pregnant dams were placed in clear plexiglass tubes (inner diameter 3 cm, length 12 cm) under bright light (∼2400 lux) for three 45-min sessions per day (morning, midday, afternoon). The day of birth was recorded as P1. Male offspring were weaned at P21 and group-housed at a maximum of five mice per cage. Only male offspring were used for subsequent behavioral and molecular analyses.

Method details

Drug treatments in cultured cells

Fludarabine and Stattic were used as pharmacological inhibitors of STAT1 and STAT3 signaling, respectively. Fludarabine was dissolved in DMSO, whereas Stattic was dissolved in a mixture of 10% DMSO and 90% corn oil to prepare stock solutions. For in vitro experiments, both inhibitors were diluted in culture medium to a final concentration of 5 μM, and cells were pretreated for 2 h before rIL-27 stimulation. Control cells received equivalent volumes of the corresponding vehicles.

Stereotactic injection

Stereotactic injections were performed as described previously.72 Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and placed in a stereotaxic frame. Reagents were delivered using a 10-μL Hamilton microsyringe coupled to a microinjection pump (KDS 200, KD Scientific) at 0.20 μL/min.

Intracerebroventricular injection (P2). The needle was inserted into the left lateral ventricle at the following coordinates relative to bregma: x = +1.0 mm, y = −0.6 mm, z = −2.5 mm. On P2, mice received AAV9-F4/80-IL-27-P2A-GFP (7.63 × 10ˆ13 vg/mL, 0.4 μL) or control AAV (6.81 × 10ˆ13 vg/mL, 0.4 μL), AAV-CMV-DIO-IL-27-P2A-GFP (9.12 × 10ˆ13 vg/mL, 0.4 μL) or control AAV (9.15 × 10ˆ13 vg/mL, 0.4 μL), AAV-CAG-DIO-IL-27shRNA-ZsGreen (1.0 × 10ˆ13 vg/mL, 0.6 μL) or control AAV (1.0 × 10ˆ13 vg/mL,0.6 μL), and Trem2 siRNA (100 pmol in 2 μL) or control siRNA (100 pmol in 2 μL). From P3 to P5, rIL-27 (15 ng/μL, 1 μL) was administered once daily into the lateral ventricle using the same coordinates and infusion rate.

Hippocampal injection (P21). For experiments requiring direct hippocampal delivery, P21 mice received a unilateral hippocampal injection of rAAV-hSyn-GCaMp6s-WPRE-hGH-polyA (2.0 × 10ˆ12 vg/mL, 0.4 μL) at the following coordinates relative to bregma: x = +1.5 mm, y = +1.5 mm, z = −1.8 mm, using the same infusion rate.

Assessment of depression-like behaviors

For all behavioral assessments, mice were transported to home cages with mesh lids at least half a day prior to testing to allow for habituation. The testing room was dimly lit to minimize anxiety among the subjects. The experimenter conducting the tests and subsequent quantitative analyses was blinded to the group assignments of the animals. A 24-h rest period was provided between each test. All behavioral experiments were video recorded and the movements of the mice were tracked and analyzed using TopScan software, version 3.0.

Open field test

The OFT was conducted in accordance with methodologies established in previous research.73 Mice were individually placed at the center of a chamber measuring 35 cm × 35 cm × 25 cm (20 × 20). They were allowed to explore the area freely for 10 min, during which their locomotor activity was monitored. Both the total distance traveled and the amount of time spent in the central area were recorded and analyzed as indicators of motor activity and anxiety-like behavior. To eliminate olfactory cues between tests, the floor of the arena was cleaned with a 5% ethanol solution.

Elevated plus maze test

The EPM test was conducted following protocols established in prior study.74 The EPM apparatus consisted of two open arms and two closed arms, emanating from a central platform and elevated 50 cm above the floor. Each mouse was placed in the central area and allowed to explore the maze for 6 min. The percentage of time spent in the open arms and the number of entries into the open arms were recorded as measures of anxiety-like behavior, using a tracking system for accurate analysis. To prevent olfactory cue contamination, the floor of each arm was cleaned with a 5% ethanol solution between tests.

Tail suspension test

Depression-like behavior in mice was evaluated using the TST, adapted slightly from methods previously described.51,73 For the test, each mouse was suspended by the tail using tape, which was positioned 1 cm from the tip of the tail to ensure uniform application. The entire 6-min test duration was videotaped for later analysis. An observer blinded to the experimental conditions reviewed the recordings, assessing mobility, which was defined as movement of the hind legs. Mice that climbed their tails were excluded from the analysis to maintain the integrity of the data.

Forced swimming test

The FST is employed to evaluate depression-like behavior in mice.75 The test involves an open glass cylinder, 25 cm high and 18 cm in diameter, filled with water to a depth of 18 cm maintained between 22°C and 25°C. Each mouse was placed in the cylinder to swim for 6 min. To ensure consistent test conditions, the water was replaced after each session. An observer, blinded to the treatment groups, monitored the mice. Immobility was defined as the mouse ceasing to swim and remaining motionless, performing only the necessary movements to keep its head above water.

Sucrose preference test

To assess depression-like phenotypes, a 4-day sucrose preference protocol was employed.4 Initially, after acclimatization to individual cages, mice were provided with two identical drinking bottles. During the initial two days, both bottles contained regular drinking water. Over the following two days, both were filled with 1% sucrose solution. Subsequently, the animals underwent a 24-h water deprivation period, after which they were given access to one bottle of 1% sucrose solution and one of water for 2 h. To control for side preference, the positions of the bottles were switched after 1 h. The volume of liquid consumed from each bottle was measured by weighing, with sucrose and water consumption denoted as S and W, respectively. The relative sucrose preference for each mouse was calculated daily using the formula: 100% × (S/(S + W)).

Neuron-microglia co-culture

Primary neuronal and microglial cell cultures were prepared as described in previous studies76 In our co-culture paradigm, cortices from P0–P3 mice were dissociated to establish mixed glial cultures; after 7–10 days in vitro, microglia were isolated by the shaking method, the supernatant was collected, and centrifuged to harvest cells. Microglia were then pretreated with rIL-27 or LPS, pelleted (800 × g, 5 min), resuspended in Neurobasal medium (Gibco, USA, 21103049), and seeded onto DIV11 neurons at a 1:10 ratio.

Calcium imaging

After being washed three times with PBS buffer, cells were incubated with Fluo-4 AM working solution (2 μM, Beyotime, Shanghai, S1060) at 37°C and 5% CO 2 for 15 min. Following incubation, cells were washed again and then stimulated with potassium chloride (KCl) at a final concentration of 100 mM. Time-lapse images of neuronal Ca2+ transients were acquired using an Olympus IX series inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), with an image acquisition interval of 1 s. The recorded Ca2+ dynamics were analyzed using ImageJ software. Intracellular Ca2+ changes were expressed as F/F0, where F represents the fluorescence intensity at each time point, and F0 is the average baseline fluorescence before stimulation. Quantitative analysis of the F/F0 traces was performed using Imaris 7.2.3 software, focusing on two primary metrics: Area under curve (AUC) and Peak response analysis, which together reflect the amplitude and duration of neuronal responses to stimulation.

Immunofluorescence staining, imaging, and image analysis

Mice were deeply anesthetized using isoflurane (∼15 s) and subsequently perfused with saline for 3 min followed by a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 4 min. The brains were then excised and post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4°C. To ensure proper dehydration, the brains were immersed in 15% and then 30% sucrose solutions, each for 2–3 days at 4°C until they reached isotonicity. The preserved brains were sectioned coronally into 20 μm slices at −20°C using a cryostat.

For immunofluorescence staining, brain sections were initially washed three times with PBS, each for 5 min. They were then permeabilized and blocked with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS containing 10% normal donkey serum for 2 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies were diluted 1:200 in a solution of 0.3% Triton X-100 with donkey serum and incubated overnight at 4°C. After rinsing three times with PBS, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional PBS washes, sections were stained with 2 μg/mL 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for nuclear visualization. To quantify the immunohistochemical analysis, slide images were observed and acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51; Olympus, Shinjuku, Japan) and a high-resolution live cell imaging platform (confocal type) (LSM 900 with Airyscan 2). Analysis was performed using ImageJ software (Fiji version, NIH).

Three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction

Coronal sections of 20 μm were first stained with anti-Iba1 antibody for 24 h, followed by incubation with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI to highlight cellular structures. Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM900 microscope equipped with a 40×/1.3 NA oil immersion objective. To ensure consistency, imaging parameters such as laser power, gain, and offset were standardized across all experiments. z stack images of sections stained for PSD-95 were captured using identical exposure settings. Image analysis was conducted using IMARIS software version 7.2.3 (Bitplane), which facilitated detailed three-dimensional reconstructions and quantitative analysis of the labeled structures.77

Phagocytosis assay

BV2 and primary microglia were seeded into 24-well plates at densities calculated to achieve confluence at the time of the assay, approximately 30,000 cells per well. The phagocytosis assay was initiated by stimulating the cells with LPS (500 ng/mL) and rIL-27 (200 ng/mL) as previously described.78,79 Initially, the culture medium was switched to DMEM alone and incubated for 30 min at 37°C. This medium was then replaced with DMEM supplemented with 5% FBS. Fluorescent latex beads (1 μM, Sigma L2778) were preconditioned in a 50% FBS and PBS solution. These beads were then added to the cells at concentrations of 50 beads per BV2 cell and 100 beads per primary microglial cell, followed by incubation at 37°C for 2 h for BV2 cells and 3 h for primary microglia. Post-incubation, unbound beads were gently washed away, and the cells were fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature. Subsequently, cells were stained with DAPI for 10 min at room temperature. Images were captured using an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope and analyzed using Fiji ImageJ software.

Transcription factor binding-site prediction

Putative STAT1 binding sites within the Trem2 promoter region were predicted using the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) with default parameters.

Magnetic bead-based cell isolation (MACS)

Brain tissues were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using an enzymatic digestion kit (RWD, Cat. No. DHABE-5003). Cell suspensions were passed through a 40-μm cell strainer, resuspended in PBS containing 0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA, and subjected to magnetic separation. Microglia were isolated by positive selection using a CD11b+ microglia magnetic separation kit (RWD, Cat. No. K1306) via sequential incubation with a CD11b biotin-conjugated antibody and streptavidin magnetic microbeads, followed by column-based separation to collect the CD11b+ fraction. Astrocytes were isolated by positive selection using a magnetic separation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. No. 130-097-678) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Neurons were isolated using a negative selection magnetic separation kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. No. 130-126-602) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified cell fractions were used for subsequent western blot analysis.

In vivo fiber photometry

Mice were injected with rAAV-hSyn-GCaMP6s into the hippocampus, and an optical fiber was implanted above the same site. The fiber ferrule was secured to the skull with dental cement. After approximately 3 weeks to allow sufficient expression of GCaMP6s, fluorescence signals were recorded during behavioral testing using a fiber photometry system (ThinkerTech, Nanjing, China). GCaMP signals were excited at 470 nm, and a 405-nm isosbestic reference channel was recorded simultaneously to control for motion-related artifacts and photobleaching. Fluorescence signals were sampled at 40 Hz with low excitation intensity to minimize photobleaching and phototoxicity. Raw fluorescence traces were processed using FiberAnalysis software (version 1.x; ThinkerTech, Nanjing, China). Baseline correction was applied to remove slow signal drift, and motion correction was performed using the 405-nm reference channel. Fluorescence changes were calculated as ΔF/F = (F − F0)/F0, where F0 was defined as the mean fluorescence during the pre-event baseline window (−2.0 to −0.5 s relative to event onset). For state-dependent analyses during the tail suspension test (TST), struggling and immobility epochs were annotated from synchronized behavioral video recordings and used for quantification of GCaMP signal changes.

Western blotting

After discarding the culture medium, cells in Petri dishes were washed three times with precooled PBS. The cells were then centrifuged at 800 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, lysed using RIPA buffer, and incubated on ice for 40 min. Following this, cells were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations in the samples were determined with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Protein samples (3 μg each) were loaded onto a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel for separation. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred from the gel to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane using a semi-dry electrophoretic transfer tank (Bio-Rad, USA). The membrane was washed three times with 1x TBST and blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature. It was then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies, as specified in the reagent list, on a shaker. Post-primary antibody incubation, the membrane was washed three times with 1X TBST and incubated with a horseradish peroxidase-coupled secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Proteins were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection system (Millipore, USA) and captured on film. Band intensity was quantified with ImageJ software (Version 1.46r).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The ultrastructure of CA1 neurons was examined using a Philips Tecnai 20 U-Twin transmission electron microscope (FEI Company, Eindhoven, The Netherlands). Tissue samples from the bilateral CA1 region were meticulously dissected to a size of 1 mm × 1 mm x 1 mm, fixed in a 1% osmium tetroxide solution for 2 h, and subjected to a graded ethanol dehydration series. The samples were then infiltrated with a semi-epoxy propane mixture overnight and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections, 70 nm in thickness, were prepared and sequentially stained with 4% uranyl acetate for 20 min followed by 0.5% lead citrate on copper grids. A minimum of 30 micrographs were randomly selected from each specimen for analysis using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Plasmid transfection

Cells were first cultured to 70–80% density. Subsequently, plasmid DNA was purified using a plasmid purification kit. Next, plasmid DNA was mixed with Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) in serum-free medium (Opti-MEM I) to form a transfection complex. After incubation, it was added to the cell culture medium. Finally, the cells were incubated in an incubator for 48 h to knockdown IL-27Ra. The plasmid (IL-27Ra knockdown) was purchased from MiaoLingBio, China.

HE staining

Brain, heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were fixed with 4% PFA and then frozen or paraffin sectioned. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to observe the neuronal morphology of the brain hippocampus.

Golgi staining

Mouse brains were harvested and subjected to Golgi-Cox staining following the manufacturer’s protocol outlined in the FD Rapid Golgi Staining Kit (PK401, FD NeuroTechnologies, Inc, Ellicott City, MD, USA). The brain tissues were sectioned into 150-μm thick coronal slices using a vibrating microtome (Leica VT1200S, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were collected onto gelatin-coated glass microscope slides, dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol, cleared with xylene, and subsequently coverslipped. The prepared slides were then stored at room temperature in a light-protected environment. Pyramidal neurons within the hippocampal region were morphologically assessed, and representative images were captured using an Olympus VS120 microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Nissl staining

Nissl staining was employed to delineate neuronal cell bodies in the mouse hippocampus. Brain tissue sections were air-dried at room temperature, postfixed in 4% PFA, rinsed in PBS, and stained with a 0.1% Cresyl Violet Acetate solution for 20 min. The sections were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a coverslip using Permount medium. The Nissl staining solution was prepared by dissolving 0.1 g of Cresyl Violet Acetate in 100 mL of double-distilled water with 250 μL of glacial acetic acid, stirred overnight at room temperature, and filtered before use. This method allowed for detailed observation and analysis of neuronal morphology.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells utilizing TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and RNA concentration was measured via spectrophotometry (Bio-Rad, USA) at 260 nm wavelength. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a PrimeScript RT kit with gDNA Eraser (TOYOBO). qPCR was then conducted to assess the mRNA levels of various genes, employing the SYBR Green QuantiTect RT-qPCR Kit (Toyobo, Japan) along with gene-specific primers listed (Figure S1; Table S1). The qPCR conditions included an initial pre-denaturation at 95°C for 10 min, followed by 39 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 15 s, and annealing at 60°C for 1 min.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were conducted using the Simple ChIP (R) kit (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Cultured microglia were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 15 min to facilitate cross-linking between histones and DNA. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS and lysed using SDS lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. The lysates were sonicated to shear the chromatin into 200–500 bp fragments. Chromatin fragments were then immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C using either a specific anti-p-STAT1 antibody or a control anti-IgG antibody. Following immunoprecipitation, the chromatin complexes were washed, eluted, and the cross-links were reversed as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA extracted from these complexes was subsequently analyzed using real-time fluorescent qPCR with Trem2 primers (Forward primer: TGGCTACAGCGTCAGGGTA, Reverse primer: GGGAGGAAGGAAAGGAGG).

Long-term toxicity

For the toxicity study, mice were randomly divided into two groups, including the Control and rIL-27 groups. Body weight and food consumption were measured. After the behavioral tests, the hematology, coagulation, and blood biochemical indexes were measured once each. Hematological parameters included white blood cell count (WBC), lymphocyte count (Lymph#) and its percentage (Lymph%), monocyte count (MON#) and its percentage (MONO%), red blood cell (RBC) count, hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean red blood cell volume (MCV), mean erythrocyte hemoglobin content (MCH), mean erythrocyte hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelet count (PLT), Neutrophil count (Gran#) and its percentage (Gran%), red blood cell distribution width (RDW), mean platelet volume (MPV), and platelet distribution width (PDW). Coagulation parameters included prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and fibrinogen (FIB). Blood biochemical parameters included alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphate Enzyme (ALP), creatinine (CREA), uric acid (UA), creatine kinase (CK), glucose (GLU), total cholesterol (T-CHO), potassium ion concentration (K+) and phosphorus ion concentration (P+).

Human sample collection and processing

Participants meeting the inclusion criteria underwent fasting venous blood collection at 8:00 a.m. This procedure was carried out by professional healthcare personnel. All participants were required to complete the HAMD and PHQ-9 scales for further screening and underwent a socioeconomic status (SES) assessment. Subsequently, samples were centrifuged at 2500 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and plasma was carefully collected from the supernatant. Total RNA was isolated from plasma using the MolPure Blood RNA Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), followed by reverse transcription and qPCR for gene expression analysis.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Human data analysis

Independent samples t-tests, chi-square tests or fisher’s exact test were used to compare differences in sex, age, and socioeconomic status between the case group and the control group. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to compare the differences in IL-27 between the case group and the control group. The model controlled for sex, age, and SES of participants in both groups. The SES was calculated by adding the socio-demographic response values (living location: urban = 0, rural = 1; family total income over the past year: <50000 RMB = 1,≥50000 RMB = 0; father’s education level: primary school education or lower = 0, Junior high school and above = 1; mother’s education level: primary school education or lower = 0, junior high school and above = 1, with the total possible SES score ranging from 0 to 4. Subsequently, we selected only the participants from the case group and used multiple linear regression analysis to examine the associations of HMAD and PHQ-9 scores with IL-27 levels. The model again controlled for sex, age, and SES of the participants. Multiple imputations with Markov chain Monte Carlo methods were used to handle incomplete data (five imputed datasets).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Normality of the data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed Student’s t test was applied. When comparing multiple groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by a Tukey’s multiple comparisons test for post hoc analyses. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Asterisks indicate statistical significance as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
