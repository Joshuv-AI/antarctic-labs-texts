Early ICU glycemic trajectory phenotypes predict short- and long-term mortality in critically ill patients: a dual-database cohort study
Authors: Deyu Kong, Yang Long, Tingting Huang, Yonghu Zhang, Wen-Jie Liu, Cheng-Xi Liu, Shan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsDysglycemia is common in critically ill patients and is associated with adverse outcomes, but the prognostic significance of early ICU glycemic patterns remains unclear.MethodsIn this dual-database cohort study, 60,365 critically ill patients from MIMIC-IV were used for model development and 81,237 from eICU for external validation. Early blood glucose trajectories were clustered using dynamic time warping (DTW). Associations with mortality were assessed using survival and multivariable regression analyses. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO)-selected predictors were incorporated into machine-learning models, which were externally validated and interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsFour glycemic trajectory phenotypes were identified in both cohorts. A rising stress hyperglycemia-like trajectory was consistently associated with the highest mortality risk. In MIMIC-IV, this phenotype was associated with higher in-hospital, 180-day, and 1-year mortality; in eICU, it was also associated with the highest in-hospital mortality. Machine-learning models integrating trajectory phenotype with other clinical variables outperformed Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS). Random Forest was selected as the representative model, and a web-based tool was developed for individualized risk estimation.ConclusionsA rising stress hyperglycemia-like phenotype identifies a high-risk subgroup of critically ill patients and may improve mortality risk stratification beyond conventional severity scores.Keywords: Critical illness, Glycemic trajectory phenotype, Stress hyperglycemia, Mortality prediction, Machine learning

Introduction

Dysglycemia is common in critically ill patients and is consistently associated with adverse outcomes, including mortality, organ dysfunction, and prolonged ICU stay [1,2]. Hyperglycemia, hypoglycemia, glycemic variability (GV), and stress hyperglycemia(SHG) have all been linked to poor prognosis, although much of the available evidence comes from selected patient populations [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Because glucose responses in critical illness are highly dynamic and influenced by underlying disease, stress severity, and treatment exposure, how best to characterize dysglycemia across the broader ICU population remains unclear.

Most studies still rely on static or summary measures, such as admission glucose, mean glucose, peak glucose, and stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) [7,8]. Although clinically informative, these metrics reduce complex glucose behavior to single values and provide limited insight into its temporal evolution. SHR improves the assessment of relative hyperglycemia by accounting for chronic glycemic status, but it remains largely a single time-point measure, and its prognostic threshold varies across studies. GV, the most widely used dynamic metric, reflects the degree of fluctuation but not trajectory direction, persistence, or overall pattern [9]. Consequently, patients with similar glucose summaries, SHR, or GV may still follow biologically distinct glucose courses with different prognostic implications.

Trajectory-based methods, including latent class modeling, group-based trajectory modeling, and clustering approaches, have been increasingly used to characterize temporal heterogeneity in glucose profiles among critically ill patients. Emerging evidence suggests that longitudinal glucose trajectories may define subgroups with different mortality risks [10,11]. However, in the ICU, clinically relevant glycemic phenotypes may vary not only by absolute glucose level but also by the direction, timing, and persistence of change over time [12]. Patients with similar overall glucose courses may differ substantially in the temporal pattern of glycemic evolution, such as transient early hyperglycemia with subsequent recovery, persistent hyperglycemia, or progressive worsening [13]. Conventional trajectory methods may be less sensitive to these distinctions when differences are driven primarily by the timing of change rather than by average trajectory level. Dynamic time warping (DTW), which aligns time series on the basis of shape similarity while accommodating local temporal shifts, may therefore provide a useful approach for identifying early glycemic phenotypes in critically ill patients [14,15]. We therefore applied DTW-based trajectory phenotyping to glucose data collected during the first 48 h after ICU admission in two large ICU databases to identify early glycemic phenotypes, examine their associations with mortality, and assess their incremental prognostic value beyond conventional severity scores.

Methods

Data sources

We conducted a retrospective observational cohort study following the RECORD guideline, using a two-stage development–validation design to assess the robustness of dynamic glucose trajectory phenotyping across different ICU eras and heterogeneous critically ill populations.

The development cohort came from MIMIC-IV v3.1 (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, 2008–2022; Meta Vision system). The external validation cohort was drawn from the eICU Collaborative Research Database, a multicenter critical care repository capturing diverse practices and patient populations. Both databases are publicly available, fully de-identified, and compliant with privacy regulations. Access required completion of mandatory training and certification.

Study population

Adult critically ill patients aged ≥18 years admitted to the ICU were included. Eligible patients needed sufficient blood glucose data within the first 48 h after ICU admission to characterize early glycemic dynamics under real-world monitoring conditions. Specifically, we included patients with at least three glucose measurements within the first 48 h and at least one glucose measurement in each 24-h interval during this period. We excluded those with ICU stay <24 h or inadequate glucose monitoring frequency.

Data extraction and processing

Clinical variables were extracted via SQL scripts and imported into R for cleaning and analysis. Clinical variables collected included age, sex, race, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Charlson comorbidity index (CCI); early interventions within the first 24 h (mechanical ventilation use, renal replacement therapy (RRT) use, vasopressor use, sedative therapy, and insulin therapy); comorbidities (hypertension, heart failure (HF), chronic kidney disease (CKD), liver disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), stroke, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)); vital signs within the first 24 h (temperature, respiratory rate, mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2), and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)); laboratory tests within the first 24 h (white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin, platelet count, lactate, creatinine, and international normalized ratio (INR)).

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes were 180-day and 1-year all-cause mortality, ascertained via in-hospital timestamps and linked out-of-hospital records. Missingness was assessed for all candidate covariates before model development. Covariates with more than 30% missingness were excluded from downstream analyses. For the remaining covariates, missing values were imputed using chained equations with predictive mean matching. Imputation was applied only to covariates, and the completed analytic dataset was used for subsequent analyses.

Glucose trajectory phenotyping

Glucose trajectories were characterized over the first 48 h after ICU admission. Blood glucose measurements obtained during this period were aligned to consecutive 1-h intervals indexed from ICU admission. If more than one glucose measurement was recorded within the same 1-h interval, the mean value was used as the observed glucose value for that interval; if no glucose measurement was available, the corresponding hourly value was coded as missing. After applying the predefined eligibility criteria, missing hourly glucose values were imputed using Stineman interpolation to generate a complete hourly glucose trajectory over the first 48 h for each included ICU admission. The interpolated trajectories were then standardized using Z-score normalization within each included ICU admission before dynamic time warping-based clustering.

Pairwise similarity was measured with dynamic time warping, which aligns sequences under boundary, monotonicity, and continuity constraints to handle temporal shifts. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering with Ward's linkage was applied to the DTW distance matrix. Cluster number was selected via the elbow method on within-cluster dispersion, combined with clinical interpretability and balanced group sizes.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized by trajectory group. Continuous variables were assessed for normality via Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and reported as mean ± SD or median (IQR) as appropriate, with comparisons using t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as n (%) and compared with χ2 or Fisher's exact tests. All tests were two-sided at α = 0.05.

Associations with mortality were evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests for in-hospital, 180-day, and 1-year outcomes. Independent effects were assessed using Cox proportional hazards models and generalized linear models (GLM) under multiple adjustment strategies, including models adjusted for all candidate variables, variables selected by univariable analyses, and variables identified by the Boruta feature selection algorithm based on random forest.

Machine learning modeling and explainability

Supervised models for in-hospital mortality were developed in the MIMIC-IV cohort and externally validated in eICU to evaluate the added prognostic value of glucose trajectory phenotypes beyond conventional severity scores. The development cohort was split 7:3 into training and internal test sets with outcome stratification. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) with 10-fold cross-validation (lambda.1se) performed feature selection in training, but the high-risk trajectory phenotype was forced into all models.

Eight algorithms were trained: logistic regression, random forest, extra trees, AdaBoost, gradient boosting decision trees, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost. Hyperparameters were tuned by grid search with cross-validation. Performance was assessed primarily by area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in internal test and external validation sets, supplemented by accuracy, balanced accuracy, Matthews correlation coefficient, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). To further assess cross-cohort generalizability in the external eICU validation cohort, model calibration was additionally evaluated using the Brier score, calibration intercept, calibration slope, and decile-based calibration plots. Models were benchmarked against SAPS II and OASIS (Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score) alone.

A web-based interface was built from the best-performing model for individualized risk assessment. Interpretability relied on SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values (TreeSHAP for ensembles). Mean absolute SHAP values indicated global importance, while signs showed directional effects. Interaction values explored non-linear relationships, with particular attention to interactions involving the glucose trajectory phenotype and major clinical predictors.

Results

Patient selection and identification of glycemic trajectory phenotypes

A total of 94,444 critically ill patients from MIMIC-IV were initially screened. After excluding patients with an ICU length of stay <24 h (n = 19,956), those with fewer than three blood glucose measurements during the ICU stay (n = 14,100), those without International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnostic information (n = 17), and those with missing discharge outcome data (n = 6), 60,365 patients were included in the development cohort. In the eICU cohort, 200,818 critically ill patients were screened. After excluding patients with an ICU length of stay <24 h (n = 67,959), age <18 years (n = 248), fewer than three blood glucose measurements during the ICU stay (n = 45,923), no ICD diagnostic information (n = 4665), and missing discharge outcome data (n = 786), 81,237 patients were included in the external validation cohort (Fig. 1).

Study flowchart. (A) MIMIC-IV (development cohort) and (B) elCU (external validation cohort).

Based on dynamic time warping (DTW) clustering of blood glucose trajectories, four distinct subphenotypes were identified in each cohort (Fig. 2). In MIMIC-IV, the four subphenotypes included 15,675, 20,640, 11,862, and 12,188 patients, respectively, whereas in eICU, the corresponding numbers were 19,658, 23,697, 20,136, and 17,746. In MIMIC-IV, subphenotype 3 showed a progressively rising glucose trajectory, consistent with a stress hyperglycemia-like pattern, whereas in eICU, a similar rising trajectory was observed in subphenotype 1. The other subphenotypes displayed heterogeneous temporal patterns, including early decline, transient elevation, or biphasic changes in glucose levels. Consistently, t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) visualization demonstrated moderate separation of the four subphenotypes in both cohorts, supporting the stability of the identified trajectory phenotypes.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the MIMIC-IV and eICU cohorts are summarized in Table 1. Compared with patients in the eICU cohort, those in the MIMIC-IV cohort were slightly older (67 [56, 77] vs 66 [54, 76] years), less often female (42.38% vs 45.29%), and had a lower SOFA score (4 [2,7] vs 6 [4,8]) but higher SAPS II score (36 [28, 45] vs 31 [23, 42]) and Charlson comorbidity index (5 [3,7] vs 4 [2,6]) (all p < 0.001). Mechanical ventilation (51.14% vs 43.66%) and renal replacement therapy (4.61% vs 3.75%) were more common in the eICU cohort, whereas vasopressor use (35.32% vs 23.02%), sedative therapy (43.77% vs 32.30%), and insulin therapy (39.56% vs 29.92%) were more frequent in the MIMIC-IV cohort (all p < 0.001). Hospital mortality was slightly higher in MIMIC-IV than in eICU (12.36% vs 11.13%; p < 0.001).

In the MIMIC-IV cohort, baseline characteristics differed across the four glycemic trajectory subphenotypes. Subphenotype 3, corresponding to the rising stress hyperglycemia-like trajectory, was characterized by an older age distribution, a lower proportion of female patients, a higher Charlson comorbidity index, and higher prevalences of heart failure, chronic kidney disease, liver disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes mellitus compared with the overall MIMIC-IV cohort. Specifically, patients in subphenotype 3 were older (65.91 ± 15.68 vs 65.49 ± 15.87 years), less often female (41.37% vs 42.38%), and had a higher Charlson comorbidity index (5.55 ± 3.01 vs 5.28 ± 2.98). This subphenotype also had higher hospital mortality (14.46% vs 12.36%), 180-day mortality (30.11% vs 25.81%), and 1-year mortality (35.67% vs 30.65%). Detailed baseline characteristics across MIMIC-IV subphenotypes are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

In the eICU cohort, baseline differences across subphenotypes were also observed, although the magnitude of these differences was generally modest. Subphenotype 1, which corresponded to the rising stress hyperglycemia-like trajectory, was characterized by a slightly higher severity burden and a higher prevalence of selected comorbidities. Compared with the overall eICU cohort, patients in subphenotype 1 were slightly older (66.00 [55.00, 76.00] vs 66.00 [54.00, 76.00] years), less often female (44.65% vs 45.29%), had a slightly higher SAPS II score (32 [24, 42] vs 31 [23, 42]), and had higher prevalences of heart failure (24.86% vs 22.99%), chronic kidney disease (20.61% vs 19.14%), liver disease (6.34% vs 5.36%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (22.28% vs 20.54%). This subphenotype also showed the highest hospital mortality among eICU subphenotypes (12.05% vs 11.13%). Detailed baseline characteristics across eICU subphenotypes are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Association with short- and long-term mortality

Kaplan–Meier analyses demonstrated significant differences in survival across glycemic trajectory subphenotypes in the MIMIC-IV cohort (all log-rank p < 0.001), with the rising stress hyperglycemia-like trajectory showing the lowest survival probability throughout follow-up (Supplementary Fig. 1A, 1C, and 1D). In the development cohort, subphenotype 3 was used as the reference group. In the fully adjusted Cox model, subphenotypes 1, 2, and 4 were all associated with significantly lower risks of in-hospital, 180-day, and 1-year mortality. For in-hospital mortality, the corresponding HRs were 0.86 (95% CI, 0.80–0.91; p < 0.001), 0.79 (95% CI, 0.74–0.84; p < 0.001), and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.77–0.90; p < 0.001), respectively. Similar associations were observed for 180-day mortality and 1-year mortality, with HRs and corresponding p-values reported in Table 2. These findings were directionally consistent across alternative Cox and logistic regression models, including models adjusted using covariates selected by univariable analyses and by the Boruta algorithm, further supporting the robustness of the association. Detailed variable selection results and model-specific covariate sets are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2A and Supplementary Tables 3–30.

In the external validation cohort, Kaplan–Meier curves likewise showed significant survival differences across trajectory subphenotypes (all log-rank p < 0.001), with the corresponding rising stress hyperglycemia-like trajectory, namely subphenotype 1, exhibiting the poorest survival (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Using this subgroup as the reference, subphenotypes 2, 3, and 4 were associated with lower in-hospital mortality risk in adjusted analyses. In the fully adjusted Cox model, the HRs were 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.93; p < 0.001), 0.90 (95% CI, 0.85–0.96; p < 0.001), and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.86–0.97; p = 0.004), respectively. Similar findings were observed across alternative modeling strategies, with HRs and corresponding p-values reported in Table 2. Detailed variable selection results and model-specific covariate sets are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2A and Supplementary Tables 3–30.

Feature selection for in-hospital mortality prediction

To identify a parsimonious set of predictors for mortality prediction while minimizing overfitting, LASSO regression was applied in the training set, and the tuning parameter was selected by cross-validation (Supplementary Fig. 3). Using the lambda.1se criterion to favor model simplicity and stability, the final model retained predictors from several domains, including age, use of mechanical ventilation and vasopressors, major comorbidities, vital signs, laboratory markers, and the SHG trajectory phenotype.

Model performance and comparison with traditional severity scores

Using the LASSO-selected predictors, we trained multiple machine-learning models and evaluated their performance for in-hospital mortality prediction in the MIMIC-IV cohort and the external eICU cohort (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Tables 31–32). In the MIMIC-IV cohort, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting showed the best discrimination, each with an area under AUC of 0.81, whereas Extra Trees, AdaBoost, and Logistic Regression showed slightly lower performance (AUC 0.77–0.80). In the external eICU validation cohort, model performance remained stable, with AUCs ranging from 0.77 to 0.79. CatBoost, XGBoost, LightGBM, Gradient Boosting, and Random Forest each achieved an AUC of 0.79, outperforming OASIS (AUC 0.73) and showing slightly better discrimination than SAPS II (AUC 0.77). Across models, specificity remained consistently high in both cohorts, whereas recall was relatively modest, which was consistent with the low event rate in this setting.

To further assess cross-cohort generalizability, we evaluated the external calibration of probability-based prediction models in the eICU validation cohort. Several machine-learning models showed more favorable calibration than SAPS II, as reflected by lower Brier scores and calibration intercepts and slopes closer to the ideal values. The final Random Forest model selected for main presentation showed acceptable external calibration, with a Brier score of 0.0876, calibration intercept of −0.386, and calibration slope of 1.136. These values compared favorably with SAPS II, which had a Brier score of 0.1092, calibration intercept of −1.014, and calibration slope of 0.657. Decile-based calibration plots showed better agreement between predicted and observed in-hospital mortality for the Random Forest model than for SAPS II, whereas SAPS II tended to overestimate risk, particularly in higher-risk strata (Supplementary Fig. 4A and B and Supplementary Table 33).

Given its strong and stable discrimination across both cohorts and acceptable external calibration, Random Forest was selected as the representative model for subsequent interpretability analyses. Overall, these results suggest that machine-learning models integrating trajectory phenotype and other clinical variables provided better predictive performance than conventional severity scores.

Model interpretability and web-based application

To further examine the clinical utility of the prediction model, we used SHAP to interpret the Random Forest model in both cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 5). Although multiple variables contributed to risk prediction, the SHG trajectory phenotype consistently showed a positive contribution in both the MIMIC-IV and eICU cohorts, indicating that this trajectory-related feature provided incremental prognostic information within the multivariable machine-learning framework. The overall attribution pattern was broadly similar across the two cohorts, supporting the stability of the model and the reproducibility of the prognostic value of the SHG trajectory phenotype.

To facilitate individualized risk assessment, we further developed a web-based prediction interface based on the Random Forest model (Supplementary Fig. 6). The interface integrates the selected predictors and enables user-friendly estimation of in-hospital mortality risk at the individual patient level, which may improve the accessibility and potential clinical applicability of the proposed model.

Discussion

The main findings of our study were as follows: (1) DTW-based clustering of glucose trajectories during the first 48 h after ICU admission identified distinct glycemic phenotypes in critically ill patients, and the rising trajectory, which may reflect SHG, was associated with increased short- and long-term mortality; (2) incorporation of trajectory phenotypes into machine learning models improved the prediction of in-hospital mortality compared with conventional severity-based models; and (3) the robustness of these findings was supported by external validation in the eICU cohort, and a web-based prediction tool was further developed to enhance model transparency and facilitate individualized risk assessment.

Among the identified phenotypes, the SHG trajectory was of particular clinical relevance, as it was consistently associated with worse short- and long-term outcomes. Several mechanisms may underlie this association [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. Persistent hyperglycemia itself can exert harmful effects by enhancing oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, inflammatory injury, and microcirculatory impairment, thereby contributing to progressive organ damage in critical illness. At the same time, in severely ill patients whose condition remains inadequately controlled, rising glucose levels may also serve as a direct and readily measurable manifestation of ongoing metabolic dysregulation. Therefore, the prognostic value of this phenotype may reflect both the detrimental effects of hyperglycemia and its role as a visible marker of unresolved physiologic stress. Moreover, trajectories derived from glucose measurements over the first 48 h using DTW may provide greater clinical insight than isolated glucose values or static summary indices, because they preserve the temporal pattern of glycemic change. By capturing the overall direction and evolution of glucose disturbance, this approach may better distinguish sustained deterioration from transient abnormalities during the early phase of critical illness [18,19].

Although glucose trajectory phenotyping alone enabled clinically meaningful risk stratification, a single phenotypic label may not fully capture the heterogeneity of outcome risk in critically ill patients [6,20]. We therefore integrated trajectory phenotypes with other clinical features in a machine learning framework to construct an individualized prediction model. Compared with SAPS II and OASIS, the final model showed better performance for predicting in-hospital mortality, suggesting that DTW-derived glycemic trajectory features, when combined with other clinical variables in a machine-learning framework, add prognostic information beyond conventional severity scores. This improvement is likely multifactorial and may reflect both the prognostic relevance of early glucose trajectory patterns and the ability of the modeling approach to integrate multiple predictors and capture complex associations among them [21]. Temporal trajectory information may be particularly helpful in identifying patients with sustained dysregulation and a persistently high-risk clinical course [22]. The model's performance was further supported by external validation in the eICU cohort, suggesting good reproducibility across different ICU populations. In addition, the web-based implementation of the final model may improve its practical utility by enabling transparent and individualized risk estimation in routine clinical settings.

Previous research has made important contributions by demonstrating the prognostic relevance of dysglycemia in critical illness using conventional glucose metrics, and some studies have further explored glycemic heterogeneity through unsupervised approaches such as latent class analysis and k-means clustering [10,11,19]. Our findings are broadly consistent with prior evidence linking stress hyperglycemia to worse outcomes in selected critically ill populations [8,23,24]. Notably, the highest-risk phenotype identified in our trajectory analysis was also characterized by a stress-hyperglycemia pattern, providing further support for the prognostic importance of this metabolic response. However, our study differs from previous work in several important respects. First, while many prior studies focused on selected disease-specific cohorts, we evaluated glucose trajectories in a broad and heterogeneous ICU population, allowing the identified phenotypes to be interpreted as a more general marker of early metabolic stress across critical illness. Second, rather than relying on isolated glucose values, summary indices, or feature-based clustering alone, we applied DTW-based clustering to characterize the temporal evolution of glucose during the first 48 h after ICU admission. This approach may better distinguish patients with similar overall glucose profiles but different dynamic patterns of change. In particular, the rising trajectory identified in our study suggests that the prognostic significance of dysglycemia may depend not only on its magnitude, but also on its direction and persistence over time. Taken together, our findings complement prior studies by extending glycemic phenotyping toward a more dynamic, time-sensitive, and clinically generalizable characterization of early metabolic instability in critical illness.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, because of the retrospective observational design, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of causality. Although stress hyperglycemia-related phenotypes, particularly the rising trajectory, were associated with adverse outcomes, it remains unclear whether early correction of these abnormalities would translate into clinical benefit or whether they mainly serve as markers of more severe underlying illness.Second, trajectory construction was based on routine clinical glucose measurements and may therefore have been influenced by differences in monitoring intensity and treatment exposure. In addition, the requirement of at least one glucose measurement in each 24-h interval during the first 48 h and at least three measurements in total may have excluded patients with very short ICU stays, early death, or infrequent glucose monitoring. Consequently, the included cohort may not fully represent patients at the mildest or most severe ends of critical illness, which may limit the generalizability of the findings.Third, some covariates had missing data. Covariates with substantial missingness were excluded from the revised analyses, and missing values in the remaining covariates were imputed using chained equations. Nevertheless, missing-data handling may not fully eliminate potential bias. In particular, lactate was retained because of its clinical relevance and missingness below the prespecified exclusion threshold, but related findings should still be interpreted with caution.

Fourth, only the first 48 h after ICU admission were evaluated, and later glycemic patterns were not assessed. Finally, although external validation and calibration assessment were performed in the eICU cohort, explicit cross-cohort recalibration was not conducted. Because both MIMIC-IV and eICU are U.S.-based ICU databases, the transportability of absolute risk estimates to other healthcare systems or practice settings may still be limited.

Future prospective studies are needed to validate these phenotypes in broader populations and, more importantly, to determine whether trajectory-informed early glucose management can improve outcomes rather than simply enhance risk stratification.

Conclusion

Our study identified distinct early glucose trajectory phenotypes in critically ill patients and showed that a rising trajectory was consistently associated with adverse short- and long-term outcomes. Our core finding highlights that glycemic assessment in critical illness should move beyond isolated glucose measurements to incorporate the temporal evolution of glucose disturbances, which may improve early risk stratification and individualized prognostic evaluation.
