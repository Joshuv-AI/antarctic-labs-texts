Introduction

The purpose of this case report is to explore a possible association between development of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC). This will be elucidated through a patient with resected MTC with none of the known risk factors for this malignancy such as a family history or previous radiations exposure. Other cases of cervical metastasis and MTC development following cervical spinal procedures were researched, and there were cases of cervical metastasis following cervical disc replacement. One described cervical metastasis in a patient with preexisting cervical disc replacement and fusion after 2-year symptom-free interval. A separate report discussed a case of metastasis development at the site of cervical spine arthrodesis. However, none of the similar cases additionally led to MTC, and there were no cases found of MTC development following cervical spine procedures.

The tumors of MTC are of neuroendocrine origin and furthermore are derived from the parafollicular C cells of the thyroid gland. While some of these rare malignancies are familial, most are sporadic. [1] The pathogenesis for the familial type often involves germline RET mutations and typically presents bilaterally, while the sporadic type is more commonly associated with somatic RET mutations and usually presents unilaterally. [1] Sporadic MTC arises from molecular mechanisms that have yet to be clarified and has a polyclonal origin. Notable factors associated with increased risk of MTC development include history of thyroid nodules, hypertension, gallbladder disease, allergies, and in women having first birth after 25 years of age. [2] Total thyroidectomy is the treatment of choice for MTC, with management including preoperative and postoperative calcitonin measurements as well as evaluation for pheochromocytoma and hyperparathyroidism before surgery. [1].

The prevalence of cervical intervertebral disc herniations in asymptomatic subjects increases significantly with age, and as the aging population continues to grow in the United States, so will the rate of spinal fusion procedures [3]. The anterior approach for access to the cervical spine has been utilized since its introduction in the 1950s in order to overcome the limitations of the posterior approach. [4] It has been utilized, not only for degenerative cervical disc disease, but also, traumatic, neoplastic, and infectious indications. [4] The most common complications related to anterior cervical discectomy are dysphagia, hematoma, worsening myelopathy, recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks, wound infection, radiculopathy, Horner’s Syndrome, respiratory insufficiency, esophageal perforation, and instrument failure. [5].

Patient case presentation

A 57-year-old non-Hispanic White female with a medical history notable for type 2 diabetes mellitus, nonfunctioning right kidney, dilated cardiomyopathy with heart failure, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia presented with a two-day history of throat tightness, dysphagia, and right-sided neck swelling. Past surgical history included an anterior cervical discectomy performed in 2002 for right-sided upper-extremity numbness, followed by a total thyroidectomy with cervical lymph node dissection in 2013. There was no known family history of thyroid cancer, medullary thyroid carcinoma, or hereditary cancer syndromes, and no known germline mutations were reported. Approximately one year after her cervical discectomy, the patient noticed a small lump on the right side of her neck. Over the following decade, the mass enlarged progressively. Ten years after the initial onset, she experienced more rapid growth of the mass, prompting evaluation with ultrasound, which demonstrated thyroid enlargement. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) subsequently revealed atypical cells suspicious for malignancy.

Six days after the FNA, she presented to the emergency department with worsening throat tightness and hoarseness. Physical examination revealed a well-healed anterior cervical surgical scar without acute tenderness or erythema. Her vital signs were stable, and she remained alert and oriented. Laboratory testing showed preserved renal and hepatic function.

A chest radiograph demonstrated leftward tracheal deviation. Soft-tissue computed tomography (CT) of the neck revealed a markedly enlarged multinodular thyroid gland with several lobulated nodules and prominent right cervical lymphadenopathy. The morphology of the nodal tissue closely resembled that of the thyroid lesions, raising concern for metastatic thyroid carcinoma. The mass effect resulted in slight leftward displacement of the trachea and esophagus and focal narrowing of the proximal trachea.

The patient underwent total thyroidectomy with resection of involved cervical lymph nodes. The operative time was approximately eight hours. Histopathological analysis confirmed medullary thyroid carcinoma with metastatic involvement of right cervical lymph nodes.

Discussion

In this case of a 57-year-old non-Hispanic White female patient with previous history of anterior cervical discectomy, thyroid enlargement eventually led to the diagnosis of medullary thyroid carcinoma. MTC can present in a variety of different ways, and this clinical presentation of MTC is not uncommon. [6] However, the clinical significance of this case lies less in proving a causal relationship between prior cervical spine surgery and MTC, and more in highlighting the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis when evaluating persistent or progressive cervical symptoms in patients with a history of prior neck procedures.

Although the temporal relationship between the prior anterior cervical procedure and later discovery of malignancy is notable, this case does not establish that the surgery contributed to the development or progression of medullary thyroid carcinoma. The long interval between the spinal operation and the eventual diagnosis, along with the absence of direct biologic evidence linking the two events in this patient, makes any causal inference speculative. A more cautious interpretation is that prior cervical surgery may complicate the clinical picture by anchoring later symptoms to postoperative or musculoskeletal explanations, thereby risking delayed consideration of non-spinal etiologies such as thyroid malignancy or metastatic cervical disease. In such patients without pathological cervical imaging, interdisciplinary diagnostics may be needed in order to differentiate vertebrogenic from nonvertebrogenic causes of recurrent cervicobrachialgia. [7].

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is one of the most common cervical spine procedures performed each year. [5] Even though the mortality and complication rates have shown to be low, with good to excellent clinical results; these operations have high morbidity rates from 13.2% to 19.3%. [5] Recurrent complications such as cervicobrachialgia are not uncommon, even after a one-to-two-year symptom-free interval. Symptom recurrence is common and unsurprising, but the contributing factors are multifactorial, and we should always account for metastatic tumor and other malignancies in the differential diagnosis. [8].

This case also underscores an important diagnostic principle: recurrent neck symptoms, dysphagia, hoarseness, or enlarging cervical masses in patients with a history of cervical spine surgery should not automatically be attributed to postoperative change or degenerative disease. In the present case, imaging demonstrated a markedly enlarged multinodular thyroid gland, right cervical lymphadenopathy, and mass effect with tracheal displacement and focal airway narrowing, all of which appropriately shifted the evaluation toward structural and oncologic causes. These findings reinforce the value of careful clinical reassessment and cross-sectional imaging when symptoms evolve or when examination suggests a process not fully explained by prior orthopedic or neurologic history.

The discussion should therefore focus on diagnostic vigilance rather than mechanism. While prior literature has described cases in which metastatic disease or other nonvertebrogenic pathology was identified in patients with prior cervical procedures, such reports are best used to support awareness of alternative diagnoses, not to imply that cervical surgery is an established risk factor for MTC. These previously documented cases of undiagnosed metastatic disease should alert surgeons and other care providers to this possibility at sites of previous cervical spinal procedures. [13] Another strength of this case is its illustration of how indolent progression can still culminate in clinically significant locoregional disease. The patient noted a neck lump approximately one year after surgery, with gradual enlargement over time and more rapid progression later, ultimately prompting fine-needle aspiration and definitive surgical treatment.

The five-year survival rate for resected MTC is 80–97%, indicating that this patient has a good prognostic survival rate. [16] Despite the advent of highly selective RET-targeted tyrosine-kinase inhibitors (TKIs), resistance mechanisms and the tumor microenvironment continue to pose significant barriers to durable responses, underscoring the need for earlier detection and appropriate recognition of concerning clinical findings. [18] Overall, this case should be interpreted as a report of medullary thyroid carcinoma identified in a patient with prior anterior cervical discectomy, rather than as evidence that the spinal procedure precipitated malignancy. Its principal message is that clinicians evaluating postoperative cervical complaints should remain alert to alternative and potentially serious diagnoses, especially when symptoms are progressive, structural findings are present, or the clinical course deviates from expected postoperative recovery.

Conclusion

Patients receiving anterior cervical discectomy tend to have high associated morbidity rates, and their symptomology is multifactorial. [5] Various spinal procedures that are done may reveal incidental metastatic or malignant lesions, a common example is MTC, and follow-up imaging after such procedures may play a vital role in early diagnosis of such cancers. [14] Interdisciplinary team discussions regarding such findings can provide a breakthrough that would aid patient care and hence lead to decreased morbidity and mortality in such cases. [15].
