Trends in utilization of an anonymous abortion and miscarriage hotline before and after Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sara K Redd, Alexandra A Gavidia, Taylor Jones, Sage Dillon, Alina A Luke, Emma Chew Murphy, Marissa J Arzate, Ashley Martinez, Mindy Brittner, April Lockley, Whitney Rice, Jennifer Karlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision significantly altered abortion access in the U.S., increasing reliance on resources outside the formal healthcare system. This study examined utilization of a medical hotline supporting people managing their abortions and miscarriages, comparing trends before and after Dobbs.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of de-identified data of US-based English and Spanish-speaking individuals contacting the Miscarriage and Abortion (M + A) Hotline between June 24, 2021 (one year pre-Dobbs) and June 24, 2023 (one year post-Dobbs). We assessed changes in contact volume, characteristics, and reason for contact over time and by state abortion ban status using descriptive statistics. We applied comparative interrupted time series analyses to test the association of Dobbs with changes in Hotline contacts across state abortion policy environments.FindingsAmong 16,429 unique Hotline contacts, 4009 (24%) occurred pre-Dobbs and 12,420 (76%) post-Dobbs, a 210% increase (p < 0.001). Pre-Dobbs, Hotline contacts increased 10% per month in no-ban states (p < 0.001). While baseline contact levels were comparable between ban and no-ban states, pre-Dobbs trends diverged significantly, with ban states experiencing 7% higher increases in monthly contacts (p < 0.001). Immediately post-Dobbs, monthly Hotline contact levels did not differ between ban and no-ban states; post-Dobbs trends diverged modestly between groups (p < 0.05).InterpretationHotline use was increasing rapidly prior to Dobbs—particularly in states that later enacted bans—indicating substantial unmet need preceding the policy change. Post-Dobbs, trends diverged modestly without an immediate surge, suggesting a more complex response than a simple policy-driven increase and underscoring persistent gaps in reproductive healthcare access.FundingSociety of Family Planning, the Center for Reproductive Health Research in the Southeast, and the US National Institutes of Health.Keywords: Abortion hotline, Self-managed abortion, Dobbs decision, United States

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed and Google Scholar for studies on abortion access, self-managed abortion, telemedicine, and alternative care models in the United States following the Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision. Search terms included “abortion,” “self managed abortion,” “telemedicine,” “medical hotline,” “abortion pill,” “reproductive justice,” and “trigger laws.” We also reviewed grey literature reports from the Guttmacher Institute. The search was first conducted in January 2025, without language restrictions and concluded in December 2025. Existing evidence shows that Dobbs substantially reduced access to facility based abortion care, increasing travel, cost, and delays. Studies document rising use of medication abortion, telemedicine, and self-managed abortion, as well as the safety and acceptability of these approaches. Although abortion hotlines have been described globally and qualitatively, we found no studies that quantitatively assess use of clinician-supported hotlines providing real-time guidance during an abortion or miscarriage in the United States, particularly across the pre- and post-Dobbs periods.

Added value of this study

This study provides the first quantitative analysis of utilization trends for the Miscarriage and Abortion (M + A) Hotline, a free, clinician-staffed service offering real-time medical guidance. Using more than 16,000 contacts spanning one year before and after Dobbs, we show a marked increase in demand in the months leading up to the Dobbs decision, with greater growth in states with abortion bans. By examining temporal trends and geographic variation, this study offers novel insight into how individuals who access the Hotline navigate abortion-related care when access to facility based services is restricted or unavailable. It highlights the role of clinician-supported hotlines as a critical, yet previously underexamined, component of the reproductive health care landscape in the United States.

Implications of all the available evidence

Restrictions on abortion care through gestational age bans have shifted demand toward alternative pathways rather than eliminated need. Increased Hotline utilization highlights widening gaps between reproductive health needs and accessible services. Monitoring and strengthening confidential, evidence-based support systems including clinician-supported hotlines, is essential to mitigate the public health effects of restrictive policies and to support reproductive autonomy and equity.

Introduction

Increasing numbers of individuals have been managing abortions and miscarriages without formal medical supervision,1, 2, 3, 4 a practice sometimes referred to as self-managed abortion (SMA),5 even prior to the Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization decision, which eliminated the constitutional right to abortion in the U.S. Although SMA often refers to obtaining abortion medications outside the formal medical system, the management of a medication abortion almost always occurs outside the clinical setting as most people do not take the pills or experience the symptoms in clinic. Since the Dobbs decision, SMA rates have grown further amidst restrictive abortion laws,6 with one study estimating that self-managed medication abortions increased by over 26,000 vs. what would have been expected had Dobbs not occurred.7,8

In abortion-restrictive contexts globally, hotlines have provided critical support for SMA.9,10 In the U.S., the Miscarriage and Abortion (M + A) Hotline, established in 2019, offers free, confidential medical information and support via phone or text messaging.11 Staffed by volunteer healthcare professionals, it operates 17 h daily, 7 days a week, serving individuals who are managing abortions or miscarriages, seeking post-clinic care support, or exploring pregnancy options. Despite growing evidence that abortion access has become significantly more challenging post-Dobbs with increased travel times, costs, and delays in services,12 no studies have characterized how individuals seek help with abortion and miscarriage management support through a U.S.-based medical hotline. We aimed to capture the experiences of Hotline users, regardless of how their medications were sourced, as there is limited understanding of how hotlines in the U.S. might provide personalized support by offering on-demand clinical information amid rising legal restrictions.

We examine trends in Hotline utilization before and after Dobbs, quantifying usage over time, exploring geographic variations, and identifying reasons for contact. We use a comparative interrupted time series (CITS) design to quasi-experimentally evaluate the association of the Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization ruling with changes in Hotline contacts. By analyzing how the Dobbs decision and subsequent state-level abortion bans have shaped Hotline usage, this research offers insight into how abortion experiences are evolving in the post-Dobbs U.S. and highlights gaps in reproductive healthcare access.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study analyzed de-identified user interaction data collected by the M + A Hotline. This study was reviewed and deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Boards at the University of California, Davis (February 2023) and Emory University (February 2024). The analysis used de-identified data, and informed consent was waived.

Data source

This study analyzed de-identified user interaction data collected by the M + A Hotline volunteers between June 24, 2021, and June 24, 2023. As part of their practice, Hotline volunteers gather limited data about each interaction, including dates of contact, state of contact, and relevant content disclosed during interactions (e.g., reason for contacting the Hotline, demographic information if disclosed by caller). To protect the anonymity and privacy of callers, no demographic or protected health information is collected by the Hotline. Data were stored in an encrypted database. For the purposes of this analysis, eligible interactions were required to meet the following criteria: (1) originating from a U.S. area code, (2) conducted in English or Spanish, and (3) occurring within the defined study period. All protocols and findings were reviewed periodically with a community advisory board throughout the research period.

Measures

Individual-level

Data from Hotline user interactions were extracted and manipulated into several relevant fields for analysis. For all contacts, these measures included: date of contact (initial date and last date), contact type (i.e., text or phone call), state of contact (inferred by area code if not explicitly mentioned by caller), and reason for contact. See Table 1 for definitions of each measure.

To characterize the reasons for contact, data were sorted into five main categories: questions about abortion prior to the abortion experience (“pre-abortion”), questions about abortions during the abortion experience (“during abortion”), questions about abortion experience after seeking in-clinic care (“after in-clinic care”), questions about miscarriage management (“miscarriage management”), and questions about general sexual and reproductive health (SRH) concerns apart from medical management of abortion and/or miscarriage (“additional SRH questions”). See Supplementary Table S1 for detailed sub-reasons for contact.

State-level

To examine differences in Hotline usage between varying state abortion environments (determined using state of contact), we compared Hotline usage between states with and without post-Dobbs bans. Using state policy data from Kaiser Family Foundation and the Center for Reproductive Rights, we categorized states as having post-Dobbs bans if they used the Dobbs Supreme Court decision as a legal precedent to further restrict abortion access prior to the viability standard set by Roe v. Wade at any point during the post-Dobbs study period (i.e., June 24, 2022 to June 24, 2023). For example, states like Alabama, which implemented a total abortion ban on the day of the Dobbs decision, and Georgia, which implemented an early (“6-week”) gestational limit on July 20, 2022, were both considered states with post-Dobbs bans (hereafter referred to as “post-Dobbs ban states” or “ban states”). Although Texas technically had an unconstitutional, pre-viability gestational limit in effect during the pre-Dobbs period (i.e., SB8), because the state used Dobbs to further restrict abortion access via implementing a total abortion ban, we also considered Texas a ban state. Ban states included Alabama, Arizona, Arkansas, Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Louisiana, Mississippi, Missouri, Nebraska, North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Utah, West Virginia, Wisconsin, and Wyoming. No-ban states included Alaska, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Hawaii, Illinois, Kansas, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, Michigan, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, Virginia, Washington, and Washington, DC.

Statistical analysis

We conducted all data management and analyses using SAS 9.4 and choropleth data visualization using Stata 19. We followed STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies.

Descriptive

To describe the sample of contacts during the study period, we present univariate statistics on characteristics of M + A Hotline contacts during the pre-Dobbs (June 24, 2021 to June 23, 2022) and post-Dobbs (June 24, 2022 to June 24, 2023) periods, including: the total number of unique Hotline contacts, average unique daily and monthly contacts, contact type (i.e., text vs. phone call), average number of text interactions per unique contact, and the primary reason for Hotline contact. We present means and standard deviations for continuous variables, numbers and proportions for categorical variables, and the magnitude of difference (including percent change or 95% confidence interval [CI]) for all variables pre- and post-Dobbs. To test the statistical significance of pre-post changes, we used t-tests for continuous variables, Satterthwaite estimates to account for unequal variance between groups, and X2 analyses for categorical variables. To examine differences in Hotline usage by state abortion environment, we present data on all measures stratified by post-Dobbs ban status, using t-tests and X2 analyses to assess statistical significance of group differences pre-post. Finally, we used simple linear regressions to test trends in the number of daily and monthly Hotline contacts overall, during pre- and post-Dobbs periods, and by state ban status, reporting beta coefficients and 95% CIs. We created choropleth maps of the number of unique contacts per state population of women aged 15–44 in 2022 (using population data from the American Community Survey) in the contiguous United States to depict geographic variation in Hotline contacts over time.

Comparative interrupted time series

We used a CITS design to examine whether the Dobbs ruling and associated implementation of state-level abortion bans was associated with changes in Hotline usage, while controlling for history bias due to time-varying confounders. Our CITS models compare post-Dobbs changes in M + A Hotline contacts for each group of states (i.e., ban vs. no-ban states) with their own baseline trend. Unobserved confounding would require factors coinciding with Dobbs that differentially affected ban states while leaving no-ban states unaffected.

We hypothesized that there would be an increase in contacts to the Hotline among states that implemented total abortion bans or early gestational limits post-Dobbs (“ban states”) compared to states that maintained or expanded abortion access post-Dobbs (“no-ban states”). To assess this hypothesis, we conducted a series of CITS models to examine the changes in M + A Hotline contact volume pre- and post-Dobbs, comparing states with and without post-Dobbs bans. This comparison allows us to estimate immediate and long-term changes in Hotline utilization attributable to post-Dobbs abortion policy changes, while accounting for pre-existing trends.

We analyzed both monthly and daily aggregations of Hotline contacts; while we primarily focus on analysis and interpretation of monthly contacts, we also analyzed daily contacts to test the association with increased statistical power. For analyses of monthly contacts, we defined our outcome as the total number of Hotline contacts per state per month from June 24, 2021, through June 23, 2023. For daily contacts, we defined our outcome as the total number of Hotline contacts per state per day over the same period.

Because Hotline contacts are count data and exhibited overdispersion, we modeled monthly counts (Yst) in state (s) during month (t) using generalized linear models with a negative binomial distribution and log link. To account for within-state correlation over time, we used generalized estimating equations with state-level clustering and an independent working correlation structure. Specifically, we estimated the following segmented regression models:

Yst∼NegBin(μst,k),log(μst)=β0+β1τt+β2Treats+β3(τt∗Treats)+β4Postt+β5(τt∗Postt)+β6(Treats∗Postt)+β7(τt∗Treats∗Postt)

In the above equation, time (τt) is a continuous variable representing the baseline pre-Dobbs time trend in Hotline contacts in comparison (no-ban) states, coded as months since the start of the study period (τt = 0 at June 2021). Treats is a binary 0/1 variable indicating ban (1) or no-ban (0) states, representing the difference in baseline contact levels between treatment groups. The interaction τt ∗Treats estimates the difference in baseline pre-Dobbs trends in Hotline contacts between ban and no-ban states. Postt is a binary 0/1 variable indicating pre- (0) or post-Dobbs (1) period, capturing the immediate change in contact levels in no-ban states at the time of the Dobbs decision, relative to the expected level based on the pre-Dobbs trend. The interaction τt∗Postt estimates the change in monthly trend in no-ban states after Dobbs. To assess differences between ban and no-ban states, we included two interaction terms representing our key parameters of interest. Treats∗Postt estimates the differential immediate change in contact levels in ban states relative to no-ban states post-Dobbs, relative to their expected level based on the pre-Dobbs trend, and the three-way interaction τt∗Treats∗Postt estimates the differential post-Dobbs trend change between ban and no-ban states. Because we used a log link distribution, we interpret exponentiated coefficients as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals. The counterfactual represents the expected trajectory of Hotline contacts in ban states if, following the Dobbs decision, they had experienced the post-Dobbs pattern observed in no-ban states, while maintaining their pre-Dobbs trends. Counterfactual trajectories for ban states were generated by setting post-Dobbs terms to zero to represent expected outcomes in the absence of post-Dobbs policy changes, while applying the post-Dobbs pattern observed in no-ban states while preserving pre-Dobbs trends.

Sensitivity analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. First, we estimated models excluding Texas to evaluate the influence of early enforcement of pre-viability abortion restrictions (e.g., SB8, enforced beginning in September 2021) and disproportionately high Hotline contact volume in that state. Second, to assess potential anticipatory effects related to the leak of the Dobbs decision in May 2022, we estimated models using the date of the Politico leak (May 2, 2022). Third, we examined robustness to model specification by estimating alternative Poisson regression models. Across sensitivity analyses, we assessed consistency in the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of key CITS parameters.

Role of the funding source

The funding sources that supported investigators during this study were not involved in any of the following activities: study design; data collection, analysis, and interpretation; manuscript writing; or deciding to submit for publication.

Results

Descriptive analyses

Trends in hotline contact characteristics, pre- and post-Dobbs

From June 24, 2021, to June 24, 2023, the M + A Hotline received 16,429 unique contacts (Table 2). Nearly one quarter (n = 4009) of these contacts occurred during pre-Dobbs with the remaining three-quarters (n = 12,420) occurring post-Dobbs. Average unique daily and monthly contacts increased by over 200% from pre- to post-Dobbs (Δdaily: 11 to 34, Δmonthly: 308 to 955, p < 0.001). Most Hotline contacts occurred via text, comprising 60% of contacts (n = 2390) pre-Dobbs and 52% of contacts (n = 6457) post-Dobbs. Each unique text contact had approximately 1.5 text interactions with Hotline volunteers, which remained unchanged pre-post (see Table 2).

Trends in hotline contacts by state ban status

Individuals contacting the Hotline from ban states comprised most contacts in both periods, accounting for 59% (n = 2382) of contacts pre-Dobbs and 67% (n = 8320) of contacts post-Dobbs (Table 3). Average daily and monthly contacts increased in both state groups, with markedly higher increases among post-Dobbs ban states (p < 0.001). Average contacts increased by nearly 250% pre-post in ban states (Δdaily: 6.7 to 22.7, Δmonthly: 183.2 to 640), compared to a roughly 150% increase in no-ban states (Δdaily: 4.6 to 11.2, Δmonthly: 125.2 to 315.4).

Trends in reasons for contacting hotline, pre- and Post-Dobbs and by state ban status

Users’ primary reason for contacting the Hotline changed substantially pre-post (Table 2; detailed sub-reasons in Supplementary Table S2). Pre-Dobbs, the most frequent reason for Hotline contact was questions before the abortion experience (48.9%), followed by questions during the abortion experience (34.9%). Post-Dobbs, the most frequent reason for contact was questions during the abortion experience (46.9%), followed by questions before the abortion experience (42.5%). In both periods, questions pertaining to abortions after in-clinic or telemedicine care, miscarriage management, and general SRH comprised notably smaller proportions of reasons for contact.

The number of contacts increased substantially for all reasons pre-post. Notably, the proportion of contacts with questions during an abortion experience increased by 12 percentage points post-Dobbs (34.9%–46.9%), while proportions of contacts with questions before an abortion experience, questions after in-clinic care, and additional SRH questions declined. The proportion of miscarriage management contacts remained stable from pre- to post-Dobbs. When examining changes in contact reason by state ban status over time (Table 3), the magnitude of increased contacts for all reasons was driven largely by contacts from ban states. Pre-post changes among contacts with questions before or during the abortion experience by state group were statistically significant (p < 0.001).

CITS analyses

Monthly comparative interrupted time series analysis

Fig. 3 displays monthly trends in M + A Hotline contacts before and after Dobbs, comparing ban states to no-ban states and including a counterfactual trajectory representing the expected pattern in ban states had they followed the post-Dobbs trends observed in no-ban states and is not constrained to align with observed values at the time of the Dobbs decision. IRRs from the monthly CITS model are shown in Table 4. Prior to Dobbs, trends in Hotline contacts diverged significantly between state groups, with ban states experiencing 7% higher monthly increases relative to no-ban states (IRR = 1.07; 95% CI: 1.02–1.12). In no-ban states, Hotline contacts continued to increase by 10% per month over time (IRR = 1.10; 95% CI 1.07–1.13). Baseline Hotline contact levels between ban and no-ban states were not statistically significantly different during the pre-Dobbs period (IRR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.41–1.61). Immediately following the Dobbs decision, contact levels in no-ban states remained stable and did not significantly change (IRR = 0.96; 95% CI: 0.83–1.11). Compared to the pre-Dobbs trend in contact levels in no-ban states, the post-Dobbs monthly contact trend in no-ban states declined by approximately 5% per month (IRR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.92–0.98). Regarding our parameters of interest, contact levels did not change significantly in ban states relative to no-ban states post-Dobbs (IRR = 0.98; 95% CI: 0.74–1.30). Post-Dobbs monthly trends diverged between the two groups, with growth in monthly Hotline contact levels decreasing by 5% per month in ban states relative to no-ban states post-Dobbs (IRR = 0.95; 95% CI: 0.91–1.00).

Sensitivity analysis

Results were generally consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table S3). CITS models excluding Texas did not significantly alter the direction, magnitude, or statistical significance of key estimates. Models assessing potential anticipatory effects related to the Dobbs leak using a May 2, 2022 intervention date produced similar divergence in trends between ban and no-ban states (Supplementary Fig. S1). Ban states demonstrated differential changes in Hotline contacts over time compared with no-ban states, consistent in direction and magnitude with the main analysis. Finally, model estimates were robust to alternative distributional assumptions. Poisson regression models yielded consistent findings with the primary negative binomial specification.

Daily comparative interrupted time series analysis

Daily CITS analyses further support findings from analysis of monthly contacts (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S4). Patterns observed in daily Hotline contacts mirrored those in the monthly analysis, with no statistically significant immediate post-Dobbs contact level changes and with evidence of differential trends in Hotline contacts between ban and no-ban states over time. These findings suggest that observed differences were not driven by temporal aggregation and were consistent across time scales.

Discussion

This study shows Hotline use was increasing rapidly prior to Dobbs—particularly in states that later enacted bans—suggesting that unmet need preceded the policy change. Post-Dobbs, trends diverged modestly without an immediate surge, indicating a more complex response than a simple step increase. Descriptive analyses reveal sizable increases in Hotline contacts post-Dobbs, with notably higher increases in states enacting post-Dobbs abortion bans compared to no-ban states. Findings from CITS analyses suggest that, after accounting for underlying baseline trends in contact usage in both groups—which were significantly increasing during the pre-Dobbs period, disproportionately so among ban states—the Dobbs decision was not associated with a differential immediate increase in contact levels between ban and no-ban states. Results indicate modest divergence in post-Dobbs trends between ban and no-ban states. Although contacts were increasing over time in both groups during the post-Dobbs period, the rate of contacts in ban states grew less during that period than that of no-ban states. We observed these patterns across multiple sensitivity analyses, including exclusion of Texas, alternative timing, and alternative model specifications.

While CITS analyses did not show a significant immediate post-Dobbs increase in ban states relative to no-ban states, we observed pronounced divergence in trends prior to Dobbs, with Hotline use increasing more rapidly in ban states before the Dobbs decision. This anticipated pre-Dobbs acceleration likely reflects responses to major policy signals and restrictions including Texas SB8 in September 2021, Dobbs oral arguments in December 2021, and the May 2022 opinion leak. The coefficient for the pre-Dobbs divergence between ban and no-ban states decreased by 3 percentage points (from a 7% per month increase among ban states relative to no-ban states to a 4% per month increase), demonstrating that Texas was a substantial driver in traffic to the Hotline prior to Dobbs (Supplementary Table S3, Model 1). Yet, even without Texas, contact rates were significantly diverging between ban and no-ban states pre-Dobbs.

Our analysis lays bare the following: that although the Dobbs decision precipitated the most severe, systematic legislative elimination of abortion access at the state level to date, all states enacting post-Dobbs abortion bans already had effectively implemented hostile environments towards abortion pre-Dobbs through complex webs of state laws restricting abortion access and service provision.13,14 Ultimately, we see the most pronounced divergence in Hotline contact rates between ban and no-ban states prior to the Dobbs decision, making the post-Dobbs slopes appear attenuated in comparison. This elevated pre-Dobbs growth in ban states helps explain the apparently attenuated post-Dobbs slope: when baseline trends are already steep, subsequent changes may appear muted in relative terms.

Nonetheless, we admit that several mechanisms may contribute to this post-Dobbs pattern. First, legal risk and concerns about anonymity may suppress help-seeking in ban states. For example, while ban states were absolutely enacting restrictions prior to Dobbs making it difficult if not impossible to access care formal healthcare prior to Dobbs, the national and public discourse around Dobbs instilled confusion about regulations in all states. The repercussions of misunderstanding the law thus becoming detrimental in ban states, likely leading to over-careful interpretation of laws in such environments.15 Second, demand may have shifted geographically, with cross-state information seeking. Third, increased awareness of abortion resources may have sustained growth in no-ban states. Finally, and most importantly, our data capture only individuals who contact the Hotline and cannot account for those who do not seek support. While we know that live birth rates are increasing, so too are the number of people who are obtaining medication abortion pills and having abortions outside the formal healthcare system.6,8,16 Thus, the observed attenuation does not necessarily indicate reduced need in ban states but may instead reflect a combination of pre-existing upward trends, changes in help-seeking behavior, and cross-state spillovers following Dobbs. As abortion restrictions grow more extreme in the post-Dobbs era, the demonstrated overall demand for the Hotline and observed divergence in Hotline utilization between post-Dobbs ban and no-ban states highlights the increasingly imperative role of abortion information and medical support hotlines as critical access points for individuals navigating new legal and logistical barriers. As abortion policy continues to evolve across states, demand for information and support services may serve as an indicator of access barriers. Our findings underscore the critical and indispensable role played by resources like the M + A Hotline for individuals navigating the abortion information ecosystem amidst extreme restrictions on access to care.

Importantly, this study provides additional perspective on related research demonstrating that the provision of abortion medications outside the formal healthcare system increased significantly after the Dobbs ruling17 and reaffirms that people have been navigating abortion information through formal and informal networks prior to the overturn of Roe v. Wade.18 The continued usage of the M + A Hotline in states without bans highlights that, even in legally supportive contexts, individuals seek informational support for abortion outside of the formal healthcare system due to various factors including privacy concerns, accessibility barriers, or personal preference.17 These findings underscore the need to maintain diverse options for abortion access and information irrespective of legal landscapes.

Additional notable findings include shifts in methods of communication (i.e., text vs. phone calls), timing, and nature of user inquiries. Regarding communication, the preference for text messaging over phone calls among Hotline users pre-Dobbs likely reflects convenience and perceived anonymity of texting,19,20 as well as the ability to retain information for later use. However, post-Dobbs, there was a relative decline in text-based interactions in favor of phone calls, suggesting a growing demand for real-time, personalized human support to navigate increasingly complex reproductive healthcare needs in the wake of heightened restrictions, concerns around anonymity, and increased anxiety post-Dobbs. Some users may perceive phone calls to facilitate more private and discrete communication by avoiding text messages that could be read or discovered by others.

This research also reveals a shift in the timing and nature of user inquiries. Post-Dobbs, more individuals contacted the Hotline during their abortion experience, compared to pre-Dobbs when users primarily sought information before initiating the process. This shift indicates that post-Dobbs users were more likely to have sought confirmation or reassurance that the abortion process had been successfully completed. This trend may reflect improved access to preliminary information about how to obtain the medications to manage an abortion or miscarriage, or an increased urgency to act amidst restrictive legal environments. This finding also underscores the adaptability of support networks and the importance of providing accurate, accessible guidance throughout all stages of the abortion process.21,22

Limitations

Despite these important findings, the study faced limitations due to the lack of standardized data collection. To maintain anonymity, Hotline volunteers did not systematically gather consistent information from users, leaving researchers reliant on raw text message conversations and phone call summaries. Importantly, the absence of individual-level demographics—including race and ethnicity, language proficiency in English and Spanish, and socioeconomic status—precludes assessment of equity or disparities in access to the M + A Hotline itself. As a result, this analysis cannot determine whether individuals who are most vulnerable to abortion restrictions were able to access Hotline support. Hotline users may represent a subset of individuals with greater awareness of the service, digital access, or resources, and therefore may not fully reflect the populations most impacted by post-Dobbs abortion bans.23,24

In addition, the anonymized nature of the dataset and limited geographic identifiers precluded robust state-level analyses. As a result, we were unable to examine variation about how state-specific abortion access barriers—such as travel distance, clinic availability, or gestational limits—may have shaped trends in Hotline utilization during the study period. Furthermore, state of contact was inferred using telephone area codes when callers did not explicitly report their location, which is an imperfect proxy for physical location given geographic mobility and the portability of mobile phone numbers within the United States. In the post-Dobbs environment, individuals seeking to preserve anonymity may also use non-local cell phone numbers, burner phones, or VOIP services associated with states perceived as more legally protective. Together, these factors may introduce misclassification, potentially inflating apparent Hotline utilization in states without abortion bans while underestimating the magnitude of demand in post-Dobbs ban states.

Notably, while this analysis is focused on medication abortion using misoprostol with or without mifepristone, a small number of individuals contact the Hotline with support needs regarding other methods of managing a miscarriage or abortion such as using herbs. Our measures did not account specifically for individuals seeking support regarding methods other than medication abortion. Additional research using survey and interview methods aims to incorporate demographic data and qualitative insights to better understand user experiences and needs. Taken together, this work is critical for developing tailored and effective support networks that meet the diverse needs of individuals seeking support during an abortion experience.

Conclusions

Our findings show that the Dobbs decision was associated with changes in patterns of contacts to the M + A Hotline, particularly from states with post-Dobbs abortion bans, most notably in the South. We identified divergence in trends between ban and no-ban states, suggesting evolving reliance on the Hotline in response to changing policy environments. These findings suggest that legal restrictions have reshaped demand for the type of clinical and logistical information and support provided by the hotline and have altered how and when individuals access that information and support. Our results illustrate how even in states with the most restrictive bans, people continue to have abortions and continue to seek out clinician support. As in-person options become less accessible, hotlines play an increasingly important role in providing evidence-based information and support.
